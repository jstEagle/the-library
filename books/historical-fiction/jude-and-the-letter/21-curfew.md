## 21. Curfew

The order went up on the customs house door at dusk, nailed there by a boy soldier whose hands shook so badly it took him three attempts to drive the nail. Beatrix watched from the window of the old surveyor's room across the square, where they had been quartered — the word *quartered* was generous; *stored* was more honest, the way one stores a document one intends to consult later — and read the proclamation twice through the falling snow.

*By order of Colonel Vess, Military Governor of Aldenmoor and Surrounding District: as of this evening, curfew is in effect from sundown bell to morning bell. All gates are closed. All movement between districts is forbidden without written pass. Any person found abroad after curfew will be detained for questioning.*

*Questioning.* The word sat on the page with the blandness of all bureaucratic language, and like all bureaucratic language, it was a curtain hung over something. Beatrix had spent their life behind such curtains. They knew what was asked in rooms without witnesses.

Below, the town was going dark in a way it never had before. Aldenmoor's winters had always been lit — windows glowing amber, the tavern spilling its noise onto the cobblestones, the lamplighter making his rounds with his long pole and his whistle. Now the windows were shuttered one by one, and the lamplighter walked his route at a hurry, glancing over his shoulder at the soldiers at the corners, and no whistle. Only the wind off the hills, carrying that sound that had become the town's second heartbeat: the far artillery, patient as weather.

The mill gate had died at four o'clock that afternoon. Beatrix had watched it happen from this same window — two wagons of soldiers arriving without warning, the timber gate barred and studded with new iron, the guard doubled, and old Master Corren, who had run the mill's weighing office for thirty years and who owed Jude's family favors stretching back past memory, marched out of his own doorway with his hands visible and his face gray. The grain requisition Jude had forged, the whole careful architecture of stamps and signatures and Corren's complicity — dead in an hour. Beatrix did not know yet whether Corren had talked. They only knew that he had not looked back at his office door, the way a man does not look back when he already knows what will become of what he leaves behind.

Three crossings. The bridge — guarded, but governed by ritual, by grief, by the slow traffic of funerals and their exemptions. The mill gate — closed now, sealed, its favors spent or betrayed. And the marsh ford.

The marsh ford, which was not a crossing at all in any official sense, which existed only in the memory of a smuggler's daughter and the legs of her child, which froze in January and swallowed livestock in thaw, and across which lay six miles of reeds and ice and darkness to the partisan camp where a hymnal full of names had already gone, and where men with their own ledgers were waiting for what Beatrix still carried against their ribs.

Beatrix touched their coat lapel, where the letter rode in its oilcloth sleeve, sewn flat into the seam by Jude's needle in a night of lamplight and held breath. It had become so much a part of them that they noticed its absence the way one notices a missing tooth — a wrongness in the architecture of the jaw. They had carried dispatches for eleven years. They had never before carried one that warmed.

* * *

Jude came at the morning bell, which was itself a small treason — moving through a locked town required either passes or paths, and Jude possessed paths the way the river possessed water. Beatrix heard them come up the stairs, no knock, the particular rhythm of boots that knew which stair creaked and stepped beside it, and then the door opened and Jude stood in it with snow in their hair and a look on their face that Beatrix had learned to dread, because it was the look of a clerk who has finished the arithmetic.

"The mill gate's gone," Jude said, though they both knew it. "Corren's been taken to the barracks. His daughter came to our door at dawn asking whether we'd seen him." A pause, precise as a ledger line. "She thinks we're the reason."

"Are you?"

"I'm one of the reasons." Jude shed their coat and stood dripping in the middle of the room, unwilling to sit, unwilling almost to exist within the walls. "Vess didn't just close the gate. He posted a manifest check at the bridge too — they're opening coffins now, Beatrix. Opening them. There's a woman down the lane who buried her husband Tuesday and they made her stand in the snow while a corporal put a bayonet through the shroud." Jude's voice stayed level, which was worse than breaking. "So the bridge is dying too. Grief doesn't buy passage anymore. He's learned our currency and devalued it."

"Then it's the ford."

"Then it's the ford." Jude turned to the window, to the gray light and the snow. "Six miles. Ice in the reeds — Hal says the shallows have frozen through, and where they haven't frozen it's peat soup to the thigh. At night, with the sentries sweeping the embankment with lanterns every quarter hour. Carrying weight." Their eyes went to Beatrix's coat, to the seam, without pointing at it. "Carrying you, if you go down. Because I've seen the marsh take a healthy man in December, and it took him slowly enough that we heard everything."

"We could wait for—"

"For what?" Jude turned, and there it was at last, the thing under the level voice. "For the convoy? The convoy's a rumor. For Vess to relax? Vess doesn't relax, he *audits*. For another requisition, another evacuation order, another list? Every day the letter sits in your seam is a day it can be found there. You know that better than anyone. Paper wants to be read. That's its whole nature."

"Yes," Beatrix said. "I know what paper wants. I've been its servant since I was nine years old."

They hadn't meant to say it that way, with the bitterness fully loaded, but it was out, and Jude received it the way they received everything — completely, without flinching, filing it somewhere Beatrix couldn't reach.

* * *

They argued the way the tide argues with a sea wall: in surges, in withdrawals, each leaving something behind on the other.

"It's one letter," Jude said, standing at the window with their arms folded. "One piece of paper, however heavy the seal. One promise from a republic that is currently being eaten alive from both ends. We've established what its promises weigh. I've read it, remember. Amnesty, signed, sealed, undelivered. It proves the republic knew and chose not to send it. That's worth something at a tribunal someday, maybe. Worth something to historians. Worth something to the partisans as leverage, God help us." They unfolded their arms and refolded them, and Beatrix saw that the hands beneath wanted very much to do something else entirely. "It is not worth your life. It is not worth a life. That's the whole entry in the ledger. Debit: one courier. Credit: a document saying sorry."

"You don't understand what it is," Beatrix said.

"Enlighten me."

And Beatrix found that they could not — not quickly, not cleanly — because what the letter was had shifted under their hands in these weeks until it was no longer a dispatch at all. It had begun as duty: sealed orders, a delivery obligation, the chain of custody that was the only parent the diplomatic service had ever given them. Somewhere between the torn corner and the marsh it had become evidence. Somewhere between the ledger reading and the burning convoy it had become a weapon. And sometime in the last few days, walking home through a curfewed town where every shuttered window held a name from a list, it had become the only thing in the world that said, in ink and wax and an official hand: *these people were promised. These people counted. Someone wrote it down.*

"They struck the transport lists once already," Beatrix said instead. "When the copies reached the camp. The partisans traded them back — thirty names pulled from the March transport. Thirty. That was the hymnal alone, copied names, secondhand paper. This—" their hand pressed their ribs "—this is the original. Signed. If it reaches the border, if it reaches the neutral archives with my testimony attached, it isn't thirty names. It's the whole machinery exposed. Every list Vess ever filled. It makes what he's doing *documented*, and documentation is the one thing men like him cannot survive. It's worth more than my life. That's not sentiment, Jude. That's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic." Jude laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as a snapped quill. "You sound like me. Do you know that? Three months ago you filed tariffs and apologized to me for existing, and now you stand in my— in this room doing sums with human beings on one side of the equals sign." They crossed the floor in three strides and stopped too close, close enough that Beatrix could see the sleeplessness in them, the marsh cold still living around their eyes. "Here's my arithmetic. The ford takes one life out of this town tomorrow night, and it's yours, and this town has lost enough names to lists. I am not going to carry your letter to the partisans and tell them the courier drowned six miles out with the proof still sewn in her coat because we decided a republic's apology was worth more than the person who delivered it."

*Her.* Beatrix heard it land — Jude's pronoun, chosen and deployed like a signature, the way they'd begun doing lately in the moments when precision mattered more than caution. It disarmed them more effectively than any argument could have. That was the trouble. That had been the trouble for weeks now: that every practical conversation between them had a second column running beneath it, unacknowledged, unaudited, and both of them kept books on it.

"If I don't go," Beatrix said quietly, "then Vess wins. Quietly. No drama, no patrol, no bayonet in a shroud. Just a letter that never moves, sitting in a seam, in a town that gets emptied next month and erased the month after. He won't even have to burn this one. Time will burn it for him."

"And if you do go, and the marsh takes you, he still wins. Same outcome. One fewer witness."

"Not the same. If I'm taken on the road, the letter's found. It enters his files. It becomes—" Beatrix stopped.

"Become what?"

"A copy with my name in the margins." They heard their own voice go thin. "He keeps copies, Jude. I've seen his habit now. Whatever happens, some version of me ends up in his papers. That's the price of having been audited by him at all."

The room went very quiet. Outside, a patrol passed below the window — four boots in the snow, the crunch and pause, crunch and pause — and they stood motionless until it faded, closer together than the argument warranted, and neither of them remarked on it.

Jude spoke first, and the fury had drained out of their voice, leaving something lower and more dangerous. "My mother ran the marsh forty times. Did I ever tell you why she stopped? Not the soldiers. Not the debt. She stopped because Tam Weller drowned beside her in the shallows — fourteen years old, her sister's boy — and she held his collar in the peat for as long as a person can hold anything, and the marsh let him go anyway. She never went past the churchyard wall again. She died three streets from the water." Jude's hands had gone still at last, hanging open at their sides. "She told me once: *the marsh doesn't care what you're carrying. It only weighs.*"

"So we don't go by the marsh."

"There is no by. There's only the ford, and the ford only cares about weight and time, and you want to bring it both."

* * *

They did not resolve it. Arguments like this one do not resolve; they exhaust, and then they change shape, and what remains standing afterward is rarely the position either party entered with.

What remained, by the time the light failed and the curfew bell tolled its flat iron note across the rooftops, was this: that Beatrix would not give up the letter, and Jude would not let Beatrix cross alone, and both of them understood that these two positions could not coexist past a certain point and had agreed, silently, in the manner of people who have argued themselves to the edge of a cliff, to postpone mathematics until they had gathered more terms.

Because there were other things to settle first, and Jude laid them out now at the table in the last of the light, in the low voice of a clerk dictating minutes: which night — not tomorrow, tomorrow the patrols would be thick with whatever Corren had or hadn't said; the night after, perhaps, when Vess's vigilance would have found its plateau. Who would hold the lantern and who the rope. Which stretch of reed bank Hal swore was frozen through and which was a lie the ice told. Where the partisans' picket would be waiting, and what countersign the hymnal's journey had bought them.

And underneath the logistics, both of them listening to the other's voice the way you listen to a instrument you are afraid you will not hear again.

"One more term," Beatrix said, when the list was done. "If I go through the ice. If I'm going down and you can't reach me — you cut the letter out of my coat and you keep moving. That's not negotiable. That's the condition of my going."

Jude looked at them across the table for a long moment, snow-dark light, the far guns muttering their one dull syllable beyond the hills.

"No," Jude said. "But I'll be there, and we'll see what I choose when it's real. That's the only honest answer I have left tonight."

Beatrix should have pressed. Eleven years of protocol said press; the service had raised them on contingency and fallback and the doctrine that every instruction must survive the failure of the instructor. But they looked at Jude's face — at the stubborn, rooted, infuriating, beloved face of the only person in this collapsing republic who had ever chosen them for anything other than their usefulness — and they heard themselves say, in a voice they barely recognized as their own:

"All right. That's enough. That's the agreement."

It was the first clause they had ever signed without reading, and they felt it take hold somewhere under the ribs, beside the letter, like a second document with a different seal.

Later — much later, past midnight, in the dark of the surveyor's room with the town holding its breath around them — Beatrix woke to the sound of boots in the square again. More boots than a patrol. Boots in numbers, and wagon wheels, and the creak of the customs house doors thrown wide, and a voice giving orders in the clipped cadence of a man who has been waiting for permission.

They went to the window.

Down in the square, torchlit despite the curfew, soldiers were carrying the ledgers out of the customs house — armfuls, cratefuls, the bound gray record of every name Aldenmoor had ever trusted to paper — and loading them into a wagon under a tarpaulin, while Colonel Vess stood on the steps with a lantern held high, checking each load against a list in his gloved hand.

And beside the wagon, wrapped in a soldier's blanket, gray-faced and talking — head bent toward the colonel's ear, pointing once, briefly, up at the dark windows of the town —

stood Master Corren.
