## 22. The Hymnal Returns

The messenger came back on the third morning, and he came back wrong.

Jude knew it before the man said a word — knew it in the way Sela Marta's boy walked up the lane from the marsh side, hatless, his coat buttoned to the wrong eyelet, as though he had dressed in the dark or in a hurry or both. His name was Piet. He was nineteen and he had a milkman's shoulders and he had volunteered for the crossing because he could swim the reed channels blind, having done it since he was eight, poaching eels. Now he stood at the smokehouse door with marsh mud dried gray on his boots and would not look at Jude directly.

"They kept the hymnal," he said.

The word *kept* sat in the cold air between them like a coin someone had dropped and refused to pick up.

"Kept it," Jude repeated.

"Passed it along like you said, to the woman with the red scarf, and she took it and read it by the fire and then a man came — an officer of theirs, or what they call an officer — and he turned the pages slow, and then he put it inside his own coat, and nobody gave it back." Piet finally raised his eyes. "They sent a message instead."

The message was written in pencil on the back of a ammunition requisition form, which Jude supposed was the only paper the partisans owned that nobody else wanted. It was signed with nothing but a drawn bird — some crude swift, wings swept back — and it named a place and an hour: *the heron stand, two nights hence, one person only, come alone or not at all.*

Below that, in smaller letters, almost apologetic: *We have your names. We will discuss what they are worth.*

*What they are worth.* Jude read the line four times, and each time it meant something worse. Not *we will protect them.* Not *they are safe now.* Worth. As if the hundred and fourteen conscripted souls of Aldenmoor had been carried across the marsh not to sanctuary but to market.

Marta's boy shifted his weight. "I'm sorry," he said. "I did it right. I swear I did it right."

"You did it right." Jude folded the requisition form along its existing creases, which were many, and put it inside a glove that had lost its pair two winters ago. "Go home. Sleep. If anyone asks, you delivered eels and got paid in salt."

Piet went. Jude stayed in the smokehouse a long while among the hanging hams and the sweet rot smell of applewood, listening to the wind come off the marsh with that thin edge it carried now, the ice already thinking its way into the water.

The hymnal was gone. The copies of the conscription lists — every name, every street, every age, copied by lamplight in Beatrix's careful clerk's hand and Jude's quicker one, hidden between *Abide With Me* and *Now the Day Is Over* — were in the hands of strangers who spoke of worth.

And somewhere beneath the floor of the world, under the ice that hadn't formed yet, the original letter still waited in its oilcloth, and the partisans knew it existed, and now everyone's arithmetic had changed.

* * *

Two nights later Jude crossed the marsh in the dark, following the stakes.

There were no paths out there. There never had been, not truly — there was only a grammar of stakes and bent willows and certain hummocks that held weight and certain ones that didn't, passed down through three generations of Jude's family like a language spoken only at night. Jude's mother had taught it as a catechism. *Left hand on the crooked stake, count nine steps, trust the sedge but never the shine.* The shine was the killer. Standing water looked solid in moonlight; new ice looked old; old ice lied about its thickness the way a debtor lies about his debts. You learned the marsh by nearly dying in it, and then you taught your children, and that was the whole inheritance.

The cold had teeth now. Each breath came in with a rasp and went out as a flag of white. Jude wore two coats and wool wrapped to the eyes and moved at the pace of certainty, not speed, because the marsh forgave slowness and punished nothing else. Somewhere far behind, Aldenmoor lay dark under its curfew, one lantern at the bridge checkpoint and one at the mill gate and none at all in the windows of houses where people lay awake doing sums about their children's names.

An hour in, the reeds opened onto the long shallow where the herons nested in summer, and there was the stand itself — a platform on pilings built by duck hunters before the war that ended, repaired since by men who shot at things other than ducks. A single fire burned on the shore beyond it, banked low, and around the fire Jude counted five shapes, maybe six, wrapped in army blankets that had been army blankets once.

"One person," said a voice from the stand itself, and Jude started, badly, because the speaker had been above the whole time — a woman coming down the ladder with a rifle slung backward so it rode silent against her spine. She was perhaps fifty, weathered down to tendon and opinion, with a red scarf wound twice around her throat. "Good. You can walk. Most clerks can't."

"I'm not most anything," Jude said, "most days."

She looked Jude over with the unhurried thoroughness of someone appraising livestock or timber. Then she jerked her chin toward the fire, and they walked, and the shapes around the fire resolved into faces: young, mostly, hollowed, with the particular stillness of people who had stopped expecting anything good.

"I'm called Sela Ryn," the woman said, when they reached the warmth. No rank. The drawn bird on the note, Jude understood suddenly, was not an emblem but an evasion — these people signed nothing. "You're the clerk. The smuggler's daughter's child."

"Son," Jude said. "But yes."

"Your mother ran the east channel in the famine year. Ran it past the customs cutters. I was a girl then; I remember her name being said the way you say a saint's." She said this without warmth, as if reciting evidence. "Which is why you're standing here instead of face down in the reeds. Understand that. Sentiment bought you this meeting. It buys nothing else tonight."

Around the fire, the young ones watched with their hollow faces, and one of them — a boy no older than Piet — had the hymnal.

He was holding it loosely, half-tucked under his arm like a prayer book at a funeral, and when Jude's eyes fixed on it he noticed, and turned it over in his hands, examining it again as if he'd only just been told it mattered.

"We read it," Sela Ryn said. "All of it. Every name. One hundred and fourteen conscripts, ages fifteen to fifty-one, with streets and trades and next-of-kin." She crouched and fed a stick to the fire, and the sparks went up sideways in the wind off the water. "Do you know what we do with lists like that?"

"Protect them, I hoped."

"Hoped." She smiled without any of her face joining in. "We use them. A list of names is a lever, clerk. Those hundred and fourteen are bakers' sons and mill hands and one schoolteacher, and every one of them has a mother, and every mother in this province knows someone who works for the requisitioning army — cooks for them, launders for them, sells them eggs. Names are how we find the doors. Your town's names open perhaps forty doors for us. That's what your hymnal is worth to us. That's what it cost us to keep."

"It isn't ours to sell," Jude said, and heard how it sounded even as it left — the voice of a man defending a ledger, precise and bloodless, while around this fire sat boys who had eaten their boots in some winter Jude had only read about. "It isn't mine. It isn't yours either. Those names belong to the people carrying them."

"And the letter?" Sela Ryn let the question fall gently, the way you set down a heavy thing on purpose. "Where does the letter belong?"

The fire cracked. Out on the black shallows, ice sang — a high glassy note, the sound the marsh made on the first hard nights, expanding, testing itself.

"We know about the letter," she went on, before Jude could arrange a face. "Your boy Piet is brave and honest and cannot hold water in his hands, metaphorically speaking. He told the woman with me that the courier carried more than tariffs, that there was a sealed diplomatic pouch, that the whole town whispered about it. We put the rest together ourselves. An amnesty letter, undelivered. Signed. Sealed. The republic promising, in its own hand, to spare the towns it was already feeding to the transports." She looked into the fire. "I've seen documents like that before. In the last collapse. They burn well."

"It's evidence," Jude said. "It has to reach the neutral border. It has to reach the archives, the newspapers, whatever exists after —"

"After." She tasted the word. "There is no after yet. There is before, and there is now, and in the now, that letter is the single most valuable piece of paper between here and the sea. Do you know what my people could trade it for? Arms. Medicine. Passage for our wounded. One signature from a dead republic, properly placed, and we could stop burying children in frozen ground." Her eyes came up, and they were not cruel eyes, which was somehow the worst of it. They were simply accounting. "We want it, clerk. And here is our price, so there's no misunderstanding later."

She laid it out plainly, the way her drawn bird promised nothing and her voice promised everything: the partisans would take the hymnal's work upon themselves — would see the hundred and fourteen names struck from the transport lists by whatever means their levers allowed, bribery of quartermasters, substitution of falsified rolls, pressure on the garrison's frightened suppliers. In exchange, the original letter came to them. Not to the border. To them. To be spent as they saw fit, on whatever bargain bought the most rifles, in whatever hall the bargaining happened, and the fate of Aldenmoor's amnesty would rest in the hands of people who signed with birds because signatures were things that could be found afterward and followed home.

"And if you spend it badly," Jude said quietly. "If you trade it for guns and the guns lose anyway, and the transport lists stand — then one hundred and fourteen people go east because I handed away the only proof the republic ever promised otherwise."

"Yes," said Sela Ryn. "That is possible. We weigh possibilities every day out here. So does your colonel Vess, I'd wager, except he weighs people." She stood, brushing sparks from her coat, and for a moment the firelight made her tall as a stilted heron, patient and terrible in the shallows. "You have until the ford freezes. Three nights, maybe four, if the wind stays kind. Bring the letter to the heron stand and your names go free. Don't bring it, and we keep the hymnal — and we'll still use it, understand, we'll still work your forty doors, because we're not monsters, only poor. But the striking of names goes last on our list instead of first, and last, in wartime, usually means never."

The boy with the hymnal shifted, and for a heartbeat Jude thought he might step forward and give it back — there was something in his face, young and unhappy, a conscience arriving late. Sela Ryn put out one hand without looking, the way you quiet a dog, and the boy went still.

"Walk carefully," she said. "The ice is lying tonight."

* * *

The way back was longer than the way there; fear always adds distance.

Jude moved from stake to stake through the dark with the partisan's price riding in the chest like a swallowed stone, and the marsh talked all around — the tick and groan of forming ice, the sudden collapsing splash of some channel giving way, the wind combing the dead reeds with a sound like pages turning. Pages turning. Everything was paper now. The whole world had become a ledger, and every path forward ran through somebody's column of figures.

At the halfway hummock Jude stopped, because stopping was discipline, and stood breathing through wool in the freezing dark, and let the thought arrive fully for the first time:

The partisans were right about the letter's value, and that was exactly the problem. A thing worth that much gets people killed for it — not enemies, necessarily. Allies kill for valuable things too. They just feel worse about it after, and write it down as necessity in ledgers of their own.

And Beatrix — Beatrix, who had lain awake two nights ago saying *it has to reach the border, that's the whole point, that's the only thing I am* — Beatrix would have to be told that the allies wanted to divert the river of history through their own mill. Would have to choose, again, and sooner, and harder than ever.

The ford would freeze in three nights. Four, if the wind stayed kind.

Jude's mother had a saying for nights like this, said over cards or debts or the question of whether to run a load through a channel patrolled by gunboats: *When everyone wants your cargo, that's when you learn what you're really carrying.*

Jude put a hand on the crooked stake, counted nine steps, trusted the sedge and not the shine, and went on toward the lights of Aldenmoor, where a courier was surely awake, watching the ceiling, holding the lie together by threads — and where, unknown to either of them, in the customs house on the square, Colonel Vess had already begun to write down discrepancies in his neat, patient hand.

The first hard frost came that same night. By morning, the marsh had begun to close like a book.
