## 23. Vess Suspects

The summons came at the hour when Aldenmoor's light turned to tin.

Beatrix saw it first as a folded slip of paper delivered by a boy too young to shave, one of the requisition corps' runners, standing in the doorway of the courier's quarters with his cap crushed in both fists like something he'd been ordered to kill. *Customs house. Six o'clock. Colonel Vess.* No salutation. No seal. The absence of a seal was itself a message: this was not official business, and therefore there was no procedure to hide behind, no regulation Beatrix could cite, no form to fill out that would make what was about to happen into something with edges and rules. It was just a man asking another man to come to a room.

They dressed carefully. That was the strange arithmetic of fear — it made you think about your collar. Beatrix chose the gray coat, the one without the diplomatic braid they had quietly unpicked two nights before with a seam ripper borrowed from Frau Halloran downstairs, and boots that had been polished but not too well polished, and the pocket watch that did not work because a working watch could be checked against a working watch and a stopped one could only be apologized for. Small deceptions, layered like felt against frost. They had been lying for weeks now — small lies, structural lies, lies of omission laid down like courses of brick — and still, standing before the spotted mirror in the courier's quarters, Beatrix felt each one shift and settle against their ribs like ballast in a hull taking water.

Outside, the town had gone quiet in the way towns go quiet under occupation: not silent, but muffled, every sound pulled inward. A dog barked twice behind the tannery and was silenced by a boot. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, apologetic columns. At the corner by the baker's — Brenner's bakery, Brenner whose name sat on a transport list Beatrix had read by lamplight with Jude's breath held beside them — two soldiers stood warming their hands over a brazier, and one of them watched Beatrix the whole length of the street, unhurried, the way a man watches a cart he has already decided to stop tomorrow.

The customs house had been theirs once. Beatrix had filed dispatches at the second-floor window desk for three years, cataloguing the republic's attention as it flickered over Aldenmoor like sun through moving cloud — tariffs here, a census there, nothing that mattered, because nothing in Aldenmoor mattered, and Beatrix had loved the town precisely for its irrelevance, the way an orphan loves any room where no one asks about their parents. Now the window desk belonged to a sergeant with ink-stained cuffs, and the ledgers were gone from their shelves, and the smell of the place had changed. Paper and sealing wax and cold tea had become leather and tobacco and the particular metallic odor of soldiers, of weapons oil and boot blacking and the faint copper undertone of a building that had recently been shouted in.

Colonel Vess received them in the customs master's old office, which he had arranged exactly as the customs master had kept it — same map on the wall, same ledgers' ghost-shapes on the empty shelves, same chipped blue cup on the saucer by the inkwell — and this fidelity was the most frightening thing about him. Other men conquered a room by changing it. Vess conquered it by making you understand it had never been yours.

"Courier Thornbury." He did not rise. He was a narrow man, gray going to white at the temples, with the unremarkable face of a good auditor and hands that lay flat and motionless on the desk like two dead birds. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. Would you take tea? Marta makes it very strong; I've told her the republic runs on it."

"I don't drink tea after noon, Colonel. Thank you."

"Nor do I." He moved the cup six inches to the left, out of his own reach, a gesture so small and so deliberate that Beatrix understood it was meant to be noticed. "Then we'll be uncomfortable together. Good. Honesty begins with shared discomfort, my mother used to say. She was a schoolmistress. She believed all errors were pedagogical." He folded his hands again. "I have been reviewing the requisition inventories. You were custodian of the dispatch archive for how long?"

"Three years in October."

"Three years in October." He nodded slowly, as if tasting the phrase. "And in that time, incoming diplomatic pouches were logged within—"

"Within twelve hours of arrival. Standing instruction from the regional directorate, section fourteen."

"Section fourteen." His eyes did not move, did not blink at the speed other eyes blinked. "There is a pouch logged on the ninth of last month. Diplomatic classification, priority red. Logged, yes. But the accompanying manifest — the itemized inventory that section fourteen requires — is missing. Not unsigned. Not incomplete. Missing. As though it had never existed." He let that sit in the room like smoke. "Curious, don't you find?"

"The manifests for that week went into the general file, sir. There was an error in sequencing. I recall the pouch; it arrived during the artillery, and the clerk who normally handled manifests had left for the census work in the valley villages. I logged it myself, under duress of the standing order, and flagged the manifest for follow-up."

"You recall it."

"I do."

"And the flag?"

"Verbal, sir. To Sergeant Iles, who was reassigned before he could action it. I have regretted the informality since."

Vess smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which was worse. It was the smile of a man watching a clock run. "Sergeant Iles," he said. "Yes. We wrote to him. He does not remember the conversation." He opened a folder on the desk — a plain manila folder, soft-cornered with handling, and Beatrix's heart performed a single enormous contraction and then resumed beating in a body gone cold. "You will forgive me. Auditing is my trade. Before the army, I spent nineteen years reconciling grain accounts for the commissary. Numbers confess more readily than people. They have no shame." He turned a page. "Item: the guard rotation log for the night of the eleventh shows the customs house side door unbarred between the hours of two and four. Item: the mill gate register shows a grain requisition stamped with the customs house counter-seal, number 4471, which corresponds to a seal recorded as lost in the requisitioning. Item: a coffin crossed the bridge on a Thursday bearing a hymnal, according to a sentry with good eyes and bad judgment, and the coffin contained a woman who had died of the fever on a Tuesday and should by then have smelled of it, and did not, according to the men who carried her." He closed the folder. None of it was aimed. All of it was aimed. "None of these things proves anything, Courier Thornbury. That is what disturbs me. Coincidence has a texture. I have audited enough of it to know its weave. This—" he touched the folder with one finger, almost tenderly, "—this is not coincidence. This is pattern. And patterns want authors."

The room ticked. Somewhere below, a soldier laughed at something, and the laugh cut off as if a door had closed on it.

"Colonel," Beatrix said, and heard their own voice arrive steady, which astonished them, "you are describing a town under pressure. People move oddly under occupation. Doors fail. Seals are stolen or mislaid. Grief makes clerks careless. If I could help you untangle it I would, but I am a courier, not an investigator. My work ended when your requisition sealed the archives. I have been waiting since, as instructed, for the relief convoy."

"Yes." Vess leaned back. Outside the window behind him the sky was doing something theatrical, bruised purple stacking over the western hills where the front had been grinding for a month. "The relief convoy. Let us speak of it plainly, since we are being plain. You are a courier in the service of a republic that has, functionally, ceased to transmit. Your last confirmed orders are five weeks old. The convoy you wait for may be a week away or may be burning in a ditch outside Harsk. And yet you sit in Aldenmoor, in a town you might have walked out of a dozen times, past a dozen unguarded fields, waiting. For what, Courier Thornbury? For paper?" He tilted his head. "You must love it very much."

"I was raised by it," Beatrix said, and the truth of that came out before they could weigh it, and hung between them, and Vess received it the way a jeweler receives a stone — turning it, weighing it, setting it down without comment.

"So was I," he said. "Ledgers, though. Ledgers are honest. Paper lies beautifully. That is why I prefer mine numbered." He stood, and crossing to the window, put his hands behind his back, and spoke to the glass. "Here is what will happen. Tomorrow evening at this hour, you will return to this room. You will bring me either an explanation that survives my audit, or the manifest, or whatever object it is that the manifest would have described. One of the three. I am not a cruel man, Thornbury. Cruelty is inefficient; it produces confessions instead of facts. But I am thorough, and thoroughness, given time, arrives at the same place cruelty does. Only slower, and with better records." He turned. The lamplight had come up somewhere without Beatrix noticing — the soldier at the door must have lit it — and it caught the colonel's face in half, leaving the other half in the color of the coming night. "You have one night. Consider it a gift of the same republic you serve. It, too, gives people one night, and then it audits them."

* * *

Walking back through Aldenmoor, Beatrix discovered that a town can be full of people and still be the loneliest architecture ever built.

Lamplight showed in windows. Behind one, on Tanner's Row, a family moved past the glass in silhouette, a mother lifting a child, the arc of it gentle as a pendulum. From the well square came the sound of voices, low and rhythmic — the evening recitation had begun, Jude's invention, the town's names spoken aloud into the cold air like seeds sown into winter ground, and Beatrix stopped at the edge of the square long enough to hear a woman's voice say *Halloran, Marta, spinster, third house on Tanner's Row,* and a man's voice answer *Halloran, Marta, present,* and the small terrible comfort of it, the ledger made of breath. They could not go into the square. A soldier leaned against the pump, ostensibly guarding the water, actually counting heads. So Beatrix stood in the dark at the square's edge, twenty steps from thirty neighbors, and listened to their names read out like a lesson, and understood with perfect clarity that if Vess was right — if his pattern had an author, and the author was them, and the author was caught — then every voice in that square would go quiet in a different way, and it would be their throat that closed it.

That was the loneliness. Not the dark street. Not the soldier's eye. The arithmetic: that they had chosen, alone, at a desk, in an hour no one had witnessed, to convert themselves from instrument to accomplice, and that no one could now share the weight of it, because sharing it would double the danger rather than halve it. Jude knew. Jude knew everything, and Beatrix loved them for it with a ferocity that frightened them — but knowing was not carrying. When the knock came at Jude's door, it would be Jude's wrist the soldiers took, and Beatrix would stand in this same square and hear the recitation skip a name.

They walked past the bakery. Dark. Brenner's shutters up early, the smell of yesterday's bread gone stale in the doorway, and Beatrix thought: *his name is on a list because I logged the pouch that held the letter that could strike it, and I hold it in a hiding place I will not name even inside my own skull, and tonight a man with a folder and a schoolmistress's patience has asked me to choose.* The watch in their pocket, the stopped one, ticked never, and they checked it anyway, twice, the way the frightened check things.

* * *

Frau Halloran had left a lamp burning on the stairs of the courier's house, as she did every night now — *so the house knows someone's expected,* she said — and its small yellow circle on the third step undid Beatrix more thoroughly than Vess's folder had. Kindness, unasked and unverifiable, was the one document left in Aldenmoor with no discrepancies in it.

They sat at the little desk under the window and took out paper and did not write. What explanation survives an audit? Vess had said. Beatrix turned the question over the way one turns a coin found in the street, checking both faces. An explanation would need a thief — a clerk reassigned, a seal mislaid, a dead woman's coffin — but every thief they invented would need a history, and histories could be written to, and letters to the valley villages took three days they did not have. The manifest could be forged; Vess would know its paper, its watermark, its age; forging for an auditor was like lying to a mirror. The object itself — the letter, the amnesty, the republic's signed and sealed abandonment — could be surrendered, but surrendered letter meant surrendered town, and surrendered town meant the recitation at the well ending mid-name.

Or confession. The word sat down across from them like a guest.

Confession, and what followed: Vess's protection, offered once before over tea, offered again with interest. Their own survival purchased at the price of the route, the hymnal, the partisan line across the marsh, and Jude's name entered into a folder with soft corners. They tried to imagine it — the exchange conducted in this same polite register, *thank you for coming, would you take tea* — and found they could not complete the image. Not because they were brave. They catalogued their bravery honestly and found it nearly empty. But some ledger in them refused the entry, some inherited column that would not balance with betrayal written into it, and Beatrix recognized, sitting alone in the lamplight with the ink drying unused on the nib, that they had finally come to the thing the diplomatic service had never taught them: that loyalty to paper was easy, because paper did not bleed; and that the night's true audit was not Vess's but their own, and it had only one line item, and it was being drawn up right now, in the dark, by a hand they could not see.

A floorboard shifted below. Frau Halloran, banking her fire. Or not.

Beatrix rose, snuffed the lamp, and stood at the window with the town laid out beneath them — the well square, the bridge, the black line of the marsh beyond the mill where ice was forming in the reeds — and counted the hours until the appointment. Fourteen. In fourteen hours they would sit across from those dead-bird hands and either produce a miracle or begin the long polite unraveling.

Down the street, at the corner by the tannery, a figure detached itself from a doorway and crossed toward the courier's house without hurry, hood up, gait familiar as a heartbeat. Jude. Come in the dark, past the sentries, because word traveled fast in a small town and a summons at six o'clock was town-crier news by eight. Beatrix watched the figure pause at the corner, check the street both ways with a smuggler's economy, and start toward the door, and felt the loneliest hour of their life crack open at exactly one point, letting in a draft of cold air and one person.

They went down to unbolt it before Jude could knock.

Below the hill, at the edge of hearing, the front cleared its throat — one gun, then another — and the lamplight in the windows of Aldenmoor shivered, and held.
