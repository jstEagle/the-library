## 24. A Night of Small Treasons

The curfew bell had stopped meaning anything by the second week. It still rang at nine — old Maren pulled the rope out of habit more than obedience — but the soldiers no longer patrolled in pairs as they had at first. They patrolled alone now, collars up, breath smoking, and they looked at the ground instead of the windows, because a man who looks at windows must decide whether to report what he sees, and it is winter, and everyone is tired.

Jude counted on that tiredness the way other people counted on God.

The night began at the smokehouse, where Jude's brother Tam had not spoken to them since the confrontation among the hanging hams — since the word *informant* had been said aloud and neither of them had taken it back. But Tam kept the keys to the ice cellar, and the ice cellar opened onto the drainage culvert that ran beneath the tanner's row and came up behind the well on Marsh Lane, which was forty paces from the house of Widow Corrag, who owned a boat.

Jude stood outside the smokehouse for a long moment, hands in their pockets, watching their own breath climb into the dark like small ghosts abandoning ship. The hams hung pale and heavy inside, and through the fogged window a lamp moved — Tam, still awake, still working at this hour, because sleep was another thing the family had lost along with trust.

Jude knocked twice, paused, once. The old signal from childhood, from the years when the worst thing they conspired at was stealing plums from Deel's orchard.

The lamp stopped moving. A long silence. Then the bolt went back.

Tam stood in the doorway with the lamp held high, as if he needed to be sure which sibling had come to torment him. His face was thinner than Jude remembered it being a month ago, and there was a bruise-yellow stain along his jaw that he had not explained and Jude had not asked about.

"You have some nerve," Tam said quietly.

"I need the cellar key."

"Of course you do." He didn't move from the doorway. Behind him the hams swayed faintly in a draft that shouldn't have existed. "You need something, so we're family again. That's how it works."

"Tam." Jude made their voice as flat and steady as a ledger line. "In three days the colonel loads every ledger in the customs house onto his wagons, and every name in this town goes where those lists say it goes. Mama's name is on one of them. You've seen it. I know you've seen it, because you read everything, you always read everything, even when you were small enough to stand on a stool to reach the desk."

Something moved behind Tam's eyes — the old shame of the boy who read other people's letters.

"I'm taking the names across the marsh," Jude said. "Tomorrow night, maybe the night after. I need the culvert to come up clean behind the well, and I need nobody to have seen me take the key."

"And if I say no?"

Jude looked at him. There was no good answer to that, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them like the third person in the room.

Tam reached inside his coat and brought out the key ring. He worked the cellar key off it with fingers that were steadier than his face, and held it out — but when Jude took it, he did not let go immediately.

"The sentry at the ford rotates at midnight," he said. "There's a gap of about four minutes when Halder comes off post and before Brenner takes it. Halder stops to warm himself at the brazier by the customs house wall. He always has. Even before the war he was slow in the cold." He released the key. "I haven't told them anything about the ford, Jude. Whatever else I've done. That much I never gave them."

And then he shut the door, softly, before Jude could decide what to feel.

* * *

Widow Corrag's house smelled of eucalyptus and lamp oil, and she answered the door already dressed, which told Jude she had been expecting someone tonight and had simply been waiting to learn if it would be them or the soldiers.

"You'll want tea," she said, which was not a question, and by the time Jude had gotten halfway through explaining, the tea was on the table and Corrag was looking at them over the rim of her cup with the flat black appraisal of a heron watching water.

"My mother's boat," Jude said.

"Your mother's boat is under my dock tarpaulin, bottom-side up, for eleven years," Corrag said. "Caulk doesn't keep. Rope rots. And your mother is dead, child, God rest her, and smuggling died with her, or was supposed to."

"The caulk can be redone by morning if I start tonight. I'll pay for materials."

"With what?"

Jude put the coins on the table. Not many — the courier's expense fund had dwindled to this, and after this there would be nothing but Beatrix's sealed dispatch case and whatever promises remained in it. Corrag looked at the coins the way an accountant looks at an insult, then swept them into her apron pocket anyway.

"For your mother," she said. "Not for you. Your mother ran the marsh in weather that would freeze the tears on a woman's face, and she never once got wet feet, do you understand? She knew the reed channels better than the eels did. She'd have done this run in her sleep." Corrag drank. "You are your father's child. Careful. Careful people drown in two feet of water."

"Then teach me not to drown."

For a long moment the widow studied Jude, and Jude understood that something larger than a boat rental was being weighed in that look — that all down Marsh Lane, all through Aldenmoor, similar weighings were happening behind lit windows and dark ones, and that a town is never more alive than on the night it decides.

"Bring the boat to my dock at dusk tomorrow," Corrag said finally. "I'll see to the caulk myself; you'd botch it. And I'll walk you the first channel — the one your mother called the Needle, because you thread it or you bleed. After that you're on your own, because after that I'm an old woman asleep by her stove and I don't know what you're doing on my marsh." She set down her cup. "One condition."

"Name it."

"When it's done — when the names are over and the letter's wherever letters go — you come back here and tell me how it ended. Old women die of not knowing. It's the only dishonest way left available to us."

* * *

The forge was cold — Ferrant only banked his fires these days, coal being requisitioned like everything else — but the light under his door burned past midnight, and when Jude knocked, the big man opened it with a hammer still in his fist and fear all over his face until he saw who it was.

"God's teeth, clerk, I thought—" He didn't finish. He stood back to let Jude in.

The workshop smelled of iron scale and tallow. On his bench, half-finished, lay horseshoes — real ones, army-pattern — and Jude understood that Ferrant had been making himself useful to the occupation in small dull ways, the way a man keeps his head down in floodwater.

"Ferrant, I need bolts. Six of them, cut fresh, no heads, and a cold chisel filed down small enough to pass in a hymnal's binding."

Ferrant set down his hammer very slowly. "Bolts without heads," he said, "and a chisel in a hymnal. That's not carpentry, clerk. That's the kind of thing men hang for, and they don't even hang you facing the right way anymore."

"Yes."

Another silence. Outside, somewhere toward the bridge, a patrol's boots rang on frozen cobbles, unhurried, and both of them listened until the sound faded.

"My boy is on the transport list," Ferrant said at last. It wasn't a question either. Everyone in Aldenmoor knew the shape of the list even if they hadn't seen its pages; the knowledge had traveled through the town the way damp travels through plaster. "Sixteen years old. They wrote him down as seventeen because the arithmetic suited them."

"They wrote a great deal of arithmetic that suits them."

"Aye." Ferrant turned away, and his shoulders moved once, and when he faced Jude again his face was composed into the expression of a man who has finished deciding. "Come back at five. Bolts will be done. Chisel too. And clerk—" He picked the hammer back up, needing the weight of it in his hand. "I've been shoeing their horses. Don't think I don't know what that makes me. A man tells himself he's only keeping the forge lit, only feeding his family, only, only. Then someone knocks at midnight and asks for headless bolts, and he finds out what he's been saving himself *for*."

Jude took his hand. Ferrant's palm was hot from work, from holding on.

"Five o'clock," Jude said. "Back door?"

"Front door," Ferrant said. "Let them watch, if they're watching. Let them wonder."

* * *

At the church, Pastor Ilse was awake because Pastor Ilse was always awake, sitting in the vestry over the parish registers with a candle guttering low, doing the thing she had done every night since the requisition: copying baptisms, marriages, burials into a fair hand, as though ink were a form of prayer.

She heard Jude out without interrupting. Then she rose, went to the cabinet where the hymnals lived, and began taking them down one by one, opening each, thumbing the sewn spine, checking the give of the binding with the expert fingers of a woman who had repaired books longer than she had saved souls.

"This one," she said, setting a fat brown volume on the table. "Rebound in '09, poor stitching, the spine will open and close without complaint. You'll want to slit between the gatherings and resew after." She looked up. "How many names?"

"Two hundred and six. Conscription, transport, and the requisition debts. Plus the letter itself travels separately."

"Plus the letter." Ilse said it the way she might read a difficult verse aloud, testing it. "And you trust the courier."

It was not the question Jude had expected, and it landed strangely — because Jude realized, standing in the candlelight among the smell of old glue and vellum, that they had stopped asking it themselves. Somewhere in the weeks of copying and lying and planning, the question of Beatrix Thornbury's loyalty had quietly resolved into something else entirely, the way a sum resolves when you stop doubting the figures.

"Yes," Jude said, and heard the certainty in their own voice with mild surprise, like finding money in a winter coat.

"Then take the hymnal." Ilse pressed it across the table. "And take this." From her sleeve she produced a small folded paper, wax-sealed with no device at all, just the plain blob of an ordinary household candle. "Names of the three families on Marsh Lane whose sons aren't yet written down anywhere. If your copies survive and mine don't, I wanted the number to be larger by three."

Jude took it. Their hands were not quite steady. "Pastor. If any of this goes wrong—"

"Then it goes wrong in decent company," Ilse said, snuffing the candle with a pinch, so that the dark arrived gently. "Go home, clerk. Sleep two hours at least. Your mother ran the marsh on clear eyes, Corrag tells me, and I imagine the marsh hasn't changed its mind about that."

* * *

The last stop was the customs house itself, though Jude had no errand there, or none they could have named. They simply stood in the shadow across the lane and looked up at the building where they had spent ten years of their life among the ledgers — the building where Colonel Vess now kept his polite, thorough fire burning late, his silhouette crossing and recrossing the upstairs window like a pendulum that had gotten loose from its clock.

Two hundred and six names in Jude's satchel, counting the pastor's three. A boat under a tarpaulin getting new caulk by lantern light down on the marsh. Headless bolts in a forge. A coffin-shaped favor already spent at the bridge, a forged grain docket already spent at the mill, and tomorrow night, God willing, the last of it threading the Needle while ice gripped the reeds like small patient hands.

And not one household in Aldenmoor had refused them. Not one door had stayed shut, not one hand had come up empty — Tam's key given with a wound in it, Corrag's boat given with a debt of memory, Ferrant's iron given like absolution, Ilse's hymnal given like a blessing. All of it quiet, all of it deniable, all of it treason in amounts small enough to fit through the eye of a curfew.

*That's how a town chooses,* Jude thought, standing in the cold with their breath rising around them. *Not in the square with banners. In doorways. At midnight. In whispers.*

Across the lane, the customs house door opened.

Jude flattened into the shadow of the tanner's wall, heart slamming, as a figure came down the steps muffled in an army greatcoat — a junior clerk, one of Vess's borrowed pens, hurrying with his collar up toward the officers' quarters. He passed within ten feet of Jude without seeing, and Jude watched him go and felt the night tilt slightly, because the man's face wore the particular gray look of someone carrying news he wished he didn't have.

The figure knocked at the officers' quarters. Was admitted. The door closed.

Above, in the customs house window, Vess's silhouette had stopped pacing. It stood perfectly still, bent slightly forward, in the attitude of a man reading something twice.

Jude stood in the dark a long moment, doing the grim arithmetic of a planner: a courier arriving at midnight meant orders; orders at this stage of a collapsing front meant either reinforcement or evacuation or worse. Whatever the paper said, it had made the colonel stop walking.

The town had chosen its side tonight. Now, it seemed, the other side had chosen its hour.

Jude pulled the satchel tight against their ribs and slipped away down Marsh Lane, toward home, toward two hours of sleep, toward the ford — while behind them the light in the customs house window burned on, motionless, like a held breath.
