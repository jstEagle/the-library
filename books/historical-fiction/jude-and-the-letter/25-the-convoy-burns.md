## 25. The Convoy Burns

The first sign was the birds.

Beatrix noticed them before anything else — a dark ribbon of starlings lifting off the poplars along the north road, rising in a single startled sheet, wheeling once, and scattering. In six months of watching that road from the customs house window, they had never done that. Birds rose for footsteps, for dogs, for the crack of a hunter's gun. They did not rise for nothing.

Then came the sound.

It was not artillery. Beatrix had learned the grammar of artillery over the winter — the deep abdominal thud of it, the way the ground answered each report like a struck drum. This was different: a long, ragged tearing, as though the sky itself had snagged on something sharp, followed by small bright pops that scattered echoes down the valley. Then a silence with weight to it. Then more tearing.

They were in the back room when it started, Beatrix and Jude, sorting through the last pages of the hymnal copy, and they both went still in the same instant, heads lifted like animals.

"The convoy," Beatrix said. The word came out flat, stripped of feeling, because there was no other way to say it.

Jude's hands stopped moving over the paper. "You're certain?"

"The relief was due today. From the north road." Beatrix stood, crossing to the narrow window that looked out over the rooftops toward the ridge. Smoke was beginning to gather there — not the thin domestic smoke of chimneys but something darker, thicker, climbing in a fat column that bent eastward with the wind. "That would be it."

"Then someone is burning it."

"Yes." Beatrix watched the smoke a moment longer. "Or someone is being burned."

They did not say the rest of it aloud. They did not need to. The relief convoy had been the last thread connecting Aldenmoor to anything beyond its own valley — the last proof that the republic still remembered this town existed, still intended to honor even a fraction of what its papers promised. And now the sky above the ridge was filling with smoke the color of old bruises.

* * *

By midmorning the whole town had found reasons to be outdoors.

They stood in knots at the well, at the corner of the market square, along the low wall by the millrace, all of them facing north as if the road might unspool its news directly. No one spoke much. The sound had stopped an hour ago, which was somehow worse than the sound itself. Silence meant the thing was finished, whatever it had been.

Beatrix walked among them and felt the strangeness of standing inside a rumor. Word arrived in fragments, carried by a carter who had turned back two miles out, by a boy who had climbed the church tower with his father's field glasses, by an old woman whose cousin kept a tavern at the crossroads. Each account contradicted the others. The convoy had been shelled from the hills. The convoy had been waved through a checkpoint and then attacked from within. The convoy had turned around before reaching the ambush. The escort had fought. The escort had fled. There were no survivors. There were hundreds of survivors walking south with their hands raised.

The only point all the tellers agreed on was the smoke, because the smoke was still visible, still climbing, a black exclamation mark against the pale winter sky.

Beatrix thought: *I was supposed to meet that convoy.* The thought arrived without panic, almost gently, the way one notes a change in the weather. The dispatches in the diplomatic pouch — the letter, the amnesty, the whole fragile architecture of promises — had been meant for the relief officer's hands, to travel under military protection to the border and beyond. That was the plan Vess knew about, the official plan, the one Beatrix had been ordered to hold for. And now the hands that were meant to receive it were somewhere on the north road, burned or captured or scattered into the woods.

Which meant the letter had nowhere to go. Which meant every plan built around it — including the ones made in back rooms by lamplight — had just changed shape entirely.

Beatrix bought a roll of bread they did not want, purely to have a reason to stand at the baker's stall and listen. The baker's boy worked the oven behind his father, and Beatrix saw him differently now, knowing what Beatrix knew — that this boy's name sat in a seized ledger on a list for transport, that the ink recording him was at this moment packed in crates somewhere in the occupied customs house. The boy caught them looking and smiled, flour on his cheek. Beatrix smiled back and felt the floor of the world tilt a degree further.

* * *

Colonel Vess made his announcement at four o'clock, in the market square, from the steps of the customs house.

He did not use a megaphone. He did not need one. His voice carried the way cold carries, finding every gap. The soldiers arranged themselves at intervals along the square's edges with the unhurried precision of men setting a table, and the townspeople gathered below the steps in the gray light, breath steaming, faces tilted up.

"The relief column traveling the north road has been destroyed by enemy forces," Vess said. His tone was the tone of a man reading a shipping manifest. "There will be no further supplies arriving by that route. There will be no further orders arriving by that route. Effective immediately, this town and its surrounding district are under martial law."

A murmur moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. Vess let it run its course. He was very good at letting things run their course; Beatrix had learned that about him across the winter. He stood with his hands clasped behind him, his cap set squarely, his face holding exactly the amount of concern the situation required and not a grain more.

"You have been told," he continued, "that the republic's army will return for you. You have been told that your records are safe, that your claims will be honored, that your names are known to those in power." A pause, calibrated. "I am now the nearest representative of lawful authority. What I say, in the absence of other orders, is law."

*In the absence of other orders.* Beatrix turned the phrase over and understood it for what it was: an admission dressed as procedure. There were no other orders. There would be no other orders. Whatever chain of command had issued Vess his commission had been on that convoy or was fleeing ahead of it, and the colonel knew it, and he was choosing — calmly, deliberately — to become his own republic of one.

"The ledgers and civil records currently held in secure storage will be transported for safekeeping," Vess said. "Loading begins at dawn. Heads of household with claims pending should understand that records remaining in my custody will be preserved; records abandoned or removed from my custody cannot be guaranteed."

The threat was so smooth it took a moment to find its teeth. Beatrix found them anyway. *Records removed from my custody cannot be guaranteed* — spoken to a crowd that did not know a hymnal existed, did not know copies had already crossed a bridge hidden in a coffin, and yet aimed with terrible accuracy at the two people on that square who did. Vess could not know how close his words had come. Or perhaps he could. Perhaps that was why he'd said it.

Beside Beatrix in the crowd, Jude stood very still. Beatrix did not look over. Looking, in that moment, would have been a confession written in fire.

"Curfew begins at dusk and ends at dawn," Vess concluded. "Soldiers are authorized to search any building and detain any person. Cooperation will be remembered." Another pause, another degree of temperature dropped from his voice. "Non-cooperation will also be remembered."

He descended the steps. The soldiers tightened their perimeter. The crowd dispersed in the slow, stunned manner of people who have just been told, in polite language, that no one is coming — that the world they had been waiting to resume simply no longer existed anywhere except in the papers locked in the customs house, and that even those papers were leaving at dawn.

* * *

Beatrix walked home the long way, past the millrace, and let the full arithmetic arrive.

The floor had given way. That was the honest word for it, and Beatrix had always insisted on honest words, even internal ones — especially internal ones, since those were the only ones no one could requisition. For six months the structure of everything had been *hold until the convoy comes.* Hold the pouch. Hold the lie told to Colonel Vess. Hold the fear at arm's length and trust that the machinery of the republic, however distant, however wounded, would grind eventually toward Aldenmoor and collect what was owed to it. The convoy was the deadline and the deliverance both. Its arrival would mean surrendering the letter and losing the town. Its arrival would mean the whole terrible choice resolving itself.

Now the choice had not resolved. It had simply been abandoned on the north road with the rest of the cargo, smoldering.

And Vess — Vess, who had been the instrument of a larger will, patient and procedural because his orders were patient and procedural — was now free. Free of oversight, free of schedule, free to load the ledgers at dawn and carry every name in Aldenmoor wherever his own judgment took him. Beatrix tried to imagine what that judgment looked like tonight, alone in the customs house with his maps and his lists and no superior left to audit him, and found the imagining worse than anything the enemy on the ridge could devise.

At the door of the lodging house, Jude was already waiting in the shadow of the lintel, having crossed the town separately, by a different route, the way they had agreed weeks ago. Neither spoke until the door closed behind them and the shutter was drawn.

"Dawn," Jude said.

"At dawn."

"He'll use army wagons. Four wagons at least, for the crates I've seen carried down to the cellar. Loading takes hours — they'll start with the tariff files, the heavy ones, the conscription ledgers near the end."

Beatrix looked at him. "You've been counting."

"I count everything. You knew that when you trusted me." Jude's face in the lamplight was calm, but Beatrix could see the calculation running beneath it like current under ice. "The marsh ford is the only road left. After tomorrow, if those ledgers leave, the only copy of every name in this town is either in a hymnal in a church wall or in Vess's baggage train."

"And the letter."

Jude was quiet a moment. "And the letter."

Outside, a patrol passed in the street, boots striking cobbles in the new rhythm of martial law — not marching so much as pacing, the measured tread of occupation settling in for whatever came after the republic. The lamplight through the shutter trembled slightly with each footfall and steadied again.

"We can't move tonight," Beatrix said slowly. "Curfew. The searches."

"No. But we can decide." Jude sat down at the small table where the hymnal work had lived for weeks, where the lamplight fell in its familiar circle, and looked up with something in his expression that Beatrix had not seen before — not the clerk's careful neutrality, nor the smuggler's daughter's inherited cunning, but plain, uncovered fear, worn openly like a garment that no longer mattered enough to hide. "Tomorrow they load the ledgers. Tomorrow night or the night after, Vess rides out with them, and whatever chance this town had of proving it ever existed goes with him. So whatever we're going to do at the ford — we do it tomorrow night. Before the wagons are gone. Before he does."

Beatrix stood in the circle of lamplight and felt, with perfect clarity, the sensation of the floor giving way — and beneath it, impossibly, ground. Not the solid institutional ground of commissions and seals and chains of command. Something older. A ford through icy water. Names copied by hand. A town deciding, one quiet treason at a time, whom it belonged to.

"Dawn first," Beatrix said. "We watch the loading. We learn the count, the guards, the order of the wagons."

"And then?"

Beatrix looked at the shuttered window, beyond which the smoke from the north road had finally begun to thin, and heard their own voice answer with the steadiness of someone stepping off a ledge.

"Then the marsh."
