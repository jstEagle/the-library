## 27. Caught at the Far Bank

The water did not feel like water anymore. It felt like a decision the body makes too late — cold that was not temperature but verdict, closing over Beatrix's knees, then thighs, then waist, so that each forward step was less walking than surrender to a current that wanted them back on the occupied bank.

They had entered the marsh an hour after full dark, following Jude's silhouette and Jude's murmured instructions — *left of the dead alder, where the reeds thin, don't trust the ground until it smells of iron* — and for a long while the world had been reduced to sensation: the bite of ice in the reeds, the suck of mud at every lift of the foot, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth pressed against Beatrix's chest like a second heartbeat, the letter sealed inside it, sealed inside them both.

Behind them, Aldenmoor burned its lamps against curfew. Ahead, nothing. The marsh breathed fog. Somewhere out in that whiteness a sentry's lantern swung on its slow arc, and they had frozen twice already, thigh-deep, letting the light pass over them like a hand searching a pocket.

Jude moved ahead now, testing each step with a pole cut from the alder stand. *My mother crossed this ford forty times in one winter*, they had said on the bank, and had not said anything else, and the silence had been its own kind of inheritance.

Twenty yards from the far shore the bottom shelved away and came back, and Beatrix felt the change — firmer ground, gravel under the silt, the water dropping to their hips — and allowed themselves the first thought of the far bank as a place rather than an idea. There were trees there. Black pines, a fence line, a cart track going north into partisan country. Safety was a geography again. It had been so long since safety was anything but a word in a dispatch.

That was when the lantern came out of the fog behind them.

Not the sweeping, bored arc of the marsh sentry. This light moved with purpose, low and fast along the near bank, and beneath it Beatrix heard the sound they had learned to dread above all sounds: boots in water. More than two pairs.

*Don't run*, Jude had said. *Running is a confession the body makes before the mouth can.*

But the far bank was twenty yards off and the patrol was already shouting — a voice cracked with cold and authority, *HALT, IN THE WATER, HALT OR WE FIRE* — and the arithmetic of the moment refused all of Jude's proverbs. Twenty yards of hip-deep water. A rifle's reach: forever.

Beatrix turned. The lantern glare hit the surface of the marsh and shattered into a thousand burning coins, and through that blaze Beatrix could see them: four soldiers in the grey-green of Vess's requisition army, three wading in with rifles slung for the crossing, one holding the light. Young faces. Chapped lips. The nearest one was no older than the boy who swept the customs house floor.

"Hands out of the water," the oldest soldier said. "Show me what you're carrying."

The oilcloth bundle sat against Beatrix's chest like a stolen child. Every name in Aldenmoor. The amnesty letter, signed and sealed and never delivered, the republic's broken promise made paper again. Everything. All of it, one bundle, and four rifles coming on through the reeds.

"We're refugees," Beatrix heard themselves say, and marveled distantly at how steady the lie had become — how easily the tongue now bent around falsehood, as if all those years of filing true statements had only been practice for this. "Fleeing the evacuation. We have nothing."

"Then your hands will come up empty." The soldier waded closer. Water peeled away from his coat in grey sheets. "Both of you. To the bank. Slowly."

Beatrix looked at Jude. In the lantern light Jude's face was unreadable — clerk's face, the face of a person who has spent a life keeping other people's secrets off the record — but their eyes went once, quickly, to the bundle, and then to the deep channel ten feet to the north where the current ran black and fast between the reed beds. The message required no cipher. Beatrix had spent a career learning to read what officials did not say.

*If we cannot keep it, drown it.*

It would mean the ledger lost — every name, the whole town's proof of ownership, of existence. It would mean the letter lost — the only original, the seal, the signature, the thing they had bled three checkpoints to carry. It would mean the crossing had been for nothing except survival, and survival alone suddenly seemed like a very small cargo for so much water.

"To the bank," the soldier repeated, and leveled his rifle, not quite at them, not quite away.

They waded. Jude first, then Beatrix, the patrol fanning out behind them in a loose crescent, and Beatrix counted steps because counting was the only prayer they knew. Fourteen. Fifteen. The gravel rose underfoot. Sixteen. The water dropped to their thighs, their knees—

The night tore open.

There was no single sound to it, no clean beginning. It was as if the marsh itself exhaled violence: a whistle from the treeline, then a crash of shot from three directions at once, and the lantern went spinning end over end into the water, a drowning star, its light wheeling crazily across the surface and showing Beatrix one final image — the young soldier's face turning, mouth open, confusion rather than fear — before the dark took everything.

Then the shouting started, and it was impossible to tell whose shouting was whose.

"Down!" Jude's hand closed on Beatrix's collar and hauled them sideways into the reeds, both of them crashing into the icy shallows as shapes poured out of the pine darkness ahead — men who had been waiting on the far bank all along, who had watched the patrol walk into the water the way one watches a trap close. Rifles flashed from the treeline. One of the soldiers screamed and went down into the marsh. Another turned and floundered back the way he'd come, boots throwing up sheets of black water, abandoning the crescent, abandoning the lantern, abandoning everything except the animal imperative to be elsewhere.

In the chaos — in the strobing half-dark of muzzle flash and drowning lantern — Beatrix lost their footing.

It happened stupidly, the way catastrophe always does: a root under the water, a twist of the ankle, a fall that should have meant nothing. But the oilcloth bundle slipped from their arms as they went down, and the current, that patient adversary that had wanted the bundle all along, took it.

Beatrix saw it go — saw the pale shape of the wrapped ledger turn once in the black water like something surfacing to look at the world before descending — and lunged, and missed, and their hands closed on reeds and cold nothing. The channel was pulling it north, toward the deep pool below the ford where Jude's mother's stories said the drowned things gathered. The letter. The names. Thirty-nine chapters of risk dissolving into a marsh that did not care what republics promised.

"*Jude—*"

Jude was already moving. Beatrix never saw the decision happen; there was no pause, no weighing, none of the deliberation that Beatrix would have brought to it, that Beatrix had been trained to bring to everything. Jude simply went — a dive more desperate than graceful, flat into the filthy water, arms outstretched — and for one long second neither of them existed in the visible world. Shots still cracked in the treeline. A soldier wept somewhere in the reeds, high and continuous, a sound Beatrix knew would stay with them. And the marsh held its two thieves under and gave nothing back.

Then Jude came up, gasping, one fist thrust above the water.

The oilcloth bundle. Or part of it.

"The ledger—" Jude's voice was shredded by cold and coughing. "It opened. In the water. I got—" another cough, wracking, "—I got the middle of it. The rest went down the channel."

Beatrix waded to them, hooked an arm under Jude's shoulders, and together they dragged toward the far bank, toward the pines, toward the voices now calling out of the darkness in a dialect Beatrix half-recognized — marsh-country words, partisan words, *here, here, low, come low*. Behind them the last of the patrol fled or lay still. The lantern finally drowned itself with a hiss, and the marsh returned to its ancient business of being dark.

What Jude clutched was half a ledger. Beatrix could feel it even through the soaked wrapping: the pages torn free, the binding gone, perhaps a third of the conscription names dissolved into pulp or carried north by a current that had never signed any requisition. The rest — the letter, thank whatever governed such things, the letter sealed separately inside its own oilskin — was intact, pressed hard against Jude's ribs, Jude's arm locked around it the way a drowning person locks around a spar.

Sacrifice, Beatrix thought, hauling Jude's dead-weight coldness up the muddy bank, was supposed to be legible. It was supposed to come with trumpets or at least with witnesses. Instead it came like this: half a ledger's worth of names traded for the whole of a promise, weighed on scales no one had built, paid to a river that issued no receipt.

* * *

The partisans made them kneel first. That was procedure, someone said, in a voice that suggested procedure was the only law left in the world, and Beatrix knelt in the mud with Jude shaking beside them and watched hooded figures strip the drowned patrol's survivors of weapons and boots and identity papers with the brisk economy of clerks. There were six of them, maybe seven — hard to count in the dark — wrapped in sheepskins and army coats worn inside-out, faces wrapped to the eyes. Their leader was a woman with a scarred cheek and a schoolteacher's precise diction, and she crouched in front of Beatrix and Jude and looked at them for a long, silent minute, the way one looks at a document whose authenticity is in question.

"A customs courier," she said at last. She had taken something from Beatrix's inner pocket — the commission, the seal, the laminated identity of a lifetime. "And the town clerk of Aldenmoor. Crossing my marsh at night, carrying—" she weighed the sodden bundle in her gloved hand, "—half a ledger and, unless I'm much mistaken, something heavier than paper always feels."

"You know who we are," Beatrix said.

"I know everyone who matters and most who don't." The woman turned the commission over. "Marta Havel. These are my reeds you've been bleeding in. My mother gathered peat from them before your republic drew its first border." She stood. "Bring them to the camp. If they freeze to death en route, note the time."

They walked north through the marsh for an hour, along paths Beatrix could not have found in daylight, guided by markers invisible to anyone not born to them — a stripped branch, a stone stack, a smell of woodsmoke that appeared and vanished like a rumor. Jude stumbled twice and twice was held up by strangers' hands, and Beatrix, carrying the surviving fragment of the ledger against their own chest now, understood with a clarity that felt like fever that they had never in their life been so completely in the power of other people. No seal protected them here. No office. No name. They were two wet, exhausted bodies in the dark, and everything they had done — every lie told to Vess, every checkpoint gambled, every hour of fear — had purchased exactly this: the privilege of kneeling in the mud while strangers decided whether the truth they carried was worth the trouble of keeping them alive.

And yet. Beatrix searched themselves for terror and found instead something adjacent to it, something almost like relief. The commission was gone from their pocket. For the first time since childhood, no document defined them. Only their choices had followed them across the water.

* * *

The camp was a fold in the earth — sunken carts, turf-roofed shelters, a fire burned down to a red heart and ringed by sleeping shapes. Marta Havel's people put Beatrix and Jude near the flames, forced hot broth into their hands, and otherwise ignored them with the courtesy of professionals who had seen couriers before and expected to see more.

Jude could not stop shivering. The marsh cold had gotten into the marrow, and Beatrix sat pressed shoulder to shoulder against them, feeding sticks to the fire, watching steam rise off both of them like the ghosts of everything they'd waded through.

"You dove," Beatrix said quietly.

"The letter was going downstream."

"So was the ledger. You chose."

Jude stared into the fire for a long time. When they spoke, their voice was scraped hollow. "Half the town's names are in that channel, Beatrix. The Halbers. The Ostrovs. The widow Krail and her sons. Names I've copied out three times, names I can recite in my sleep — and some of them are gone. Pulp. Fish bedding. And I chose, in the water, in about half a second, that the letter mattered more." A muscle worked in their jaw. "I don't know yet if I chose rightly. I know I chose quickly. That's all I know."

Beatrix looked at the ruined fragment where it steamed on a flat stone beside the fire, its torn pages legible in patches, whole columns of names dissolving at the margins into grey blooms like frost. Half a census. Half a town.

"You kept the letter dry," Beatrix said. "Whatever else — you kept the promise dry."

"It isn't a promise yet." Jude's eyes came up, and there was something in them Beatrix had not seen before, something harder than grief. "It's a betrayal that was written down. Those aren't the same thing. Not until somebody delivers it, and somebody honors it, and somebody lives long enough to see it honored. Right now it's just paper that proves the republic lied. And paper that proves a lie—" Jude looked toward the fire's far side, where Marta Havel sat conferring with two of her people, their voices too low to carry, their glances returning again and again to the oilskin packet drying on its stone, "—is worth a great deal to people who trade in leverage."

The cold walked up Beatrix's spine on small feet.

Across the fire, Marta Hvel rose, crossed the trampled snow, and crouched before them again. Up close her scarred cheek shone with heat from the flames, and her eyes were patient the way rivers are patient.

"You saved it," she said. "Most of it. Good. Because in the morning, courier, clerk — you're going to tell me exactly what this letter is, exactly who it was meant for, and exactly why I shouldn't simply keep it and negotiate with it myself." She smiled without warmth. "You've reached the far bank. Whatever you were to each other on the water — that's finished. On this side of the ford, everything has a price, and everything has a buyer. Sleep if you can."

She stood and walked back to her lieutenants, and Beatrix watched the oilskin packet on its stone, drying in the firelight, already looking less like a deliverance and more like merchandise — and wondered, with a weariness beyond fear, which of them history intended to pay for it.
