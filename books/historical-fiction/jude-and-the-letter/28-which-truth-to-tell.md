## 28. Which Truth to Tell

The partisan camp lived inside a bowl of hills that the maps had forgotten to name. We reached it near dusk, two soldiers of the new republic of the marshes walking ahead of us with their rifles slung casual as fishing poles, and by the time the firelight found us I had stopped trying to count the tents. There were more of them than there should have been. There were more of everything than there should have been — men in mismatched coats, women with bandoliers worn like sashes at a harvest dance, boys too young to have learned to shave who carried themselves with the terrible certainty of people who have already decided how they will die.

Beatrix walked beside me with the letter pressed flat against their chest, inside the coat, inside the shirt, against the skin. They had been carrying it that way since the far bank. I think they believed the warmth of their own body could keep it honest.

I had nothing left to carry. The true ledger was somewhere under forty feet of black marsh water, sunk in its oilcloth shroud like a coffin given a burial no one sang for. My hands still remembered the weight of it sliding out of my grip — the moment when the lantern light swept the reeds and I had to choose, in half a heartbeat, between the book and the letter, and my hands chose before my mind did. I had watched the water close over four hundred years of Aldenmoor's names and felt, absurdly, nothing. Or not nothing. A hollowness with edges. Grief postponed until there was time for it.

There was no time for it now. A woman detached herself from the firelight and came toward us, and the camp went quiet around her the way water goes quiet around a stone.

She was older than the rest, fifty perhaps, with gray shot through her braid and a scar that pulled the corner of her mouth into something that was almost a smile and never became one. She looked at me first, and I understood immediately that she knew who I was. Not my name — my mother's routes. Her eyes did the arithmetic of the marsh behind me, the distance we had covered, the hour we had crossed, and she nodded once, the way one tradesman acknowledges another's workmanship.

"So," she said. "The smuggler's line still breathes."

"My mother's line," I said. "I only borrow it."

"Everything is borrowed now." She turned to Beatrix, and her voice changed — not warmer, but more careful, the way a hand changes when it reaches for glass instead of wood. "You are the courier."

It was not a question, and Beatrix did not treat it as one. They stood very straight. I had seen them stand that way before Colonel Vess, in the customs house with the soldiers going through the shelves, and I understood then that it was not courage I was looking at but discipline — a spine trained into its posture the way a sapling is trained against a stake.

"I am," Beatrix said.

"The letter."

"I have it."

"You will give it to me."

* * *

They put us in a tent while the camp ate, which was either hospitality or custody, and from the murmur outside we could not tell which. Someone brought stew — barley, a strip of salt pork gone mostly to fat, bread that tasted of ash — and left without meeting our eyes. The tent smelled of pine pitch and wet wool and, beneath it, faintly, the iron scent of the marsh that had followed us all day and would follow me, I suspected, for the rest of my life.

Beatrix sat with their back against the center pole and did not eat.

"You should eat," I said.

"Should I." It was not defiance. It was exhaustion wearing defiance's coat. They pulled the letter from their shirt and held it in both hands, low, in the space between us where the lamplight could find the seal. The wax had cracked in the water. Through the tear in the corner — the same tear I had glimpsed weeks ago in the customs house, when the world still had a shape — the word was visible, or the ghost of it: *amnesty*, its letters bleeding slightly where the river had gotten in.

"They'll take it whether you give it or not," I said quietly. "You know that."

"I know that." Beatrix traced the torn corner with one finger, not touching the paper itself. "That isn't the question."

"Then what is?"

They were quiet long enough that I thought they wouldn't answer. Outside, someone laughed, and someone else shushed them, and a sentry's boots passed close and faded.

"When I took this commission," Beatrix said finally, "I swore an oath. Not to a minister, not to a government. To the document itself. That is what the service is — you are loyal to the paper, because the paper is the only thing that outlives everyone who touches it. Ministers fall. Republics fall." Their thumb moved along the edge of the seal. "This letter was signed by a man who is probably dead now. Sealed by hands that didn't know what they were sealing. And it is still true. That is the whole religion of it, Jude. The paper is still true even though everything it promised has already been betrayed."

"And if you give it to them—"

"It stops being testimony and becomes currency." The word came out of them flat and heavy, a coin dropped on a table. "The moment it becomes currency, it can be spent on anything. A prisoner exchange. A weapons shipment. A favor from some neutral consul who will read it, shrug, and file it. The amnesty in that letter was owed to a town. If I hand it over as a bargaining chip, it gets spent on whatever the bargaining needs, and Aldenmoor gets — what? A memory that justice was almost done once?"

"Aldenmoor," I said, "gets to stay alive. Which is the precondition for remembering anything."

They looked at me then, really looked, and I saw the thing underneath their calm — fear, yes, and grief for the drowned ledger, but also something rarer: the vertigo of a person discovering that the floor they have stood on all their life is only one floor among many, and every other one is shaking.

"You don't understand what you're asking," they said.

"I'm not asking anything yet. I'm sitting here."

"No. You're arguing for them. The partisans. You want me to give it up because it saves your town faster."

"Yes," I said. "And I want you to hear yourself say that like it's a crime. Whose town do you imagine saved you at the mill gate? Whose guard walked you past the checkpoint because his father owed my family a debt from before either republic? You talk about paper outliving people. Fine. But paper doesn't walk itself across a marsh. Every truth that ever mattered got where it was going on somebody's back, and somebody's back got whipped for it, and the paper doesn't remember *that*. So don't lecture me about loyalty, Beatrix Thornbury. I've watched you learn whose names are in these ledgers. You learned it slower than I did, but you learned it."

The lamp guttered. Neither of us moved to fix it.

"That was cruel," Beatrix said at last, and their voice had gone quiet, the anger burned down to coals.

"Yes," I said. "Most true things are, the first time you say them."

* * *

They came for us at full dark. The woman with the scar — her people called her Maren, I learned, or Marshal, depending on how much they feared her — received us in a dugout that served as her headquarters, lit by a single storm lantern and a map pinned to the earthen wall. The map was old. The borders on it belonged to a country that had already stopped existing, and someone had drawn new lines over it in charcoal, and someone else had drawn newer lines over those in red, and none of the lines matched the ground we had just crossed, where the only border that mattered ran between the water and the reeds.

Maren sat behind a plank table with a rifle across her knees and did not offer us chairs.

"The letter," she said.

Beatrix did not move. "Before I answer that, tell me what you intend to do with it."

Maren's almost-smile deepened by a fraction. "You are in no position to ask terms."

"I am in exactly one position to ask terms," Beatrix said. "I am the only person in this room who can testify to what that document means. Anyone can carry paper. Not anyone can stand before a tribunal, or a newspaper, or whatever passes for history when the shooting stops, and say: I was sworn to this, I carried it, I know why it was never delivered. Kill me and the letter is just wax and rag. Keep me alive and it's evidence. Evidence has conditions."

For a long moment nobody breathed. I watched the young partisan by the door shift his rifle, and I watched Maren watch him not use it, and I understood that this was a performance being performed for the room — that power here was a theater with a very short ticket price for failure.

"Speak your conditions," Maren said.

"First: it goes to a neutral authority. Border archive, neutral consulate, I don't care which — but a party that cannot spend it. Second: my testimony travels attached to it, sworn and signed. Third—" Beatrix paused, and I heard the cost of the next sentence arrive in their throat before it arrived in their voice. "Third: whatever else it buys you, it does not buy the transport of civilians. The names in the conscription lists are struck before the letter moves one step farther. That is not a condition. That is the meaning of the document. Amnesty that arrives after the trains leave is an apology, not a law."

Maren leaned back. The lantern threw her shadow huge against the map, across the dead country's borders, so that her silhouette seemed to hold the whole collapsing territory in the crook of one arm.

"You want the letter to be pure," she said. "Clean hands. History like a church service."

"I want it to be *true*," Beatrix said. "Purity is for people who've never been hungry. Truth is for everyone."

Something moved behind the marshal's eyes — recognition, maybe, or contempt, or the place where the two become indistinguishable in an old soldier. She looked at me.

"You," she said. "The clerk. Your mother ran the marsh for eleven years and never once gave up a cargo, not to the customs men, not to the press gangs, not even that winter they shot the Ostrow boy. Why should I believe you'd counsel differently?"

I thought of my mother's hands, cracked with cold, tying oilcloth around a bundle of contraband tea as tenderly as if she were wrapping a christening gift. I thought of the debt that had bought my clerkship — her last run, the one that went wrong, the fine that bound our family to respectability forever. I thought of the ledger under the water, and of the hymnal hidden in the false bottom of a grain cart somewhere south of the mill gate, four hundred names copied in lamplight by two frightened people who had told each other it would be enough.

"She gave up cargoes when the cargo wasn't worth a life," I said. "She kept the route sacred and the goods negotiable. That's the whole trade. Beatrix is offering you the same bargain the marsh always offered: the route stays honest, the cargo bends. Take the letter. Attach the testimony. Strike the names. And in twenty years, when somebody asks how the amnesty survived the war, the answer will be that it traveled in the hands of people who argued about it first. That argument is the proof it was real."

Maren was silent. Then she laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years.

"Your mother argued too," she said. "Every run. Drove the customs men mad." She stood, and the room stood with her. "Bring the letter."

* * *

It happened at the plank table, by lantern light, with three witnesses and me watching. Beatrix drew the letter from inside their coat and held it a moment longer — I saw their thumb press the broken seal, once, gently, the way you press a wound to test whether it still hurts — and then laid it on the table between themselves and the marshal.

But they did not let go of it.

"One thing more," Beatrix said. "Before my hand opens."

Maren waited.

"When it reaches the border archive — when some clerk in a clean office opens this and reads it a hundred years from now — he will read the signature and the seal and the beautiful legal language, and he will think the republic kept its promise. He will file it under *fulfilled*. Nobody in that clean office will ever know that the promise was broken before the ink dried, that the trains ran anyway, that a ledger with a town's whole life in it is at the bottom of a marsh because a clerk dropped it saving this page." Beatrix's voice did not rise. That was what made it terrible — the flatness of it, the precision. "So my testimony will say all of it. The betrayal, the requisition, Colonel Vess, the checkpoints, the ford, the boy who swept our office floor and whose name is on a transport list tonight. The letter goes to history, but the *truth* goes with it, tied on with string. That is my final condition, and it is not negotiable, because it is the only part of this that I actually own."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere outside, a night bird called across the marsh and got no answer.

Maren studied Beatrix for a long moment — and then she did something I did not expect. She reached across the table and turned the letter around, so that it faced the courier, and slid it back two inches.

"Sign your testimony," she said. "Then it rides with the letter. Both of them together, wherever they go. My runner leaves for the border at first light, if the fog holds." Her scarred mouth twitched toward its almost-smile. "My superiors wanted the letter to trade for prisoners. Let them be angry. Angry men negotiate harder."

Beatrix picked up the pen. Their hand hovered over the paper — and I saw, in the small motion of their fingers, the whole architecture of the choice settling into place, load-bearing walls finding their footings. Whatever oath they had sworn to paper, they were rewriting it now, in front of witnesses, in their own hand.

They wrote. Name, commission number, date, and then the testimony itself, page after page in that careful customs-house script I had noticed months ago across a counter in Aldenmoor, back when history happened elsewhere. I watched them write Vess's name. I watched them write mine.

Outside, near dawn, one of the scouts came in fast from the southern ridge with news that emptied the camp of sleep: smoke on the road behind us, at the customs house in Aldenmoor. Fires in the yard. Soldiers loading wagons through the night.

Burning what they couldn't carry — and carrying the rest.

Maren read the scout's report twice, then looked up at Beatrix with something new in her face, something urgent and cold.

"Your colonel," she said, "is leaving. And I am told he keeps records of everyone who helped you cross."
