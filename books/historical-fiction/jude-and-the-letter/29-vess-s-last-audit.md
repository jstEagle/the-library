## 29. Vess's Last Audit

The smoke came first, before I understood what it meant.

I had learned the town's smokes by then — bread smoke from the bakery ovens at dawn, thin and sweet; coal smoke from the customs house stove, gray and familiar as my own breath; the wet green stink of the tannery when the wind turned wrong. This was none of those. This was paper burning in quantity, paper with ink on it, and there was only one building in Aldenmoor that held enough paper to make a column of smoke stand up against the winter sky like a dark finger pointing at heaven.

I was halfway across the market square when I saw it rising from the customs house chimney, and I stopped so suddenly that a woman behind me walked into my shoulder and swore. She didn't notice who I was. Nobody noticed anything anymore except the smoke. The whole square had gone quiet the way a field goes quiet when a hawk passes over — the fishmonger with his hands frozen mid-gesture over his scale, the two boys by the well turning their faces upward, an old man taking off his hat as though the smoke were a funeral procession.

Every name in Aldenmoor was going up that chimney.

* * *

They let me into the building because they always let me into the building. That was the obscenity of it — my pass still worked, my face still opened doors, my signature still moved soldiers aside like water parting around a stone. Corporal Fenn, who three weeks ago had been a boy who blushed when I thanked him for holding the gate, stood at the entrance with his rifle slung wrong and wouldn't meet my eyes.

"Colonel's orders," he said. "Nobody past the second landing."

"I'm expected," I said, which was true, though not in the way he understood. Vess had summoned me for the evening audit every third day since the convoy burned, and the summons tonight had come at noon, written in his careful hand: *Come at four. Bring nothing.*

The stairwell smelled of scorched ink. On the landing outside the records room, a soldier sat on an overturned crate feeding documents into the stove in armfuls, without reading them, the way a man feeds hay to animals he has stopped loving. He was young. His hands were steady, and that frightened me more than trembling would have. A man who trembles is still arguing with himself.

"Where's the rest?" I asked him.

"Burned yesterday. And the day before." He nodded at the stove. "This is the tariff correspondence. Colonel says start with the boring things."

*Start with the boring things.* As if the boring things were not the whole texture of the town — the grain levies, the toll receipts, the letters of complaint from mill owners with handwriting like spiderwebs, forty years of ordinary life recorded in ordinary ink. I thought of chapter and verse: the ledger I had kept myself in my first month here, the entry that had cracked my complacency open like an egg, a neighbor's name in my own careful hand. It would be ash by now, indistinguishable from everything else.

The door to Vess's office stood open.

He was standing at the window with his back to me, and for the first time since I'd known him, Colonel Vess looked like a man instead of an instrument. His coat was unbuttoned. His collar was open at the throat. On the desk behind him lay a map of the province with pins in it, and I could read the story the pins told even from the doorway: red pins clustered along the northern road like a rash, and beyond them, no pins at all, because beyond them there was nothing left to pin. The front wasn't a line anymore. It was a tide, and the tide had come in everywhere at once.

"You're punctual," he said, without turning. "That's the trouble with you, Thornbury. Punctual people believe the schedule will save them."

"The convoy," I said.

"The convoy." He said it the way one reads an epitaph. "Three hundred men. My nephew among them, as it happens, though that's a detail your republic never required me to share." He turned then, and his face was composed, but composed the way a ruined wall is composed — same shape, nothing holding it up. "Ambushed at Kessel Ford. No survivors reported, though reports are the one thing nobody believes anymore, including me."

I knew about Kessel Ford. Everyone in the partisan camp's orbit knew; the news had crossed the marsh days ago and reached me through channels I could not admit to knowing. But I had not known about the nephew. I watched grief move through him like weather through a valley and I made myself remember, deliberately, that this man had signed transport lists. Grief does not balance a ledger. It only changes whose hand holds the pen.

"You're burning the records," I said.

"I'm conducting an audit," he said. "My superiors' audit. They sent a directive two days ago — I'll show it to you, you'll appreciate the language — instructing all provincial commands to prevent the capture of sensitive materials by hostile forces." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "*Prevent the capture.* They might have said *burn everything,* but bureaucracies die the way they lived, choosing words that let everyone pretend the fire was someone else's idea."

"And your superiors? Where are they now?"

"Gone. Evacuated through the neutral corridor ten days ago, ahead of their own directive, with their personal effects crated and marked. I received my orders from a ministry that no longer exists, delivered by a courier who wept while handing them over. A courier, Thornbury." He looked at me then with real attention, and I felt the old fear rise in my throat like water. "You people are the last honest thing in this system. You carry the paper. You don't ask what's in it. Somebody should write a hymn."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're going to help me inventory what's left." He crossed to the cabinet against the far wall — the tall one, iron-banded, the one I had watched soldiers carry down from the records room weeks ago with every ledger inside it that proved who owned what in Aldenmoor. He unlocked it with a key from his watch chain. Inside, I saw, there were perhaps a dozen folders left, thin ones, where once there had been hundreds. Everything else was already heat and wind.

Except.

At the back of the cabinet, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, sat a single volume. Not a folder. A book. I knew its shape the way you know the shape of your own bed in a dark room.

"That one's not for the stove," Vess said, watching my face. He enjoyed watching my face; he always had, the way a card player enjoys it. "You recognize it."

"No," I said, and it was the second great lie of my life, and easier than the first.

* * *

We inventoried the remaining papers together, because that was what he wanted, and because refusing would have told him everything. He read each folder's label aloud; I wrote it in the manifest column; he consigned it to the stove or, rarely, to the small dispatch case that sat by the door. The case was for things he intended to carry out himself. There were very few things in it.

Outside the window, dusk came down on Aldenmoor the way it always did, indifferent, blue-gray, smelling of woodsmoke and coming frost. Somewhere a dog barked itself hoarse. Somewhere — I thought of the well, the evening recitation, the names spoken aloud by people who had learned that memory was the only archive left — someone was saying the names of neighbors into the cold air, keeping them alive against the fire I was helping to count.

"Item forty-one," Vess read. "Correspondence, provincial tax appeals, 1841 to 1844. Burn."

I wrote it down. My pen did not shake. I had learned that much about myself in these weeks, that my hands were traitors to my heart, calm when everything in me was screaming.

"Item fifty-two." He held up a folder, and I saw the seal on it, the double-eagle wax, broken and re-sealed, broken again. Diplomatic pouch materials. My stomach dropped through the floor of the world. "Field directives, undelivered. Burn?"

"Burn," I said.

He held it over the stove a moment longer than necessary. Then he dropped it in, and I watched the corner catch, and flare, and curl. If the letter had been in that folder — but no. I knew where the letter was. Or rather, I knew where it was going: north, by partisan hand, toward the border, with my testimony folded behind it like a shadow attached to a body. What burned just now was only the envelope it had traveled in, and some other promise, some other failure, addressed to some other town that had believed the republic loved it.

"You want to know why I keep the volume," Vess said, when the ash had settled. It wasn't a question. He untied the oilcloth anyway, laying it open on his desk like a surgeon laying back skin.

There it was. The copy ledger. Not the original — the original had gone into the stove weeks ago, or so he had claimed, and I had spent sleepless nights hoping the claim was true. But this was the fair copy, the clerk-made duplicate, the bureaucratic habit of redundancy that had saved more lives than any general. And in its margins, in a small precise hand I knew better than my own heartbeat, annotations. Dates of household composition. Ages of sons. Notes on loyalties, on debts, on who might be turned and who would have to be taken.

And beside one entry — beside a name that was not mine but that I had entered myself, months ago, in another life — a marginal note in red: *Courier resident at this address. Verify.*

Not my name. Worse than my name: my function, attached to an innocent family's address like a disease. Anyone reading it would pull that household apart looking for me. Would find — I counted it in my head, fast, the way Jude had taught me to count doors — a grandmother, a daughter-in-law, two children under ten.

"You're taking it with you," I said.

"I'm taking it with me." He retied the cord with unhurried fingers. "You think that makes me a monster. Consider, instead, that it makes me a debtor." He said the word carefully, tasting it. "Everyone believes the men who burn things are the powerful ones. Nonsense. Burning is what the powerless do. The powerful keep copies. Copies are leverage, Thornbury. When the new authorities arrive — and they will arrive, they're eleven miles east as of this morning — they will want to know who lived where, who owed what, who can be taxed and who conscripted. I will have the only book in the province that answers those questions. Which means I am no longer a colonel of a dissolved command. I am a merchant, and this is my stock."

"And us?" I asked. "The names in it. Are they stock too?"

"They're collateral," he said, without shame, and that absence of shame was the most honest thing he had ever shown me. "As are you."

* * *

He offered me tea. I sat in the chair across from his desk because sitting seemed safer than standing, and the room was warm from the stove that was eating the town's history, and somewhere below us a soldier sang tunelessly while he worked, and the whole arrangement had the quality of a dream where the house is on fire and everyone discusses wallpaper.

"You have until dawn," Vess said. "Then I ride west, toward the corridor, ahead of the advance. I've requisitioned horses — my last official act, appropriately enough, conducted entirely on paper." He poured the tea; it was weak, over-steeped twice, the taste of scarcity. "Here is what I know, Thornbury, and I offer it freely, because free is the only currency I have left. The partisans took your convoy. Your letter is moving — don't insult us both by denying it; a thing like that leaves ripples. And when the new army comes, they will not care about your republic's promises or its crimes. They will care about order. Order requires lists. I have the lists."

"You'll sell the town to them."

"I'll *negotiate* with them. Whether it becomes a sale depends on terms." He looked into his cup. "Unless someone negotiates first. Unless someone offers them something better than what I hold. Leverage, Thornbury, is only worth what the other side doesn't have."

I heard it then, the shape beneath the words, the way you hear a footstep on a stair before you see the foot. He was afraid. Not of me — he had never been afraid of me — but of the transaction to come, of arriving at the new authorities' table with one book and no army and a title from a country that had stopped existing. The hunter had become a debtor, and debtors bargain, and bargaining men make mistakes.

"What do you want from me?" I asked.

"Your silence, for one night. Whatever your friends across the marsh intend, they must not intercept my departure. I shoot straighter than I bargain, and I'll be carrying enough grief to use it." He set down the cup. "And your answer by dawn to a simpler question: whether you leave with me or stay for them. I can erase you, Thornbury. A word in the right margin, a line through your function, and you stop existing in any record anywhere. Some people spend their whole lives wanting that."

For a moment — I confess it, I want it written somewhere, even if only in this telling — the offer opened under my feet like ice. Erasure. No commission, no seal, no number, no file in some border archive reducing me to testimony. To walk out of history the way I had never walked into it, chosen.

"I'll give you my answer at dawn," I said.

He nodded, satisfied, and returned to his inventory, and I went down the stairs past the boy feeding the stove, out into the square where the smoke had thinned to nothing and the stars were coming out over a town whose paper self had just ceased to exist.

Jude would be waiting at the smokehouse. There was a bargain forming in the dark across the marsh, I could feel it the way you feel weather in an old wound — and I knew, walking fast through the freezing streets with my collar up and my breath tearing white, that whatever Jude was about to trade, I would have to hold my tongue to make it hold.

Dawn was six hours away. Vess would have his answer.

But not the one he was expecting.
