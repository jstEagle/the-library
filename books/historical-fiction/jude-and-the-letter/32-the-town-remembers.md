## 32. The Town Remembers

The first evening after the burning, no one spoke of it.

That was the strangest part, Jude thought afterward — not the fire itself, which they had all watched from doorways and windows as the smoke rose black and greasy from the customs house yard, carrying up into the winter sky the shredded ghosts of forty years of tithes and debts and births. Not even the ash that settled over Aldenmoor for two days afterward, fine and grey, dusting the well rope and the church steps and the loaves in Maren Ostley's window until it seemed the whole town had been baked in a dirty oven. What struck Jude was the silence. The people of Aldenmoor had spent their lives being written down, measured, entered, signed for, and now that the writing was gone they did not wail or curse. They simply went about their evenings with their heads bent, like mourners at a funeral where the name of the dead cannot be spoken.

Jude understood why on the second night, when old Widow Cetch came to the well.

She came late, after the water-gatherers had gone home, when the square was blue with dusk and the mountains to the east had turned the color of a bruise. She carried no bucket. She walked to the well's stone lip, laid both her hands flat upon it the way one lays hands on a coffin, and said, in a voice pitched for no one and everyone:

"Halloran Cetch. Cooper. Born under the sign of the Fish Moon, forty-one years past. Married me with a ring of barrel hoop iron because he hadn't two coins to rub together. Dead eleven years. Father of Tam and Ansel. Owed nobody."

Then she turned and walked home.

Jude, who had been latching the office shutters across the square, stood very still in the cold and felt something move through them that had no proper name — part grief, part awe, part the cold clarity of a lock turning. *Owed nobody.* That was ledger language. That was the closing line of an account. The widow had not come to pray. She had come to *enter* him.

The ledgers were ash, but Ashenmoor — no, Aldenmoor, Jude corrected themself fiercely, though the slip felt prophetic — Aldenmoor would not let its names go into the fire uncounted.

* * *

By the fourth evening there were six of them at the well.

Jude went deliberately, deliberately being the operative word, because everything they did now was weighed against the possibility of soldiers' ears, and the garrison still held the customs house even if its colonel had fled east two nights past with a wagon train that did not come back. But the square was dark, and the soldiers had begun keeping to their walls — unpaid, unfed, uncertain whose flag would fly over them by spring — and so Jude crossed the snow with their collar up and joined the small knot of figures at the well as if joining a queue for water.

Maren Ostley was there, flour still on her knuckles despite the hour. The Ferro brothers, who ran the mill race. Old Doctor Ilse, who had buried half the town's grandparents. A girl of perhaps fifteen whom Jude did not know, which itself was becoming remarkable — Jude had known every face in Aldenmoor since childhood, and the war had brought strangers the way a breached dike brings silt.

They were reciting.

"Pieter Ferro. Miller's third son. Lame in the left leg from the cart accident, danced anyway at his sister's wedding. Called to the transport lists, March last. Taken from the mill gate at dawn. Twenty-two years old."

"Emelie Ferro. His mother. Followed the wagon as far as the bridge before the guards turned her back. Died of the fever in November. Never believed he wouldn't come home."

One voice, then another, around the circle. No order to it that Jude could discern at first, and then, listening longer, an order beneath the disorder: debts first, always, then kin, then the small particular that made the name a person and not an entry. *Lame in the left leg. Danced anyway.* No ledger in the customs house had ever contained such a line. The republic's clerks had recorded what a man owed; they had never once recorded that he danced.

When the circle thinned and the others drifted home, the unknown girl lingered, and Jude, unable to help themself, asked her name.

"Sereth," she said. "From Corvane, downriver. We heard—" She stopped, started again. "They say there's a town here that says its dead out loud. So they can't be lost twice."

"Who says that?"

"People walking the roads." She looked at the well, at the frost furring its stone lip. "It's true, then."

Jude thought of their mother, generations back, running the marsh paths with contraband salt and untaxed cloth, carrying in her head the names of every farm that would buy and every farm that would report. Memory as cargo. Memory as route. Perhaps it had never been any different; perhaps the whole world had always run on what people carried between them in the dark, and paper had only ever been the shadow such cargoes cast.

"It's true," Jude said. "Come tomorrow. Bring anyone who has a name to keep."

* * *

It grew the way fire grows, or floodwater: quietly, then all at once.

By the second week there were thirty at the well each evening, standing in the trampled snow in whatever light the sky gave, because someone — Maren, Jude suspected, though she denied it — had spread word that lanterns drew soldiers and memory needed none. They had developed rules without ever discussing them, the way congregations develop liturgy. The names went oldest first, those taken by the transports and the requisitions, then the recent dead of the occupation, then — and this was the part that undid Jude nightly — the living. The absent. Pieter Ferro was read every evening as *taken*, not dead, because no one knew, and not knowing was its own category now, its own column in the invisible book.

Doctor Ilse kept the order of it, mostly, tapping her stick when a recitation strayed into argument. Because arguments came. Grief does not stay tidy.

"He did *so* owe somebody, he owed the excise three years—"

"And paid it in work when his roof burned, which your precious ledger never once recorded, so we'll have it said properly now—"

"Enough," the doctor's stick would crack against the well stone. "Next name."

And the next name would come, and the next, voices layering in the cold air with breath steaming like incense, and Jude stood among them each night with the hymnal-shaped absence against their ribs — for the true book, the copied book, lay plastered into the church wall where Jude had sealed it with their own hands, and only Jude and Beatrix knew, and Beatrix was gone across the marsh with the letter and had been gone twenty-three days.

Twenty-three days. Jude counted the way the well counted: aloud to no one, owed to nobody but themself.

* * *

On an evening of hard frost, with the stars sharp as nailheads, Tam came.

Jue saw him at the edge of the circle before they heard him — saw the set of his shoulders, the soldier's roll in his walk that he'd never quite shed even after mustering out, the way he stood apart from crowds the way a man stands apart who has decided he deserves whatever happens. Since the smokehouse, since the confrontation that had opened a wound between them wider than any the war had yet cut, Tam had kept to the far side of town, sleeping in the loft over Hargrave's stable, taking shifts as a hired guard for the occupying garrison that no one else would take.

He had talked. He had sold the town's fear to Vess's clerk for a promised pass east. Everyone knew it now; Sereth's road-walkers had carried even that. And still the circle did not close against him when he stepped forward into the space by the well. That was the ritual's terrible courtesy: everyone could come. That was also its judgment, sharper than any banishment, because coming meant speaking, and speaking meant entering himself.

Tam stood a long moment with his jaw working. Snow creaked under his boots. Thirty faces watched him without warmth and without cruelty, the way a ledger watches a hand.

"There's a name I owe," he said finally, hoarse.

No one answered. Doctor Ilse gave the smallest nod.

"Tobin Marsh." Their cousin — second cousin, the one who had run the marsh paths with their mother, drowned in the weir flood when Jude was nine. "I'm the one who dared him onto the ice. Nobody ever wrote that down. Mother took it for an accident and I let her take it, thirty years, because it was easier." His voice cracked like river ice. "Tobin Marsh. Drowned at twelve years old because of me. There. It's entered."

Silence held the square. Somewhere a dog barked twice at the edge of town and stopped, as if ashamed.

Then Maren Ostley — broad, floured, unsentimental Maren — said, "Entered and witnessed," and the circle murmured it after her, *entered and witnessed*, and Jude felt tears freeze in the corners of their eyes because they understood suddenly what they had built here, what the town had built through them and despite them. Not a memorial. Memorials are for finished things. This was a working ledger, open, alive, still taking entries — and it had just accepted the hardest kind: the entry a man makes against himself.

Tam walked away across the snow before the next name was called, and no one followed him, and no one turned their backs either. Both mercies, Jude thought. Both maybe the same mercy.

* * *

Afterward, Jude stayed behind as the square emptied, as they had done most nights, running the count.

Not the town's count — their own. The letter, gone east with Beatrix and the partisans, twenty-three days on the road. The hymnal, in the church wall behind the third station of the cross, where the mortar had been cut and reset by lamplight with flour paste and river sand. The names in it: one hundred and forty-seven, conscription and transport, copied in two hands, theirs and Beatrix's, on the nights when a knock at the door had nearly ended everything. The informer: accounted for, wounded, witnessed. The checkpoints: the bridge open but watched, the mill gate dead since curfew, the ford passable only to those who knew the reed channels, and the reed channels were freezing over.

And the front — the *new* front, the other army, the one with different flags that everyone said marched faster and forgave nothing — was fourteen miles east and closer each day. The refugees coming up the river road no longer spoke of the republic at all. The republic had become a rumor about the past. What they described now had no politics in it, only movement: columns, wagons, requisitions, the same hunger wearing a newer coat.

Jude rested a hand on the frozen lip of the well, on the stone where a hundred mouths had breathed their dead into the winter air, and thought: paper burns. Stone doesn't even remember us. Only this — only mouths and ears and the passing-on. Their mother had known it. The widow Cetch had known it. Maybe it had taken the burning of everything for Jude to learn the family trade at last: that the safest vault in the world walks around on two legs and can be killed but not seized.

A sound made them turn.

Beatrix stood at the mouth of the lane by the bakery, travel-worn, thinner, hood back despite the cold, one shoulder lower than the other as if still braced against a pack that wasn't there anymore. Snow was falling lightly through the lamplight from the bakery window, catching in their hair.

Jude did not run. There was a full second — Jude would remember it all their life — where neither of them moved, where the whole oral ledger of the past month hung unspoken between them: the marsh, the patrol, the ledger lost to the water, the letter moving on without them toward the border. Then Beatrix said, in a voice scraped raw by roads:

"The well's still talking. I heard it from the bridge. I thought I was dreaming."

"It talks," Jude said. "Mostly it listens." And then, because some things cannot wait for ceremony, "The letter?"

"Delivered. Moving again, with my testimony attached. It's out of our hands now." Beatrix came closer, close enough that Jude could see the frost-nipped exhaustion, and something underneath it, something newly hollowed and strangely light. "I have no commission. No seal. No name that means anything to anyone." A pause, and the ghost of the old careful irony: "I've come to be entered, Jude. In your book. If you'll have me."

Behind them, in the dark above the square, the church bell stirred in the wind without ringing — and down the river road, faint under the snow-silence, came a sound Jude had learned to dread in a different uniform's colors: wheels. Many wheels, and marching feet, coming north through the night toward Aldenmoor.
