## 34. The Front Arrives

The bells woke them before the guns did.

Jude had learned, in these last months, to sort the sounds of war the way a merchant sorts coin — by weight, by ring, by the small dishonesties of each. The old army's artillery had been a deep-throated thing, a bellows cough somewhere south of the marsh, close enough some mornings to rattle the frost from the windowpanes. What came now was thinner. Sharper. A different foundry, a different doctrine, a different set of men who had decided that Aldenmoor was worth passing through on the way to somewhere that mattered.

But it was the bells that told him first. Not the church bells — Father Anselm had ordered those silenced weeks ago, saying there was nothing left in the town worth celebrating and no one left with the heart to hear it — but the small alarm bell behind the well house, the one the fire brigade had rung in his grandfather's time for chimney fires and flooded cellars. It rang twice, stopped, rang again. *Come and look. Come and look.*

Jude dressed in the dark, hands finding each garment by habit, and paused at the shelf above the cold stove. The hymnal sat where it had sat since the night of the copying, wrapped in oilcloth, ordinary as a loaf of bread. He did not touch it. He had made himself a promise in the smokehouse, in the days after Tamlim walked out with his split lip and his silence: *when they come, you will not be holding it.* A man caught with contraband in his hands is a smuggler. A man caught with clean hands is only a man. So he let it sit, and he let his fingers ache with not reaching for it, and he went out into the blue hour before dawn to see what history had brought him this time.

* * *

They came down the east road in a gray drizzle, and they were magnificent, and that was the most frightening thing about them.

The old republic's soldiers had arrived in Aldenmoor eight months ago looking like an argument that had been going on too long — coats patched at the elbows, boots resoled with cart leather, officers whose accents drifted further from the capital with every retreat. They had been tired, and their tiredness had made them human, and Colonel Vess had been the exception that proved the rule: pressed and polished to the last, courteous as a knife laid on clean linen.

These ones were not tired.

They marched in columns of four with the easy, rolling stride of men who had been winning, and their flags were new — a device Jude did not recognize, some heraldic knot or crossed implement, stamped on cloth so fresh it still snapped in the wind. Their wagons were painted a uniform green. Their horses were fat. And at their head rode a major with a waxed mustache and a voice built for parade grounds, who reined up in the market square, surveyed the handful of townsfolk gathered under umbrellas and shawls, and announced — as though reading from a document he found genuinely moving — that the Provisional Authority of the Liberated Territories extended its protection to the loyal inhabitants of this township.

*Liberated.* Jude stood at the edge of the crowd with rain running off his hood and turned the word over like a coin he suspected of being shaved. Liberated from what, and for whom, and at what rate of exchange?

He got his answer within the hour. It came in the form of a lieutenant with a clipboard — a clipboard, Jude thought, they had traded Vess's leather ledgers for clipboards, progress of a kind — who requisitioned, in this order: the mill's remaining stores of flour, twelve draft horses, all copper cookware above a specified weight, the customs house for use as a district records office, and every able-bodied man between sixteen and fifty for three days' labor unloading barges at the wharf.

Three days. The number had a familiar shape. Everything had a familiar shape. The flour, the horses, the copper, the ledgers, the lists — it was the same music transposed into a new key, and the town stood in the square listening to it played again, older now, knowing how the melody went.

Old Marta Kessel, who had buried two sons in the last war and one grandson in this one, said it aloud before anyone could stop her, in her cracked voice that carried further than a bugle: "You're the fourth army to promise us protection, boy. The first three are all dead."

The lieutenant wrote something on his clipboard. Jude watched him do it and felt the cold go down his spine, because he understood, in the way one understands things without wanting to, that the writing-down was the whole point. Names on paper. It always came back to names on paper. Somewhere in a wagon or a satchel or a headquarters tent there was a blank form waiting for Aldenmoor's households, its debts, its secrets, its boys between sixteen and fifty, and some clerk in a better coat than Jude's would fill it out neatly, and it would be true, and truth would be the dangerous thing it had always been.

* * *

By noon the customs house had new occupants, and Jude made himself walk past it.

This was discipline, not curiosity. He had spent thirty years learning the grammar of officialdom — how occupiers moved, what they counted first, how long the honeymoon of *protection* lasted before the requisitions grew teeth. The answer, invariably, was: less time than you hope. So he strolled by with a basket of turnips he did not need, nodding to the sentry at the door like a man with nowhere better to be, and catalogued what he saw the way his mother had taught him to catalogue a toll station forty years ago on the marsh road: two sentries visible, probably a third in the shadow of the arch; the windows of the back office papered over already, which meant paperwork worth hiding; a signals wagon in the yard, which meant the new army talked to itself over wire, which meant orders came from far away, which meant local commanders had discretion, which meant favors could still be bought, for a while, until the wire grew longer and the discretion died.

The ledgers were gone — Vess had taken them or burned them, Jude still did not know which, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. But paper bred paper. Within a week the new masters would want a census. They would want tax rolls. They would want to know who owned the mill and who owed the granary and which families had kin across the marsh with the partisans, and there would be no ledger to tell them.

There would only be memory. His memory. The town's.

And the hymnal.

* * *

Father Anselm was in the churchyard when Jude came up the path that evening, cutting dead heads off the rose bushes with a paring knife, because even at the end of the world a priest keeps a garden, and even a garden that produces nothing but thorns wants tending.

"They took the pews' copper fittings," the old man said without turning around. "Did you know there were copper fittings in the pews? Neither did I. A soldier showed me." He snipped a withered bloom and let it fall. "He apologized while he did it. That was new. Vess's men never apologized."

"Will you take an old friend's confession, Father? Or at least his company?"

"I'll take the company. Your sins are beyond my jurisdiction — I've heard them all already, secondhand, through your mother."

They went inside. The church smelled of candle wax and cold stone and, faintly, of the coffin that had passed through it in the autumn carrying more than old Widow Harsch's body — Jude still could not enter without his eyes going, involuntarily, to the flagstones by the transept, where the bearers had rested their burden, where grief had done its work as cover and he had walked behind the coffin with his head bowed and his guilt arranged as solemnly as any mourner's. He had wept that day, genuinely, for Widow Harsch and for the uses to which he had put her death. He suspected he would weep for it again someday, when there was time. There was never time.

"You've come about the wall," Anselm said.

Jude stopped halfway up the aisle. "I haven't said anything."

"You've been looking at the east chapel for three weeks, my son. Every visit. You look at it the way your mother used to look at the marsh before a run — measuring." The priest set his knife down on the ledge beneath the saint's niche. "During the last war, my predecessor hid refugees in the sacristy loft. Before that, in the plague years, they hid tithes. This church has been smuggling things into safety since before either of us had names, and it has never once lost a cargo." He looked at Jude with eyes gone pale as river ice but still, unmistakably, amused. "I assume whatever you're carrying weighs less than a family of five."

"It's a book."

"I rather assumed it was a book. You're a clerk; it would be a book." Anselm folded his hands. "I won't ask what's in it. If I don't know, I can't be accused of knowing, and if they arrest me they'll arrest me for the copper fittings like an honest man. But I will tell you this." He crossed to the east chapel, knelt, and pressed his thumb against the base of the wall where the plaster had crumbled away from the stone — the same wound in the masonry that had been there since Jude was a boy, when everyone said the church would repair it and no one ever had. Behind the plaster, Jude saw now, the stones were not mortared at all. They were fitted. Fitted dry, by hands that wanted them removable, generations ago, by people who had expected exactly this kind of visitor exactly this kind of forever.

"There's a cavity the depth of a forearm," Anselm said. "Dry. Sound. My predecessor kept the parish silver in it through two winters of occupation, and before him someone kept something older — I found a scrap of linen and a coin when I first investigated, both older than the republic, older than the kingdom it replaced." He sat back on his heels. "The men who built this wall knew that armies come and go and books outlast flags. They built for the book."

Jude knelt beside him. The stone was cold through his trousers, and the draught from the cavity breathed against his knuckles — the breath of the building itself, the slow exhale of a thing that had stood through everything and meant to stand through more.

He had carried the hymnal for months the way one carries a coal — cupped, protected, burning a little, impossible to set down. Every knock at his door, every clipboard in the square, every soldier's laugh had landed on its cover. And underneath all of it, under the fear, there had been a question he had not let himself ask: *what happens to the names when I die?* Because he would die, eventually, everyone in Aldenmoor would die, and a list kept only in one man's skull dies with the skull. Memory was where the town kept its names now — the recitations at the well each evening had seen to that, forty voices in the dusk, taking turns — but memory needed a seed. A root. Something buried deep enough to survive the burning of everything above it.

A wall built for the book.

"We'll do it tonight," Jude said. "After curfew. I'll come alone."

"You'll come after Compline," Anselm corrected gently. "I keep the keys, and I say the office at nine, and God has never once informed on anyone to an occupying army." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and looked down at Jude with something that was not quite a smile and not quite a blessing, though it might have been both. "Bring your mortar mix. You'll want to seal it properly. Plaster hides nothing if the seam shows."

* * *

He came at half past nine, in the black interval between the end of Compline and the start of the patrol's round, with the hymnal under his coat against his chest and a bucket of lime paste in one hand, and his heart behaving disgracefully the whole way, hammering at the sight of every lantern, though the streets were empty and the drizzle had thickened into honest rain, good cover weather, his mother would have called it, *the sky working for us tonight.*

Inside, the church was dark except for one candle Anselm had left burning at the altar — deliberately, Jude realized; a lit church was a church in use, a church with a priest saying his office, unremarkable. A dark church was a mystery, and mysteries invited investigation. The old man missed nothing.

They worked by feel and by the candle's borrowed light. Anselm drew out the fitted stones one by one and stacked them in order on a cloth, each in its place like words removed from a sentence to be returned exactly, and the cavity opened in the wall — shallow, dry, smelling of dust and centuries — and Jude unwrapped the oilcloth and held the hymnal for the last time.

It looked so small. A psalter, bound in scuffed boards, its spine repaired with thread that Tamlim's late wife had put there twenty years ago when it was only a hymnal and hung on the pew rack where anyone might read it. Inside, between the psalms of David, in the neat hand of a clerk who had learned penmanship copying tariff schedules: four hundred and eleven names. The conscription lists. The transport rolls. The bakers and the sweeps and the landlords, the midwives and the millers, the boy who had carried water to Beatrix's office every morning at eight. Written small enough to hide, complete enough to answer for.

He read three of the names aloud, very quietly, in the dark — the recitation at the well had become a habit of his soul, and he would not surrender this page of it even here, even now — and then he wrapped the book back in its oilcloth, and laid it flat in the cavity, and Anselm began fitting the stones home around it like a man closing a tomb.

"Whose names?" the priest asked, as he worked. Not *what* names. Whose.

"Our people," Jude said. "All of ours. Everyone the last army meant to take, and everyone this one will ask after, and everyone the next one will want counted." He heard his own voice go strange, go formal, the voice of the recitation at the well. "Kessel. Harsch. Brandt the baker. Old Sorrel who sweeps—"

"I married half of them," Anselm said. "Baptized the rest." The final stone slid into its seat, flush, seamless. He handed Jude the trowel. "Your turn. Make it look like neglect."

Jude plastered the seam with lime paste, and then, because neglect is an art, he ruined his own work slightly — dragged a fingertip through the wet edge, scattered dust, cracked the surface with the trowel handle so it matched the other wounds in the old wall. When it dried tomorrow it would be one more scar among scars, invisible precisely because the whole church was falling down in exactly that manner.

He stepped back. Nothing to see. That was the whole achievement: nothing to see.

"Forty years ago," Anselm said, watching him wash his hands in the stoop water, "the kingdom's men came through here doing what the republic's men did, doing what your new graycoats are doing in the square tonight. Different flags." He nodded toward the wall, toward the hidden weight inside it. "Same requisitions. And that book's contents survived all of them because somebody — some clerk with ink-stained fingers, I'd wager, some ancestor of yours — trusted it to stone instead of to governments." He blew out the candle, and the dark came in gentle and enormous. "Governments forget, Jude. It's their defining talent. Stone just waits."

Jude walked home through the rain with empty arms and did not feel light. He had thought — he had told himself, on the walk over — that hiding the hymnal would ease him, that laying down the coal would cool his hands. Instead he felt the specific vertigo of the climber who has finally planted his last piton and now must simply trust the mountain. The names were out of his keeping. In the morning the graycoats would begin their census, and their clipboards would multiply, and some lieutenant with a fine mustache would ask the town to account for itself, and the town would have to decide, household by household, tongue by tongue, how much of the truth it could afford — and all the while the real accounting, the true ledger, lay sealed in the east chapel breathing its thousand-year breath, waiting, patient as masonry, for whoever would come to open the wall.

He paused at his own gate. Down the valley, toward the marsh, the night was torn once by a distant flare — white, then red, hanging and dying over the far reeds. The partisans, signaling, or the army answering, or both; there was no longer any telling hunter from hunted by the color of their fires. And below it, fainter, almost imagined: wheels. Many wheels, on the border road, moving west.

Toward Aldenmoor. Toward the customs house with its new papered windows and its clipboards, where — he had heard it that afternoon from the cooper's boy, who had heard it carrying messages he was not supposed to carry — a prisoner was being held under quiet guard in the back office. A foreigner. A courier, someone said, the one who'd been staying with the widow on Chapel Lane all winter. The one the old colonel had kept close and questioned and never, quite, charged.

Beatrix.

The flare's light faded. The wheels came on through the rain, patient as the wall, and Jude stood at his gate counting them, the way his mother had taught him long ago on the marsh road: *count what's coming, child, before it counts you.*
