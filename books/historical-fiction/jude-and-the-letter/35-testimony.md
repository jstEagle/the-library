## 35. Testimony

The archive stood at the edge of the neutral territory like a lighthouse built for a sea that had never learned to be dangerous — a long, low building of grey stone on a hill above the river crossing, its windows tall and clean, its chimneys smoking gently into the winter sky. Beatrix had passed buildings like it all their life without once considering what went on inside them: the quiet repositories of the world, where paper went to become permanent.

They had crossed the border two days ago, or three. The days had begun to blur together somewhere in the marsh country, in the aftermath of the ford, in the strange weightless weeks of travel with partisans who asked few questions and shared their bread as if questions were a currency they had outlawed. Beatrix remembered the border post itself only in fragments — the stamp coming down on a document that was no longer theirs, the clerk's voice saying *next*, the sound of their own boots on the floor of a country that did not know their name.

That was not quite true. It knew their name. That was precisely the problem, and precisely why they were here.

* * *

The reading room smelled of cold iron and old glue. Radiators ticked along the walls, fighting a losing war against the damp that came up from the river, and beneath it there was another smell, fainter but unmistakable to someone who had spent their whole adult life among documents: the sweetish, papery decay of things written long ago by hands now dead. Shelves ran away into the gloom in ranks, each one numbered, each one holding the residue of some administration or other — treaties, tax rolls, grain quotas, the manifestos of parties that no longer existed. Whole governments lived here now as dust and ink.

The examining officer was a woman named Halven. She was perhaps fifty, with spectacles that kept sliding down her nose and a manner so dry it seemed almost a kindness, as if she had decided long ago that solemnity was a cruelty and briskness a mercy. Her desk bore a carafe of water, two glasses, a recording apparatus of brass and rubber that looked like something salvaged from a ship's engine room, and a single sheet of paper with eleven numbered lines.

"You understand the procedure," she said, not looking up as she adjusted the machine's brass horn. "You will speak your testimony aloud. It will be transcribed. You will read the transcription and sign each page. What you sign becomes part of the permanent record of this conflict, available to any court, commission, or historian that may hereafter concern itself with the matter. Once signed, it cannot be withdrawn. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"There is water. People forget to drink, and then their voices go, and we must begin again." She looked up at last, and her eyes behind the slipping spectacles were sharp and unromantic. "You are courier seven-four-one-nine, diplomatic service, commissioned under the sealed protocols."

It was not a question, but it waited like one.

Beatrix sat with it. Seven-four-one-nine. They had carried that number for nineteen years, pressed into them the way a brand is pressed into livestock — at fourteen, an orphan of the last war, standing in a white corridor while a kind-faced official explained that names were for families and numbers were for the service, and that Beatrix would be given both, in a sense: a name to wear in the world and a number to be in the eyes of the ministry. *Loyal to paper, not to people.* That had been said to them once, as praise, by a supervisor whose face they could no longer recall. They had treasured it. They had built a life on it, floor by floor, dispatch by dispatch, until the night in Aldenmoor when a ledger full of neighbors' names had cracked the foundation like frost in stone.

"Yes," Beatrix said. "I am — I was — courier seven-four-one-nine."

Halven made a small note. "'Was,'" she repeated. "You anticipate my next instruction. Good. It saves time." She set down her pen and folded her hands, and for a moment the briskness fell away and something older and wearier showed through, like bedrock through topsoil. "I will tell you what most couriers do not understand until they sit in that chair. This archive does not want your number. Numbers we have in thousands; they are filed, cross-referenced, and forgotten. What this archive wants — what it exists to hold — is the truth from the mouth of someone who was there while it was still happening, before memory begins to arrange itself into something more comfortable. Your number got you across the border alive. It has done its work. From the moment you begin speaking, you are not an instrument of a state. The state is gone. You are a witness. Do you feel the difference?"

Beatrix thought of Jude, who knew every family in Aldenmoor by its debts and secrets, who had refused to flee because ledgers proved who owned what — who had waded an ice-choked river at night to keep a promise made of ink. Jude had never once confused themselves with their papers.

"Yes," Beatrix said again, and this time it felt less like answering and more like setting something down. "I think I finally do."

"Then let us begin." Halven reached for the machine and, with a click, released the cylinder. It began to turn with a soft, patient whirring, like a very small animal running in its sleep. "State for the record your name as you wish it to appear in history."

And there it was. Not *state your name* — *as you wish it to appear.*

Beatrix opened their mouth and found the corridor of the white building waiting there, and the kind-faced official, and nineteen years of being exactly what the paper said they were. They thought of Colonel Vess, polite and thorough, keeping his copy of the margins where their name appeared. They thought of the letter — the amnesty, signed and sealed and knowingly undelivered, the republic's promise left to rot in a pouch in a provincial customs house while the transport lists filled with bakers and sweeping-boys. They thought of the well in Aldenmoor, where — Jude had written, in the one letter that had reached them — the townspeople gathered each evening to recite the names from memory, since memory was the only ledger left.

"My name," Beatrix said, and their voice held steady in the ticking, gluing-smelling cold, "was assigned to me by an institution that no longer exists."

Halven did not stop the machine. She watched over her spectacles, waiting.

"I was raised by the diplomatic service," Beatrix went on, and the words came slowly at first, like water feeling for a channel, and then faster. "Orphaned in the last war. Trained to carry, not to read. Taught that loyalty meant the safe arrival of a document, regardless of its contents — that the courier's virtue was in the carrying, and the contents belonged to other consciences. I believed that. I want that in the record plainly: I believed it, and it was not a stupid belief, and men and women better than I have believed worse ones in better causes. But it was a belief designed by people who benefited from couriers who didn't read."

The cylinder turned. Somewhere in the stacks, a clock struck the half hour, muffled by paper.

"In the winter of the collapse, I was stationed at Aldenmoor customs house, holding a sealed pouch against the arrival of a relief convoy. I broke the seal. I read the letter. It was an amnesty — a full, signed, sealed promise of amnesty for the province, dated months earlier, never delivered. The republic knew what the requisitioning army intended for these towns. The republic wrote the apology in advance and locked it in a bag." Beatrix's hands had closed on the edge of the table, knuckles pale. "I can describe the seal, the countersignature, the watermark, the docket number. I can do it from memory. I did nothing else for weeks but memorize it, in case the original was lost. It is not lost. It is delivered. And I will say how, and by whose hands, and at what cost, and this archive will hold those names, because the people who saved that letter deserve to be in the permanent record even if—" their voice caught, just slightly, "—even if they would never ask to be."

Halven poured water and pushed the glass across without comment. Beatrix drank. Outside, a bird — some grey river bird indifferent to the fall of republics — called twice from the eaves.

"Proceed," Halven said quietly.

And Beatrix proceeded. For three hours, with breaks for water and once, at Halven's flat insistence, for soup brought in on a tray, they gave the whole of it: the requisition, the ledgers seized, Vess's tea and his terrible courtesy; the hymnal with its false spine and its true pages; the coffin on the bridge; the forged grain docket at the mill gate; the marsh ford with ice in the reeds and the lantern swinging; the patrol, the partisans, the letter going into the water and Jude going in after it, surfacing with the satchel held high and the true ledger surrendering to the current like a soul given up to God. They told about the brother — the informant, the smokehouse, the wounds that talking had made and talking could not close. They told about the town reciting names at the well.

At some point, deep in the account of the ford, Beatrix realized they were no longer performing testimony. They were simply speaking, and the machine was drinking it in, and the difference between themselves and the truth they carried had thinned to nothing — the way, in flood season, a river forgets which bank is which.

* * *

They broke at dusk. Halven switched off the apparatus and sat back, rubbing the bridge of her nose beneath her spectacles.

"The transcription will be ready by morning," she said. "Nine pages, perhaps ten. You'll read every word. Where the transcript differs from your meaning, you correct it. Then you sign."

"And after that?"

"After that, your commission is dissolved. Formally. The service that issued your number no longer exists to receive it back, so the archive dissolves it by act of record." She took off her spectacles entirely and polished them on her sleeve, and without them her face looked strangely young and undefended. "I will tell you something I am not required to tell witnesses. I examined forty-one people from that side of the river this year. Soldiers, clerks, one minister's mistress, two smugglers. Every one of them wanted something from this room — asylum, vindication, revenge, a new name for an old self. You are the first who came to give something away." She put the spectacles back on, and the briskness returned like a shutter closing. "There is a guest house by the mill road. Tell them Halven sent you. The soup is bad and the beds are honest."

* * *

Beatrix slept badly and woke before light, as they had every morning since the marsh, the body still keeping its sentry's hours. In the grey dark of the guest-house room they sat at the window and watched the river run below, brown and patient, carrying whatever it carried toward the low countries and the sea.

Nineteen years, and the sum of it was this: a number they would never hear spoken again, a skill set belonging to a government that had ceased, a name that had been issued like a uniform and could now, at last, be taken off. There should have been terror in it. There was, a little — the same vertigo as the first lie, the same falling feeling as the convoy burning. But beneath the vertigo was something else, something that had been growing in them since a lamplit kitchen in Aldenmoor where they had copied conscription lists into a hymnal while a knock at the door nearly ended everything: the strange, terrifying lightness of being no one in particular.

Identity as something assigned, not chosen — they had thought that was their tragedy. Now they understood it differently. Assigned identity was a debt. Chosen identity was a practice. And practice, like testimony, happened daily, in the mouth, out loud, witnessed.

In the morning the transcription waited on Halven's desk, ten pages in a clerk's careful hand. Beatrix read every word, twice. On page six they corrected *courier 7419* to *the courier then serving Aldenmoor*, and Halven raised an eyebrow but made no objection. On page nine they added a sentence of their own dictation, about a clerk named Jude who knew the back roads and the back rooms, and about what the town owed and to whom. At the bottom of the final page, beneath the line marked *Signature of Witness*, there was a smaller line: *Name, for the record (optional).*

Optional. No one had ever offered them that word before.

Beatrix picked up the pen. Their hand — the careful hand, the hand that had been trained until handwriting became a kind of silence — hesitated above the paper for one long breath, listening, it seemed, for instructions from a government that no longer existed.

Then they wrote, in letters neither hurried nor ornate, simply their own: *Beatrix Thornbury, witness.* And beneath it, because it seemed right that the record should know, in small characters: *late of Aldenmoor.*

Halven blotted the page, filed the testimony into a grey folder, and wrote a number on the spine — a different number now, an archival one, belonging to the truth rather than to the state.

"That's everything," she said. "You're a free person with a bad soup recommendation and no papers worth the name."

"Thank you," Beatrix said, and meant the whole impossible size of it.

They were at the door, coat half on, the winter morning bright and cold beyond the archive steps, when Halven spoke again from behind the desk, and her voice had lost its briskness entirely.

"One thing more, witness. A letter arrived for you yesterday — no, don't look alarmed, it came by the partisan post, which means whoever sent it trusts you and is not wrong to. It's been searched, logged, and left unopened; we are neutral, not curious." She held it out across the desk: a thin envelope, resealed with plain wax, addressed in a hand Beatrix knew better than their own — the careful clerk's hand, the hand that had copied names by lamplight, the hand that kept the town's memory at the well.

"It's from Aldenmoor," Halven said. "I'd guess it's important. I'd also guess," and here something almost like a smile touched the dry old face, "that whatever it says, it isn't orders. Couriers get orders. Witnesses get news."

Beatrix took the letter. The paper was warm from the archive's failing radiators, light as a leaf, heavy as the ford at midnight. They turned it over once in their hands, and then, standing in the doorway with the whole unwritten future open on the hill below, they broke the seal.
