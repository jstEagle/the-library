## 38. Beatrix Returns

The road to Aldenmoor had been a line on a map once, and then a set of coordinates in a courier's satchel, and then a thing Beatrix carried in their body like a splinter worked toward the skin's surface. Now it was simply a road again — mud froze in the wheel ruts into brown glass, and the hedges on either side had gone wild without anyone's shears, and somewhere beyond the grey lip of the hills the wind was moving, patient and enormous, pushing winter ahead of it like a herd.

Beatrix walked. There had been no other way. The partisan columns took payment in usefulness, and Beatrix had none left to sell; the border archive had taken their testimony and given them a receipt for their own name; the neutral towns past the river took nothing and gave less. So they had traded the last of their money for a coat with someone else's buttons and set out east, following the old dispatch routes in reverse, the ones they had memorized at nineteen when the republic still believed in itself and expected its couriers to believe too.

No commission. No seal. No letter. The thought arrived each morning like a report from a distant province: *you are carrying nothing.* For twenty years Beatrix had been a vessel, a hinge between documents and destinations, and the weight of the satchel had been the weight of being real. Now their pockets held a tin cup, half a loaf gone hard as masonry, and a folded paper that said, in an archivist's indifferent hand, that one B. Thornbury, courier second class, serial 4471, had surrendered all credentials and was discharged from the service of a state that no longer existed. The paper protected them from nothing. It simply proved that they had been erased properly, by the book.

They found that they loved it anyway.

* * *

Aldenmoor announced itself the way it always had — by smell before sight. Woodsmoke first, then the wet-stone breath of the millrace, then, faintly under everything, the tannery, which no one had ever successfully pretended did not exist. Beatrix came over the last rise in the late afternoon with the light behind the town and stopped without meaning to.

It was smaller than memory insisted. That was the first lie memory told, and Beatrix stood in the road correcting it, taking inventory the old way: the church spire, the customs house roofline with two slates missing where artillery had kissed it and moved on, the well-square, the bridge. The town had not burned. It had been handled — rough hands, but hands that wanted the machinery intact. Windows were patched with oilcloth. A chimney had shed itself into a heap of brick beside a house that otherwise stood, absurdly, whole. The new army's flags hung off the customs house façade, wrong color, wrong beast on the cloth, and nobody in the square so much as glanced up at them.

People moved through the square doing the ordinary arithmetic of survival: wood, water, bread, gossip. A woman beat a rug against a railing with the rhythm of someone keeping time for a much longer labor. Two boys argued over a handcart. And beneath it, threading through the ordinary sound like a bass note through birdsong, came a sound Beatrix did not recognize and could not place — many voices, low, in unison, rising from somewhere near the well and falling away again, like a tide.

Names, they would learn. But they did not know that yet. They only knew that the town was speaking to itself in the evenings, and that the speaking sounded like prayer, and that prayer had never once figured in any intelligence briefing about Aldenmoor.

Beatrix pulled the borrowed coat tighter and started down into the town, and their heart performed a maneuver they had not authorized: it began, quite without permission, to run.

* * *

The bridge was the first checkpoint. It had always been the first checkpoint — Chapter Nine of Beatrix's private ledger of fear, the crossing that demanded the lie about the grain merchant's cousin, the one where Jude had walked beside a coffin and weaponized a neighbor's grief. Beatrix remembered standing on the far bank of their own dread and mapping the guard rotations while their hands went cold inside their gloves.

Now they approached it as a courier approaches anything: cataloguing exits, counting sentries, measuring the sightlines from the customs house windows.

There were no sentries.

There was no sandbag emplacement at the near end. There was no barrier, no table of papers, no stove with its pipe venting the smell of cheap tobacco, no bored soldier with a list and a stamp. The bridge stood bare over the water, its stone parapet furred with frost, its planking patched in three places with lumber that had clearly been donated from somebody's fence. Someone had hung a lantern from the old mounting bracket — unlit, waiting for evening — and beneath the bracket, carved small and recent into the stone, someone had cut a single letter or symbol that Beatrix couldn't read at this distance, or perhaps wasn't meant to.

An unguarded bridge. Beatrix stood at the head of it for a long moment, longer than any checkpoint had ever held them, because it turned out that arriving without papers was harder than arriving with false ones. With false papers you had a role, a story, a self to perform. With none, there was only the person walking, and the wind, and the question of what remained of them once the documents were subtracted.

They stepped onto the planks. The sound of their boots was very loud and very ordinary.

Halfway across, the water below caught the last of the light and held it, a seam of molten pewter running under the stone. Beatrix looked down and saw the river that had carried smuggled goods in Jude's mother's day, that had frozen hard enough the previous winter for two people to wade beside reeds full of ice with a republic's broken promise pressed against a chest. The river kept no ledgers. The river accepted everyone.

"Stop."

The voice came from the far end, and Beatrix's body reacted a full second before their mind — the flinch, the hand moving to a satchel strap that wasn't there, the rehearsed calm sliding down over the face like a visor. Then the mind caught up, because the voice had been light, dry, familiar as a key worn smooth by one lock.

Jude stood at the far end of the bridge with a bundle of firewood under one arm and no weapon anywhere visible, wearing a coat that had been mended more times than a coat should survive, looking exactly as they had looked across a lamplit table while a hymnal filled with stolen names lay open between them — except thinner, and grayer around the eyes, and smiling in the careful way of someone who has learned that joy arrives faster if you give it permission slowly.

"That's far enough," Jude said. "State your business."

"I—" Beatrix's voice cracked on the first attempt, rusted from disuse. They tried again. "I have no papers."

"No papers." Jude shifted the firewood to the other arm and came forward one plank, two, unhurried. "No seal?"

"No seal."

"No commission? No letter? No number they pinned on you at birth?"

"Gone. All of it. Surrendered at the border archive. They gave me a receipt." Beatrix heard how it sounded and almost laughed. "For my name."

Jude stopped an arm's length away, close enough now that Beatrix could see the frost melting out of the air between their mouths, and studied them with those clerk's eyes that had once inventoried every household in Aldenmoor — debts, secrets, doors. They were reading Beatrix the way they read a ledger, line by line, checking for forgery.

"And what does the paper say you are," Jude asked quietly, "now that it says nothing?"

"Nothing," Beatrix said. "It says I'm a witness who used to be a courier. It says the state that issued me has dissolved. It says—" Their throat closed, briefly, on twenty years of believing that identity was something assigned. "It says I'm only myself. Which is either the worst thing that's ever happened to me or the best. I've had four hundred miles to decide and I haven't."

Jude nodded slowly, as though this answer satisfied some entry they'd been waiting to reconcile.

"Then you can cross," they said. "No charge. No stamp. First checkpoint in Aldenmoor's history that lets a person through on their face alone." A pause, and the careful smile loosened into something real and slightly crooked. "Hello, Beatrix."

"Hello, Jude."

"You look terrible."

"I've been informed the alternative is suspicious."

Jude made a sound that was half laugh, half exhale held since autumn, and then — this surprised them both, Beatrix saw it land on Jude's own face like unexpected weather — stepped forward and pressed their forehead briefly against Beatrix's shoulder, firewood and all, a contact lasting three seconds and containing roughly a war's worth of unsaid things. When they drew back neither of them mentioned it. Some transactions are recorded nowhere and are therefore permanent.

* * *

They crossed the bridge side by side, and Aldenmoor received Beatrix the way a house receives someone returning during the owner's absence: with small evidence everywhere of change they hadn't witnessed. Jude talked as they walked, low and steady, a clerk's briefing delivered like a love letter.

"The new administration requisitions, but slowly. They're stretched thin. Vess is gone — fled east, then further east, they say, though no one says it loudly. The ledgers he didn't burn are ash, and the ones he burned are also ash, and what remains of this town's legal existence fits inside a hymnal hidden in the church wall, which we'll discuss later, indoors, after dark, in the manner of people who have earned their paranoia."

"The partisans' bargain," Beatrix said. "The names struck from the transport lists. Did it hold?"

"It held. Whether because of the letter, or because the new army hasn't found the old lists, or because luck is a currency that spends fastest right before it's worthless — no one knows. The town doesn't ask. The town recites."

"That's the sound. From the square. Every evening?"

"Every evening since the thaw started. Names at the well. Whoever remembers, speaks; whoever hears, keeps. Marta Halloran. The Ostrowski family, all six. The boy who swept your office — Tomas — his name goes third from the top now, because Ansel always forgets him and Ansel's daughter corrects him, and the correction has become part of the ritual." Jude glanced sideways. "You'd have hated it at first. Paper man like you. Nothing written down. But it grows on you. Memory is just a ledger that audits itself nightly."

"I'm not a paper man anymore," Beatrix said. "I have a receipt."

They had reached the edge of the square, and the well stood there in the failing light with a knot of perhaps thirty townspeople gathered loosely around it, and Beatrix understood with a lurch that the evening recitation was about to begin, and that they were about to stand in the middle of it, anonymous, undocumented, unannounced. Nobody looked at them. Or rather — everybody looked at them exactly as long as one looks at a stranger, which is to say once, briefly, with assessment, and then away. It was the most extraordinary experience of Beatrix's life. In twenty years of moving through checkpoints, every eye had weighed them and every weighing had been a threat. Here the glance passed over them and dismissed them kindly, the way one dismisses weather, and underneath the dismissal was something Beatrix had never once been offered by any town they'd passed through on official business:

Indifference. Ordinary human indifference. The profound, restful indifference of a place that owes you nothing and fears nothing from you.

The recitation began. A tall old man spoke the first names into the gathering dusk, unhurried, and the crowd repeated them back like a congregation, and the names rose off the cobbles and up past the patched roofs into the darkening sky where no archive would ever hold them. Beatrix stood at the edge with cold hands and listened, and somewhere in the middle of it — around the fourth name, or maybe the ninth — realized that their face was wet and that Jude had, without comment, positioned themselves slightly upwind, between Beatrix and the town's noticing, guarding them the way one guards a flame.

* * *

Later there was soup in Jude's kitchen, and the kitchen was warm, and the firewood Jude had been carrying turned out to have been carried home for precisely this contingency, which meant — Beatrix understood slowly, and then all at once — that Jude had known they were coming. Had known for days.

"How?" Beatrix said, over the rim of the tin cup.

"A letter arrived three weeks ago from the border archive. To the town, care of whoever keeps records." Jude's mouth curved. "That's me, apparently. It contained a copy of sworn testimony, redacted, and a note that the witness might someday return and should be received without— " Jude consulted the ceiling, remembering. "*Without prejudice to the matters testified to.* Bureaucracy's idea of a blessing. So I've been walking to the bridge most evenings. Just in case the witness came home the obvious way."

"You waited at a checkpoint that isn't a checkpoint anymore."

"Someone had to staff it." Jude turned their cup in their hands, and for a moment the lamplight caught the exhaustion underneath the steadiness — a winter's worth, two winters', a lifetime's. "First checkpoint in Aldenmoor's history," they said again, softer, "and I'm its entire garrison. One clerk with firewood." They looked up. "You're staying."

It wasn't a question, but Beatrix answered it anyway, and found the answer had assembled itself somewhere on the four-hundred-mile road without requiring their signature.

"I have nothing to deliver," they said. "Nowhere to file myself. I don't know how to be a person without a destination." They set down the cup. "But I'd like to learn here. If the position exists."

Jude was quiet for a moment. Outside, the last voices of the recitation faded from the square, and the town settled into its night sounds — a dog, the millrace, wind testing the shutters of a republic that no longer owned them.

"There's work," Jude said at last, and there was something in their voice now, a gravity that hadn't been there at the bridge, something held and deliberate, saved up. "Actually. Work only you in the world can do."

"Jude."

"The hymnal's safe in the church wall. It's been safe all winter. But it's one copy, in one wall, in a town that has already been burned once and requisitioned twice, and I am — " the word cost them — "tired, Beatrix. Tired of being the only vault. The names need a second copy. Made carefully, by two people, one who reads and one who writes, decided together about who else ever sees them." Jude looked across the table, and the lamplight stood in their eyes. "I've been waiting at a bridge every evening for three weeks for a courier with no letters left to carry. Because I thought — if you came back — you might be willing to carry this one. Not because a state commands it. Because we choose it."

The fire ticked. Somewhere a shutter banged and settled.

Beatrix looked down at their hands — hands that had held seals and satchels and a letter that could save or damn, hands now holding nothing but warmth — and felt the strange lightness of the past months turn, finally, into something with weight and shape and a direction.

"All right," they said. "Tomorrow. You read. I write."

Jude reached beneath the loose hearthstone they had never noticed, and brought up a candle stub and a pen, and set both on the table between them like instruments of state.
