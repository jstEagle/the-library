## 39. The Second Copy

The church wall had given up the hymnal the way a river gives up a drowned thing — pale, swollen at one corner, smelling of lime and old stone. Jude laid it on the table in the vestry and did not touch it again for a long moment, the way one does not touch a sleeping animal one has feared dead.

Beatrix stood at the window with their back to the room. Outside, Aldenmoor was doing what it always did in the hour before dusk: smoke rising crooked from damp chimneys, the blacksmith's hammer keeping its stubborn time, a dog complaining at nothing. The new army's banners hung over the customs house now, a different color than Vess's, and the soldiers beneath them were younger and hungrier and no better. But tonight none of them were looking at the church.

"You're certain the wall kept it dry enough," Beatrix said. Not a question, really. A prayer shaped like a question.

"The mason's boy owed my mother," Jude said. "He lined the cavity with waxed canvas. He didn't ask why a hymnal needed a coffin." They turned the cover back. The ink had held. Of course it had; they had used the good iron-gall, the batch that ate into paper like conscience into memory. The names were all there. Three hundred and eleven of them, copied in two hands across forty pages dressed up as verses, numbered like psalms, hidden in plain scripture the way everything true in this country had always been hidden in plain scripture.

Beatrix came to the table slowly, the way people approach graves of people they loved badly.

"We should not leave it to chance twice," Jude said. "One copy is a hope. Two copies is an intention."

So it was settled the way everything between them had ever been settled — not with an agreement but with a silence that neither of them broke, because breaking it would have meant admitting how much it mattered.

* * *

They worked by lamplight in the vestry, and the work itself became a kind of liturgy.

Jude read. That was decided without discussion too: Jude's voice knew these names as names, knew the woman behind *Marta Ostell, weaver, three children* — knew she sang off-key at funerals and paid her debts a week late but always paid them — and a name read aloud by someone who knows it sounds different than a name read by someone who doesn't. It comes out rounder. It comes out claimed.

Beatrix wrote. Their hand, once the most disciplined hand in the diplomatic service — the hand that had filled dispatches for a republic while it died, each letter formed to regulation, each page initialed and sealed — had loosened somehow over the winter. The letters still marched, but they marched like people now instead of soldiers. Jude noticed this and said nothing. Some things you notice about a person the way you notice weather: it simply is, and then one day you realize it changed while you weren't watching, and you cannot say when.

"Ostell," Jude read. "Marta. Weaver. Three children — Anselm, Petra, and the baby, the baby never got a first name on paper, she was born in the week the front came through, so write *the child called Sparrow*, that's what they call her."

Beatrix wrote *the child called Sparrow*. The nib caught on the word *called* and threw a small black star onto the page, and Beatrix left it there. Mistakes were evidence of a human hand. Let whoever found this someday know that human hands had made it, on purpose, at risk.

"Dorn," Jude read. "The baker. You know Dorn."

"I know his bread," Beatrix said. "I did not know I knew his debt." They had learned, in the months since the requisition, to read a ledger the way other people read faces. Every entry was a biography compressed to its essentials. *Dorn, Josias, baker. Oven repaired, lent against. Four sons, two taken already.* The two taken — that meant conscripted, which in the grammar of this war meant *gone*, and gone had stopped meaning dead only because no one had proof yet either way.

"He'll want to know," Jude said quietly. "When the lists are voided. He'll want to stand in the square and hear both his boys' names read out among the spared."

"He'll hear them," Beatrix said. "That's what the letter bought. That's what it was *for*." And there it was, the old flint striking in Beatrix's chest — the courier's faith, the belief that a document properly delivered could bend the world. Except the world *had* bent. The amnesty had been read aloud in the market square six days ago by a republican officer who no longer had a republic to serve, and the transport lists under the new flags had been voided with a bureaucratic shrug, and Dorn's sons were spared by a promise signed eleven years ago by men who had never intended to keep it and never knew that, in the end, they accidentally had.

Paper could be dangerous. Beatrix had learned that in the first chapter of all this, filing a neighbor's name in a routine ledger. Paper could also be merciful. That lesson had come later, and cost more.

*Halloway,* Jude read. *The boy who sweeps the office.*

"He can't be more than fourteen," Beatrix said.

"Fifteen. He lied on the census. I let him." Jude turned the page. "I've been thinking about that. About all the small things I did with the ledgers over the years. Ages adjusted. Debts misfiled. A widow listed under her dead husband's trade so she'd count as essential. I thought I was being a good clerk when I bent the record, and a bad clerk when I kept it straight, and I never could tell which days were which."

"And now?"

"Now I think the ledger was never the point. The point was always the people it pointed at. The ledger is just a finger. You can burn the finger and the hand still knows where it pointed." Jude looked up. "That's why we're doing this. Not because paper is holy. Because memory needs a place to stand while the people are still alive to remember."

* * *

Past midnight, the questions began — because they always did, and because between the two of them there was nowhere left to hide from them.

"If we make two copies," Beatrix said, laying down the pen and flexing their cramping hand, "we have to decide where the second one goes. And deciding where it goes means deciding who sees it. And deciding who sees it—" They stopped.

"—is deciding who owns it," Jude finished.

The lamp guttered. Outside, a patrol went past on the road, boots in slush, four of them, unhurried. The new garrison did not impose a curfew anymore; they didn't need to. Fear did the work for free.

"When Vess took the ledgers," Beatrix said, "it was ownership by seizure. When the partisans wanted the letter, it was ownership by force of arms. When the archive at the border took my testimony, it was ownership by procedure. Everyone who ever touched these names tried to own them." They turned the hymnal toward themselves, looked at the pages of their own writing. "I gave my name away at that border. Signed myself into history as *a witness, number forty-one*. And I thought I'd feel erased. Instead I felt — unburdened. Do you understand? For the first time in my life, no institution owned me, because I had given the last thing they could take."

"But you're sitting here," Jude said. "You came back."

"I came back because there was nothing left to be loyal *to*. The service raised me, Jude. Orphan of the last war, ward of the chancery, taught to love paper because paper never dies the way parents die. Loyalty wasn't a choice they gave me. It was issued, like a uniform. And I spent my whole life believing that wearing it made me good." Beatrix's voice stayed level; it always stayed level; but Jude had learned to hear the seams. "Then I sat in this town and read a ledger and found my baker in it, and my landlord, and a boy with a broom, and every loyalty I was ever issued fell off me like wet cloth. What was left underneath — I didn't know what to call it. Until I watched you walk into a coffin-laden cart at dusk to save names that weren't yours."

"That was grief," Jude said. "And guilt. My mother ran this marsh and I filed papers about it. That's not loyalty, that's arrears."

"It's the same thing," Beatrix said. "It's the same exact thing. Loyalty isn't what they issue you. It's what you owe and choose to pay. I learned that word's real meaning here, in a town I once wrote down as *provincial, low strategic value* in a dispatch I'm now glad is ash."

They sat with that. The lamp hissed. Somewhere a nightbird made its wrong-season call over the frozen gardens.

"So who sees it?" Jude asked finally. "You've argued us into a corner and out the other side. Say it plainly."

Beatrix picked the pen back up. Dipped it. Watched the black gather at the nib like a held breath.

"No one whole," they said. "Not the archive — archives get burned, I've seen it done, I nearly did it myself. Not the partisans — they bargain with whatever they hold. Not the new government, not the old government, not any government, because governments keep records the way armies keep prisoners: until they're no longer useful." They set the nib to fresh paper. "We decide. Together. Page by page if we have to. We give pieces to people who will hold them *as* pieces — the priest keeps the deaths, the midwife keeps the births, the schoolmaster keeps the children — so that no one hand, including ours, ever holds the whole again. A ledger scattered is a ledger that can't be seized. A truth divided among friends is a truth that survives its friends."

Jude was quiet a long moment. Then: "My mother used to say something like that about the smuggling routes. Never carry the whole cargo on one mule."

"Your mother," Beatrix said, "would have run the diplomatic service better than anyone in it."

"She'd have been appalled to hear it. She hated paperwork." But Jude smiled, and it was the smile of someone setting down something carried a long way.

* * *

Toward the fourth hour, the copying slowed, not from weariness but from weight.

There is a particular fatigue that comes from writing other people's survival. Beatrix felt it settling into the wrist and shoulder like cold water. Forty pages in the original; thirty-eight copied so far; and on every page, the same strange doubling — these words existed once as a weapon someone aimed at these people, and now they existed again as a shield, the very same strokes of ink reversed in meaning like a palimpsest of mercy written over condemnation.

"Holday, Tomas, ferryman," Jude read, and their voice caught, just slightly. "He drowned pulling the last family across during the evacuation. His name shouldn't be on a conscription list. It should be on—" They stopped.

"Put him down anyway," Beatrix said. "With the others. And put next to his name what he did." They wrote: *Holday, Tomas. Ferryman. Gave the river more than he took from it.* It wasn't a legal notation. It wasn't anything the border archive would recognize. It was the first line Beatrix had ever entered in any record that was written for love rather than authority, and their hand did not shake, and that surprised them.

Page by page. Name by name. Once, near the end, Jude reached over without looking and steadied the candle with two fingers as a draft came under the door, and Beatrix kept writing through the movement as though the hand and the steadying had been one motion planned long in advance — and maybe, in some register deeper than planning, they had been. Two winters ago they had been a courier and a clerk exchanging suspicious glances across a customs-house counter. Now their breathing had synchronized over a table of names, and neither of them remarked on it, because things that matter are rarely remarked upon while they are happening.

At last: the final leaf. *Thornbury, B.,* Jude read, half-laughing. "Courier of dispatches. Resident of the customs house attic. No known debts."

"One known sin," Beatrix said. "Wrote this town off in a report. Write that down."

"No." Jude's voice went firm. "Write: *came back.*"

Beatrix looked up. The lamplight moved on Jude's face and made the moment older than it was, or newer.

"Write *came back*," Jude repeated. "Because that's the whole story, Beatrix. Everything else is detail."

Beatrix wrote it. *Came back.* The ink sank in and was fixed forever, iron and oak-gall and intention.

* * *

They finished as the sky behind the east windows turned the color of dishwater going pale, and the question of the second copy's hiding resolved itself the way the best answers do — by having been obvious all along.

"Not the wall again," Jude said, wrapping the new copy in the waxed canvas. "Vess is gone but walls get searched by every army that follows. Not the church — churches are the second place everyone looks. Not anywhere of mine or yours, because we are, respectively, the smuggler's daughter and the witness who testified at the border, and both of those are names that draw eyes."

"Then where?"

"With the people who have no names worth stealing," Beatrix said slowly, working it out as they spoke, "and no reason to be suspected and no politics anyone believes they hold. With the ones history walks past. Old Marta Ostell, who sings off-key. She buried a husband and two conscriptions; nobody watches a grieving weaver. Give her the births and the living, in pieces, memorized — she has the memory for it, she keeps forty years of customers' patterns in her head. The priest holds the deaths. The schoolmaster—"

"—holds the children," Jude finished. "And we hold nothing but the knowledge of who holds what. The map lives in two heads. Yours and mine."

"Yours and mine," Beatrix agreed, and heard how the possessive had quietly rearranged itself, and saw by the flicker in Jude's face that they had heard it too, and neither of them corrected it.

Jude blew out the lamp. Gray daylight came through the vestry windows, honest and cold, lighting the two copies side by side on the table — identical twins, born eight months apart, one conceived in terror and one in something that had no single name but had components of love, of grief, of stubbornness, of two people answering at last the question that loyalty had asked them all winter: *who do you serve?*

Not a republic. Not a flag. Not a seal or a file or an archive. Each other. This town. The names.

"There's still one thing missing," Jude said, gathering up the pens.

"What?"

"A name. The original has no title. Just names inside. If this is ever found — in fifty years, in a hundred — whoever finds it deserves to know what they're holding." Jude took up the pen one last time, opened the old hymnal to the flyleaf, hesitated, then handed the pen across the table. "You do it. Your hand is the fairer one."

Beatrix took the pen. Thought. The diplomatic service had taught them a hundred formats for documents — protocols, conventions, instruments of ratification, all the grand machinery of state — and none of them fit. What fit was smaller. What fit was true.

They wrote:

*This book contains the names of the people of Aldenmoor, taken from lists made to move them and copied out by two who refused. Read them aloud. That is the whole instruction. Reading them aloud is how they are kept.*

Below it, after a pause, two signatures: *B. Thornbury, sometime courier* and *J. —, town clerk, daughter of a smuggler.* Surnames omitted deliberately, the first surrender of identity freely chosen rather than imposed, and both of them smiling faintly at the symmetry of that without quite saying so.

Jude wrapped the second copy, slid it into the canvas, and tucked it under their arm. Through the window, the sun was coming up over the marsh, turning the frost to fire for a few minutes before it melted into ordinary mud, and down in the town the well was drawing its morning water, and Marta Ostell's chimney was smoking, and the child called Sparrow — saved by a promise nobody meant to keep and two people nobody had counted — was surely awake somewhere, wanting breakfast.

"One more thing," Beatrix said, reaching for their coat.

Jude looked over.

"Spring is close. The ice on the marsh is singing at night — I heard it walking home. And I was thinking—" Beatrix paused, and for once in a life trained in precision, chose imprecision on purpose. "I was thinking the customs house has a very large blank wall behind the clerk's desk. And a frame would fit nicely there. And there's a letter we should hang where everyone who files a tariff has to look at it every day for the rest of their lives."

Jude slung the wrap over their shoulder and opened the vestry door onto the cold bright morning, and the smell of thaw, and the sound of the town waking.

"Then we'd better start," Jude said, "before someone else decides what that wall is for."
