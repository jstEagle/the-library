## 1. The Weight of Lead

There is a particular hour in the Sterling workshop — just before dawn, when the stove has burned down to a red murmur and the frost on the windows has begun to weep — when the type speaks.

Not words, exactly. Jude Marsh had learned long ago not to expect words from the cases that lined the eastern wall, three hundred drawers of beech and brass fittings, each compartment holding its little civilization of letters: the a's crowded together like villagers at a market, the z's rattling lonely in their narrow cell, the e's so numerous they required two boxes and still overflowed. What the type said was older than words. It said *I am here, I am heavy, I endure.* Lead does not rust the way iron does; lead simply waits, patient as a stone in a riverbed, for the hand that will lift it out of darkness and set it against all the other waiting letters, and make of it something that outlasts the hand itself.

Jude had been that hand for six years, and he loved the hour before dawn more than any other, though he would have been embarrassed to say so aloud. He loved the smell most — the workshop smelled of ink and oil and lamp-black and the faint animal sweetness of the hide glue they used for the press rollers, and beneath it all the cold mineral breath of lead itself, which most people could not smell at all but which Jude could taste on the back of his tongue the moment he came through the door, like walking into a cellar full of rain. In winter the smell thickened. The cold kept it honest.

He sat at his stone now with the candle guttering beside him and the composing stick balanced in his left hand, setting the funeral notice for old Mother Ansel, who had died on Tuesday of her heart and been mourned on Wednesday by everyone whose debts she had forgiven. *Died peacefully in her sleep, surrounded by family.* Two lies in nine words, by Jude's count — she had died alone at her loom, and her only family was a nephew in Vellay who owed her money — but lies were part of the trade, the way chaff was part of wheat. A compositor set what he was given. That was the whole of the covenant. You did not write; you arranged. You were the servant of other people's certainty, and there was a comfort in that which Jude could not have explained to anyone without sounding like a fool or a coward, and possibly both.

The letters went into the stick one by one, nick toward the thumb, feet down, snug but never forced. Ambrose Sterling had beaten the lesson into him during his first month, when Jude was fifteen and all thumbs: *You do not push type, boy. You persuade it. Lead remembers rough handling, and it takes its revenge on the page.* Ambrose had a way of speaking about type as if it were livestock — skittish, noble, prone to injury — and about paper as if it were weather, changeable and unforgiving. *Kern that bracket or I'll know it in my sleep,* he would say, and he would too. The man could feel a misprint through three sheets of packing felt. He claimed, and no one had ever proved otherwise, that he could tell which of his apprentices had set a page by the pressure of the impression alone, the way a shepherd knows his sheep by their tread on a hillside.

Through the floor above, the boards creaked in the rhythm of someone moving carefully. That would be Elodie, up early to bank the fire in the living quarters and put water on for the barley coffee that none of them liked and all of them drank. She moved through the house the way she moved through everything else — quietly, precisely, like a needle through cloth — and Jude had lived under her footsteps long enough that he could read them the way sailors read water. Quick and light: she was well. Slow, with pauses: she was thinking. Silent for an hour past waking: something was wrong, and she would say nothing about it until it had resolved itself, because Elodie Sterling treated her own troubles the way her father treated type — as private material, not to be shown until it was properly set.

The stair door opened and she came down into the workshop with the lamp held high, and the light swung shadows across the hanging reams of paper so that for a moment they looked like sails, like the whole room might weigh anchor and drift upriver.

"You've lit the stone already," she said. It was not quite an accusation. With Elodie, observations often did the work of complaints.

"The notice won't set itself."

"Nothing sets itself." She set the lamp on the ledge above the imposing stone, and the candlelight found the planes of her face — the sharp chin, the brow that was always slightly furrowed over the left eye, as if permanently squinting at fine print. She was twenty-two, a year older than Jude, and she wore her dark hair pinned up with two steel quills that she had made herself from broken composing rules, and if you asked Jude — and no one had, mercifully — he would have said she looked like exactly what she was, which was the daughter of the best type-cutter in the valley, raised among matrices and moulds, fluent in a language most men never learned existed. "Father wants the Ansel notice pulled before eight, and then we start on the tariff schedules for the wool guild. Forty pages."

"Forty," Jude repeated, and let the number sit in the air between them with all its grey weight. Tariff schedules were the dullest work the workshop took, and the wool guild paid promptly, which meant neither of them could pretend to resent it.

"I'll galleys, you'll imposition," Elodie said, taking down her apron from its hook — canvas, ink-burned at the hip where she leaned against the stone — and tying it with a single practiced motion behind her back. "Unless your hands are frozen. They look frozen."

"They're warm as bread."

"Liar. Come here." She took his right hand without asking, the way a sister does, and pressed it briefly between both of hers, and her fingers were colder than his anyway, and she made a small dismissive sound and released him, and the whole transaction took four seconds and left Jude oddly unbalanced for the next ten minutes, which was a thing he had decided years ago never to examine too closely.

* * *

Ambrose Sterling descended at half past six, and with his descent the workshop changed character, the way a sanctuary changes when the priest enters. He was sixty-one years old and built like the doorframe of an old church — narrow, upright, unexpectedly sound — and he wore a leather apron gone stiff with age and a pair of spectacles he pushed up onto his bald head when he wanted to look at the world instead of the work. His beard was white and trimmed with the exactness he brought to everything, each side measured against the other, and his eyes were pale grey, nearly colorless, the eyes of a man who spent his life looking at very small things very closely.

"Read me your stick," he said, before he had even finished descending, because Ambrose believed greetings were a formality the day could do without.

Jude read the funeral notice line by line, holding the composing stick up, and Ambrose stood in the middle of the floor listening with his eyes shut like a man at a concert, and at the fourth line he raised one finger.

"'Beloved by all who knew her.'"

"Yes, sir."

"Is that true?"

"It's what the nephew wrote."

"The nephew owes her money. Set 'long mourned by the parish.' True enough, and kinder, and it fills the measure better." He opened his eyes. "And your spacing on 'surrounded' — don't argue, I can see it from here — is loose as a cart-rut. Fix it before you lock up." And he passed on into the back room where the casting bench lived, and began his morning ritual of touching each of his tools in turn, the graver and the file and the punch and the drive, the way other men say their prayers.

That was the whole of Ambrose's religion, as far as Jude could tell: precision. He cut punches for typefounders in three cities, and his faces were known by name in places he had never been and would never go — a spare, upright text face called Sterling Primer, and a display roman so clean that a printer in the capital had once written to ask whether the letters had been engraved rather than cast, and Ambrose kept the letter folded in the drawer of his private desk like a love letter. He was, by the common agreement of everyone within fifty miles, a master. And he was, by Jude's private observation, a man slowly being worn down by the century, the way a staircase is worn down by feet — not ruined, merely diminished, each year a fraction lower in the shoulder, each invoice from the paper merchant in Vellay a fraction longer in coming to be answered.

The regime's currency came through the workshop twice a year, banknotes printed in Vellay and sent upriver for the valley's use, and last spring Ambrose had taken the commission to reprint the smaller denominations locally, since the capital's presses could no longer keep schedule. Everyone understood why the capital's presses could not keep schedule. Nobody said it. The money Ambrose printed bore the treasury seal and the portrait of a minister nobody had ever seen, and it bought less every month, and the townsfolk had begun to joke bitterly that Sterling notes were honest at least in this: they depreciated at exactly the rate of the government's promises. Ambrose laughed at such jokes in public and said nothing in private, and Jude had learned to read his silences the way he read Elodie's footsteps, though Ambrose's silences were harder, denser things, like locked type cases.

* * *

By noon the frost had melted off the windows entirely, and the light came through in that flat white February way that makes even handsome river towns look like engravings of themselves. Vergey lay below the workshop hill in the fold of the valley where the river made its slow elbow bend — four hundred roofs, two mills, one bridge, and the garrison barracks squatting at the bridgehead like a warden's chair set down in the wrong century. From the workshop window you could see the whole arrangement of the place: the town, the river, the bridge, and beyond the bridge the road climbing out of the valley toward the pass, which was closed with snow five months of the year and open for the other seven, and which connected Vergey to everything that mattered — the market at Vellay, the rail terminus at Corveil, and beyond them the capital, where history was rumored to occur.

Jude had been staring at that road for six years. He had come to Vergey at fourteen, an orphan out of the charity school at Saint-Aubin with good eyes and steady hands and no prospects, and Ambrose had taken him on because his hands were steady and because — though Jude learned this only later — Ambrose had once owed a debt to Jude's father, a printer of pamphlets in Corveil who had died of the fever with his debts unpaid and his press sold. So the trade ran in Jude's blood after all, though he tried not to think of it as inheritance. Inheritance was a word for people who owned things.

He meant to leave. He had meant to leave since his second year, when a journeyman printer passing through had shown the apprentices a newspaper from the capital, four pages of close-set type on paper so thin it seemed woven from light rather than pulp, and Jude had read every word of it twice, including the advertisements, including the shipping notices, and lain awake that night understanding for the first time that somewhere, typesetters were setting words that thousands of strangers would read before breakfast — words that argued, accused, celebrated, decided. Here he set funeral notices and tariff schedules and the occasional playbill for the amateur theatricals, and the highest purpose his work had ever served was informing the valley that the wool prices had shifted by two sous. He was a tradesman. He said it to himself the way other men said their prayers, as a protection: *I am a tradesman. I keep my hands clean of ink and my opinions out of the stick.* History happened elsewhere, to larger men, and arrived in Vergey only as news, already set, already cold, like the ice that came downriver in March.

And yet he was content. That was the treacherous part, the part he kept in the same mental drawer as the letter from the capital printer — folded away, occasionally taken out and looked at. He was content in this room with its three hundred drawers and its smell of lead and lamp-black, content with Elodie's cold fingers and Ambrose's impossible standards, content with the barley coffee and the forty pages of tariff schedules stretching ahead like a long grey road. Contentment, he suspected, was just restlessness that hadn't yet found the door.

"Marsh." Ambrose's voice from the casting bench. "Come hold this."

Jude went. Ambrose was working on a new punch — a lowercase g with a double-story bowl he had been cutting for three weeks, for a client in Corveil who wanted a book face with, in his words, "authority without arrogance," a specification Ambrose considered oxymoronic and professionally insulting, and therefore irresistible. The punch was steel, the size of a thumbnail, clamped in the holder, and under the loupe its surface showed the letter in reverse relief, bright as frost.

"Hold the lamp. Steady." Ambrose bent to the graver, and the small tool sang against the steel with a sound too high for description, and curls of metal no bigger than eyelashes lifted away. Jude held the light and watched the master work, and thought, as he always thought at these moments, that he was watching a man make history in miniature — that every letter Ambrose cut would carry his judgment forward for fifty years, would print books he'd never read for readers he'd never meet. Ambrose himself would have laughed at the notion. Ambrose would have said he was making tools, not history. But Jude had noticed something his master perhaps had not: that the men who actually made history never used the word. They only cut, and set, and pulled the bar, and let the impressions fall where they fell.

Elodie passed behind them with an armload of galley proofs, paused, and looked over her father's shoulder at the new g — a liberty she took that no apprentice would dare — and said, "The ear's too long."

"It is not too long."

"It's a fishing hook, Papa. Fish will sue us."

Ambrose made a noise like a millstone adjusting itself, which was his laugh, and returned to the steel. Elodie went on with her proofs. Jude held the lamp steady, and the light trembled very slightly, and he told himself it was the draft from the window.

* * *

Evening came early, as it did in February, and with it the sound that had become the town's winter clockwork: the bugle from the barracks at the bridgehead, calling the garrison's supper. Six o'clock, the note hanging flat in the frozen air. Below in the street, lamplighters moved along the rue Basse, and a cart ground past loaded with faggots, and somewhere a dog was barking at the ice on the river.

They ate upstairs — barley coffee, black bread, a cheese Elodie had carried back from the Tuesday market aged in walnut leaves — and Ambrose read aloud from the *Vellay Courier*, three weeks stale, which was the freshest news the valley ever got in winter. Shipping tonnage, down. The ministry's new loan, oversubscribed, or so the ministry claimed. A harvest festival in the south. And on the third page, in a column of small type that the compositor in Vellay had set with visible reluctance, a paragraph about disturbances in the capital — crowds, the word *reform* appearing and disappearing like a fish surfacing, the Chamber prorogued, the guards increased.

"Students and lawyers," Ambrose said, folding the paper. "It's always students and lawyers. By thaw they'll have discovered something else to be passionate about."

"The price of bread isn't a passion," Elodie said, not looking up from her mending. "It's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic doesn't build barricades."

"No," she said. "Hunger does."

There was a silence of the particular quality that indicated, in this household, that a subject had been set and locked and would not be distributed further. Ambrose refilled his cup. Jude looked at the fire and thought about the road over the pass, closed now under twelve feet of snow, and about the letter he had drafted eleven times and sent zero times — to a printing house in the capital, his father's old firm, still operating under another man's name, inquiring whether a compositor of six years' experience might find work in the spring. Eleven drafts. Each one truer than the last and none of them sent, because sending it would mean the door was open, and as long as the door stayed shut he could go on loving this room without having to admit he was leaving it.

After supper Ambrose went down to the workshop, as he always did, to check the type was distributed and the stones wiped and the presses cloaked, his evening devotion. Elodie sat by the lamp with her mending, and Jude took up a book, and for an hour the only sounds were the fire and the turning pages and the occasional snap of thread.

Then Elodie said, without preamble, "Do you ever think about what's in the news, Jude? Actually in it. Not the words. The — underneath."

He looked at her. The lamplight made her face half gold and half shadow, and her needle had stopped moving.

"There's usually not much underneath a shipping notice."

"No," she agreed softly. "Usually not." And the needle started again, quick and precise, and she said nothing more, and Jude returned to his book and read the same paragraph four times without retaining a word of it, because some part of him had registered the pause before *usually* — the hairline hesitation, fine as a crack in a punch face, invisible unless you spent your life reading impressions — and some quiet machinery in him had begun, without permission, to turn it over.

Downstairs, faintly, he heard the workshop door open and close. Ambrose, returning from some errand he had not mentioned at supper. And beneath the ordinary sound of it, the river muttered under its ice, patient, carrying whatever the winter would bring down from the capital toward the bridge.
