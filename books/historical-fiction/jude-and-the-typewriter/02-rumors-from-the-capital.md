## 2. Rumors from the Capital

The news came the way all news came to Vellmouth: late, damp, and secondhand.

It arrived on a Tuesday in the mouth of Herr Fossen's mail satchel, folded inside a three-week-old copy of the *Gazette of the Capital* that had been wrapped around a bolt of blue cloth ordered by the draper's wife. Herr Fossen, the post rider, was a stooped man with a cough like a saw leaving wood, and he deposited the bundle on our counter with the indifference of a man delivering stones.

"Something's burning in the capital, they say," he remarked to no one in particular, unwinding his scarf. "Barricades in the streets. The king's run off or hasn't — depends who tells it."

Ambrose Sterling did not look up from his composing stone. His hands went on choosing type — an *n*, an *e*, a *b* — each letter lifted with the tweezers and set into the stick with a click as soft as a knuckle cracking. "Kings have been running off since kings were invented," he said. "The type doesn't care."

But I confess I cared. I set down my brush and the pot of lye I'd been using to clean the rollers, and I took up that newspaper as though it were a window someone had left open onto another world.

* * *

You must understand what a newspaper meant in Vellmouth that winter. It meant the capital — Harenz, four days' hard travel east when the roads were good, which they never were. It meant gas lamps and parliament and men whose names appeared in print so often that the letters of their names must have worn smooth in every typesetter's case in the country. It meant everything that Vellmouth was not: large, loud, lit, and *happening*.

Vellmouth happened slowly, if at all. We had the river, wide and brown and patient; we had the bridge, a single span of iron and timber that carried the road out of the valley like a held breath; we had the mill, the church, the market square with its statue of some duke nobody could name, and we had us — nine hundred souls, give or take the drowned. And we had the workshop, which sat on Tanner's Lane with its south-facing windows and its smell that I loved more than any smell in Christendom: oil and lead and lampblack and the sour-sweet ghost of paste, all of it warmed by the stove until the air itself seemed inked.

I had been apprenticed to Ambrose Sterling for six years, since I was eleven, and in six years I had learned the weight of every letter in both cases. Roman and italic. Upper where the capitals lived, lower where the rest crowded together in their wooden compartments like townsfolk at a hanging. I could find a *k* blindfolded. I knew which sorts were worn — the *e*s always first, poor things, worked half to death in any language — and I knew Ambrose's rule, spoken so many times it had worn smooth too: *A compositor sets what is given him, Jude. He is not the author. He is the hand, not the voice.*

I had built my whole self around that sentence. I meant to be the hand. Hands didn't go down in history; hands didn't need to. History, I had decided somewhere in my thirteenth year, was a thing that happened to other people, in other places, and got printed afterward for tradesmen like me to set in columns. Kings ran off, wars started and stopped, parliaments rose and fell, and all of it arrived in Vellmouth already finished, already cold, needing nothing from me but clean margins and even spacing. There was a comfort in that arrangement, the way there is comfort in a wall. You don't admire a wall. You lean against it.

So I stood there leaning against mine, reading Herr Fossen's stale newspaper while the kettle muttered on the stove, and the words were three weeks old and still they had heat in them:

*Crowds assembled in the Place Royale despite the prohibition of assemblies. The guard called out. Reports differ as to who fired first. Barricades raised in the rue St.-Aubin and the quarter of the tanners. The ministry has resigned. It is said — though nothing is confirmed — that His Majesty has left the city.*

"Run off or hasn't," Ambrose said again, without looking up, as if I'd asked. "Fossen repeats himself like a bad press. Give it here when you're done. The margin rules on that sheet are decent; Elodie can cut them for patterns."

* * *

Elodie was at the far press, the smaller one, the proof press her grandfather had bought when the workshop was young, and she was working the leather roller back and forth over a jobbing form — invoices for the flour merchant, two hundred of them, the dullest work imaginable — but when the word *barricades* crossed the room I saw her roller stop. Just stop, mid-stroke, for the length of one breath, and then continue.

She did not look up. She was very good at not looking up. But I had spent six years learning to read that workshop the way I read type, and Elodie Sterling stopping her roller mid-stroke was a sentence printed in letters a foot high.

She was twenty-one that winter, a year older than me, and she had grown up inside these walls the way ivy grows up a chimney — so thoroughly part of the structure that you forgot she hadn't been built here, that she'd simply taken root. Her father taught her the cutting, the engraving of punches, the hardest art in the trade, the one he said separated a printer from a mere pressman. She could cut a letter into steel with a graver no bigger than a darning needle, and she had steady hands and a still face and a way of listening that I had never thought much about, because Elodie listening was as ordinary as the river running.

She was listening now. That was the difference. Everyone else in the room — Ambrose, me, old Petya the pressman who was mostly knees and opinions — everyone else was treating the newspaper like weather from another country. Elodie was treating it like a summons.

"Fossen." Ambrose beckoned the post rider toward the stove. "Sit. Warm your cough. What are they saying at the relay station? Riders hear more than newspapers print."

Herr Fossen lowered himself onto the bench by the fire, grateful as a dog, and unpacked everything he'd gathered on the road like a peddler laying out ribbons. "That the students burned the customs houses. That the guard wouldn't fire, or fired and then wept about it, depending. That the price of bread—" he coughed, a long tearing cough — "that bread's the whole of it, Mr. Sterling. Bread and taxes and the winter. A man can stand being governed. It's the hunger he can't stand."

"A republic, then?" Petya put in from his corner, hopeful as ever. Petya believed in republics the way other men believed in saints.

"A republic, a new king, the old king back with German soldiers behind him — how would I know?" Fossen shrugged. "It's four hundred miles away. Whatever it is, it'll be settled before the bridge opens."

There it was, you see. Settled before the bridge opens. That was the whole theology of Vellmouth in one sentence. The valley was a pocket, the river was a moat, the bridge was the only gate, and history — noisy, bloody, magnificent history — was a traveler who might pass over our bridge someday but would certainly never stop. We were not on the way to anywhere. Even our wars had always been fought by other men in other valleys. I had heard it said my whole life: *nothing ever happens in Vellmouth.* People said it the way you'd praise a child for being quiet.

I looked at the newspaper again. *Reports differ as to who fired first.* Somewhere in the capital, men were stacking furniture in the streets and dying behind it, and here I stood holding the account of it, and my only portion of the whole affair was that someday — next month, next season — someone might bring us a commemorative broadside to print, and I would set it nicely, and justify the last line of every column, and that would be my entire inheritance from the fall of a king.

I found, to my surprise, that something in me objected to this. Quietly. The way a floorboard objects underfoot. Not enough to wake anyone. Just enough that you know it's there.

* * *

Ambrose dismissed us early that day, on account of the ink batch having gone wrong — too much varnish, gummy as pitch — and I stayed only to wash the stones. When I came out into the yard with my collar wet and my fingers gray, Elodie was standing at the gate, looking east.

Not south, where the market was. Not west, where the sun was going down in its usual respectable manner. East, down Tanner's Lane, past the roofs, to where the road ran invisible between the hills toward the bridge, and beyond the bridge toward the capital, where barricades were standing in streets she'd never seen.

"You believe him, then," I said. "Fossen. About the king."

"I believe that he believes whatever the last person told him." She didn't turn. Her breath made small clouds that the wind took apart. "But three different drivers told him three different things, and all three agreed the crowds aren't dispersing. When the details disagree and the shape agrees, you attend to the shape."

"That sounds like something your father says."

"It sounds like something Papa used to say, before he decided the world ended at the tollhouse." Now she turned, and the lamplight from the workshop window caught her face sideways, and I saw something in it I couldn't name yet — a tightness, an attention, like a hand hovering over a case of type, choosing. "Jude. Do you ever think about what it means? Bread prices going up four hundred miles away?"

I laughed, because the honest answer embarrassed me. "It means the flour merchant's invoices will get harder to collect."

"Yes," she said. "That's exactly what it means." And she said it in such a flat, careful voice that my laugh died in the air between us like a moth against glass.

* * *

That night I lay in my loft above the stables and listened to the wind come down the valley, and I thought about the shape of things instead of the details, the way she'd said, and it was a long time before I slept.

Here is what I did not know, that night, and could not have known: that the shape Elodie was attending to had already reached inside our own workshop, quietly, months before, the way water finds the crack in a wall before anyone sees the stain. I did not know what she cut after midnight, or why the floorboards near the old bindery sounded different when she walked over them, or why certain deliveries came to the side door after dark. I was a compositor. I set what was given me. I noticed everything about letters and nothing about people, which I mistook, in my youth, for professionalism.

But I remember this, because memory is the one thing I've learned to set in permanent type: I woke once, near the coldest hour, and heard the workshop door below my window open and shut — softly, softly, the way you'd close a door you didn't want to wake a sleeping house — and I heard footsteps cross the yard and pause, and I looked out through the frost-furred glass, and I saw Elodie standing in the middle of the yard with her shawl over her head, facing east again. Facing the capital. Standing there in the falling snow like a woman waiting for a letter, or a war.

And then she went back inside, and the door closed, and I told myself I had dreamed it, because that was the kind of man I was in those days. I believed in walls.

* * *

Three mornings later the town had made up its mind about the capital, the way towns do, and its mind was: *nothing to do with us.*

I heard it at the baker's, where I'd gone for the morning loaves. Old Marta Kessel was saying that kings came and went but the mills kept grinding, and her son-in-law was saying the whole business would blow over by planting season, and the miller's clerk — a fat, comfortable man named Dubs who had never in his life had an unscheduled thought — declared roundly that "they'll all be shooting each other over there and it won't move the price of flour here one kreuzer," and everyone nodded, because it was a comforting arithmetic. Distance equals safety. Four hundred miles of it, plus a river, plus a bridge that closed every winter like a shop pulling down its shutter.

Only the baker himself, Grell, a silent man with forearms like ham hocks, said otherwise, and he said it quietly while he handed me the loaves. "Flour's already moved," he said. "Up two pfennig since the new year, and the factor says the capital granaries are thin. Distance doesn't feed anyone, boy. It only delays the bill."

I carried the loaves home warm against my chest and thought about bills and distances and the particular expression on Elodie's face when she'd said *you attend to the shape.*

At the workshop, Ambrose had a commission waiting: five hundred playbills for the Winter Assembly at the town hall — dancing figures, ornamental border, the sort of cheerful nonsense work that paid for lamp oil. I set to it gladly. There is a real peace in ornament, in borders that mean nothing, in type arranged for beauty rather than truth. I chose a fourteen-point roman with generous serifs and lost myself in it for hours, and by noon the trouble in the capital had shrunk to exactly the size of my composing stick, which is to say: manageable, orderly, mine.

Then Elodie came in from an errand with color high in her cheeks and snow melting on her shoulders, and she set a folded paper on the edge of Ambrose's stone without a word, and went to hang her cloak.

It was a handbill. Printed — not written, *printed*, on rough paper on some press I couldn't imagine, in a town where we owned the only two machines with beds and rollers. The type was clumsy, badly spaced, the work of someone passionate and untrained. But I read it, standing there with my tweezers still in my hand, and the words went through me like a splinter finding flesh:

*BREAD OR BULLETS. They have answered with bullets in the capital. Will your town wait to be asked?*

Below it, no signature. Below that, a date. Not three weeks old. Not a week old. *Four days.*

Ambrose read it once. His face did what Ambrose's face did, which was nothing, which was a stone wall with a door hidden somewhere in it. He picked up the tallow candle beside his stone and held the bill close to the flame, and I thought — absurdly — that he meant to burn it, and something in my stomach dropped.

He didn't burn it. He studied it. Turned it over. Held it nearly touching the flame so the light shone *through* the paper, and I watched his eyes move across the back of the page, reading the impression that had come through from the other side, the way a bank clerk reads a watermark.

"Cast type," he murmured, almost to himself. "Hand-inked. Platen press — small bed, see the bite marks at the corners. Someone's press, Jude. In someone's barn, someone's cellar, someone's *something*. Within forty miles." He set the bill down on the stone among his neat rows of dancing-playbill type, and it lay there looking ragged and alive, like a sparrow that had flown into a cathedral.

"Should we—?" I began.

"We should set the Assembly playbill," Ambrose said, in the voice that closed subjects like doors. "Borders don't rebel, and neither do we. Mind your spacing on the ornaments; the last ones came out gappy."

I minded my spacing. Of course I minded my spacing. But all afternoon the handbill sat at the edge of my eye like a lit window in a dark street, and all afternoon I set my harmless beautiful letters, and I understood — dimly, the way you understand a change in the weather before the first drop falls — that the distance I had always trusted, the four hundred miles and the river and the shuttered bridge, had just been measured out for me in a single line of bad typography.

*Within forty miles.*

And across the workshop, at her cutting table, Elodie had not even glanced at the paper. She bent over her steel and her graver, cutting letters no bigger than grains of rice into the metal, and her hands were perfectly steady, and she did not look at the handbill even once — which, I would understand later, was itself the loudest thing in the room.

That evening, as I banked the stove, I heard her ask her father, lightly, too lightly, whether he thought the thaw would come early this year.

"Soon enough," Ambrose said, locking the type cases, each lock clicking like a period at the end of a sentence. "Soon enough. And when the bridge opens, the world will come pouring over it, and we'll set whatever it brings, and take their money, and none of it will be our affair. Go to bed, the pair of you. Ink waits for no man, and neither does morning."

I climbed to my loft. Elodie lingered in the yard below, and through the window I watched her look east one more time before she went in.

Two weeks later, the floods came, and the soldiers came with them, and the bridge closed — and none of us would be spectators ever again.
