## 3. The Bridge Closes

The river told them first.

Jude heard it before dawn on the last morning of November — a sound like a great animal turning over in its sleep, a low grinding groan that came up through the foundations of the town and set the type cases in the workshop rattling on their shelves. He lay in his cot above the pressroom and listened to the Verrel change its voice. For weeks it had been speaking in the cold's thin syllables, ice clicking against the pilings, but now the ice was breaking up early, shoved downstream by water that had no business moving so hard so soon. The autumn rains had come to the high valleys and stayed. The river was rising.

By the time he dressed and came down the stairs with their familiar complaint of wood and nail, the sky through the workshop's eastern window was the color of wet slate, and Ambrose Sterling was already at the door with his coat over his nightshirt, looking out at the street as though it had personally offended him.

"Flood's coming up Pasture Lane," he said without turning. "Get your boots. If the water takes the cellar, I'll want those sandbags moved before it does, not after."

The cellar held the paper stock — four months of it, rag paper bought at autumn prices from a mill two days downstream, and beneath the paper, in crates stenciled with the name of a wine merchant in the capital, something else entirely that Jude had never been asked to inventory. He hauled sandbags until his shoulders burned, mud sucking at his boots, while the townsfolk came out in the grey light to stand along the lane and watch the water advance across the fields like an occupying army. Old men who remembered the flood of '29 shook their heads and quoted figures. Children were sent to fetch things and came back having forgotten them. Somewhere a dog barked itself hoarse at the river, which did not care.

By noon the water stood knee-deep in the lower streets, and by dusk it had found the church steps, and the whole town had turned its face toward the bridge.

* * *

The Verrel Bridge was the only way out. Jude had known this the way he knew his own hands — as fact, as limit, as the shape of his life. The valley ran forty miles from the gorge at its head to the gorge at its foot, and the river ran down the middle of it like a spine, and every road, every track, every goat path eventually came to the same crossing: a stone bridge with three arches, built before anyone could remember, wide enough for two carts, tall enough for the spring floods, and for two hundred years equal to everything the valley and the weather could do to it. On the far side, the road climbed out of the gorge and went north, and after six days of going north it became the capital.

Six days. Jude had counted them so many times they had worn smooth, like coins handled too much. Six days by the mail coach if the roads held, and the coach left from the bridgehead every Thursday morning, and he had been saving for the fare since summer. He had the sum sewn into the lining of his good coat — eleven thalers and six groschen, counted nightly by touch through the cloth, each coin a small round promise. In December he would go to the capital. In December he would present himself, letters of introduction and a specimen book of Ambrose's finest cutting in his case, to Herr Fassbinder of the Fassbinder & Sons Printing House, who wanted a compositor who could read a hand-cut matrix and set display type without a single wrong sort. The letter offering him a trial position lay folded in the same lining as the money, read so often the creases had gone soft as cloth.

He had said nothing to Elodie yet. That was the cowardice he carried alongside the coins — that he rehearsed the telling of it in every spare hour and could not, in any actual hour, manage the words. She would be pleased for him. She would say all the correct things. And then the workshop would be one pair of hands short, and she would have to learn to lock a chase by herself, and Ambrose would grumble that apprentices these days used a man up and discarded him like worn type, and Jude would stand there with his ears burning and his coat heavy with escape.

Thursday morning he rose before light, put on the good coat over three shirts, and walked down through the waking town to the bridgehead with his breath smoking ahead of him.

The bridge was closed.

Not flooded — the river, for all its noise, still ran below the arches — but closed, and by soldiers. There were six of them at the near end, wrapped in grey greatcoats that might once have been white, a brazier between them spitting sparks into the wet air, and a barrier of crossed timbers set across the road with a notice nailed to it. A lieutenant stood apart from the men, holding his watch open in his gloved hand as though time itself required supervision.

Jude joined the little crowd that had gathered — the mail driver among them, whip in hand, arguing himself purple at a distance of ten feet because closer than ten feet seemed unwise.

"Orders from the garrison command," the lieutenant said, in the flat voice of a man saying something for the ninth time. "The bridge is closed to all civilian traffic until further notice. Flood conditions. Military necessity."

"It's a *bridge*," the mail driver said. "It's stood since before your grandfather was whelped."

"And the roads beyond it are under eight inches of water, and the military courier service requires priority of passage when conditions improve, and my orders are my orders." The lieutenant closed his watch. "Go home, all of you. It will open in spring."

A murmur went through the crowd — not anger, not yet, but the dull collective sound of people being told what they already feared. Spring. The word hung in the cold air like breath. Four months. Five, if the thaw came late, and in the mountains the thaw always came late.

Jude did not argue. Arguing required a belief that the world could be talked out of itself, and he had been raised by Ambrose Sterling's workshop to know that some things were simply locked in the chase, tightened past appeal. He stood a moment longer, looking past the soldiers at the span of wet stone running out into the fog, at the far bank where the road went north and vanished, and felt something in his chest contract with a click he would remember later, though he could not have named it then. Then he turned and walked home through the drowned streets, and the water of the risen river followed alongside him behind the house fronts, patient, gray, going nowhere fast.

* * *

"You'll get over it," Ambrose said that evening, not unkindly, not looking up from the galley he was correcting. His composing stick moved in his hands with the unconscious grace of long marriage; lead sorts came and went under his fingers like birds returning to a roost. "Floods go down. Orders change. Fassbinder isn't the only printer in the capital, and a man who can wait is worth more than a man who can arrive." He blew a fleck of ink from the galley. "Besides," and here came the edge of a smile, the rarest tool in the shop, kept oiled but seldom used, "if you'd left in December you'd have missed the winter catalog. Nobody sets italics like you when you're sulking."

"I'm not sulking."

"You've sorted the same case of nonpareil twice today. Either you're sulking or your eyes are failing, and your eyes are twenty-two years old."

Jude took the rebuke and the comfort together, as one took bread and salt, and said nothing more. But that night, lying awake under the eaves with the river muttering in the dark like a creditor at the door, he understood that Ambrose had missed the point entirely, or had chosen to miss it, which amounted to the same thing. It was not the job he mourned. It was the arithmetic of his life, which had suddenly refused to balance. He had spent twenty-two years believing that the world was arranged in distances — the workshop here, history elsewhere, the capital six days north — and that a man need only save his coins and keep his creases soft and one day walk out of the valley into the middle of everything. Now the bridge was shut and the distances had collapsed, all of them, and he discovered that the world he had thought was far away had been pressing close all along. The soldiers at the bridgehead were not passing through on their way to somewhere important. They were the somewhere important, arrived, billeted in the old granary, buying candles and eggs and resentment in equal measure. History had not failed to reach the valley. It had reached it and closed the door behind itself.

* * *

December came in on a wind that smelled of snow, and the town learned the new shape of its confinement the way a hand learns a tool: slowly, then all at once, then forever.

There were more soldiers now. A company had come down from the garrison at the head of the valley before the floods cut the high road, and they drilled in the market square on mornings when the rain allowed, their boots sending up spray, their sergeant's voice cracking over the rooftops like a whip. Their requisition notices appeared on the church door and the toll house and, Jude noted with a professional's eye, badly printed on a hand press someone had brought over the pass — crooked margins, an orphaned line, ink so thin it looked apologetic. Even the regime's lies were set carelessly here. He found this observation both satisfying and unsettling, the way one finds satisfaction in a rival's bad work and then wonders what the rivalry will cost.

Money grew strange. The banknotes Ambrose printed under contract — Jude set the ornamental borders, the guilloche work, the portrait of a minister whose face changed design every few months as governments rose and dissolved upstream in the capital — were worth less each week. The baker quoted prices in thalers in the morning and eggs by noon. A farmer paid his account in salt pork and everyone pretended this was normal. The regime, Jude understood, watching the notes come back to the workshop grimy and creased and passed hand to hand like gossip, was not merely far away and failing. Its failures were arriving in the valley as physical objects, printed by his own hands, spent at his own counter.

And the town talked. It talked the way flooded rivers run — finding every low place, filling every hollow. In the taproom of the Copper Kettle, where Jude allowed himself one beer on Saturday nights, the conversation had acquired a new undertone, a lowered-voice quality it had not possessed in October. Men spoke of cousins in the mining towns to the south who had stopped writing. Women spoke of prices. Someone's brother-in-law had seen troops moving artillery toward the pass before the roads closed, and someone else said no, that was rumor, and a third man said quietly that rumor was all that traveled freely anymore, so they ought to let it.

Elodie was at the Kettle that night too — a rarity, and one Jude noticed the way he noticed changes in the weather, without admitting he was tracking them. She sat with the cobbler's daughters near the fire and said almost nothing, but her stillness had a quality of attention that was nearly loud. When a traveling carter described the barricades he'd seen in a river town downstream — students, clerks, a printer among them, God help them, a *printer* — the room laughed nervously into its beer, and Jude happened to be looking at Elodie, and saw her not laugh. Saw her set down her cup with exaggerated care, the way her father set down a fragile forme, and look into the fire with an expression Jude could not read and did not try to, because trying felt like trespassing.

She caught him looking. For half a second their eyes met across the smoky room, and something passed between them that neither of them named — an acknowledgment, perhaps, that they had both heard the same sentence and filed it differently. Then she smiled her ordinary smile and asked the cobbler's daughter about a wedding, and the moment closed over like water over a dropped coin.

* * *

The workshop in winter was a world entire, and Jude tried to lose himself in it, and mostly succeeded, which was the trouble. There was always work: the winter catalog for the ironmonger, hymnals for the chapel, the endless currency plates with their careful frauds, a funeral sermon for old Widow Hahn that Ambrose cut a new memorial border for as a courtesy and then complained about for a week. Jude threw himself into the composition like a swimmer into cold water — better to be numb and moving than standing on the shore thinking. His hands learned the work so well they could do it while his mind wandered, and his mind wandered constantly now, up the drowned lanes, out along the closed road, over the hills it could no longer cross, to the capital where his letter sat in a drawer and his position, he told himself nightly, would surely wait until spring. Surely. Fassbinder was a reasonable man. Reasonable men waited.

But at odd moments the claustrophobia came upon him like a smell. It came on Tuesday when he climbed to the roof to mend a slate and saw, from that height, the exact geography of his imprisonment: the valley a long grey trough, the river a band of hammered pewter down its center, the bridge a small dark stitch connecting nothing to nowhere, soldiers on it like flies. It came on Friday when the mail sack arrived — carried now by a garrison rider who brought it only as far as the bridgehead and sent it over by boat — and contained three letters for the whole town and none for him. It came most sharply on Sunday, in chapel, when the minister read the banns and prayed for travelers and Jude realized that there were no travelers, that the prayer was addressed to an empty category, that he lived now in a place from which no one could leave and therefore, by some inversion he felt in his teeth, to which anything could happen.

After the service he stood in the churchyard in the falling snow and watched the river run below the bluff, dark and swollen and purposeful, carrying broken ice toward the gorge and the lowlands and, eventually, the sea — everything in the valley able to leave except the people.

"You're doing it again," Elodie said beside him. She had appeared the way she sometimes did, without announcement, her breath steaming, a wool scarf wound to her chin so that only her eyes showed. "Sorting the same thoughts twice."

"Ambrose has been talking."

"Ambrose has been talking since before either of us was born." Her eyes crinkled above the scarf. "You'll get your spring, Jude. The river can't stay angry forever. And meanwhile—" she nodded back toward the workshop, its windows glowing amber in the blue dusk, smoke standing straight up from the chimney in the windless cold, "—meanwhile there's the Harkness ledger to set, and Papa wants the currency borders recut by Epiphany, and I cannot lock a chase by myself to save my life, so you are not permitted to drown yourself in the Verrel out of sheer ambition. Is that understood?"

It was said lightly, and Jude answered it lightly, and they walked home through the snow with the lamps coming on one by one along the street, and he told himself the heaviness in him was only thwarted plans, which time would dissolve.

But that night, descending the stairs late for a forgotten errand, he paused on the landing where the stairwell window gave onto the yard, and saw a thread of lamplight lying under the door of the composing room where no lamp should be burning at that hour, and heard, very faintly, the small precise sounds of type being sorted — not his father-figure's heavier rhythm below, but quicker, lighter, working alone.

Elodie, midnight, sorting type.

He stood in the cold stairwell a long moment, telling himself it was only the catalog, only insomnia, only a girl who loved her letters the way he loved his. And then he heard the sound he knew better than his own heartbeat: the click of a composing stick taking a sort that was *not* from the ordinary cases — a different weight, a different ring, lead cut rather than cast — and the hair rose on his arms, and he went quietly back up to bed and lay staring at the rafters while the river ground its ice in the dark.

There was a second press running in the Sterling workshop. And Jude Marsh, stranded, watchful, unable to leave even his own curiosity behind, was going to find out whose.
