## 4. What Elodie Cuts After Midnight

The workshop kept two clocks, though only one of them was honest.

The first hung on the wall above Ambrose Sterling's desk, a brass-faced thing with a pendulum that ticked like a patient conscience, and it governed the daylight hours: the hour the shutters opened, the hour the apprentices were fed, the hour my father set down his graver and pronounced the day's labor sufficient. The second clock was the river. It had no face and no hands, but I knew its hours by heart — the low mutter of morning ice settling, the midday rush when the mill upstream released its sluice, and then, deep in the night, a long exhalation of water against stone that meant the town was asleep and the valley belonged to whoever was still awake in it.

I was still awake in it.

Three floors above my bedroom there was a loose board at the back of the composing room, beneath the case where my father kept his nonpareil, and if you knelt and pressed the right corner and lifted with your fingertips rather than your nails — nails clicked, and clicks carry in a silent house — the board came up like the lid of a coffin, and beneath it lay the other half of my life.

I went up to it the way other girls went to meet lovers: by the back stair, in stockinged feet, with a candle cupped in one hand so the flame wouldn't gutter. The stair treads knew me. The fourth one groaned if you stepped on its left side; the seventh had a nail head worn smooth as a coin. I had learned the geography of silence the way Jude had learned the geography of type, case by case, drawer by drawer, until my body moved through the dark house without waking it.

Jude slept in the loft over the drying room. Some nights I could hear him turning over, the cot ropes creaking their small complaint, and I would stand motionless on the stairs with my heart going like a fly against a windowpane, waiting for the sound to settle. He believed the workshop slept when he slept. He believed the presses rested and the lead grew cold and the whole establishment of Sterling & Son dissolved into innocent darkness until morning.

Sterling & Son. My father's little joke, painted on the sign himself — there had never been a son. There was only me, and there are men who would sooner paint a lie on wood than admit their trade might pass into a daughter's hands, and my father was not one of those men, but he was careful, and care looks like concealment from the outside. So the sign said Son, and by day I was the daughter who swept the floor and fetched the ink and knew her place behind the counter, and by night —

By night I was the Son.

* * *

That particular night — the third of December, if the brass clock is to be trusted, and I have no reason now to doubt it — I knelt on the cold boards of the composing room and lifted the loose plank and breathed in the smell of what I kept down there. People think lead has no smell. Lead smells like rain about to fall, like the inside of a struck match, like something waiting. Beneath the floorboards lay four galley plates wrapped in oilcloth, and each plate held a page of sedition, and I had cut every character of it myself.

Not cast — cut. That was the part that made my hands sweat even now, remembering. A compositor merely arranges what exists; she takes letters from the case and stands them in rows, and if she is caught, she can say *someone else cut this face, someone else wrote these words, I am only a hand*. But there was no one else. Over the summer, while my father slept and the river muttered its midnight hour, I had taken blanks of type-metal from the scrap bin and carved into them, letter by letter, a face of my own. It was crude work beside my father's. Ambrose Sterling could cut a lowercase *g* so fine that under a loupe you could see the hairline of the bowl thinner than a spider's thread; his faces were famous two counties over, and the garrison officers who commissioned proclamations from him did it partly for the beauty of the thing, which I always found obscene — lies dressed in loveliness. My own letters were blunt things, thick-stemmed, with serifs like clenched fists. But they were mine, and no other press in the province owned them, and therefore no other press could be blamed for them.

The plates held, in order: an open letter to the townspeople concerning the grain requisitions; a list of names of men taken from the valley in October whose families had received no word since; the second half of a speech printed in the capital's banned papers, copied out for me in a hand I did not recognize, delivered to me in a manner I will come to; and a single-sheet appeal beginning *TO THE WORKING MEN OF THIS VALLEY*, which was, of all the four, the one I feared most and loved most, because I had written it myself. Every word. At midnight. With my father's dictionary open on my knee and my candle burning down faster than my courage.

I unwrapped the third plate only — the list of names — because that was tonight's work. There were corrections to make. Two days before, a woman named Marta Kessel had come to the counter while my father was at the bank, and stood turning her bonnet in her hands, and asked in a voice like a draft under a door whether we printed memorial notices, and when I said yes, ma'am, of course, she said, *not the kind they let you print.*

And then she told me her husband was not dead, whatever the soldiers' ledger said. Dead men do not leave wives who find their boots gone and their supper uneaten and a neighbor's testimony of wagons heading south in the rain. She had nine names besides her husband's. Neighbors' sons. Men who had been standing in doorways, walking to market, minding their own fields, when the requisition patrols came through angry about resistance in villages we'd only ever heard of as songs.

I gave her bread and tea and told her nothing, and after she left I sat with my hands flat on the counter and felt the whole building humming under my palms like a struck bell.

* * *

You must understand what the workshop was by day, so you will understand what it was by night, and why the space between the two was narrowing like the last inch of a candle.

By day, Sterling & Son printed money.

Not counterfeit money — God, no, my father would have gone to the scaffold cheerfully before touching a false coin — but government notes. Banknotes for a treasury that could no longer pay its own clerks, scrip for a ministry that had begun printing its survival in green and black ink and calling it currency. The commission had come through official channels two years ago, with wax seals and a polite covering letter, and it had saved us. It had also enslaved us, though my father would never have used that word. When the regime's credit began to rot — and everyone in the valley could smell the rot, the way you smell a cellar flood before you see it — the treasury's orders doubled, then tripled, and the price they paid per sheet stayed fixed, and my father worked longer hours for less real money printing symbols of money, and the irony of that was not lost on any of us, least of all him.

He never spoke of it directly. He spoke of it in the language of the bench: *the paper stock is thinning again*, he would say, or *the green ink has gone muddy, they're cutting the pigment*, and only later did I understand he was saying *the state is dying and it is taking its color from our ink pots.* He kept the regime's plates locked in the steel cabinet in his office, and he kept the key on a chain inside his shirt, next to his skin, and some nights when he embraced me goodnight I could feel that key pressing between us like a small cold question neither of us asked aloud.

So that was the daylight master: the ministry, distant and dying, paying us in promises to print more promises.

And the night master paid in nothing at all. In rightness. In the weight of names.

* * *

How it began — I have never told this in order, even to myself, but here in the dark with the plate in my lap I can try.

It began with a hymn, of all things. Last spring, before the bridge closed, before the floods, a traveling bookbinder came through town with a cart of damaged volumes and a limp and a name he gave as Ferenc, though I doubted even then it was his. He brought a psalter in for rebinding — water damage, mold in the gutters — and while my father examined it, Ferenc looked around the shop with the particular attention of a man taking inventory, and his eyes stopped on the specimen book of my father's faces that we kept open on the counter, and he said, *that's a fine bold face for broadsheets. Does anyone use it?*

My father said no one had yet; he'd cut it on speculation.

*I know some people,* Ferenc said mildly, *who speculate.*

My father handed back the psalter and quoted him a price for the binding and did not look at him again, and I thought that was the end of it, and I was young enough to be relieved. But a week later a parcel arrived at the shop containing no letter and no return address, only a bundle of printed sheets — capital newspapers, the banned ones, smuggled and folded inside a ream of blotting paper — and tucked among them a note in that same unrecognized hand: *If the speculator's face should ever be needed, leave word in the hymnal at the Reformed church, third pew, by marking the page number.*

I should have burned it. I want to be honest, here in the dark where honesty costs nothing: my first feeling was fear so pure it was almost physical, a taste like coins at the back of my tongue. Treason was not an abstraction in this province. Men had been flogged in the marketplace square in V—— for less than a parcel of newspapers. And my father — my father with his regime contracts and his locked cabinet and his chain around his neck — my father would hang twice over if the authorities ever connected his bench to a rebel press, and worse, he would hang knowing his daughter had tied the knot.

But I read the newspapers. That was my sin, if it was a sin; I read them before I hid them. And the words in them did something to me that I have spent every night since trying to repay. They described the world I already half-knew — the requisitions, the vanished men, the magistrates who ruled for the garrison and called it law — but they described it *in print*. Typeset. Numbered. Permanent. And I remember standing in the washroom with ink to my elbows, reading about a demonstration in the capital broken by cavalry, and thinking with sudden terrible clarity: *this happened, and it is true, and it is true because someone set it in lead, and lead does not apologize and does not retract.*

History, I understood then, is not what happens. History is what gets set.

And nobody in this valley was setting anything. Our town paper printed wedding announcements and grain prices and the garrison's communiqués verbatim, and between one line and the next, whole truths fell through the gaps of the type like spilled sorts swept off the stone.

So I marked a page in the hymnal. Psalm sixty-eight, I remember — *let the righteous be glad; let them rejoice before God; yea, let them exceedingly rejoice* — and I chose it deliberately, and I have never repented of the choice.

* * *

Tonight, though. Back to tonight, because the candle is shortening and I have corrections to make and a house to keep sleeping.

I worked by feel mostly, my fingers reading the face of the plate the way a blind man reads, finding the line where Marta Kessel's testimony ended and the list of names began. I had set the list in my own blunt face, ragged-right, each name on its line like a stone laid on a grave:

KESSEL, JÁNOS — taken 14 October, road to V——.
HORVÁT, PETER — taken 14 October, from his own field.
BARTOS, IMRE — seventeen years of age. Taken 15 October.

Seventeen. I had stopped after setting that line the first time and put both hands flat on the cold floorboards and breathed until the shaking stopped. Seventeen was two years younger than Jude. Seventeen was a boy who had probably carried firewood for his mother the morning he was taken.

There were nine names. Tonight I added none and corrected one — the widow Bartos had come by the back way yesterday, quick and quiet, with a spelling correction: *not Bartos with one r, child, Bartos with two, his grandmother was Hungarian proper.* Even in grief she cared about the orthography of her son's name, and I understood that too. If he was going to survive anywhere, it was going to be in correctly-set lead.

I scraped the offending letter from the plate with the smallest of my father's gravers — his tools, borrowed nightly, cleaned and returned before dawn, another theft I intended someday to confess — and I cut the corrected letter freehand, breathing shallow, the burin whispering into the metal, and the sound it made in the sleeping house was the sound of a mouse in a wall, deniable, ignorable, safe.

Safe. I laughed without sound at the word. Nothing about this was safe, and I knew precisely how unsafe it was, because three weeks ago I had woken to hoofbeats in the street and lain rigid in my bed listening to dragoons dismounting outside the bakery across the lane, and heard them laughing, heard the baker's terrified answers, and understood with total clarity that if those hooves had stopped at *our* door instead — if the loose board had been found, if one of my plates had been unwrapped by a bored soldier — there would be no trial worth the name. There would be a post and a rope and a proclamation afterward, set in my father's beautiful type, announcing that justice had been done.

And yet. Fear was the constant, the way the river's sound was constant; you did not stop living because the river ran. What surprised me, what I could not explain to anyone without sounding mad, was that the fear had become a kind of companion, a pressure at my back like a hand between my shoulder blades, urging me forward. Because every hour of fear was purchased against an act. I was afraid *because* I had cut the plates, and the plates existed *because* nine names deserved better than silence, and the arithmetic of that seemed to me, at nineteen, the only honest mathematics left in the province.

The regime printed money that bought less every month. My father printed it, and aged. I printed names, and they stayed.

Two masters, one roof. And I knew — I had known for months, in the way you know a thaw is coming under a frozen river before the ice shows a crack — that the two masters could not share the workshop forever. Either the regime's rot would reach us and take my father's cabinet and his contracts and his liberty, or the other master's cause would come to power and ask why Sterling had printed the collapsing currency to its last dishonest sheet. There was no version of the future in which the workshop went on being two things at once. The presses could hold two masters' work in a single week. They could not hold two allegiances in a single ending.

I did not know yet which ending was coming. I knew only that the floorboard over my secret life was growing easier to lift, the wood wearing smooth with use, the house itself learning my duplicity — and that sooner or later someone else would learn it too.

Someone else. As if summoned by the thought, there came a creak from the direction of the loft stairs — soft, ambiguous, the kind of sound old houses manufacture in their sleep — and I froze with the graver still in my hand, the corrected plate naked on my knees, the candle flame leaning toward the draft from the stairwell like a thing straining to listen.

The river exhaled against the stones. The house settled. Nothing followed.

But as I lowered the board over the plates and swept the wood shavings into my palm and pinched out the candle, my heart still hammering its midnight hour, one thought pursued me down the dark stairs and into bed and would not leave me even in sleep:

Jude Marsh had been restless for weeks. Restless the way I was once restless before I became dangerous. And in the morning, when he came down to the stone and sorted his type and glanced — just glanced, barely a glance — toward the back of the composing room where the floorboards ran oldest and loosest, I saw it plainly, and my blood went cold and hot at once:

He was starting to wonder what his master's workshop did after midnight.
