## 5. The Soldier at the Counter

The bell above the workshop door had a voice Jude knew the way he knew his own breathing. Merchants gave it a flat clank, hurried and transactional. Children trying it for mischief produced a thin, bright jangle. Ambrose Sterling never rang it at all; he came through the back from the house, so that his arrival was announced only by the creak of the stair and the smell of his tobacco arriving a half-second before he did.

On the morning the soldier came, the bell made a sound Jude had never heard before — a hard, martial clang, as though someone had struck it rather than let it speak.

He looked up from the case. His fingers had been moving through the upper tray on their own errands, finding e's and t's and the small crowded company of vowels without any instruction from his eyes, and for a moment the interruption felt like being woken. The light through the south window fell in long panes across the stone floor, and in one of those panes stood a man in the grey-blue coat of the garrison, snow melting off his boots into dark crescents.

"Proclamations," the officer said, before anyone had greeted him. He held out a folded paper with the flat formality of a man handing over a summons. "Two hundred copies. Your master takes government work, I'm told."

Jude took the paper. His first thought — absurd, he would think later — was of the type itself: what face would carry those words, whether they had enough of Ambrose's twelve-point for a text this length or whether they'd need to fall back on the worn pica, the letters gone soft at the edges like river stones. It was a compositor's reflex, to meet every sentence in the world as a question of setting. Only afterward did he actually read what he was holding.

*By order of the Military Governor. Whereas certain persons have spread false accounts concerning events at Millbrook Hollow, and whereas such accounts are injurious to the public peace—*

Millbrook Hollow. Even the name had begun to arrive differently lately, wrapped in lowered voices. Three weeks past harvest, soldiers had gone up the valley road toward the hollow, and something had happened there. Something had come back down the valley in pieces: a woman walking to town with her eyes fixed on nothing; a farmer who would not pass the garrison wall anymore but took the long way through the mill yard, adding half an hour to his day forever; a silence whenever soldiers entered the tavern, not a frightened silence exactly, but a careful one, the silence of men measuring their words like coin.

Jude had set no type about it. That was the thing he kept returning to in the nights that followed, turning it over like a misprint. Nothing about Millbrook Hollow existed in lead. Whatever had happened up there lived only in mouths, and mouths were water — they ran downhill, thinned, vanished.

"We can have them by Thursday," said Ambrose from the doorway to the back room, wiping his hands on his apron. He had appeared the way he always did when money entered the shop, which is to say instantly and without visible appetite. "Two hundred, you said."

"Two hundred," the officer agreed. He was young, Jude saw now. Twenty-five at most, with the raw pink complexion of a boy who had grown into his coat rather than into himself. He kept his eyes on the presses the way a man keeps his eyes on dogs. "The wording is fixed. No alterations. You'll print it exactly as written."

"Naturally." Ambrose took the paper from Jude's hand with a smoothness that made Jude realize his own grip had tightened on it. "If your man will initial here against the proof."

The officer initialed. His signature was cramped and violent, all downward strokes. Then he paused, one boot still on the threshold, and looked around the workshop — at the cases, at the iron press with its great wing screw, at the drying rack where yesterday's currency notes hung like laundry, each one bearing the fine engraved promise of a government that could no longer honor it.

"You're Sterling," he said. "The cutter."

"I am."

"They say you cut the finest faces in three counties." The young man's voice did something complicated then, something between contempt and envy, and left before Jude could name it. "Fine work for a small town."

Then the bell spoke its hard clang again, and he was gone, and the smell of wet wool and horse went with him.

* * *

Ambrose read the proclamation twice, standing by the window, his lips moving slightly around the words as if tasting them. Then he folded it and set it on the counter with a precision that told Jude everything his father-in-trade would not say aloud.

"You'll set it," Ambrose said. "Not Elodie."

"She sets faster than I do."

"She does." Ambrose picked a sliver of tobacco from his teeth and considered the window, where the officer could be seen mounting his horse with more anger than grace. "And she has opinions. Opinions are excellent things in a person and terrible things in a galley. Set it yourself, Jude. Straight matter, no display work. Distribute Thursday morning, before market."

He went back to his bench then, to the commission he'd been cutting for a fortnight — a merchant's mark, a sheaf of wheat worked into a monogram — and the graver began its small dry whisper, and the workshop closed over the incident like water over a thrown stone.

But Jude stood at the counter with the paper in his hands, and the stone stayed thrown.

* * *

He began the setting that afternoon, because there was nothing else honest to do with it, and because Ambrose had given him the order and because — this was the part he examined least — some part of him wanted to know how it felt in his hands.

It felt like anything else. That was the first horror of it.

The words came off his fingertips the same as scripture came off them, the same as wedding announcements and grain prices and the obituary of old Widow Hartline, whom he had never met but whose death he had set in three lines with a border of stars. *False accounts.* The phrase sat in his stick like any other phrase. His thumb found each letter, seated it, drew the next. *Injurious to the public peace.* The pica was soft-edged; he chose it deliberately, so the thing would look older and more ordinary than it was, so it would look like it had been printed a hundred times about a hundred matters no one remembered.

Somewhere in the second paragraph he stopped.

*Whereas the disturbances at Millbrook Hollow resulted from the unlawful assembly of armed persons, and were resolved with regrettable but necessary force—*

Necessary force. He sat with the composing stick balanced on his knee and tried to make the words assemble into a picture. He had been up the hollow road once, years ago, with a delivery of playbills for a fair that never materialized because rain had drowned it. He remembered the orchards, the crooked stone bridge over the stream, a dog that had followed him half a mile for nothing but the pleasure of company. Armed persons. In Millbrook Hollow there had been, so far as he knew, forty-one souls, two muskets between them, and a grist mill whose wheel had not turned since spring.

Resolved. The word was a beautiful word, typographically speaking. Six letters, no awkward pairs. It set clean.

His hands, he noticed, had continued working while his mind stood apart from them, the way a man continues walking after hearing bad news, his body refusing yet to admit what his ears have taken in. There were four lines in the stick already. They looked right. That was the second horror: that lies, once composed, look no different from truth. Lead has no conscience. Metal takes whatever impression is struck on it and holds it, faithful as a dog, and there is no face in any font that will tell you the words it carries are false.

"You've got the spacing wrong," Elodie said behind him.

He hadn't heard her come in. She stood at his shoulder with a slate of fresh ink in her hands, her sleeves rolled, a smudge of black at her jaw where she pushed her hair back with inky fingers a dozen times an hour. She looked at the galley, not at him.

"The third line. You've doubled a space after 'whereas.'"

"It's justified that way."

"It's ugly that way." She set down the ink and, before he could stop her, picked a space out of the line herself, her small quick fingers adjusting the metal with the tenderness other people reserved for infants. Then she read the line. Then she read it again.

The workshop was very quiet. At his bench, Ambrose's graver whispered on. Outside, sleet ticked against the south window like something wanting to be let in.

"Government work," Jude said. It came out as an excuse, which it was.

"I know what it is." She put the ink down and stepped back, and her face had closed the way a door closes — not slammed, just shut, the latch turned quietly from within. "I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"No." She took the slate again and carried it to the press, and her voice when it came over her shoulder was level and cold and worse than anger. "I was going to say that you set a very clean line, Jude Marsh. You always do. Whatever's in it."

She inked the platen rollers for the currency run and did not speak to him again until supper.

* * *

Thursday he distributed.

This was the trade's least-loved labor, tramping the streets with a satchel of damp sheets, leaving proclamations at the tavern, the corn exchange, the garrison gate itself, where the same young officer accepted the bundle without meeting his eyes. The papers were still faintly cool from the press. Jude handed them over and watched the town take them in — the tavern keeper pinning the sheet to a beam already furred with old notices; two teamsters reading it side by side, their lips moving; the schoolmaster folding his copy into his coat with a small nod, the way one pockets a summons.

And at the corner by the mill yard, he saw the farmer. The one who took the long way around the garrison wall.

The man stood with the proclamation in both hands, and Jude watched him read it, and Jude watched the reading do what reading does. First the face goes still — that is the body conserving itself, Jude would understand later, holding everything back for the moment it will be needed. Then the jaw works, though the mouth is shut, chewing on nothing. And then the eyes fill, without drama, without sound, the way a well fills in winter when no one is watching, and the farmer folded the paper very carefully along its printed creases, and put it inside his coat, over his heart, and walked away down the long way, past the garrison wall, weeping without any tears falling, and Jude stood in the street with an empty satchel and understood that he had done this.

His hands had done this. His hands, which could cut a perfect quoin and tie a neat shoot of twine and set a psalm so even that the minister had once asked who composed it. He had taken the government's lie and given it a body — two hundred bodies, cool and creaseable, portable into coats and over hearts — and somewhere up the valley, in the hollow with the crooked stone bridge, there might be a widow holding this sheet, reading *necessary force*, and feeling the force necessary all over again, administered a second time in ink.

That night he could not sleep. He lay in the loft above the workshop listening to the building settle and the river grinding at its ice, and he performed the small dishonest arithmetic he would perform for many nights after: that the orders were orders, that the printing trade prints, that a press is like a scale, it weighs what is put upon it and asks nothing about the merchandise. He believed it the way you believe a roof over your head in calm weather. He believed it perhaps four parts in ten, and the remaining six parts lay awake in the dark reciting the sentence he had set with his own thumbs: *resolved with regrettable but necessary force.*

Regrettable. He had set that word too. He had selected the r himself, checked its shoulder, wiped its face.

* * *

The thaw of the argument came a week later, and it came, as most things in the workshop came, through Ambrose, indirectly, sideways, in the form of a lesson.

They were distributing the new currency notes — the regime had changed the engraving again, a smaller portrait, a different seal, as if shame could be reissued — and Ambrose held one of the fresh sheets up to the window light, examining his own registration marks, and said, without preamble:

"A man came to my father's shop in the capital, when I was your age. A bookseller. Wanted us to print an edition of Voltaire with the offensive passages cut out. Paid double." He set the note down. "My father took the money. Cut the passages. Printed the book."

Jude waited. The graver did not whisper; Ambrose had put it down, which he almost never did in daylight.

"When he died, we found in his desk every uncut page he'd ever withheld. He'd printed them all privately — the true texts, complete — and sewn them into false covers, and locked them away, and never sold one, never showed one, never told a living soul. Two hundred volumes of everything he'd agreed to erase." Ambrose took up his tobacco pouch, worked it, did not fill his pipe. "I used to think it was cowardice, hoarding truth like that. Now I think I don't know what it was. But I know he died with it under lock and key, and the lock was the only honest thing in the house."

He filled his pipe then, struck his match, and the lesson — if it was a lesson, if it was anything but an old man talking — ended the way Ambrose's lessons always ended, with smoke and silence and work resumed.

But Jude carried it to his bed that night, and it lay down beside the other thought, the one from the street corner, and the two of them kept him company in the dark: the farmer weeping without tears, and the father with two hundred true books locked in a desk, and between them, growing slowly, unmistakably, like ice crystals forming in still water, the understanding that a man who sets type chooses — every time, silently, forever — which words deserve to be made permanent.

He did not know, then, why Elodie came down the loft ladder past midnight, nor where she was going with a candle guttering low in her hand, nor why she paused at the trapdoor and looked back at him lying awake, and said nothing, and went on down into the dark of the workshop instead of the dark of the house —

Only that the floorboards below his room, that night, creaked longer than floorboards should.
