## 6. A Ledger of the Missing

The widow came on a Thursday, in the hour when the workshop smelled most of itself — ink and oil and the cold mineral breath of lead — and Elodie was alone at the stone sink, washing the rollers with turpentine until her fingertips came away gray.

She heard the door before she saw the woman: the small hesitation of a hand that had never opened this door before, testing the latch as though it might be locked against her. Then the bell above it gave its thin brass note, and the winter light shifted, and Marta Kessler stood in the doorway with her shawl drawn tight and something bundled against her chest like an infant.

Elodie knew her by sight, as one knows everyone in a valley town — the face from market, from mass, from the long procession of funerals that had thickened since autumn. But she had never seen her up close, and up close she was older than rumor made her, her skin stretched fine over the bones, her eyes red-rimmed and dry in the way of eyes that have wept past their capacity and now burn instead.

"Madame Sterling is not—" Elodie began, out of habit, because her mother had been dead six years and the correction still rose automatically.

"I know." The widow's voice was low and roughened. "I've come for you. Or your father. I don't know which. I only know there's nowhere else."

* * *

Elodie dried her hands slowly, giving herself time. In the workshop's back room, through the wall, she could hear her father at his bench, the small precise music of his graver on brass — *tik, tik, tik* — cutting a new fount of pica for the garrison's next commission. He would be absorbed for another hour. She did not call to him.

"Sit," she said, and pulled the stool from beside the composing stone.

Marta Kessler did not sit. She stood in the middle of the floor as though sitting were a surrender, and looked around the workshop with an expression Elodie had learned to read in the last months: the look of a person seeing a press for the first time not as a machine but as a mouth. Something that could speak. Something that could say what needed saying, in a thousand identical voices, faster than any crier, more permanent than any whisper.

"You print things," the widow said. It was not quite a question.

"We print proclamations. Bills of sale. Notices of auction. Prayer cards." Elodie kept her voice level, kept it the voice of a tradesman's daughter reciting inventory. "Whatever is commissioned."

"And if it isn't commissioned?" The woman's hands tightened on the bundle at her chest. "If someone simply — asked?"

Through the wall: *tik, tik, tik.* Elodie thought of the plates beneath the floorboards under her feet, wrapped in oilcloth, sleeping in the dark with their rebel letters face-down like children hiding from a searchlight. She thought of the pamphlets she had set and printed after midnight while the town slept, the ones that had gone out in bread carts and flour sacks, unsigned, untraceable. She thought: *this is how it ends, then. Not with soldiers. With a grieving woman and a question.*

But grief, she had learned, was not the enemy. Grief was the raw ore of everything she had been doing, smelted down night after night into type metal.

"What do you want printed, Madame Kessler?"

The widow unfolded the bundle.

It was paper — many sheets, sewn together at the spine with black thread, the stitching uneven, done by hand in bad light. A ledger. On its cover, in a careful, schoolroom copperplate: *A Record of Those Taken.*

"My husband kept it," Marta said. "Before they took him too."

* * *

Elodie read standing at the window, where the light was best, while the widow sat at last on the stool and watched her with the terrible patience of someone who has nothing left to do but wait.

The ledger was not elegant. Anton Kessler had been a wheelwright, not a writer, and his entries had the blunt architecture of a man recording measurements he could not bear to take. Names. Ages. Dates. Places. And always, in the final column, in a hand that grew shakier as the pages turned, the same word or its variations: *taken. taken. taken away. taken, no one will say where.*

Tomas Brenner, forty-one, miller. Taken the ninth of October, from his own doorstep, before his wife and daughters.

The Voss brothers, nineteen and twenty-three. Taken the fourteenth of October, from the road between here and Halden. Their cart was found overturned. The grain was gone.

Anna Reiss, sixty-seven. Taken the second of November. Her neighbors say she asked them why, and no soldier answered, and she went with her hands folded like a woman going to prayer.

Page after page. Thirty-one names by the end of the ledger, thirty-one lines in a dead man's careful script, and Elodie understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down her spine, that she was holding the only copy in the world of these thirty-one lives. That the garrison's own records recorded none of it. That the official notices posted on the church door spoke of *bandits apprehended* and *order restored*, and that in a year, perhaps two, the names would be gone entirely — not disputed, not denied, simply *unwritten*, as though the men and women had dissolved like breath on glass.

This was what the erasure looked like from inside. Not fire. Not decree. Just absence, patiently administered.

"Why bring it to us?" she asked quietly. "Why not the priest? Why not the magistrate in Halden?"

"The magistrate signed the transport orders." Marta Kessler said it without bitterness, the way one states the weather. "And Father Anselm prays for the souls of the departed, which is holy, but my husband used to say a name in ink outlives a name in prayer. He said — " her voice caught, steadied — "he said the Sterlings' trade was the only trade in this valley that made things *permanent*. Everything else rots or rusts or burns. But you people put words in metal, and metal doesn't die."

*tik, tik, tik,* went the graver through the wall, cutting letters that would spell whatever her father was paid to make them spell.

"If I print this," Elodie said, "and it is traced to this shop, they will not fine us, Madame Kessler. They will not close us. They will arrest my father for treason, and treason in wartime has one sentence. You must understand what you are asking."

"I understand better than you think." The widow rose. Up close, her eyes were the color of river ice — beautiful and implacable. "I have buried a husband and I do not know where, because they would not give me the body. They would not even tell me *where*. There are twenty-nine other families in this valley who cannot mourn properly because mourning requires a grave and graves require truth. You ask if I understand. I understand that the truth is the only coffin those people will ever get, and you are the only joiner in the valley who can build it."

* * *

After the widow left — the ledger staying behind, sewn in its black thread, hidden first in Elodie's apron and then, that night, in the false bottom of the type case alongside the rebel plates — Elodie sat alone in the workshop until the candles burned to stubs.

Her father had gone to bed at his usual hour, ten o'clock, after checking the formes on tomorrow's garrison work with the tenderness he otherwise reserved for his finest punches. She had heard him pause outside the back room where she sat, heard him consider the door, heard him walk on. He knew something was wrong; she had inherited his gift for reading silence, and he hers. But he had not asked. That was the arrangement between them, unspoken since autumn: each of them maintaining the fiction of the other's innocence, two liars keeping faith by pretending not to see.

She spread the ledger open on the composing stone.

Thirty-one names. She began, without quite deciding to, setting the first of them in type — Tomas Brenner, forty-one, miller — picking the letters from the case with the automatic grace of ten years' practice, and found that her hands were steady and her eyes were not. The tears fell on the brass and she wiped them with her sleeve and kept setting, because the work required precision and precision was a kind of prayer, and because somewhere in the alchemy of that hour the grief in her chest changed state, the way water changes to ice, formless sorrow hardening into something with edges. Something that could be carried. Something that could be *used*.

She thought of her father's creed, spoken over years of supper tables: *We are tradesmen, Elodie. Small men keep small justice — we keep the shop alive, we feed the family, we survive. Survival is not cowardice. Survival is the seed of everything else.* She had believed him. Part of her believed him still. But survival for whom, and so that what? A shop full of presses printing lies forever, surviving like a body survives a fever, diminished and grateful?

No. There was a third thing, and she had known it since the night she cut her first forbidden plate: the truth itself was a currency, and someone in this valley had to mint it.

By three in the morning she had set the first page. By four she had decided — the decision arriving not as a thunderclap but as a quiet settling, like snow taking hold on a roof — that it would be printed. Not asked about. Not negotiated. Printed, on the proof press, after midnight, in editions small enough to vanish into coat linings and flour sacks, and distributed the way the other pamphlets went out, and if the garrison ever traced it, then let them trace it. Let them come. Some debts were worth the collection.

She blew out the candles. In the dark, the workshop breathed around her — the smell of ink and turpentine and cold iron, the smell of her whole life — and beneath the floorboards the plates lay waiting, and in the type case the ledger's first page slept in metal, thirty-one names rendered permanent, and Elodie pressed her palm flat against the composing stone as though taking an oath upon it.

Above the town, the garrison's watchfire glowed on the hill like a red eye that never blinked. Below, the river moved under its ice toward the bridge, patient, unhurried, certain of the thaw.

She was carrying the candle toward the stairs when she heard the floorboard creak behind her — soft, deliberate, unmistakable — and turned to find Jude standing in the doorway of the dark shop, half-dressed, his hair sleep-tangled, looking from her face to the type case in her arms with an expression she could not yet read.

"You're awake," she said, and heard how much the words cost her, and how little they concealed.
