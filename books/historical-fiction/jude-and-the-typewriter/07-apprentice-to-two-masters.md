## 7. Apprentice to Two Masters

I did not sleep that night. I lay in the narrow room above the workshop with the blanket pulled to my chin and listened to the house breathe — the tick of cooling lead in the stove below, the groan of ice on the river, Elodie's floorboards giving up their secrets one soft creak at a time.

She had gone down at midnight. I had heard her pass my door, her candle cupped in one hand so that its light traveled along the wall like a small obedient animal. I had told myself she was only fetching something forgotten, a spool of thread, a book. I had told myself a great many things since autumn began, and each of them had been a kind of type: characters cast in comfort, set in rows, locked into a forme I could not break without spilling everything.

But I had seen what I had seen. Three nights past, coming back late from the Widow Harsch's with a borrowed awl, I had looked through the workshop's frost-furred window and watched the press move under no hand but hers — watched the platen rise and fall, rise and fall, patient as a heart, while the town slept around it like a man sleeping beside a thief.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of snow that would not commit to falling. I went down early, earlier than was decent, and found the workshop exactly as it should be: the stone level, the ink slab wiped, the cases squared away in their cabinet like soldiers at inspection. Ambrose had not yet come down from his rooms. The fire was fresh-laid but unlit.

Everything was correct. That was the first wrong thing. A workshop after a night's honest work has a particular disarray — a scatter of quoins, a film of ink on the near edge of the stone, the faint sweetish reek of lamp oil. This room had been tidied the way a liar tidies his face.

I stood in the middle of it with my hands hanging at my sides and let myself look properly for the first time. Not at the presses, not at the stone, but at the floor. At the boards beneath the composing frame, where the grain ran differently — where two planks were newer than their neighbors, their edges cut clean instead of worn round by forty years of boots.

Ambrose's tread sounded on the stair behind me, heavy and deliberate, and I turned away too quickly, and he saw me turn away, and neither of us said anything about it. He lit the fire. He took down his apron. He said, "The garrison wants five hundred more of the proclamations by Thursday," and his voice had the flatness of a man reading from a form, and I understood that we were both standing inside the same lie now, though we had entered it by different doors.

"Five hundred," I said. "Of the curfew notice."

"Of the curfew notice." He set his spectacles on his nose and squinted at the morning light as if it owed him money. "It says the assembly of persons in groups exceeding four is forbidden. It says this for their safety."

"For their safety," I repeated.

"You may put your irony back in the case, boy. Irony doesn't print well; it slurs." But there was no bite in it. There was something worse than bite — there was tiredness, deep as a well, and I thought: *he knows*. He knows what she does down here at midnight, and he lets the floorboards keep her secret because keeping it is safer than speaking it, even to himself.

I sorted type all morning. Lowercase e's into their compartment, lowercase a's, the fussy little world of letters that make up most of any sentence and get none of the glory. My hands knew the work and did it without me, which left my mind free to do what it wanted, which was to walk again and again through that frosted window, that moving press, that figure bent over the tympan with her sleeves rolled and her hair coming loose from its braid.

Elodie came down at nine. She nodded to me the way she always did — a single dip of the chin, economical, a woman who spent her gestures like coins she might need later. She tied on her apron. She checked the galleys. She asked her father whether he wanted the ink ground fresh or taken from last week's cake, and he said fresh, and she ground it, and the smell of linseed and lampblack filled the room like a second weather.

She never once looked at the floorboards. That was how I knew they mattered.

* * *

I waited three days. I am not sure why — some instinct for the shape of traps, perhaps, or simple cowardice wearing the mask of prudence. On the third night I heard her go down again, and this time I followed.

The stairs are old and I know every board that speaks. I went down them the way you learn to go down stairs when you are an apprentice and late: weight on the outside of the foot, hand on the rail to take half your body's honesty away. The workshop door stood open a finger's width. Candlelight lay along the gap like a blade laid flat.

Inside, Elodie stood at the far press — the old Albion, the one Ambrose called the widow because it had outlived three better machines — and she was setting a galley by feel more than sight, her fingers walking the type with the certainty of long practice. On the stone beside her lay a printed sheet, still wet enough to shine where the candle caught it.

I should have knocked on the doorframe. I should have coughed, announced myself, given her the courtesy of warning. Instead I pushed the door wide and stepped into the light, and she spun with the composing stick raised like a weapon, and for one absurd moment we faced each other across the stone — the girl armed with a stick full of other people's words, the boy armed with nothing at all.

"Jude." My name in her mouth had no welcome in it. "How long have you known?"

"Three days. Since—" I stopped. Lying to her seemed suddenly both impossible and obscene. "Since I saw the press moving through the window. I wasn't spying. I was coming back from the Harsches'. I just—"

"Saw." She lowered the stick slowly. Her eyes went past me to the dark doorway, to the stairs, calculating exits the way her father calculated em quads. Then, apparently finding none worth the cost, she straightened and did the thing I would remember for the rest of my life: she picked up the printed sheet from the stone and held it out to me.

"Then read it," she said. "If you're going to know, know all of it."

I took the sheet. The paper was cheap — the gray stock Ambrose kept for wrapping parcels — but the impression was clean, deep, even, better work than the garrison proclamations by any measure a compositor would use. And the words:

*NAMES OF THE TAKEN. On the night of the ninth of November, soldiers of the garrison entered the houses of the river quarter and carried off the following persons, whose families have received no word since—*

And then the names. Forty-one of them. I knew some. Tomas Beck, who mended nets. The Voss brothers, who were not brothers at all but cousins everyone pretended otherwise about. Old Marta Kellner, who was sixty-eight years old and could not have lifted a pike if her life had depended on it, which apparently it had.

"They were taken for questioning," Elodie said quietly. "That is what the proclamation says. Questioning. Marta Kellner was taken for questioning and her daughter has been to the garrison four times and four times has been told to come back when the thaw comes." Her voice did not rise. It got smaller and harder, like metal cooled in water. "Paper burns, Jude. Memory rots. But type — type is repeatable. You can kill a man and burn his house and the record dies with him. Unless someone sets it. Unless someone makes it permanent. That's what we do. That's what this shop does, when it isn't printing lies for men with swords."

"We," I said. The word came out before I could weigh it.

Her chin lifted. "My father prints their currency and their curfews and sleeps at night because survival is a virtue too, he says. I print this and don't sleep at all. Between us we make one honest tradesman." She held out her hand for the sheet. "You can give it back and go upstairs and tomorrow you'll sort e's and a's and none of this will have touched you. Or—"

"Or?"

"Or you can help me lock this forme. The chase is heavy and my wrists ache and I've been at it since eleven."

I looked at the forme. The type stood in its bed, mirror-reversed, a whole page of truth waiting to be made permanent, and I felt something turn over in my chest that I had no name for yet — terror and exhilaration sharing a coat like two men too cold to quarrel over it.

I locked the chase. My hands shook, and the quoins chattered against the iron, and Elodie watched me without comment until the last wedge bit and the forme sat solid and true. Then she showed me how she'd been doing it — the register, the packing, the trick of feeding the thin gray stock so it didn't double. We pulled two hundred impressions between midnight and the hour before dawn, and neither of us spoke above a whisper, and every creak of the building made us both go still as set type.

When it was done she washed the ink from her hands and said, "You understand what you've done tonight."

"I've helped print a pamphlet."

"You've become a conspirator. There's no un-pressing a page, Jude. What's printed is printed." She said it without menace — as fact, the way her father would say *the forme is locked, mind the corners*. "Go to bed. Come down tomorrow looking bored."

I went up the stairs with my heart going like a press at speed, and lay in the dark, and discovered that I was smiling, and that frightened me more than the soldiers ever had.

* * *

After that, the workshop had two shifts and two truths.

By day we served the regime. I set the curfew notices and the price schedules and the garrison's tedious requisition forms, and Ambrose supervised with his spectacles down his nose, and the shop smelled of ink and linseed and obedience. By night — not every night, only when Elodie had copy ready, only when the wind was right and the watchman's rounds predictable — we served the other master.

She wrote the copy herself, mostly. She had a hand for it I hadn't expected: plain, hard sentences with no ornament, the prose equivalent of her father's finest face. *They say these men were traitors. They will not say where the men are buried.* She gathered names from widows and ferry-men and the doctor who signed fewer death certificates than there were bodies. I taught myself to set fast in the dark, by touch, the way blind men read. My fingertips learned the difference between a b and a d in a way my eyes never had.

We hid the plates beneath the floorboards — those two newer planks by the composing frame — wrapped in oilcloth, laid in a shallow box Ambrose must have built years ago, though neither Elodie nor I ever said so aloud. Some silences are load-bearing. You don't knock on them.

The danger changed the texture of everything. Bread tasted louder. The bell of the church on Sunday struck me as a summons. When Sergeant Brandt came to collect the garrison's printing and stood in the doorway scraping mud from his boots, I smiled at him and hated him and feared him and noticed, with a clarity that appalled me, that I was beginning to enjoy the fear — that it had a flavor, sharp and bright, like the first cold day of winter.

One night, pulling sheets, Elodie said, "Why aren't you afraid? Properly afraid, I mean. You were, the first week."

"I am afraid."

"No. You're *lit up*. There's a difference." She cleaned the rollers with a cloth gone black as tar. "My father says fear keeps small men alive. Maybe that's true. But I think you've stopped being small, Jude Marsh, and you haven't decided yet whether you're glad."

I had no answer for that. The press thumped. The pages came off warm and damp and true.

* * *

On the last night of January, we printed the names of eleven more of the taken, and among them, for the first time, a date of death — reported by the gravedigger at the chapel yard, who counted shovelfuls and remembered. Elodie set the line herself: *Buried on the seventeenth, in coffins too short for grown men.*

I was washing the stone when the knock came at the street door. Not the garrison's knock — that was a fist, authoritative, spaced. This was quick and small, three raps and a pause and one more, and Elodie blew out the candle so fast the smoke stung my eyes, and we stood in the dark listening to the door handle turn.

A voice came through the wood, low and cracked: "Mistress Sterling. Please. It's the Voss woman. I have more names. I have — God forgive me — I have a list of the ones who gave the soldiers the addresses."

Elodie's hand found my sleeve in the dark and gripped it. Through the frozen window, far down the valley road, I could see the garrison's lanterns moving along the bridge — more lanterns than usual, moving in formation, coming home from somewhere in the dark.

"Tomorrow," Elodie whispered through the door. "Come at dawn, through the alley. Not tonight."

The footsteps retreated. The lanterns on the bridge kept coming. And I stood in the black workshop with ink to my elbows and the plates under the floor and understood that whatever we had built in the dark was about to be tested by daylight — and that dawn was only four hours away.
