## 8. The Currency of Lies

Ambrose Sterling had learned long ago that a man could measure his own soul the way he measured type: by weight, by depth, by how much pressure it could bear before the face of it began to crack.

He stood at the banknote press on a morning in late January, watching the first proofs come off the cylinder, and he did this measuring now, as he did every morning, and found—as always—that the numbers were worse than the day before.

The notes were beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. He had cut the vignette himself two winters past: the river with its bridge, the hills rendered in fine parallel lines that caught the light like water catching sun, the guilloche borders so intricate that no counterfeiter in the province could hope to imitate them. He had cut them with pride. He had been *asked* to cut them with pride—the commission had come with a letter signed by a deputy minister whose name Ambrose had since watched dissolve into scandal, then into silence, then into nothing at all—and pride was what he had felt, standing over the engraving table with his loupe screwed to his eye, making the river flow.

Now the river flowed on paper that was worth less every week than the rag pulp it was printed on. The regime's treasury issued decrees; the decrees were printed on his press; the currency those decrees backed bought half of what it had bought at Michaelmas and would buy half again by Lady Day. And still the orders came down from the garrison, week upon week, in envelopes sealed with wax that cost more than the labor of the men who melted it: *ten thousand notes, small denominations, urgent.* As though urgency were a thing that could be printed. As though a press could stamp value into paper the way it stamped ink.

Ambrose lifted a proof sheet to the window and held it against the gray light. The bridge on the note gleamed. Out the actual window, the actual bridge stood closed behind its barricade of military wagons, its far end lost in river fog, and beyond that fog the capital went on burning—or didn't; one heard everything and knew nothing—while here in the valley the money rotted in men's pockets and the soldiers' boots got louder on the frozen streets.

*Survival is the only justice available to small men.*

His father's words. His father had said them in another winter, in another trouble, with the same tired mouth, and had died at his bench anyway, which Ambrose had always taken as either a refutation or a confirmation and had never decided which.

He set the proof down and went to find his ledger.

* * *

The workshop woke around him as he worked. He heard Jude arrive through the side door, stamping snow off his boots, breathing on his fingers before he touched the type—good habits, learned fast, the boy had hands the trade had been waiting for. He heard him sort in the case room, the soft iron rain of lead on lead, the sound Ambrose had slept to for forty years and would know deaf. He heard Elodie come down later than usual, her tread careful on the stairs, and something in the care of it snagged his attention the way a hairline crack snags light—but he let it go. He let a great many things go these days. It was becoming his principal craft.

"Master Sterling." Jude appeared in the doorway with the galleys for the garrison proclamation. "The colonel's office sent amendments. Third paragraph."

"Read it."

Jude read it. It was the same paragraph they had amended twice already, each amendment adding a clause about the *bandits* who had fired on a patrol near Verron—except that Ambrose had talked, three nights ago, with the carter who drove flour up from Verron, and the carter had talked, because carters talk when they're paid in anything at all, even attention, and what the carter described was not bandits firing on a patrol but a patrol firing on a granary crowd, four dead, two of them boys, and the bodies left in the road overnight as a lesson.

The amendment made the bandits eleven instead of six. The amendment gave them rifles they had not possessed. The amendment was, in its way, a work of composition, and Ambrose supposed that somewhere in the ministry there was a compositor like himself, an old man at a case, setting each lie letter by letter, justifying every line, locking it all up tight in a chase so it could not shift or speak out of turn.

"Set it as amended," Ambrose said.

Jude hesitated. Only a moment—a breath's worth—but Ambrose saw it, and recognized it, because he had worn that same hesitation himself once, thirty years ago, on his own apprentice face, before he had sanded it smooth with use. "Yes, sir," Jude said, and went back to the case room, and the iron rain resumed.

Ambrose sat down at his desk and opened the ledger and entered the day's figures in his small exact hand, and beneath the figures, where no auditor would ever look, he kept a second accounting. Not in numbers. In names. He did not write the names—he was not a fool, and correspondence killed men—but he kept them in his head, in a case of his own, each one a piece of type he had set and locked up and never distributed:

The carter from Verron, who had seen the bodies in the road.

The widow Aubry, whose son had been taken in October and whose questions at the garrison gate had been answered with rifle butts.

The schoolmaster Pellissier, who had stopped greeting him in the street since the proclamations began, and whose stopped greeting said more than any speech.

The printer Duret in the capital, his old journeyman friend, whose shop had been sealed in November and who had not answered two letters.

Each name a sort. Each sort a line. Each line a sentence in a book he hoped never to print, because printing it would require a courage he had spent his whole life carefully not acquiring.

* * *

At midday the money came back.

It always came back. That was the joke the valley played on him, the private joke of God or whatever ran the machinery: every note his press produced went out into the town and circulated and wore soft in strangers' pockets and came home to him eventually, folded into payment for paper stock or lamp oil or the butcher's bill, and Ambrose would take the creased, greasy, human-warmed paper in his hand and see his own river gleaming up at him, his own bridge, his own fine lines, and feel the transaction close like a small cold hand around his throat.

Today it was a five-franc note, returned as part of the coal merchant's settlement. Someone had written on it in pencil, faintly, across the vignette of the bridge: *worthless.* Just that. No anger in the hand. A kind of tiredness. The handwriting of a man who had done arithmetic by lamplight and reached the end of what his wages meant.

Ambrose looked at it a long time. Then he fed it into the stove, because keeping defaced currency was the kind of evidence that got workshops searched, and watched his river curl and blacken and go up the flue.

"You could refuse the commission."

Elodie. She had come to the stove to warm her hands, and she was looking at the fire, not at him, and her voice was level in the way hers was level—she had her mother's levelness, God keep her mother's memory—and only someone who had known her since birth would have heard underneath it the thing that was not level at all.

"The commission keeps the roof over your head," Ambrose said.

"The commission keeps the roof over the *press's* head. There's a difference, and I think you know it."

He turned from the stove. His daughter stood with her sleeves pushed back and ink at the edge of her right jaw where she always got it, a small crescent moon of black, and she looked at him with her mother's eyes and waited, and he understood suddenly that she was not arguing. She was offering him a door. She was standing beside a door and holding it open and asking him, without asking, whether he meant to walk through it, and if he walked through it she would walk with him, and if he did not—

If he did not, she would go through alone. He saw that too. He had spent nineteen years learning to read this child the way he read proofs, under raking light, hunting flaws, and there was no flaw in her resolve. There never had been. It was the one impression he had ever pulled that came out perfect on the first pull.

"The press is the family," he said. "You protect the family by keeping it alive."

"And what does the family protect?" she asked. "Papa. What does it *protect?*"

She left the question in the room the way one leaves a form in the press—locked in the chase, under full pressure—and went back to her stone, and Ambrose stood alone at the dying fire with the answer he had carried for thirty years going soft in his hands.

Nothing. It protected nothing. It preserved itself. It was a wheel that turned to keep turning, printing the faces of ministers on paper that bought bread today and would buy ash by spring, and the wheel turned because Ambrose Sterling turned it, and the turning was called survival, and survival was called justice, and the calling of things by gentler names was the last trade he had mastered.

* * *

That night he could not sleep, and so he did what he always did on such nights: he went down to the workshop and lit one lamp and took out the finest thing he had ever cut.

It was not the banknote vignette. It was not any commissioned work at all. It was a face—a woman's face, his wife's, cut in steel the winter after she died, when his hands needed work that grief could not spoil and his heart needed work that grief could not reach. He had cut her at twenty-six, from memory, from the inside of his own eyelids, and the plate lived wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the locked case, and some nights he inked it and pulled a single proof and looked at her until the lamp burned low.

Tonight he pulled the proof and looked at her and she looked back with the patience of the dead, and he said aloud, to the empty workshop, in a voice gone rusty with disuse:

"I know what you'd say."

What she'd say was what Elodie said. What she'd say was *what does it protect.* She had married a man who believed in small survivals and she had died believing in larger ones, or perhaps she had simply died, but the distinction had been eating him for nineteen years and showed no sign of digesting.

He put the proof away. He wrapped the plate. He locked the case.

And then, because the night was long and guilt is a compositor that never stops setting, he found himself doing the other thing he did on bad nights: walking the workshop, touching the machines, taking inventory of everything the confiscation would take. Because he knew—it had been clear to him since autumn, clearer every week—that the day was coming when the garrison would stop needing his press and start wanting it gone. Regimes in their death throes do not leave witnesses to their signatures. Every note he printed was a confession with a date on it. When the thaw came and the bridge opened and the reinforcements marched in, there would be an audit, and the audit would be conducted by men with bayonets, and the ledger of his compromises would be read aloud in the only court that still functioned, which was the court of force.

Unless.

He stopped at the window. Below, the river moved under its ice with a sound like slow breathing. On the far bank, a light moved in the garrison outpost—one lantern, pacing, a sentry's circuit, patient as a clock.

And there—what was that? Down the lane, along the shadowed side of the bakery, a figure moving away from his own workshop's delivery door. Small. Quick. Gone between one blink and the next, leaving nothing but the door's latch sitting a finger's width less true than he had left it.

Ambrose stood very still.

He thought of Elodie's careful tread on the stairs. Of the lamp-oil that went faster than the ledgers said it should. Of paper counted twice and coming out different both times. Of his daughter's question hanging in the dark of the afternoon like a form locked in the chase: *what does it protect?*

He was not a fool. He had known for weeks—had known and had chosen, with the same exquisite cowardice he brought to everything now, not to know. Whatever she was cutting after midnight, whoever she was feeding it to, it was the opposite of his banknotes: it was the same press, the same ink, the same trade, pointed the other way. And every night he had descended to his bed and left the stair unbarred and told himself that a father's blindness was a kindness, when he knew—I have always known, he admitted now, to the lamp, to the dead, to the river breathing under the ice—I have always known exactly what my house contains, and I have let it grow there like a second press, hidden beneath the floorboards of my own conscience.

A small man survives. That was the creed. But his daughter was not a small woman, and his apprentice was not a small boy, and the creed had been cut in a smaller age, and the ice on the river was beginning, ever so slightly, to groan.

Ambrose turned up the lamp and sat down at his desk and drew a fresh sheet toward him—not the ledger, not the garrison accounts—and began, in his small exact hand, to write.

Not a confession. Not yet. A record. Names, dates, places—the carter's account of Verron, the widow Aubry's son, the patrol, the granary, the bodies in the road—set down the way he set all his best work: slowly, exactly, each fact justified flush to the margin of the truth. If the audit came, let it find this. If the bayonets came, let them find that the old man had kept, all along, in the locked case of his skull, a second edition.

He wrote until the lamp guttered. And as he blotted the final line, he heard—faint, from above, from behind Elodie's door—the unmistakable whisper of a composing stick being laid down, and then silence, and then the house settling into its lies around him like snow.
