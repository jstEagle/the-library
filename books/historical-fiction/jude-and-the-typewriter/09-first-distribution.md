## 9. First Distribution

The pamphlets came off the press on a Tuesday, in the hour before dawn, when the workshop smelled of tallow and wet paper and the river fog had crept so far inland that even the type felt damp to the touch. We printed two hundred copies. Two hundred. I had set the forms myself, letter by letter, and when Elodie pulled the lever for the last time and the screw gave its long satisfied groan, neither of us spoke. The silence had the quality of a held breath.

They lay stacked on the stone slab by the window — two hundred slim quartos, eight pages each, the ink still sharp enough to sting. *A True Account of What Was Done at Millbrook, Vasseau, and the Ford at Saint-Remy.* That was the title I had set, and I remember how my hands shook setting it, because titles are promises, and I had never before made one so large. Inside were the names. Forty-one names in all, set in the small pearl type Ambrose had cut for a prayer book twenty years ago and never used, as though his hands had known, all along, that someday we would need letters fine enough to hold grief without breaking it.

Elodie counted them twice, her finger moving down the stack like a woman counting rosary beads. Then she wrapped twelve copies in oilcloth and buried the bundle in the flour barrel, six more beneath the kindling, and the rest went back under the floorboards, into the dark seam of the house where our other secrets lived.

"Now we wait," she said.

I had never been good at waiting. I am worse at it now, if anything, because I have since learned what waiting costs.

* * *

The plan was Tomas's, though Tomas did not know it yet. He is the baker's son — fourteen, freckled, with a laugh like a cart going over cobbles — and every morning but the Sabbath he drives his father's little gray mare on her rounds: the mill first, then the farms along the north road, then the cottages past the churchyard. Bread goes everywhere in a town. Bread is the one thing no soldier thinks to search, because bread is innocent, bread is common, bread is life, and men who have spent a season burning granaries do not like to be reminded of what bread means.

It was Elodie who saw it. She had watched Tomas's route for three days from the upstairs window, a spyglass of her father's balanced on the sill, and when she finally told me, she did it in the manner she had for everything since the night I found her floorboards open: plainly, without decoration, as if she were reading a manifest.

"He stops at the well on the green to water the mare," she said. "He lingers. Ten minutes, sometimes fifteen. His father thinks he's flirting with the Corbet girls. He'd take payment for delivery. A coin, maybe two. He won't ask what he's carrying."

"And if he does ask?"

"Then we choose someone else." She looked at me across the workbench, and the lamplight made small copper fires in her eyes. "Jude. There is no version of this where we know everything beforehand. That's what it means to do it at all."

I want to record, honestly, what I felt in that moment, because honesty is the whole point of the thing we printed. I felt sick. Not with doubt exactly — the doubt had been settled somewhere in the weeks since I found her at the hidden press, settled the way a bone sets, badly but firmly — but with a physical dread, a cold weight behind my breastbone, like lead like the sort we kept in ingots by the casting bench. Somewhere between the moment Elodie slid those floorboards aside and the moment I set the forty-first name, I had crossed a line that could not be uncrossed, and my body knew it even while my mind pretended to deliberate. If Tomas talked — and boys talk; boys are made of talking — then the garrison would come for the workshop, and for Ambrose, and for me. My hands, which could pull a perfect page out of a form faster than any apprentice in the valley, would be worth nothing at the end of a rope.

And yet underneath the fear, in the cellar of myself where I keep the things I don't say, there was something else. Something that felt disgracefully like eagerness. Like a man who has spent his whole life looking through a window and has just discovered the window opens.

* * *

We approached him on the Thursday. It was Elodie who did the talking — of course it was; I stood three steps back holding a sack of day-old loaves like a prop, feeling absurd — and she did it in the yard behind the bakery, in the blue hour after the ovens went cold, with flour dust hanging in the air like the ghost of the morning's work.

Tomas listened. He looked at the sample copy Elodie let him read for exactly as long as it took him to reach the fourth name on the list — the Delacour boy, the one who used to fish off the bridge pilings — and then he closed the pamphlet and stared at the ground for a long time. When he looked up, his freckles stood out stark against skin gone pale.

"The sergeant at the bridge," he said. "He buys rolls from us. Every second day."

"I know," Elodie said.

"And you want me to carry these past him."

"In the false bottom of the bread baskets. Under the loaves. He'll never—"

"My da gets whipped for this." It wasn't a question and it wasn't a refusal. It was arithmetic. I recognized it, because I had been doing the same sums for a week.

Elodie did not flinch and did not hurry. "Your da delivered grain to the Vasseau families last autumn," she said quietly, "when the requisitions took theirs. He didn't have to do that either."

I had not known that about the baker. I looked at Tomas and saw the information land on him too, saw him understand that Elodie had been watching more than cart routes from that upstairs window — that she had been watching the town the way her father watches type, sorting its characters, judging which ones will hold together under pressure and which will crumble at the first pull of the lever.

Tomas took the sample copy. He folded it small — smaller than it needed to be, the way a boy hides a love letter — and put it inside his shirt, against his chest.

"Saturday," he said. "Market day. Most carts on the road, most soldiers drunk on their pay." He paused at the yard door. "The Delacour boy taught me to swim," he said, to nobody in particular, and left.

* * *

Saturday came up gray and windless, the fog lying on the river like a sheet over furniture in a shut room. I did not go to market — a compositor's apprentice has no business lingering at wells — but I found reasons to be outdoors anyway, reasons I invented one after another and abandoned just as fast. In the end I told Elodie I was walking to the tannery to collect hide glue, which was even true, or true enough, because the tannery road crosses the north road at the stone bridge over the millrace, and from there a man can see the green, and the well, and the whole first leg of Tomas's route.

I have set thousands of words about other people's lives. Wedding announcements. Funeral notices. The price of hops. I have arranged other people's joys and sorrows into neat columns and locked them into formes and never once thought of them as real, because they belonged to the category of things that happen elsewhere, to others, beyond the valley, beyond the reach of my ink-stained fingers. I was a tradesman. I made objects. What people did with the objects was their affair.

Tomas's cart came down the north road a little after nine, the gray mare stepping carefully in the mist, the bread baskets riding behind, and I watched — from a distance I chose deliberately, the distance of a coward or a conspirator, I still haven't decided which — as he drew up at the well and began handing round the morning's loaves.

There were soldiers at the green. Two of them, off duty, lounging against the horse trough with their shakos pushed back, and my heart went into my throat and stayed there while Tomas watered the mare and joked with the farrier's boy and passed out bread with both hands, easy as Sunday, easy as anything. One of the soldiers bought a roll. I watched the transaction the way you watch a lit fuse. The soldier bit the roll, chewed, laughed at something, tossed a coin. Tomas laughed back.

And then, in the space between one customer and the next, Tomas bent to tighten a harness buckle that did not need tightening, and a woman came out of the cottage by the churchyard wall — the Perrin cottage, old Madame Perrin, whose son was one of the names, number nineteen on my list, *Antonin Perrin, laborer, taken from the ford at Saint-Remy, October the twelfth* — and Tomas handed her a loaf.

She carried it inside. That was all. She carried it inside, and the door closed, and the world continued exactly as before, and I stood on the tannery road with hide glue I didn't need in my bag and understood that the revolution I had been imagining in grand and terrible terms was, in fact, a loaf of bread crossing a distance of thirty feet.

* * *

I should have gone home then. Any sensible man would have gone home. But the route ran on, and the pamphlets rode in those baskets like coals, and I followed — not openly, not close, but the way a shadow follows, along hedgerows and back lanes, telling myself at each crossroads that this was the last field, and lying.

It was at the Ferreol farm, past the millrace, that I stopped being able to keep the distance.

Gaspard Ferreol is sixty if he is a day, a widower with hands like tree roots, a man who has said perhaps four hundred words in the eleven years I have lived in this town, half of them prices. His farm borders the requisition road, and it was his nephew who was taken at Vasseau — not taken, I correct myself, because the pamphlet taught me the difference — killed at Vasseau, in the lane, in front of the church, in the month of the first frost. The family was made to sign a paper saying otherwise. Gaspard signed it. Everyone signs such papers now; that is how the erasure works, that is why Elodie cuts type by candlelight.

Tomas handed him the loaf at his gate, took his coin, rattled off down the lane toward the last cottages. And Gaspard Ferreol stood at his gate weighing that loaf in his hand — and I saw it, I saw the exact moment — he felt the wrongness of it. Too heavy. Or folded strangely. Some small treason of geometry. He looked up the road after the cart, then down it, and then he stepped behind the gatepost, into the shelter of the hedge, and opened the bread with his root-hard fingers, gently, the way you'd open a bird's egg.

I was forty yards away, flat against a hawthorn like an idiot, close enough to see his face and far enough to see it whole, and what I saw has rearranged something in me that I do not think will ever settle back into its old shape.

He read it standing up. His lips moved — I could see them move, shaping the words, shaping *my* words, the ones I had set at the case at midnight with a candle guttering and Elodie reading the widow's list aloud in a voice that never once broke until it was finished. He read the account of Vasseau. He read the sentence with his nephew's name in it. And then this enormous, silent, rooted man — this man who had signed their lie with his own hand — sat down. Right there, on the ground beside his gate, in the mud, in February, with the loaf-wrapper open on his knees, and he wept.

Not loudly. That was the terrible part. He wept the way old men weep, almost invisibly, shoulders shaking once or twice, a hand pressed flat over his mouth as though to keep the sound of it in, as though grief were another thing the requisition might confiscate. And I stood behind my hawthorn with my heart hammering and did not go to him. I wanted to. God knows I wanted to. But what could I say? *I made that? Those are my letters?* It would have been obscene. It would have been like interrupting a prayer to claim credit for the church.

So I stayed hidden, and I watched, and I understood — slowly, the way ice understands the river — that I had been wrong my entire life about what my trade was.

I thought I made objects. Quires. Forms. Impressions on paper. I thought history was made elsewhere, by great men in great rooms, and that we here in the valley merely received it, the way fields receive weather. But Gaspard Ferreol was sitting in the mud at his gate weeping over *my composition*, over a paragraph I had justified by hand, over a hyphen I had chosen, and there was no distance in it at all. There was no *elsewhere*. The words had traveled thirty feet from a bread basket into a broken man's hands, and they had done what armies cannot do and laws refuse to do: they had told him he had not imagined it. They had said to him, in Ambrose's finest pearl type, *your nephew existed, and what happened to him happened, and you are not mad, and you are not alone.*

Memory can be typeset. I don't know how else to say it. It can be sorted and spaced and locked into a forme and pulled on a press and made permanent, made portable, made *multiple* — and once it is out in the world it cannot be unreprinted, no matter how many signatures they force, no matter how many pages they burn. The widow knew it. Elodie knew it. And now, kneeling in the wet grass behind a hawthorn forty yards from a weeping farmer, I finally knew it too: I had never been outside history. I had only been waiting for someone to hand me the type.

* * *

I got home before noon. Elodie was at the case, sorting sorts, the small click and slide of metal the only sound in the workshop, and Ambrose was out — called to the garrison, again, to print their proclamations, again, and neither of us had said aloud what that arrangement meant, what it made of us, what it would cost.

I hung up my coat. I picked up my composing stick. And then I set it down again, because I found I could not hold it without saying it.

"He read it," I said. "Ferreol. At his gate. He sat down in the mud and he wept."

Elodie's hands stilled over the type case. She didn't look up right away, and when she did, her eyes were wet — not crying, just wet, the way metal sweats — and she blinked once, hard, and returned to her sorting.

"Two hundred copies," she said. "One down."

But her voice wasn't steady, and we both heard it, and neither of us minded.

That evening the fog lifted off the river for the first time in a week, and from the workshop door I watched the water run black and quick under the lamplight, carrying ice downstream toward the low country, toward the capital, toward everywhere. The pamphlets were out in the town now, folded inside loaves on shelves and in cupboards and under beds, multiplying in the dark like seeds. By Sunday, people would talk. By Monday, someone would talk to the wrong person.

I fell asleep thinking about hyphens — about the one I'd placed in the Vasseau account, breaking *irreparable* across a line, and whether I'd chosen rightly — and woke near midnight to a sound below my window: footsteps in the yard, soft and many, and the low murmur of voices, and then a single knock at the workshop door, measured and official, like a lever coming down.

Lantern light moved against the shutters.

"Open, in the name of the garrison."
