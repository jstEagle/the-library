## 10. The Raid That Missed

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, and it was not a customer's knock.

Elodie knew the difference the way she knew the difference between papers — by weight, by texture, by something felt in the wrist before the mind could name it. Customers knocked twice, apologetically, and waited. This fist struck four times, then four again, before any answer could be given, as though the door were already condemned and they were merely informing it.

She was at the stone sink washing ink from her fingers when the sound came through the workshop's back wall. The water went cold around her hands — or perhaps her hands went cold around the water. Across the yard, in the composing room, she heard her father's knife stop mid-stroke on the pearwood, and she heard Jude's stool scrape back from the stone table, and for one full second the workshop held its breath the way the press held its breath at the top of the pull, that instant of suspension before all the weight came down.

"Through here, Papa," she called, and was proud that her voice came out level. "A customer."

Her father appeared in the doorway wiping his hands on his apron, and behind him Jude, and the three of them stood in the passage between the shop and the house like figures set in a chase, locked and waiting for the impression. Ambrose Sterling had a face built for composure — forty years of telling merchants that no, the job would not be ready by Tuesday, and yes, the price was the price — but Elodie saw his eyes go once, quickly, to the floorboards beneath which two hundred pamphlets lay in oilcloth, and she saw Jude see it too, and she prayed to a God she had stopped consulting some years past that neither of their faces did what their eyes had done.

The soldiers did not wait for the door to be opened politely. It swung inward under a gloved hand and the morning came in with them, all grey light and boot-leather and the smell of wet wool and gun oil, six of them and then a seventh, an officer with a paper in his hand and an expression like a man reading someone else's laundry list.

"Ambrose Sterling." He did not make it a question. "Printer."

"I am a printer," her father said, which was not quite the same thing, and Elodie loved him for the distinction even as her stomach turned over.

"You are suspected of printing matter seditious and unlawful. By order of the garrison commandant, these premises will be searched." The officer folded his paper along its crease, once, precisely, and Elodie thought: *he has handled documents all his life; he folds like a compositor.* Then she thought: *they have a document. Someone wrote down our name.* And the cold that had lived in her hands moved up into her chest and settled there, permanent as a cast-metal slug.

* * *

They began with the front of the house, because men who search begin where they enter; that was the first mercy and she took inventory of it the way she took inventory of type, counting, sorting, calculating what remained.

Her father offered tea. It was such a small, human maneuver — the courtesy of a tradesman, the stalling of a man who needed ten minutes he could not ask for — and the officer accepted with a nod that cost him nothing, and two soldiers were detailed to the kettle while the rest spread through the workshop like ink through damp paper.

Elodie stationed herself at her father's side and made herself useful and invisible in the same motion: fetching cups, answering questions about stock and orders in the flat voice of a clerk. *How many presses? Two, sir, and a handpress older than I am. What do you print? Whatever is commissioned, sir. Ledgers. Bills of lading. Proclamations, lately, for the garrison.* Every word true. Truth was the best packing material; she had learned that long ago. A searcher digs through lies because lies smell of disturbance. Truth lies flat and lets itself be stepped on.

Jude, she saw, had gone back to the stone table. She nearly gasped aloud at the audacity of it — a boy of nineteen, setting type with six soldiers in the room — until she understood what he was doing. There was a commission half-finished on the stone, the garrison's own proclamation, *BY ORDER OF THE COMMANDANT*, and Jude was setting it, calmly, letter by letter, in plain sight, his hands steady as a surgeon's. He had made himself a piece of furniture. He had made the work itself a shield. *We are the men printing your master's words,* his bent head said. *Look elsewhere.*

She could have kissed him. She filed the impulse away with everything else that could not be afforded this morning, and poured the tea.

* * *

They found the trapdoor to the cellar within twenty minutes, because cellars are found by anyone who walks a floor and listens for the hollow note. Two soldiers went down with lanterns, and Elodie listened to their boots cross the earth floor above the old cistern where the real contraband was not, and she performed in her head the arithmetic of the oilcloth bundles and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, how close everything sat to everything else.

Because the plates were not in the cellar. The plates — the stereotype plates for the record of the massacres, cast from her own night compositions, each one a page of names and dates and methods, the whole terrible ledger of what the garrison had done at Millbrook Ford and the quarry road — those lay directly beneath the floor of the composing room, under the very stone where Jude now worked, wrapped in oilcloth and tallow-paper against damp. She had chosen the spot months ago for exactly the reasons that now made her sick: it was the warmest place, dry, safe from frost-heave; it was beneath the heavy stone table no two men could shift without an hour and a crowbar; and it was the last place a searcher would look because it was the first place, and men do not dig where they stand.

But the pamphlets, the printed run from the February pressing — two hundred of them, waiting for distribution — those were in the cellar, in the cistern, under a false bottom of rubble and broken flagstone, and if a soldier's boot happened to kick the wrong stone, or a lantern happened to swing its light into the right shadow —

She counted boots on the ceiling of her hearing. Down there: two men, moving slowly, commenting to each other in the bored cadence of men who expect to find nothing. Above: one at the window casements, tapping the shutters for hidden cavities. One going through the type racks, running his fingers along the cases the way she ran hers, and she watched him touch the case — *her father's finest cut, the eight-point roman with the hairline serifs, the face she had used for every secret page* — and she felt her heart stop and start like a press jamming on a sheet.

He drew a specimen. Slid a composing stick along the rack, pulled out a few sorts, held them to the light. Anyone might; apprentices did it idly; it meant nothing. Except that a man who knew letters might notice the wear pattern, might notice that this beautiful face showed the particular polish of heavy use on a body of text far longer than any commission in the shop's book —

"Good steel," the soldier said to no one, and put the sorts back wrong-side up, and moved on.

She breathed again. Somewhere in the walls of her fear she noted, coolly, the way one notes a technical problem: *the wear on the case is evidence. If they ever look properly, the case convicts us.* Another thing to fix. Another screw to tighten.

* * *

In the cellar, a shout.

Her whole body turned to lead — the metal, not the weight; molten, not heavy — and she was crossing the kitchen before she knew she had decided to, her father's hand catching her arm and holding, hard, hissing *"Steady"* through his teeth, and she stood in the doorway with the teapot still in her grip and watched the lantern-light climb the cellar stairs and a soldier come up flushed and triumphant with something in his fist.

Bread. A heel of black bread, three days stale, wrapped in a rag.

"Hidden food, sir. In the wall."

The officer looked at it. Looked at Ambrose. "Why is bread in your wall, printer?"

"Because my daughter bakes more than we eat and less than we want," her father said, "and because I am a miser of sixty-one years, and because rats take an unguarded loaf faster than a soldier takes a drink. Search the rest of my walls and you'll find worse sins. Onions. A ham at Christmas, what's left of it."

It was so perfectly the truth — because Elodie *did* hide bread in walls, everyone in town hid bread in walls, it was what you did with surplus in a valley that remembered famine — that the officer could not disbelieve it, and disbelief was what he wanted anyway, wanting his tip to be wrong or right and finding only a household. He waved the soldier back down. The moment closed over like water over a dropped coin.

But Elodie stood very still with the teapot and thought: *the tip said sedition. The tip was specific enough to bring seven men and a warrant. And whoever gave it did not know where the plates were — or the pamphlets — or they would have said so, and we would be finished.* Whoever informed had known *something*: enough to say *search the Sterlings*. Not enough to say *where*.

She counted the town's faces behind her eyes like sorts in a case, sliding each into the stick, testing the fit. The baker who collected the pamphlet from inside the flour sack. The widow Verret, whose grief was public and therefore above suspicion, or was grief ever an alibi? The ferryman. The schoolmaster. The boys who carried bread deliveries — the delivery boys, who went into every house, who saw everything, who were paid in coins small enough not to be noticed and bribes large enough to change a boy's winter—

She stopped the thought. Not here. Not with the officer folding and unfolding his paper ten feet away. But the thought did not stop; it simply went quiet, the way a press goes quiet between pulls, all its force gathered and waiting.

* * *

They came last to the composing room, and God arranged the minutes the way a cruel typesetter arranges a line — spaced too wide at the start, jammed tight at the end.

The officer entered. He walked the perimeter. He looked at the framed posters on the wall, garrison work, harmless. He looked at Jude, who did not look up, whose iron kept pulling and setting with the rhythm of a man who had done nothing else his whole life. He looked at the stone table — and here Elodie's blood went thin as wash-water — he put one gloved hand flat upon the stone and leaned on it, testing its weight, and the glove's fingers drummed, once, twice, and beneath his palm, eighteen inches of oak flooring and packed ash below that, lay every plate of the truth.

"Solid," he said.

"My grandfather's mason overbuilt everything," said Ambrose. "I've cursed him for it every time I've had to move it."

And the officer laughed — a short, surprised laugh, the laugh of a man finding a tradesman funny when he had been told to find him guilty — and took his hand off the stone, and Elodie's soul came back into her body through the top of her head.

Then the order came from outside — the sergeant at the door, reporting the street patrol ready, the colonel wanting the search party returned by nine — and the minute broke. The officer gave the workshop one last sweep of professional regret, the look of a fisherman passing a pool he was sure held fish he could not reach. "You'll hear nothing further unless something new arises," he said. "Keep your accounts in order, printer. Paper leaves trails."

"Paper is my life, sir," said her father. "I know exactly where every sheet goes."

They let themselves out. The door closed. The boots faded down the lane. Somewhere a cock crowed, absurdly late, and the river made its low perpetual sound under everything, and the workshop was suddenly, ringing silent.

Jude set down his composing stick. His hands, she saw, were shaking now that it was permitted to shake, fine tremors like a just-cast slug cooling. Her father sat down slowly on the proofing bench and put his face briefly into his clean hand, and Elodie stood in the middle of the room with the cold teapot and felt the whole architecture of her secrecy rearrange itself around a single new fact, cold and certain as a locked form:

*Someone told them.*

Not guessed. Told. Seven men, a warrant, a specific charge — and a search that had missed by minutes and by inches, by a heel of bread and a grandfather's overbuilt stone. Today they had survived luck and good masonry and Jude's bent head. Luck did not issue warrants twice.

She went, alone, to the corner of the composing room, knelt, and pressed her palm to the floorboard over the plates, feeling the faint warmth of the ash below, the solid sleep of the metal, her father's finest type frozen into pages of testimony. *By minutes,* she thought. *By minutes, and next time by nothing.*

There was an informer in this town. Someone who had seen — something, a bundle, a face at the wrong hour, a rumor shaped like a fact — and sold it. And every soul she had trusted now wore, in her mind's eye, the neutral readable face of type, waiting to be sorted, waiting to be read.

Below the floor, the plates lay quiet. Above them, the town went about its morning, and somewhere in it walked a mouth that had priced their lives, and Elodie rose from her knees already composing the question she could not yet ask aloud:

*Who?*

Across the room, Jude caught her eye, and she saw the same question land in him — saw him glance, almost without meaning to, toward the yard door through which the delivery boys came and went with their baskets and their small coins and their knowledge of every house in town.
