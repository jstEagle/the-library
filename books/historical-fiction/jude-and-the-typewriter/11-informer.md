## 11. Informer

The raid had missed by minutes. That was the arithmetic Jude could not stop doing, there in the dark behind his closed eyes: minutes. The width of a kettle coming to boil. The time it took Elodie to braid her hair. Somewhere in that sliver of time lived a man — or a boy, or a woman with a basket of eggs — who had carried a tip to the garrison, and who would carry another one when the luck ran out.

He rose before dawn because sleep had become a country he could no longer enter. The workshop was cold enough that his breath ghosted in the light of the banked stove, and the type cases sat in their long ranks like a congregation at prayer, each compartment holding its little leaden souls. He lit the lamp and turned it low and began, without quite deciding to, an inventory.

Not of type. Of people.

Who knew? That was the first cut of the stick. Who knew that something moved beneath the floorboards of Sterling & Son besides himself and Elodie? Ambrose, certainly, though Jude was no longer certain how much the old man permitted himself to know. And then the circle widened outward like a ripple in standing water. The pamphlets went out in bread. Bread came from Frau Kessler's ovens and left by cart every third morning. The bread deliveries were made by —

Jude set down the burnishing stone he had picked up without seeing it.

Tam.

Tam Vossel, fourteen years old, all elbows and quick apologies, who ran loaves and proofs and galleys up the hill and along the quay, who whistled through the gap in his teeth, whose mother coughed blood into a rag in a single rented room above the cooper's yard. Tam, who since the raid had not come to work. Tam, who had sent word — through whom? Through the cooper's man — that he'd taken a fever.

Jude stood very still in the cold workshop and listened to the river grinding its ice below the window, and understood with a clarity that felt like a blade laid flat against the skin that the fever was a lie, and that it had been told by a frightened child, and that children who lied about fevers had usually been paid for worse lies than that.

* * *

He spent the morning setting stock for the regime proclamations — grain quotas, a curfew extension, the usual gray gruel of authority — and let his hands do the thinking they were trained for while the rest of him circled the problem like a dog around a wound.

The evidence assembled itself with terrible patience.

First: the soldiers on the raid had gone to the floorboards. Not to the press, not to the type racks where contraband could be disguised in plain sight among legitimate faces, but *down*, immediately, as though working from a drawing. Someone had seen the boards lifted. Only three people alive had seen them lifted, and two of them slept under this roof.

Second: the timing. The raid came four days after the first distribution. Four days was exactly one full bread cycle — two runs to the hill quarter, two along the quay. If you wanted to know what was hidden in the loaves, or what the bakery's back room smelled of at five in the morning, you needed someone inside the rhythm of the deliveries. You needed the boy on the cart.

Third — and this was the splinter that worked deepest — Tam had stopped looking at Jude. A week ago the boy would have talked his ear off about a magpie he'd seen fight a crow, about the price of tallow, about anything. Now Tam received his instructions with his chin tucked and his eyes on the cobbles, and a boy who cannot meet your eyes is either ashamed or afraid, and in Jude's experience the two wore the same coat.

There had been money, too. Jude had noticed it without noticing it: new boots on the boy's feet a fortnight past, good ones, stitched double. And the smell of tobacco on him that no fourteen-year-old in this valley could afford. He had catalogued these things as envies, as small mysteries, the way one notes weather. Now they reassembled themselves into testimony.

He said nothing to Elodie over the midday meal of black bread and hard sausage eaten standing at the stone sink. She was deep in the composition of the widow's ledger, her fingers moving among the cases with a tenderness Jude had only ever seen her give to type, and he watched her and thought: *if I tell her, she will go to him herself, and she will be either gentler than I can be or harder than I dare.* He did not yet know which frightened him more.

"I'm walking to the cooper's yard," he said, when the plates were done. "We're out of lamp oil."

Elodie looked up. Her eyes were shadowed underneath; she had been cutting type past midnight again, he was sure of it, though the second run had not resumed since the raid. "Oil comes on Thursdays."

"Then I'll walk on Thursday," Jude said, "and today I'll walk anyway."

She held his look a moment longer than comfort allowed. Whatever she read there, she let him carry it out the door unopened.

* * *

The cooper's yard smelled of oak shavings and the sour-sweet breath of new barrels. Tam's mother rented the corner room above it, and her cough carried down through the floor in the winter months so that the coopers joked grimly that the building itself was consumptive. Jude crossed the yard slowly, nodding to the men at the shaving bench, and found the boy sitting alone on an upturned bucket by the woodpile, mending a strap with numb red fingers, hunched against the wind off the water like a small bird making itself smaller than the cold.

"Tam."

The boy's head came up, and Jude watched the lie arrive before the words did — watched it form behind his eyes, *I was going to come back tomorrow, honest, the fever broke* — and then watched the lie die unfinished, because whatever Tam saw in Jude's face told him it would not be believed, and perhaps had never been believed.

"Mister Marsh." His voice cracked in the middle like green wood in fire.

Jude sat down on the woodpile across from him. It was the wrong posture for an accusation and the right one for a conversation, and he had chosen it deliberately, because his heart was hammering and he did not trust himself to stand over the boy. From here he could see the river between the sheds, the gray ice with its seams of black water, the bridge far downstream wearing its collar of snow, closed, impassable, a fist of timber and iron shutting the valley like a book.

"You've got new boots," Jude said quietly.

Tam looked at the boots as if they had betrayed him personally. His hands stopped on the strap.

"And tobacco you didn't pay for. And a fever that nobody in this yard remembers hearing about." He kept his voice level, almost gentle. Almost. "I set type for a living, Tam. My whole trade is reading letters, and most of what people say is letters too. You've been spelling something out for three weeks and I've just now finished reading it."

The boy's face did something terrible then — it simply opened, all the way, the way young faces still can, and Jude saw the fear in it undressed: not the fear of punishment, which is a clean fear, but the fear of a boy who has done a thing that cannot be undone and has been carrying it alone in a single room with a dying mother and no one to tell.

"I didn't think they'd search the floors," Tam whispered.

* * *

It came out of him in pieces, in the order that shame arranges things rather than the order that truth does.

They had approached him outside Frau Kessler's bakery, a sergeant with a soft voice and a captain's money, six silver thalers pressed into his palm with a warmth that Tam had never once felt from any adult in his life except his mother. All they wanted, the sergeant said, was to know what the Sterlings printed after dark. Just to know. Knowing was not hurting anyone. And Tam — who had watched his mother's medicine cost more each month, who had carried the arithmetic of their survival in his chest since he was eleven — had told himself the same sentence over and over until it sounded true.

Then the raid had come, and the soldiers had gone straight for the floorboards, and Tam had learned in one sickening hour the difference between knowing and hurting.

"They'll come back," he said now, and his teeth were chattering though he sat in his own wool coat in daylight. "The sergeant. He told me next time there'd be more than knowing wanted. He told me—" The strap fell from his hands. "He knows about my mother's medicine. He knows what it costs. Mister Marsh, what do I do?"

And there it was. Jude sat on the frozen woodpile and felt the whole weight of the question settle onto his shoulders with the particular, intimate gravity of type metal — dense, cold, unforgiving of error.

Because here was the thing he had come to understand these past weeks, the thing the pamphlets and the hidden plates and Elodie's midnight ink had been teaching him lesson by lesson: there was no court for this. No magistrate in this valley would hear the case of Tam Vossel, informer, age fourteen, bribed with six thalers and his mother's life. The law was the garrison; the garrison was the crime. If Jude denounced the boy, the boy would hang or be shot or simply disappear into the same ledger of the missing that the widow had brought them, and his mother would cough alone. If Jude forgave him, the sergeant remained in place, patient as ice, and the next tip would not miss by minutes.

There was no law between those outcomes. There was only judgment — his own, made in a cooper's yard on a frozen afternoon, answerable to nothing but the man he would have to continue being afterward.

*I am a tradesman,* he had told himself a hundred times in this valley. *I move letters. I don't make history.* But history, it turned out, was not made only in assemblies and battlefields. It was made here, on an upturned bucket, by a compositor deciding what a frightened boy deserved.

"What does your mother take?" Jude asked. "For the cough."

Tam blinked. "Opium syrup. Cod-liver oil when we can get it. The syrup's — it's nine groschen a bottle now."

"How much did the sergeant give you?"

"Six thalers. For the first time." The boy's ears went scarlet. "There wasn't a second. Yet."

Jude took out his purse. He did not have much; apprentices were paid in coin thin enough to see light through. He counted out two thalers and held them, and the gesture felt enormous and absurd at once — buying a boy's silence with the garrison's own currency, honesty purchased at usury.

"Here is what happens," Jude said. "You'll go back to work tomorrow, and you'll be exactly what you always were, because being watched begins today and you must give them nothing new to see. When the sergeant comes to you again — and he will — you'll come to me before you speak to him. Before. Do you understand the difference that word makes?"

Tam nodded, eyes wide.

"And you'll tell him what I tell you to tell him. Which will be nothing, for as long as nothing can be given. And if the day comes when nothing isn't enough—" Jude stopped. He had walked to the edge of his own sentence and found it dropped off into dark. "That day hasn't come. We'll spend everything we have keeping it from coming."

"Why?" The word burst out of the boy raw. "Why aren't you turning me in? Anyone else would. Why—"

"Because whoever set those plates under the floorboards knew the risk and chose it anyway, and so did I, and we don't get to feed other people to the machine to make our own risk cheaper." Jude heard the words come out of him and recognized, with a strange floating sensation, that they were true — that he believed them, wholly, the way he had never wholly believed anything he had ever composed for the garrison. "Justice that only runs one direction isn't justice, Tam. It's just revenge wearing better boots. And I've spent my life setting other men's words. This—" he pressed the coins into the boy's cold palm and closed the small fingers over them, "—this is the first sentence I've written myself."

Tam was crying now, silently, the way boys cry, furious at their own eyes. He clutched the thalers like a talisman.

"The medicine," Jude said, standing. His joints ached from the cold and from everything else. "That's yours. That part is settled and I won't ask it back, not ever, not even if — it's settled. The rest you owe in truth. To me. Every time."

"Yes, mister Marsh." A whisper.

"Jude."

A wet, astonished little laugh. "Yes, Jude."

Jude walked out of the cooper's yard with the wind at his back and did not feel triumphant. He felt the way a galley feels after the impression is pulled — used, altered, the mirror-image of something now fixed forever. He had held a trial today. He had been its only judge, its whole jury, its entire apparatus of law, and the verdict was already typeset in him, unalterable, and he would carry the page for the rest of his life and could never pull a proof to check it.

*This is what Elodie has known all along,* he thought, crossing toward the workshop as the light went long and coppery over the ice. *This is what she was doing at midnight while I slept. Choosing. Over and over, with no one watching.*

He was halfway home, actually composing in his head the careful, partial truth he would give her — when he saw the smoke.

Not chimney smoke. The workshop chimneys smoked thin and domestic, gray threads. This was a cookfire's worth of it, thick and wrong, rising from behind the print house, and beneath it, faint on the wind, the sound of boots — many boots, in step, on frozen ground.

Soldiers. Coming up the lane from the bridge road. Not searching this time. Not missing by minutes.

Jude began to run.
