## 12. Ambrose Taken

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a knock so much as a verdict delivered in wood.

Jude had been awake already — he was always awake now before the light came, his body still keeping the hours of the secret press run, still tuned to the rhythm of the night's work even when there was no work to do. He lay in the loft above the workshop listening to the river ice groan and shift in the dark like some vast animal turning over in its sleep, and he was thinking about Elodie, which had become his default condition of mind, when the sound arrived: three blows, spaced evenly, struck with the flat of something hard. A rifle butt. He knew it before he knew it. Every boy in the valley knew that sound.

Below him, through the floorboards, he heard Ambrose stir in his room at the back of the shop, heard the creak of the old man's bed, the scrape of his slippers on cold boards. Then Ambrose's voice, steady as a plumb line: "I'm coming. There is no need to break my door."

There never had been any need. That was Ambrose Sterling entire — a man who believed that precision, courtesy, and exactness could hold back anything, even soldiers, even history. Jude dressed with fingers that would not obey him, fumbling buttons he had fastened ten thousand times, and came down the narrow stair into the workshop just as Ambrose lifted the bar on the front door.

The cold that came in with them was nothing beside what came in behind it.

Four soldiers. Jude counted them the way you count stitches in a wound. Four soldiers in garrison grey, their boots leaving slush prints across the clean-swept floor, and behind them the lieutenant from the counter — Captain Voss, the one who had commissioned the proclamations, the one whose lies Jude had set in type line by obedient line — standing in the doorway with his hands folded behind him like a man inspecting a delivery of paper stock that had come up short.

"Ambrose Sterling," Voss said. Not a question. He did not need it to be one.

"You know me," said Ambrose. "You've stood at my counter twice this winter."

"And now I stand here." Voss produced a document, folded in thirds, and did not open it. He held it as one holds a thing already read. "By order of the garrison command, you are detained on charges of treason against the Crown: specifically, the printing and distribution of seditious materials. You will dress warmly. The roads are bad."

Jude heard himself make a sound — half word, half breath — and Voss's eyes moved to him for the first time. They passed over him the way light passes over glass. An apprentice. Nothing. In that instant Jude understood that invisibility could be either a mercy or an indictment, depending entirely on what you did with the minutes that followed.

He stood still. God help him, he stood still.

Ambrose did not argue. That was the thing that unmade Jude later, remembering it — the old man did not protest, did not demand papers, did not invoke the twenty years of currency he had printed with flawless fidelity while lesser men debased their dies. He simply looked around his workshop the way a man looks around a room he suspects he will not see again, and his gaze snagged — Jude saw it catch, saw it hold — on the locked type case beneath the window. For less than a heartbeat. Then it moved on, smooth as a well-set galley.

"Get your coat," Voss said.

"I will get my coat," Ambrose agreed, "and I will also tell these men where they may stand. That corner is where the ink drums live and I'll not have boots on my ink drums." And the astonishing thing, the thing Jude would carry like a splinter under the skin for the rest of his life, was that the soldiers moved. They moved because Ambrose Sterling spoke with the same authority whether ordering type or ordering armed men, and authority, unlike loyalty, asks no permission from its audience.

He put on his coat slowly. He wound his scarf with care. He took his hat from its peg, and then — casually, the gesture of a man tidying — he turned once, fully, toward the workshop, and his eyes made a circuit of the room. Jude watched that circuit and understood it the way one understands a sentence read too quickly and only grasped afterward: *the floorboards. The case. The girl.*

Elodie was upstairs. Thank God, thank every indifferent god, she was upstairs asleep after the long night's work, and the plates were under the floor, and her father was memorizing his own workshop like a man composing his last testament in the language of objects.

"Mind the press," Ambrose said to Jude. His voice was level. Only his eyes were not. They held Jude's for a moment, and in them Jude saw something he had never seen in eleven years of apprenticeship: fear, admitted. Not of the soldiers. Of the unfinished thing in the locked case. Of the pages not yet complete. "Oil the screws before the thaw or the platen will stick. You always forget the left screw."

"Yes, sir."

"That's all, then." He settled his hat. At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and looked out at the street — at the grey morning, the frost-furred roofs, the river running black between its banks of rotting ice — and Jude would have sworn the old man drew one long breath of it, deliberately, the way a compositor takes a measure of type into his hands before committing it to the stone.

Then the door closed, and the bell above it gave its small domestic ring, absurd and obscene, and Ambrose Sterling was gone.

* * *

They did not go quietly, the soldiers. That was the cruelty of it — not the arrest itself but the theater of it. They marched Ambrose up the high street at regulation pace, slow enough to be seen, past the bakery and the cooper's yard and the church where Mrs. Aldous was already sweeping her steps, and Jude followed at a distance he told himself was prudent and knew was cowardly, watching the town watch the old man pass.

Faces at windows. A woman crossing herself. The cooper's boy standing slack-jawed with a hoop iron in his hands. Nobody spoke. Nobody moved. The town that had bought its bread and banknotes and wedding notices from Ambrose Sterling for two decades stood at its windows and let him be taken, and Jude walked among them wearing the same face, the same stillness, complicit in silence now as he had been complicit in type before.

At the corner by the fountain, Voss stopped the column and posted a notice on the board where the market prices usually hung. Jude drifted close enough to read it. It was one of his own proclamations — he recognized the setting, his own spacing, the particular tightness of his justification on the third line — amended in manuscript above the official text: *By order of the garrison: the printer Ambrose Sterling stands accused of treason. Citizens possessing materials bearing this man's imprint are required to surrender them forthwith. Failure to comply constitutes sedition.*

His type. His hands had set those words. Now the words pointed backward down the chain that made them, link by link, to him.

And beneath the proclamation, in smaller characters that Voss must have added himself with a pen, a final line: *The premises of the said Sterling are forfeit to the Crown and shall be seized upon the opening of the bridge.*

Upon the opening of the bridge. Jude read it three times before the arithmetic finished itself in his chest with a sensation like a floor joist giving way. The thaw was coming — days now, perhaps a week; the ice on the river had begun to darken and the eaves ran loud each noon. When the bridge opened, the garrison's reinforcements could cross, and with them would come the seizure. They were not going to leave the workshop alone until spring. They were going to wait, patient as frost, for the river to hand them the key.

He turned and walked home — walked to the workshop, which was home, which had been the whole architecture of his life for eleven years — and the streets felt wrong around him, familiar streets suddenly typeset in a foreign alphabet. The smell of the town was the same: woodsmoke, wet wool, bread. But underneath it now lay another smell, faint and acrid, that he would associate forever with this morning: gun oil and boot leather and the cold iron scent of other people's power.

* * *

Elodie was standing in the middle of the workshop when he came in, and the sight of her stopped him in the doorway.

She was in her nightdress still, shawl clutched at her throat, and she had not lit a lamp. Grey dawn light came through the windows and lay across the presses and the cases and the swept floor, and she stood in the middle of it with her father's absence all around her like water rising. Her face was very pale and very composed, and Jude understood, in the way that he had come to understand her over these winter weeks, that the composition was costing her everything she had.

"They took him," she said. Not a question either. She had heard the bell. She had perhaps heard more than that from the window.

"This morning. Before first light. Voss and four soldiers. Treason." Jude crossed the room and took her shoulders, because his hands decided before he did, and she did not pull away; she stood within his grip like something braced against wind. "Elodie — they didn't search. Did you hear me? They didn't search the shop. Voss had a warrant and a speech and not one soldier touched a case or a floorboard. They're waiting."

"For the bridge." Her voice was flat and clear. "I saw the notice. Seizure upon the opening of the bridge."

"They mean to take everything. The presses, the type—" He stopped, because the inventory of what they meant to take was also the inventory of what they meant to destroy, and the record lived in that inventory: Ambrose's testimony in the locked case, the plates beneath the floor, the whole fragile machinery of truth they had built inside a lawful business like marrow inside a bone. If the garrison seized the workshop, they seized the evidence. Worse — they erased it. All winter the authorities had been erasing the massacres, unpicking memory page by page, and now they would walk into the last room where the account was kept and burn the ledger.

"The final pages aren't done," Elodie said. Her composure cracked then, one hairline fissure straight down the center of her voice. "He hadn't finished dictating. There are names missing — the Marchetti brothers, the ferryman's son, half of Halloran's list — he said he needed two more nights. Two more nights, Jude, and I gave them to him because I thought we had until the thaw, I thought we had *time*—"

"We have time," Jude said, and heard how thin it sounded, a lie printed in eight-point. "We have days. Maybe a week."

"A week." She laughed, and there was nothing of laughter in it. She pulled free of his hands and went to the locked type case and knelt before it and laid both palms flat on its lid, the way you'd lay hands on a sick animal or a sleeping child. "They'll take this. Everything he ever cut. Forty years of punches and matrices — his masterwork among them, the twelve-point he cut the year I was born — melted down or carted off, and the testimony inside it gone with it, and no one the wiser. They won't even know what they've destroyed. That's the worst of it, Jude. Erasure doesn't even require malice. Carelessness will serve."

Jude stood in the grey light and felt the full weight of the morning settle onto him at last. Eleven years he had worked in this room believing it was a shelter — believing that lead and ink were honest trades, that a man who set type for banns and bills and banknotes stood outside the current of events, safe on the bank while history flooded past somewhere else. Even after the night work began, some stubborn part of him had maintained the fiction: *we print pamphlets; the world does its own weather.* Now soldiers had walked into the shelter with the weather on their boots, and the fiction lay dead on the swept floor alongside everything else.

The floor dropping out of everything. That was the sensation, precise as a press impression: the moment the tympan lifts and the paper that seemed supported is revealed to have been held over emptiness all along.

"He knew," Jude said quietly. "When they took him. He looked at the case, Elodie — one look, quick, and then away, and then he told me to oil the left screw. He was telling us something. He knew exactly what they'd come for and exactly what they'd miss."

"Father." She said the word like a key turned in a lock. Then she rose, and the fissure closed, and the woman who had cut rebel type at midnight for months looked at Jude across the body of her father's workshop. "Then we don't have a week. We have whatever time it takes them to decide the thaw is near enough. We finish the record. Tonight. Tomorrow. However long it takes, we finish it, and we get it out of this building, because this building is already theirs — they just haven't collected it yet."

"The final pages are with him," Jude said. "In the garrison. Whatever remains undone, only Ambrose knows how it goes — he was dictating from memory, from his own eyes. Elodie, the rest of the testimony is in the head of a prisoner they're holding for treason."

She was silent. Through the walls came the sound of the river, the ice talking in its sleep, loosening by fractions every day.

"Then someone has to reach him," she said at last.

Jude looked at her, and looked at the notice's ghost still legible in his mind — *failure to comply constitutes sedition* — and understood what she was asking, and understood, with a calm that frightened him more than panic would have, that he was going to say yes. The apprentice who had once insisted he was a tradesman and not a maker of history was already composing the argument he would make to a garrison clerk: a loyal apprentice, petitioning to deliver his master's warm clothing. Who would suspect the messenger who asked permission at the gate?

"There's a procedure for prisoners' effects," he heard himself say. "Blankets. Clothing. Petitioned through the garrison office. I've seen the forms on Voss's counter — we printed half of them ourselves."

Elodie stared at him. Then, slowly, terribly, she smiled — not hope, not yet, but the place hope comes from.

"Jude Marsh," she said, "you terrible, beautiful liar. You've thought of this already."

Outside, far off down the valley, the river boomed like a drum as a field of ice gave way, and neither of them moved, and the thaw had begun.
