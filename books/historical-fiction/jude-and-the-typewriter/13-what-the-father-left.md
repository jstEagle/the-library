## 13. What the Father Left

The workshop was quieter with my father gone than it had ever been with him in it, and I could not have imagined, before the soldiers came, that silence could have a weight — that it could press down on the shoulders like a forme locked tight, every element of it fixed and unyielding. Three days he had been in the garrison house. Three days, and the presses had not turned, because the presses were his breath, his pulse, the metronome by which all our hours had been measured, and without him they sat under their dust sheets like furniture in a dead man's parlor.

I did not weep. There would be time for weeping later, or there would not, and either way the ink had to be cleaned from the rollers before it hardened into stone. Grief is a luxury for people whose presses are not marked for confiscation. I learned that on the first morning, when I came downstairs and found myself checking the floorboards before I checked my own face in the glass.

Jude slept in the loft above the composing room now. He had moved his pallet without being asked, without a word between us on the subject, the way he had begun doing everything since the night he found my hidden run — quietly, completely, as though some part of him had been waiting all his life for permission to be brave and had only needed the excuse of my fear to find it. He kept watch until the small hours. I took the dawn. It was an arrangement made without language, and it held us together better than any promise would have.

On the third evening, after the garrison's patrol had passed — I knew their rhythm now, boots on cobbles at half past six, again at ten, the sergeant's cough like a signature at the corner of Mill Lane — I told Jude I was going to sleep. He believed me, or pretended to, and climbed to the loft with the candle stub he allowed himself. I waited until his breathing slowed into the long even cadence of real exhaustion, and then I went down to the type room with a lamp and my father's keys.

* * *

Ambrose Sterling was not a sentimental man. In thirty years of cutting punches he had thrown away nothing worth keeping — no first attempts, no failures preserved for instruction, no letters tied in ribbon. His desk held ledgers and a whetstone and a tin of licorice pastilles he believed nobody knew about. When my mother died he had sold her piano inside a fortnight and given the money to the parish, and when I asked him why, he said: *Because a thing you cannot use is a thing that uses you.* That was his whole philosophy, pressed flat and polished, and I had lived beneath it my entire life the way one lives beneath a ceiling — unnoticed, structural, assumed.

So it was not sentiment that sent me to the type cases. It was arithmetic.

They had taken his correspondence. The sergeant had been very thorough about the correspondence, turning over papers with the butt of his halberd while two soldiers held my father's arms, and my father — Ambrose, who once broke a boy's fingers for bending a quoin — had stood perfectly still and let them, his eyes moving across the shop in that quick inventorying flicker I knew better than my own reflection. I had thought, in that moment, that he was saying goodbye to his presses. Now, three days later, in the dark, with the lamp guttering and my heart going like a platen, I understood he had not been looking at the presses at all.

He had been counting. And what a man counts, in the last minute he has in his own shop, is the thing he is afraid will be found.

The correspondence was a decoy. My father printed currency for a collapsing regime; he understood better than any man alive that paper's value lies not in itself but in what people believe about it. Let the soldiers read his letters. Let them find complaints about ink prices, a dry note to a cousin in Lyon, invoices for lead. Paper that says nothing protects paper that says everything — this was compositor's wisdom, typesetter's cunning, and he had arranged it the way he arranged everything, with an exactness that would outlast him.

Which meant there was something else. Which meant the something else was still in the shop.

I began with the type cases themselves, because he had been looking at the racks — I was almost sure of it — and because if I were Ambrose Sterling and I had one secret to keep, I would keep it in the one place no searcher would ever think to look twice: among ten thousand identical pieces of metal, sorted by a system only he fully held in his head.

The cases were oak, brass-fitted, each drawer labeled in his cramped engraver's hand. Most of them I knew the contents of blind — the long primer roman, the pica italic, the great primer black that we used only for mourning cards and ballad broadsides. But there was one case I did not know, a small one, deeper than the rest, sitting at the bottom of the rack beneath the window where the light never reached. It had always been there. It had always been locked. As a child I had assumed it held his tools, the gravers and the punch-cutting files, too fine and too sharp for a child's hands, and I had built a whole mythology around it — my father's iron chest, the dragon's hoard of the Sterling trade — until the mythology wore smooth from familiarity and became simply *the locked case*, part of the shop the way the smell of oil was part of the shop, and I stopped seeing it at all.

That is how well he hid things. Not in darkness. In plain sight, worn smooth.

The key ring held eleven keys. The tenth one fit.

* * *

Inside there was no treasure. Inside there was paper.

A manuscript — quarto leaves, folded once, perhaps sixty of them, written not in my father's hand but in several different hands, dictated, I realized, turning the pages with cold fingers, *dictated*, set down by Ambrose himself in his careful clerk's script while other voices spoke through him. The names at the heads of the sections were names I knew. Tomas Weir, ferryman. The Aldous brothers, tenant farmers off the north road. A woman named Marta Kessler whose husband had been taken in October and whose testimony began: *They came at dusk and he was carrying a sack of turnips and they shot him in the dooryard and left him there two days because no one would come near enough to move him.*

I sat down on the floor. I am not ashamed of that. My legs went, and I sat among the dust and the spilled spacers with my back against the type rack, and I read.

It was a record. Not a pamphlet, not an argument — my father had no gift for argument, he was a man of tolerances and alignments — but a ledger of the dead and the disappeared, compiled with a tradesman's terrible precision. Dates. Places. Numbers of soldiers, direction they came from, words spoken, shots fired. Witnesses named, and beside each name, in the margin, a small notation: *will speak*, or *afraid*, or *gone*. He had been gathering it for months. Perhaps longer. Since before the widow Kessler came to me with her list — my list was a tributary of his river, and I had flattered myself that I had started something my father had been building all along, alone, in the evenings, after I went up to bed.

And there, at the bottom of the case, wrapped in a sheet of printer's blotting paper, a single page in his own hand alone. Not testimony. Address.

*To whoever opens this case,* it began, and my eyes blurred, and I wiped them with my wrist and read on.

*If you are reading this, then I am either dead or arrested, and the difference matters less than you would think, because in both cases the work falls to you.*

He wrote the way he cut steel — no wasted stroke, no ornament.

*You will want to burn these pages. I have wanted to burn them every night since November. Understand that I print money for men I despise so that this shop survives, and this shop must survive because these pages must someday be printed, and printing requires a press, and a press requires a shop, and a shop requires a living man to stand behind it. Survival is not cowardice, Elodie. Survival is the frame that holds the type. But hear me: survival is only the frame. It is not the book. Do not let me die having done only the frame.*

*I had meant to finish it. The last section — the events at the mill in December, and the names of the officers — is not yet set down. The witnesses would not come to me while the garrison watched my door, and then it became unsafe for me to go to them. If I am taken, the final pages are lost unless someone finishes what I could not. Find the miller's daughter, Anka, at the tannery lane. She saw. Her account is the keystone; without it the record has a gap exactly the size of the truth, and gaps are what they will use against us. They do not need to erase everything. They need only leave one honest hole, and men will say, you see, the rest is holes as well.*

*Do not come for me. Do not trade yourself for me. I am an old man and a printer, and printers are replaceable; presses are replaceable; what is not replaceable is set down in this case, and its value is realized only when it is in every hand in this town. Memory made permanent. That is the whole craft, girl. Everything else is lead.*

*I am proud of you. I have said it seldom, which is a fault of mine I will not apologize for now, at the end, because apologies are also a kind of waste. You cut finer than I ever did. Finish the book.*

There was no signature. There was his maker's mark instead — the little star-and-serif he struck into every punch he ever finished, the mark I had followed around the edges of type since before I could read.

* * *

I do not know how long I sat on the floor of the type room. The lamp burned low twice and I trimmed the wick without thinking, the way my father taught me when I was seven years old — *thumb and forefinger, never the whole hand, a steady hand is a habit before it is a talent* — and the manuscript lay open on my knees, sixty pages of other people's grief in my father's exact hand, unfinished, missing its final pages, a weapon with the edge unwrought.

Above me, Jude turned over in his sleep. The loft boards spoke.

I thought: *I cannot do this.* I thought it clearly, calmly, almost with relief, the way you think a true sentence that frees you from pretending otherwise. I am twenty-three years old. I have spent my life in this shop cutting matrices and minding my tongue, and the one bold thing I ever did — the midnight runs, the rebel pamphlets — I did in secret, in the dark, telling myself it was small, telling myself it was safe, and now the dark had produced this, this ledger of the massacred, this inheritance heavier than the entire rack of type above my head. My father was in a cell. The soldiers would come back for the shop when the ice went out of the river. Anka the miller's daughter was a name and a lane, and going to her meant walking past the garrison, past informers, into a story that ate fathers and burned shops.

And then I heard his voice, not remembered but *known*, the way you know a pitch by ear: *A steady hand is a habit before it is a talent.* And underneath it, older, from the year of the fire in the drying room when I was twelve and panicked and he put the flames out with sand and then handed me the shovel: *Fear is information, girl, not instruction. Read it. Don't obey it.*

I got up off the floor. My knees cracked like frozen quoins. I wrapped the manuscript in its blotting paper, and then — because I am my father's daughter, because caution was bred into me deeper than courage — I did not put it back in the case. A locked case is a confession to anyone who finds it. Instead I took down the double-pica roman, the case Jude used, the case that would be the first searched if searchers came, and I slid the wrapped pages flat along the underside of the lowest drawer, behind the paper lining, where a hand reaching in for type would touch nothing but lead. Then I relocked the empty inner case and left it where it had always been, worn smooth, invisible.

Then I climbed the ladder to the loft and woke Jude, because the hour was late and the hour was early, both at once, and there was no more time in any direction.

He came up out of sleep the way he did everything now — instantly, silently, one hand finding the candle, his eyes going straight to my face and reading it. He had gotten good at reading faces. Living with liars teaches you the alphabet.

"What is it," he whispered. "What happened."

"I found my father's testament." My voice sounded strange to me, flattened, like a voice heard through a wall. "Sixty pages. Every massacre since October. Names, dates, witnesses. He'd been writing it for months — dictating it, hiding it, printing money for the men committing the crimes so he could stay alive long enough to record them." I made myself say the next part plainly, because plainly was the only way it would survive being said. "It isn't finished. The worst of it — the mill in December, the officers' names — he never got it down. There's a witness, Jude. A girl called Anka, on tannery lane. She's the keystone. Without her account, the record has a hole exactly the shape of the truth, and they'll use the hole to call it all a lie."

Jude sat with that. Outside, far off, the river boomed — the ice shifting, groaning, the thaw beginning somewhere in the darkness upstream, water remembering how to move. He looked toward the sound, and then back at me, and I watched him understand, faster than I had, what it meant.

"The bridge opens soon," he said quietly. "When it opens, the reinforcements come. And the order for confiscation is already signed."

"Yes."

"So we finish it." He said it simply, the way he said *there's ink on your cuff* — a fact of the world requiring action, nothing more. "We finish the record. We find this girl, we get her account, we set the whole thing, and we print it before the soldiers get here." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes that might have been terror wearing courage's clothes. "Elodie. Your father wrote *do not come for him.*"

"I know what he wrote."

"He wrote *finish the book.*"

"That too." I looked down at my hands. They were steady. Of course they were steady; steadiness was a habit before it was a talent, and I had been apprenticed to it my whole life without knowing what it was for. "The final pages exist nowhere but in his head and in that girl's memory. He can't write them from a cell. But paper goes into a prison, Jude. Handbills go into prisons. Printers' boys deliver blankets and bread. If the last pages are to be written, someone has to carry the question in to him — and someone in that tannery lane has to answer it."

Downriver the ice boomed again, louder, a long splintering crack that ran under the floorboards and up through the soles of my feet like a word beginning to be spoken.

Jude reached for his boots.
