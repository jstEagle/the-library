## 14. The Last Pages

The type case sat between us on the workbench like a body at a wake.

Elodie had turned it over three times before she found the catch — a brass lever disguised as a knot of ornamentation along the lower rail, so fine that only a man who had cut metal his whole life would think to hide a lock inside a flourish. Inside, nested in cotton wool that had once wrapped finished matrices, lay six sheets of paper folded to the size of a prayer book. Ambrose's handwriting covered them front and back: small, upright, merciless in its precision, the hand of a man who had spent forty years correcting other people's spacing.

We read them by candlelight because Elodie would not light more than one candle. The garrison had eyes on the street now; she had shown me where they stood, the shadow in the doorway of the cooper's shop across the lane that never seemed to shift even when the weather did.

*They came to Vasseau on the ninth of October,* the first page began. *I have it from the miller's boy, who saw it from the high field, and from Madame Corbet, whose husband did not come back. There were eleven men in the lane behind the church. They had laid down their arms on the priest's word. The soldiers shot them where they knelt and burned the church with the wounded inside it.*

Page by page the account went on. Names. Dates. Descriptions of the second massacre at the ford, the third at the granary outside Perrin's Hollow — places we had heard whispers about, whispers that dissolved like breath in cold air because no one had written anything down, because the garrison's proclamations called those same events *the restoration of order*, and a proclamation printed in ten-point roman carries more weight than any number of trembling voices.

Ambrose had set it all down as testimony. He had signed it. He had dated it. And the last page broke off mid-sentence, the ink trailing into nothing:

*There remains only the matter of the orders themselves, which I saw with my own eyes in the commandant's —*

Nothing after the dash. The paper was creased there, as if he had been interrupted while writing, or as if the page had been torn from beneath his pen.

"He was still finishing it," Elodie said. Her voice had gone very flat, which I had learned was how she carried the heaviest things. "When they took him. He must have been writing it the night before, or that morning. The last pages were never hidden."

"Then they're in the workshop," I said. "He hid everything else here—"

"No." She shook her head slowly. "Jude. Think. If they were here, he'd have told me where. This—" she touched the locked case "—he meant me to find. A man who hides a testament under floorboards doesn't forget to mention the ending. The pages aren't missing from this room. They're missing from him."

I understood then what she was saying, and the understanding went through me like a slug through wet paper.

The garrison had seized Ambrose's correspondence when they arrested him. His letters, his ledgers, his papers — all of it carted off to the fort. If he had been carrying the final pages when they came for him, if he had been writing them up to the very hour, then those pages were sitting now in an officer's strongbox, or worse, already read by men who understood exactly what a written record could do and had built their whole strategy upon preventing one.

Or — and this possibility arrived quietly, the way the worst ones do — Ambrose still held them. Still had them on his person, or knew where they were, and was keeping silent in a cell while the thaw advanced day by day and the ice on the river sang its long cracking song of deadline.

"We need him to finish it," I said. "The record is useless half-made. A testimony without its conclusion is just an accusation, and accusations can be denied. He knows. He *saw*. Whatever it was in the commandant's possession — that's the proof that turns rumor into fact."

"I know."

"And he's behind a wall we can't pass, guarded by soldiers who would shoot us both for standing in the road after curfew."

"I know that too." She closed the case and rested her hands flat upon it, the way she sometimes rested her hands on the press when she was thinking. In the single candle's light her face was all planes and shadows, her father's face younger, and I felt the old ache of admiration that I had stopped pretending wasn't love some weeks ago, somewhere between the first pamphlet and the raid that missed us by minutes.

"There's one way in," she said.

* * *

The prison took deliveries twice a week. We learned this from Berthe Fournier, who washed linens for the garrison and had a brother among the taken at Vasseau and had been slipping us names and fragments since the widow first came to our counter with her list. Berthe came to the back door on Thursday, moving through the fog like something the river had coughed up, and she stood wringing her hands while she talked.

"Bread, blankets, laundry bundles. The prisoners' wives bring things on Tuesday and Friday mornings. It goes through the gatehouse, and a sergeant inspects it, but he inspects it the way sergeants inspect anything — quickly, and thinking of his breakfast." She lowered her voice though there was no one to hear. "But paper is different. Paper is searched for like knives are searched for. There was a woman in March tried to send her husband a letter sewn in a jacket lining. They flogged her in the square."

"Not paper, then," I said. "*Printed* paper."

Berthe frowned. Elodie didn't. I watched the idea land in her the way ideas landed in her — all at once, silently, like a forme locked into the bed of the press.

"A handbill," she said. "An official proclamation. Something with the garrison's own authority on it."

"The sergeant sees a printed bill," I said, warming, the words coming faster than my caution could catch them, "he sees the commandant's seal — we have copies of the seal from the proclamations we printed for them in January, I cut a matrix of it myself without thinking, it's habit, cutting what passes through my hands —"

"You forged the commandant's seal," Berthe said faintly.

"I *replicated* it," I said, and heard how little the distinction was worth, and did not care. That was new. Two months ago I would have lain awake over a miscounted em-quad. Now I stood in a fog-bound alley discussing forgery and felt nothing but the clean burning urgency of a plan assembling itself.

"Here is the thing about print," I went on. "Nobody reads it closely, because nobody expects print to lie about its own authority. Handwriting is suspect. Print is trusted. It's why their proclamations work. It's why ours will work too." I looked at Elodie. "We compose a bill. Some plausible garrison notice — a ration schedule, a sanitation order, whatever reads dullest. And in the composition, worked into the text so no casual eye would ever find it, we put a message to your father."

"What message?" Berthe asked.

"That's the difficulty," Elodie said quietly. "It has to be a message only he would understand, short enough to hide in a notice, and precise enough to tell him what we need. And it has to survive being read by the sergeant, who will read it, because printed bills get posted, not pocketed. It has to look finished."

We stood in the fog a moment, three conspirators and a problem.

Then I thought of the composing stick in my hands, and the thousand hours of it, and something opened in me the way a line opens when you find the right break.

"Type has conventions," I said. "Quirks. Every compositor has them, and every cutter teaches them. Your father taught me mine. He'd know — if he read a bill set in his own faces, in his own house style — that it came from his workshop. From us. Beyond that, I need only a few words. I can hide a sentence in plain sight using the oldest trick in the trade." I smiled, and felt my mouth do it crookedly, unaccustomed. "A printer's error. Deliberate. Certain words set in italic instead of roman, scattered through the notice. Read the italics alone, and you have the message. Read the bill, and it's garrison drivel. Your father proofed copy for thirty years. He'll catch a wrongface word faster than the sergeant will catch a knife in bread."

Elodie stared at me. Then, slowly, the flatness broke and something kindled underneath — the fierce bright thing I had first seen on the night I found her at the midnight press.

"Italic for the true words," she said. "Hide the truth inside the official type."

"That's rather the whole trade lately, isn't it?"

Berthe crossed herself, muttered something about damnation, and agreed to carry the bill in her laundry bundle on Friday morning.

* * *

We composed it that night, and it was the strangest setting of my life.

The notice itself was easy — I lifted a ration adjustment from a genuine bill the garrison had commissioned in February and altered the numbers and dates. Plausible. Dull. The kind of paper a sergeant's eye would slide across like water off oiled stone. *By order of the garrison commandant, weekly flour distributions at the market hall shall proceed henceforth according to district...*

Into it, word by word, I set the trap. *Districts* became *districts*, italic, followed later by *shall* in italic, then *bring*, then *your*, then — working forward through the bill like laying fuse — *final*, *pages*, *finish*, *them*, and near the end, tucked into the closing formula, *through*, *the*, *boy* — because Berthe's nephew delivered laundry to the cell block and could be trusted to linger, and because I could not compress a fuller instruction into the available words.

Read straight, it was bureaucratic nothing. Read by a man hunting wrongface letters, starved and frightened in a cell, it said: *districts shall bring your final pages finish them through the boy.* Not elegant grammar. But Ambrose Sterling had spent his life reading broken copy and mending its meaning; if any imprisoned man in France could reconstruct a sentence out of italic fragments, it was he.

Elodie stood over my shoulder the whole time I set it. Neither of us spoke. The candle burned down. Outside, far off, the river boomed once — a great internal cracking, like the earth clearing its throat — and we both froze, and listened, and went on working. The thaw was coming for us. We could hear it gathering.

When the bill was locked in the chase and the forme was ready, I pulled a single proof on soft paper, and it was perfect, and perfection gave me no comfort at all. Perfect meant convincing. Convincing meant that if it failed, it would fail convincingly, and we would hang beside Ambrose.

"Jude." Elodie's hand on my arm. "If they search the bundle properly — if anyone looks at the bill with a compositor's eye —"

"Then they'll find it."

"Then don't be the one who delivers it."

"Berthe is delivering it."

"And if they take her, they'll ask who set it. And they'll come here." Her fingers tightened. "I'm asking you to consider that we might lose everything tomorrow, and decide tonight whether this is worth it. Because I've decided. But you still had a plan to leave. You still had a bridge to cross. You could burn nothing, change nothing, and walk out in spring the man you were in autumn."

I looked at the proof drying on the stone. At the small italic truths threaded through the official lies. At the signature block where I had set, with unconscious fidelity, the commandant's stolen name in his stolen seal — a man I had never met, whose crimes I had spent weeks typesetting into permanence.

"I was never going to be that man again," I said. "Not after the farmer read my words and wept. You can't unknow that your hands make things that happen in the world. I could cross the bridge in spring with clean hands and they wouldn't feel like mine."

She didn't argue. She kissed me once, briefly, like a seal pressed into wax, and went to fetch the ink for the final pull.

* * *

Friday morning came down gray and hard, the fog thicker than Thursday, smelling of iron. I walked Berthe to the corner of the market square and no further — a man lingering near the prison gate collects attention like shingles collect moss — and I watched her small bent figure diminish toward the gatehouse with the laundry bundle under her arm and my bill folded inside a prisoner's shirt, and I stood there in the cold doing the arithmetic of courage.

Courage, I discovered, is not a thing you summon. I had imagined it as a resource, a store laid up against need, and I had feared I possessed none because my life had never required any. But standing in that square I understood it differently. Courage is simply motion continued past the point where fear says stop. You keep walking. You keep setting type. You do the next small necessary thing, and the next, and somewhere in the accumulation of small necessary things the fear stops governing you — not because it dies, but because your hands are full.

My hands were full. They had been full since the widow laid her list on our counter. Full of lead and ink and other people's names, and there was no putting any of it down now that my father-in-everything-but-blood sat behind two feet of fortress wall with the ending of the truth locked somewhere in his silence.

Berthe passed through the gate. The door swallowed her.

Then I waited, and waiting was the hardest labor of all, harder than any night at the press. I waited through the morning and the afternoon, splitting stock for the big run we could not yet begin, oiling the machines, doing all the thousand preparations of a man who intends to print at scale on short notice, and every hour I did not hear boots on the stair was an hour of grace and an hour of torment together.

At dusk the back gate rattled. I was across the yard before the latch cleared.

Berthe stood in the gateway, pale as the fog, and her face was unreadable — and then it resolved, and it was terror, and it was triumph, and she gripped my sleeve with fingers like talons.

"He sent nothing out," she whispered. "No note, no word. But the boy says — the boy says the old man read the bill once, and his face changed like weather, and he tore the bill apart and ate the pieces."

*Ate them.* I nearly laughed aloud, there in the dark, a wild unsoundable laugh — the old fox, the careful old fox, destroying the evidence in the safest vault in France, his own belly.

"And the boy waited," Berthe went on, "and when he collected the supper bowls, the old man had scratched something into the bottom of the tin with the edge of his spoon. The boy copied it as best he could."

She pressed a scrap of paper into my palm. By the light spilling from the workshop door I made out the scratched capitals, crude but unmistakable, in the hand I knew better than my own:

**ORDERS IN COMMANDANTS OWN SAFE. TELL THE CHILDREN. BRING ME NOTHING MORE.**

Elodie read it over my shoulder, and I felt her go still.

"The safe," she breathed. "The final pages aren't with Father at all. He got them out before the arrest — he hid them in the one place no rebel would ever look. Inside the garrison itself."

And below the fortress wall, far downriver, the ice boomed again — closer now, and longer, a sound like a great door beginning to swing open.
