## 15. Through the Prison Wall

The handbill came back to us folded inside a loaf of bread, which felt like an answer in itself — the garrison's bread, baked in the garrison's ovens, carried out under the arm of a woman who curtsied to no one. I turned it over three times before I believed it. *A Prayer for the Afflicted*, we had titled it, set in a script face so small and so old that only a compositor or a priest would give it a second look. The verses were scripture enough on their surface. Beneath them, hidden in the descenders and ligatures like worms under river stones, ran the true message: a request to Father Ambrose Sterling, printer, for corrections to his forthcoming devotional — and a list of words, ordinary words, that meant other things. *Blanket* meant yes. *Thaw* meant we are coming. *Ink* meant danger.

Jude had cut those letters himself, kneeling at the stone with a graver borrowed from my father's own drawer, his breath held so long I thought he would faint. It was the first thing he had ever designed rather than merely composed, and when he finished he looked at the little plate as though it had been handed to him by someone else.

"It's ugly," he said.

"It's perfect," I told him, because it was. Ugly things pass inspection. Beautiful things get read twice.

The woman who carried it in — Marta Brenner, whose brother was among the missing from Hollow Ford — had a cousin who cooked for the prisoners' ward. The cousin could not carry messages out; her hands were searched at every door. But she could carry in a handbill, leave it among the chapel papers where the prisoners were permitted an hour of devotion each Sunday, and she could wash the blankets of the sick, carrying them home to be mended before returning them.

So we waited for a blanket.

I had never known waiting could have a texture, but it did: it was the texture of type dust in the grain of the floorboards, of cold tallow, of the river's groaning beneath the ice. Three days after the handbill went in, I could not keep my hands still. I sorted case after case that needed no sorting. I wiped the press with a rag gone gray long ago. Jude caught me standing at the window with my arms crossed over my apron like a woman at a graveside, and he did not tease me, which somehow made it worse.

On the fourth evening, Marta Brenner knocked twice, paused, knocked once — our signal, chosen because it sounded like nothing — and put into my arms a blanket so wretched that any soldier glancing at the exchange would have pitied rather than suspected. Gray wool, unraveling at one corner, stained at another with something I chose not to identify. It smelled of lye and fever.

I carried it upstairs as though it weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

We laid it across the worktable and Jude held the candle low while I went through the hem with my fingers, seam by seam, feeling for what should not be there. Wool. More wool. A knot. Then — paper. Folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth, sewn flat against the blanket's inner edge with thread the same color as the wool, stitches so fine they must have taken my father half the night and cost him whatever light reached his cell. He had torn the paper into strips first — three strips, each narrow as a ribbon — so they would lie against the hem without bulking. Even imprisoned, even stripped of his tools and his name, my father thought like a printer. He had engineered his escape the way he engineered a forme: every element considered, every weight distributed, nothing left to chance but chance itself.

I cut the threads with the smallest blade in the shop, the one he used for cleaning out counters in the finest faces, and drew the strips out one at a time. My hands shook. Jude's did not, or he pretended they didn't, holding the light steady while I unfolded each strip and flattened it against the table with the heels of my palms.

The handwriting was my father's, but changed. The letters I knew were built like his type — upright, economical, certain of their proportions. These leaned. They thinned toward the ends of lines where his strength must have failed him. At the top of the first strip, in characters cramped nearly to illegibility:

*Elodie — if this reaches you, the chaplain is honest, the blankets are not searched, and I am prouder than I have ever said. Finish it. The pages follow.*

And then the pages followed. Two of the four missing sections of his testament — his account of what he had seen from the print shop's own counter, what soldiers had told him carelessly over invoices, what the widow Anselm had whispered and the ferryman confirmed, dates and names and places, all in his dry ledger-hand, all the more terrible for being so orderly. He wrote of Hollow Ford the way he wrote of ink viscosity: without adjectives, without mercy, without once raising his voice on the page. It was worse than screaming. Screaming can be dismissed as grief. This could not be dismissed as anything, because it was true, and truth typeset is a different substance than truth spoken. Spoken truth evaporates. Set truth sits in the box waiting.

"Read it aloud," Jude said quietly. "I want to hear how it sounds."

"You'll hear it in your sleep either way."

"All the more reason to choose the words deliberately."

So I read it, there in the workshop where I had grown up among founts and chases, my father's voice rising out of me in his cadences, his pauses, his terrible evenness. When I reached the passage about the millrace at Hollow Ford — eleven men, the miller's two sons among them, driven in at dusk so the bodies would travel downstream and arrive somewhere else, arriving nowhere else — Jude sat down slowly on the composing stone's bench, as if his legs had received new information about the world.

"They'll hang him for less," he said.

"They'll hang him for nothing. They don't need reasons anymore. They need examples."

"Then we finish it fast."

"We finish it *right*," I said, and heard my father in my own mouth, and did not apologize for it.

There was a postscript on the third strip, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*One caution. The lieutenant who questioned me asked not whether I printed treason but HOW I would distribute it. He knows there IS a press. He suspects the town. Pray he does not suspect the town has only one.*

I read that twice, and the second time my mouth went dry, because I remembered the raid that had come within minutes of finding the floorboards, and I remembered that raids do not miss twice by accident, and I remembered the delivery boy Tomas's face the last time he stood at the counter, the way his eyes had gone everywhere except toward us, the way fear looks when it has been paid for.

But that was a fire for another hour. That night, there was only the paper, and the ink, and the work.

* * *

We began the composition of the final testament that same night, though neither of us said aloud that we had begun. Saying it aloud would have made it real, and real things attract attention the way spilled ink attracts flies. Instead Jude banked the stove low and I pulled down the case my father had kept locked since before I could read — the case containing the type he cut in the years before I was born, the face he called his testament fount, though I understood now he had never named it that in hope. He had named it in preparation. It was the finest work of his life, letters like frost ferns, a face that let ten thousand words fit inside a single sheet, and cutting it had consumed two winters and most of his eyesight in his left eye. He had always said a cutter's best face should be reserved for what deserves to last. I had assumed he meant Bibles.

He had meant this.

Setting type in that fount was like writing with a needle. The letters demanded a steadiness I did not always possess at the end of a day already spent setting pamphlets, currency notes, and the garrison's lying proclamations — for we still took the garrison's work, we had no choice, refusing would have been a confession — and some nights my hands betrayed me, transposing letters, dropping sorts, and I would sit with my head in my hands among the little metal voices and hate my own fingers. Jude learned to work beside me without speaking, sliding the correct letter into my hand before I had finished cursing the wrong one, reading my breath the way he read justification, knowing by its rhythm where a line would end.

We worked in the hours between midnight and four, when even the garrison's sentries grew stupid with cold. We worked with shutters closed and candles shielded behind tin. We worked as the ice on the river began, very quietly, to change its mind.

I remember one moment from those nights more clearly than any other. It was past two, and Jude was standing at the stone with a line of my father's prose in his composing stick, and the candlelight caught the sweat at his temple, and his lips were moving as he counted em-quadrate units, and I thought: *this boy who wanted only to leave, who counted the days until the bridge opened, is spending the nights the bridge is closed building a weapon out of my father's alphabet.* And I thought: *when it opens, will he still want to go?* And then I thought nothing at all for a while, because thinking about the bridge opening meant thinking about everything the opening would bring, and there was a limit to what a person could hold in mind and still keep her hands level.

Because the thaw was coming for everyone. The river would open, and with it the road, and the road would bring reinforcements, and reinforcements would bring the confiscation order that was already drafted — we knew it was drafted; the clerk who bought his tobacco from the chandler's wife had seen the list of properties marked with red. The Sterling workshop was second from the top.

Second. Someone above us on that list had fallen first. I lay awake wondering who, and pitying them, and being ashamed of my relief.

* * *

It was the tobacco clerk's wife who brought the warning, five days after the blanket, and she brought it badly, breathless in the doorway with snow in her hair, saying it too loud, saying it in front of the apprentice from the bindery next door who was nobody's fool.

"They're asking at the garrison," she said. "About presses. About who in town owns type beyond what the law allows. There's a survey being drawn. Names."

"Names of owners?" Jude asked carefully.

"Names of owners." She looked at him a long moment, and then at me, and lowered her voice to almost nothing. "And names of boys who run deliveries."

She left. The bindery apprentice left soon after, walking too quickly.

Jude and I stood in the middle of the workshop, in the lamplight, surrounded by the evidence of everything we were: the garrison's proclamations drying on one side, the testament pages locked in their forme on the other, my father's secret case open between them like a wound.

"The survey is a net," I said. "They cast it wide so the guilty move."

"Or it's exactly what it looks like."

"Tomas."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I've been watching him for a week and I keep telling myself I'm wrong, the way you tell yourself the forme isn't loose until the whole chase spills across the floor." I pulled off my apron, more to occupy my hands than anything else. "He knew where the plates were. Not where — but that there were plates. He carried the bread deliveries. He saw what went into which basket. A boy doesn't need to understand a conspiracy to sell one."

"If you're right, then going near him is dangerous."

"If I'm right, then *not* going near him is more dangerous. Every day he carries our baskets is a day he decides again what they're worth." I looked at the forme, at my father's fine frost-fern letters holding two-thirds of the truth of Hollow Ford in their small iron grip, waiting for the final pages that would never come by blanket now, because the garrison was learning, because the walls were closing. "We have maybe half the testament. We can print half. Half the truth, set perfectly, might be enough."

"Might," Jude said.

"Or we get the rest, and print all of it, and it *will* be enough." I heard my own voice go hard and did not soften it. "My father tore his account into ribbons and sewed it into a blanket with a bent needle by the light of whatever they give a traitor to see by. I will not publish it half-finished out of caution. Caution is what killed the men at Hollow Ford — the caution of everyone who watched and said history happens elsewhere."

Jude was quiet. Then he said, "Then someone has to get word to him. Through the wall. Again." He picked up the graver, turned it in his fingers, set it down. "And this time the message has to ask him to risk a second blanket, in a prison where they've started asking questions about presses."

"And someone has to face Tomas before Tomas faces a garrison officer again," I said. "Two doors, both opening onto precipices."

"That's printing," Jude said, with a ghost of my father's dryness. "Every job, two ways it can break."

Outside, far down the valley, the river spoke — a long cracking report, like a shot, like a door. We both turned toward the window. The ice was still whole, white under the moon, but underneath it something had shifted, some vast weight beginning to move, and we stood listening until the sound faded, each of us privately counting what remained.

Three days later, Marta Brenner's cousin did not come to collect the mended blankets. The blankets stayed in the prison laundry. And a corporal appeared at the workshop counter with a requisition for five hundred broadsheets, payment in advance, and instructions that the entire edition be delivered directly to the garrison — *not* released to the town — and he lingered at the door on his way out, looking at our presses the way a man looks at horses he has been ordered to shoot in spring.
