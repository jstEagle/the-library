## 16. The Thaw Begins

The river spoke first.

I heard it before I understood it — a sound beneath the floorboards of my sleep, deep and grinding, like a millstone turning somewhere under the town. I lay in my cot above the workshop and listened to the Verre crack its own spine in the dark, and I knew, before any bell rang or any crier opened his mouth, that winter was ending.

For three months I had prayed for this. All through the frozen weeks after Ambrose was taken — through the interrogations we heard about secondhand, the seized letters, the red wax seal slapped across the workshop's ledger of debts — I had told myself that spring was mercy. That the thaw would come like an army of its own, flooding the valley with water instead of soldiers, and everything the garrison had promised would simply melt away with the ice. Spring was when the river ran again. Spring was when the world outside returned to us.

I had not thought hard enough about what else it would return.

* * *

Elodie was already at the window when I came down the stairs, her shawl around her shoulders though the fire had been banked hours ago and the room held its breath of cold. She did not turn when I entered. Below us, the workshop lay exactly as we had left it after our night's work — the stone cleared, the ink stones wiped, the type distributed back into its cases so that no casual eye would read what we had been composing. Only the locked case sat apart on Ambrose's desk, holding the testament pages sewn into its false bottom, waiting for the rest.

"Listen," she said.

"I hear it."

"The whole river is moving." Her voice was very quiet. "It hasn't made a sound since December."

We stood together at the glass. The Verre ran below the bluff in a long grey curve, and where for months there had been a single unbroken plane of white, there were now seams — black veins opening down the middle of the ice, widening as we watched, or as it seemed to us we watched. The moon was down but the snow still gave light, a bruised blue luminescence, and along those veins the water showed itself, quick and dark and utterly indifferent to everything that had happened on either of its banks.

"The bridge will be passable within the week," I said. "Maybe less."

"Yes."

"And the first thing to cross it won't be merchants."

She didn't answer, which was its own answer. We both knew the order of things. Ice goes out; roads open; garrisons reinforce. The lieutenant-commander who now occupied the customs house had said as much to the town council in February, in that tone of bored courtesy he used for threats: *Come spring, gentlemen, the valley will have proper governance again.* Proper governance. More muskets. And orders — everyone knew there were already orders, written and sealed, waiting only for the river to let them cross — for the seizure of this workshop, the destruction of its presses, and the transport of its master to the capital for trial. Treason trials in the capital were not trials. They were announcements followed by an appointment with the drop.

Ambrose had sent out the final pages more than two weeks ago, sewn into a blanket by a guard whose name we would never know and could never safely learn. The pages were here now, in the locked case. The record was complete. What we lacked was time, and time was flowing past our window faster every hour, running south with the broken ice toward the capital and the soldiers coming north against it.

"We should start tonight," Elodie said. "Set the whole edition. Every page. So that when we're ready, we only have to print."

"It isn't ready to set. You said yourself the last pages changed the order of things — the names, the dates—"

"Then we'll set what can be set, and reorder as we go." At last she turned from the window, and in the half-dark her face had the particular stillness I had learned, over these weeks, to fear and admire in equal measure: the stillness of someone standing in a doorway between rooms, belonging fully to neither. "Jude. Say it plainly. How many days do you think we have?"

I did the arithmetic I had been refusing to do. The ice would clear in five days, six at most. The garrison's relief column would not be far behind the first thaw-crossing — they would have scouts on the far bank already, watching the river the way we were watching it. Once reinforced, the commander would move within a day; he would want the workshop destroyed before word of the pamphlet could outrun him, before a town that had read the truth could become a town prepared to act on it.

"Four," I said. "Perhaps three if they've built pontoons upstream."

Elodie nodded once, as if I had confirmed a figure in a ledger. Then she lit the lamp and went down to the shop, and I followed her into the smell of oil and lead and the small warm universe of the press, and we began.

* * *

Setting type is slow work made slower by fear. Your hands know their business — the eye travels down the copy, the fingers find each character in its compartment without looking, the word assembles itself upside down and backward in the composing stick, and there is a music to it, a tick and click like rain beginning. But fear gets into the hands. Twice that first night I dropped a line and had to redistribute it letter by letter, and each dropped *m* sounded to me like a boot on the stair.

We worked by one candle and a shaded lamp, the shutters closed, the door bar dropped. Outside, all night long, the river talked. It never stopped. The cracking came in reports now, sharp as distant gunfire, and between the reports a long subterranean groaning, the sound of a weight the river had carried for four months finally being put down. I found I could not keep my mind on the copy. The words of the testament — the names of the dead of Martel's field, forty-one of them, the oldest sixty-three years, the youngest eleven; the dates; the plain, terrible inventory Ambrose had dictated through a prison wall — kept dissolving into the sound of the ice, so that setting the sentence *and the bodies were left until the crows* felt like handling the actual cold flesh of the thing.

"Jude." Elodie's voice came low across the shop. "You're shaking."

"I'm fine."

"You've set the same line twice." She crossed to me and gently slid the stick from my hand, read it, and shook her head. "Sit. Ten minutes. Drink something."

There was nothing to drink but melted snow boiled with chicory, bitter as penance. I sat on Ambrose's stool, which still held the shape of him, and watched her work. She set type the way her father had taught me to imagine angels might do it — without haste, without error, the candlelight moving along her jaw and wrists as her hands performed their blind liturgy among the cases. She had been doing this in secret for two years. Two years of midnight composition while her father slept above her, cutting rebel plates beneath the floorboards of the man who printed the regime's currency. I thought of how much courage can accumulate in one small body, silently, the way water accumulates under ice.

"What happens," I asked, "when we finish? After the run. They'll know it was us. There's nowhere the truth can point except back at its printers."

"I know."

"And you're not afraid?"

The stick paused. She looked at me over her shoulder, and there was something almost gentle in her expression, the way one looks at a person asking an honest question whose answer must hurt them.

"I'm afraid all the time," she said. "I have been since before you knew what we were doing here. Fear isn't the question, Jude. The question is what you do while you're afraid. My father printed counterfeit money for men he despised because he told himself survival was the only justice a small man could afford. He was wrong, and he knew he was wrong, and the testament is his correction. Ours has to be finished. That's all. Finish it, and let the fear ride along in your pocket like a stone."

I drank my chicory. I went back to the case. The stone rode along in my pocket, and my hands steadied, mostly, and by dawn we had set eleven pages of what would be twenty-two.

* * *

In the morning the town knew. Of course it knew; a river breaking up makes more noise than a regiment, and by eight o'clock the market street was full of people who had ostensibly come for flour and salt and were actually standing in knots, looking south toward the water, speaking in the lowered voices of people at a wake. I went out for bread — one of us had to appear ordinary, and we had agreed never to be seen together buying supplies, never to give the informer, whoever he was, a shape to describe — and I walked through the crowd listening.

The talk had changed. All winter the town had spoken of the arrests and the massacres in the subjunctive, in whispers, in a grammar of *if it is true* and *they say*. But the blanket had gone out of the prison two weeks ago, and pages had gone out with it, and copies of copies had moved through the valley like smoke finding drafts. Now, in the baker's queue, a carter said aloud, without whispering, "They shot the Vasseur boys on the road and called it banditry. It's in the record. Forty-one names." Nobody hushed him. An old woman beside him said, "My cousin's husband is on that list," and the carter took off his hat, and several others did too, right there in the street, in front of the bakery, in front of the garrison's corporal loitering by the well.

The corporal was very young. He looked at the bare heads and then studied the well-pump with great attention, and I saw something pass over his face that I recognized, having worn it myself not long ago: the discomfort of a man discovering that he is standing on the wrong side of a fact.

So the truth was loose in the town already, incomplete as it was, wearing its borrowed clothes. But incomplete truth has a shelf life shorter than fish. If the full record did not appear soon — all forty-one names, the dates, the places, the officers' signatures copied in facsimile from Ambrose's stolen correspondence, the whole architecture of proof — then the partial version would fade to rumor, and rumor is a currency the garrison can devalue at will. Everything depended on the days between the ice going out and the soldiers crossing. A window measured in heartbeats.

On my way back I passed the bridge road. I couldn't help myself. I stood at the top of the bluff where the road bent down toward the far-spanning timbers, all of them furred with frost going soft and wet, and I looked at the structure I had dreamed about all my life. As a boy I had stood on this same spot imagining the day I would walk across it — the capital beyond, the great shops with their steam presses, history happening everywhere at once, and me arriving late but arriving. The bridge had been a promise then. Now it hung over the blackening river like a drawn bolt, and I understood that I was looking at the barrel of a gun aimed at everything I loved.

A soldier stood at the near end of it. Just one, stamping his feet against the cold, guarding — absurdly — a crossing that no one could use. But he was a signpost. He was the future standing at attention, waiting for the river to finish its work.

* * *

That afternoon, Colonel Vairet's aide crossed on horseback where the shallows had opened early, a stunt calculated to be seen, and posted a notice on the customs-house door: the bridge would reopen to traffic upon formal inspection, expected within the week; upon reopening, the district would receive *reinforcement of civil authority*; all printing offices in the district would submit their presses and stock for registration within ten days of reopening.

Ten days. He was lying, of course, or rather offering a number designed to make the true number feel like mercy. Registration meant seizure. Seizure meant the melting-pot for our type and the axe for our press frame, and worse for anyone found holding the testament pages.

Elodie read the notice in the crowd, her face arranged into the dull civility of a tradesman's daughter reading administrative news. Then she bought onions at the next stall, and salt at the next, and walked home unhurried, and only when the door was barred behind us did she let me see what was underneath.

"Three days," she said. "He'll move in three days after the bridge opens, whatever the paper says. Which means we print on the second night, maybe the third, and distribute before dawn, and be gone or be dead by noon."

"Gone," I said. "Not dead. Say it properly, Elodie. Gone."

She began unpacking the onions, and her hands, I noticed, were perfectly steady.

* * *

But dread does not stay properly said. It sharpened as the week wore on, the way ice crystals grow, along a hundred invisible planes. Every hour had a new edge. The ice boomed at night and I counted the cracks like a man counting shots in a duel he cannot see. The fire needed feeding and I resented the minutes it took. Sleep became a country I visited briefly and illegally. I caught myself doing arithmetic in the margins of my mind — pages remaining, sheets per hour, candles left, distance to the bridge, speed of a relief column on wet roads — arithmetic that always came out just barely sufficient, provided nothing went wrong, provided no informer spoke, provided the guard who smuggled the blanket had not also sold his knowledge, provided the river cooperated, provided, provided.

On the third morning the ice went out all at once.

It happened near dawn, with a sound I will carry to my grave — a vast tearing roar, as if the valley's floor were being pulled up by the roots — and Elodie and I were both at the window before either of us was fully awake, watching the whole grey surface of the Verre heave and fracture and begin to move. Floes the size of barn roofs ground downstream, riding up over one another, splitting, spinning. The river that had been a wall became a flood, and the flood became a road, and the road ran south toward the capital carrying winter away on its back.

By noon the first raftsmen were on the water. By evening, we learned, riders had crossed at the ford upstream. The bridge inspection would happen tomorrow, the miller's boy reported, breathless, at the kitchen door where we had instructed him never to come again and where he came anyway. Soldiers on the far bank were making camp. Torches, he said. Dozens of them. Like a fair.

Elodie stood in the doorway a long time after he'd gone, looking at nothing, her arms folded tight.

"Tomorrow night," she said at last. "We start the run tomorrow night. Whatever isn't set by sundown, we set as we print."

"The last four pages aren't composed—"

"Then we compose them by candlelight with ink on our fingers and God helping us." She turned, and the lamplight caught her eyes, and beneath all the resolve in them I saw, plainly, the fear she carried in her pocket — and something else besides, something that had no name yet but had begun, these last weeks, to look at me whenever I looked at her. "Jude. If this ends badly—"

"It won't."

"If it ends badly, the type case goes in the river. Not the press. The case. Promise me. The faces my father cut — they're the only part of him they can't hang."

I promised her. And then, because the hour demanded it and cowardice had grown too heavy to keep carrying, I did the thing I had been circling for days: I took her hands, ink-stained and cold, and held them, and she did not pull away, and we stood together in the doorway between the shop where the press waited and the stairs where sleep waited, neither of us moving toward either, while outside the freed river ran loud and endless in the dark.

Near midnight, a knock came at the loading door — three slow strikes, pause, one. A signal we had arranged with no one.

Elodie's hand closed hard on my wrist. From somewhere down the alley, through the gap in the shutters, came the orange flicker of torches moving along the bridge road, and a smell of pitch smoke, and voices — too many voices, and boots, many boots, already on our side of the water.
