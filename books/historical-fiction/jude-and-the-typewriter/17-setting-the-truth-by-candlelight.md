## 17. Setting the Truth by Candlelight

The candles were my father's last extravagance, bought in the autumn when he still believed winter would be kind. There were forty of them, tallow and beeswax mixed, packed in straw in a crate beneath the counter, and I had hoarded them the way other people hoard coin — against a darkness I could not yet name. Now the darkness had a name, and it was coming up the valley with the thaw, and so I took out all forty candles and set them burning at once.

The workshop filled with light such as it had never known. Forty flames made forty shadows of every letter case, every press, every hanging apron, and the shadows trembled on the walls like a congregation of thin black ghosts. The smell of tallow rose with the smell of ink and oil and cold iron, and underneath it all, faint as a memory of bread, the ghost of my father's pipe tobacco, which had soaked into the beams over thirty years and would soak into them long after we were gone.

Jude stood in the middle of it all with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, his forearms inked to the wrist, looking around at the blaze of light as though the room were a stranger.

"It looks like a church," he said.

"It looks like what it is," I said. "A place where words are made."

He almost smiled. "That's not what most people would call it."

"Most people have never set type past midnight." I pulled the locked type case from under the floorboards and set it on the stone. My hands knew the ritual by now: the key from its hiding place inside the hollow leg of my father's stool, the small brass lock that turned with a sound like a held breath released. The lid came up. Inside, nested in cotton wool like relics, lay the finest type Ambrose Sterling ever cut.

* * *

You should understand what that type was, because I did not fully understand it myself until that night.

My father cut letters the way other men prayed — slowly, obsessively, in solitude, with an attention that bordered on devotion. Most of his faces were honest workmanlike things, built for ledgers and proclamations and the town's annual almanac. But there was one face he kept apart, cut over three winters in the years before I was born, for reasons he never explained and I never thought to ask. It was smaller than any commercial size, delicate as insect wings, each serif sharpened to a point fine enough to catch candlelight and hold it. He called it only *the small work*. When I was a child I used to think he meant the type itself was small. It was only now, lifting the first tray free, that I understood: he had named it for what it was *for*. Small work. Quiet work. Work that must fit inside a seam of a coat, inside a loaf of bread, inside a lie told to a soldier at a checkpoint.

He had cut it for exactly this night, thirty years before it came. Or perhaps he had cut it for nothing at all, simply because his hands demanded perfection even when his purposes were humble. Either way, the letters waited for us like seeds in a drawer.

"We'll need every stick of it," Jude said, running his fingertip along the tray's edge without touching the type. Compositors learn early never to touch the face of a letter; the oils of the skin are enemies of the ink. His finger hovered above them the way a believer's hand hovers above a flame. "The full record. Names, dates, places, witnesses. At this size it will run to sixteen pages if we're lucky. Twenty-four if we're honest."

"We're going to be honest," I said.

"I know." He looked at me across the candlelight. "I'm counting what honesty costs."

* * *

We began at half past nine, though neither of us consulted a clock. Time had stopped being something measured in the workshop; it was something we spent, deliberately, like the candles, knowing the supply was finite.

The manuscript lay open between us on the composing stone: my father's testament, recovered from the locked case, joined now to everything else we had gathered through that terrible winter. The widow Marta's list of the taken, twenty-three names in her shaking hand, corrected and expanded by the testimony of the ferryman's wife. The account of the mill yard, where eleven men had been made to kneel in the snow — I set those names first, because they had been first to die. The testimony of the shepherd boy who had watched the column march prisoners toward the quarry and heard the shots come back alone. The dates. Always the dates, because erasure begins with dates; a thing without a date becomes rumor, and rumor can be denied, but a date is a nail driven through the coffin lid of forgetting.

Jude took the left side of the stone and I took the right, and we composed toward each other like two people building a bridge from opposite banks.

There is a rhythm to composition that outsiders never see. To them it looks like tedium — pick, place, pick, place, ten thousand small decisions an hour. But done well, done by two people who know each other's hands, it becomes something closer to music or to breathing. Jude read aloud in a low voice, steady as a metronome, and I reached for letters while he read, my fingers finding their positions in the case by touch alone, and when he paused to turn a page I was already setting the last line of the old one. We had worked side by side for months, but this was different. This was not two apprentices sharing a bench. This was one mind operating four hands.

"The mill yard," he read. "Eleventh of December. In the presence of witnesses Henrik Voss, Anna Kessler—"

"Voss is in the second case," I said. "Bottom row. I've been using him heavily."

Something in the way I said it made him stop. *Using him heavily.* A dead man's name, reduced to a frequency of retrieval. Jude looked down at the galley where the names were assembling themselves line by line, upside down and backward, mirror-writing that would become truth when the press kissed the paper, and I saw his throat move.

"They're just lead," he said quietly. "It's just lead. And it's the most important thing I've ever touched."

"That's what lead is for," I said. "To be heavy enough that a lie can't blow it away."

* * *

The candles burned down. I replaced them in twos and threes without breaking stride, and the wax pooled on the composing stone in little white lakes that hardened as the hours passed, so that by midnight the stone was ringed with tiny frozen moons.

At some hour past midnight — I no longer know which — my father's ghost entered the room.

Not literally. I am my mother's daughter, practical to the bone, and I do not believe the dead walk. But the type itself seemed to summon him. Every time my fingers found the small work — that impossibly fine, impossibly sharp face he had ground and filed and polished in some other winter, alone, for no commission and no payment — I felt his hands over mine, felt the particular pressure of his thumb correcting my grip. *Feel the letter, Elodie. Don't look at it. Your eyes will lie to you; your fingers won't.* He had said that when I was eight years old, standing on a stool to reach the cases, and I had resented it, and I had obeyed it, and tonight my fingers did not lie to me once.

"He'd have hated this," Jude said. He was squaring a page of type, tapping the sides with a block until the lines sat flush. "All this. The risk. He'd have said a printer survives by keeping his head below the type line."

"He printed currency for a regime that murders people," I said. "His head was already above the line. He just refused to look up."

Jude was quiet for a moment. "Do you hate him for it?"

I considered the question honestly, because the night deserved honesty. The candles burned. Somewhere outside, far off, the river groaned as the ice began its long argument with the spring.

"No," I said at last. "I hate that he was right that survival comes first, and wrong that survival is enough. Both things. He taught me the trade and he taught me the compromise, and I get to keep both, and I have to live with both. That's inheritance." I picked a capital V from its row. "He also taught me that a compositor's job is to make the reader forget the letters and remember the words. So that's what we're going to do. Make them forget the letters. Remember the dead."

* * *

Toward the smallest hours, fatigue did something strange to the work. It thinned the wall between the words and the world.

I was setting the account of the quarry road — fourteen prisoners marched out on the twentieth of January, none returned — and the type grew warm under my fingers, or I imagined it did, and each name I placed felt less like metal and more like a hand I was holding. Henrik Voss. Tomas Brandt. Little Pieter Lund, who was seventeen and had been taken for a man twice his age because he'd stolen a coat too big for him. I set Pieter's name and my eyes blurred and I let them blur, because the tears fell on the stone and not the type, and a compositor learns to cry where it does no damage.

Jude noticed anyway. He always noticed. He didn't say anything; he simply reached across the stone and turned the manuscript to the next page himself, reading on in that low level voice, giving me the length of a paragraph to recover, and then we went on. That was the whole of his tenderness and it was enormous. Tenderness, I thought, is mostly timing. Knowing when to speak and when to keep setting type.

Once, near three o'clock, he stopped entirely.

"What?" I said, alarmed.

"Listen."

We listened. The river, groaning. The wind in the chimney. Our own breathing. Then, distant but distinct, boots — a patrol passing along the quay, unhurried, their lantern-light sliding yellow across the frost-furred window glass and away.

"They're walking their own streets like they own them," Jude said when the sound faded.

"They do own them. For now." I nodded at the galley between us, three pages deep now, dense with the small work. "But look at what we own. Fourteen names they wanted nobody to know. Eleven more from the mill yard. Twenty-three from Marta's list. Every one of them set in metal. You can burn paper, Jude. You can break a press. But you cannot unmake type. It's cast. It's permanent. That's the whole secret of our trade and tonight it's the whole secret of the world."

He looked at me for a long moment across the candlelight, and I saw something settle in his face — not resolve, which he'd had since the raid, but something rarer. Recognition. The look of a man who has finally understood what trade he belongs to.

"When this is printed," he said, "every hand in this valley will hold these names. Strangers will read Pieter Lund and feel something they can't explain, because a boy they never met died in a coat too big for him, and we made sure they'd know it."

"Yes."

"And after — whatever happens after — those letters exist. Even if they burn the shop tomorrow. The type could be buried in a field and dug up in fifty years and every name would still be there, sharp as tonight."

"That's what my father would have said," I whispered. "Almost word for word."

* * *

Dawn was a gray threat behind the shutters when we finished the composition. Not the printing — that remained, the press, the ink, the race we both knew was coming — but the setting, the slow sacred labor of it. The last line of the last page went into the forme at twenty minutes past five, and it was a line I had chosen myself, from my father's testament, the sentence he had written nearest the end:

*Let it be set down that these things happened here, in this valley, to these people, whose names follow.*

Jude locked the chase. The quoins tightened with their small decisive clicks, and the forme sat complete on the stone — a slab of type dense as a brick, holding within it every dead man and grieving woman and frightened witness of that winter, locked in metal, ready to become paper.

Then, because we were young and the night had been holy and terrible, Jude put his arms around me, and I let him, and we stood among the guttering candles and the pools of hardened wax and the smell of ink, saying nothing, because there was nothing left to compose.

It was I who broke the silence, because I was watching the window, and the gray beyond the shutters had begun to take on color.

"Jude," I said. "Look at the river."

Through the gap in the shutters, downstream where the current ran fastest, the ice had gone dark and wet-looking, and along its middle a seam had opened — a long black ribbon of open water, widening even as we watched, carrying broken plates of ice seaward like a fleet of small white ships in retreat.

The thaw had begun in earnest. And somewhere upriver, I knew, the garrison's reinforcements were already marching toward the bridge that would soon carry them.

We had perhaps a day. Perhaps less.

Jude followed my gaze, and his arms tightened around me once, and then he stepped back and rolled down his sleeves.

"Then we print tonight," he said. "And God help anyone who tries to stop the presses."
