## 18. The Presses at Midnight

We did not sleep, and there was no pretense of trying.

By nine o'clock the workshop had been stripped and reorganized for war. Elodie moved through it like a general surveying a battlefield she had walked in dreams — pulling the locked type case from beneath the floorboards, carrying Ambrose's finished manuscript pages to the stone composing table where I had learned my letters in lead, lighting every lamp and candle the workshop owned until the room blazed like something about to be launched rather than something about to be lost.

"Read it to me," she said, "one last time. All of it."

So I read. I stood at the high table with my father's master's pages trembling faintly in my hands — not from fear, I told myself, only from the cold we could not afford to fight with fires that might draw eyes to the windows — and I read aloud the record of what had been done at Millbrook Ford and at the chalk quarry and in the lanes behind the church of Saint-Aubin. Names. Dates. Numbers of the taken, numbers of the dead. The testimony of the widow Perrin, set down in her own plain words because Ambrose had refused to polish them. *"They came at dusk and they did not come back for their bread, and no one will say their names, so I am saying them."*

It was not elegant prose. Ambrose had been a cutter of letterforms, not a maker of sentences, and the testament read like what it was: a ledger kept by an honest man in a dishonest season. But as I read it aloud in the lamplight, with the river cracking and groaning beneath the window, I understood for the first time why Elodie had been willing to burn for these pages. Elegance was beside the point. What Ambrose had cut into type over those hidden months was not a pamphlet. It was a wall. It was the thing that would stand between the town and the forgetting, and forgetting, we had learned, was the garrison's truest weapon — sharper than sabres, cheaper than powder.

"It's good," Elodie said quietly, when I finished.

"It's true," I said. "That's better than good."

She almost smiled. Then she pulled the stopper from the ink pot and said, "Compositor. To your station."

* * *

Setting the truth by candlelight is a different trade from setting lies by daylight.

I had set thousands of forms in my seven years at the Sterling workshop — proclamations and price lists, grain tariffs, the colonel's lying notices about the peace and prosperity of the valley — and I had done it with the smooth, half-absent fluency of any tradesman at his work. My hands knew the California case the way a ploughman's hands know the furrow; the eye goes to the word and the fingers go to the letter without the soul being consulted at all.

But tonight the soul was consulted at every letter.

I took up the finest type Ambrose had ever cut — his nonpareil, the face he had spent two winters perfecting and never sold to a single customer because, he told me once, he was saving it — and I began to compose the first page of the record, line by line, word by word, and every name I set felt like a hand closing around mine. *Perrin, Auguste.* I drew the p, the e, the double r, the i, the n, from their compartments and laid them in the composing stick left to right, upside down and backward, the way all truth must be assembled before it can be read. *Perrin, Marguerite.* His wife. She had brought us the list in chapter and verse; her grief was now standing upright in lead in my hand. *Fossard, the brothers Étienne and Luc.* I remembered them — big laughing men who hauled chalk down to the barge in summer and once bought me a cup of cider at the fair when I was twelve years old. Dead at the quarry. Their names went into the stick one letter at a time, and each letter clicked against its neighbor with a small sound like a lock turning.

Behind me, Elodie worked the second case, setting the garrison's own orders — the ones the colonel believed destroyed, the ones her father had copied in secret — and neither of us spoke except to pass words across the room like tools. *Hyphen? No. Break the line after *taken*. It wants the weight.*

The candles burned down. We replaced them. Somewhere past midnight the wind changed and came off the river carrying the smell of open water, and both of us paused at our cases for a moment, listening, because that smell was the sound of a clock.

"How many pages?" she asked.

"Sixteen, if we hold to the nonpareil throughout. Fewer if we drop to brevier for the orders."

"Sixteen." She looked at the press — old Balthazar, the iron hand-press Ambrose had bought before I was born, which had printed currency and catechisms and a thousand small respectable lies and never once, until this winter, anything that mattered. "At four hundred impressions an hour, if we don't foul the forme, if the paper holds, if we take turns at the bar and don't ruin our backs—"

"Then we begin now and stop when the soldiers knock."

"Yes," she said simply. And that was the whole of our strategy, spoken aloud at last between us: we would print until they came, and then we would do whatever remained to be done.

I locked the first forme onto the coffin of the press, quoins tight, and pulled the proof, and held it to the light.

There it was. Black on white. Permanent.

*The True Record of What Was Done in This Valley, November Past to February. Set by J. Marsh and E. Sterling. Let no man say it was not written down.*

* * *

The run began at half past one in the morning.

Elodie took the first watch at the press because her arms were longer accustomed to the pull — she had been running her secret editions alone for months, and it showed in the economy of her motion, the way her body fell into the rhythm of it: dampen the sheet, tip it onto the tympan, fold the frisket down, crank the carriage under the platen, haul the bar, release, lift, lay the wet page on the pile, reach for the next. Pull. Lift. Lay. Reach. Pull. The press breathed like a great animal, sighing and clacking, and the pages accumulated at its side in a stack that grew with a steadiness that felt almost indecent, given everything.

I stood at the case beside her and composed ahead of the press, racing my own formes, breaking up the pages she had already printed and redistributing the type into the next. It is the compositor's curse and gift, to be both creator and destroyer of his own work: the same letters that carry the widow Perrin's grief at two o'clock are broken apart at three to carry the list of the taken at the quarry. Lead does not remember. That is what paper is for.

"You're smiling," Elodie said, on her fiftieth pull or her five hundredth.

"I am not."

"You are. You look like a man who has just gotten away with something enormous."

I considered denying it and didn't. "Do you know what I thought, when I first came here? Seven years ago. I thought I'd learn a trade, and a trade meant a life that stayed put. The world has its history — kings, wars, whatever the capital is doing this month — and I'd have my little lane of it, ink under my nails, and never the two would touch." I spread a fresh line of the nonpareil in the stick. *And on the fourteenth day of December, forty-one souls were marched from the ford and did not return.* "Every letter I set tonight touches it. I can feel it in my fingers. History isn't elsewhere, Elodie. It never was. It's just typeset badly, most of the time, by liars."

"Then we are correcting the proof," she said, and hauled the bar, and another four hundred copies of the truth came one page closer to existing.

Toward three o'clock my turn came at the press and she took the case, and we traded the ache in our arms for the ache in our backs and kept on. The stack rose. Sixteen pages, gathered in order, folded — we could not spare the time for stitching, so we folded and tucked, folded and tucked, until the workshop's long table disappeared under drifts of white like a field under snow. Four hundred copies. Then six hundred. Elodie had found a hoard of paper her father had been keeping against the regime's currency contracts — good rag stock, the garrison's own requisitioned supply — and the irony of it made her laugh once, briefly, a sound I hadn't heard from her in weeks.

Somewhere in the smallest hours the boy Pieter knocked — our delivery boy, our recovered informer, pale as one of his own unpaid debts — and we put a satchel of pamphlets in his arms and gave him the route we had planned: the bakery first, because Madame Lisle baked before anyone in the town woke and her loaves went to every street, every quarter, every door. Tucked into the bread baskets. Slipped under the strings. By dawn there would be a copy of the Record beside or inside nearly every loaf in town.

"They'll hang me if they catch me," Pieter said, but his hands closed on the satchel strap.

"They'll do worse to all of us if they catch us," Elodie said, "and you chose your side a week ago. Go out the alley door."

He went. And an hour later he came back, breathless, grinning like a thief in reverse — *they took them, all of them, Madame Lisle asked no questions, she only said God keep you both* — and we loaded him again and sent him out again. Twice more he ran the alley. The stack at the press table shrank and the town's silent inventory grew, eight hundred copies of the truth traveling by darkness into eight hundred kitchens.

It was near five when I stepped into the yard to cool my face and heard it.

Not the river. Not the wind. Boots. Many boots, on the far road, keeping poor step — tired men, conscript pace, but unmistakably soldiers, and unmistakably coming down toward the bridge end of town, which meant coming toward us. I stood very still in the cold and counted what my ears told me. Two dozen at least. Perhaps more behind them in the dark that I couldn't hear.

Inside, the press was still clacking. Elodie, bent over the tympan in the candle-glow, hair escaping its pins, ink to her wrists, printing the final signature — the last page, the one that ended with Ambrose's sentence, the one I had set with my hands shaking and my heart steady for the first time in months: *Here ends the record. It is now yours. Carry it.*

I came in fast and shut the door behind me.

"Elodie."

She heard it in my voice. She finished her pull — she actually finished the pull, laid the sheet on the pile with perfect composure — and looked up. "How long?"

"Minutes. They're on the far road."

She crossed to the window and listened, and I watched her do the arithmetic I had already done. The stack on the table: perhaps sixty copies remaining beyond our hidden reserve. The forme: still locked on the coffin, ink still wet on the platen. The type — Ambrose's nonpareil, two winters of his genius distributed across sixteen pages of the only honest document the valley had ever produced — sitting in the press like a confession.

"We can break it down," I said. "Distribute the type back into the cases. Twenty minutes and the evidence is gone."

"And print nothing more."

"There's nothing more to print. The Record's complete. Sixteen pages, eight hundred copies, and the town wakes to them within the hour." I put my hand on the press. "Balthazar can't be saved and neither can we, if we stand here arguing. But the type—"

"No." She said it quietly, and I heard her father in it, clear as a bell. "Look at the stack, Jude. Sixty copies. The bakery has maybe half the town covered. The rest go out with the morning milk, with the fish carts, hand to hand — *you* told me yourself, a pamphlet that doesn't travel is just paper. Those sixty copies need delivering the moment it's light, and if the soldiers take the workshop first, they take the whole remaining run and burn it, and half the town never reads what the other half is weeping over." Her eyes went to the press, and then to the far wall, and I saw the plan assemble itself behind her face even as she spoke. "We don't hide it. We spend it. Every copy out the door in the next ten minutes, and we—" she stopped, and laughed, low and disbelieving, at whatever she saw herself agreeing to. "And we stay at the press. We keep printing until they're at the door, because a press running when they break in means their eyes are on us, and their eyes on us are eyes off the streets."

"That's not a plan, that's a pyre."

"It's a press at midnight," she said. "It's what we have."

* * *

What happened in the next ten minutes happened with the terrible clarity of things that cannot be undone.

Pieter took the sixty copies — the entire remaining run, save the three we slipped into the false bottom of the type case — and went out through the alley with the satchels strapped across his chest like bandoliers. Elodie threw open the shutters of the front room, which faced the market square, so that the light of our blazing candles spilled openly into the street for the first time in the workshop's history. A beacon. An admission. Let them see where the truth was made.

And I stood at the press and pulled.

The bar came to me and went from me and the pages came off wet and warm and I did not stop to count them. There was no one left to distribute them to; they piled on the table in a white drift that had nowhere to go, and I printed them anyway, because Elodie was right about what a running press means, and because — I will be honest, since honesty is the whole of this account — because it was the sweetest work of my life. Every pull was a word spoken aloud after years of whispering. Every sheet was one more copy of the widow's names that no colonel's fire could wholly erase, because somewhere in this town, already, in a kitchen or a loft or a baker's basket, other copies slept and would wake with the morning.

Boots in the square now. Lantern-light swinging wild across the shutters. A voice — the captain's, the one with the soft mouth and hard eyes — calling for the door to be opened in the name of the Provisional Authority.

Elodie blew out none of the candles. Instead she took down her father's heaviest hand-mallet, the one he used for planing wood type, and stood at the end of the table with her feet planted, and nodded to me to go on working.

I dampened the sheet. I tipped it to the tympan. My hands were steady — that is the part I will remember to the end of my days, that they were steady — and I folded the frisket down over the forme of the final page, over the names, over the sentence Ambrose Sterling had died-in-all-but-fact to dictate, and I cranked the carriage home.

The door shook under the first blow of the ram.

I hauled the bar.

The door shook again, and split along its mullions, and through the gap came the smell of torch pitch and the square full of red coats, and across the town — faint, impossible, unmistakable — I heard the first rooster crow, and beneath it, rising, voices. Not soldiers' voices. Doors opening. The town waking, and finding paper on its doorstep.

I lifted the sheet. Another copy of the truth, black and permanent in the candlelight.

The door came down.
