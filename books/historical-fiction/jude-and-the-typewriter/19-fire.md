## 19. Fire

The smoke came before the flames — a grey tongue sliding under the workshop door while the presses were still turning, still throwing off their warm animal smell of oil and ink and hot lead. I smelled it first the way I smelled everything in that place: as a change in the air, a wrongness in the ordinary chemistry of our days. Ink, tallow, river damp, my father's pipe ghosting through the rafters though he had not smoked in this room for three years. And now beneath it all, something burning that was not meant to burn.

"Jude," I said. My voice came out very small, swallowed by the clatter of the iron hand-press, the rhythmic sigh of the platen, the whole breathing machinery of the only home I had ever truly known.

He did not hear me. He was at the press with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms black to the wrist with ink, feeding sheets into the machine's hungry mouth with the tenderness of a man laying out a child for sleep. Two hundred copies done. The record of the massacres — names, dates, the testimony of widows, the final pages my father had smuggled out sewn inside a blanket — set in the finest type Ambrose Sterling ever cut. It was beautiful. I had read the first proof four times and wept twice, and I am not a woman who weeps easily, or so I told myself in those years when telling myself things was how I survived them.

"Jude." Louder this time.

He looked up. His eyes went from my face to the thin grey ribbon curling along the floorboards by the door, and everything in him went still — that terrible compositor's stillness, the same stillness his hands had when he found a flaw in a form, a letter set backward, an error that could not be unmade.

"The torches," he said. "I heard boots on the quay. I thought we had more time."

"We have what we have."

Outside, down the lane, the garrison drums had begun — not the march-step but the quick double beat they used for alarm, for riot, for fire. Perhaps they were even honest about it; perhaps some part of the town believed the soldiers had come to put a blaze out rather than start one. It did not matter. What mattered stood in front of us in the shape of two hundred damp pamphlets strung across the drying lines like laundry, and one locked type case beneath the east window, and the presses themselves — God help me, the presses — which could not be carried and could not be saved.

* * *

You cannot understand, unless you were raised inside one, what a press is to a printer's child. Our kitchen was the composing stone. Our lullabies were the creak of the tympan. My father's hands taught me letters before my mother's voice did; I learned the alphabet upside down and mirrored, because that is how type lives, and for a year of my childhood I wrote my own name backward on every slate fence in town until my mother despaired of me entirely. The Washington hand-press in the center of the room had been hauled upriver on a barge before I was born, and my father liked to say it weighed more than sin and would outlast us all.

He was nearly right. It would outlast him in the garrison's way — as scrap, as slag, as a story.

"Take the case," Jude said, and there was no discussion in it. He pulled the drying sheets from the lines in armfuls, folding them against his chest, ink smearing across his shirt, across his jaw where he pushed the hair from his eyes. "Elodie. The case. Now."

But I could not move. I stood in the middle of the workshop with smoke threading down through the ceiling boards like fingers feeling for us, and I looked at the presses — both of them, the great Washington and the little jobbing press my grandfather had bought secondhand from a card-printer in Basel — and I understood that they were going to die, and that I was going to live, and that these two facts were the same fact, arriving together, inseparable, like ink and paper pressed into one permanent page.

My father had printed currency for a collapsing regime at that Washington press. He had believed — he told me once, in the visitation room of the garrison jail, his wrists manacled and his voice perfectly level, because Ambrose Sterling never wasted motion on anything, least of all regret — that survival was the only justice available to small men. He had been wrong, and then he had been righter than anyone, and now his presses were going to pay for both.

"Elodie!"

I moved.

* * *

The type case was oak and iron, made to my father's specification, its drawers fitted so precisely that a coin dropped on top would not slide. Inside it lived the finest work of his life: the ten-point text face he had spent two winters cutting, each punch graven in steel by his own hand, matrices struck and justified, the whole alphabet of it — including the ligatures, including the old-fashioned long s he loved against fashion — cast in foundry type hard enough to print a hundred thousand impressions without wearing round. It was, he said, the only thing he had ever made that deserved to outlive him. He had not known, when he said it, how soon he would be proved right, nor what that alphabet would be asked to say.

It weighed as much as a man. I got my arms under it anyway.

"No," Jude said, seeing me. "Through the cellar — the coal hatch gives onto the alley—"

"I know where the coal hatch is. I've lived here since before you came with your one carpetbag and your capital ambitions."

Something crossed his face — fear, and love, and the ghost of a laugh, all pressed together the way we pressed everything in that house. Then the window at the front of the shop burst inward, glass blooming across the composing stone where we had worked side by side all those nights, and the first torch came through the broken frame trailing orange, and there was no more time for anything but motion.

The soldiers came behind the torch. I did not count them. I heard their boots, their shouting, the crash of a chase being swept from the stone — my father's forms, tomorrow's pages, scattered and ground under heels — and I went down the cellar stairs with the case crushing my ribs and the darkness swallowing me whole.

* * *

The cellar was our other house. Every family has rooms the visitors never see; ours happened to hold a secret press run, hidden plates beneath the floorboards, and the particular smell of concealed history — lamp oil, dust, the cold mineral breath of stone foundations. I knew that dark the way I knew my own hands. I crossed it by memory, by the count of steps, twelve and then the turn and then eight, to the coal hatch my grandfather had installed for deliveries and my father had never gotten around to replacing with anything more convenient, because Ambrose Sterling distrusted convenience the way other men distrusted debt.

The hatch was barred from outside with a winter latch rusted half through. I set the case down in the dark, felt for the bar, and pulled until my palms screamed. It gave on the third pull with a sound like a tooth leaving its socket.

Behind and above me, the workshop caught fully alight.

I heard it happen through the floor. The roar is what stays with me — not crackle, roar, the sound of a living thing inhaling, of a fire drawing its first full breath in a room built of seasoned pine and saturated for forty years with ink and linseed oil and paper. Paper. Thousands of pounds of paper. Quire upon quire of it in the storeroom, blank stock my father had bought against better seasons, all of it going up at once, and the heat coming down through the boards above my head like a hand pressing me flat.

And underneath the roar, the presses dying.

I want to tell you what that sounded like, because no one else will ever say it and someone should. The Washington press did not scream. Iron does not scream. But iron sings when it heats — a low note, a tuning-fork hum in the beams, rising as the fire rose — and the wooden frame of it groaned in long descending complaints, timber surrendering joint by joint, wedge by wedge, forty years of my family's labor loosening itself back into raw material. The jobbing press went faster. I heard its iron platen let go somewhere and crash, and the sound was so much like the sound of the platen falling in ordinary work, the sound of my whole childhood, that I put my hand over my mouth and stayed crouched in the dark of the coal hatch and shook.

Then I remembered what I was carrying, and whose hands had cut it, and I stopped shaking, and I climbed out into the alley.

* * *

The snow was filthy with ash already, grey falling like a second weather over the rooftops. The alley ran behind the tannery toward the river lane, and I dragged the type case along it inch by inch, the runners of the sled we kept for paper deliveries — I had thought to hook the case onto it, in the last sane minute, God knows how — scraping a furrow through the slush. Behind me the sky over the workshop turned the color of a forge. Windows I could not see shattered; I heard them. Somewhere in the heat the store of rosin and turpentine went up, and the fire leapt, and a cheer went up from the square, soldier voices, young men made cruel by boredom and orders and the peculiar cowardice of crowds.

I did not look back. Looking back is for people who can afford to stop moving.

Jude found me at the corner of the tannery wall, where the lane bent toward the water. He came out of the smoke sideways, coughing, his eyebrows singed, both arms wrapped around a bundle of pamphlets he had shielded with his own body — I counted later, ninety-one copies saved of the two hundred, and I never asked him at what cost to his hands, because I saw the burns and he saw me see them, and we agreed without speaking never to speak of it.

"The cellar hatch," he said. "I couldn't find you. The stairs were already—" He stopped. Started again, the way he restarted a sentence that had come out misaligned, hunting for the true line. "I thought you were still inside."

"I was under it. Where I've always been."

He took the sled rope from me without asking. We went down the lane together, fast, the case jolting, the pamphlets hugged to his chest, and the light of the burning workshop climbing the eastern sky like a false dawn, and neither of us said anything for a long time. There was nothing that needed saying. We had said it all in type.

* * *

We hid in the boathouse under the bridge pilings, among the tarred nets and the smell of frozen river, because it was the one place in town the garrison would not search — military men distrust boats in winter the way cats distrust water. Through the gap in the boards I could see the fire across the water, reflected doubled in the black ice-edged current, the workshop's skeleton glowing at the joints. Forty years of my family, and it took the garrison less than an hour. By midnight the roof fell. I watched it go down — a slow collapse, almost gentle, a breath let out — and the sparks stood up over the town like wheat, thousands of them, drifting on the wind toward the rooftops of the very people who would wake tomorrow to find the truth on their doorsteps.

Because here is the arithmetic the captain had not done, standing in our yard with his torch: you cannot burn a press and keep the pages. He had destroyed the instrument. He had not destroyed the argument. Ninety-one pamphlets sat wrapped in sailcloth beside me in the dark, and every one of them would be copied by hand where copies were needed, read aloud in kitchens, passed from sleeve to sleeve, because my father had set the truth in ten-point text face cut to last a hundred thousand impressions, and truth, once typeset, is a stubborn metal. You can melt it. You cannot unmake what it has already said.

"They'll come at dawn for the town," Jude said. His voice was raw from smoke. "Reinforcements cross when the ice breaks. They'll want to make an example."

"They waited too long to make an example," I said. "You can't make an example of everyone. And by morning, everyone will have read it."

He looked at me then across the dark boathouse, our faces lit orange through the board gaps by a fire that had taken everything we owned, and he smiled — a small, cracked, astonished smile, the smile of a man discovering mid-ruin that he is free. I knew the feeling. It was sitting in my own chest like a second heartbeat: grief and liberation packed into the same space, indistinguishable, the way wet ink and dry paper become one thing under the press and can never again be separated into what they were.

"My father is still in the jail," I said, after a while.

"Yes."

"And the crowd will be at the jail by noon. A town that has read the truth about the massacres will not leave the man who printed it to hang for it."

"You believe that?"

I watched the sparks cross the river and settle, dying, on the ice. Somewhere in the burning ruin behind me, the last beam of my father's workshop went down, and I felt it more than heard it, a shudder through the pier boards, the earth taking back its own.

"I believe," I said, "that we finally have the whole town reading from the same forme. And I believe what my father taught me about impressions, Jude. Enough of them, and nothing stands unchanged."

Jude shifted the sailcloth bundle closer between us, against the cold, an absurd tenderness — sheltering paper from winter as if it were a living thing, and perhaps it was. Down the river lane, faint through the boards, came the first sounds of the town waking: a dog, a door, a voice calling another voice, and another answering. Word traveling. Pages passing hand to hand in the dark before dawn, exactly as designed, exactly as set.

By noon, I was certain, the crowd would stand outside the garrison jail. And whatever happened next, we would need to be there when it did.
