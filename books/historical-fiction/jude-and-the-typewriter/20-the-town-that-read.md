## 20. The Town That Read

I did not sleep. No one in the Sterling house slept that night, and by the grey hour before dawn I understood that no one in the town was sleeping either.

We had run out of paper twice and started again on the cheap stock Ambrose kept for handbills, the kind that yellowed in a season and tore at the folds. It didn't matter. The words held. I had set them myself, letter by letter, and there is a strange permanence in lead that even fire cannot entirely undo — a truth we were about to test with our lives, though I did not yet know it.

By first light Elodie and I had carried four hundred copies up from the basement in flour sacks, and what we could not carry we left stacked beneath the floorboards like grain against famine. The town lay white under frost, chimneys breathing smoke into a sky the color of unprinted paper, and we moved through the streets the way thieves move — quickly, quietly, and certain of our right to what we took. Except we were leaving things behind, not stealing them. We slid a pamphlet under every door. We tucked them into milk pails on stoops and into the cracks of market stalls. Elodie wedged one into the stone lion's mouth outside the assay office where the garrison kept its records, a small deliberate blasphemy that made me laugh once, high and unsteady, in the freezing air.

The document was eight pages long. It listed names — forty-one of them, though by the time we printed the final count it had grown to forty-six — and dates, and places: the mill at Verchères where the soldiers shot nine men against their own sluice gates; the ferry crossing where a boy of fifteen drowned trying to swim from a press gang; the barn outside Sainte-Marthe that was burned with three families locked inside, and the names of every soul within, taken from the widow Corbeau's ledger and from Ambrose's own testimony, dictated in a cell and smuggled to us sewn into a blanket that smelled of straw and his particular ink-stained patience.

Every name set in the finest type Ambrose Sterling ever cut. I had handled that face for six years without understanding it — a serif so precise it seemed drawn rather than cast, letters that stood on the page like witnesses who would not sit down. Now I understood what he had been making all those years at his graver. He had been making something that could not be denied.

* * *

The town woke to paper the way a field wakes to rain.

I saw it happen from the workshop window. First one man, then three, standing on their doorsteps in nightshirts with the pamphlets open in their bare hands. A woman came out with a candle despite the daylight, holding the page close as if the words might change if she looked away. Old Gaspard Fenn, who had not read anything harder than a price list in twenty years, sat down on his own front step in the frost and read all eight pages through, and when he finished he did not fold the pamphlet but held it flat across both knees, the way one holds something sacred or breakable.

By noon the reading had become a kind of weather. You could feel it moving through the streets. Men stood in clusters of two and three, passing single copies between them because there were not enough, reading aloud over each other's shoulders, and the sound they made was not the sound of argument. It was quieter than that and heavier. It was the sound of people discovering they had all been carrying the same grief alone and were now, suddenly, not alone.

I heard my own sentences spoken back to me in the mouth of the baker's wife — *no official record exists; therefore we make this one* — and I confess I stood in the shadow of her doorway like a thief listening to his own confession. She did not know whose words they were. She never would. But she read them aloud twice, and the second time her voice broke on the name of her cousin's son, and I understood something that has stayed with me since: that a compositor's work does not end at the press. It ends in someone's throat.

Elodie worked beside me all morning and said almost nothing, but I watched her watch the town, and her face had in it something I had never seen there — not triumph, which would have been too simple, but a kind of fierce tenderness, as though she were watching a sick man sit up and take broth. Once she gripped my arm hard enough to bruise and pointed across the square, where the schoolmaster had pinned a copy to the church door itself, in open defiance of the curate's loyalty to the garrison, and nobody — not the curate, not the sentries, not God himself apparently — moved to take it down.

* * *

The garrison knew by mid-afternoon.

We learned of it the way you learn of an approaching storm: a change in pressure, dogs barking, the market emptying in a slow tide. Captain Rousseau — and yes, his name was in the pamphlet too, spelled correctly, with his rank and the date of the Verchères shooting — sent four men into the square to seize the copy from the church door. I watched from the upper window with Elodie's spyglass, my heart going like a fly against glass.

The corporal reached the door. He put his hand on the paper. And then a woman stepped in front of him — the baker's wife, of all people, still wearing flour to the elbows — and behind her stood a dozen others, and behind them two dozen more, and none of them said a word. They simply stood, the way type stands in a forme: each one nothing alone, locked together immovable.

The corporal looked at the crowd. He looked at the paper. Then he took his hand away, turned, and marched his men back the way they came, and the crowd did not cheer, because cheering would have been smaller than what they felt. They watched the soldiers go in silence, and the silence was the loudest thing I had ever heard.

"They can't shoot us all," Elodie said softly beside me. "Not anymore. Not now everyone knows the names."

"That was always true," I said. "They just counted on us not knowing."

She lowered the spyglass. "Ambrose used to say ignorance was the regime's best binding glue." Her voice caught on his name. It caught every time now, and she let it.

"He also said," I reminded her, "that survival was the only justice available to small men." I thought of him in his cell across the river, and of the testament we had finished for him, page by smuggled page, setting his careful dictation beside the widow's ledger until the two accounts matched like a forme locked in its chase. "I think he'd be pleased to be wrong."

* * *

They came for the workshop at dusk, as we knew they would. Twenty soldiers and a wagon for the presses, an officer with a warrant signed by Rousseau, and torches, because warrants are for courts and torches are for nights when courts are inconvenient.

But the town had decided otherwise while we worked, and we did not know the half of what had been decided.

They came down every street at once — not a mob, nothing so disorderly, but a crowd with the terrible coherence of a paragraph. Gaspard Fenn walked at the front with his cane, and beside him the blacksmith Duval, and behind them perhaps three hundred souls, most of them holding pamphlets, some holding them raised above their heads like lanterns, so that as the crowd filled the square it seemed to carry its own light, page after white page catching the torch glare.

The officer read his warrant. His voice shook slightly on Rousseau's name — he had read the pamphlet too, then, or heard it read; everyone had; there was no longer any such thing as a person in this valley who had not heard those forty-six names — and the crowd answered him with silence, that same silence, thick as wet paper.

"We are come to confiscate a printing press," the officer said.

"You are come to burn evidence," said the schoolmaster, from somewhere in the middle distance, and a low sound went through the crowd like a press screw tightening.

What happened next happened without a leader and without a plan, which is why it worked. The blacksmith simply walked forward and stood between the soldiers and the door. Then Gaspard. Then ten more. Then fifty. They did not raise their hands. They did not shout. They became, in effect, a wall of flesh between the garrison and the type, and I stood in the upstairs window watching men I had sold bread to and played cards with and never once truly seen perform the single bravest act of their quiet lives: standing still.

And here is the arithmetic that saved us, the cold sums a captain must do at midnight: three hundred townsfolk who know your name and your crimes; two companies of soldiers who have read, in print, exactly what happens to officers after massacres when the record survives — how the capital devours its own instruments the moment they become inconvenient. Rousseau could burn the workshop. He could probably even take the town, for a week. But he could no longer do it invisibly, and invisibility had been the whole of his power. A massacre needs darkness. We had flooded the valley with lamps.

At full dark, a rider went out across the bridge road toward the fort. At half past nine, the wagon came back through the square the other way, empty. At ten, word ran the streets faster than any press could have spread it: the garrison was withdrawing. Moving out before dawn, before reinforcements could arrive and find themselves implicated, before the report reached the capital with the pamphlet stapled to it — because a copy had already gone east, sewn into a peddler's coat, three days ago. Elodie's doing. My Elodie, who thought of everything.

They withdrew. Rather than slaughter a town that knew, they packed their wagons in the night and slipped away down the frozen road like thieves who have found the house awake.

* * *

But they had not gone yet at dawn, and Ambrose was still in the cell across the river, and that is the part I will tell my grandchildren about, if I am ever granted grandchildren: the part where the town crossed the bridge.

It began with Gaspard Fenn, of course. He came to the workshop at first light, leaning on his cane, and said only, "The jail. They're leaving. Who's to say what orders the last men carry?" And that was enough. Within the hour there were two hundred people on the ice-glazed span, walking with the unhurried menace of winter itself, and I was among them, and Elodie was beside me with her father's heavy coat around her shoulders because he would need it more than anyone.

The sentries at the jail did not fire. I want to be exact about this, because it matters: they did not fire, and later some would say it was cowardice, and some would say it was wisdom, and I believe it was neither. I believe they had read the pamphlet. I believe a young private stood at that door with forty-six names in his head, several of them boys he'd drunk with in Verchères, and found he could no longer see the prisoners through the sights of his musket. Memory, typeset and distributed, had done what no army had done: it had disarmed them.

They broke the lock because no one had a key, and the sound of the cell door opening was the ugliest, sweetest sound I have ever heard — iron shrieking on iron, and then Ambrose Sterling's voice from inside, dry as always: "Mind the hinges. Good hinges are scarce."

He came out squinting into the snowlight, thinner, one wrist wrapped in filthy bandage where the irons had chafed, and for a moment he looked at the crowd the way I imagine a man looks at a sentence he has just read and cannot believe — checking it for errors, for forgery, for the catch. Then Elodie pushed through and took his face in both hands and pressed her forehead to his, and whatever proof he needed, that was it.

The crowd did not cheer then either. But when he descended the jail steps, three hundred people removed their hats at once, in the snow, in silence — the salute owed to a man returned from the dead — and Ambrose, who had spent sixty years bowing to type cases and never to any living creature, bowed back, low and long, and straightened with tears freezing in the seams of his face.

"Who printed it?" he asked, hoarse, searching the faces. "The final edition. The last pages. Whose composition?"

Elodie's hand found mine, cold fingers around colder ones.

"Mine," I said. My voice cracked like green wood. "Yours and mine, master. Mostly yours. Every word."

He looked at me for a long moment — my old master, my second father, the man who taught me that a compositor serves the reader and not the powerful — and then he said the words I had waited six years to hear, though I hadn't known it:

"Then you were never a tradesman, Jude Marsh. You are a maker of history. Get your coat. We have work."

* * *

Behind us, down the valley, the last garrison wagons creaked away north, and ahead of us the river ice groaned and shifted, beginning its long thaw. The workshop still stood, the presses still lived, and the type case — Ambrose's finest cut, the face that had named the dead and shamed the living — sat safe in Elodie's keeping.

Ambrose settled the coat on his shoulders, looked once at the withdrawing soldiers, once at the crowd still holding its papers aloft like a field of white flowers, and then at the two of us.

"Now," he said. "Tell me everything. And then tell me what comes next — because the capital will want this story told properly, in a proper shop, with proper type." His eyes glinted, sharp as a graver's edge. "And I intend we should be the ones to tell it."
