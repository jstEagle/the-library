## 21. The Bridge Opens

The river spoke first.

I heard it before dawn on the third of March — a low grinding under the birdsong, like millstones turning somewhere beneath the world. I rose from my cot in what had been the composing room and went to the window with my blanket still around my shoulders, and there in the grey light I watched the ice on the Vell give up its long argument with spring. The whole frozen sheet had shifted, cracked into plates the size of chapel floors, and where the plates ground against one another they reared up and shone pale green in the early light, like the scales of something vast turning over in its sleep.

The bridge would open within days. Everyone knew it; everyone said it. The ferryman's boy ran the length of the embankment shouting it before the sun was fully up, and by noon the news had been set into every conversation in town as firmly as if it were lead: *the bridge opens when the ice goes out, and the soldiers' barge comes upriver behind it, and after that nothing will be as it was.*

They were wrong about that last part. Nothing had been as it was since midwinter. But a town learns to hold two truths at once, the way a compositor holds a line of type in the corner of his hand while his fingers go on choosing the next letter.

* * *

We kept the workshop running. That was the strange mercy of it — Ambrose freed by the crowd three weeks past, thin and stooped and blinking at the light like a man hauled out of a cellar, but alive, and back at his cutting bench within a day, though his hands shook now when he worked the fine gravers and he let Jude do all the pulling of proofs. The garrison had withdrawn to the far bank of the river, a smoke-column of a camp visible on clear days across the water, waiting for reinforcements that the bridge would bring them. They did not come into town anymore except in pairs, unarmed or pretending to be, buying bread with currency our presses had printed and which no one any longer pretended had value. The town fed them anyway. It cost less than fear.

And every morning the pamphlet went out again. We had printed it eleven times now — the full record, Ambrose's testament and the widow's ledger of names and the accounts of the massacres at Marrec Ford and the granary road, all of it set in the ten-point text face Ambrose had cut the winter I turned sixteen, the finest thing he ever made. People came to the door for copies now instead of finding them on doorsteps. A woman from the flax district walked nine kilometers each Friday for twenty copies, which she carried home sewn into her petticoats and read aloud in her neighbors' kitchens by candlelight. Names were still being added. The list of the missing grew longer each week as the frightened came forward — men who had hidden in the marshes rather than be taken, boys presumed dead who walked in half-starved — and each time, Elodie Sterling went to the case and set the new names in lead, because a name once typeset cannot be unmade.

That was the work. That was mine now, as much as the workshop was my father's.

But this chapter belongs to the bridge, and to Jude, and I must tell it plainly even though telling it plainly is the hardest setting of all, harder than any justified line.

* * *

Jude began packing in February.

He did not announce it. He simply began — a canvas satchel produced from under his cot, filled a little at a time when he thought I wasn't looking. Two shirts. The copy of Virgil he'd brought with him four years ago when he arrived off the last open ferry, seventeen years old and burning to reach the capital. His finishing tools wrapped in oilcloth. And one morning I found him standing at the type cabinet with the top drawer open, holding the brass composing stick Ambrose had given him at the end of his second year — the one with his initials stamped in the wood, *J.M.*, cut by Ambrose himself in an hour's idle excellence — and he stood there a long time without taking it out, and then closed the drawer and went back to washing the rollers.

I knew what the satchel meant. He had told me often enough, in the old days, before everything: *when the bridge opens I'm gone, Elodie. There's a compositor's berth at the Courier in the capital, they'll take a man who can set display faces blindfolded, and I'll see the Chambers of State, I'll see history being made instead of printing other people's accounts of it.* He had said it jokingly at first, then wistfully, and finally not at all, because after midwinter neither of us had said anything about the bridge at all, and the silence had been its own kind of sentence, awaiting composition.

Now the ice was going out, and the sentence had to be finished somehow.

* * *

On the fifth of March the ferryman sounded the channel at first light and pronounced it passable, and by afternoon the whole town had walked down to the embankment to look at the bridge. I went too. So did Jude, though he walked a little apart from me the way he had done all week, hands in his pockets, wearing the face of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

The bridge itself had suffered for the winter. Ice had stove in two of the pilings on the northern span and buckled the planking near the third arch, and a crew of townsfolk was already at work with timber and iron, hammering, shouting measurements across the water. But it would hold. By Sunday, the ferryman said, carts could cross. By Monday, soldiers.

We stood at the rail of the embankment in the cold bright afternoon, watching the river carry its broken plates downstream, and I said, because someone had to say it:

"Monday, then."

"Monday," Jude agreed.

"The Courier won't hold the berth forever."

"No." He picked at a splinter of frost on the railing. "It's a good position. Steady work. Union scale." He said these things the way a man recites reasons he wrote down long ago and has been reading over so many times the words have worn smooth of meaning. Below us the river heaved and groaned. "Ambrose says my hand is ready. He says I could cut faces myself now, if I wanted. He said—" He stopped. He looked at the water for a while. "He offered to make me partner."

My heart did something complicated, which I kept entirely off my face. Compositor's discipline. "And you told him?"

"I told him I'd think on it."

"Jude Marsh," I said, "you have never once in your life thought slowly about anything you truly wanted. You decided against the apprenticeship in the time it took the ferry to dock. You decided to help me print the truth in the space of one held breath. Whatever this is—" I nodded at the satchel-weight of his silence, at the whole careful edifice of his hesitation— "it isn't thinking. It's grieving in advance."

He turned and looked at me then, really looked, and I saw how tired he was, and how young, and how much older than his years the winter had made him. "Of course I'm grieving," he said quietly. "You're here. The work is here. Four years ago I'd have crossed that bridge on fire to leave this valley, and now—" He laughed, short and unhappy. "Now I stand here making bargains with myself. *Stay through the spring. Stay until Ambrose's hands steady. Stay until the list is finished.* And every bargain I make, I know it's a lie, because there will always be another list."

"Yes," I said. "There will always be another list."

"That's not a reason to stay, Elodie."

"It's not a reason to stay." I looked at the river. The sun was lowering behind the western ridge and the broken ice burned gold along its seams, and I thought of the type case we had carried out of the fire, heavy as a coffin and precious as one, and of all the names inside it. "It's just true. Reasons are thinner than people think. Mostly we choose, and then we build reasons under our feet like planks, the way the crew is building the span. But you don't get to pretend the choice isn't yours. Not anymore. Not after this winter. You learned that, didn't you? That's the thing you actually came here to learn, only you didn't know it yet when you got off the ferry."

"You've rehearsed this speech," he said.

"Since February," I admitted, and that startled a real laugh out of him, brief and warm, the first in a week.

* * *

We walked back through the town as the lamps were lit. It was market evening and the square smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and frying onions, and everywhere there was paper — the pamphlet folded in coat pockets, nailed to the churchyard gate, weighted under a stone on the fountain lip where anyone might read it. A knot of people stood at the fountain while the schoolmaster read the newest additions to the list aloud, and when he reached the name of Théophile Rance, the cooper's son, taken in November and returned alive in January, his mother cried out — the good cry, the living cry — and pressed her hands over her ears as if she couldn't bear the size of her own joy.

Jude stopped walking. He watched the woman and her son holding each other by the fountain, and I watched him watch them, and I saw the arithmetic finish behind his eyes — the sum that no position at the Courier, no salary, no view of the Chambers of State could balance against this: that his hands, his eye, his exactness, the patience Ambrose had hammered into him over four years of pulling proofs and rejecting them, had made this moment possible. That memory made permanent in lead had pulled a living boy back into his mother's arms.

"You could have this anywhere," I said softly. "That's the honest counterargument. Print is portable. Type is portable. The capital needs its truth-tellers worse than we do, maybe."

"I know."

"And you should also know that if you leave, I am not going to dissolve. The press will run. Father and I will manage. You wouldn't be abandoning anything. This isn't a debt, Jude, and it isn't a chain."

"I know that too." He was quiet the rest of the way to the workshop. But at the door, in the dark under the lintel where the lantern didn't reach, he stopped and said, "When I was packing, I left room. In the satchel. For the composing stick. I've been leaving room for weeks." He said it like a confession. "I couldn't take it out of the drawer. Every night I'd open the drawer and stand there and close it again. Do you know why?"

"Tell me."

"Because taking it would mean deciding. And some cowardly part of me thought that if I never decided, the bridge would decide for me — it would open, and the current would carry me out the way it carries everything, and none of it would be my fault." He exhaled. "But that man died in the winter. The one things happened to. I don't want to be carried anymore, Elodie. Whatever I do next, I want to choose it, with both hands, the way you set a line you intend to stand behind."

Inside, the workshop was warm. My father had gone to bed early, exhausted by his hour at the bench, and the banked fire ticked in the grate, and the great press stood in the corner like a sleeping animal, inked and cleaned and ready, patient as everything else in this trade that waits for the human hand to tell it what matters.

Jude hung up his coat. He went to the type cabinet. He opened the top drawer, and took out the composing stick with *J.M.* stamped in the walnut, and weighed it in his palm, and did not put it in the satchel, which sat by his cot with its two shirts and its Virgil and its room that would now go unfilled.

"Monday," he said, "the soldiers cross."

"They do."

"And the pamphlet can't stop. If their reinforcements come and the town forgets what it knows—" He shook his head. "The record has to keep moving. Faster than they do. Names, dates, witnesses. While the press stands, the record stands."

"While the press stands," I agreed, and felt the shape of what he was building toward arrive in me like a forme locked into the chase.

"So I'm staying," he said. "Not because of the bridge closing. Not because of circumstance. I could cross it Sunday and I'm not going to. I'm staying for the work, and for your father's hands, and—" he looked at me across the dark little workshop that had remade us both— "and for the compositor. If she'll have an apprentice with slow decisions and one very well-traveled satchel."

"She'll have a partner," I said, "or nothing. Partner means you pull your own proofs and I reject half of them."

"Half?" He grinned, and it was the old grin from before the winter, but coming from a new face. "Your kerning alone should disqualify you from judging anyone."

* * *

Later, when he had gone up to his cot and the fire had settled to embers, I sat a while at my father's desk with the lamp turned low, and I let myself feel it — not triumph, nothing so clean, but a settling, like the river settling into its open channel. All winter we had acted under compulsion: the raid, the arrest, the countdown, the fire. Even courage, then, had been circumstance wearing a brave coat. But tonight a man who had spent four years longing for the door had stood in front of it with the latch in his hand and chosen the room. Choice replacing chance. That was worth more than the bridge, worth more than the berth at the Courier, worth perhaps more than anything we had printed.

Outside, the river went on breaking itself open in the dark, plate after plate grinding downstream toward the sea, carrying the winter away.

I snuffed the lamp. And as I climbed the stairs I heard, faintly from above, the sound of Jude at his window, and beneath it — far off, across the black water, from the direction of the enemy camp — a different sound entirely: bugles. Three notes, repeated. The signal I had learned to dread, the call that meant movement, muster, the arrival of something crossing water come Monday.

The bridge opened in two days. So did whatever came next.

Downstairs in the dark, twelve hundred sorts of ten-point text waited in their cases, and somewhere among them, already, the letters of the next name.
