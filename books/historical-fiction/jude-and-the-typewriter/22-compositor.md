## 22. Compositor

*Jude*

The bridge opened on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning the whole town knew we meant to cross it.

I suppose I should have expected no less. A town that had learned to read its own history in pamphlets and broadsheets had also learned to read its own neighbors, and there was no hiding anything in Verraneux anymore — not grief, not guilt, not the sight of two young people carrying a locked type case down the cobbled hill toward the river like pallbearers at a funeral that refused to be one.

The case itself weighed more than I did. Ambrose Sterling had built it thirty years ago from Hungarian oak, banded in iron, and he had filled it with the finest type ever cut in that valley — the letters he had been carving the year I was born, when his hands were steady and his eyesight perfect and the world still believed in kings. We had carried it out of the burning workshop between us, Elodie at one handle and I at the other, the heat of the flames on our necks and the weight of every word Ambrose had ever set pressing down through our arms and into our spines. I had thought, that night, that I would never feel light again.

Now, standing at the southern foot of the bridge with the case strapped to a handcart I had borrowed from the baker, I felt almost weightless.

The river was running high with snowmelt, brown and muscular, carrying branches and slabs of ice downstream toward the lowlands. The bridge itself — the great covered span that had been closed since the first week of winter, garrisoned, chained, declared shut by men with rifles who are now gone — stood open like a mouth that had finally finished speaking. There were no soldiers on it. There was a boy from the millers' row sitting on the far abutment fishing for trout, and an old woman driving two goats across as if the whole thing were the most ordinary thing in the world, which, God willing, it would be again.

"You're staring," Elodie said.

"I'm memorizing."

"Memorize while walking. The capital won't come to us."

She said it lightly, but I heard what was underneath it, because I had spent a winter learning to hear what was underneath everything she said. Her father was in the town behind us, in the house of the widow Charbonnier, whose husband's name he had set into print three days before the soldiers came for him — set into print in twelve-point Caslon, each letter chosen by hand, so that even if the man himself could not be freed until summer, his name would outlive every officer who had taken him. Ambrose had shaken my hand at the door last night and held it a moment too long, and he had said, *Mind the kerning*, which was the closest thing to a blessing the old man had ever given anyone.

"He'll follow when the roads are better," Elodie said, watching my face instead of the bridge. "He said so."

"He said the type must go first."

"He said both."

She picked up her end of the handcart before I could argue, and together we rolled it onto the planks of the bridge, and the boards sang under our feet the way they always had, a low wooden chord I had known since childhood, back when this bridge was only the sound of somewhere else.

I had crossed this bridge a hundred times in dreams during the winter. In the dreams I was always alone, and always leaving, and always relieved. In the dreams there was no type case, no smoke smell baked into my coat, no girl beside me who could set eight hundred ems an hour by candlelight and cry while she did it without dropping a single letter. In the dreams I was escaping something. This morning I understood, stepping plank by plank over the swollen river, that I was carrying something instead. It is not the same motion. The legs do the same work but the heart does entirely different work, and mine was hammering not with fear but with the particular terror of a man who has gotten exactly what he wanted and must now find out whether wanting it had been the whole of him.

Halfway across, Elodie stopped.

The river roared beneath the boards. Through the open side of the covered span I could see the valley spread out below us — the rooftops of Verraneux, the market square where three weeks ago ten thousand pamphlets had changed hands like communion wafers, the black scar on the hillside where the workshop had stood, already littered with spring weeds. And beyond it, the road north, open now, running all the way to the capital if you had the legs and the years for it.

"They'll want us there by June," she said. "Marchand's people. There's a constituent assembly being planned, and they need a press that can't be bought." She looked sideways at me. "You know that's why they gave us the letter of introduction. It isn't charity. It's employment."

"I've never been employed by a revolution before."

"There's a first time for everyone. You told me once you were a tradesman."

"I was wrong."

"You were," she said, without cruelty, and pushed the cart onward.

At the far end of the bridge I stopped one last time and looked back. The town lay in the morning light with its chimneys just beginning to smoke, ordinary and miraculous, a place where history had happened after all — not the history in the almanacs, with its generals and its dates, but the other kind, the kind made of names remembered and lies refused and ink worked into paper by people who were supposed to be minding their own business. I had spent twenty-two years believing I lived outside that story. I crossed the rest of the bridge knowing I had been one of its hands all along, and that my hands, at least, would not be clean of it again.

The fisherboy watched us pass. So did the goats. Neither of them applauded, and I found that fitting. Revolutions, I have decided, are mostly witnessed by goats.

* * *

*Elodie*

The road north smelled like thaw.

That is the thing no one tells you about the end of a winter like ours: the world does not come back gently. It comes back all at once, mud and water and green insistence, the ditches roaring, the forests dripping, the air so thick with the smell of wet earth and rotting leaves and beginning things that you breathe it like a meal. Jude and I walked the first day through country I had never seen, though I had printed maps of it often enough — the rebel maps, cut in secret after midnight, the roads I had rendered in six-point lines while Father slept above. Now the lines had become actual mud on actual boots, and I kept laughing at odd moments, quietly, and Jude kept pretending not to notice.

We slept the first night in the loft of a barn whose farmer had read the pamphlet. I knew because he said nothing about it, but he had nailed the front page to the barn door, weather-spotted and curling, and when he brought us bread and milk in the morning he looked at the type case on the cart and then at me and touched two fingers briefly to his forehead. Not a salute. Something older than that. The gesture people make toward a reliquary.

"That's going to take some getting used to," Jude said after the farmer left.

"What?"

"Being carried around inside other people's heads. The words, I mean. They don't stop when they leave the press. They just keep walking."

We walked eleven hours that day and I thought about that the whole way. My father used to say that type was the most honest material in the world because it did exactly what you told it and nothing else; that a compositor's sin went onto the page in his own hand and stayed there for the life of the edition. He meant it as a warning against carelessness. But lying in the hay with the iron bands of the case cold against my shoulder, I understood it differently. Everything I had ever set — the currency notes for a dying regime, the midnight placards, the list of the missing, the record of the massacres, every lie and every truth that had passed through my fingers in metal — all of it was still walking. Outliving its compositors. Outliving, perhaps, its republics. Somewhere in the capital there were still proclamations glued to walls in my earliest style, the tight leading and timid spacing of a girl learning her father's trade in secret, arguing with dead men about the size of the world.

I wanted, suddenly and fiercely, to be part of what came next. Not the paper trail of what had been survived, but what would be built.

On the third day we reached the high road and the traffic thickened — carts, pilgrims, deserters walking home in their shirtsleeves, a printer's apprentice from Lyon heading south who examined our type case with the naked envy of his trade and taught us a new way of tying the corner knots that saved half the rope. The world, it turned out, was full of printers. The world, it turned out, had been waiting all along for enough of them to wake up at once.

Jude noticed them too. He had gone quiet in the way he goes quiet when something large is rearranging itself inside him, and on the fourth evening, camped in the ruin of a roadside chapel with rain coming down hard on the slate roof, he finally said what it was.

"When the bridge closed," he said, "I had a ticket. Coach to the capital, third class. I'd saved for it for two years. I was going to find work at one of the big houses — Didot, maybe, or Firmin's people — set fashion plates and theater bills and forget I'd ever lived in a valley."

"And now?"

He was quiet a long time. Rain drummed. Our little fire threw shadows up the broken saints on the chapel wall, headless and patient.

"Now I keep thinking about the farmer who wept," he said. "Over my type. Over letters I chose and spaced and locked into a chase at two in the morning with my hands shaking. That man read his son's name and wept, and I was three streets away eating bread, and I have never in my life felt so close to another human being or so completely responsible for him." He looked at me across the fire. "Didot doesn't do that. Fashion plates don't do that. I can set beautiful pages for people who will never feel anything when they read them. Or I can spend my eyesight on pages that someone will nail to a barn door and touch their forehead to." He turned his palms up. "It isn't really a choice, is it? It never was. I just hadn't read far enough ahead in the forme."

"It is a choice," I said. "You make it every day. Everyone does. Father made the wrong version of it for thirty years, printing money for men he despised, because he thought survival was the only justice available to small men. And then one night he sat down and dictated the truth into a locked type case instead, and it cost him everything, and he'd tell you himself it was the first thirty years that were the waste." I poked the fire. "The choice isn't once, Jude. It's every stick of type."

He smiled — the slow, crooked smile I had first seen across the stone sink of the workshop washroom, a lifetime ago, when we were two apprentices arguing about hyphenation — and he said, "Then tomorrow I choose north."

And that night, for the first time since the workshop burned, neither of us dreamed about fire.

* * *

*Jude*

We saw the capital on the sixth morning, from the crest of the last ridge, and I will set down here what it looked like because I am a compositor and setting things down is the only prayer I know.

It lay in the valley of the Marthe like a form locked in its chase — every piece in position, every street a line of type running north and south, smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys the way ink rises off a freshly inked roller, in that same slow, inevitable bloom. Bells were ringing somewhere in the middle of it, several towers out of rhythm with each other, and flags hung from windows that were not the flags I had been taught in school. The city had already begun printing itself anew.

Elodie stood beside me with the wind pulling at her hair and her father's type case at her feet, and she did not say anything for a long while, and neither did I. Then she took off her glove and put her bare hand flat on the iron banding of the case, the way you'd check a horse, the way you'd steady yourself against a wall.

"Twelve hundred pounds of lead and oak," she said. "The truest thing my family ever owned."

"It'll set a million pages yet."

"It had better. Marchand says the assembly needs a gazette by midsummer, and the garrison loyalists are already printing counter-sheets, calling us murderers and thieves. There's a war on, Jude. Not the shooting kind. The other kind. The kind fought with paper."

"The kind we know how to fight."

"The only kind worth winning," she said, "because nobody burns for it. Only lies do."

We started down the ridge road, the case rumbling on the cart between us, and by noon we were in the streets themselves, in the roar and stink and glory of the capital — past barricades already half-dismantled and already half-rebuilt, past newsboys shouting four editions in an hour, past a wall plastered six sheets deep with decreits and denials and manifestos, one of which, peeling at the corner, I recognized by its spacing. Rebel type. Valley type. Set, unless my eye had lost all judgment in the fires, by hands smaller and faster than mine, on a press that no longer existed, in a town that had refused to forget.

Elodie saw it the same moment I did. She stopped. She read her own composition in the middle of a city of two hundred thousand strangers, and her eyes went bright, and she laughed once, sharply, like a stitch breaking.

"Well," she said. "We're already here."

We found Marchand's address by evening — a former convent off the Rue aux Ours with presses already installed in the chapel, compositors' stones still being leveled, apprentices hauling cases of type up stairs built for nuns. The man himself met us in the doorway, gray-bearded, inked to the elbows, and looked at the type case the way other men look at artillery.

"Sterling?" he said. "Ambrose Sterling's shop?"

"His daughter," Elodie said. "And his apprentice."

"His successor," I said, and felt the word settle into place like a quoined forme, tight and true.

Marchand led us inside to the empty stone where our case would sit, and as we lifted it down together — the same lift, the same shared weight, our hands finding the handles without looking, the way they had in the fire — I caught the smell of the room. Ink and oil and candle smoke and cold stone. It smelled like home. Not the home I had left, which was ash, but the home I had not known I was building all winter, plank by plank, letter by letter, on the far side of a closing bridge.

Tomorrow we would sort and distribute the whole case, every character back into its compartment, and begin setting the first number of a paper that had no name yet. Tonight Elodie stood at the empty stone with her sleeves rolled, testing the level of it with a composing stick as though it were a spirit level for the future, and outside the bells rang on, unsynchronized, unmastered, free.

"First headline," she said, not looking up. "Go on. Compositor's privilege."

I thought of a valley filling with spring. Of a bridge singing underfoot. Of my own hands, ink-stained and steady, no longer at the edge of history but squarely in its forme, locked in tight, ready for the pull of the bar.

I reached for the stick.

"*The People's Record,*" I said. "*Continued.*"

And we began to set it.
