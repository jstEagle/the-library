## 6. Camille Delacroix

The clinic smelled of carbolic and wet wool, and by November the two smells had married so thoroughly that Camille Delacroix no longer noticed either one. It was only when strangers came—soldiers on leave, inspectors from the prefecture, once a bishop who had misplaced his gloves—that she understood what the first breath of this place did to people. They flinched. Their eyes watered. A woman from the Croix-Rousse fainted dead away in the doorway, though Camille suspected the woman's fainting had less to do with the smell than with her husband's hand, mangled at Chemin des Dames, which Camille had been obliged to unwrap that morning.

Camille had stopped flinching years ago. That was the whole of nursing, as far as she could tell: you taught your body not to do the things bodies wanted to do. You taught it not to look away from a wound. Not to run from a scream. Not to weep in front of a man who was weeping worse. And when the war took everything else—her fiancé to Gallipoli, her lodgers to conscription, her brother to Verdun and to silence long before that—you taught yourself not to reach for letters that were never coming.

"Madame Delacroix." The voice belonged to Petit-Jean, the boy of fifteen who swept floors and carried bedpans and had lied about his age twice, once to the army and once to her. "The stove in the east room's gone out again."

"Then light it."

"I did. It won't stay lit. The coal's wet through."

Everything in Lyon was wet through that autumn. The rain came down the hillsides in ropes. The coal barges sat stranded somewhere south, and the gas was rationed to nothing, and the men in the east room lay shivering under blankets while their fevers climbed. Camille set down the basin she was carrying—the water went gray almost before it hit the porcelain—and followed the boy back through the corridor, past the cots, past the curtain that divided the surgical ward from the convalescent ward, past the window where an old woman named Madame Roux sat every day watching the street as if the street owed her money.

The stove had gone out because the chimney drew badly and the coal was half mud, and Camille knelt and fed it paper until the flames caught, and while she knelt she thought, as she always thought when she knelt at some small domestic failure, that her mother would have managed this better, and that her mother would also have written to Henri about it, and that neither fact made her sorry.

* * *

Her brother had been dead for two years and four months, and Camille had gotten very good at his death the way one gets good at any difficult thing—by practice, repetition, daily drilling. The official letter had come in the spring of 1916, printed on paper thin enough to read a newspaper through: *Monsieur Henri Delacroix, soldat de deuxième classe, mort pour la France à Verdun.* Mort pour la France. She remembered thinking, with a cruelty that still shamed her, that the phrase sounded like something off a pharmacy label. She had framed it for a week out of duty and then put it in a drawer, where it remained among the receipts and the spare keys.

What she had never told anyone—not the nuns at Saint-Vincent, not the other nurses, not the priest who came on Thursdays to hear confessions he found tedious—was that she had grieved the brother long before the war killed him. There had been a death, slow and unremarked, sometime around the winter of 1911, when Henri had begun staying out until the lamps were lit and then until they were extinguished, when the money began disappearing from the household tin, when their father had died and Henri had stood at the graveside looking not stricken but *cornered*, like a man doing arithmetic he couldn't finish. When she had asked him—she was seventeen, furious, already armored—where he went at night, he had said, "Out," in the tone of someone testing whether a door would hold, and after a while the door hadn't held, and he had gone out one final time with a carpetbag and left behind a rent debt, a disappointed landlady, and a sister who learned that month how to be angry in a way that kept a body warm.

Then the war, absurdly, had made him respectable. He'd enlisted in '14 like everyone, written her three letters full of the kind of sentimentality that reads well from the trenches, and then Verdun had closed over him like water. The last letter arrived in March 1916. She had answered it. Her answer had been, she realized later, the coldest thing she ever wrote—a page of practicalities, the state of the roof, the price of shoes, ending with a sentence she had composed carefully and now recited to herself on bad nights the way other people recited prayers: *I forgive you nothing, but I hope you die knowing I said so.*

He had died before it reached him, or so the arithmetic suggested. That was the mercy she chose to believe in: that the last words Henri Delacroix got from his sister were about the roof.

* * *

The note came on a Tuesday, tucked into the delivery of surgical gauze.

She nearly threw it out with the wrapping. It was a scrap of cheap paper, folded twice, unsigned, the handwriting hurried and slightly wrong-footed, as if the writer were using his off hand. Someone had scrawled six lines in pencil:

*Madame—at the risk of great foolishness. Your brother lives. He is north of the lines and cannot write himself. He speaks of you. If you wish proof, ask at the house of Mme Vasseur, Lyon, for the driver who reads.*

Camille stood in the dispensary with the note in one hand and a bundle of gauze in the other and felt the blood leave her face and then, immediately, furiously return.

The driver who reads. What sort of phrase was that? Who wrote such a thing? She read it again, and the second reading was worse than the first, because the second time she noticed the way her own heart had lurched at *your brother lives*, the animal part of her answering before the reasoning part could intervene, the way a body answers a shout of *fire* even in a stone building where no fire could live.

She should have burned it. She knew that within a minute, standing there with the matchbox in her hand. Instead she folded it small and put it in the pocket of her apron, where it lay against her hip all afternoon like a coal that wouldn't cool, and every hour or so she took it out and read it again and hated it more.

Because it was cruelty. Of course it was cruelty. She had seen this species of thing before—a shell-shocked lieutenant at Bourg-en-Bresse who'd been convinced for a month that his whole company had survived and were waiting for him at the station; mothers who received letters from dead sons because some forger in a café had discovered that grief paid better than work. The occupied regions were full of rumors, and the rumors were full of dead men walking. Half of France had a brother across the lines. It was the cruelest joke of the war that being declared dead meant nothing certain at all; men wandered for years in the fog of it, prisoners, deserters, amnesiacs, corpses without papers. And the people who preyed on that uncertainty—God. She had bandaged men who wept over less.

But it wasn't the fraudulence that stung. Fraud she could dismiss; she was competent at dismissing things; she had built a whole life on the skill. It was the specificity that stung. *He speaks of you.* Four words designed to slip past every defense, because there was no version of Henri alive or dead who wouldn't speak of her—guilt saw to that. Whoever wrote the note had understood that the wound wasn't grief. The wound was grievance. Grief you could bury. Grievance stayed sharp.

At seven o'clock, between the evening dressings and the locking of the drug cabinet, she took the note out a final time and read it by the light of the spirit lamp, and let herself, for exactly the length of one held breath, imagine it true.

Henri alive. Henri north of the lines—which meant not a prisoner in Germany, which everyone could imagine, but in the occupied zones, the forbidden country, the places the newspapers described in a vocabulary of violation. Henri moving among smugglers and informers, unable to write, sending messages through strangers' hands. Henri *speaking* of her—to whom? In what tone? Asking after her? Apologizing? Or simply, in the way of brothers, assuming her, assuming she existed in a fixed place like a church or a river, unchanged since he'd walked out?

And then the held breath ended, and she let it go, and the imagining collapsed into its components: an unsigned note, a preposterous instruction—*ask for the driver who reads*, like something out of a serialized novel—a handwriting that didn't match anything she remembered of her brother's circle, and a timing so perfect it insulted her intelligence. Two years of silence, and now, when the whole country was bracing itself for peace, when every swindler in France was sharpening his pencils for the great harvest of reunion—now a stranger discovered her brother lived.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty dispensary, and the word came out harder than she intended, and she was glad of it.

She did not burn the note. She told herself she was keeping it in case the police should want it—there were laws now about fraudulent correspondence, about profiteers of grief, and she would take it to the commissariat herself if the sender tried again—but she knew the shape of that lie the way she knew the shape of every lie she'd ever told herself, and the shape of this one was a rectangle, a folded piece of paper, warm from her pocket.

* * *

That night the east room lost another patient. A boy from the Vosges, twenty years old, gangrene of the lung, and he went quietly between two rounds, which the sisters called God's mercy and Camille called God's efficiency. She sat with him afterward because someone had to, and because sitting with the dead was the part of the work no one else wanted and she had discovered she was strangely suited for. It required precisely the talent she had most cultivated: the ability to remain in a room with an unbearable thing and do nothing.

The rain worked at the windows. Down the corridor someone moaned in a dream, and Madame Roux's chair creaked in its corner where the old woman slept upright, still waiting for whatever the street owed her.

Camille thought about her answer to Henri's last letter. *I forgive you nothing.* She had been proud of that sentence once. Now, holding the cold hand of a boy whose mother would receive a telegram in three days, she examined it the way she examined a wound that wasn't healing—turned it, probed it, looked for the foreign body. And found, as she always found, the shard beneath: not that she'd sent it, but that she'd composed it. Composed it, revised it, chosen *nothing* over *anything*. Even her unforgiveness had been a construction, a little monument raised on the grave of the relationship, and monuments, her father used to say, were how people apologized to themselves in stone.

If Henri were alive—if—then the monument was rubble. Then she would have to stand in front of a living man and say the terrible sentence out loud, and discover whether it had ever been true or only useful.

"He speaks of you," she murmured to the dead boy, testing the words in the open air for the first time. They sounded different out loud. Smaller. More like bait, and more like a bell.

Petit-Jean put his head around the curtain. "Shall I fetch the stretcher, madame?"

"In a moment." She rose, straightened the blanket to the dead boy's chin—a habit, pure habit, tenderness misdirected onto the wrong temperature of flesh—and paused. "Petit-Jean. If someone sent you a letter saying a person you hated was alive. What would you do?"

The boy considered this with the seriousness he brought to floor-sweeping. "Depends if I hated them proper," he said. "Or just hated them near."

She looked at him. Fifteen years old, lying about his age to everyone, and wiser in nine words than she had been in nine years.

"Hate isn't the same as finished," he added, and went for the stretcher.

* * *

She lasted four days. She was, in fairness to herself, proud of lasting four days—the old Camille would have gone the same afternoon, would have crossed Lyon in the rain demanding her miracle at the service door of a stranger's house. The new Camille, the armored one, the one who ran a clinic on wet coal and unpaid bills and the labor of women nobody thanked, held out four days, and then, on Saturday, closing the clinic early because the gas had failed again, she found herself standing at the corner of the rue Royale with her umbrella up and no memory of having decided to walk there.

That was the thing about armor. People praised it, and it did save your life, but it was heavy, and sometimes it moved you without your consent, the weight of it tipping you forward down a street toward a door you'd sworn not to knock on.

Mme Vasseur's house was handsome and shuttered, the sort of façade that gave away nothing, and Camille stood across the street for ten minutes telling herself the truth in an orderly fashion: the note was a fraud; the driver was a fiction or a fool; her brother was bone meal under forty feet of earth at Verdun; and whatever she hoped to find behind these shutters was not information but punishment—she wanted, some rotten part of her wanted, to be proven right that the world was exactly as cruel as she'd decided.

She did not cross the street. She stood there until the rain found the gap in her collar and ran a cold finger down her spine, and then she turned around and walked home, and the note stayed in her apron pocket, and the apron went into the wash, and she forgot—deliberately, expertly, with the whole force of a will trained on larger sufferings—to rescue it.

The ink ran. The pencil blurred into gray weather. By morning the note read like something recovered from a drowned man's coat, six lines dissolving into smudge, and she looked at it drying on the laundry line and thought: *good*, and thought: *there*, and did not think at all, for the rest of the day, about the driver who reads, or about how a woman who spent her life teaching bodies not to flinch had just flinched from a piece of paper smaller than her palm.

On Monday the mail came early, before the patients, and among the bills and the circulars there was an envelope with no return address, postmarked Lyon, addressed in a careful commercial hand to *Mademoiselle C. Delacroix, Clinique, rue des Capucins.* Not madame. Mademoiselle. Whoever wrote it had done his research.

She stood in the hall with it in both hands. Around her the clinic was waking—Petit-Jean arguing with the stove, the first coughs starting behind the curtains, the smell of carbolic rising like weather. She turned the envelope over. It was sealed properly, wax and all, which no bill ever was, and through the paper, when she tilted it to the gray light, she could see the ghost of several pages folded tight.

Her name, and underneath, in the same hand but smaller, almost apologetic, the single word: *Urgent.*

Camille Delacroix had spent five years learning not to reach for letters that were never coming. She stood in the hallway, heart hammering like a fist on a locked door, and understood—with rage, with terror, with something dangerously close to hope—that this one was coming anyway.
