## 10. The Route Through Bresse

The map came out of Marguerite Vasseur's desk drawer like a confession of its own—unfolded soft as cloth from handling, its creases gone the colour of weak tea. She spread it across the dining table after supper, weighted the corners with salt cellars and a candlestick and Luc's feverish, unsteady hands, and said, in the tone she used for dictation: *Now. Show me where.*

Odile stood over it and found that his own hands were steady.

That surprised him. For three days since Luc had laid the bundle of Henri's letters on the kitchen table—since Odile had read the last one, the one addressed to Camille, the one with his own habit written into it like a fingerprint—his body had been running on some fuel he didn't recognize. Not fear exactly, though fear was in it. Not duty either, though he had promised. It was closer to what he felt when the car needed work: an engine problem laid out before him, all its parts visible at once, demanding only that he be methodical. He had spent five years being methodical about hiding. Here, at last, was something to be methodical about in the open.

"Bourg-en-Bresse," he said, and set his finger on it. "She's at the clinic there. Sainte-Odile—" He stopped. The name of the clinic always did that to him, snagged him by the collar. "It's a hospice-nursing house. Run by secular sisters since before the war. Camille Delacroix has been on their register for three years; I checked the professional bulletins."

"You checked." Marguerite's eyebrows went up a fraction. On anyone else it would have been surprise. On her it was more like an auditor confirming a figure she'd already suspected.

"I've been reading other people's mail for five years, madame. I know how to find out things quietly." He heard how it sounded—the confession dressed as a qualification—and did not take it back. That was new too.

Luc, wrapped in one of the cook's shawls though the stove was going, coughed into his fist and traced the road east from Lyon with a cracked fingernail. "Not the direct road," he said. His voice still had the wet rattle of someone who'd slept in ditches between here and the occupied zone. "The gendarmes have taken to stopping cars on the Bourg road. Nothing official. Men in uniform without papers, which is worse than official—it means someone's paying them and no one will admit to it."

"Someone is paying them," Marguerite said flatly. "Prefect Chevalier's office has been asking questions about smuggled correspondence for two weeks. I hear things, gentlemen. My movement receives enough confiscated pamphlets to know what the police consider dangerous reading." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "They are right to worry. Print frightens them. Handwriting frightens them even more, because handwriting has an author."

Odile looked down at the map and let the geography arrange itself in his head the way routes always did—not as lines but as time. Hours behind the wheel. Places where the road narrowed. Places where a car could be seen a long way off, and places where it could not be seen at all.

* * *

He worked through the evening and most of the next morning, and it was the happiest he had been in longer than he could honestly remember.

There was a shame in that, and he examined it carefully, the way he examined everything now, holding each feeling up to the light of the letters he'd read and burned. Happiness in planning a delivery that might get them all charged—happiness was not the appropriate response. But the happiness wasn't about the danger. It was about the *having of a task*. For five years his days had been shaped entirely around other people's purposes: Marguerite's meetings, Marguerite's mail, Marguerite's war conducted from the passenger seat while he sat in front of it with his cap pulled low. He had been a hand on a wheel. A pair of ears. A shadow with a chauffeur's licence.

Now there was a thing only he could do. The letter had to reach Camille Delacroix, and it had to reach her soon, and it had to reach her carried by someone who understood why the whole enterprise was damnable and necessary both—and that someone, whatever else he was, whoever else he had failed to be, was him.

So he spread the atlas of the Ain across the workbench in the garage, where he was supposed to be greasing the chassis, and he planned.

The speaking tour gave him the shape of it. Marguerite had engagements already fixed—a meeting hall in Villefranche, a hired room in Mâcon, and then, crucially, a Sunday afternoon gathering in Bourg itself, arranged weeks ago with the women of the Ain, seamstresses and schoolteachers and the widows of mobilized men, who came to hear about the vote because the vote was the one promise the war had made them hungry enough to believe in. Marguerite would travel with him as far as Mâcon by the main roads, loud and public and entirely herself, giving interviews to any newspaper man who materialized, being precisely the nuisance the police expected her to be.

Then the tour would split. She would go on to Mâcon by train with her secretary—which was to say, with the cook's nephew pressed into a suit—and Odile would take the car east with a boot full of pamphlets, a list of provincial subscribers who wanted literature delivered, and one envelope hidden among forty others in handwriting no living soul but Odile could identify at a glance.

"It has to be me driving," he said, when he brought the plan up to the study. "If a gendarme asks, I'm delivering literature for the tour. That's true. I want it to be true all the way through. The moment I have to construct a lie, I'll construct it badly. I'm out of practice—I've never actually lied to anyone official. I've only ever withheld."

Marguerite set down her pen. She had a way of looking at him when he said these things, these small self-indictments delivered in his mild, apologetic voice, as if she were translating him from a language she was only beginning to learn.

"And the envelope?" she asked. "Is it true?"

"No," Odile admitted. "The envelope is the lie. Everything else is scaffolding around it."

"Good," said Marguerite. "That's the correct architecture. Never build a wall you can't point at honestly." She turned back to her correspondence, then paused, pen hovering. "Bourg. You'll need somewhere to stay the night if the clinic refuses you. Refusals take time, in my experience."

He hadn't told her yet that refusal was likely. He must have shown it in his face, because she went on without looking up: "Camille Delacroix cut her brother out of her life four years ago, and now her brother is legally a traitor, and you propose to hand her proof he's alive in territory we hold. She may throw the letter in the stove in front of you, Mr. Sandoval. Plan for the stove."

* * *

He planned for the stove. He also planned for everything else, and the planning filled whole hours the way water fills a lock—steadily, lifting him.

He drew the route three times and rejected it twice. The direct road through Meximieux was fastest but watched; Luc's warning had the ring of specific knowledge, and Odile had learned to trust men who looked the way Luc looked, as though their bodies were the only luggage they had left. The southern loop through Amberieu added ninety minutes and a river crossing where the bridge guard had been checking identity papers since spring. What remained was a thread of departmental roads north of the marshes—Dombes country, flat and ponded and thinly populated, where the light in late morning came off the water so white it erased the road's edges, and where a lone motorcar was more likely to be mistaken for nobody's business than for everybody's.

He copied out the distances in his neat driver's hand. Lyon to Villars, forty kilometres. Villars to Saint-André, twenty-two. Saint-André to Bourg, thirty-one. Ninety-three kilometres of France between him and the sister of a dead-alive man, and every kilometre of it ordinary, unremarkable, legal—if the envelope stayed hidden, if the car held together, if the men Luc called *the prefect's people* were watching the main roads instead of thinking like drivers.

He serviced the car himself rather than send for the mechanic. This was another novelty: doing things with his own hands that he might have delegated, because delegation meant witnesses and witnesses meant stories, and the story had to live in one skull only. He drained and refilled the crankcase. He patched the spare with a kit bought from a supplier in Vaise who asked no questions because Odile overpaid him. He cleaned the plugs until they gleamed, adjusted the carburettor by ear, listening for the idle to settle into that low, contented hum that meant the machine trusted him. Grease to the wrists. Petrol under the nails. His father had been a locksmith, dead these fifteen years, and Odile thought of him oddly often now—of the way the old man's hands had moved inside locks, patient, exploratory, certain that every mechanism yielded to attention. *You cannot force a lock,* his father used to say. *You can only understand it until it opens.* He had applied the principle to envelopes for years. He was applying it to his own conscience now, and finding the tumblers stiff with rust.

On the third day he wrote out the cover story and rehearsed it aloud in the garage, to the car, feeling like an idiot and doing it anyway. *Literature run for Madame Vasseur's tour. Eleven addresses in the Ain, all subscribed to the bulletin. Returning Thursday or Friday depending on roads.* He memorized eleven names. He invented plausible delays. He packed the pamphlet bundles himself, and when he reached the last one—the largest, tied with the same plain string as the rest—he slid the envelope flat against the cardboard backing in the centre, where fingers probing a bundle's edges would find nothing but paper and indignation. Then he retied the string with hands that did not shake, and stood looking at the finished stack, and understood that he had just done the truest work of his adult life and disguised it as parcel-wrapping.

Honestly this time, he reminded himself. Honest in intention even when concealed in string. There was a difference, and the difference was everything, and he was only now learning its weight.

* * *

Marguerite found him in the garage at dusk, two days before departure.

She came down the steps from the kitchen with a lamp, and the light swung her shadow huge and wavering along the whitewashed wall, and she stood for a moment at the edge of the lamplight watching him check the tie-downs on the roof rack for perhaps the fourth time that hour.

"They'll notice," she said. "The police. A speaking tour is one thing; a speaking tour with a motorcar making deliveries into the Bresse is a pattern, and patterns are what they're paid to see."

"I've thought about that."

"I assumed. Tell me anyway. If I'm to lend you my name to hide behind, I want to know exactly how thin the wall is."

He wiped his hands and told her. The tour's public itinerary was printed in the bulletin—Villefranche, Mâcon, Bourg—so the Bourg stop explained his presence on those roads without explanation. The literature deliveries were real; eleven women in the Ain genuinely wanted their bulletins, and genuine errands were the best camouflage because they required no acting, only doing. If he was stopped, the pamphlets were legal—censored in spirit, tolerated in fact, too small a fish for anyone to fry. And the envelope—

"The envelope is in the middle of bundle nine," he said. "Bundle nine goes to the hospice nursing house on the rue de la République. Which happens to be true. Camille Delacroix subscribes. She's been receiving the bulletin for two years under her professional name." He hesitated, then finished it: "I didn't choose that address for the bundle. I chose it because it was already the truth. I'm trying to keep as much of this true as possible, madame. It seemed safer."

Marguerite was quiet for a while. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them the house went on with its evening noises—the range door, a chair scraped, the muffled radio that Luc was not supposed to be listening to and was.

"Do you know," she said at last, "when I took you on, I assumed I was hiring a pair of hands and a licence. A quiet young man who drove well and asked nothing." She lifted the lamp a little, and the light caught the grease on his knuckles, the map folded in his breast pocket, the pamphlet stacks squared like bricks. "I am not certain whether I misjudged you or judged you perfectly. A quiet man who reads everything and asks nothing is either a coward or a keeper of promises, and it has taken me this long to work out which."

"Which?" Odile asked, though his voice came out smaller than he intended.

"That's what Bourg is for," she said. "Go and find out. And Mr. Sandoval—" She turned toward the stairs, then paused with her back to him, and he saw her shoulders square themselves the way they did before she stepped onto a platform. "If they stop you, and it goes wrong, you say nothing about me. Not my knowledge, not my permission. You acted alone, out of some private sentimentality I'd have forbidden had I known. I'll confirm it myself if I have to. Do you understand?"

He understood, and it landed in him strangely—that she was offering him the exact shape of betrayal he had practiced on others, wearing it as protection. That she would let him make her the villain of a gendarme's report to keep his name clean.

"Madame," he said, "I've spent five years keeping other people's secrets safe. Let me keep yours properly. I'm better at silence than you think."

"I know precisely how good you are at silence," Marguerite said dryly. "That is rather the entire problem." And she went up the stairs with her lamp, leaving him alone in the garage with the smell of petrol and the dark coming down.

* * *

The night before departure he did not sleep well, but neither did he pace. He lay in his narrow room above the garage and listened to the wind coming off the Rhône and ran the route one final time, kilometre by kilometre, turn by turn, the way a priest runs through an office. At some hour past midnight he got up, struck a match, and took out the one letter he permitted himself to carry separately—the last of Henri's, the one addressed to Camille, which Luc had pressed on him with shaking insistence and which Odile had sworn, this once, not to read.

He had read it. Of course he had read it. That rule of his—never touch what isn't yours—had been broken so thoroughly in the service of everyone else's secrets that he sometimes wondered if it had ever existed, or if it was simply the story he told about himself to bear the reading he couldn't stop doing. But this letter he had read differently. Slowly. As a pilgrim reads.

It was short. It did not apologize. Henri Delacroix had never apologized in his life, Odile remembered that much from 1913—the man's particular pride, his terrible flaw, the wound he'd given his sister without ever bending to close it. Instead the letter said: *I saw what I saw. I wrote down the names. Whatever they call me for it, I was there, and I was not silent.* And beneath that, in a hand gone ragged with cold and worse: *Tell Camille I do not ask to be forgiven. Only to be believed.*

Odile had sat with that sentence a long time, the match burning down toward his fingers.

Believed. Not forgiven. Even now, half-dead in occupied country, hunted by his own army's paperwork, Henri Delacroix understood that the debt he owed his sister was not contrition but credibility. It was the proudest, loneliest, most infuriating thing Odile had ever read, and he had read thousands of letters, and none of them—not love letters, not death notifications, not the frantic apologies men sent to wives they'd abandoned—none had ever made him feel so completely that he understood its author.

He blew out the match. He put the letter back inside his shirt, against his chest, where it rode warm and thin as a second heartbeat, and he lay down again and let the route carry him toward sleep: Villars, Saint-André, the white flats of the Dombes, the ponds holding pieces of sky, and at the end of it all a clinic on the rue de la République where a woman who did not know he existed would open a door.

They left at six the following morning, in fog thick as wool, Marguerite on the seat beside him with her hat pinned against the damp and her satchel of speeches at her feet. Lyon woke behind them in bells and smoke. The road unwound ahead, straight and pale and empty, and Odile drove with both hands on the wheel and the letter over his heart, and for the first kilometres he allowed himself to feel it plainly: not fear, not yet, but readiness—sharp and clean as the smell of cold petrol.

By Villefranche the fog had thinned to a bright haze, and by the road beyond it, standing at ease outside a café with their caps set at an angle that had nothing casual in it, three gendarmes were watching the traffic go by—and, Odile understood with a lurch that dropped through him like a stone through water, watching one motorcar in particular.
