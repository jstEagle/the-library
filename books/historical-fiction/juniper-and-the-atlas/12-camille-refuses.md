## 12. Camille Refuses

The clinic smelled of carbolic and wet stone, and by seven o'clock in the evening the light through its tall windows had gone the color of watered milk. Camille Delacroix counted it a good smell, the carbolic—that sharp, antiseptic honesty. It was the only thing in her life that had never lied to her. Stone kept its dampness honestly too. People did not.

She was wrapping a splinted wrist when the motorcar arrived. She heard it before she saw it: the wrong kind of engine for her street, too well-fed, too urban, coughing itself to a stop outside the gate where usually only cart wheels and clogs passed. Through the window she watched two men get out—one tall, one thin as a fence post—and she felt the small automatic tightening in her chest that five years of war had taught her body to produce at the sight of any man in civilian clothes approaching a hospital. Civilian clothes meant bad news. Uniforms at least told you whose bad news.

"Madame Delacroix?" The tall one had a driver's flat cap pulled low and a voice that had been trained quiet, the way some men train dogs.

"Sister Delacroix," she said, not looking up from the bandage she was tying off on old Madame Perrin's wrist. "If you've come about a patient, wait. If you've come about money, we have none. If you've come from the prefecture—" she tied the knot, snugged it, allowed herself one glance— "you're late. They came Tuesday."

"We've come about your brother."

The name landed in her the way a dropped instrument lands on a tile floor—a bright, clattering shock, and then silence, and then everyone pretending nothing had happened.

"My brother is buried at Verdun," she said. "Douaumont sector. There is no grave marker worth the name, which suited him, I'm sure. He was never much for staying put."

She turned back to Madame Perrin, adjusted the sling, murmured something soothing about keeping it dry, and sent the old woman out with a touch on her shoulder that was gentle precisely because Camille's voice could not be. Then she took off her apron, folded it once, laid it on the table, and looked at them properly.

They were an odd pair. The tall one had the stillness of a man used to sitting behind things—wheels, hedges, other people's conversations. His hands were clean but chapped at the knuckles, a driver's hands, a servant's hands. The thin one stood badly, weight shifted off one hip like a man carrying an injury he'd stopped complaining about, and his eyes went around the room cataloging exits, which made her mark him as someone recently and unhappily acquainted with the police.

"You said 'your brother,'" she said. "Not 'the late Monsieur Delacroix.' Choose your words carefully. I have buried forty-one men this year and I haven't the patience left for riddles."

* * *

Odile had rehearsed this moment the whole way from the checkpoint—the words, the order of them, the tone. He had imagined every version of her face. In his imagination she had wept; he had prepared for weeping. What he had not prepared for was this: a woman built entirely of straight lines, arms crossed, chin level, looking at him with the frank appraisal of someone triaging a wound and finding it already gangrenous.

He had thought, absurdly, that she might see Henri in him. That some resemblance of loyalty might show in his face, the way a family resemblance shows in a jaw. But she looked at him and saw nothing—no recognition, no curiosity even, just the flat professional patience of a nurse with one more stranger asking one more thing of her.

And why should she see anything? He had spent five years making himself unseeable. He had gotten good at it. God help him, he had succeeded.

"I have letters," Odile said. "Written by your brother. Not old ones. Recent ones."

Something moved behind her eyes—quick, suppressed, like a fish turning under ice—and was gone.

"If this is a scheme," she said, "it's an old one. Men have come before. After Verdun, twice. Once with a wedding ring he supposedly mailed from the front, wanting money to retrieve it from a postal office in Vichy. Once with a story about prison camps." She uncrossed her arms only to fold them tighter. "Do you know what my brother's last letter to me said? Before Verdun. Do you know what four years of estrangement produced, at the end, when he knew he was going into the line?"

Camille heard her own voice rising and hated it, and kept rising anyway, because some griefs are doors that will not stay shut no matter how many times you close them.

"He wrote to apologize," she said. "Four pages. I have them still. I could recite them. He apologized for leaving, apologized for the quarrel, apologized for Father, apologized for everything except the one thing that mattered, which was that he *left*, and left me alone in that house with a dying man and a failing farm and a town full of women who asked after him every Sunday at Mass until I couldn't bear to go to Mass anymore. And then he signed it, and put a stamp on it, and died, and I was expected to be grateful for four pages of ink."

She stopped. The clinic was very quiet. Somewhere down the ward a man coughed in his sleep, a deep wet sound, and turned over.

The thin one—Luc—had gone pale. The tall one hadn't moved at all. That was the strange thing, she would think later. Most men, shouted at, flinched or argued or fled. This one simply stood there and received it, the way a wall receives weather, as if he had been standing in other people's storms so long he had forgotten he was permitted to be indoors.

"I'm sorry," he said finally, and his voice was quieter than hers, which somehow infuriated her more. "I didn't know about the quarrel. I knew some of it. Not all."

"You know nothing about it."

"No," he agreed. "I know about the letters, though."

* * *

He brought the packet out from inside his coat, and Camille saw it and felt the floor tilt under her professional composure.

It wasn't the sight of paper. Paper meant nothing; she handled paper all day, requisitions and death notices and the endless forms of a country bleeding into ledgers. It was the handwriting visible on the topmost envelope—just three words of an address, the beginning of *Camille Delacroix, Rue de la—*—and the hand was unmistakable. That slanting, impatient, oddly beautiful script, the letters leaning forward like runners at a starting line. Henri had written the way he rode horses and left rooms: fast, leaning into whatever came next.

Five years. She had seen that hand on envelopes perhaps two hundred times in her life, and every envelope had been an event. Now here were dozens of them, bundled in string, creased and stained, dated across years in which he had been officially dead, and her body reacted before her mind could intervene—a hot flush, a ringing in the ears, the primitive animal conviction that the dead do not write.

"That's impossible," she said. Her voice came out very small. She despised it for that.

"The dates are real," the tall one said. "You can check them against events. One mentions a specific shell of a church in a village you'll recognize. Another refers to a flood—he mentions the Saône coming up over the quay, spring of 1916. You'd have read about it. He did."

"How." It wasn't even a question. It was a hand reaching out to grab the world and finding nothing solid. "How does a dead man write letters."

"He isn't dead," Odile said. "He was wounded at Verdun, taken prisoner, and he's been in the occupied territories since. He's been sending letters out through smugglers for years. Those are copies he kept, or ones that were intercepted, or—I don't fully know. There's a man who can explain better than I can. But they're his. You know the hand as well as I do."

And there it was—the mistake, the crack in her armor, made not by his argument but by his grammar. *As well as I do.* Spoken with the easy intimacy of a man who had indeed seen that handwriting often enough to know its moods.

"When," Camille said slowly, "did you learn my brother's handwriting?"

The tall one's face did something very slight and terrible. A stillness came over it that was not calm.

* * *

Later, Luc would tell Odile that he had watched the whole exchange the way one watches a fuse burn toward a charge, knowing exactly where it would end and powerless to stop it. Because Camille Delacroix was not a stupid woman, and grief had not dulled her; it had sharpened her, the way repeated freezing sharpens a road into glass. She had spent five years being lied to by omission—the army, the town, the polite faces at Mass—and her mind had become an instrument for finding omissions.

"Answer the question," she said.

"I delivered mail," Odile said. "In Lyon. For the past five years."

"You delivered mail." She let the words sit. "For a lady suffragist. Whose mail you opened first."

It was not even a question, and the silence that followed it confirmed everything, and Odile stood in that confirmation like a man standing in the wreckage of his own house, and said, "Yes."

"And how many of his letters did you open?"

"I don't—"

"*How many.*"

"All of them," Odile said. "I read all of them."

* * *

What she felt then was not what anyone would have predicted, least of all herself.

She had expected, if this moment ever came—if the dead brother ever sent word—it would come as rescue. All these years she had carried the quarrel like a stone in her shoe, small and constant and maddening, and the fantasy of his return had always ended the same way: he walks in, he explains, the stone comes out. She had hated herself for the fantasy and kept it anyway, the way one keeps any vice committed in private.

Now the moment had come, and it was not rescue. It was arithmetic. Every letter in that bundle was a letter that should have reached her and had not. Every year of silence—every Sunday she had skipped, every condolence she had swallowed, every night she had lain awake composing answers to a dead man—had been manufactured, maintained, sustained by the quiet hands of strangers opening envelopes in hallways and deciding what she deserved to know.

"You read them," she said. Her voice had gone flat and precise, which those who worked with her knew was far more dangerous than the raised voice. "All of them. His and everyone else's. For five years."

"Yes."

"And you decided—" she picked up the bundle from the table and held it, feeling its obscene living weight— "what arrived. What didn't. Whether a sister got word her brother lived, or spent the armistice season believing he was under the mud at Douaumont."

"There were reasons—"

"I don't doubt it." She set the bundle down again, deliberately, gently, which was worse. "Everyone has reasons. The censor has reasons. The prefecture has reasons. My brother had reasons for leaving. I have heard reasons my whole life, monsieur, and I'll tell you what I've learned about them: they are always the property of the person who benefits from the silence."

Luc started to say something and she silenced him with a look.

"And now," she continued, "now that the armistice is days away, now that it's dangerous, now that the police want this correspondence—you bring it to me. Not in 1915. Not in 1917. Today. When delivering it costs you something, and keeping it would cost you more." She untied her apron from the hook, though her shift was not over, though part of her knew she was retreating and could not stop. "You want absolution dressed as a delivery, monsieur. I don't perform absolutions. I set bones. Go find a priest."

She held out her hand for the letter—not to keep, she told herself, only to see it, only to verify—and then, when Odile took the single sealed envelope from the top of the bundle and offered it, the final letter, the promise-letter, the one that had crossed a continent and a checkpoint to reach this room—

She looked at it. At the address in that forward-leaning hand. At her own name written by her brother's living fingers, dated eleven days ago.

She looked at it, and every hour of the last five years rose up in her at once—the skipped Masses, the condolences, the empty chair at her father's funeral where Henri should have sat and did not because he was busy being dead, being mourned, being forgiven in advance by everyone including herself—

And she closed her eyes, and took her hand back.

"No," she said.

* * *

The refusal came out of her like a tooth coming out—root and all, a snap, and then a strange hollow bleeding space where the ache had lived so long she had mistaken it for part of her jaw.

"No," she said again, steadier. "I won't take it. I won't read it. Tell him—" and here her voice did break, once, briefly, and she let it, because she was done managing herself for the comfort of strangers— "tell him he can write to me when he can walk through that door and say it to my face. Not before. Not through smugglers, not through strangers, not through *drivers*. Face. Or nothing."

Odile stood holding the letter out into the empty space where her hand had declined to be. He held it a long time. Outside, the evening had gone fully dark, and the clinic windows threw their lamplight onto the gravel, and somewhere a dog was barking at the new sound of engines in a world where engines were still rare enough to be remarkable.

Then, slowly, he withdrew the letter. And Camille, watching him do it, saw for the first time something in his face—not guilt exactly, and not hurt, but the particular devastation of a man discovering that the debt he had crossed a province to pay was not the debt that was owed. He tucked the envelope back inside his coat, over his heart, which she noticed without meaning to, because it was such an odd place to carry a letter, unless it was the only warm place he had.

"For what it's worth," Odile said at the door, "he never once believed you wouldn't take it. It was the one thing he was sure of. He said—his sister doesn't refuse letters. She refuses people."

The door closed behind them. Camille stood in her ward among the sleeping wounded, in the smell of carbolic and honest stone, and found that her hands were shaking—not with rage now, she realized. With something that had been waiting under the rage for five years, patient as water under ice.

She wanted the letter. She wanted it so badly her teeth ached.

* * *

They did not speak until the engine was running. Then Luc, hunched in the seat beside Odile, watching the clinic lights shrink in the mirror, said what both of them were thinking.

"She took the whole bundle's weight and put it down again. She touched it and gave it back. That's not refusal, Odile. I've been refused plenty. That's a woman setting something down so her hands are free."

"Free for what?"

"For whatever comes next." Luc glanced sideways. "Which brings us to the problem. We showed her face, the car, the plates, the whole business, hours after the prefecture started pulling smugglers in. She knows your name now—or she can have it by morning. If the official's men come to her tomorrow with questions and a warrant, what does she say? She hates you. She hates Henri. She has every reason in France to hand that letter over and wash her hands of the entire Delacroix affair."

Odile drove in silence for half a kilometer, poplars flickering past in the headlamps like the spokes of an enormous wheel.

"She won't," he said at last.

"You're betting her brother's life on that."

"I'm betting more than that." He changed gears. The engine climbed. "But it doesn't matter what she does, because we won't be here when they come. Turn the map light on, Luc. There's a printing works on the rue Sainte-Hélène, and Marguerite wants the contents of that letter set in type by Thursday—and there's something in it, something Henri saw, something with names in it, that I haven't told you yet."

Luc reached for the map case. "Something bad?"

Odile's eyes stayed on the road, on the long dark running ahead of the lamps.

"Something that explains," he said, "why the prefecture wants this letter destroyed so badly—and why, once Camille reads what's in it, she's going to wish she'd taken it from me tonight."
