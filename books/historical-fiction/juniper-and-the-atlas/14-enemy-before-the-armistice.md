## 14. Enemy Before the Armistice

Marguerite Vasseur had learned to read newspapers the way other women read men's faces: for what was withheld. The headline in *Le Progrès* that November morning told her almost nothing and everything at once.

**COLLABORATION CHARGES PREPARED AGAINST LOCAL MAN — ALIVE BEHIND GERMAN LINES**

No name. Not yet. The law, it seemed, moved faster than grief, faster than the armistice itself, which had spent three days now refusing to be signed while delegates rearranged their chairs and their pride at Compiègne. The war could not end until every document was properly stamped, and apparently neither could its betrayals.

She sat in the morning room with her tea going cold — coffee had become a rumor weeks ago; tea she hoarded like contraband and drank like a penance — and read the article four times. A man from the region. A man presumed dead at Verdun who had surfaced, according to unnamed sources within the prefecture, in territory still held by the enemy, sending correspondence through smuggling channels. Charges drafted before the guns fell silent. The phrase the reporter chose, with a delicacy Marguerite did not believe he possessed, was *preemptive justice*.

Henri Delacroix.

She set the paper down on the arm of her chair, aligned its edge with the upholstery seam, and made herself admit how long she had known.

Not his name — his name had arrived only this week, folded inside Odile's confession like a blade wrapped in linen. But the shape of it. For five years something in her household had been slightly out of true, the way a door swells in wet weather and closes with a sound you learn to ignore because correcting it costs more than tolerating it. Letters that came already opened — not crudely, not all of them, but enough. A driver who watched everything and offered nothing, whose silence had the particular density of a thing maintained rather than natural. She had told herself it was discretion. She had told herself it was loyalty. She had told herself a great many things about Odile Sandoval, and the trouble with telling yourself things is that eventually you must either act on them or admit they were lies.

The fire needed tending. She let it need tending.

Outside, Lyon went about its gray business. Coal smoke stood over the rooftops in columns, and somewhere down the rue Sainte-Hélène a knife grinder cried his services in a voice worn down to a single note. Four years of war had taught the city to keep working through anything — through ration queues and air raid drills and the lists of the dead posted each week outside the mairie, names printed in columns so long the paste never dried before the next sheet covered them. Now the papers promised peace the way a moneylender promises relief: technically true, payable later, interest accruing.

Marguerite had spent the war learning exactly one lesson, and she had paid dearly for the tuition: power is not a possession. It is a position under siege. Every inch of ground she held — the newsletter, the speaking circuit, the grudging respect of even the men who despised her — was held the way soldiers hold a trench, by constant expenditure, by sleeplessness, by the willingness to be hated in specific and escalating degrees. The vote would not be won by women who appeared compromised. This was not cynicism. It was arithmetic. One scandal, one whiff of association with collaboration, one photograph of her household dragged into a treason proceeding, and twenty years of work would be repurposed as evidence against her. *These are the women who sheltered traitors.* The sentence wrote itself; some ambitious prosecutor or newspaperman was probably drafting it at this very moment, in an office that smelled of cheap tobacco, feeling clever.

And Odile.

She thought of him standing in the courtyard yesterday evening after his confession, hands loose at his sides the way soldiers stand when they've decided not to reach for anything. He had laid out his years of small crimes with the flat thoroughness of a man reading an inventory — every letter opened, every secret carried, and finally the promise made in peacetime to a lodger who was supposed to be dead, and the letter sitting somewhere near him at that moment like a coal that could not be put down and could not be swallowed.

She should have dismissed him then. Any prudent woman would have. Prudence, however, was a virtue she had noticed tended to be prescribed most enthusiastically by people who had never needed courage, and she found herself unwilling to take advice, even her own, from that quarter.

Instead she had said: *Tell me when you leave, and tell me when you return.* Which was not forgiveness and not permission but something more dangerous than both — involvement.

Now the involvement had a name in the newspaper, though not yet his name, not yet hers.

* * *

The newsletter office occupied two rooms above a shuttered milliner's shop off the Cours de Verdun, and it smelled of ink, cold candle wax, and the particular staleness of rooms where women worked too many hours without adequate food because the budget demanded sacrifice and the cause demanded the budget. Marguerite arrived at ten to find Élodie Brissac already at the composing frame, sleeves rolled, hair escaping its pins in a manner that would have scandalized Marguerite's mother and which Marguerite privately considered a form of rhetoric.

"You've seen it." Élodie did not look up from the type tray. She was forty-two, widowed at Mons along with her husband's entire regiment, and she had the habit of stating questions as facts because facts required no permission from anyone.

"The prefecture is preparing charges against a man who may be alive."

"A man who sent mail through smugglers while France couldn't be bothered to send his family so much as a correction of his death notice." Élodie set a line of type with small precise violence. "If he carried letters across lines, half the pays behind the front has done the same for bread and news since 1914. They'll hang one man to warn the rest."

"They'll hang him," Marguerite said, "because someone useful wants him hanged, and I don't yet know who." She removed her gloves finger by finger. "Which means we print nothing. Not yet."

Élodie turned. Her face, usually so mobile, had gone very still. "You're protecting your driver."

"I'm protecting—" Marguerite began, and stopped, because the honest continuation was *myself*, and she had built a movement on the principle that women must stop lying about what they wanted and why. "Both. I refuse to distinguish at the moment. It's a luxury I'm allowing myself."

"Then allow me to describe what happens if you're wrong." Élodie wiped her hands on her apron, leaving faint black crescents. "When the name prints — and it will print, the prefecture leaks like the Rhône in spring — every enemy we have will connect him to this house within a week. You know they already watch us. Berthier's people attend our meetings and write down what we say. If a collaborator's correspondence passed through the hands of the suffragists' chauffeur—"

"He is not a collaborator."

"You know that how?"

It was the right question. It was the question she had been circling since dawn, and the answer embarrassed her: she knew it the way she knew when a government minister was lying on the floor of the Assembly — not from evidence but from the architecture of the lie, from having spent a lifetime studying how power arranged its justifications. Henri Delacroix was being charged not for what he had done but for what someone needed done to him. But she could not print *I have a feeling*, and she could not testify to a tribunal on the strength of her instincts about a man she'd never met, filtered through the silences of a driver she'd trusted precisely because he never asked to be trusted.

"I know it insufficiently," she said at last. "Which is why we wait."

Élodie studied her for a long moment. Then she began putting away the type she had set, letter by letter, reversing her own work. "One week," she said. "After that I go to press with whatever exists, because if we sit on news of a miscarriage of justice to protect ourselves, then we are the women our enemies say we are — interested only in our own enfranchisement, indifferent to everyone else's chains."

"And if printing it destroys the movement?"

"Then the movement deserved to be built on better foundations." She snapped the last piece of type into its slot. "You taught me that, Marguerite. In 1911, in a rented hall in Villeurbanne, when there were eleven of us and two were your cousins. I took notes."

Marguerite had no memory of saying anything worth noting in 1911. She suspected, uneasily, that this was because she had been young enough then to speak without first calculating the cost. There were versions of herself she had buried deliberately, and she sometimes caught them looking up at her from the bottoms of decisions.

"One week," she agreed.

* * *

Berthier came to see her at four o'clock, which meant he had wanted her to know he was coming and had chosen the hour for maximum inconvenience.

Aristide Berthier held some title involving agricultural supply coordination that functioned, in practice, as a license to move through three departments like weather. She had met him at official functions before the war, where he'd been the sort of man who shook women's hands briefly and looked past them while doing it, and she had crossed him twice since — once over grain requisition quotas affecting the families of striking textile workers, once over a permit for a public meeting that he had revoked on grounds of public order and she had restored through the press. He did not like her. The dislike was professional, mutual, and in his case, she had long suspected, useful: hatred of inconvenient women played well in certain offices.

He sat in her morning room without being invited to, accepted nothing, and placed his hat on his knee like a man posing for a portrait titled *Patience*.

"Madame Vasseur. You've seen the papers."

"I read several. It's a vice."

"There's a matter touching your establishment." He said *establishment* the way another man might say *den*. "Correspondence moving illegally between the occupied zones and this region passes through channels. Some of those channels appear to intersect with certain households of progressive reputation." He smiled without warmth, an expression like a lamp with the flame turned too low. "You understand I mention this purely as courtesy. There are those in the prefecture less courteous than I."

"Name the households," Marguerite said, "or withdraw the insinuation."

"Insinuation is such an ugly word. I'd call it foresight." He examined the room — the books, the map of the departments pinned above the mantel with red thread marking her speaking routes, the photograph of her late father in his magistrate's collar. "You've built something remarkable here. A newspaper of your own. An audience. Influence." His eyes came back to hers. "Influence is like credit, madame. Extended generously — until one bad quarter."

"Is that the courtesy? A warning about my credit?"

"That's the courtesy." He rose, settled his hat. At the door he paused, and she saw that everything before had been preamble, that this was the actual message, delivered now because he wished her to receive it alone and standing. "The Delacroix affair will develop quickly. When it does, associations will surface. Some people, finding themselves implicated, choose loyalty. Admirable, often fatal. Others choose clarity." He put on his hat. "Clarity ages better."

The door closed. Marguerite stood in her own morning room, in her own house, and felt the temperature of the offer precisely.

He was telling her that the charges were manufactured — he could not have resisted letting her see the machinery, men like Berthier always needed their audience to glimpse the machinery; power unobserved bored them. He was telling her that Henri Delacroix's crime was knowing something, and that the something touched Berthier himself. And he was telling her that if she surrendered whoever in her household connected her to the affair — a driver, a nobody, a man who read other people's letters — she could walk through the coming storm untouched, perhaps even strengthened, the suffragist who cooperated, who kept her accounts clear.

All it would cost was one invisible man.

That was the corruption, she thought, and it did not arrive as temptation arrives, dramatic and recognizable. It arrived as reason. *He deceived you for five years.* *He opened your correspondence.* *He is one man; the vote is half of humanity.* Every clause defensible. The whole of it a betrayal dressed in arithmetic, and she could feel how easily her hand would sign it, how little it would even cost, because Odile Sandoval had made himself so small for so long that handing him over would feel less like sacrifice than like tidying.

She thought of him in the courtyard, offering up his years of thefts like coins counted onto a table, waiting to be refused.

Her father the magistrate had once told her, when she was young enough to ask dangerous questions, that the law was the story a society told about itself on its best behavior. She had built her life on a parallel conviction: that principles were the stories people told about themselves on their best behavior, and that the test was never whether you believed them in the hall in Villeurbanne with eleven supporters and your cousins taking notes. The test was a gray November afternoon, tea gone cold, a man with a hat on his knee offering you a clean escape priced at one human being.

She crossed to the window. Below, in the courtyard, Odile was cleaning the car's windscreen with a rag, methodical, unhurried, a figure so thoroughly composed of usefulness that she understood now the invisibility had been armor and not nature. Somewhere north of the city, in a cell or an office, Luc — the stranger, the courier — was presumably answering questions from Berthier's colleagues about routes and contacts. Somewhere beyond the lines, a living dead man waited to be declared a traitor by a country that had already declared him dead once and found it convenient.

*Clarity ages better,* Berthier had said.

Marguerite Vasseur rang the bell for her housekeeper, and when the woman came, gave instructions in the voice she used for resolutions: supper for two in the morning room, the good lamp brought down, and the car ready at eight the following morning.

Then she sat at her writing desk, drew a fresh sheet toward her, and began, in a hand that did not hesitate, a memorandum of everything Aristide Berthier had said — dated, timed, to be witnessed and sealed before midnight.

If the storm intended to find her household, it would find a house prepared.
