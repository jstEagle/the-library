## 15. The Official in Question

Luc had learned in the war that a man's name was the first thing taken from him. At the triage posts outside Verdun they did not ask who you were; they asked where you bled, and they wrote a number on your forehead in grease pencil, and after that you were a wound with a bed number until someone remembered to give you back your name. He had carried that lesson out of the trenches like shrapnel, too deep to cut out: *learn who holds the ledger, because the ledger does not care what is true.*

Now, standing in the corridor of the prefecture annex on the quai, hat in his hand like any other petitioner, he read the ledger.

The name at the top of every document the clerk let him glimpse—every requisition, every memorandum stamped with the prefect's seal and annotated in a small, fastidious, self-satisfied hand—was **Bresson**. Deputy Bresson. Commissioner of Supply for the Rhône department. The man whose portrait hung in three shop windows along the rue de la République beside the tricolor and the notice *IL A DONNÉ POUR LES POILUS*—*he gave for our soldiers*—above photographs of crates of tinned beef and bolts of army wool.

The same man whose warehouse Luc had crawled past at two in the morning in February, in the rain, counting lorries by the sound of their axles. The same man whose signature, half-erased but unmistakable, sat at the bottom of delivery orders routing American relief flour to a trading house in Geneva that existed, so far as Luc could determine, on one floor of one office occupied by one clerk and a very good telephone.

And the same man who had, four days ago, personally requested that the police intensify their inquiries into smuggled correspondence crossing from the occupied zones.

Luc stood very still in that corridor and felt the whole architecture of it assemble itself behind his eyes, brick by cold brick, the way a fever assembles its pattern before the sweat comes. It was not a conspiracy in the way the newspapers meant it—not men in a room dividing spoils over candlelight. It was worse than that. It was a conspiracy of convenience, each piece doing its ordinary work: a deputy protecting a supply line, a police service hungry to look useful before the armistice made everyone account for themselves, a public so starved for traitors that any traitor would do. And threaded through all of it, thin as wire and just as cutting, one soldier from Lyon who had seen the wrong lorries in the wrong place and written it down, in his beautiful patient hand, and sent it through channels that were supposed to be invisible.

Henri Delacroix had named names. Luc had told Odile as much, or nearly enough as much—the letter was testimony, the letter named an official. But he had not said the name aloud, even then, even in the safe dark of Marguerite Vasseur's kitchen, because a name spoken is a thing that can be reported, and Luc had spent four years learning what reports do to men.

He said it now, silently, to himself, reading it again in the margin of the memorandum the absent-minded clerk had left facing outward:

*Bresson, A.*

So it was settled. The charges being drafted against Henri Delacroix—collaboration, correspondence with the enemy, traffic in contraband mail—were not the law catching up with a man. They were a weapon being sharpened for use. Henri had witnessed the profiteering; the profiteer had learned that a witness lived; and the fastest way to kill a witness in France in November 1918 was to make him a collaborator first, because no one would listen afterward. Dead men could be decorated posthumously, rehabilitated, grieved. Accused men could simply be erased while still breathing.

Luc turned from the clerk's desk and walked out into the grey light with his heart beating slow and enormous, like footsteps coming up a stair.

* * *

He should have gone straight back to the house on the montée du Chemin-Neuf. That was the plan, such as plans survived contact with these days: tell Odile the name, tell Madame Vasseur the shape of it, get the letter printed or copied or scattered like leaflets from a plane before anyone could close a hand around it. Every hour mattered now. He understood that the way a man understands frostbite—by the numbness spreading, by how little time remained before the feeling went out of the fingers altogether.

Instead he did something foolish, which was to go by way of the Palais de Justice, because he wanted to see with his own eyes whether the machinery was already turning. And there, in the marble hallway where clerks hurried with files under their arms like men carrying water from a burning house, he saw a gendarme he knew.

Brissac. A sergeant, thick-necked, decent in a dull way, the sort of policeman who beat suspects only when instructed. They had drunk wine together twice during the occupation years, in the days when Luc still passed for a minor functionary of the postal control service—a fiction he had worn so long it had begun to fit him better than his own face. Brissac raised his eyebrows in greeting and then, oddly, lowered them.

"Nystrom." Not hostile. Almost apologetic. "You're here about the Delacroix file."

The hallway did not stop. Luc kept his body still, kept his breathing level, and noted with a strange clinical detachment that his hands had gone cold at the fingertips exactly as they had in the aid post in '16, when the number-pencil came toward his forehead.

"I don't know what you mean," he said, which was true in the way that all his best sentences were true.

"Sure you don't." Brissac glanced down the corridor, and his voice dropped into the register men used for things they were ashamed to say loudly. "Look. There's a request in the file. From upstairs. Anyone connected to the Delacroix matter gets—talked to. Names taken. You understand me. I'm saying it plain because we've drunk together." He shifted his weight. "Go home, Nystrom. Whatever it is, it isn't yours."

"Who signed the request?"

"You know who signs things."

Yes. Luc knew. Bresson's small fastidious hand, marching across the page like a column of well-fed infantry.

"I appreciate the warning," Luc said, and meant it, and walked out of the Palais de Justice without hurrying, because hurrying was what guilty men did and also because his legs had begun to feel like borrowed legs, and he did not trust them to hurry honestly.

They took him at the corner of the rue Sainte-Hélène. Two men, plainclothes, one of them the one called Ferrand whom Luc had seen at the prefecture annex holding his hat the same way Luc held his own, so that all the hats in Lyon seemed part of the same conspiracy of politeness. They did not shout. They did not even grip him hard. One took each arm as if helping him across an icy street, and a black touring car stood at the curb with its engine running, and a third man inside opened the door, and the whole operation had the smoothness of something rehearsed against men exactly his size.

"Sergeant Nystrom," said Ferrand, conversationally, as the car pulled away from the curb and the rain began again, fine as sieved flour. "Or is it Monsieur Nystrom? We've been trying to establish that."

"The war's over," Luc said. "Nearly."

"Not for some people."

The car crossed the Saône. Through the wet glass Luc watched the river carry its grey freight of light toward the south, and he thought, with a clarity that felt almost like peace: *they will hold me somewhere quiet, and they will ask questions, and meanwhile no one will bring the letter to the sister, and the letter will sit in Odile Sandoval's careful hands going soft with fear.* He thought of the young driver's face—that particular stillness he had, the stillness of a man who has spent years making himself into furniture, into wallpaper, into a thing that sees and is never seen. Luc had chosen him precisely because of that invisibility, and now he understood, riding through the rain between two policemen who smelled of tobacco and wet wool, that he might have chosen him into a trap of a different kind: that invisibility, once needed, could be denied, doubted, punished.

"Where are we going?" he asked, mostly to hear how they answered.

"Someplace we can talk," Ferrand said. "About letters. You'd know about those."

"I know about stamps," Luc said, and closed his eyes, and began, methodically, to memorize the turns of the car.

Left at the bridge. Right along the quay. Left again, uphill, the engine straining—Fourvière, or the streets below it. He counted them the way he had once counted shells: not to prevent anything, only to keep for afterward, whatever afterward turned out to be.

* * *

The room they put him in was on the top floor of a requisitioned schoolhouse, and it contained a table, two chairs, a window with the paint fogged over, and a radiator that knocked at intervals like a patient asking for water. They left him alone for two hours, which he recognized as a technique and respected as craftsmanship. Hunger was applied next, also professionally. By evening a man came in whom Luc had never seen, older, unhurried, with the eyes of a bookkeeper, and set a single sheet of paper on the table and turned it around so Luc could read it.

It was a draft. Charges against one Henri Delacroix, listed dead 1916, resurfaced in the occupied zone, suspected of organizing illicit correspondence between civilians in the north and families in the free zone, suspected of collaboration with German administrative authorities, and—one item added in the small fastidious hand Luc now knew better than his own—

*and of circulating false and defamatory statements concerning the conduct of officials of the Department of the Rhône, in violation of the decrees on press and public order.*

"There," the bookkeeper-eyed man said, tapping that last line with one finger, gently, almost fondly, the way one taps a key point in a lesson. "That's the interesting part. You've seen this letter. Everyone says you've seen this letter."

"Everyone says," Luc agreed. "Everyone says many things. In my experience everyone is not a reliable witness."

"Where is it?"

"With its author, I assume. He wrote it."

The man smiled without warmth and sat down opposite, laying his hands flat on the table like a man settling in for accounts. "Sergeant Lucien Nystrom, second class, discharged June 1917, section seven, postal control. Except section seven doesn't remember you, Nystrom. We've asked. Your file ends in March 1915. After that—" he spread his hands, showing empty palms, a conjurer revealing nothing—"nothing. A man with no record, no unit, no visible employment for three years, found carrying military correspondence and consorting with known persons." He leaned forward an inch. "In wartime that makes you a spy. In peacetime it will make you worse. Do you see the position you're in? Do you see how easy it would be for us to simply decide what you are?"

Luc looked at the man for a long moment. Outside, the radiator knocked. Somewhere below, a door slammed, and a voice called something about papers, and the ordinary city went on with its ordinary evening while he sat in a warm room being offered, politely, the chance to disappear.

He thought of Henri, twenty-two years old in a lodging house on the Croix-Rousse in 1913, laughing at his own solemnity as he pressed a sealed envelope into the hands of a boy of nineteen and said, *if anything happens, take it to Camille, promise me, she'll want to hate me less if she knows I thought of her at the end*—no, that was Odile's memory, not his. Luc's memory was different and colder: Henri in a cellar in Lille in 1917, thinner than hunger, copying out by candlelight the numbers of lorries and the dates and the names, saying, *someone has to write it down properly, Luc, or when the war ends they'll call us liars, they'll call everything a lie, and Bresson will get a decoration instead of a trial.* Henri had been right, which was the cruelest thing about it. He had been right and it had cost him everything and now the draft on the table proved it: the lie was arriving first, as lies always did, wearing uniforms and carrying proper papers.

"I see the position I'm in," Luc said. "Do you see yours? When the armistice is signed—and it will be signed within days, your own memorandum says so—there will be an accounting. There always is. Men like Bresson survive accountings only when no one has written anything down." He tapped the draft of the charges, mirroring the gesture, finger to finger. "But he wrote it down. Dates. Lorries. Names. It exists. You can arrest me, and it will exist. You can arrest the boy who carries it, and it will exist. You can burn one copy, and it will exist. That is what writing is for. I'm surprised a man of your responsibilities needs telling."

Something moved behind the bookkeeper's eyes—not anger. Calculation. The calculation of a man discovering that the arithmetic on his page has stopped coming out even.

"We'll start," the man said quietly, "with the boy, then. The driver. The one who reads other people's letters."

And there it was. Luc kept his face still, kept his hands loose on the table, and felt the wire pull tight through his chest. They knew about Odile—or guessed, or had extracted the guess from some informer's haze—but knowing and proving were different countries, and he would not build the road between them for these men, not tonight, not ever.

"I don't know any drivers," he said. "I know stamps. Ask anyone."

The man rose, gathered his single sheet of paper, and paused at the door with his hand on the frame—an actor who knew the value of an exit.

"They'll move you tomorrow," he said. "Somewhere with fewer windows. Think about cooperation. Think about how much easier everyone's winter becomes." Then, almost kindly, at the threshold: "The war is ending, Nystrom. Men like you always think that means something. It means the paperwork changes. Nothing else."

The lock turned. The radiator knocked. Luc sat alone in the lamplight of the requisitioned schoolroom and let himself, at last, be afraid—thoroughly, coldly, professionally afraid—because fear was information too, and the information was this: they had nothing yet. If they had the letter they would not need him. If they had Odile they would not be asking. The machinery was turning, yes, greased and oiled by Bresson's small precise hand, but it was grinding on rumor, and rumor could be outrun by ink.

Which meant the letter was still moving. Which meant the boy was still carrying it, or preparing to, out along the roads toward Bresse with a bundle of pamphlets and a dead man's testimony hidden among the votes for women.

Luc laughed once, softly, alone in the room, and it hurt his ribs like real laughter, because it was.

Then he got up and examined the window—painted glass, third floor, a drainpipe furred with ivy—and began, with the patience of a man who had survived Verdun and Lille and four years of invented identities, to scrape the paint away with the edge of a chair rung, one small square at a time, keeping his eyes on the lights of the city below him: somewhere down there Marguerite Vasseur's house, somewhere down there a car that might already be gone, and somewhere beyond the eastern hills a clinic in Bresse where a nurse named Camille was about to learn that her brother's handwriting was not the only thing that had come back from the dead.

Tomorrow they would move him. So tonight, then. Everything, tonight.
