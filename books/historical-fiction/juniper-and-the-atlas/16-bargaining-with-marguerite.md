## 16. Bargaining with Marguerite

The rain had been falling since dawn, a thin, insistent rain that turned the courtyard stones the color of old pewter and made the plane trees drip in long, irregular counts, like a clock that had forgotten how to keep time. Odile stood at the kitchen window with his hands around a cup of coffee he had not drunk, watching the water gather in the seams between the flagstones, and told himself—for perhaps the fortieth time since Luc's arrest three days ago—that there was still an order to things. There was still a sequence. He would drive Marguerite to her meeting; he would stand beside the car with his cap pulled low; he would be furniture, and furniture was never interrogated.

Then Marguerite Vasseur came down before her breakfast, dressed for no meeting at all, in the grey wool she wore when she intended to stay somewhere until she had won, and she set a folded newspaper on the table between them—the *Progrès de Lyon*, third page—and said, without sitting down:

"The prefecture has drafted charges against Henri Delacroix."

Odile did not move. He had spent five years learning how not to move: not when he read a widow's letter to her dead son, not when he opened an envelope sealed with wax and found love inside it addressed to someone other than the addressee, not now. But Marguerite watched him the way she watched deputies in the Chamber gallery—patiently, with the particular attention of a woman who had learned that what people did not say was usually the whole of what they meant.

"You knew," she said.

"Madame—"

"Don't." She pulled out a chair and sat, folding her hands on the newspaper, and Odile understood with a cold settling in his chest that this was not a conversation he had been summoned to but a hearing. "Don't give me the face you give gendarmes. Sit down, Odile."

He sat. The rain counted against the glass. Somewhere above them a shutter banged once and settled.

"I have known for some time that you read my correspondence," Marguerite said. "I want you to understand that I am not accusing you of it now. I am establishing facts, the way one does before signing anything. You read my letters. You have read them for years. You seal them again so carefully that I only discovered it because Madame Perrin's hand slants differently after she's been drinking, and yours doesn't, and one morning her invitation arrived re-glued with the flap creased from the wrong side." She let that sit. "I did not dismiss you. I should like you to consider why."

Odile's throat was dry. The coffee smell had gone sour in the cup. "Why," he said, because she seemed to require it.

"Because whatever you were looking for, you never used. Not once. Not against me, not against the girls who board here, not against anyone. And because a man who reads everything and repeats nothing is either a saint or a coward, and I've watched you long enough to know you are not the first." Her eyes were very level, brown shot through with grey, eyes that had stared down prefects and editors and her own mother. "So. Now tell me about Henri Delacroix."

* * *

He told her.

Not well, at first—he began with Luc, with the fevered man at the service entrance, because that was the nearest edge of it—but she stopped him twice, made him go back, made him start where things actually started, and so he found himself in 1913 again, in the small rented room on the rue Sainte-Hélène, with the young lodger who worked at the customs house and read Verhaeren aloud badly and laughed too easily, the lodger who was leaving for his military reserve exercises and had, the night before he went, sat on the end of his bed with a sealed envelope in his hands and said, in a voice that was trying to be light and failing:

"If anything happens to me—anything, Odile, even something stupid, a horse, a train—you'll take this to Camille. My sister. In Bourg-en-Bresse. You won't read it. You'll put it in her hand."

And Odile, twenty-three years old and flattered to be trusted with anything at all by anyone at all, had said: *I swear it.*

"That was the first letter," Odile said now, and heard his own voice come out flat and strange, like a confession read by someone else. "Except it wasn't, madame. Because after Verdun, when they printed his name among the dead, I told myself the promise had died with him. It was easier than thinking about it. And then—" He stopped. The rain kept its broken count.

"And then," Marguerite prompted, and there was nothing soft in it, but nothing cruel either. Only the exactness.

"And then two letters came for him. Before the official notice, or just after—I've never been able to fix the date. One from a woman in Nîmes. One from his sister." Odile looked at his hands. They were steady; he had trained even that out of himself. "They were forwarded to the house because he'd listed this address once, for a week, when he was between lodgings. I took them in. And I read them. And then I burned them."

"Why?"

"Because the one from Camille was angry, and I thought—if he's dead, let him be dead cleanly. Let her grief be simple. And because reading them was already a theft, and burning them was only the second theft, and I told myself the second didn't count because the first had already happened." He heard it as he said it, the shape of the lie, worn smooth by five years of handling. "That's how it works, madame. That's how it has always worked. You take one small thing and you tell yourself the harm is already done, and then you are a man who takes small things, and taking becomes what your hands are for."

Marguerite said nothing. Outside, a cart went past through the wet street, the iron rim of its wheels crying against the stone.

"So when Luc came to the door," Odile went on, "with letters in Henri's hand—I knew the handwriting before I saw the name. I'd read enough of it by then. Notes about the gas meter. A page of Verhaeren copied out because he thought it was funny. And the last thing he ever gave me, which I kept and did not deliver, because delivering it meant admitting I existed, that I had a name and an address and obligations, that anyone in the world could look for me and find me." He laughed, briefly, and it was an ugly sound. "You asked once why I drive the way I do. Why I never take the direct road if there's an angle available. This is why, madame. I have spent five years trying to be a man no one could ever need to look for. And a dead man found me anyway."

"He isn't dead," Marguerite said. "That is apparently the entire problem."

"No. He's alive, and he's across the lines in occupied territory, and he sent testimony through the smugglers' mail—testimony about Prefect Barthe and the requisition contracts, names and dates, the kind of paper that hangs men. And the charges being drafted against him call it collaboration, which means every letter he sent is evidence, which means everyone who touched those letters—" He stopped himself. He had been circling it for three days, the arithmetic of it, and now he laid it down plainly, because he owed her plainness if he owed her anything. "Everyone who touched those letters is exposed. Including me. Including, if anyone traces them to this house, you."

* * *

For a long moment Marguerite did not speak. She rose, walked to the window, and stood with her back to him, and Odile watched her the way he had watched her for four years—with the particular knowledge of a man who has observed another person's life entirely through its paperwork. He knew her by her correspondence: the fierce looping hand she used for allies, the deliberate copperplate she used for enemies, the notes she wrote and rewrote when her father's death anniversary came around. He knew she underlined words when she was afraid. He had seen, over four years, exactly how much fear a fearless woman could produce and conceal in a single afternoon.

Now he watched her hands, and they were still, and that frightened him more than shouting would have.

"Do you know what I have built, Odile?" she said at last, to the window. "Do you know what it costs? When I began, the newspapers called me a hysterical widow. Then a foreign agent. Then worse. I have been spat on outside the Bourse. I have been told by men whose salaries I pay that my movement is a fashion that will pass. Every single thing I have—a roof, a press, the loyalty of forty women who could each be ruined by association with me—that has been purchased by being careful. By being *impeccable*. One scandal and it all comes down, and not onto me alone. Onto Élise, who has a child. Onto Madame Perrin, who has nothing else." She turned. "Do you understand that I am not weighing my reputation? Reputations mend. I am weighing theirs."

"Yes, madame."

"And yet." She came back to the table and sat, and folded the newspaper in half, and then in half again, smaller and smaller, a gesture he had seen her make before verdicts and votes, the folding of a thing into a thing she could hold. "Yet here is what troubles me. If I turn you out today—if I hand the prefecture a driver and wash my hands—what have I defended? A clean reputation, and nothing underneath it. I would have proven that my care was only armor, that there was nothing beneath the armor willing to bleed." She looked up at him, and for the first time in four years Odile saw her look tired—not the performance of weariness she deployed in negotiations, but the true kind, the kind she hid even from herself. "I have spent ten years arguing that silence is complicity. Political silence. Public silence. And now a man stands in my kitchen telling me he was silent, personally, privately, for five years—and he is right that it was cowardice, and I am right that silence is complicity, and if I do the safe thing this morning then every speech I have ever given was a fraud I performed in public while practicing the opposite at home."

"Madame, no one would—"

"I would know." It came out quiet and absolute. "That's rather the point of a conscience, Odile. It doesn't require witnesses."

She was silent a moment. The rain thinned, hesitated, thickened again.

"Here are my conditions," she said then, and her voice changed—became the voice she used for terms, for clauses, the voice of a woman who had negotiated with ministers and knew that mercy given without structure was merely sentiment. "First: you will tell me everything. Not the shape of it—the whole of it. Every letter you intercepted, every name, every envelope you burned, starting tonight, in writing, in your own hand. If this comes apart, I will not be surprised by it. I will not learn about my own household from a prosecutor."

"Yes."

"Second: Luc. Where is he being held?"

"The prefecture. Since Tuesday. No charge posted."

"Third." She pushed the folded newspaper square across the table toward him, and he saw now that she had folded it deliberately to the article, the small notice of the pending charges, the words *collaboration with the enemy* sitting in their column of type like a nail waiting to be driven. "We do not hide the letter. Do you understand me? Your instinct—and I watched you wrestling with it all through breakfast—your instinct will be to bury it deeper, move it further, find some darker corner. We are done with dark corners in this house. If Henri Delacroix carried testimony out of occupied France at the risk of his life, then that testimony is going to serve the living. The question is not whether it becomes public. The question is whether it becomes public while we still control the telling—or after Barthe controls it."

Odile stared at her. "You're saying we publish."

"I'm saying I'm considering it, which should terrify you more." Almost, almost, the old sharpness came back into her mouth. "Barthe wants this quiet—quiet charges, quiet conviction, a dead man officially dead again and conveniently unable to answer. Quiet is his weapon. So quiet is what we refuse to give him. But I will not gamble forty women's futures on your word alone, Odile. I will gamble them on the letter itself. I need to see it. All of it. What Henri witnessed, whom he named, whether it holds. Before I decide anything, I read it myself."

"It's not here."

"Where."

"The chapel at Saint-Irénée. Loose stone behind the saint's feet. Luc chose it; I moved it there the night before the police took him." He hesitated. "It's wrapped in oilcloth. There are four letters. Three are copies of the testimony. The fourth—" His voice caught on it, the small human thing amid all the dangerous machinery. "The fourth is the one he gave me in 1913. He wrote a new one, through the smugglers, to replace it. Same sister. Same instruction. *Whatever the cost.*"

Marguerite regarded him for a long moment, and Odile felt the full weight of what he had just handed her—not the letter, but himself: the inventory of his failures, signed in his own hand, to be delivered tonight.

"You understand," she said quietly, "that I cannot forgive you this morning. Perhaps not for a long while. Two of those women upstairs trust you with their post—their mothers' letters, their lovers' letters. What you did to me, you did to them. Trust isn't a thing I can extend on their behalf." She rose, smoothing her skirt with two brisk passes of her hands, and the tiredness went back into its drawer wherever she kept it. "What I can extend is this: work. Real work, done in the open, under my eye, until you have earned back what you spent. Starting with the letter. We go to Saint-Irénée this afternoon, together, in the car, in broad daylight—because Barthe expects hiding, and I have survived ten years by doing the opposite of what he expects."

She gathered her coat from the chair. At the door she paused, without turning, and said:

"One more condition, Odile. The fourth letter—Camille's letter. When we go to Bresse—and we will go—it goes into her hand by your fingers, not mine, not anyone's. You made a promise in peacetime. You will keep it in wartime, with witnesses if necessary. That is not mercy. That is the price."

Then she was gone up the stairs, her step firm and unhurried, and Odile sat alone in the kitchen with the cold cup of coffee and the sound of the rain, which had begun, at last, to ease.

He should have felt lighter. Confession was supposed to be a lifting. Instead he felt flayed open, emptied, a house with all its shutters torn away—and beneath the rawness of it, faint but unmistakable, something he had not felt in five years: the terrifying lightness of being a man who could be *found*. Who had an address in the world.

He was washing the cup when Élise came down for the morning post and handed him a small envelope, damp at one corner, with no return address.

The hand was unfamiliar. But the postmark, smudged though it was, read *Bourg-en-Bresse*—and inside, when he cut it open with shaking fingers, was a single line in a woman's angular script:

*I know my brother is alive. I know someone in this city knows more than they are saying. Come to me before the police do.*

*—C. Delacroix*
