## 2. Ledger of Small Lives

The morning of the delivery began, as all good mornings did, with ink on his fingers before breakfast.

Rosalind had risen before the gulls to finish the last of the survey maps—six plates of the eastern approaches, showing the shoals beyond the harbour bar in careful gradations of blue-grey wash. Master Hollis had taught him that a map should be honest even when it was unwelcome: *the sea does not flatter, Rosalind, and neither should we.* So he had drawn the sandbar where it actually lay, not where the harbourmaster wished it lay, and he had marked the old wreck of the *Perseverance* with its true depth of water over her ribs, because some fisherman might one day owe his life to knowing.

He wrapped the plates in oilcloth, tied them with twine, and set them in the leather satchel that had belonged to Hollis for thirty years. The satchel still smelled faintly of him—tobacco and linseed oil and something like old paper, which was to say something like time itself. Three months since the funeral. The smell was fading. Rosalind did not let himself think about that.

Vellamouth woke around him as he walked down from the widow's house on Copper Lane. Smoke stood up straight from a hundred chimneys in the windless grey. Mrs. Petchey was beating a rug across her balcony rail with the violence of a woman at war with dust. Two boys raced hoops down Fishtail Alley, and old Tam Weller sat outside the Anchor as he always sat, as though the town had been built around him and would one day be dismantled around him too. Rosalind knew every face, every door, every crooked slate roof—the mapmaker's curse and blessing, that he could never again see a place as anything but its parts.

He turned onto Clerk's Walk, and the town clerk's office rose before him: a squat stone building squeezed between the wool factor's warehouse and the excise house, its windows so small it seemed embarrassed to look out at the world. A brass plate read **TOWN CLERK — RECORDS, TITHES, REGISTRATION**, polished by weather until only half the letters survived, which Rosalind had always thought rather fitting for a records office.

Inside smelled of dust and sealing wax and the particular staleness of paper that had been important once and was now merely stored. Mr. Ambrose Quill, town clerk of Vellamouth for nineteen years, sat behind his counter on a stool so high his feet barely reached its rungs—a small, neat man made smaller by the great ledgers stacked around him like fortifications. His spectacles sat low on his nose. He looked up at the bell over the door with the expression of a man interrupted at prayer.

"Apprentice Okonkwo." He said the name carefully, both syllables given their due, as Hollis had taught him to. "Six plates, is it?"

"Six, sir. Eastern approaches, verified against the spring soundings."

Quill received them the way a priest receives offerings. He unwrapped the first plate, held it to the weak light from the window, examined the coastline inch by inch with a fingernail tracing the shore. Rosalind waited. He knew better than to speak while Quill worked; criticism arrived either as silence or as a single dry word—"adequate"—and both meant approval.

"Adequate," said Quill at last, wrapping them again. "Master Hollis trained you properly, I'll say that for him." He paused, quill hovering over his receipt book. "You'll be continuing his commissions, then."

"I hope to, sir."

"Hm." Quill wrote the date. He wrote *six plates*. He wrote *received R. Okonkwo, apprentice*. Then he looked up, and something in his face had shifted—not warmer, but more careful, the way a man's face shifts when he remembers there are things in the room he'd rather not discuss. "You'll have heard, I don't doubt, that there's been correspondence from the Crown office. Surveys wanted. More than surveys."

"Surveys of what?"

"Of you, mostly," Quill said, and almost smiled. "Who lives where. Who works what trade. How many souls under each roof." He tapped the counter with one blunt finger, and beneath his knuckle Rosalind saw, for the first time, the great ledger lying open.

He had seen the census book before, of course—in the abstract way one sees furniture. It was simply part of the office, dark green boards, brass corners gone brown, a spine worn pale where hands had dragged it from shelf to desk for years. But today it lay open under the window's light, two vast pages of columns ruled in red, and the columns were full of names.

"There's nothing new in counting people," Quill went on, watching him. "Governments have always counted. Sheep, tons, souls—it's how they keep the arithmetic of the realm straight." He adjusted his spectacles. "Though this one wants more than numbers. It wants particulars. Age. Trade. Years of residence. Whether the dwelling is owned or rented, and to whom." He closed the ledger gently, as if shutting a lid on something alive. "Not my asking. Theirs."

Rosalind found he had leaned forward without meaning to. "May I see it? The entries—I've never—"

"You may not," Quill said, without heat. "It's not for public handling. Names go in, names stay in." And then, because he was perhaps a kinder man than his stool suggested, he added: "Your sister's in there, if it's her you're wondering. Fisherman's Walk, dock labourer, aged twenty-two. Neat hand wrote it, mine own. Why do you look like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like someone handed you your own obituary."

Rosalind straightened. "I didn't know we were *in* it. Both of us. Written down somewhere I've never seen."

"We are all in it," Quill said. "Every soul from the bar to the millrace. That's what a census *is*, boy. The town, but flat." He slid the ledger off the counter and into a drawer, and locked the drawer, and put the key in his waistcoat pocket, all with the economy of long habit. "You'd best get used to the feeling. There's more coming, if the correspondence means what I think it means."

"What do you think it means?"

Quill looked at him for a moment over the spectacles. Nineteen years behind a counter will teach a man to weigh words before spending them, and Rosalind watched him spend this one deliberately.

"It means," he said, "that somebody in London has developed an interest in Vellamouth, and nobody develops an interest in Vellamouth out of affection. Take your receipt. Mind the wet paint on the excise house door. Good morning."

* * *

The receipt went into the satchel beside Hollis's maps, and Rosalind walked back out into the street carrying, along with the oilcloth and twine, a new and uncomfortable awareness: that somewhere behind that locked drawer, in ruled columns and a clerk's neat hand, he existed as a line. *Okonkwo, R., apprentice mapmaker, aged nineteen, Copper Lane, lodging.* Adaeze existed as another line. Margaret Hollis existed as another. The whole living town—Mrs. Petchey and her rug-beating fury, Tam Weller and his permanent occupation of the Anchor's bench, the hoop-racing boys who would one day be fishermen or drowned men—all of it flattened into columns, rolled up, and locked away.

He tried to shake the feeling walking along the quay. It was absurd, he told himself, to feel watched by a book. A ledger couldn't watch anyone. It just sat there, being written in, the way a mirror just hung there being looked into—and no, that was precisely the wrong thought, because a mirror did nothing until someone stood before it, and then it had you entirely, every angle, whether you combed your hair or not.

The harbour was busy with the ordinary business of the morning: herring boats coming in with the grey tide, gulls screaming their ancient grievances, barrels rolling, men shouting measurements at each other across the water. Adaeze was somewhere among the dock gangs—he could pick out her laugh even at distance, bright and cutting above the general roar—and Rosalind felt the small steadying comfort of knowing exactly where everyone in his world was standing.

Then he noticed the soldiers.

There were six of them, in undress reds without crossbelts, and they were not doing anything soldiers usually did. They were not drilling. They were not loitering the way bored garrison men loitered, shoulders against walls, eyes following the girls. They were spread along the quay at intervals, and each of them held a long pole with graduated markings painted up its length, and they were measuring.

Rosalind stopped walking.

One of them stood at the harbour steps with a notebook, calling out figures. Another had waded—boots and all, to the evident delight of the herring boys—to the mooring chains and was holding his pole against the links, reading marks. Two more paced the length of the main quay, counting paces, one dropping pegs at intervals while the other wrote. At the far end, near the old crane, a sixth man who was not a soldier at all but wore civilian black with a military bearing stood with a small brass instrument on a tripod, sweeping the horizon slowly, the way a man sweeps a room he intends to rearrange completely.

They were measuring the harbour. Depth of water at the steps. Height of the quay walls. Spacing of the moorings. The bearing of the channel. All the questions, Rosalind realised, that a mapmaker asks before drawing a map—or that an engineer asks before building one.

He had grown up on these stones. He knew the harbour the way he knew his own hands: the way the tide ran lazy past the breakwater, the sandbar's slow migration eastward year by year, the exact height of spring tides against the second step of the harbour stairs. Nobody measured a harbour that had fished peacefully for three hundred years. Harbours got measured when somebody intended to change them.

The soldier with the notebook caught him staring. A young fellow, freckled, bored-looking—but his eyes when they met Rosalind's were not bored at all. He looked at Rosalind the way Quill had looked at the ledger: recording. Taking him in. Adding him to some column.

Rosalind nodded, the neutral nod one gives a stranger. The soldier did not nod back. He wrote something.

Rosalind walked on, unhurried, though his pulse had picked up in a way he couldn't account for. Past the fish market with its reek and racket. Past the chandler's, where Mr. Cade was hauling rope and stopped to call after him—

"Young Okonkwo! Those army louts been at my moorings since seven. You know what they're about?"

"No, Mr. Cade."

"Nobody knows what they're about. That's what I don't like." Cade heaved the coil of rope onto his shoulder. "Soldiers don't measure things, lad. Soldiers tear things down and build them different. Measuring's just them deciding where to start."

Rosalind made some noncommittal answer and kept walking, but Cade's words followed him up Fisherman's Walk like a dog that wouldn't be shooed. *Measuring's just them deciding where to start.*

* * *

By late afternoon the unease had settled into him like damp into stone—not dramatic, not alarming, merely present, a weight he couldn't locate. He spent three hours at the drawing board correcting the northern parish boundary for Farmer Dill's tithe dispute, and twice caught himself having drawn nothing for whole minutes together, pen poised, thinking about columns in a locked drawer.

It was the particular quality of it that bothered him, he decided. Not menace—there had been no menace. A young soldier writing in a notebook. A clerk locking a drawer. A tripod instrument sweeping the horizon. None of it, taken alone, amounted to anything you could name as a threat. Taken together, it amounted to attention. Vellamouth, which had passed its entire existence unregarded by the great world—too small for the mail coaches to bother with, too poor for the newspapers, too far from anywhere for history to trouble itself—was suddenly being *looked at*. Weighed. Entered into columns. And Rosalind, who had always believed that history happened elsewhere, to other people, in capitals and on battlefields he had only ever drawn as small decorative marks on the margins of larger maps—that Rosalind had the growing, sickening sense of standing in the path of something enormous that had begun, quietly and without malice, to take his town's measurements.

Hollis would have known what to make of it. Hollis, who had read the papers obsessively and muttered about enclosures and clearances over supper, who had once told him, *a map is never innocent, boy—ask always who paid for it and what they wanted shown.* Hollis, who had died—no one could quite say how—in a fall on the marsh road at night with his satchel missing and his skull broken on the stones, and whose death the coroner had called misadventure in a hearing lasting less than an hour.

Rosalind pushed the thought away, as he had learned to push it away, because it led nowhere except to grief, and grief did not help draw parish boundaries.

Margaret found him at dusk, as she often did, appearing in the doorway of the workroom with tea he hadn't asked for. She studied his face the way she studied everything, directly and without apology.

"You've been to Quill's," she said.

"This morning. Why?"

"Because you wear a certain expression when you've been to Quill's. Disappointed in the world." She set the tea beside his elbow. "And because he sent a note round at four o'clock asking whether you'd completed an inventory of Master Hollis's effects. Which is an odd question to ask a widow, unless somebody has asked *him* the question first."

Rosalind turned in his chair. "An inventory? Of what effects?"

"Of everything. Papers especially, the note said." Margaret's hands folded themselves together, and he saw that they were not entirely steady. "Somebody in the government, Rosalind, wants to know what my husband left behind when he died. And I have been sitting here all evening trying to decide what to tell them. Because most of what he left behind isn't in the house at all. It's in that room upstairs, in the atlas, and I am beginning to fear—" she stopped, and started again, more quietly, "I am beginning to fear that whatever killed Elias is connected to whoever is measuring our harbour."

The candle guttered. Outside, far off toward the water, came the sound of boots in formation—many boots, more than six—and Rosalind sat very still, listening, his tea going cold beside him, while somewhere below the town the tide came in over measurements that had been taken that morning, and a ledger in a locked drawer held every name he loved.
