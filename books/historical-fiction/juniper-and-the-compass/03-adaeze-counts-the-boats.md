## 3. Adaeze Counts the Boats

The night shift at Calloway's Wharf began when the lamps along the quay were lit and ended when they guttered out, and in between there was rope to coil, crates to shift, and the long gray patience of the tide. Adaeze Okonkwo had worked the wharf for six years—since she was fourteen and strong enough to carry a barrel of salt herring on each shoulder—and in that time she had learned to read the harbor the way her brother read his maps: by habit, by pattern, by the small betrayals of things that did not fit.

Tonight, the harbor did not fit.

She stood at the end of the pier with a coil of hemp on her shoulder and counted them again. Nine. Nine ships riding at anchor in the outer roads, where ordinarily at this hour there would be three—a collier from Dunmark, perhaps, and a coastal trader or two, their riding lights swinging lazy as lanterns on a porch. These were not colliers. They sat low and heavy in the water, black-hulled, their gunports shuttered but unmistakable, and around them the smaller transports clustered like goslings. Troop ships. She had seen troop ships twice before in her life: once when she was nine, during the French scare, when the whole town had turned out on the seawall to watch four frigates pass eastward with all sails set, cheering as though the war were a parade; and once last spring, a single transport that had landed marines to put down the mill trouble upriver.

Nine was not a scare. Nine was not a parade.

"Adaeze! The tide won't coil itself." Tam Broadbent, the foreman, waving from the warehouse door, his voice carrying the particular irritation of a man who has decided not to notice something because noticing would require him to act.

She swung the rope down onto the deck of the lighter they were loading and went back to work, but her eyes kept returning to the roads. Between loads she counted again. Ten now. A sloop had come in with the flood tide, running dark, no lights at all until the moment its anchor splashed. Warships did not run dark into friendly harbors for their health.

By midnight the fog had come off the water in slow rags, and through it the anchors' lights smeared into orange ghosts. Adaeze paused in the lee of the crane, wiped her palms on her apron, and made herself do the arithmetic properly. Each transport of that class carried two hundred men, if packed as soldiers liked to pack them. Ten transports. Two thousand men. Vellamouth's whole garrison numbered fewer than eighty, half of whom mostly guarded the customs house and drank at the Anchor's Rest. You did not bring two thousand men to reinforce eighty.

You brought two thousand men for the town.

The thought sat in her stomach like swallowed seawater.

* * *

Rosalind did not hear her knock at first. She could tell, through the door of the old map room on Chandler's Lane, that he was deep inside the work—the particular silence he fell into, the one Margaret called his burial, though she only said it when Rosalind was out of earshot and sometimes not even then. Adaeze knocked harder.

"It's open," came his voice, distant, distracted.

He was bent over the great table under a caged lamp, sleeves rolled, the fingers of his right hand blue-black to the second knuckle. The room smelled of linseed oil and candle wax and that dry mineral smell of powdered pigments that Adaeze had known all her life and could never separate from her brother. Every surface was paper. Coastlines hung from the rafters like flayed skins; drawers stood half-open, spilling rolled charts; and in the middle of it all, under his hands, lay the atlas—Master Hollis's atlas, the one that had got him killed, or so everyone said quietly and Rosalind never said at all. Three months since the funeral. Three months of Rosalind coming here every evening after his survey work and every night until the candles died, finishing a dead man's book.

"You've ink on your face," Adaeze said. "Under your eye. You look like you've been punched."

"I feel like I've been punched. This stretch of coast—" He tapped the page without looking up. "Hollis drew it from memory after the fire at the survey office took the originals, and his memory is good but his bearings drift by half a degree every mile north. I keep having to correct him. It feels like arguing with him."

"He'd have liked that."

"He'd have hated it. He'd have told me my penmanship was an insult to the profession and then shown me how it was done." But he smiled saying it, the small private smile he kept for the dead master, and set down his pen, and finally looked at her properly. His face fell. "It's late. Why are you standing there looking like the weather?"

"Because the weather is standing in the harbor," Adaeze said. "Rosy. Come and look."

"Don't call me Rosy."

"Come and look anyway."

He protested—there was ink drying, there was a coastline that would not wait—but he came, because he always came when she asked in that voice, and together they walked the sleeping lanes down toward the seawall, the cobbles slick with fog-damp, the town so quiet they could hear the anchor chains of the fleet clinking far out in the dark like enormous patient cattle.

At the seawall Adaeze pointed. "Count them."

Rosalind squinted into the murk. "Count what? I can see lights. Fog on the water."

"Ten. Eleven if the sloop is what I think it is. Transports, Rosy—troop transports, black hulls, sitting low. They started coming in at dusk and they're still anchoring. That's two thousand men minimum, out there in the fog, tonight, three miles off our quay. Now tell me what that is."

"A maneuver?" He said it without conviction, his cartographer's instinct already measuring the spacing of those ghost-lights and finding it too regular, too deliberate. "Exercises. There's been talk of naval exercises on this coast all spring."

"Talk in whose papers? Have you ever seen an exercise that anchored in the dark and ran dark and didn't signal the harbor master? I asked Mr. Calloway. He sent a boy to the fort with a polite inquiry and the boy came back with a soldier who told him to mind his cargo and leave the roads to the Crown." She heard her own voice climbing and forced it level. "Something is happening, Rosalind. Something big enough that they don't want us to see it until it's done."

Rosalind was quiet a while. Out in the roads the fog shifted, and for a moment the nearest transport showed itself entire: black flank, shuttered ports, the great bulk of her riding the swell with the stillness of a thing waiting for orders.

"They're just ships," he said at last, but softly, the way a man says *it's just a cough*.

"Ships are never just ships to you people. You draw coastlines all day—you know what comes out of nowhere along a coastline. It comes out of somewhere. Somebody planned it. Somebody measured it." She turned to face him fully. "I'm not asking you to panic. I'm asking you to prepare. Quietly. Margaret has savings; get them out of the bank this week, in coin, not paper. Get your important papers together—your indentures, Hollis's letters, whatever matters. Keep a bag packed. If the troops land, they'll requisition first and explain later, that's how it goes, ask anyone from Kesselton—"

"Kesselton was seven years ago and half of it was rumor—"

"—and the other half happened, and nobody there had a bag packed either." She grabbed his sleeve. He was taller than her by a head and she had been hauling cargo since before his hands were steady enough to hold a pen, and he knew better than to pull away. "Listen to me. You live inside your maps, Rosalind. In your maps history happens somewhere else, to other people, in places with Latin names. But I work the docks. History arrives by ship. And it's arriving."

He covered her hand with his own. His fingers were cold, ink-stained, gentle. "Adaeze. We were born here. Papa moored his boat where you load herring now. Nobody is going to requisition Vellamouth. What would they want with a fishing town and a salt marsh?"

"I don't know. But I know what two thousand men look like, and I know what a harbor full of warships looks like, and I know that tomorrow morning, when the fog lifts, the whole town is going to see what I saw tonight—and either we'll laugh about it over breakfast or we won't." She released his sleeve, slowly. "Promise me the bag, at least. The papers. The coin. Humor me. You humor Margaret about eating vegetables."

"I humor Margaret because she feeds me."

"Humor me because I love you, you impossible boy."

That earned her the crooked grin, the one that meant he had already gone back to his coastlines behind his eyes. "All right. The bag. The coin. Tomorrow." He kissed the top of her head, fog-damp hair and all. "Go home and sleep, Adaeze. You've been counting boats all night."

"Not boats," she said. "Troopships. Learn the difference. It might matter."

* * *

She walked him as far as Chandler's Lane and then went on alone toward the dock cottages, past the Anchor's Rest where last-call light still burned yellow through the fog, past the dark hulk of the customs house. At the corner by the fish market she stopped and looked back once more at the water, because she was her father's daughter and her father had taught both his children the same rule: *trust the water to tell you the truth, because everything else will lie politely.*

The fog was thinning near dawn. Through the thinning she watched the transports settle on their anchors, patient as debts, and she counted them one more time—eleven, twelve—a thirteenth standing in from the south with the first gray light behind it, and on the seawall road below the fort, she now noticed, soldiers had begun to arrive in twos and threes, unspooling something long and pale along the harbor fence. Rope. Pickets. The bones of a barricade, laid out quiet as morning chores.

Adaeze stood very still in the empty street and felt the floor of ordinary life tilt beneath her feet, and thought, with a clarity that frightened her more than any number of ships: *They are sealing the harbor. By tonight there will be a ledger with every name in Vellamouth written in it, and some clerk will decide which names are worth saving—and Rosalind will still be arguing with a dead man's bearings.*

She turned and ran for Chandler's Lane.
