## 4. The Widow's Warning

Margaret Hollis had learned to measure grief in small domestic units: the second teacup she still set out each morning out of habit, then put away; the coat on its hook by the door that she could not bring herself to move to the back of the wardrobe; the half-finished letter in her husband's hand that sat in the writing desk like a patient waiting for a physician who would never come. Forty-one days since they had pulled Edmund from the canal below the mill weir. Forty-one days, and the house had not yet agreed that he was gone. It kept his shape in every room—the dent in his chair, the inkstone with its crusted rim, the smell of linseed oil and wet paper that clung to the stairs no matter how hard she scrubbed.

She was polishing the good candlesticks when Rosalind Okonkwo knocked.

She knew his knock before she reached the door—three soft raps, a pause, as though he were apologizing in advance for disturbing her. The boy had been coming three evenings a week since the funeral, ostensibly to return borrowed surveying instruments, ostensibly to ask after small matters of the estate, but really because he and she had both understood without saying it that the map room upstairs was not finished, and that neither of them could bear to enter it alone.

"Mrs. Hollis." He stood on the step with rain beading on his shoulders, a canvas satchel slung across his chest, and behind him Vellamouth lay grey and dripping under a sky the color of dishwater. "I've brought back the parallel rule. And I—" He hesitated, and she saw the thing he was carrying underneath the thing he was carrying. "I've been working on the coastlines. The northern sheets. I thought you should know."

*The atlas.* Even now, forty-one days on, the word made something clench behind her ribs.

"You'll take tea," she said. It was not a question. She stepped aside to let him in, and as he passed she caught the scent of him—ink and river water and youth—and thought, not for the first time, *Edmund loved this boy like the son the Lord declined to give us,* and thought, immediately after, *and it may yet be the death of him.*

That thought had been visiting her more often lately. It had arrived first as a whisper, ten days after the funeral, when she had found the loose panel in the atlas's binding. Now it spoke at a volume she could barely live with.

* * *

They took tea in the front room, where the light came weakly through leaded glass and the fire did its modest best. Rosalind sat on the edge of his chair the way he always did, as if prepared to spring up and apologize for taking up space. He was twenty-three years old and had hands like a much older man—stained at the nails, nicked with blade-marks, the right forefinger calloused into a writer's bump that no glove would ever flatten. He talked about the northern coastline while she poured: the difficulty of the shoals off Gull Point, the way the soundings in Master Hollis's notes contradicted the admiralty charts, the hypothesis—which Edmund had held and Rosalind now defended—that the channel had shifted two furlongs eastward after the storm of '43.

He spoke beautifully. That was the ruin of him. Edmund used to say the boy didn't draw maps so much as listen to the land and write down what it said, and watching him now, his face lit with the small ecstasy of a man describing a beloved thing, Margaret felt the full weight of what she was about to do settle onto her like a wet cloak.

Because there were two kinds of knowledge in this world, she had come to understand. There was the kind you carried lightly, like a coin in a pocket—useful, spendable, harmless. And there was the kind that carried *you*, whether you willed it or not, down roads you would never have chosen. Edmund had died of the second kind. And Rosalind, sitting here with his tea going cold because he could not stop talking about shoal-lines, already held the same knowledge without knowing what it weighed.

"Mrs. Hollis?" He had stopped. He was looking at her with those dark, careful eyes. "Forgive me. Have I said something wrong?"

"No, child." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "No. You've said everything exactly right. That's rather the trouble."

She set down her cup. Her hands, she noticed, were not entirely steady, and she was sixty-one years old and had buried a husband and two infants and a father consumed piecemeal by the King's Evil, and she had thought herself long past being frightened of anything that lived inside a house. But her hands shook all the same.

"There is something you must see," she said. "And once you have seen it, you cannot unsee it. Do you understand me? I want you to choose it knowingly. Edmund never had the choice. They simply came and took it from him, and then took him." She rose. "You have the choice. So sit a moment longer, and think whether you want to keep being a boy who draws coastlines, or become something else. Because I cannot give it back to you afterward. Neither could he."

Rosalind looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened against the glass, and somewhere down the lane a cart groaned past through the puddles. Then he set his cup on its saucer with great deliberation, the way he did everything, and said, "Master Hollis told me once that a map is a moral document. I didn't understand him. I've been trying to, these six weeks." He stood. "Show me."

* * *

The map room had not changed since the night she had last climbed these stairs with a lamp, except that dust had begun its patient work on the flat files and the smell of Edmund was thinner now, as though even that were emigrating by degrees. The atlas lay where it had always lain, on the large table beneath the north window—a great folio bound in green morocco, its spine tooled in gilt, its brass clasps tarnished. *An Atlas of the Eastern Maritime Counties, Being a True and Particular Survey.* Eleven years of her husband's life between two covers. And other things besides, though she had not known that until ten days ago.

"Edmund fell ill in March," she began, and then stopped, because that was the story she told visitors, the story she had needed to believe herself, and this was no hour for stories. "No. Let me begin properly.

"In March, men came to this house. Two of them. Civil-looking, but not civil—one of them had a way of standing in a doorway that I have seen in bailiffs and press-gangs, filling the frame so nothing gets past him. They said they were from the Board of Ordnance. They said the Crown wished to purchase Master Hollis's survey entire—all his plates, all his notes—at a generous price. Edmund received them in this room and I heard most of it through the door. He refused them. Politely. He was always polite; it was one of his armorings. They came again in April. Again in May. Each time the price went up and each time Edmund said no, and each time I asked him why we were refusing money we badly needed, and each time he told me some portion of the truth and no more."

Margaret crossed to the table and laid her palms flat on the green morocco. Under her hands the atlas felt less like a book than like a chest, a coffin lid, a door.

"It was the margins," she said. "That's what he wouldn't sell, though I didn't know it then. You've worked on these sheets, Rosalind—you know Edmund's marginalia. Little annotations in his crabbed left-slanting hand. Soundings corrected. A church moved to its true position. Notes on who owned what field." She looked up at the boy. "And names. Families. Did you ever wonder why my husband, a mapmaker, kept writing down families?"

Rosalind had gone very still. "I thought it was habit. Antiquarianism. He liked knowing things."

"He did. God forgive him, he liked knowing things past all sense." Her voice cracked on the fondness and she let it. "In May, after the third visit, he grew secretive even with me. He slept in this room twice. He stopped letting me carry the atlas downstairs. And I was angry with him—I want you to know that, because anger at the dead is a shameful thing and I have carried enough of it. I was angry because he trusted paper over his own wife. And then on the ninth of June he went out to deliver proofs to the clerk at Ashcombe and never came home, and they found him at the mill weir two days later, and the coroner—a coroner I had never seen before, sent from outside the county, mind—wrote *misadventure* and was gone before the ink dried."

The rain ticked against the window. Rosalind's breath had gone shallow.

"And then, ten days ago," Margaret said, "I came up here at night, because I couldn't sleep for being angry, and I decided to look at what he'd died protecting, since no one else seemed interested in asking. And I found the binding was wrong."

She lifted the atlas. Beneath it the table showed a pale rectangle, ghost-shaped, where eleven years of daylight had darkened the wood around it. She turned the folio over, opened the back cover, and pressed the hidden catch Edmund had shown her exactly once, years ago, laughing, calling it *the widow's door*—she had thought then that he meant it as a joke about locks, and understood only now that he had been naming its heir.

The back board swung open on a hinge no binder had intended. Inside, wedged into a cavity cut between the boards and lined with oilcloth, lay a packet of papers.

"Take it out," Margaret said. "It should be your hands. Mine have held it long enough."

Rosalind drew the packet free as though it might wake. He unwrapped the oilcloth on the table with the delicacy of a man defusing something, and inside were letters—dozens of them, folded and sealed and slit open again, arranged by town in Edmund's meticulous hand—and beneath them a single sheet covered edge to edge in columns.

"What am I looking at?" he whispered.

"A ledger," Margaret said. "But not the Crown's kind. Read the heading."

He bent close. She watched his lips move.

"*A true register of persons displaced from the parishes of Ashcombe, Ferrisden, and Lower Marle, collected from their own testimony, in the hope that they may one day be restored to record.*" He looked up. "Displaced? Ashcombe isn't displaced. Ashcombe is—" He stopped. Something moved behind his eyes, some tectonic shift, the first crack running through the world he had trusted.

"Ashcombe," Margaret said quietly, "was cleared in the winter of '41. Fourteen households. The official record says the parish was depopulated by fever and the land returned to common. Ferrisden followed in '44—twenty-two households, removed within a fortnight of the survey that found iron in its hills. Lower Marle last year. Thirty-one households, and the story given out was coastal flooding." She laid her finger on the columns of names. "None of it is true, Rosalind. None of it happened as written. What happened is that certain men in certain offices discovered that a parish which ceases to exist ceases to have owners, and land without owners can be had for the price of a clerk's silence. Edmund spent the last four years of his life finding these people—refugees mostly, scattered to hiring fairs and workhouses—and writing their names down, because he understood before any of us that erasure begins on paper and therefore must end on paper. Every name in that register is a family the Crown says never lived anywhere at all."

Rosalind's fingers hovered above the page without touching it, the instinct of a man trained never to handle documents with damp hands, and Margaret thought: *even now, even terrified, the craftsman.* 

"These are in Edmund's hand," he said at last. His voice had gone strange and flat. "These testimonies—they're copies. He copied them himself. Look—the dates of collection, cross-referenced to the survey sheets. He was building evidence. Chain of custody, almost. Like a barrister." He flipped to one of the sealed letters, unfolded it, read a few lines. "'I, Thomas Wicker, late of Ferrisden, do attest that I held copyhold of twelve acres...'" He looked up at her, and his eyes were bright and appalled. "Mrs. Hollis, this isn't a grievance. This is a *case*. This could go to a magistrate. To Parliament. Why did no one—"

"Who?" Margaret said. "Which magistrate? The coroner who wrote *misadventure* rode in the same coach as the men who offered Edmund gold for this book. Edmund tried, near the end. There's a letter in that packet addressed to a reforming member, a Mr. Finch's circle, the printing people—he had begun corresponding. Three weeks later he was dead." She watched the boy arrive, step by step, at the place she had been living for ten days. "The past doesn't stay buried, child. It waits until someone digs. And then it wants company."

Rosalind stood motionless over the register for a long moment. When he finally spoke, his voice was very quiet.

"The northern sheets," he said. "The ones I've been finishing. The margins are empty where his notes should continue. I thought he'd simply run out of time." He touched the edge of the register as if it might burn. "He wasn't run out of time. He was making room. He wanted me to—" He stopped himself.

"He trusted you," Margaret said. "Yes. He chose you, and he never told either of us that he'd done it, which was cruel of him and exactly like him." She sank into Edmund's chair at last, because her legs would hold her no further, and looked up at the apprentice who had become, in the space of ten minutes and forty-one days, something far more dangerous. "Now you see what I see. Now you know why I set a second teacup and jump at knocks. Those men will come back, Rosalind. Men who kill a mapmaker over a book do not stop wanting the book because the mapmaker stops breathing. And the army has been measuring our harbor this fortnight past—Adaeze told my girl, and my girl told me—and soldiers do not come to garrison a town for nothing."

Outside, the rain had slackened. Through the window Vellamouth's rooftops ran down toward the grey water, chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless air, the whole town innocent and unaware, every one of those roofs holding a family with a name.

Rosalind closed the register with great care. When he raised his head, the fear in his face had not gone—but it had changed shape, hardened into something with edges.

"Mrs. Hollis," he said, "how many names are in that register?"

"Sixty-seven families," she said. "Three towns. And counting."

He looked at the atlas, then at the window, then at her—and she saw the exact moment the boy became the keeper.

"Then we had better make sure," he said, "that nobody counts ours next."

Down in the lane, faint through the settling dusk, came the sound of boots—many boots, in step, marching toward the harbor.
