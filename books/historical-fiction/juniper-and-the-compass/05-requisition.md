## 5. Requisition

Rosalind woke to the sound of boots.

Not the scattered, amiable clatter of the fishing fleet setting out—the town had learned that rhythm the way one learns a mother's footsteps in a hallway—but something heavier, more ordered, arriving in waves from the direction of the quay. He lay for a moment in his narrow bed above the map room, staring at the water stain on the ceiling that he had long ago decided resembled the coast of some undiscovered country, and told himself it was nothing. Deliveries. Drills. The garrison stretching its legs.

Then Adaeze's fist struck the door three times, hard as a hammer on a chisel.

"Rosalind. Get up. They're at the harbor."

He dressed in the dark, fingers finding shirt and waistcoat by habit, and took the stairs two at a time. Adaeze stood in the street with her shawl thrown over her shoulders and her hair still braided for sleep, and her face—her face was not afraid, exactly. It was worse than that. It was vindicated. She had counted the boats, she had warned him, and she had been right, and there was no satisfaction anywhere in it.

"How many?" he asked.

"More than I could count before the light came." She turned toward the water without waiting for him. "Come and see."

The sky over Vellamouth that morning was the color of pewter, a low ceiling of cloud pressing down on the rooftops so that the whole town seemed to have been drawn smaller overnight, compressed into some reduced scale. Rosalind and Adaeze came out onto Cobbler's Row and joined the thin stream of neighbors all drifting the same direction, silent as iron filings following a magnet, and when they reached the head of Fore Street where it opened onto the quay, Rosalind stopped walking and simply stood.

The harbor was gone.

Not the water—the water remained, grey and patient, lapping at the stone steps as it had lapped for two hundred years. But everything else had changed in the night. A line of redcoats stretched along the quay's edge, bayonets fixed, spaced at intervals so precise they might have been set out with a ruler. Beyond them, in the roads, the transports Adaeze had counted lay at anchor in a great crescent, their hulls black against the pale water, and among them now flew a ship Rosalind did not recognize—a mail packet, white and swift-looking, its decks already crowded with soldiers. Boats were moving between ship and shore in steady procession, and each boat came back heavier than it went out.

"They're loading cargo," Adaeze said quietly. "Crates off the customs wharf since before first light. Army crates."

A workman beside them, a cooper named Ellery whom Rosalind had known all his life, crossed himself without seeming to notice he'd done it.

Along the front of the old fish market, soldiers had pasted proclamations to the shutters with paste and brush, working left to right like men papering a wall, and a town clerk's assistant—pale, apologetic, holding the documents flat against the wind—stood reading aloud in a voice pitched to carry. Rosalind pushed forward through the gathering crowd until he could see the printed text itself, and read it twice, because the first time through his eyes refused to make sense of the words.

*By order of His Majesty's Government, and pursuant to powers vested in the General Officer Commanding the Southern District: the town of Vellamouth and its harbour are hereby requisitioned for reasons of sanitary necessity. The inhabitants will be conveyed to ports eastward aboard His Majesty's vessel* Perseverance, *now anchored in the roads. Owing to limitations of tonnage, only those persons whose names appear upon the sailing list shall be permitted to embark. The sailing list shall be compiled from the census ledger of the parish of Vellamouth. All persons not listed will be removed by road.*

*Embarkation commences upon the fourth day.*

Sanitary necessity. Rosalind read the phrase again and felt it dissolve under examination like ink held under running water. Vellamouth was clean. Its wells ran sweet; its streets were swept; the fever that had visited Kettering-upon-Marsh two summers past had never crossed the river here. Everyone knew this. Sanitary necessity was a phrase from a government memorandum, and it had no more business attached to Vellamouth than an anchor had business hanging from a gull's neck.

"The ledger," someone said behind him, and then the words passed backward through the crowd like a current through water—*the ledger, the ledger decides, the ledger*—and Rosalind turned and saw Mr. Pell, the town clerk, coming down Fore Street at a half-run with the great census book clasped to his chest the way a man carries a child across a flooded ford.

He had seen that ledger four days ago, delivered survey maps to Pell's office, and watched with mild professional interest while the clerk entered a birth and scratched out a death. Every soul in Vellamouth lived between those covers in copperplate rows. His own name. Adaeze's. Margaret Hollis's, alone now in the house on Chandler's Lane. Old Mrs. Tregaskin and her goats, which were not in the ledger but ought to have been, for the goats were more regular in their habits than their mistress.

And now that book, that ordinary book of births and burials and chimney-tax assessments, had become the gate through which an entire town must pass or fail to pass.

"Names," said Ellery the cooper, slowly, as though tasting something spoiled. "They're saying only some names go. How do they choose which?"

Nobody answered him. The question hung over the quay, and beneath it Rosalind could feel the crowd beginning to change—to shift its weight, to glance sideways, to begin, already, in these first minutes, the terrible arithmetic of *who*. He saw it happen in the faces around him the way one sees weather change over open water: Mr. and Mrs. Cade from the ropewalk, whose name was common enough in the parish that there might be three Cades on any list; Widow Marsh, who had no family and therefore perhaps no one to speak for her; the Penhaligon brothers, who owned two houses between them and had quarreled for a decade and were now, suddenly, jointly interested in the same single hope.

Adaeze gripped his sleeve. "Where's your name written?" she asked.

"In Pell's book. Same page as yours, near enough."

"Then we hold to each other," she said. "Whatever the list says. We hold."

He wanted to say yes. What came out instead was the truth, which was quieter and more useless: "They haven't made the list yet, Ade. There's nothing to hold to." And even as he said it, he heard how it sounded—not comfort but warning—and saw it land in her face the same way.

* * *

By mid-morning the town had reorganized itself around the proclamation the way a river reorganizes around a fallen tree, every current bending to the new obstruction. Soldiers at the harbor mouth turned back the fishing boats—one skipper, Brannagh, argued until a sergeant put a hand flat on his gunwhale and said, quite politely, that the boat would be confiscated if it touched water again before orders changed. Brannagh came ashore white-faced and sat down on the steps among his nets as though his legs had been taken from him. Forty years on that water. Rosalind watched him and understood, in a way no proclamation had yet taught him, what *requisitioned* meant: not seized, exactly. Not stolen. Erased. As if the harbor had been drawn over in heavy ink until whatever lay beneath could no longer be seen.

He went to the map room because going there was the oldest instinct he had, the way other men reached for drink or prayer. The room above the chandler's shop had been Master Hollis's kingdom for thirty years and was his now—or as much his as anything could be said to belong to an apprentice whose master was eleven weeks dead and whose wages had died with him. The smell of the place met him at the door: linseed oil, lamp-black, the sour-sweet breath of old vellum. Charts in the drawers, coastlines in the presses, and on the great table under its dust sheet the atlas—Hollis's atlas, the thing he had died rather than surrender, though the coroner had called it a robbery gone wrong and everyone in authority had been very quick to agree.

Rosalind drew back the sheet and looked at the atlas without touching it. He had worked on it until two in the morning, finishing plate after plate in Hollis's style so faithfully that sometimes, waking, he could not remember which lines were the dead man's and which were his own. He had believed—he had genuinely believed, standing at that table in the small hours—that this work mattered more than almost anything in the world.

Now he stood in full daylight and heard boots on the cobbles below and a lieutenant's voice calling measurements to a sergeant, and he understood that history, which he had always assumed happened elsewhere, to other people, in other centuries—the sort of thing one mapped in retrospect with confident arrows—had come to Vellamouth in the night and was standing in the street outside his window, counting his neighbors.

He worked anyway. That was the strange part, and he would remember it afterward: that his hands went on doing what hands do. He ruled a margin. He mixed a wash of Prussian blue. Somewhere below, a soldier's voice sang out a number, and another answered, and he realized they were measuring the chandler's shop itself, chain by chain, writing Vellamouth into the army's books even as the army wrote the town's people out of their own.

A shadow crossed the light, and he looked up to find Adaeze in the doorway, dock rope still over one shoulder, her face tight.

"They've taken the customs house," she said. "Pell's office too. Two soldiers at the door and the ledger's inside—they carried it in themselves an hour ago, under guard, like it was the crown jewels." She came further into the room, dropping her voice though there was no one to hear. "Rosie. Margaret Hollis sent the boy Tansy up from Chandler's Lane. She wants you. She says—" Adaeze hesitated, and he saw that the message had frightened her, whatever it was. "She says to bring nothing and tell no one. She says it's about what Edmund found."

* * *

Margaret Hollis opened her door before Rosalind had finished knocking, as though she had been standing behind it for hours, and perhaps she had. The house smelled of yesterday's fire, of cold tea and lavender and the particular stillness of rooms where a sickbed has recently ended. Eleven weeks, and the stillness had not begun to leave.

She was a spare woman, straight-backed, sixty and proud of every year of it, with white hair pinned so severely it seemed to discipline the rest of her. Today that discipline showed cracks. Her hands, folded at her waist, were not entirely steady, and when she spoke his name her voice came out lower than usual, sanded down by a sleepless night.

"You've seen the harbor," she said. It was not a question.

"The whole town's seen the harbor."

"Then you know what the ledger is now." She stepped back to let him in, closed the door, and shot the bolt—an act so foreign to Chandler's Lane that Rosalind felt his scalp tighten. "Sit down, child. I have spent the night deciding whether to show you a thing, and cowardice lost, which is the only reason you're here."

On the table under the window lay a letter. It was old—Rosalind could see that from across the room—the paper foxed and brittle, folded in thirds and sealed with wax that had cracked along its seam. Beside it lay a second sheet in a different hand, cramped and crowded, columns of names running down the margins like water stains climbing a wall.

"Edmund hid things in that atlas of yours," Margaret said. "In the binding, in the plates, in places only a mapmaker would think to look. I let myself believe, these eleven weeks, that it was caution. Habit. An old man's paranoia about government surveyors." Her mouth thinned. "Last night, when the proclamation went up, I stopped believing it. I went to the book you left with me to finish, and I took the back board loose the way I once watched him do, and this is what was inside."

Rosalind sat down slowly and drew the letter toward him. It was addressed in Hollis's hand—*To whoever continues my work*—and dated the previous spring. He read it once in silence. Then he read it again, because the first reading had left a ringing in his ears like a struck bell.

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead or I soon shall be, and I do not write it to frighten you but to arm you. Three towns before this one. Thorne Quay, Ashmill, Lower Fenney. Each declared insanitary. Each requisitioned. Each emptied of its people by a list, and each list compiled from a census ledger altered first. In each case the land passed within the year to parties who had surveyed it months before the 'necessity' arose. I have gathered the names of the displaced families from the refugees themselves—names the official record no longer contains, because the record was made to forget them. They are written here and in the margins of my plates. The atlas is not merely a map of the coast, Rosalind. It is a register of the erased. Guard it as such, and finish it, and if the day comes that Vellamouth stands where those towns stood, you will know that what looks like rescue is purchase, and what reads as sanitation is theft with clean hands.*

The second sheet was in Hollis's later hand, tighter, hurried. Names. Dates of removal. Parishes. And in the final column, in each row, a name or company that Rosalind did not know—except that he had seen two of them recently, stamped in red ink on the crates being loaded at the customs wharf.

The floor of the ordinary world, the world of surveys and apprenticeships and quiet evenings over coastlines, dropped away beneath him. He felt it go—a physical sensation, a falling without motion—and gripped the edge of the table.

"He refused them," Margaret said into the silence. "Government surveyors came in March, wanting the atlas. He would not give it up. A fortnight later he was beaten to death on Marsh Lane and his pockets turned out, and the men who did it were never found, and the magistrate did not look very hard, and I was too deep in grief to ask why." Her voice did not break; it simply ran out, briefly, like a tide withdrawing before returning. "I am not too deep in grief to ask why now."

"Mrs. Hollis—" Rosalind began.

"Margaret."

"Margaret. If this is true—if Thorne Quay and Ashmill and Lower Fenney—" He stopped, because saying it made it real, and real meant that the white ship in the roads was not a rescue but a purse, and the ledger in the guarded customs house was not a record but an instrument, and every name in it—including Adaeze's, including his own—was a line item in somebody's acquisition. "Then the fourth day is not a sailing day. It's a harvest."

"Yes." Margaret sat down opposite him, and for the first time since he had known her—for the first time in the whole of his memory of her—she reached across the table and took his hand. Her grip was dry and strong. "Edmund believed one thing at the end, and he wrote it plainly, so I will say it plainly to you. Truth is also cargo, Rosalind. It can be loaded and shipped and landed elsewhere. It weighs less than a crate and outsails any packet in those roads. But it must be moved before the harbor closes entirely—and the harbor," she added, with terrible gentleness, "closed this morning at dawn."

Through the window, faint on the wind, came the sound of the quay: hammers, shouted numbers, the creak of the derricks loading army freight. Four days. Rosalind looked down at the letter in his hands—at the neat, doomed, deliberate hand of the man who had taught him that a coastline honestly drawn was a kind of honesty itself—and felt the last hour's vertigo begin, slowly, to harden into something else. Not calm. Nothing so kind as calm. But resolve, the way soft ground hardens under frost.

Outside on Chandler's Lane, boots passed the shuttered windows in step—two soldiers, then four, patrolling a street of fishermen's cottages as though it were the frontier of an enemy province. One of them paused, and a shadow lay along the seam of the shutters for a long moment before moving on.

When Rosalind finally spoke, his voice sounded unlike itself: level, quiet, older.

"The ledger decides who goes," he said. "So tell me, Margaret—before they start altering it. Who holds the pen?"
