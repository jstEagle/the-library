## 6. Names Become Currency

The list was nailed to the door of the customs house at eight o'clock on the morning after the requisition, and by half past eight the whole of Vellamouth had gathered in the square to read its own death warrant in alphabetical order.

Rosalind stood near the back, Adaeze's shoulder pressed against his, and watched a town learn arithmetic.

The proclamation had come at dawn, read aloud by a lieutenant with a voice like a man reciting a laundry inventory. *By order of His Majesty's Government, the town of Vellamouth is hereby requisitioned on grounds of sanitary necessity. The port is closed. One vessel, the HMS Persephone, shall evacuate such residents as may be listed. Priority of passage shall be determined by the census ledger.* Then the lieutenant had rolled up his paper, tucked it under his arm, and walked away from three hundred shouting people as though walking away from a mild disagreement about the weather.

Now the list itself hung behind glass, and Mr. Pettibone the customs clerk stood beside it with a long pointer, like a schoolmaster, tapping each name as the crowd's eyes followed.

"Ashcombe, Tobias. Ashcombe, Prudence. Ashcombe, the child. Bexley, Harold. Bexley, Margaret—"

"It's the alphabet," Adaeze said quietly. Her voice had a strange flatness Rosalind had learned to distrust; it was the voice she used when she was counting something she didn't want to feel. "They're going by the alphabet. First letters first."

Rosalind looked at the list and saw it was true. The A's and B's filled the first column, whole families, whole streets—the Ashcombes from Cooper's Lane, the Bexleys from the chandlery, the Brambles, the Buckets, the Bywaters. Further down, the columns thinned. C's and D's appeared in scattered pairs, marked *provisional*. And then the great mass of the town—every Ellery, every Fenwick, every Gorse, Hollis, Ives, and beyond—was simply absent. Not refused. Not rejected. *Pending.*

Pending. As though their lives were a shipment awaiting inspection.

"Okonkwo," Adaeze said, reading down the columns with her finger an inch from the glass. "Okonkwo, Adaeze. Provisional." She stopped. Her finger did not move on. "That's all. Just me."

Rosalind read it three times, as though the letters might rearrange themselves out of shame. *Okonkwo, Adaeze. Provisional.* His own name was nowhere. Margaret Hollis was nowhere. The ledger had reached into the town like a hand into a drawer and lifted out a single thing, and the thing it had chosen was his sister, and he did not know why, and the not-knowing was worse than the cruelty itself.

"Provisional," he repeated. "What does provisional mean?"

"It means," said a voice behind them, "that it can be taken away as easily as it was given."

It was Mrs. Buckett, the fishwife, her hands still smelling of the morning's catch, her eyes red-rimmed. Her own name was on the list—B came early—but her daughter had married a Fenwick, and Fenwicks were pending, and the arithmetic of the alphabet had already begun to divide a family that had shared one roof for eleven years.

"Provisional means they haven't decided yet," Mrs. Buckett said. "And while they haven't decided, a body can *persuade* them."

She said it without bitterness, or rather with a bitterness so fresh it had not yet hardened into anything. She said it the way one reports the weather. Then she pushed past them toward the customs house door, where a queue had already begun to form—not a queue for bread, not a queue for the doctor, but a queue of neighbors waiting to plead their names to a clerk with a ledger and a stamp.

Rosalind watched her go and felt the first cold drop of the thing that would fall on all of them.

* * *

By noon, the square had become a market, and the only commodity was names.

Rosalind saw it happen with the helpless clarity of a man watching a tide come in—slow, then all at once, then everywhere. Old Gorse, who had sold rope at the harbor for forty years, was offering his shop's goodwill—*the lease, mind, the whole lease*—to anyone whose surname began with a letter that might, by some clerical mercy, be transferable. The Bramble brothers, who hadn't spoken since a quarrel over their father's boat seven years ago, stood together at the customs house door, pooling their case: *one household, one entry, surely, the ledger must see reason.* Widow Ellery had taken her late husband's discharge papers from their oilcloth wrapping for the first time in nine years and was waving them at anyone in a red coat, crying that her Thomas had bled for the Crown at Corunna and the Crown owed her a letter of the alphabet.

And the letters of the alphabet—God help them—were already for sale.

Rosalind heard it first as rumor, in the baker's queue, which had become the town's parliament since the ovens went cold under requisition. A man named Cutter, a Cornishman who had come to Vellamouth only two years past and was therefore barely a neighbor at all, had paid the widow Ashcombe twenty pounds for the right to add his name to her household entry. *Ashcombe, Tobias; Ashcombe, Prudence; Ashcombe, the child; Ashcombe, Cutter.* Four names, one roof, one berth each. The widow Ashcombe would sail with twenty pounds and a stranger's surname stitched to hers, and the stranger would sail with a life, and everyone in the queue agreed it was shameful, and everyone in the queue quietly calculated what their own roof might hold.

"It's not even clever," Adaeze said that evening, in Margaret Hollis's kitchen, where the three of them had begun to take their meals together out of something none of them named. "It's not even *clever*. Anyone could see through it. Cutter doesn't look like an Ashcombe. He's got red hair."

"The ledger doesn't record hair," Margaret said. She was sitting very straight in her chair, hands folded, the way she had sat since the morning of the proclamation—composed the way ice is composed, all its violence held in a brittle skin. "The ledger records what the clerk writes. And the clerk writes what he is told, or what he is paid, or what frightens him least. My husband used to say a census is a mirror, and every mirror shows the face of whoever holds it."

Rosalind thought of Hollis's marginalia, hidden in the atlas's binding, the lists of names from three towns—names that had once lived in ledgers like this one, and had been scraped out of them, or never entered at all. He had not told Margaret how much of the atlas he had already finished. He was not sure why he kept it back. Perhaps because saying it aloud would make it real, and real things could be demanded.

"Someone should watch the clerk," he said.

Adaeze looked at him. "You mean spy on him."

"I mean *watch* him. Pettibone. He's held that ledger for twenty years. He knows every family in this town. And now he's the gatekeeper of—" Rosalind stopped, because the sentence wanted to end in *salvation*, and he did not believe the word, and he did not want to hear his own disbelief.

"He's the gatekeeper of a piece of paper," Adaeze said. "And pieces of paper can be bought. Everyone in this town figured that out before breakfast."

* * *

He went to the customs house the next morning with a bundle of harbor survey maps under his arm—the requisition officers had demanded updated charts of the quay depths, and Rosalind, as the late Master Hollis's apprentice and the only mapmaker left in Vellamouth, had been conscripted into the service of his own town's dispossession. The irony of it sat in his stomach like a stone. He was drawing the harbor so that they could empty it.

Pettibone's office was at the back of the customs house, a low room that smelled of dust and sealing wax, and the clerk sat at his desk with the great ledger open before him like an altar book. He was a small man, Pettibone, gray and precise, with ink-stained fingers and the stooped patience of someone who had spent his life recording other people's business and had none of his own. Rosalind had known him all his life. Pettibone had once, when Rosalind was ten, given him a discarded pen nib and told him that a clerk's hand was the hand history actually wrote with, whatever the generals believed.

"Master Okonkwo." Pettibone did not look up. "The charts. On the table, if you please."

Rosalind set them down and did not leave. Through the office's single window he could see the square, and the queue, and the people in it. Mrs. Buckett was at the front of the queue now, and her voice came faintly through the glass—*my daughter, sir, my daughter married a Fenwick, surely a mother and a daughter*—and Pettibone's pen moved, and did not move, and moved.

"Mr. Pettibone," Rosalind said. "The Okonkwo entry. My sister's name. It says provisional. I've come to ask what would make it final."

Now Pettibone looked up. His face was a clerk's face—nothing in it, everything behind it. "The sailing list is determined by the census ledger under the authority of the requisition commission," he recited. "Entries are provisional until verified against the original record."

"Verified how?"

"Verified." The pen dipped. "Against the record."

Rosalind looked at the ledger. It lay open to the O's, and he could see the column of names, and his sister's among them, and beside each provisional name a small notation in the margin—tiny, cramped, in a hand that was not Pettibone's usual roundness. He leaned, casually, as if gathering his maps, and read three words of the notation beside *Okonkwo, Adaeze*: *dockworker. useful.*

Useful. The word turned over in his mind. Useful to whom? Useful for what? The army was loading cargo; Adaeze worked the docks; her name was provisional and annotated like an inventory item. His own name was absent entirely—*apprentice mapmaker* presumably useful too, but useful men could be kept without berths. The thought arrived cold and complete: the ledger was not alphabetical at all. The alphabet was the costume. Underneath, someone was sorting them by what they could carry.

"Mr. Pettibone," Rosalind said quietly, "who wrote those marginal notes?"

The pen stopped.

For a moment the room was so still that Rosalind could hear the queue outside, a low animal murmur of three hundred people waiting on a man's pen. Pettibone's hand hovered over the page, and Rosalind saw—saw with the terrible clarity that had been his gift and his curse since he was a boy bent over charts—saw the clerk's eyes flick toward the door, toward the window, toward some point over Rosalind's shoulder where authority might be standing.

Then Pettibone did the thing Rosalind would remember for the rest of his life, in all its smallness and all its enormity.

He took up his blotting cloth, laid it over the margin, and wrote a new entry.

It took perhaps four seconds. A name in the C's—Cutter, Rosalind saw, *Cutter, Josiah*—was lifted from the provisional column and set down beneath *Ashcombe, Tobias*, the household amended, the ink blotted, the page turned as though the ledger had always said so. The motion was smooth. It was practiced. It was the motion of a man who had done it before and expected, with a gray and exhausted certainty, to do it again.

Pettibone did not look at him. "The charts are received," the clerk said. "You may go, Master Okonkwo."

"Mr. Pettibone—"

"*You may go.*"

And there it was, in the crack of the small man's voice—not power, not corruption, but something worse: terror. Pettibone was not the hand holding the mirror. Pettibone was afraid of the hand that held *him*. He was altering the book of his neighbors' lives with a soldier's shadow across his desk, and the twenty pounds the widow Ashcombe had taken, and the twenty others like it Rosalind would hear of by nightfall, were only the visible commerce. Beneath it, invisible, ran the current that mattered: *dockworker, useful; smith, useful; priest, trouble; schoolmistress, trouble*—a town being sorted like cargo by someone who had never learned their faces.

Rosalind picked up his empty map case and walked out into the square.

* * *

The square had changed again. It changed hourly now; the whole town was in flux, like water finding its level around a sinking stone. The queue had dissolved into clusters, and the clusters were not talking to each other. That was the thing Rosalind noticed, the thing that sat coldest in him all that day and all the next: the clusters had stopped talking to each other.

The Brambles were no longer at the customs house door together. He saw the elder Bramble alone, and when Rosalind greeted him, the man's eyes went past his shoulder, checking who stood within earshot, before he answered. The widow Ellery had stopped waving Thomas's discharge papers and now kept them folded inside her coat, and when the young Buckett boy asked her—innocently, loudly, the way children ask—whether she was still going to show the soldiers, she went white and told him to hush, *hush, boy, don't say that word in the square.*

What word? *Soldiers? Show?* Rosalind understood: it no longer mattered. The word itself was enough. The square had become a place where speech had weight and weight could be reported, and everyone had begun, without any order given, without any notice posted, to weigh their words—and then to stop speaking altogether to anyone whose surname might be a letter ahead of their own.

He found Adaeze at the harbor wall at dusk, sitting on the bollards where she always sat, watching the transports at anchor with their riding lamps lit like a string of low, hostile stars. The Persephone lay among them, a gray shape, her decks already stacked with army crates. Cargo first. He had heard that too, all through the square: *cargo first, cargo first*, as though even the order of loading were a verdict.

"You've heard about Cutter," Adaeze said, without looking at him.

"Everyone's heard about Cutter."

"And the widow Ashcombe took the money." Adaeze turned a piece of rope over in her hands, working a frayed end between her fingers. "Twenty pounds. For a name. Do you know what Marta Ashcombe said to me this morning, Ros? She said, *at least I'll be able to buy the child shoes in England.* That's what twenty pounds and a stranger's name comes to. Shoes."

"Adaeze—"

"And the Buckets are done." She said it flatly. "The Buckets. You know them. You've known them since we came off the boat from Portsmouth with nothing. Mrs. Buckett taught you your letters, Ros. She taught you your letters with herring scales on her hands. And this morning old Buckett stood in the square and told the whole street that the Fenwicks were liars and their marriage certificate was forged—his own daughter's marriage, Ros. His own daughter. Because if the marriage is forged, the Fenwicks aren't family, and if they aren't family, the B's don't have to share their berth with an F."

The rope-end tore in her hands. She looked down at it as if surprised.

"She taught me my letters," Rosalind said. It was all he had. It was nothing.

"And Pettibone's pen is for sale, and Bray's patrols are on every corner, and half the town is informing on the other half before supper, and the other half is informing right back." Adaeze stood, and in the last light her face was hard and young and furious, and Rosalind thought: *she is twenty-three and she is already counting boats, she has been counting boats her whole life, she has always known this was coming and I called it abstraction.* "Three days, Ros. The requisition was three days ago. Three days, and we've gone from neighbors to rivals to informers. Not because we're wicked. Because they made the list short on purpose. A short list is a knife, and they've put it on the table between us, and now every one of us is watching everyone else's hands."

She threw the rope-end into the dark water and walked away along the quay toward the dockworkers' sheds, where a low murmur of voices was gathering—she had not told him yet what the dockworkers murmured about, but he had seen the way the loading had slowed, a crate here, a sling there, hours bleeding out of the army's schedule like water from a cracked cask.

Rosalind stayed on the bollards a long while. The riding lamps burned. Somewhere behind him, in the customs house, a gray little man was bending over a great book, blotting out one life and inscribing another, terrified, precise, complicit. And Rosalind Okonkwo, who had believed all his life that history happened elsewhere, to other people, in other centuries—history with a capital H, the history of atlases and armadas—sat on a harbor bollard in the cold and understood at last that history was this: a pen scratching in a small room, a name lifted from one column and set in another, a fishwife's twenty pounds, a father disowning a daughter's marriage for the length of an alphabet.

And underneath it, annotated in a hand that was not the clerk's own: *dockworker. useful.*

Useful. The word would not leave him. Someone was reading this town the way he read a coastline—looking for what could be taken, what could be used, what could be erased without disturbing the overall shape of the chart. And whoever it was had a ledger, and a garrison, and all the time in the world, while Rosalind had a hidden atlas half-finished in a dead man's map room, and a sister's name in a provisional column, and a memory, suddenly and unbearably sharp, of Pettibone's blotting cloth coming down over the margin like a hand over a mouth.

He was still sitting there when the boy found him—a customs house runner, breathless, holding a folded paper sealed with the requisition commission's stamp.

"Master Okonkwo?" the boy said. "You're to come to the fort. First light. The General wants his harbor surveyed." He hesitated, and lowered his voice, and added the part that made Rosalind's blood go cold and strange: "Not the General, sir. The Captain. Captain Vance. She said—" the boy screwed up his face, reciting— "she said to tell you she's read your coastlines, and she has corrections."
