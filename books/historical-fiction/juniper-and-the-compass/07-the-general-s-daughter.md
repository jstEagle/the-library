## 7. The General's Daughter

He had not slept, and the town had not either. Rosalind could feel it in the streets as he walked up through Vellamouth in the grey hour before dawn—the same streets he had walked a thousand times, except that now every window seemed to have eyes behind it, and every door was a decision someone had made overnight: to open it if knocked upon, or to pretend no one was home.

Redcoats stood at the corners in pairs, their breath steaming, their bayonets catching what little light there was. They did not stop him. His summons papers were enough—a folded sheet with the commission's stamp, which had already taken on, after two days, the quality of a passport and a brand. He carried it in his breast pocket, where it lay against his heart like something he had stolen.

The fort sat above the harbor on a knuckle of rock the old charts called Garrison Head, and Rosalind had drawn it perhaps forty times in his life, always from below, always as an afterthought—a hatched rectangle in the upper corner of the plate, labeled *fort.* in Master Hollis's small, exact hand. He had never been inside. Mapmakers, Hollis used to say, are like sextons: we know every town by its dead letters, but we are not invited into the houses.

The gate opened before he reached it. A sergeant with a face like a closed ledger—Bray, the paper said his name was, Sergeant Bray—checked his papers without reading them, checked his hands, checked the flat satchel of instruments over his shoulder, and jerked his chin toward the inner yard.

"Captain Vance is on the north bastion. Don't touch anything you're not told to touch."

"I'm here to draw," Rosalind said. "Touching is most of drawing."

Bray looked at him for a moment, and Rosalind understood that he had made a joke in a language the man did not speak, or spoke only to refuse. "North bastion," Bray said again.

* * *

She was standing at the parapet with his own map unrolled before her, pinned at the corners with four brass weights, and the first thing Rosalind felt—not anger, not fear, though both came soon enough—was the peculiar vertigo of seeing his own handiwork in a stranger's possession. The chart of Vellamouth harbor was three weeks of his life. He knew the exact hour he had inked the shoal line off Gull Point, knew that he had been thinking of Adaeze hauling nets in just such weather when he drew the little wave-marks in the estuary. And now it lay on cold stone under a soldier's weights, annotated in red.

"Master Okonkwo." She did not turn around. She was studying the coast through a spyglass collapsed to a single tube, held loosely, more an ornament than an instrument. "You walk loudly for a man whose trade is observation."

"The stones announce me. I can't help what the stones do."

Now she turned, and Rosalind saw Captain Elowen Vance for the first time, and understood why the customs runner had lowered his voice. Not beauty, exactly—or not only. It was precision. Every feature of her face seemed arrived at deliberately, as though she had surveyed herself and declined to round off the results. She wore her captain's coat with the collar open one button too many for regulation, which was either carelessness or a message, and Rosalind could not yet tell which frightened him more. Her hair was dark red, the color of the annotations on his map, pulled back without ceremony.

"You've drawn this coast since you were how old?"

"Ten. My first plate was the harbor. My master made me do it eleven times until I stopped lying about the depth of the channel."

"And did you?"

"Stop?" He came to the parapet beside her, keeping a careful arm's length, and looked down at the map between them. "No. I got better at knowing when I was lying."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile; a smile that had been weighed and found inadmissible. "Then you'll know what these are." Her finger came down on the red marks, one by one, and Rosalind felt each touch like a tap on glass. "Your soundings off Gull Point are three feet shallow. Your beacon bearing is wrong by half a point—you took it from the town, didn't you, not from the water? And here—" the finger moved to the estuary mouth— "you've drawn the sandbar where it was five years ago. It has migrated east. There's a channel now that will float a transport at half tide, and your map doesn't know it exists."

Rosalind stared at the estuary, and then at her, and the resentment he had carried up the hill like a stone in his satchel shifted its weight uncomfortably, because she was right. He had taken those soundings before the winter storms, and he had not gone back. The army had been in his town nine days and their captain knew his harbor better than he did.

"How do you know the bar has migrated?"

"Because I took a boat out at low water on my second morning here, in the rain, while your neighbors were being counted like sheep." She said it evenly, without triumph, and somehow that was worse. "I survey, Master Okonkwo. It's my work, before it's anyone's orders. You and I have that in common, whether it comforts you or not."

"It doesn't."

"I supposed not." She turned back to the parapet and unrolled a second sheet—one of hers, Rosalind saw immediately, done in a hand so like his master's that his throat tightened: fine, disciplined, economical, every line earning its place. Her survey of the harbor was better than his. He hated that he could recognize it so quickly, and hated more that he could not deny it. "Correct your chart. All of it. I want a fair copy of the approaches by Friday, because the ship's masters will need them, and they will need them accurate."

"The ship's masters," Rosalind said slowly, "who will carry my town away."

Her hand stilled on the brass weight. For a moment the wind off the water was the only sound, pushing against them both, flattening the grass in the embrasures. Below, the harbor lay silver-grey and indifferent, masts bare, transports riding at anchor like patient animals.

"Yes," she said. "Those masters."

"Do you correct maps for everything you empty? Is there a form for it? A regulation ink?"

"That's enough."

"It isn't nearly enough. Nothing about this is enough. Two thousand people, Captain, and your father's proclamation calls us a sanitary necessity, as if the town itself were a wound to be drained. And now you want me to make the drowning more precise—"

"I want you," she said, and her voice did not rise but dropped instead, went quiet and hard as a sounding-line hitting bottom, "to do your work accurately, because inaccurate charts sink ships, and the ships that leave this harbor will carry children." She let that sit between them. Then, quieter still: "You think I don't know what this requisition is. You've known me nine minutes."

"I know what your uniform is."

"Yes," she said. "That at least is accurate."

* * *

They worked through the morning, because there was nothing else to do and because the work, treacherously, was the easiest thing between them. She set him up in a room off the bastion stair—a table, good light, her own field books open for reference—and left him with Sergeant Bray's shadow posted at the door, and returned at intervals to lean over his shoulder and find fault.

It should have been intolerable. It was, mostly. But there was a moment, near midday, when he misjudged a curve of the deep-water channel and she reached past him to turn his sheet slightly toward the light, and her hand came within an inch of his, and he felt the warmth of her sleeve and hated himself for noticing it, and noticed it anyway, thoroughly, the way he noticed everything—which was, after all, his entire trade.

"You flinch," she observed, straightening, "every time I agree with you."

"I flinch every time you touch my map."

"It's a good map. Underneath the errors."

"Underneath the errors," Rosalind said, inking a corrected bearing, "it's the truth. That's rather the definition of my profession, Captain. We put down what is. What gets built on it afterward—the forts, the evictions, the—sanitary necessities—that's yours."

She was quiet for a long moment. When he glanced up, expecting anger, he found her looking out the narrow window instead, down at the town, at the slate roofs and the smoke rising thin and domestic from chimneys where people who were now ledger entries were boiling their ration tea.

"My mother's family," she said, "were cleared out of Strathfearn. Thirty years ago. Before I was born. My grandfather took the army's money and my grandmother never spoke to him again, and my mother grew up in a lodging house in a town that wasn't hers, and she used to stand at windows looking at harbors." She turned back to him, and her face had resumed its precision, every feature back at its post, but something behind it had moved. "I read the proclamation. Sanitary necessity. I've read the drainage reports. There is nothing in this town that a competent board of health would quarantine. You may quote me to no one."

And then she picked up the spyglass and left, boots ringing on the stone stair, leaving Rosalind alone with her words and her field books and the terrible arithmetic of what she had just handed him—for it was evidence, of a kind, or at least a confession, and he was already, helplessly, the sort of man who filed confessions.

* * *

At dusk, Bray escorted him to the gate. At the threshold the captain appeared again, holding out his original map, corrected, weighted, rolled and tied.

"For your trouble. Fair copy by Friday." And then, as he took it, lower, not meeting his eyes: "The sandbar channel. Mark it clearly. Whatever else happens, mark it clearly."

He walked home in the last light, past sentries, past shuttered shops, down to the quay where Adaeze was waiting with her arms crossed and her jaw set in the way that meant she had counted something all day and the number had come out wrong.

"Well?" she said. "What does the General's daughter want with your coastlines?"

Rosalind looked back up at the fort, black against the darkening sky, at the single lit window where a precise and dangerous woman was presumably correcting more maps of more places that would be emptied.

"She wants them accurate," he said. "God help me, Adeze—I think she wants everything accurate."

And he did not yet know, could not yet know, that accuracy was the sharpest thing he would ever be given, or that before the week was out he would be risking all their lives to put it to use.
