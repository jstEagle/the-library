## 8. What Hollis Died For

Margaret Hollis had kept her husband's death in a drawer for eleven months the way other widows kept a lock of hair—taken out at night, looked at, put away before dawn could make a witness of her grief.

The drawer was the bottom one of his writing desk, the one with the false back she had discovered only after the funeral, when she had gone looking for a lock of *him* and found instead a bundle of letters tied in sailcloth thread. She had read them once. Once had been enough to make the house feel haunted by something worse than absence: it felt haunted by *certainty*. Edmund had known he was going to die. He had written as much, in his careful surveyor's hand, the letters small and upright as fence posts, and he had written it without drama, the way he recorded depths and soundings, as though his own end were simply another figure to be entered in the ledger and initialled.

She had not read them again. But tonight, watching Rosalind Okonkwo bend over the atlas in the lamplight of the map room—the boy tracing coastlines with the same small, upright letters, the same maddening patience—she understood that the drawer could not stay closed any longer.

"Rosalind."

He looked up. The candle threw his shadow long across the floor of the map room, across the great oak table where Edmund had worked for thirty years and where, for eleven months now, an apprentice worked alone until two and three in the morning, believing himself unobserved. She wondered if he knew how like his master he had become—not in feature, for God had made them from different clay entirely, but in the *bent* of him, the way his whole body curved toward the paper like a man leaning into wind.

"Mrs. Hollis. It's late. I didn't hear you come down."

"I know you didn't." She crossed the room slowly, one hand on the wall where the plaster had begun to give way—a crack running from ceiling to floorboards like a river on one of his charts, widening each winter. "I want to ask you something, and I want you to answer me honestly, because I'll know if you don't. Have you looked in the margins?"

His hand stopped moving. The pen hovered, a bead of ink gathering at its nib, threatening to fall.

"The margins," she said again.

"I've seen them," he said carefully. "Notes in Master Hollis's hand. Soundings. Dates. Some names I took to be informants or—"

"Or families." She came to stand at the table's edge. The atlas lay open between them: the northern coast, drawn in Edmund's unforgiving exactness, and along the border of the plate, in ink so fine it might have been mistaken for decoration, a column of names. *Ashcombe. Ferris. Okonkwo was not among them; that had been Vellamouth's luck so far.* Below the names, dates. Below the dates, a single word repeated like a refrain: *cleared.*

"You took them to be notes," Margaret said. "They are not notes. They are a reckoning."

* * *

She sent him upstairs first—to fetch nothing, merely to be out of the room while she knelt at the writing desk with her heart going hard against her ribs. Her knees complained on the cold boards. Seventy years old, and kneeling on flagstones for a dead man's secrets; Edmund would have laughed at her and then helped her up, both hands, always both hands, because he had believed a person should be steadied with everything you had.

The false back slid aside the way it always did, reluctantly, with a sound like a held breath released.

The letters were still there. Eleven months of dust lay over them, and she wiped it away with her thumb, leaving brown streaks on the sailcloth thread, and carried the bundle back to the table where the boy waited, watching her with those grave dark eyes that gave nothing away unless you knew how to look.

"Sit down, Rosalind."

He sat.

"Edmund began this book eight years ago," she said. "You'll have assumed he began it last year, when the government men started coming round. He didn't. He began it the winter the Ashcombe evictions happened—you'd have been too young to mark it, but I remember. Three hundred people turned out of their houses in February, in snow, because a syndicate wanted the valley for a reservoir that was never built. You remember the reservoir?"

"There is no reservoir at Ashcombe."

"There is not." She laid the first letter on the table, face up, and watched him read. "There never was going to be one. The survey that justified it was falsified. The families were moved onto poor land, the good land passed into private hands at a fraction of its worth, and when anyone asked, the answer was *sanitary necessity*, the answer was *public safety*, the answer was whatever phrase sounded like a reason and wasn't one. Edmund surveyed that valley afterward. Officially. And unofficially—" she tapped the atlas's margin with one crooked finger "—he wrote down every name the official record left out. Every family. Every holding. What they'd owned. What they'd been paid, which was nothing. Where they went, which was nowhere."

Rosalind had gone very still. It was the stillness she had seen in him the day of the funeral, and she understood now that it was not emptiness but concentration—that this was how the boy thought, by stopping everything else.

"How many towns?" he asked quietly.

"That's the second letter."

He opened it. She watched his eyes move down the page, and saw the moment it landed—the moment the pattern became visible to him, because he was a mapmaker's apprentice and patterns were his mother tongue. Ashcombe. Then Marlow Ferry, two years later. Then the coastal clearances, the hamlets south of the point, gone so quietly most people in Vellamouth never knew they had existed. Each time, the same sequence. A survey commissioned. A finding of necessity. An eviction. A silence. And each time, the land passing onward and upward into hands that never dirtied themselves with the taking.

"It's not three incidents," Rosalind said. His voice had changed—flattened, the way Edmund's voice used to flatten when he found an error in a chart that someone had tried to hide. "It's one method. Repeated."

"One method," Margaret agreed, "repeated four times. And coming here next."

* * *

The candle guttered. She rose to trim the wick, glad of something to do with her hands, because the next part was the part she had kept out of the drawer even while it sat inside it.

"You know how Edmund died," she said, with her back to him. "Everyone in Vellamouth knows. A fall on the harbour stairs. An old man, a bad heart, a wet night. The coroner wrote *misadventure* and everyone nodded because it was easier than thinking."

"Yes, ma'am."

"He fell because he was pushed." She turned around. "And he was pushed because three days earlier, two men came to this house. Government surveyors, they said, though they showed no commission I ever saw. One did the talking—a soft-spoken creature, very reasonable, the kind of reasonable that makes your skin crawl. He said the Crown required all of Edmund's recent survey materials. All plates, all field books, all correspondence relating to the coastal work. He said it was a matter of administrative tidiness. Administrative *tidiness*. I remember the word because it was so absurd—like calling a funeral a bit of housekeeping."

Rosalind's gaze dropped to the atlas. To the margins full of names.

"Edmund told him the materials were incomplete," she went on. "That was true, in a sense—they're always incomplete, a survey is never finished, that's the first thing they teach you, isn't it? But what he meant was: *you will not have them*. The man understood. They looked at one another across this table—I was in the kitchen, listening, and ashamed of listening, and grateful forever after that I'd done it—and then the man smiled and said something I have thought about every day since. He said, 'Mr. Hollis, maps are only opinions with good posture.'"

"What did Master Hollis say?"

"Nothing. He saw them out. Closed the door. Stood with his hand on it a long while. Then he came in here and worked until morning, which was how I knew he was frightened, because when Edmund was frightened he worked, the way some men drink." Her voice caught and she let it, having decided long ago she would not perform composure she did not feel. "Three nights later he went down to the harbour to take soundings—he went at night sometimes, when the tide was wrong for daylight work and the truth mattered more than comfort. And somebody followed him up those wet stairs, and my husband, who had climbed rigging at forty and ladders at sixty, who had walked every mile of this coastline twice over, came to the bottom of the harbour stairs with his skull broken and his field book missing from his coat."

"They took his field book."

"They took what they could find. Which is why—" and here she placed her palm flat upon the atlas, upon its worn leather cover, warm now from the candlelight and the hours of the boy's hands "—this must never be found. Do you understand me, Rosalind? The marginalia is not sentiment. It is evidence. Names, holdings, dates, payments—or the absence of payments—a pattern across four towns and nine years, written by a licensed surveyor whose testimony would have stood in any court in England, if any court in England would hear it. That book is the only account of what was done to those people that exists anywhere outside the heads of the people it was done to. And the men who did it believe it burned with the Ashcombe records, or drowned with Marlow Ferry's parish chest, or otherwise ceased to matter. If they learn it exists, they will do to this book what they did to your master. And possibly to whoever holds it."

The room was quiet except for the candle's small industrious burning. Down the street a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Rosalind looked down at the atlas for a long moment. Then he said, in the flattened voice again: "The requisition. The census ledger. The ship."

"You've seen it too, then."

"I've seen soldiers measuring the harbour. I've seen the clerk with the ledger. I haven't seen what connects them."

"Then look at your margins again." She leaned over the table and turned the atlas back several plates, to the coastal surveys, and set her finger beneath one of the entries. "*Cleared, autumn '46, holdings consolidated, purchaser unrecorded.* Now think about who has been buying land round Vellamouth these two years—the factor from London, the gentlemen who come and stay at the George and say nothing to anyone. Think about why a town in decent repair, with a working harbour, needs rescuing all of a sudden. Your master spent nine years learning how this is done, Rosalind. He learned it too well. They couldn't leave him alive knowing it."

The boy's jaw was tight. When he spoke, his voice was low and very controlled, and she heard in it the thing she had come downstairs hoping to find and afraid to name: not grief, which spends itself, but purpose, which compounds.

"Mrs. Hollis. Why tell me this tonight? Why not burn it, or bury it, or give it to a magistrate?"

"A magistrate." She almost laughed. "Which magistrate? The ones who approved the Ashcombe clearance sit in judgment at the assizes not forty miles from here. No, child. There is nowhere in the law to put this book. There is only printing presses and brave fools." She drew a breath, and the breath shook more than she intended, and she pressed on regardless, because a torch does not pass itself. "I'm telling you because you're the one sitting at his table at two in the morning finishing his work. Because you have his hand. And because I am seventy years old, and my hands shake, and sooner rather than later someone is going to search this house the way they should have searched it last year. When they do, I would rather this book be in the keeping of someone young enough to run and stubborn enough not to stop."

* * *

They talked until the candle was a thumb's width from dead. She told him about the letters—how many there were, what years they covered, the code of ordinary language Edmund had used in case of interception, *soundings* for interviews with refugees, *weather* for the movements of government men, *a fair wind* for a name safely gathered. Rosalind listened the way stone listens, and asked questions that showed he had already gone further than she had: whether the marginalia established purchase prices or only evictions (only evictions—prices had been Edmund's unfinished work), whether any displaced family could be produced alive to testify (yes, at Marlow Ferry; the ferrymaster's widow kept a lodging house two counties over), whether the atlas could be copied entire before anyone came for it (his eyes had flicked to the book, calculating plates and hours, and she had watched a whole campaign assemble itself behind his face in the space of ten seconds).

Young men, she thought. They turn everything into campaigns. It is their way of being frightened.

At the door of the map room, with the letters hidden again and the atlas locked in the chart press where Edmund had kept it, Rosalind paused.

"Your husband knew the risk," he said. "The letters say so."

"He counted the cost to the farthing," Margaret said. "He was a surveyor. It's what they do."

"And he paid it anyway."

"He paid it anyway." She looked at the boy standing in the doorway with the candlelight behind him, and something in her chest turned over—an ache, but a clean one, like a bone setting. "Do you know what he wrote in the last letter? The night before the stairs? He wrote: *If I am stopped, the work is not stopped, because the work was never mine. I only held the pen awhile. Whoever takes it up next will hold it better, being younger and having less to lose and more to prove.*"

Rosalind said nothing. But his hand had gone, unconsciously, to the edge of the doorframe, gripping it, and she saw that the words had landed where they were aimed.

"Go home and sleep," she told him gently. "Come tomorrow with a clear head. We have copying to do, and I think very little time in which to do it."

He went. She stood in the map room after he had gone, in the smell of ink and tallow and old paper, and let herself feel it all at once, the fear and the fury and the strange lightness, because the secret had been a stone in her for eleven months and now it was a coal, passed hand to hand, and coals can start fires.

From the window she watched him cross the dark square toward the dockside lanes, shoulders hunched against the river cold, and she noticed what she had been too frightened to notice before: that at the corner by the customs house, where the lamp had been broken for a month, a figure stood smoking, and did not smoke like a dockhand, and watched the boy pass with the unhurried attention of a man making a note.

Margaret closed the curtain. Tomorrow, then. But tomorrow had already begun arriving, and it wore boots.
