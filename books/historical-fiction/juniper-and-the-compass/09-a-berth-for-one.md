## 9. A Berth for One

The notice came on a Tuesday, nailed to the customs house door with four brass tacks that gleamed like coins in the grey morning light. Adaeze Okonkwo saw it before she saw anything else that day—saw the crowd gathered beneath it the way gulls gather over a gutted fish—and her hands went still on the rope she was coiling.

She had counted boats all her life. She could tell you how many herring smacks had gone out before dawn, how many colliers waited at anchor for the tide, how many navy cutters had slid into Vellamouth harbor since the redcoats came. Numbers were her trade and her comfort. But the number on that notice was one, and it took her three readings to understand what it meant.

*By order of His Majesty's Commissioners for Sanitary Necessity, the following residents of Vellamouth are granted passage aboard HMS Perseverance, sailing upon the tide of Friday next.*

Below it, columns of names. Hundreds of them, drawn from the census ledger with its careful copperplate hand. She found the Okonkwo entry where the O's began, between Oakham and Odell, and read it twice because her eyes refused to believe what they had already seen.

*Okonkwo, Adaeze — dockworker — approved.*

One name. One.

No *Okonkwo, Rosalind*. No *Hollis, Margaret*. The clerk's pen had passed over their names the way a scythe passes over wheat, cutting some and leaving others standing for no reason the wheat could ever discern.

A woman beside her—a cooper's widow named Mrs. Trelawny whose husband had mended barrels for thirty years—was reading the list aloud to those who couldn't push close enough, her voice going flat as slate each time she reached a name that wasn't there. Somewhere behind Adaeze, a man began to weep without any sound at all, just the shudder of his shoulders visible above the crowd.

Adaeze did not weep. Her father had taught her, before the fever took him, that tears spent on paper were wasted; save them for flesh. So she stood in the cold and let the fury build instead, low and steady, like a fire banked for the night, and when the crowd began to disperse she turned toward home with her jaw set so hard it ached.

* * *

Home was two rooms above the chandlery now, since the army had requisitioned the lower floor for stores. Rosalind was there when she came up the stairs, bent over the great table he'd carried piece by piece from Master Hollis's map room, his good hand moving across a chart with the patient devotion he gave to nothing else—not to food, not to sleep, not lately even to her.

"Rosalind."

He looked up. He had ink on his fingers and exhaustion in the hollows under his eyes, and for one unguarded moment he smiled at her the way he had smiled when they were children, before everything, before the soldiers and the ledger and the ship waiting in the harbor like a promise made to be broken.

"You're back early. I was going to come down to the quay at midday—Margaret wanted me to fetch her some of that willow bark tea, and I thought I'd—"

"There's a list." She kept her voice level. She had learned levelness the way he had learned cartography: through long practice, through necessity. "Posted at the customs house. Names allowed on the Perseverance."

His smile faded by degrees, like a lamp running out of oil. "And?"

"And there is one Okonkwo on it." She heard her own voice crack on the last word and hated herself for it. "Me. Just me, Rosalind. You're not on it. Margaret isn't on it. Half the town isn't on it—the Fenwicks, the Carricks, old Mr. Pellow who taught you your letters. Alphabetical, they say. As if God himself had sorted us from A to Z and run out of mercy somewhere around the letter O."

Rosalind set down his pen. It was a small gesture, deliberate, the way he did everything—as though the world were a drawing that could be corrected if only one worked slowly enough. "There must be a mistake," he said. "I'll go to the clerk. There are procedures. There's an appeals process, Margaret mentioned—"

"An appeals process." Adaeze laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, nothing like laughter at all. "Rosalind. Brother. Listen to yourself. They posted our fates on a door with brass tacks. Do you think the man who wrote that list cares about your procedures? He wrote it in an office that smells of sealing wax while men with bayonets stood outside his window. There is no appeal. There is only the list, and the list has spoken, and the list says my brother drowns with the rest of them."

"I won't drown. Nobody's drowning. The town isn't sinking, Adaeze, it's being *evacuated*, which means—"

"Which means what? That the fever they invented gives us all time to file paperwork?" She crossed the room and gripped his shoulders, felt the thin bones of him under her hands, this brother she had half-raised, this dreamer who believed that maps could hold back armies. "They're taking the harbor, Rosalind. Whatever story they've dressed it in—sanitary necessity, quarantine, God knows what next—they want the harbor and they want us gone, and the ones who don't fit on their ship can walk into the marshes and be forgotten. That's the arithmetic. That's all of it."

He was quiet then. Outside, a patrol went past below the window, boots striking cobbles in unison, and they both listened until the sound faded.

"One berth," Rosalind said finally, very softly. "You have to take it."

"No."

"Adaeze—"

"*No.*" She released him and stepped back, arms folding across her chest, the dockworker's stance, immovable as ballast. "Don't stand there and tell me to board a ship while you stay here with soldiers in the streets and no place on any list. Don't you dare make me choose between my brother and my own skin, because I am telling you now, plainly, so there's no confusion later: I will not go. Not without you. Not without Margaret. Let them strike my name off too. Let them keep their rotten ship."

"That's madness."

"That's family."

The word hung between them. Rosalind looked away first, down at the chart on his table—at the coastline he'd been inking, the little careful waves he drew along the waterline, the soundings marked in fathoms. He loved this town on paper more than most people loved it in the flesh, and she knew that, and she loved him for it and against it both.

"Margaret will say the same thing I'm saying," he said at last.

"Then Margaret and I agree, and that should frighten you, because we never agree about anything else."

* * *

Margaret Hollis received the news the way she received everything these days: seated upright in her chair by the window, hands folded, spine straight as a mast, as though grief were a wind she had learned to sail against rather than be swamped by. She was seventy-one years old and had buried a husband murdered for a book, and there was very little left in the world that could surprise her.

But when Adaeze finished speaking—when she laid out the arithmetic of it, one name, one berth, one sister saved and everyone else abandoned—Margaret's folded hands tightened, and Adaeze saw something move behind her eyes that was not surprise but something older and colder. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman who had watched this country consume people before and had hoped, foolishly, that age might exempt her.

"Well," Margaret said. "That's that, then."

"That's not *that*," Adaeze said. "We fight it. There must be someone to petition—"

"Petition whom, child? The general? The Crown?" Margaret's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "I petitioned the surveyor general once, eleven years ago, about a boundary dispute on Hollis land. Do you know what I received back? A printed card thanking me for my correspondence. My *correspondence*. As though I had written to inquire after their health." She unfolded her hands and looked at them—liver-spotted, steady. "This country answers letters with cards and petitions with silence. It answers blood with lists on doors."

"Then we answer differently."

Margaret studied her for a long moment. In that look, Adaeze felt weighed and measured, the way cargo was weighed at the customs house—tonnage assessed, value determined. Whatever the old woman found, it seemed to satisfy her, because she nodded once, slow.

"Yes," Margaret said. "Perhaps we do. But hear me, Adaeze Okonkwo, and hear me clearly, because I am old and I do not have breath to waste repeating myself." She leaned forward. "That boy of yours—your brother—he will try to fix this. He will try to fix it the way he fixes everything, with ink and patience and some clever scheme he hasn't told either of us about yet. And when he does, whatever it costs him, whatever it asks of you—you hold him to one thing. You make certain he doesn't spend himself buying berths for other people while his own name goes wanting. He has a talent for that. For loving everyone except himself. It will be the death of him if we let it."

"He's not mine to command."

"No one is anyone's to command. We ask anyway." Margaret settled back into her chair. "Now sit. Have some tea. Tell me about the docks—what the loaders are saying, whether the army's cargo goes aboard before the passengers. Details matter. In my experience, tyrants are careless with details, and details are where they bleed."

So Adaeze sat, and told her about the crates stenciled with the broad arrow being stacked on the quay, about the cranes requisitioned, about the dockworkers ordered to load naval stores ahead of refugee baggage. Margaret listened the way she did everything—completely, without interruption—and somewhere in the telling Adaeze realized she had stopped shaking. There was medicine in being useful. There was medicine in being believed.

* * *

Night came down on Vellamouth like a lid. From the chandlery window Adaeze could see the Perseverance at anchor, her riding lights doubled in the black water, and beyond her the dark shapes of the transports still arriving—one, two, three more since morning, their troop decks glowing faintly with shaded lanterns. Counting boats again. Always counting boats.

Rosalind hadn't come to bed. She found him at the map table past midnight, asleep sitting up, his cheek resting on a half-finished chart, his injured pride and his clever mind both finally surrendered to exhaustion. A candle guttered beside him. On the table, beneath his slack hand, lay not the coastal survey he'd claimed to be working on but something else—a page covered in small, dense figures, columns of numbers she didn't understand, annotated in his cramped hand.

She stood over him a long time, this brother of hers, this impossible, stubborn, brilliant boy who had followed their master's ghost into a war of paper. She thought about carrying him to bed and decided against it; he'd wake stiff and sore and she'd enjoy watching him complain. Instead she pinched out the candle, draped her own shawl over his shoulders, and stood a moment longer in the dark, listening to him breathe.

One berth. The words would not leave her. They circled her mind like gulls over offal. Somewhere in the customs house, a clerk had dipped his pen and written *approved* beside her name and moved on, and that single stroke of ink had decided everything—who lived near her, who she'd eat beside, who she'd bury. Love turned into arithmetic. That was what the Crown did best, she thought. It took the vast, unruly animal of a town—all its quarrels and kindnesses, its weddings and debts and grudges—and reduced it to a column of names, and then it cut the column where it pleased.

Well. Two could play at arithmetic.

She thought of the dockworkers, forty strong, who moved the army's cargo. She thought of the cranes, and the tide tables, and the way a single delayed barge could snarl a whole day's loading. She thought of Mrs. Trelawny's name, absent from the list. Of old Pellow. Of the hundreds who would walk into the marshes come Friday if no one did anything.

Friday. Two days.

Adaeze looked out at the harbor one last time—at the ship that was supposed to be salvation and was instead a knife with sails—and made her decision the way she made all of them: quickly, completely, without looking back.

Tomorrow she would talk to the dock crews. Tomorrow she would find out exactly how slowly forty angry men could work. And tomorrow, somehow, she would stop her brother from doing whatever reckless thing was already taking shape behind those sleeping eyes—because she had read the page beneath his hand, or enough of it, and the numbers there were not surveys.

They were evidence.

Outside, the fog began to come off the water, swallowing the Perseverance light by light, until the harbor was only darkness and the sound of the tide, patient as a debt, waiting to be paid.
