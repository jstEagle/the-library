## 10. The Choice Begins

The notice arrived folded twice and delivered by a boy who would not meet anyone's eyes, and Rosalind read it standing in the doorway of the Hollis house with the fog still on his coat, because his legs decided before his mind did that he did not want to be sitting down for this.

*By order of the commanding officer, Vellamouth Garrison: the undernamed resident is granted passage aboard His Majesty's transport Perseverance, sailing upon loading's completion. One (1) berth. No substitutions. No appeals.*

One name. Adaeze Okonkwo.

He read it three times. The words did not change. He had spent his life among documents that claimed to describe the world, and he had always believed, in the way a man believes the sun will rise, that a document was at worst mistaken but never arbitrary—that somewhere behind every line of ink there stood a reason a person could walk up to and examine. This paper had no reason in it. It had an alphabet. *Okonkwo, A.* came after *Nowell, T.* and before *Okafor—* no Okafor; there was no Okafor in Vellamouth. There was only one Okonkwo, and she worked the docks, and her name sat exactly where the clerk's pen had happened to fall.

Behind him, Margaret put down the teapot she had been warming. He heard her do it very quietly, the way she did everything now, as though the house were full of sleeping people, which in a sense it was—the dead master in every room, the displaced families sleeping inside the atlas's binding, the whole erased history of three towns folded into oilcloth under Rosalind's bed.

"Read it aloud," she said. "Or don't. But your face has already read it."

"One berth," Rosalind said. "Hers."

Margaret closed her eyes briefly, the way one does at a blow one saw coming and could not soften. Then she crossed the room and took the notice from his hands and read it herself, though she did not need to, and set it flat on the table and smoothed it with two fingers as if it were something that could be reasoned with.

"They took the Okafor family out of the ledger entirely," she said. "I checked yesterday, while you were at the fort. The family that lodged with the Pentecosts—six of them, gone, as if they'd never drawn breath in this town." She said it without heat. That was the terrible thing about Margaret these last days; the grief had burned down past all its flames into a kind of white ash that did not flare at anything. "Your sister's name survived by accident of spelling. That's what decides who lives, Rosalind. A clerk's penmanship."

"And yours doesn't appear at all."

"No." She poured the tea she no longer wanted, out of forty years of habit. "Hollis, M. Widows are inconvenient cargo. We take up a berth and give nothing back."

He sat down at last. The chair creaked. Outside, a patrol went by along the quay road, boots keeping their metronome time, and neither of them looked at the window, because looking at windows was beginning to feel like a confession.

"She'll refuse it," Rosalind said.

"She'll refuse it."

"You have to tell her not to."

"I will tell her," Margaret said, setting his cup before him with a small, precise click, "exactly the opposite. And then she and I will have it out, and you will sit there like a stone and let us, because it is between us, and because you already know which of us is right and cannot bear to say it."

* * *

Adaeze came home at dusk smelling of tar and cold iron, and she knew from the way they were sitting—one at each end of the table like disputants, the notice lying between them face-up, deliberately not hidden—that the letter had come.

She did not touch it. She stood in the doorway with her shawl still on, working her hands in the wool, and Rosalind watched her understand it, watch the arithmetic move behind her eyes the way he watched coastlines resolve under a pen. She had counted boats her whole life. She could count anything.

"One berth," she said. Not a question.

"One berth," Margaret said gently. "Yours."

Adaeze pulled out the third chair and sat down hard, and for a moment she covered her face with both hands, dockworker's hands, rope-burned and cracked at the knuckles, and when she lowered them her jaw was set in the particular way that meant she had already fought the battle inside herself and won it, and was about to make it everyone else's problem.

"No," she said.

"Adaeze—" Rosalind began.

"No." She turned on him and her voice cracked like a whip across the harbor. "Don't you *Adaeze* me. You think I don't know what you're going to do? You've got the look. You've had the look since the proclamation—you get quiet and start drawing little lines in the air with your finger, like you're plotting a coastline nobody asked for. You're going to do something with that book."

Rosalind kept his face still. It was a skill he had learned young, being the dreamer between the two of them, the one whose schemes had to be hidden from his sister's practical eye. But Adaeze had watched him his whole life. Stillness told her everything.

"I knew it," she breathed. "The book. Whatever it is Papa Hollis got himself killed over—you're going to use it."

"It's not a thing I'm using," Rosalind said. "It's the truth. There are three towns' worth of families in that marginalia, Adaeze. Families erased from the record so the Crown could take their land and call it sanitation. If the atlas reaches a printer before the ship sails—"

"*If.*" She laughed, and it was an ugly sound, foreign on her. "And while you're playing printer's courier, what am I supposed to do? Get on a ship alone? Go where? With whose money, whose name, sitting in some port town waiting for news that my brother was shot on a wharf for carrying papers?" Her voice broke and she hated it, he could see her hating it. "We don't split. We have never split. When Mother died we didn't split. When they took the survey apprenticeship from you and gave it to a white boy who couldn't find north with a compass in each hand, we didn't split."

"That was different—"

"Everything is different until it happens to us, and then it's the same story again." She pushed back from the table and stood, and stood over him, and for a moment she was not his younger sister but the woman who had carried him through the worst year of both their lives, and her fury was only love wearing armor. "I'm not getting on that ship."

"Then you're a fool," Margaret said quietly.

The word dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Adaeze turned on her—but Margaret, white-haired, ash-calm, did not flinch. She folded her hands on the table beside the notice, and Rosalind realized she had rehearsed this, had composed it the way she had once helped Hollis compose his letters, every sentence weighed.

"Child, listen to me, because I buried a husband and I have earned the right to say plain things. Your brother is going to do what he is going to do. Men like him—men with books in their chests—they go off on their errands and they believe the world will arrange itself around their purpose. Sometimes it even does. But you"—she pointed one finger at Adaeze, and the finger did not shake—"you are the one thing in this world holding him to the earth. If you leave, he has nothing left to lose, and men with nothing to lose die quickly and uselessly. If you stay, he has a reason to come through it alive."

"Then he can have the reason without me being shipped off like freight—"

"No." Margaret's voice rose, just slightly, just enough. "You can be his reason here or his ghost there. Because hear me, girl: if you refuse that berth and stay, and this goes wrong—and it will go wrong, resistance always costs more than its planners promise—then the Okonkwo name ends in Vellamouth. Ends entirely. On my husband's grave there is a stone that says he made maps of places that will now exist only because you survive them. Somebody has to carry the name. Somebody has to be the record. That is what your brother's book is *about*—that someone remembers. So you will remember. From wherever you must."

The fire ticked. Somewhere down the street a door slammed, and all three of them flinched at it together, the new reflex of the occupied town, and the flinch broke something open in the argument's spine.

Adaeze sat back down slowly. She looked at the notice for a long time. Then she looked at her brother, and her voice, when it came, had gone quiet and terrible in a different register—not fury now but grief, which is fury that has accepted its own futility.

"You're not going to stop," she said. "Are you."

"No," Rosalind said. And because it was her, and because lies between them had a half-life of minutes, he said the rest of it too. "There's a copy of the altered ledger pages in my kit. The clerk changed entries—I watched him do it, Whitmore, the man with the lodgers. Names moved up the list for money. Names struck for refusing. The requisition isn't rescue, Adaeze, it's a purchase, and I have the receipts. If I can get the original atlas out of the map room and to Finch—the printer Margaret knows—before loading finishes, then it won't matter who's on the sailing list, because there won't *be* a legal sailing list. There'll be an inquiry. There'll be restitution. All three of us will walk onto that ship—or none of us will need to."

"And if you're caught?"

"Then I'll be caught."

"Don't," Adaeze said, "say that to me like it's small."

"I'm not saying it's small. I'm saying it's the only trade on the table." He reached across and put his hand over hers, rope-burned and cracked, the hand that had fed them both for six years. "You taught me to count boats. Count this one. One ship, one berth, three of us. Every other door is shut except the one made of paper. Let me build it."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she turned her hand over and gripped his, hard enough to hurt, and said to Margaret without looking at her: "I'll take the berth when he's safe on a ship with us. Not before. You want me to be the record? Fine. I'll record whatever they do to him, from close range, with my fists if it comes to it."

Margaret sighed, and it was almost a laugh, and the sound was so strange and welcome in that room that Rosalind felt his chest ache. "Stubborn," she said. "Both of you. He chose you well, Edmund. Wherever you are."

* * *

He did not sleep. He told them he would, and he lay down on his pallet above the map room, and he listened to the house settle, and then he rose and lit the candle behind its darkened shade and unrolled the copies he had made at the fort—Whitmore's altered pages, transcribed line for line, the forged stamps rendered in his best imitation hand, the discrepancies marked in red between what the census recorded and what the boarding list enforced.

He spread them on the drafting table and weighted the corners, and he looked at them the way he had been taught to look at any chart: not at the lines first, but at the errors, because errors were where the truth of a mapmaker's hand showed. Whitmore had been careless. Whitmore had moved the Pentecost lodgers' names into a column labeled *deceased prior to enumeration* in ink that was fresher than the page it sat on. Whitmore had sold berths to four merchants of his congregation for sums Rosalind could trace in the parish poor-box withdrawals. Errors everywhere, and beneath the errors, the pattern: the ledger was not deciding who fled Vellamouth. The ledger was deciding who Vellamouth would *belong to* afterward.

He thought of Elowen Vance correcting his coastlines with that unsettling precision, and the shame in her face she thought she had hidden, and he wondered—not for the first time, and with less and less comfort—whose hand had drawn the orders she served under. He put the thought away. It was a weakness. Later, perhaps, it might be a lever. Everything was potentially a lever; that was the whole of cartography, and he was beginning to suspect it was the whole of justice too.

Because here was the thing he understood now, sitting in the candle-shadow with the evidence of a crime spread out before him like an anatomy lesson: the law was not on their side. He had waited for that realization the way you wait for a tooth to finish aching, and it had finally arrived, clean and complete. The law had sealed the harbor. The law held the ledger. The law had killed Master Hollis on the marsh road and called it a fall from a horse. If justice came to Vellamouth, it would not come through the courts or the garrison or the graceful conscience of any magistrate. It would come the way truth itself traveled: folded small, carried by hand, passed from someone willing to someone willing, at cost, in the dark.

Justice outside the law. He tested the shape of it the way he tested a new projection, turning it, checking its distortions. It frightened him. It also, for the first time since the redcoats came, made the world feel like it had a north again.

He began to plan properly, as he had been trained to plan. The atlas lay under his bed, but the original could not leave the map room until the final plate was finished—it lacked two charts and the index of names, and a half-made weapon was worse than none. Two days of work, perhaps three if his injured concentration lied to him. Loading continued through the week. That gave him his margin, and margins were where he lived.

He would need Finch's press ready. He would need Margaret's marsh routes. He would need Adaeze's dockworkers to slow the army cargo without provoking a massacre—though she had already begun that work, he suspected, whether she admitted it or not; he had seen the way the crates came down the gangplank these last two days, ceremonially, tenderly, at the pace of a funeral.

And he would need luck, or its cousin. He thought again of Captain Vance, and of her hand steadying his elbow on the fort stairs, and of what it would mean—what it would *cost* her—if the daughter of General Vance were ever discovered to have helped a mapmaker's apprentice outrun her father's ledger. He put that thought away too, more carefully than the first, because it was heavier, and because he was afraid of how much he wanted to pick it up.

Toward dawn the candle guttered and he trimmed the wick and drew one last sheet toward him: a blank chart of the approaches to Vellamouth Harbor, and on it, in pencil that could be erased, he plotted not soundings but days. Sailing day here. Finishing of the atlas there. The route to the marsh, the return, the printing, the distribution—each event a mark on water that had never existed and that he would have to make exist by force of hand.

His sister slept in the next room with a refused berth folded in her pocket. His mentor's widow slept knowing she would not sail at all. And somewhere across the sleeping town, a clerk's fresh ink was drying on the lie that would decide all their lives.

Rosalind dipped his pen, touched it to the first empty chart, and began, in a hand indistinguishable from his dead master's, to draw the town as it actually was.

Outside, the tide was turning.
