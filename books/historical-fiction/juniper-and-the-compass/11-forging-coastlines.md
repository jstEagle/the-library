## 11. Forging Coastlines

The summons came on a Tuesday, folded into a square of grey paper and delivered by a boy too young to know what he carried.

*Mr. R. Okonkwo is required at Fort Cragmore on the morning of the fourteenth instant, to render survey assistance to the Office of Works. Bring your own instruments. Duration of engagement: three days.*

Rosalind read it three times by the light of the map-room window, and each reading turned a different key in him. The first was fear — the plain animal kind, cold at the root of the tongue. The second was something he could not yet name, a humming beneath the breastbone, like a struck string still sounding. The third was arithmetic.

Three days inside Fort Cragmore. Three days within reach of the clerk's office where the census ledger lived, the ledger whose altered pages Margaret had described to him with her dead husband's letter spread between them like a diagnosis: *names struck through, names inserted, whole streets re-weighted in the arithmetic of who would sail and who would stay.* He had copied nothing yet. He had only turned the knowledge over in his hands at night, the way he'd once turned Hollis's brass sextant, feeling its weight without knowing how to use it.

Now the army itself was handing him the door.

He told Adaeze only that he had survey work. She was mending a net in the doorway, her fingers quick and unlooking, and she gave him the long stare she had inherited from their mother — the one that weighed a man's words against his face and found the difference.

"Fort work," she said. "For them."

"For pay," Rosalind said. "Which we need."

"We need a great many things." She bit the thread with her teeth. "Be careful which of them you go looking for."

He almost told her then. The words were there, assembled and waiting — *Margaret found a letter, Hollis was murdered for names, I mean to copy the ledger* — and he felt the terrible pull of confession, the longing to set the weight down even for a moment in front of someone strong enough to carry it. But Adaeze's name was one of the names in that book. Telling her would not lighten anything; it would only put fear into the house with her, fear that walked beside her down to the docks and up again, and he could not do that. Not yet. Perhaps the surest mark of a coward, he thought later, is how gracefully he renames his silences as protections.

He kissed her forehead, took his instrument case, and left before his face could betray him.

* * *

Fort Cragmore sat above the town like a held breath, its grey walls catching the morning fog that came off the harbor in slow ropes. A sentry checked his papers twice, then a sergeant's clerk checked them again, and Rosalind understood that the paper of his life had been divided into copies, and some copyist somewhere was deciding which version of him was true.

The Office of Works had requisitioned a long room on the fort's landward side, and here Rosalind found the machinery of the eviction laid out with the tidy horror of a well-run shop. Tables of documents. Stacks of requisition forms in numbered bundles. And along one wall, a shelf of ledgers — tall ones, fat ones, their spines labelled in a clerk's careful hand: *Vellamouth — Census & Assessment — Current.*

*Current.* As though there were another. As though the town had versions, like a chart corrected from the Notice to Mariners.

The man in charge of him was a Captain of Engineers named Prosser, red-faced and harried, who cared about one thing in the world and that thing was the fort's drainage. "The old surveys are useless," Prosser said, thumping a roll of vellum. "Contours wrong, culverts misplotted, half the interior elevations invented by some draughtsman who clearly never stood on the ground. You're Hollis's man, they tell me. Hollis knew this coast better than God. Show me you learned something."

"I learned never to invent an elevation," Rosalind said, "and always to distrust the man who did."

Prosser barked something that was nearly a laugh and put him to work.

And so, absurdly, terribly, Rosalind spent his first day at Fort Cragmore doing honest work. He measured. He levelled. He plotted the fall of the ground along the eastern ditch until his hands ached in the cold and the numbers came clean and true, because his master had beaten falseness out of him years ago and it could not be put back in. All day the ledger sat on its shelf across the room, twenty feet away, radiating its quiet gravitational pull. Twice a clerk took it down, made an entry, returned it. Once, in the afternoon, a soldier Rosalind did not know came in — broad, deliberate, with the unhurried manner of a man who owns every room he enters — and spoke at length with Prosser near the window.

Sergeant Bray. Margaret had said the name once, flatly, the way you name a weather that killed someone. *He came to the house before Hollis died. He asked for the atlas. Hollis said there was no atlas. Eleven days later Hollis was under the churchyard stones.*

Rosalind bent over his levelling book and made figures. He did not look up again until Bray's boots had crossed the room and gone.

That night he lay in the billet assigned to civilian contractors and listened to the fort settle around him — timbers ticking, a sentry's slow tread overhead — and rehearsed the thing he had decided to do the way an actor rehearses, stripping away every hesitation, every wasted motion, until what remained was simple enough to survive fear.

Tomorrow Prosser wanted interior elevations for the old powder magazine, whose plans were stored, Prosser complained, "in the clerk's office, if that fool Merrick can find them." The clerk's office shared a wall with the records room. The records room held the shelf. The shelf held the ledger.

A mapmaker's whole training, Rosalind thought in the dark, is the art of getting close to a thing without appearing to move toward it.

* * *

The second morning broke low and colourless, the sea and sky the same wet pewter. Rosalind reported to Prosser at eight, instruments cleaned, and asked with a craftsman's mildness whether he might see the magazine plans before beginning, since working from the old contours blind would waste half a day.

"Merrick!" Prosser roared, and the machinery obliged. Merrick, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the defensive posture of someone perpetually blamed, fetched Rosalind through a doorway, down a short corridor, into the records room — and left him alone there for exactly as long as it took to shout back to Prosser that the 1811 file was mislabelled.

Eleven days after Hollis had said *there is no atlas*, he was dead. Rosalind had eleven seconds.

He had planned it in the dark with a cartographer's precision, and now the plan executed itself while his heart slammed like a fist on a locked door. The ledger lay third from the end of the shelf. He drew it, opened it flat against his forearm in the way he opened any reference — a draughtsman's habit, invisible as breathing — and turned to the Vellamouth sailing list. His eyes had been trained since childhood to take an impression whole: coastlines, soundings, the shape of a thing in a glance.

But this was worse than a coastline, because the alterations were themselves disguised. He saw it immediately with a professional's sick admiration: the erasures were beautiful. Where a name had been struck, the replacement entry flowed in the same hand, the same ink weight, the same slant. Only under the raking light from the high window did the ghosts show — faint depressions in the paper where a pen had pressed hard before scraping, the faintest halo of former ink bleeding through the sheet from the page behind. Okonkwo, Adaeze — present, unaltered, entered in a fair round hand. Okonkwo, Rosalind — scraped. The depression of the letters still lived in the paper like a footprint in snow. Hollis, Margaret — scraped. And beside certain other names, small marks in the margin: not the clerk's hand. A different hand entirely. A code of ticks and crosses, cool and administrative, deciding.

He could not memorize this. It was too fine, too deniable. He needed it in ink.

His satchel hung at his hip with his instruments. Among the pencils and the scale rules, wrapped in oiled paper against damp, lay a small notebook — and, folded flat inside its cover, three leaves of thin tracing paper he had prepared two nights ago, cut to the ledger's page dimensions. Cartographers traced; it was the most innocent act in the profession. If caught with tracings of a census, he was a thief of paper. If caught with the memory alone, he was a liar, and liars could be dismissed.

His hand shook so badly the first line came out wavering. He stopped. Breathed as Hollis had taught him to breathe over a difficult plate — *the hand follows the breath, boy; a panicked breath makes a panicked line* — and began again. Name. Street. Assessment. Marginal mark. Name. Street. Scraped ghost noted in his own shorthand: *alt., diff. hand, post orig. binding.* He worked down the column with his eyes flicking between ledger and tracing paper at intervals so brief they barely registered as movement, the way he had once copied coastlines from charts he was not permitted to remove, in another life, in an office where the worst crime possible was inaccuracy.

The door handle turned.

There was no time to be clever. He let the ledger begin to close with his tracing leaf still half upon it and stepped back toward the plan chest, lifting his free hand toward a drawer as though reaching — and the door swung open on Sergeant Bray.

The room went very small. Bray filled most of it, or seemed to, standing with the patient stillness Rosalind had noted yesterday, his eyes going first to Rosalind's face, then — slowly, deliberately, the way a man reads a document — to the ledger, still settling closed on the table, a corner of translucent paper just visible beneath its cover like the edge of a tongue.

"Okonkwo," Bray said. He knew the name. That was the first thing Rosalind understood, with a coldness that started in his scalp: the man had come looking for him specifically, and this was not luck but design. "The engineer's apprentice."

"Apprentice mapmaker," Rosalind heard himself say. "Late of Master Hollis."

"Yes." Bray crossed the room without hurry. "I knew Hollis."

The ledger lay between them. Bray looked at it the way a customs officer looks at luggage — not searching, merely permitting the searched to feel the weight of being seen. Rosalind's mind raced through explanations and found every one of them flimsy: he had been replacing the volume, he had been consulting it, he had been — and underneath all of it the tracing leaf showed its pale edge, and Rosalind calculated, with strange detachment, exactly how many steps it was to the high window and what a drop to the ditch would cost him.

"The magazine plans," Rosalind said. "Merrick went to find the 1811 file. I was just—" and he gestured at the ledger with a small apologetic motion, a workman's shrug, "—checking assessments against the old footprints. The foundations have shifted. It affects the elevations."

It was a good lie. It was even mostly true. Bray considered it with the expression of a man tasting soup for something that should not be in it.

"Foundations," he repeated.

"Everything here is built on fill," Rosalind said, warming despite his terror, because craft, once engaged, pulls the craftsman after it. "The east range especially. Hollis always said the fort was drawn by optimists."

Something moved at the back of Bray's eyes — not belief, but the absence of immediate grounds. His gaze dropped to the ledger again, and he extended one hand toward it, and Rosalind felt his whole life contract to the space of that hand's travel—

"Sergeant."

The voice came from the doorway, clear and unhurried. Elowen Vance stood there in her captain's coat, a rolled survey under her arm, taking in the room with a single sweep that rested on the ledger, the tracing leaf's pale edge, and Rosalind's face for precisely equal moments.

"Captain." Bray straightened.

"My father wants the harbour soundings finished before the tide turns," she said. "He's asked particularly for the Hollis man. Mr. Okonkwo." Her tone did not change at all. "You're wanted at the water gate. Now."

Bray's jaw tightened fractionally. "I'm conducting an inspection of the records—"

"And the records will still be here when the tide isn't," Elowen said. "The soundings won't. Captain Prosser can mind his own records; he minds everything else loudly enough."

It should not have worked. Bray outranked no one in the room but he represented, in some invisible grammar, an authority that did not answer to captains' daughters — and yet he hesitated, weighed something, and inclined his head. Perhaps he could not afford to make an enemy of the general's household over a clerk's ledger. Perhaps, Rosalind thought much later, Bray already suspected more than he could prove and preferred to hunt in silence. Whatever calculation he made, he made it visibly and swallowed it.

"Finish your inspection another time," Elowen said pleasantly, to Rosalind, meaning the opposite, and held the door.

He gathered his instruments with hands that performed steadiness while everything beneath performed catastrophe. Closing the ledger. Squaring it on the table. The tracing leaf — God, the leaf — he slid it into the plan drawer among the 1811 files in one motion as he passed, trusting later, trusting nerve, and walked out past Bray close enough to smell the tar on the man's coat.

Elowen walked ahead of him down the corridor, boots ringing on stone, and did not look back until they were through the inner arch and into the grey morning by the water gate, where the fog stood thick on the harbour and the sentries were distant smudges.

She stopped. Turned. For a moment the captain's composure slipped sideways, like a chart slightly off its true bearing, and what showed underneath was younger and far more frightened.

"I don't know what you think you were doing," she said quietly.

"No," Rosalind said. "I don't expect you do."

"Don't." The word came out sharp. "Don't be clever with me. If Sergeant Bray decides to open that ledger and think backward instead of forward, they will hang you from the north wall and my father will call it sanitation." She pressed the rolled soundings against her chest like a shield. "Whatever it is — whatever Hollis left you — it is not worth the north wall."

"You knew him," Rosalind said. "Hollis."

Something crossed her face — grief, guilt, the specific sorrow of someone who watched a thing happen from the winning side. "I dined at his house twice," she said. "He corrected my contour work when I was sixteen and told me a map that flatters power is not a map but a portrait." She looked out at the fog where the evacuation ship lay at anchor, a grey suggestion of itself. "Go and take your soundings, Mr. Okonkwo. Be exact. Be boring. Give Bray nothing to taste."

"Why?" The question tore out of him before caution could catch it. "Why cover for me? You owe me nothing. Your father's signature is on all of this."

She was silent long enough that the fog seemed to press in to hear. When she answered, her voice was very low, and very steady, and it frightened him more than Bray had.

"Because somebody in that fort has to stop being a portrait," she said, "and it appears it must be me. Go."

She walked away toward the water, and Rosalind stood at the gate with his heart hammering out a rhythm he did not yet recognize as the beginning of something, and retrieved the tracing leaf from the plan drawer on his way past, and survived the rest of that day by taking the most meticulous soundings of his life.

* * *

He did the second and third pages the next afternoon, when Merrick's chronic disorganization sent him back to the records room for the drainage files, and Bray was conspicuously elsewhere — which frightened Rosalind more than Bray's presence would have, because a hunter who lets you see him is hunting one way, and a hunter who vanishes is hunting another. He worked faster this time. Terror had burned down to something harder and stranger, an exhilaration like standing on a height above a vast drop, wind pulling at him: every name his pen lifted off that ledger and set down in his own shorthand was a person restored to existence, the Okonkwos of three towns, the scraped and ghosted families, Hollis's dead congregation gathering again stroke by stroke. He had spent his whole life believing history happened elsewhere, to other people, in capitals, in wars he drew borders around afterward. Now history sat under his hand and he was forging counter-coastlines against it, and his breath was steady, and his lines were true.

By lamplight that night, in his billet, he compared the tracings against Margaret's list from the atlas binding. They matched. They matched perfectly — the same hand behind every alteration, the same marginal code, extending back through three towns and eleven years. It was not chaos. It was policy, written in a clerk's beautiful script.

He sealed the leaves inside the false bottom of his instrument case, beneath the plumb lines, and lay listening to the fort breathe, and understood that there was no road back from tonight. He had crossed from a man who knew into a man who acted, and the state, which forgave knowing, never forgave acting.

In the morning, Prosser signed his chit and grumbled about the drainage, and Rosalind walked out of Fort Cragmore's gate with his instruments on his shoulder and three pages of stolen truth against his hip, past the sentry, past the fog, down the hill into Vellamouth — where, at the corner by the shuttered fish market, a woman he did not know fell into step beside him and said, without looking at him:

"Mr. Okonkwo. Don't go home. Sergeant Bray searched your map room an hour ago, and he's posted a man across the lane."

The fog closed behind them both.
