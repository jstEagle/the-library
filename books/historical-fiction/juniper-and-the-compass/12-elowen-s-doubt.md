## 12. Elowen's Doubt

The general's quarters occupied the top floor of the old customs house, and Elowen had climbed its stairs a thousand times without once feeling the way she felt tonight—as though each step were taking her further from solid ground.

Below her, through the tall windows of the stairwell, Vellamouth lay under lamplight and curfew. From this height the town looked almost gentle: fishing boats knocked together at their moorings like sleeping animals, smoke rising thin and domestic from chimneys that should have held suppers behind them, streets empty in the way streets are empty when everyone in them has been ordered indoors by men with bayonets. She could see the quay. She could see the great ship riding at anchor beyond the harbor chain, her lanterns strung along the yards like a festival that refused to admit it was a funeral.

*The *Resolution*. One ship. Four hundred names on a sailing list out of two thousand souls.

Elowen had spent the day correcting survey maps in the fort's drafting room, and all afternoon the numbers had been speaking to her in a voice she could not silence. Fever maps were supposed to show clusters—contagion gathered at a source, spreading outward in rings. She had drawn enough of them during the typhus year at Portsmouth to know their grammar. But the sanitary report General Vance had signed and forwarded to London described an outbreak that obeyed no such logic. Its rings were drawn where the *best land* was. Where the deepwater berths were. Where a naval depot would want its coaling wharf.

Disease did not read property ledgers. Yet somehow, in Vellamouth, it seemed to have learned to.

She reached the top of the stairs. The sentry outside her father's door saluted; she returned it and knocked twice, the family rhythm, the one he'd taught her when she was seven and he was only a colonel and the empire was still a map with soft edges.

"Come."

General Sir Alistair Vance sat at his desk with the lamp turned low, reading dispatches. He did not look up when she entered. He never did, at first; it was a habit of command, letting subordinates—and daughters—arrange themselves in the silence. Elowen had learned to use those silences for her own purposes. Tonight she could not find a purpose large enough to fill one.

"You wanted the fort surveys finished," she said. "They're done."

"Mm." He turned a page. "And?"

"And they're accurate." She set the folio on the corner of his desk. "Which is more than I can say for the sanitary report."

The page-turning stopped.

Her father looked up then, and Elowen saw the thing she had been bracing against all day and hoping all day not to see: not surprise. He had known, the moment she opened her mouth, exactly which door she had come to knock upon. Perhaps he had known for weeks. Generals, she thought, are men who win by knowing what is coming before anyone else does, and her father had been winning for thirty years.

"Close the door, Elowen."

She closed it. The latch sounded very loud.

"Sit down."

"I'd rather stand."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the memory of one, filed away with other memories of a girl who had once refused to sit through a whole Sunday sermon on the grounds that God could hear her just as well standing. He had found it charming then. She watched him decide whether to find it charming now.

"As you like." He set down the dispatch and folded his hands atop it. "Tell me precisely what you mean by *inaccurate*."

"I mean there is no fever." The words came out more steadily than she deserved; some part of her had rehearsed them all day, marching up and down the drafting room's length while the pens dried in their pots. "I've read your report. I've compared it against the burial returns from the parish clerk—I copied them myself, last Tuesday, when I took the census abstracts. Eleven burials this quarter. Two infants, one aged woman of eighty-six, a drowned man off the *Providence*, and seven others of causes any parish in England would call ordinary. There is no epidemic in Vellamouth. There is no *fever*, Father. There is a word written on a government form, and it is doing the work of a hundred soldiers who would have died taking this town by force."

The lamp flame swayed in some small current of air. Her father regarded her across it, and his face gave her nothing—nothing but attention, which was worse than anger, because anger would at least have told her the argument was still alive between them.

"The requisition order," he said at last, quietly, "is signed by the Home Secretary."

"I know."

"It cites sanitary necessity under the authority granted after the Liverpool outbreaks. You will find the precedent sound. You will find, if you look into it, that the Liverpool outbreaks themselves were handled the same way, and no one in Liverpool is asking questions now either."

"That is not an answer to what I said."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. It is something better than an answer. It is the law."

He rose, then, unhurried, and crossed to the tall cabinet against the far wall—the one she had never seen him open, the one that held, she had always assumed, brandy and old regimental things. Instead he drew from it a leather dispatch case, military-stamped, sealed with wax she recognized as coming from above even her father. He laid it on the desk between them like a man laying down a winning card, and for a moment Elowen understood that this was deliberate—that everything he did in the next five minutes would be deliberate, chosen, arranged for effect, because her father believed that truth, properly deployed, ended arguments faster than lies ever could.

"Read it," he said.

She broke the seal. The paper inside crackled with officialdom—foolscap, watermarked, initialed at every margin in hands she did not know. Orders from the Admiralty. From the Board of Ordnance. From a committee whose name alone ran three lines long. She read, and as she read, the floor of the world tilted beneath her feet in a way no sea voyage had ever managed.

*That the harbor of Vellamouth, by reason of its depth, defensibility, and position relative to the northern convoy routes, be developed as a station for steam flotilla, with works to commence immediately upon clearance of the present settlement.*

*Clearance of the present settlement.*

Not *return of*. Not *temporary relocation pending abatement of pestilence*. Clearance. The town was not being saved from anything. It was being *cleared*, the way a field is cleared of stones before the plowing. The word appeared four times in three pages, each time with the same flat administrative calm, and each time Elowen's eye snagged on it as though on a nail.

"There's a schedule," she said. Her own voice sounded far away to her. "Here. Demolition phases. Beginning—" She stopped. Counted. Began again. "Beginning six weeks from now. Whether or not anyone sails."

"Whether or not anyone sails," her father repeated. "Yes. That is why the ship matters less than you appear to think it matters, Elowen. The ship is theater. It gives the operation a merciful shape. People endure almost anything if you can make it look like rescue."

She put the papers down. Her hands were steady, she noticed distantly, when what she felt was not steadiness at all but a kind of weightlessness, as though she had stepped off the quay in the dark and was waiting to learn how deep the water was.

"Does Mother know?"

"Your mother knows what is good for her to know."

"And me?" She looked up at him. "What is good for me to know? Because you've just shown me, Father. You didn't have to. You *chose* to. Which means you believe I'll swallow it. Which means—" and here something cold moved through her chest, precise as a probe, "—which means you've already decided what I am."

Her father came around the desk slowly. Up close, in the low light, he looked suddenly older than his fifty-eight years—the campaign lines around his eyes cut deeper, the silver at his temples less distinguished and more like weathering. She loved him. That was the unbearable part. She loved him the way she loved the army itself, with her whole history, with every year of her life folded into it, and she could feel that love being used against her now like a lever against a stuck door, and there was nothing—*nothing*—she could do to stop wanting it to work.

"Sit down, Elowen," he said gently. "Please."

This time she sat.

"For twenty years," he said, standing over her, not looming—he was too good a soldier to loom—but filling the room the way a wall fills a doorway, "I have watched idealistic officers destroy themselves. I buried two of them. Men who mistook their consciences for the world. They wrote letters. They testified. They *felt*, magnificently, and the machinery rolled over them and kept rolling, and the only difference their feeling made was to break their wives and empty their children's nurseries." He paused. "You are my daughter. You are also the finest survey officer I have ever commanded, and I include men with twenty years' seniority. Do you imagine that combination escapes notice in London? Do you imagine there aren't men at the Ordnance Board who already speak of you? Your name is *known*, girl. It can be known in one of two ways."

"As what?" she asked. Her voice had gone quiet. "Known as what?"

"As the officer who rose." He let that sit a moment. "Or as the colonel's daughter who went strange at Vellamouth and had to be invalided home. Those are the shapes available to you. There is no third shape. There is no shape in which Captain Vance writes a letter of protest and the Empire says *oh, forgive us, how right you were* and puts the town back." He crouched then, so that his eyes were level with hers, and this—this was the oldest weapon in his arsenal, older than rank, older than the dispatch case: simply her father, looking at her, the man who had taught her to ride and to read contour lines and to shoot at tins off a fence post. "You asked me once, when you were nine, why we fought for a country that had never done anything for us. Do you remember what I told you?"

Elowen remembered. She had been asking about a dead soldier's funeral, one of her father's lieutenants, buried in rain in some colonial churchyard. *Because someone must hold the line,* he had said, *or the line holds no one.*

"Loyalty is the only mercy you'll be permitted," her father said now, softly. "Understand me. I do not say it is the only mercy that exists. I say it is the only one you will be *permitted*. The world is run by men who must sometimes do ugly arithmetic, and the single kindness they extend to those beneath them is certainty. Certainty about what is required. The moment you start auditing the requirement, you lose the certainty, and no one will give it back to you. Not London. Not me. Not God Himself, though many broken men have claimed otherwise." He straightened. "These people will be moved. It is decided at levels neither of us can reach, and it will happen whether you grieve it or not. The only question left in the whole affair is where *you* stand when it does."

*And where will you stand* — she almost asked it — *when they demolish the church where Mother was married? When the stones go into the harbor mole and the fishermen's names go into nobody's ledger at all?*

She did not ask. Some final instinct of self-preservation—or perhaps only cowardice wearing that instinct's coat—kept her silent.

"What do you want from me?" she said instead.

"Nothing you are not already doing. Finish the surveys. Correct the charts. Be exact, because exactness serves the work and the work is happening regardless." He returned to his chair and took up his dispatches again, and just like that the audience was over; she had been dismissed in everything but words. At the door, though, he spoke once more, without looking up, and the gentleness in his voice was the cruelest thing in the whole conversation.

"You saw the mapmaker today. Okonkwo's apprentice."

Elowen kept her face still. "I inspected his work. It's excellent."

"It is more than excellent. Hollis trained him, and Hollis was—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—a difficult man, toward the end. Asking questions. Collecting things. If the boy inherited anything of that, you will tell me. Not because I distrust you, Elowen. Because I am trying to keep you safe, which is the last mercy a father is permitted to give."

* * *

She walked back across the fort yard under a sky the color of wet slate, and the stars were out above the harbor, indifferent and beautiful, and she hated them a little for it.

The sentries at the gate touched their caps. Somewhere beyond the wall, muffled by stone and distance, a dog was barking in the empty streets, and she thought: *even the dogs know something is wrong.* She thought of the sailing list pinned up at the customs house that morning, four hundred names in careful copperplate, and how a woman had stood before it with her lips moving, searching for her own surname among all that beautiful handwriting the way a drowning woman searches for a rope.

In her own narrow quarters she lit no lamp. She sat on the edge of her cot in the dark with her father's orders still unfolding in her mind like a chart of some coast she'd never wanted to see, and she performed the exercise she had been taught at sixteen, the one they called *the long view*: remove yourself, consider the whole board, weigh forces, accept what cannot be changed.

It failed her completely. Every time she lifted above the board, she saw only faces. The clerk with ink-stained fingers altering entries while neighbors watched. Old Mrs. Prynne from the chandlery, who had sold her mother candles for twenty years, now queuing for ration bread in the rain. And under all of it, quiet as groundwater, the memory of Rosalind Okonkwo's hand on the survey table that afternoon—how he had flinched when she corrected his coastline, not from wounded pride but from something quicker and more frightened, something that had made her wonder afterward what else lived in him besides penmanship.

Her father thought loyalty was mercy. He was wrong, and she knew he was wrong, and knowing it changed nothing yet, and that was the terrible discovery of the night: that conscience could arrive fully formed, articulate, certain of itself, and still be entirely powerless—just a small lighted window in a fortress, visible from the street, shuttered from within.

Except.

Except that windows could be unshuttered. And except that her father, in his wisdom, in his terrible confidence in the sufficiency of obedience, had shown her the actual orders. Had placed the Admiralty's own seal in her hands. Had assumed, because she was his daughter and a Vance and a soldier, that the paper would close the question instead of opening it.

He had given her the map of his own position. Every fortification. Every assumption. Every route by which the thing might be flanked.

Elowen lay back on the cot and stared at the dark ceiling, and her heart was beating hard and slow, like footsteps descending stairs.

Tomorrow she was ordered to supervise the second harbor survey. Tomorrow the apprentice mapmaker would be at the fort again, working within arm's reach of the restricted files, and Sergeant Bray would be watching him, and she would be expected to watch them both.

She turned onto her side, facing the wall, and in the darkness her lips moved around a sentence she did not permit herself to finish—a sentence that began *if he asks me* and ended somewhere she could not yet see.

Outside, the tide was turning in the harbor, running strong against the anchored fleet.
