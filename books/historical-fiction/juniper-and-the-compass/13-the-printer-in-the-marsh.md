## 13. The Printer in the Marsh

The marsh began where the census ended, which Rosalind thought was perhaps the only honest border left in Vellamouth.

Margaret had dressed for the journey as though visiting a grave—dark wool, plain bonnet, her husband's old oilcloth cape hanging off her shoulders like a memory that no longer fit. She carried a covered lantern though the sun was well up, grey and thin behind cloud, and she walked with a certainty that surprised him. He had always known Margaret Hollis as a woman of rooms: the parlor, the sickroom, the kitchen where she brewed willow bark tea and spoke of her husband in the past tense only when she could bear it. He had not known her as a woman of ground.

"Keep to the peat," she said, not turning. "The reeds will take your boots if you let them. And mind the third channel—it looks shallow. It isn't."

"How do you know this place?"

"I know it because Ezra walked it every month for six years and told me he was going fishing." She said it flatly, without bitterness, but the word *fishing* landed between them like a stone dropped down a well, falling a long time before it made its sound.

Rosalind followed her along a causeway of packed earth no wider than a coffin. To their left the marsh opened into pools the color of old tea, stitched with reed beds that hissed when the wind moved through them. To their right, far off and low, the sea lay flat as hammered pewter, and above it—so distant he could pretend they were gulls—the masts of the troop transports stood in the harbor at Vellamouth, thin black strokes on a pale page.

He found himself counting them out of habit now. Adaeze's habit, caught like a fever. Nine. Yesterday there had been eleven; two had put back out to the anchorage, and he did not know what that meant, and not knowing had begun to sit in his chest like swallowed lead.

Three days since the proclamation. Three days since the ledger had become the most important document on the coast, since names had turned into coin and neighbors into rivals at the clerk's window. Three days since Margaret had come to his lodgings with wet eyes and a dry voice and said, *There is a man you must meet, if you are the apprentice I believe you are.*

He had asked who. She had only said, "A printer."

"A printer," he'd repeated stupidly.

"The Crown calls him a seditious libeller. I call him the only man in three counties who ever printed what Ezra wrote." She had fastened her cloak then, hands steady. "His name is Tobias Finch. And if your atlas is what I think it is, boy, then he has been waiting for you without knowing it."

* * *

They walked for two hours. The causeway gave way to tussocks, the tussocks to flats where their boots pressed prints that filled with water behind them, erasing their passage—which Rosalind understood, after a while, was rather the point. This was a road that kept no record. In a week when every other movement in Vellamouth was being written down—rationed, stamped, entered into columns—he found something almost scandalous in walking a path that refused to remember them.

Margaret, apparently, felt no such romance. "Ezra said the marsh was the best mapmaker he ever knew," she remarked, stepping carefully around a pool fringed with black mud. "It redraws itself every tide. No survey survives it. The admiralty tried three times." Her mouth did something that was nearly a smile. "He liked that very much."

"He would."

"He liked you too. He said you drew coastlines the way other men say prayers." She paused on a tussock, catching her breath, and looked at him directly for the first time in an hour. "I need to ask you something before we arrive, and I need you to answer honestly, because Finch will ask it harder and he won't be polite about it."

"Yes?"

"Why are you doing this? Not why is it right—I've had enough of men telling me why things are right while they do nothing. Why are *you* doing it? What does Rosalind Okonkwo stand to lose?"

He considered lying, or at least embellishing. He thought about telling her it was for justice, for the displaced families whose names crowded the atlas's margins in Hollis's cramped hand, for the principle that maps should tell the truth. All true. None of it the whole truth.

"My sister's name isn't on the sailing list," he said. "Only Adaeze's. One Okonkwo. She won't go without me and I can't make her, and so we will both stay, and Margaret—" his voice caught and he hated it, mastered it, went on—"Margaret, if the ship sails and we're not on it, and the town is never returned, then we will simply cease. Not die. Cease. Struck from the record like one of your husband's erased towns. And I cannot bear that. I have spent my whole life believing that history happened elsewhere, to other people, and now it has happened to me and I find I am not ready to be unwritten."

Margaret listened with her chin slightly raised, the way she used to listen to Hollis read aloud in the evenings. When he finished she was quiet a moment.

"Good," she said. "That's an honest reason. Righteous reasons bend under weight. Spite and love hold their shape." She resumed walking. "Finch will trust spite and love. He's been burned by righteousness before."

* * *

They found him in the ruin of a tide mill, three miles from anywhere, in a hollow of the marsh where the channels wove close enough to make approach by horse impossible. The mill's wheel had rotted to a skeleton; the roof had half fallen, and in the half that remained, impossibly, light glowed.

Not candlelight. Lamplight—steady, working light—and beneath it the smell that stopped Rosalind mid-stride and made his chest ache with sudden homesickness for the map room: ink. Oil-based, sharp, with iron underneath. The smell of press-work. The smell of his apprenticeship, of Hollis alive and humming over the copperplates.

"Hold here," Margaret said, and advanced alone to the doorway, and spoke a phrase too low for Rosalind to catch. From within, a voice answered—a voice roughened by use and suspicion—and then: "Hollis's widow brings a stranger. That's either the bravest or the stupidest thing to happen to me all year. Show him in, then, before the marsh decides for us."

Tobias Finch was perhaps fifty-five, built like the press he worked—broad, squat, immovable—with forearms stained to the elbow in a permanent dusk of ink and hair gone white at the temples in two hard wings. His shirt sleeves were rolled, his collar open, his eyes quick and unimpressed. The room behind him was a miracle of stubbornness: two presses, one large and one small, cases of type gleaming dully, paper wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, and everywhere, stacked and bundled and hanging from beams, printed matter. Pamphlets. Ballad sheets. Sermons. Sedition, depending on who was counting.

"You'll be the apprentice," Finch said, looking Rosalind over the way a horse trader looks at a questionable animal. "Okonkwo. Hollis wrote to me about you. Said you had a fine hand and a dangerous habit of finishing what you start." He jerked his chin at Margaret. "Sit her down, there's a chair that doesn't wobble somewhere. Now. Show me."

Rosalind blinked. "Show you—"

"Don't play the fool, boy, it doesn't suit you. Widow Hollis sent word you've been carrying on the master's work. The atlas. The marginalia. The names." Something changed in Finch's face—not softening exactly, but focusing, like a lens brought to bear. "Three towns, boy. Dunmarrow, Kelthwaite, and now this one. I printed what Hollis sent me about Dunmarrow seven years ago and it cost me my shop, my license, and very nearly my neck. They burned my stock in the marketplace and made me watch. So before I risk the rest of it, you'll show me what you have. Not promises. Pages."

"I don't have pages." Rosalind held up his empty hands. "I have three years of memory and a copy in progress. The atlas itself sits in the map room in Fort Garrison, behind a seal Sergeant Bray himself affixed, in a building patrolled day and night, inside a town where every soul is being counted twice a day."

The silence that followed was long and full of marsh sounds—water dripping, reeds ticking, the far complaint of a curlew.

"Well," Finch said at last, and sat down heavily on a stool beside the small press. "That's a problem."

"That's what I said," said Margaret mildly, from the wobbling chair.

"It's worse than a problem, it's a farce. You want me to print evidence I cannot see, from an original I cannot touch, and you want it done before a ship sails carrying the witnesses away forever. When does she sail?" 

"No one knows. Loading was to begin this week. It may be days. It may be tomorrow."

"Then you're asking me to set type blind." Finch rubbed his jaw with an ink-dark hand. "And if you're caught retrieving the thing—which you will be, unless you're cleverer than Bray, and I've heard about Bray—you'll be hanged, and the widow here transported, and this press broken up for kindling, and the names in those margins will stay in the margins until everyone who remembers them is dead. Do you understand the arithmetic? I'm not refusing. I'm showing you the sum."

"I understand the sum," Rosalind said quietly.

"Do you?" Finch leaned forward. "Because Hollis understood it, and look where understanding got him. Face-down in his own workshop with his skull staved in, and the inquest calling it a fall." His voice cracked on the last word and he covered it with a cough. "I loved that stubborn old man, boy, in the way you love a man who keeps sending you truths that get you ruined. So I'll ask you once more, and then I'll decide. If I print it—if I set every name in lead and run a thousand sheets and scatter them across three counties—what happens? Speak it plainly. Not the dream. The consequence."

Rosalind thought of Hollis's letter hidden in the atlas binding, the lists of families, the careful notation of dates and decrees and the quiet pattern underneath: each displacement preceding, by months, a land transfer to men whose names appeared on shipping manifests and syndicate charters. He thought of Adaeze counting boats. Of Elowen Vance correcting his coastlines with shame in her eyes. Of the clerk's pen altering entries while the queue watched and pretended not to.

"What happens," Rosalind said, "is that the lie stops being load-bearing. A requisition needs silence the way a bridge needs stone. Take enough stones out—enough printed names, enough sworn testimony, enough ordinary people saying *I remember that family, they had a orchard, they had a cow*—and the whole structure shows what it is. They can hang me. They cannot hang a thousand pamphlets. Some of them will survive. Some will be read aloud in chapels and taverns and eventually in Parliament, and once it is spoken in that room, it cannot be unspoken. That is the consequence. It is slow, and it may cost everything I have, and I accept the price because the alternative is to live erased in a town that no longer exists."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere outside, a channel shifted with the turning tide, a low rush of water rearranging the world.

Finch looked at him for a long moment, then at Margaret, who said nothing at all—only folded her hands in her lap with the terrible patience of a woman who had already buried the last man who made this argument.

"Lord," Finch muttered. "You even argue like him." He stood, went to a locked cabinet, and came back with a bottle of apple spirit and three mismatched cups. He poured. "Here's my terms, and none of them are negotiable. First: I don't print from copies. Copies can be disputed; originals hang men. You get me the atlas itself—not tracings, not excerpts, the book—or there's nothing to discuss. Second: I set the names first. The marginalia before the plates. If we're raided midway, the names go out in cheap octavo and the pretty maps can burn. Third—" he raised his cup, and his eyes were suddenly very tired and very fierce—"you don't come back here. Not once. Not to check progress, not because you're frightened, not because you want to help. Once you've delivered it, this place doesn't exist for you, and if they take me, I never saw your face. Those are the terms of every man who's ever done this work, and they keep more people alive than courage does."

Rosalind took the cup. His hand was steadier than he expected. "Agreed."

"To Hollis, then." Finch drank. "Who was the finest surveyor I ever corresponded with and the worst correspondent about danger a man could be cursed with." He set the cup down and was immediately practical again, leaning over the small press, pulling a drawer of type toward him, testing a composing stick's balance as if the conversation had been merely prologue to the real business of his life. "Now. The retrieval. Walk me through the map room. Doors, windows, patrol times, seal placement. Every detail. You're a mapmaker—make me a map of the problem."

So Rosalind did. He talked with his hands at first, then found himself crouching, sketching on the back of a spoiled proof sheet with a pencil Finch shoved at him: the fort's inner court, the archive wing, the two entrances to the map room, the window barred but with rot in the lower casement that he himself had noticed and reported, uselessly, in his apprentice days. The patrol rotation—he had timed it without meaning to, the way he timed everything now; Bray's men passed the archive door at intervals of nineteen minutes, sloppier after the changeover at midnight. The seal on the door was wax and ribbon, ceremonial more than physical; the lock was the real obstacle, but the lock was old, and Hollis had taught him locks alongside latitudes, because *a mapmaker who cannot open a cabinet deserves his empty stomach.*

Finch watched the sketch grow, nodding slowly. "Nineteen minutes," he said. "It took me four minutes to print the Dunmarrow names. It would take me four seconds to lose my nerve." He tapped the drawing. "There. And there. You'll want the casement, not the door—a broken seal announces you; a broken window only announces weather."

"The atlas is forty pounds of bound copperplate and paper."

"Then you'll want a strong back and a longer window. When does the fort next summon you? They still have you surveying for them?"

"They do." And there it was—the thought arriving fully formed, the way the dangerous ones always did. "They summon me Thursday. To revise the harbor soundings. I'll be inside the walls, with my tools, with a legitimate reason to pass the archive corridor. And Sergeant Bray—" Rosalind allowed himself the smallest smile— "Sergeant Bray believes I'm a coward. He's watched me flinch twice on purpose. Men like Bray catalog everyone, and he has filed me under *harmless.* I intend to remain filed."

Margaret spoke from her chair, softly. "Ezra used to say the safest lie is one drawn to scale."

Finch barked a laugh that startled the curlew off the roof. "Your master's widow is wasted on grief, boy. She should be running the operation." Then the laugh drained out of him and he took Rosalind's pencil and, on the corner of the sketch, wrote an address in Vellamouth's Low Quarter and a sequence of words beneath it. "Dead drop. Old chandler's shop, blue door, ask for nothing, leave the parcel wrapped in sailcloth with this marking. My boy Jem collects Thursdays after dark. If the atlas reaches that door, I swear to you on what's left of my reputation: by the following dawn there will be ten thousand sheets of it drying on lines from here to the sea wall, and every displaced family in three counties will wake to find their names in print for the first time since the Crown learned spelling."

Rosalind copied the address into memory the way Hollis had taught him to memorize coordinates—by building a room in his mind and hanging the figures on its walls. Blue door. Sailcloth. Thursday after dark.

They stayed another hour. Finch showed him the standing type already sorted and boxed—letters waiting like soldiers—and explained how he would disguise the pamphlet as an almanac correction, boring enough to escape casual notice, legible enough to detonate in the right hands. Margaret drank tea and argued points of distribution with the ease of a woman who had, Rosalind realized, been corresponding with this man for years under her husband's name. Nothing about her had been idle since the funeral. Grief, in Margaret Hollis, had simply changed occupation.

When at last they rose to go, the light through the broken roof had gone amber and low. Finch walked them as far as the door and then, unexpectedly, gripped Rosalind's forearm with ink-stained fingers, hard.

"One more thing, and it's not a term, it's a warning. Bray will catch you or he won't. But if he catches you—don't protect me, don't protect the widow, don't protect anyone. Give them the story that saves your skin and let the rest of us run our own risks. That's not cowardice. That's how the work survives the worker." His grip tightened. "Hollis never learned that lesson. Don't follow him all the way down."

Rosalind nodded, because there was nothing honest to say aloud.

* * *

The way back was faster, the tide having obligingly rearranged the marsh into a route Margaret seemed to know anyway. Dusk gathered in the reeds. Ahead, beyond the flats, Vellamouth revealed itself in fragments through the fog—rooftops, the church tower, the harbor lamps coming alight one by one, and among them, on the fort's high ground, lanterns moving in patterns that were new. Patrols doubled. He noted it the way he noted everything now, automatically, plotting it, and then felt the cold arrive in his stomach as the implication landed: fewer gaps. Shorter windows. Thursday would be harder than he had promised Finch it would be.

Margaret stopped at the last rise, where the marsh surrendered to the salt meadow and the town's lights spread out below like a chart seen in negative—all the dark places where people lived.

"He'll do it," she said. "Print it, I mean. He's been waiting seven years to be useful again. Men like Finch rust quietly otherwise." She pulled the oilcloth cape closer. "But he's right about one thing you didn't want to hear. Getting that book out of the map room isn't the end of the danger, Rosalind. It's the beginning of the part where the danger learns your name."

Below them, in the town, a bell began to ring the curfew—eight strokes, unhurried, indifferent. And somewhere along the quay, faint on the wind, he could hear the troops changing watch, boots on stone, keeping time.

Thursday, Rosalind thought. Two days.

He touched the address memorized in the rooms of his mind—blue door, sailcloth, after dark—and started down the hill toward the lights, toward the sealed map room and the book that weighed forty pounds and more, toward whatever waited for him on the other side of the seal.

Behind him the marsh closed over their footprints, redrawing itself, remembering nothing.

Which was, he was beginning to understand, exactly what he could never afford to do again.
