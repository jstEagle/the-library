## 14. Curfew Arithmetic

Adaeze Okonkwo had learned to count in the dark before she learned her letters.

Their mother, before the fever took her, used to sit Adaeze on the quay wall at dusk and make her count the fishing boats coming home—one for the widow Prentiss, two for the Cobb brothers, three for old Maren whose nets always smelled of tar—and if the count came up short, that was a grief the whole town shared by breakfast. Counting was how you loved a place. Counting was how you knew it was still yours.

Now she counted other things.

She counted the soldiers at the harbor gate—twelve at dawn shift, eight at nightfall, and the ones at the powder store who never seemed to rotate at all. She counted the sacks of army biscuit stacked under tarpaulin like a mountain of pale, airless bread. She counted the days since the requisition: nineteen, as of this morning. Nineteen days of curfew arithmetic, where everything in Vellamouth was measured out and nothing was ever enough.

The ration line outside the customs house began forming at half past five, an hour before the clerks arrived, because everyone had learned the same lesson in the same week: the ledger did not care when you were hungry, only whether your name sat above or below a certain line. Adaeze took her place behind the Widow Tull and ahead of the Fenwick boys, who were fourteen and eleven and had the hollowed look of children doing sums about food.

"Morning, love," said Widow Tull, not turning around. Her voice had gone flat as slate over the past three weeks. Everyone's had.

"Morning."

"They say the *Resolute*'s taking on cargo again today. Army freight." The widow spat, delicately, into the gutter. "Freight before folk. That's the arithmetic now."

Adaeze said nothing. She had stopped saying things in lines where anyone might be listening. Three weeks was long enough to learn which silences were safe and which were not, and this one—the silence about the ship—was neither. So she let it stand between them, and she counted instead. Forty-one people in line. Two redcoats at the door. One clerk, Pemberly's nephew, with ink on his fingers and his eyes anywhere but on the faces he was reducing to ounces.

The sun came up gray over Vellamouth, the way it had every morning that autumn, fog lying on the water like something drowned that wouldn't stay down. Beyond the customs house roofline she could see the masts of the transports, and beyond them the fort on the headland, its new flag snapping in a wind that smelled of coal smoke and cold iron.

Her name was on the sailing list. One name only. *Okonkwo, A.* She carried the notice folded against her breastbone like a splinter working its way inward. Every day she meant to burn it. Every day she didn't.

* * *

The trouble with Tam Latch started, as most troubles in Vellamouth now did, with paper.

He stood two places ahead of her in the line that morning, a herring-curer with salt-cracked hands and a laugh that used to carry across three piers. He'd been laughing less lately. This morning he wasn't laughing at all. He was holding his ration chit the way a man holds a hand he suspects is broken—carefully, hopefully, already grieving.

The clerk turned the chit over. Turned it back. Held it up to the light.

"Where did you get this stamp, Mr. Latch?"

"From your office, same as everyone."

"The stamp is wrong." The clerk's voice had the terrible gentleness of a man reciting from memory. "The border rule is double-struck. Yours is single."

"It came from your office," Tam said again, louder now, and the line went very quiet, forty-one people pretending not to listen with all their strength. "I queued four hours for it. I can show you the man who gave it me."

"You can show me at the provost's office."

Two soldiers detached from the doorway without hurry. They had done this before. You could see it in their shoulders—not cruelty exactly, or not only cruelty, but the smooth boredom of repetition. They took Tam by the arms. His chit fluttered to the cobbles, and Adaeze watched it lie there, face-up, that small rectangle of authority with its wrong stamp, worth less than the paper it punished him for carrying.

"I've a wife," Tam said. Not shouting. Worse than shouting—explaining. "I've a wife and the baby's teething and she can't queue, her legs—"

"You'll be processed at the provost's office, Mr. Latch. Deportation review. If the forgery is found to be innocent, you'll be reinstated."

*If.* The word hung there. Everyone in the line knew what *if* weighed these days. A man had been deported from Miller's Row last week on a chit smudged by rain. No review had brought him back. There was no address to write to. There was, increasingly, no *back*.

They walked him down the middle of the street, past the shuttered chandlery, past the church whose bell hadn't rung since the occupation began—the vicar having decided, with a cowardice he probably called prudence, that bells gathered crowds. At the corner Tam looked back once, not at the soldiers, not at the clerk. At the line. At all of them, standing in their patient crocodile, holding their correct stamps, saying nothing.

Then the fog took him, and the street closed up behind him like water.

Adaeze picked up the fallen chit. The clerk reached for it; she held it a moment longer, reading the stamp herself—she had good eyes for detail, dockwork taught you that—and yes, single-struck, and yes, she had seen that same stamp pressed onto a dozen chits that week, and none of those carriers had been touched. Which meant either the clerk had made an error and chosen to bury it in Tam Latch, or the clerk had been told to find an error somewhere, and Tam's face had simply come up next.

Either way, the lesson was the same: correctness was no protection. The ledger wasn't measuring truth. It was measuring obedience.

She handed the chit over. "You know that stamp came from your own desk," she said, quietly, so quietly only he could hear.

The clerk's pen did not stop moving. But his ears went red, and his hand, for one stroke of the pen, hesitated.

Small things. She collected them now, the way her brother collected coastlines. Small things were all the currency a dockworker had.

* * *

That afternoon the harbor told her what it always told her, if she listened: numbers.

She went down to the east pier with her tally-slate under her shawl, ostensibly to check the moorings on the *Kestrel*, the packet boat she helped keep seaworthy for whatever future existed after the word *after*. Really she went to count.

Army cargo had been flowing toward the *Resolute* for six days now. Crates from the fort, crates from the rail siding, crates stenciled with markings she didn't recognize but recognized the *shape* of—military, heavy, permanent. Gun-carriage wheels. Barrels marked in a way that suggested powder rather than provisions. Ironwork too substantial for any temporary camp. And here was the thing, the thing she turned over in her mind like a coin tested by teeth: they were loading *outbound* cargo faster than they were unloading anything inbound. A rescue operation, a sanitary evacuation, should have been emptying the town. Instead the town was being emptied *into* the ship, and the ship sat fat and waiting, and the passengers—Vellamouth itself—were told daily that boarding was postponed. Cargo first. Always cargo first.

"You're staring again."

Josiah Quill had come up beside her, a rope coil on his shoulder, sixty years of dockwork in his bowed legs. He didn't look at her. On the piers, you learned to talk without looking, to pass words the way smugglers passed brandy—hand to hand, eyes forward.

"Six days of loading," she said. "I make it two hundred and forty tons, near enough. And not one crate of civilian baggage aboard. Not one."

"Mm."

"They're not evacuating us, Josiah. They're *replacing* us. You don't stage a garrison's stores on a ship that's going to sail away and leave a port empty. You stage them for a port that's staying."

"Careful." But his voice was careful in the way of a man closing a door gently so the wind doesn't hear.

"Tam Latch got taken this morning. Forged stamp. Stamp his own clerks struck."

"I heard."

"And nobody said a word. Forty people watched and nobody said a word, and I understand why, I *understand*—" Her hands had closed into fists without her permission. She loosened them deliberately, finger by finger, the way you eased a cramp from a hauling-line grip. "But Josiah. There are ninety of us who work this waterfront. Ninety men and women who know every crane, every sling, every hold and hatchway on this harbor better than any soldier ever will. If we moved slow—truly slow, slow as syrup, slow as honest confusion—how many days do you think that loading takes?"

Quill was quiet a long moment. Out on the water a lighter barge bumped against the *Resolute*'s hull, and a bosun's whistle shrilled, and crates swung, and the great ship ate and ate and did not get full.

"You'd be asking men to risk their tickets," he said at last. "Their berths. Names on a list are the only thing keeping families fed this month."

"I'm asking men to risk a ticket for a ship that may never take them anywhere. Have you seen the boarding lists change? Have you seen how they change?" She had—everyone on the docks had heard the whispers, names appearing, names vanishing, favor bought with silver and with worse. "That ship isn't a rescue, Josiah. It's a door that opens only outward, and only for whoever they choose. And meanwhile every crate they load makes the staying more permanent."

Another silence. Gulls argued over the offal heap. Somewhere inland the fort drum beat the noon-late hour, late, everything late, the whole town running on the army's clock now.

"There's a way to be slow that isn't mutiny," Quill said finally, and though his eyes stayed on the water, Adaeze heard the door open. "Slinging's an art. A man can misjudge a weight. A winch can seize. Fog comes in, work stops—fog's nobody's fault. Double-checking stencils against manifests, that's diligence. Diligence takes time." He shifted the rope coil. "It would want doing natural. No talk, no meetings, no papers. Word passed man to man, at work, about work. The way the tide passes."

"The way the tide passes," she agreed.

"And it wants a reason, girl. Men need a story they can tell themselves. Not defiance—no man wants to think himself a rebel with a family. But fairness." He nodded toward the customs house, where Tam Latch's wife even now would be learning what *if* meant. "A man can slow a winch for fairness. A man can look at a wrong stamp and remember it, and work a little slower the next day, and call it grief. Grief's allowed. Nobody's arrested a man yet for grieving slow."

Adaeze looked out at the harbor—at the cranes she'd climbed since she was nine, at the bollards worn smooth by a century of her neighbors' ropes, at the water that knew her name better than any ledger did—and felt something kindle low in her chest, banked and steady, a fire built to last the winter rather than blaze for an hour.

"Then we start tonight," she said. "Fog's forecast."

"Fog's always forecast in Vellamouth," said Josiah Quill, and for the first time in nineteen days, he almost smiled.

* * *

The fog came up with the evening tide, right on schedule, thick as wool, and the loading went beautifully badly.

Adaeze worked the east crane gang until the ten o'clock relief, and it was the finest bad work of her life. The winch seized twice—old machine, damp weather, terribly sorry. A sling was misjudged, a crate of gun-carriage wheels lowered with exquisite caution and set down a full twenty feet from the waiting lighter, requiring re-rigging, requiring argument, requiring tea. The stencil check caught a discrepancy—a crate marked 114 on a manifest reading 141—and no one could proceed until it was resolved, and resolution required a clerk, and the clerks had gone home at dusk because clerks' hours were still human hours.

By midnight the *Resolute* had taken on perhaps a tenth of what the day's plan demanded. The bosun's whistles grew shrill. An officer came down the pier and asked questions, and the answers he received were uniformly respectful, uniformly regretful, and uniformly useless. Fog. Weight discrepancies. A seized winch. Old equipment. Terribly sorry, sir.

Standing in the crane cage with her hands on levers worn smooth by generations, Adaeze felt the strangest thing rise in her—not triumph, nothing so clean, but something older and quieter. Purpose, maybe. Or the memory of purpose. Her mother counting boats. Herself, counting now. The same act, inverted: then she had counted to know her town was safe; now she counted to keep it from being stolen. Either way it was the same arithmetic, the same love wearing different clothes.

Around her, in the fog, the dockworkers moved with a new patience. Nobody spoke of it. Nobody needed to. When the winch seized the second time, six men converged on it with the solemn urgency of surgeons, and when it was pronounced sound an hour later, they dispersed without a glance exchanged—and Adaeze understood that something had been born tonight on these wet planks, nameless and unofficial and real. Solidarity doesn't announce itself. It accumulates, like silt, like tide, like the slow deposit of small refusals.

She was walking home through the fog at half past eleven, curfew-be-damned because dockworkers' hours predated curfew and the sentries had grudgingly learned it, when she saw the light.

The map room window, on the hill above the town. Rosalind's window. Burning at an hour when no apprentice had lawful business with a candle.

She stopped in the lane, fog beading on her lashes, and looked at that small yellow square the way a sailor looks at a light that shouldn't be there—reef rock, or wreck, or something worse. Her brother had been strange for days. Distracted. Hands stained with fresh ink over old ink. Questions asked sideways: how many men worked the night gangs, which piers had the loosest sentries, whether she could get a message to someone at the printing shop that burned out in the Marsh Road years ago.

Rosalind, who believed history happened elsewhere. Rosalind, whose candle was lit at midnight in a room full of government surveyors' charts, in a town where a man had been deported that morning for a single wrong stamp, where Sergeant Bray walked the streets with the patience of a fisherman watching a float.

The light moved. Shadow crossed it—her brother's shape, bent over something, hurried.

Adaeze stood in the fog with her heart going hard against the notice folded over her breastbone, and did the arithmetic she dreaded most: whatever Rosalind was doing, it was bigger than a slowed winch, and smaller mistakes had already cost this town a man with salt-cracked hands and a teething baby.

She turned up the hill through the wet dark, quickening, to catch her brother before the soldiers counted what he was counting.
