## 16. Night Survey

The note was waiting for him when he came to the map room at dusk, folded once and weighted beneath a brass paperweight on Master Hollis's old desk. It bore the garrison's stamp, the one Rosalind had learned to read with a mixture of dread and necessity: *By order of Sergeant T. Bray, Royal Engineers, all survey materials in the Vellamouth map room are subject to inspection. The undersigned requires the apprentice Okonkwo to attend this evening, nine o'clock, regarding discrepancies in Plate Forty-One.*

Discrepancies. The word sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

He had been careful. He had been so careful—copying the altered ledger pages only during sanctioned surveys, returning each folio to its exact position, aligning the spines by fractions of an inch, dusting the shelf edges afterward with a dry cloth so no finger-smear would betray him. But Bray was not a careless man. Bray had the patience of a man who baited hooks and then sat very still beside the water.

Rosalind read the note three times. He could refuse it—that much was true on its face. But refusal would be an admission wearing different clothes, and besides, refusing would not save him if Bray already knew. If Bray already knew, then the copies were lost, and Margaret's warning wasted, and Finch waiting in the marsh for pages that would never come. If Bray did not know—if this were merely suspicion fishing for confirmation—then going might be survivable, might even be useful, because a man under inspection who behaves like an innocent man is often believed.

*Plate Forty-One.* He pulled it from the drawer to see what trap had been laid there. The plate showed the eastern approaches to the harbor, soundings in Hollis's meticulous hand, depth figures that had not changed since before the war. Nothing altered. Nothing incriminating. Which meant the plate itself was irrelevant—which meant the note was not about plates at all.

It was about whether he would come.

Rosalind stood in the candlelight of the room where he had spent three years learning to draw the world honestly, and he understood, with the cold clarity of a man reading his own chart, that he was being asked to walk into deep water without knowing the depth. Every instinct Adaeze had ever voiced about him—theory without weather, ink without salt—told him to stay away. But there was another voice now, quieter and harder: Margaret's, telling him what Hollis had died believing. That the truth was worth more than the body carrying it. That someone had to be willing to be caught.

He blew out the candle, tucked the copied pages into the oilskin wallet against his chest, and went to keep his appointment.

* * *

The garrison had changed the town's nights. Where lamplight used to spill from windows in warm irregular pools, now the streets ran dark between checkpoints, and the fog came up off the harbor unchallenged, thickening until the world shrank to ten feet in any direction. Rosalind moved through it listening for boots. Twice he heard them and pressed himself into doorways, heart hammering against the oilskin wallet as though the papers could feel fear through the leather.

The map room annex—a long stone building attached to the fort's western wall—was lit when he arrived. A single guard at the door, young, bored, breath steaming. He checked Rosalind against a list, glanced at the order, and waved him through with the indifference of a man guarding furniture.

Inside, the annex stretched away in ranks of flat drawers, each one labeled in faded copperplate: COASTLINES, SOUNDINGS, INLAND WATERS, ROADS AND TRACKS. Moonlight fell through high narrow windows in pale bars, laying stripes across the floor like the slats of a cage. At the far end, a desk lamp burned behind a screen, and from behind the screen came the small, deliberate sounds of a man handling paper.

"Okonkwo." Bray's voice, unhurried. "You're punctual. I admire that in a tradesman."

"Sergeant." Rosalind kept his hands loose at his sides and his voice level. "You mentioned discrepancies in Plate Forty-One."

"I did." Bray emerged from behind the screen carrying the plate itself, holding it up to the lamp as though appraising a painting. He was a broad man gone soft at the middle, with a face like a closed ledger—all columns, no sums. "Beautiful work, this. Hollis's hand, I'd say. Dead men draw such clean lines, don't they? No second thoughts." He set the plate down. "Tell me about the binding on the Hollis atlas."

"The binding?"

"Don't make me repeat myself, boy. It insults us both."

Rosalind felt the floor tilt beneath his certainty. *The binding.* Not the pages. Not the marginalia. The binding—and if Bray was asking about the binding, then somewhere, somehow, the atlas's existence had leaked, or worse, someone had talked. Margaret. Had they leaned on her? Had she—

"The atlas is Master Hollis's personal property," Rosalind said carefully. "Its disposition belongs to his widow."

"Its disposition," Bray repeated, tasting the words, "belongs to the Crown, since the Crown requisitioned every document in this building. Including, I'm told, certain items removed from it." He walked slowly along the rank of drawers, trailing one finger along the brass handles, a man playing scales on an instrument only he could hear. "Here's what puzzles me, Okonkwo. The census folios. Folio twelve through nineteen. They've been moved. Oh, don't look surprised—they're always moved slightly, everyone handles them, that's the nature of archives. But these were moved *recently*, and recently means since the requisition, and since the requisition there are exactly four people with keys to this room. General Vance. Myself. Captain Vance. And you."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, a sentry called the hour—half past nine—and the sound seemed to mark how little time remained in any version of this night.

"I survey here under written orders," Rosalind said. "I touch what my orders require me to touch."

"Yes." Bray smiled without warmth. "And tonight, your orders require you to open those drawers for me. All of them. In sequence. While I watch." He gestured to a stool beside the desk. "Sit where I can see your hands."

This was the trap, then—not the note, not the plate, but this slow ceremonial unwrapping, drawer by drawer, while a man who missed nothing watched a young man's hands for the tremor of guilt. Rosalind sat. His palms were damp. He wiped them on his trousers and told himself: *innocent men have damp palms too.*

The first drawers were easy. Coastlines, soundings—Hollis's own territory, sheets Rosalind had handled a thousand times legitimately. He opened each, lifted each bundle, laid it down. Bray watched his hands. Rosalind made himself think of nothing, which was impossible, so instead he thought of drawing: the weight of a line, the discipline of it, a hand that moves because it has decided and not because it is afraid.

Inland waters. Roads and tracks. Then the census drawers.

"Folio twelve," Bray said.

Rosalind opened the drawer. The folios lay in their neat stack, and even before he touched them he saw it—the wrongness, subtle as a shifted horizon. The stack was too tall. Or too short. Something had been added, or something taken, and Bray's silence behind him was the silence of a fisherman watching the float dip.

He lifted the top folio. Beneath it lay a sheet that did not belong—a page covered in dense official script, headed with the quartermaster's stamp, dated three days hence. A page planted among the census records like a seed in a wound.

"Read it aloud," Bray said softly.

Rosalind read it. It was an inventory acknowledgment—routine, meaningless—except that it referenced *materials removed by the apprentice R. Okonkwo, per attached schedule*, and the schedule beneath it listed, in a fair imitation of his own hand, titles that would hang him twice over: *census folios, harbor defense plans, correspondence of the late E. Hollis.*

Forgery. A forged confession, planted where only he would find it—or rather, where only he would be found *with* it. Bray hadn't needed proof. He had manufactured the moment of discovery itself.

"Well," Bray said behind him, and there was real satisfaction in it now, the satisfaction of a man closing a ledger. "What do you suppose that's doing there?"

Rosalind turned. In the lamplight, Bray's hand rested near the bell-pull that would bring the guard, and his smile had the patience of months in it. "I imagine," Rosalind said, and heard his own voice go strange and calm, "that you put it there."

The smile flickered—only a flicker, but Rosalind saw it land. "Careful."

"You wanted me to open the drawers. You wanted the finding to happen in front of witnesses. You didn't care whether I'd actually taken anything, because the page says I did, and the page is enough." Rosalind rose from the stool, slowly, his mind moving faster than it had ever moved, mapping the room the way he mapped everything—exits, distances, weights. "The question is what happens when I say so loudly enough that the whole garrison hears it. Forgeries cut both ways, Sergeant. You'd have to explain why a loyal sergeant plants evidence instead of reporting a theft."

For a moment neither man moved. Then Bray's face settled into something worse than anger—into procedure. "Guard!" he called, not loudly, but with terrible confidence. "Corporal! Attend!"

Boots in the corridor. Rosalind's eyes swept the room one final time—the high windows, barred; the door, filling now with the corporal's silhouette; and between them, the long rank of chart drawers, waist-high, running the length of the wall like a reef waiting to wreck him.

He went over them.

It was not graceful. He got one knee onto the drawer tops, scattering a cascade of coastal charts across the floor, and heard Bray roar behind him as he vaulted down the far side, shoulder hitting stone, rolling, coming up running toward the narrow service stair at the annex's end—the one used for hauling map chests, barely wider than a barrel. Behind him the corporal shouted and the lamp went over and the room filled with swinging shadows.

The stair was dark and steep. Rosalind took it three steps at a time, lungs burning, and burst out onto the roof of the annex under a sky the color of wet slate. The fog had thinned into rags. Below and behind, the door banged open, torchlight spilling into the yard.

To the left: the fort wall, twenty feet, patrolled. To the right: the rooftops of the chandlery and the cooperage, sloping slate, dropping in uneven steps toward the lanes above the harbor. He chose the roofs.

Slate is honest about nothing. It looks solid and offers only its mood. Rosalind ran the ridge of the chandlery with the wind shoving at him, slid down its flank on his heels, and leapt the gap to the cooperage—and in landing, his left hand went out instinctively to catch himself on the gutter's iron lip, and the iron, rusted through, gave way with a shriek.

Pain went up his arm like a struck bell. He rolled, clutched the hand to his chest, and felt the warmth welling between his fingers even before he looked: a long tear across the palm, deep, ugly, the kind of wound that maps itself permanently into a working hand. There was no time to consider it. Torches were moving in the lane below. He wrapped the hand in the tail of his shirt, bit the knot tight with his teeth, and kept moving—roof to roof, gap to gap, the harbor fog rising to swallow him, until the last drop brought him down into the black mouth of the fish-curing alley where the smell hid everything, even blood.

* * *

Elowen Vance found him by the blood.

She had not been able to sleep. Her father's orders sat on her desk like stones she was forbidden to move, and past midnight she had done what she increasingly did—walked the perimeter alone, ostensibly inspecting sentry posts, actually fleeing the inside of her own skull. She was cutting back along the curing sheds when she saw it: a dark smear on the whitewashed wall, low, hurried, still wet.

She followed it the way she followed anything—methodically, hating herself for the hope she refused to name.

He was in the lee of the last shed, half-sitting against the boards, and at the scrape of her boot he came up fast, back to the wall, right hand fumbling for a weapon he didn't carry. Even in the dark she could see he was swaying.

"Captain," he breathed. Of all the words in the world, that one.

"Mr. Okonkwo." She kept her voice flat, official, a shield held up between them. "There's a garrison-wide search. Sergeant Bray's men. They're saying a thief broke into the annex."

"Did they catch a description?"

"They caught a description." She stepped closer. In the thin light she saw the state of his left hand—the shirt soaked through, the dark drip marking its rhythm on the cobbles—and something in her chest went over like ballast in a storm. "You're bleeding out on my father's cobblestones."

"Then arrest me," he said, "or help me. But decide quickly, Captain, because I can't run much further either way."

The correct answer was arrest. She knew it the way she knew her drill manual—by heart, by rote, by everything she had been made to be. Her father's daughter. The uniform. The name. She heard her own voice arrive from somewhere outside all of that:

"Give me your hand."

She knelt on the filthy cobbles of a curing alley and unwound the ruined shirt-tail with fingers that did not shake, though everything in her shook. The cut was long and deep across the palm, a rusted-iron signature, still seeping. From her coat she took the small kit she carried on inspections—lint, a roll of linen, a flask of spirits—and poured the spirits over the wound without warning, because gentleness would have cost them both more time.

He made no sound. That undid her more than a cry would have. She looked up and found him watching her, gray-faced, rain-damp, and there was no mask left on either of them. Whatever they had been circling since the day he first handed her a corrected chart—wariness, resentment, the electric embarrassment of respect—it burned away now, and what remained was simply two people kneeling in the dark, one holding the other's life in her hands.

"This will scar," she said quietly.

"I'm a mapmaker," he managed. "Everything scars the work eventually."

She bound the hand in clean figure-eights, the way field surgeons taught, snug but not tight, and when she tied off the end her fingers stayed on his wrist a moment longer than bandaging required. Neither of them pretended otherwise.

"Why?" he asked. Just that. *Why.*

She thought of her father's orders upstairs. She thought of the word *loyalty* in his mouth, and what he had made it mean. "Because whatever you took from that room," she said, "I'm beginning to suspect the theft was committed by the other side first." She rose, pulling him up with her good grip. "There's a boatman's stairs below the north quay. Fog's thick. Go."

"And you?"

"I'll report that I searched this sector and found nothing." She met his eyes, and let him see, for the first time, exactly what that lie was going to cost her. "Go, Rosalind. Before I remember who I am."

He went—but at the mouth of the alley he stopped, half-turned, silhouetted against the fog, and said nothing at all, which somehow said everything, and was gone.

Elowen stood alone in the curing alley, his blood drying on her gloves, and understood that she had just chosen. Not between him and her father—she had been choosing that for weeks without admitting it. Between the woman she had been issued and the woman she had, tonight, become.

Behind her, across the town, a bell began to ring the alarm, and somewhere in the dark Sergeant Bray was already asking questions she would soon have to answer.
