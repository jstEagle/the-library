## 17. Two Maps of the Same Town

Elowen Vance had learned to sleep like a soldier years ago—flat on her back, boots within reach of her hand, one ear tuned to the corridor. It was a discipline her father had drilled into her at thirteen, the year she'd been commissioned into his household regiment of expectations: *A Vance does not dream in public. A Vance does not startle. A Vance is always awake before the drums.*

Tonight the discipline failed her entirely.

She lay in the officers' quarters of Fort Cragmore listening to Bray's lantern pass beneath her window for the third time that night, and she thought about blood drying on kid leather. She thought about how she had scrubbed the gloves in her basin until the water went pink and then grey and then clear, and how the clearing of the water had not cleared anything at all. Somewhere below, in the town whose streets she could trace from memory better than any map in her father's cabinet, Rosalind Okonkwo was running with an injured hand through fog that smelled of marsh salt and coal smoke, and she had put him there. She had opened the door of the map room with her own key and stood aside like a sentry gone blind on purpose.

*Aiding and abetting.* The phrase arrived in her father's voice, clipped and certain, the way all his phrases arrived. She turned it over anyway, examining it as she examined everything—by its edges, its scale, its accuracy—and found it insufficient. What she had done was larger than abetting. She had looked at the machinery of the requisition from the inside, where the gears were stamped with her own family name, and she had put her hand between them.

The lantern passed a fourth time. She did not close her eyes at all.

* * *

Morning came up grey and without ceremony over Vellamouth. From the fort's eastern parapet the town looked exactly as it had in every survey she had ever corrected for her father: the harbour curled like a hand cupping water, the rooftops huddled down toward the quay, the marsh beyond lying flat and silver under the low light. Beautiful, in the way things were beautiful when you refused to see who lived in them.

That was the trick her whole profession played, she realized. Cartography was supposed to be the honest science—lines and soundings and verifiable elevations—but every chart she had ever drawn had committed the same quiet crime. A map said *harbour* and never *Hollis*. It said *residential quarter* and never *Adaeze Okonkwo, dockworker, third door past the cooper's*. It rendered a town legible to the Admiralty by rendering it invisible to itself.

Her father's new map did not even pretend otherwise.

She had found it three nights ago, or rather she had stopped pretending not to know where it was. General Sir Aldous Vance kept his private papers in a locked drawer beneath the campaign desk he had carried from the Punjab, and the key lived in a compartment in his watch chain that he believed she did not know about because he had never chosen to tell her. He told her everything else. That was the peculiar cruelty of being Aldous Vance's daughter: he trusted her with his opinions, his grievances, his contempt for Whitehall's squeamishness—he simply never imagined she might turn any of it against him, the way a man does not imagine his own shadow turning.

The drawer had held two documents. The first was the order from the Board of Ordnance, signed by hands above her father's, authorizing the *provisional sanitary requisition* of Vellamouth. The second was her father's own survey, drawn in his tight, ruthless hand—the survey he had shown her only the shape of, when she had demanded to know why a fever epidemic required troop transports.

It was not a quarantine plan. It had never been a quarantine plan.

She stood at the parapet now with the memory of it open behind her eyes like a chart pinned to a wall. *Proposed Naval Depot and Coaling Station, Vellamouth Reach.* Deep-water berthing for ships of the line. A coaling wharf where the fish-market stood. Barracks on the high ground where St. Brendan's churchyard lay—the churchyard itself marked *relocated, remains consolidated*. And along the margin, in her father's handwriting, small and neat as stitches: *No return of civilian population contemplated. Town to be razed in phases following evacuation.*

*Razed in phases.*

She had read the words four times before they would behave like words. Her father had drawn the death of eight hundred people the way he drew everything else—competently, economically, with handsome hatching to indicate the proposed parade ground.

"Captain Vance."

Bray's voice behind her. She did not startle; she had been raised by a man who considered flinching a form of confession. She turned and gave the sergeant the face she had spent thirty-one years manufacturing: mild, correct, faintly bored.

"Sergeant. You're up early."

"Weren't asleep, ma'am." His eyes were red-rimmed and patient, the eyes of a man who baited traps for a living and was content to sit beside them. "There was an incident last night. Map room. Someone came for the Hollis material."

"Was anything taken?"

"That's what I'm determining." He let the silence do its work. He was good at that; she had watched him do it to dockworkers and clerks and once, memorably, to a curate. "Your key opens that room, Captain. Yours and mine and the General's."

"Then I expect you have three suspects." She kept her gaze on the harbour, where the transport ship sat at anchor like a patient animal. "I surveyed the fort approaches yesterday until midnight. My assistant will confirm it."

"I've spoken to your assistant." A pause calibrated to the yard. "He confirms it."

"Then we're agreed." She moved toward the stair, forcing him to step aside, and only when she had passed him did she add, lightly, "Sergeant—if someone wanted the Hollis atlas badly enough to break in, perhaps ask yourself why a murdered mapmaker's book frightens the Crown more than a French fleet does. Whatever answer you find may be more useful than whoever holds the key."

She heard him stop breathing for half a second. It was a reckless blow and she knew it—a woman with something to hide did not usually invite her interrogator to consider motive—but recklessness was also camouflage. Guilt ran. Innocence lectured. She descended the stairs with her spine straight and her heart going like a drum corps, and only when she reached the courtyard did she permit herself the thought:

*I just taught a hunter where the meat is. God forgive me if he follows it to the wrong door.*

* * *

The summons came at noon, and she went to her father's office knowing what it would be, because she had engineered it herself. Bray had reported. Bray always reported. Her remark on the stairs had been designed to travel upward faster than any suspicion of her, and it had worked: when she entered, General Vance was not looking at her but at the wall map of the requisition zone, and his expression was the one he wore when the war office annoyed him.

"The sergeant believes there was an attempted theft," he said without turning. "I believe there was a fool with a grudge and a lame excuse. But the fool got past a lock, which means either our security is rotten or our people are." Now he turned. "You surveyed the fort yesterday?"

"Until midnight."

"And you saw nothing."

"Nothing worth reporting."

He studied her the way he studied terrain—for weakness, for line of advance. She met it with thirty-one years of practice and felt, beneath the armour, the terrible sliding sensation of a daughter lying fluently to her father for the first time in her life. It was easier than she had expected. That frightened her most of all.

"There's a delegation from the Admiralty coming Thursday," he said at last, returning to the map. "They'll want the depot plan finalized. Which means the last transports sail by month's end, and this town becomes a coaling station with a churchyard attached." A grunt. "Whitehall will wring its hands when the figures come out, and then they'll thank me, because they always do. You understand that, Elowen? In ten years no one will remember there was ever a choice here. There'll be a lighthouse and a pier and a plaque, and the plaque will thank men like me."

"Is that what you want to be remembered by, Father? A plaque?"

"What I want," he said, with the sudden flatness that was the closest he ever came to tenderness, "is for my daughter to be standing beside me when the plaque goes up, instead of asking questions that make my sergeants write reports." He tapped the map, once, over the marked ruins of the fish-market. "Loyalty is the only mercy you'll be permitted in this life. Learn to spend it well."

*Yes, sir,* she said, and saluted, and walked out of his office with her father's private survey folded flat against her ribs beneath her tunic, where she had carried it since dawn, because she had known—even as she lied to him—that she would need a pretext to enter the room where the safe stood, and the safest lie is the one performed inside another truth.

She had taken it while he spoke. While he monologued about plaques. She had knelt at the campaign drawer with the watch-chain key trembling in her fingers and lifted out the future of eight hundred people, and her father had stood six feet away describing his own monument, and had never once looked down.

*Loyalty is the only mercy you'll be permitted.* She crossed the courtyard into the grey afternoon and thought: *then I choose to be permitted none at all.*

* * *

Finding Rosalind Okonkwo was the easy part; finding him alone was the difficulty. Vellamouth under occupation had grown eyes everywhere—redcoats at the corners, informants in the ration lines, neighbours who watched their neighbours with the desperate arithmetic of people competing for berths. She used the excuse the occupation had handed her: a resurvey of the quays damaged by army loading, conducted by Captain Vance personally because her assistants were needed elsewhere. No officer questioned it. Surveying was her function here, her visible purpose, the very reason she had been assigned to correct maps in a conquered town—as if the crime needed anything so innocent as accurate charts.

She found him at dusk, in the lee of the broken customs house, ostensibly checking a benchmark. His right hand was bound in a linen strip gone brown at the palm, and he held his drafting pencil awkwardly, in a modified grip that must have cost him, and when he saw her he went very still in the manner of a creature deciding whether the thing approaching was weather or predator.

"Captain Vance."

"Mr. Okonkwo." She glanced up the street, down it. Empty. The fog was coming in off the marsh, thickening the lamplight into soft halos. "Your hand."

"It heals."

"It's infected. The skin around the binding is red." She said it flatly, clinically, because tenderness would have been unbearable to them both. "How is the widow Hollis?"

"Well enough to frighten me, which I'm told is her gift."

"And the book?"

He understood at once—she watched him understand, watched his shoulders drop a fraction, the way a man relaxes when the gun pointed at him turns out to be extended grip-first. "Safe," he said. "Somewhere Bray will never think to look, because he thinks he already has it." A ghost of the craftsman's pride. "I drew him a beautiful forgery, Captain. Wrong in every particular and gorgeous doing it."

Despite everything, something in her chest turned over. *He outdrew them.* "That's why I'm here," she said. "What you forged, and what I'm about to give you—they should meet."

She brought the packet out from under her coat. Oilskin, tied with the plain cord her father used for dispatches. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the transport ship riding at anchor in the dark behind them.

"My father's private survey," she said. "Not the requisition order—the real document underneath it. The requisition was never about fever. There is no epidemic in the marshes; there never was. Read it and you'll find a naval depot, coaling wharves, barracks on the churchyard ground." She made herself say the rest steadily. "And a marginal note in his own hand. *No return of civilian population contemplated.* The ship isn't rescue, Mr. Okonkwo. It's removal. Vellamouth is never meant to exist again."

The fog moved between them. Down at the quay, unseen, water slapped pilings; somewhere a soldier laughed at something another soldier said, and the laugh carried across the empty street and died.

Rosalind took the packet the way he must have taken his first compass—carefully, as if it might bite, as if it might save him. "Why," he said quietly. Not *why me*, she noticed. Not even *why now*. Just *why*, the way a surveyor asks for a bearing.

"Because I read it," she said, "and then I slept, or didn't, and somewhere in the night I understood that I had a decision to make about what kind of person carries my father's name." She paused. "He trusts me. Did you know that? Completely, stupidly, the way you trust your own reflection. I could burn this fort down and he'd suspect the French. And I am going to spend that trust the way he spends towns—efficiently, and without looking back." Her voice caught on the last word and she hated it, mastered it. "There's a condition. Only one."

"Name it."

"Don't soften it. When you print—and you intend to print, don't insult us both by denying it—print the numbers exactly. Eight hundred and forty-one souls in the census ledger. Four hundred and twelve names already erased from three other towns, the ones Master Hollis collected in his margins. Don't round. Don't summarize. Let them see the arithmetic of it, every unit a person." She looked away, toward the black line of the sea. "My father lives in summaries. Take that from him."

"He'll know it was you."

"Yes."

"He'll—" Rosalind stopped. She watched him assemble the sentence and decide, characteristically, to attempt it anyway: "He could lose everything. You could lose everything. I can't ask—"

"You haven't asked. I'm giving. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point." She turned back to him, and the fog had gathered so thickly now that his face was the only fixed thing in the world, lamplit, intent, the injured hand cradling her father's treason. "Ask nothing in return—that's what you were going to say to me. I watched you compose it. So let me save us both the decency." A breath. "I want nothing in return except a town that still exists in ten years, with its names intact, and a map of it drawn by someone who knows what maps are for. Can you do that much?"

For a long moment he said nothing. Then, with his left hand, the good one, he touched the brim of his head in something older than a salute—an apprentice's bow to a master, a promise made in the grammar of craft.

"I can draw," he said, "better than anyone alive. And now I finally have something worth drawing true."

* * *

She walked back to the fort alone by the long route, past the ration queue's remnants and the shuttered chandlery and the chapel where someone had scratched, in fresh chalk on the occupied stone, a single word she chose not to read aloud in her own head. The oilskin was gone from beneath her tunic. She felt its absence the way an amputee feels a limb—as weight, as heat, as a phantom insisting on its place.

At the gate the sentry saluted her. At the stair, Bray's door stood open, lamplight spilling into the corridor, and he looked up from a table spread with the map room's inventory as she passed. Their eyes met for precisely as long as courtesy required.

"Good evening, Sergeant."

"Captain."

In her room she sat down at the small desk beneath the window and took out her own field notebook—the honest one, the one with soundings and benchmarks—and began, in her tidy professional hand, to copy her father's figures from memory: tonnage, draught, acreage, the displacement schedule, phase by phase, razing by razing. If the oilskin was taken from Rosalind, the numbers would survive in her head and her hand. If she herself were taken, the notebook would be found, and there would be no explanation she could offer that would not be a confession.

She wrote until the fort bell struck two. Then she blew out the lamp, and in the darkness she heard across the sleeping town the sound of the tide turning in the harbour—that vast slow exhale of water letting go of the land and taking it back—and she understood that she had crossed the line there was no survey for. Her father's blood in her veins, his key-chain in her pocket, his crimes in her handwriting now: she was, from this night forward, evidence against her own house.

Below, in the lane, a boot scraped stone. Then another. Then the deliberate, unhurried tread of Sergeant Bray beginning his rounds, stopping—she counted the seconds—directly beneath her window, where he stood for a long moment in the dark, saying nothing at all.

Then he moved on—not toward the gate, but down toward the town, toward the marsh road, toward the printer's parish—and Elowen rose from her bed with her heart gone cold, because she suddenly knew exactly which question she had, three days ago, handed him the answer to.
