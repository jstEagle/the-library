## 18. The Widow's Bargain

Margaret Hollis had spent sixty-one years learning that fear was a thing you could fold, like linen. It never went away—you only learned to make it smaller, to press it flat, to store it in a drawer where it could not see you and you could not see it. She had folded fear when the fever took their first child. She had folded it again when Edmund came home from the survey of Kettering with grey in his beard and a name he would not speak. And she folded it now, standing in her own parlor with her hands clasped at her waist, as Sergeant Bray's boots rang on the cobbles outside her gate like a man counting out coins.

He came up the path without hurrying. That was the worst of him, she thought—not the cruelty, which she had expected, but the patience. He was a man who understood that time did his work for him. The requisition had done most of it already. Every soul in Vellamouth was now a man drowning in slow motion, and drowning men would tell you anything for a rope.

She had asked Rosalind to leave by the garden door an hour ago. He had refused, of course—the boy was loyal past the point of sense, loyal the way Edmund had been loyal, which is to say fatally—but she had insisted, and for once he had obeyed. Not because she was persuasive. Because she had lied to him, gently, and told him that she could manage Bray with nothing but a widow's helplessness, and he had wanted so badly to believe it that he had let himself believe it.

The truth was that she could manage Bray with something better than helplessness. She could manage him with a lie of her own.

The knock came. Three measured raps, the knuckle of a man who had never once in his life knocked in haste.

She opened the door herself. Let him see her in her plain grey dress, her hair pinned without ornament, her face arranged into the particular tiredness that widows were permitted to wear in public. Let him think he had already read every page of her.

"Sergeant Bray." She stepped back. "You'll take tea. It's the last of the good China, and I'd rather it went down a soldier's throat than sit in a cupboard waiting for a ship that isn't coming."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the acknowledgment that a smile had been considered and found unnecessary. He stepped inside. He filled the doorway the way soldiers did, all of them, as if doors had been built too narrow on purpose.

"Mrs. Hollis." He removed his hat. He set it crown-down on the table by the chair, precisely, the way a man sets down a thing he intends to pick up again. "Thank you for seeing me without a summons."

"I'm an old woman in a requisitioned town, Sergeant. I see whoever knocks." She moved to the tea things. Her hands were steady. She had rehearsed this—had stood at this same table the night before, alone, pouring water into the pot and out again, until the motion belonged to her body and not to her will. "You'll forgive me for saying I know why you've come."

"Do you?"

"Edmund Hollis's widow keeps his papers. Everyone in this town knows it. And everyone in this town knows that you've been asking about his papers since before the redcoats sealed the harbor." She poured. The tea was dark and over-steeped; she had made it that way on purpose, so that her hands would have something to blame for any tremor. "I'd rather you asked me to my face than went through my linens again."

He did not deny the search. She had not expected him to. Men like Bray treated denial as a currency they could not afford.

"Your husband," Bray said, taking the chair she indicated, sitting with his back straight and his hands on his knees like a schoolboy called before a headmaster—except that the schoolboy act was itself a performance, and she knew it, and suspected he knew she knew it—"was in possession of government survey materials. Materials removed from the fort without authorization. Materials that, in the present circumstances, constitute a security concern."

"What a gentle way to put it." She sat across from him, folding her hands in her lap. "You mean he stole them. Or you mean he was given them and kept them, which is a different crime, and a more interesting one. Which is it, Sergeant?"

His eyes came up. For the first time, the patience in them showed its other face—the thing the patience was for. "You are not what I expected, Mrs. Hollis."

"Nobody ever is. That's the trouble with expectations. They're maps drawn by people who've never been to the country." She sipped her tea. "You want to know where Edmund's atlas is."

"I want to know where it is."

"Then let us not insult one another with the fiction that this is a conversation about security. This is a conversation about a bargain. You have something I want. I have something you want. Old women learn to trade, Sergeant. We spent our whole lives trading—time for money, health for children, grief for memory. We are the great merchants of the world, and nobody notices because we deal in such small coin." She set down her cup. It did not rattle against the saucer. She had practiced that too.

Bray said nothing. He was watching her the way a man watches a card game he has not yet decided to enter.

"Three berths," she said.

He blinked. Whatever he had prepared for, it was not that. "Berths."

"On the ship. The *Providence*, or whatever they've christened her this week. One for me. One for Rosalind Okonkwo. One for Adaeze Okonkwo." She named them plainly, the way one names items on a shopping list, and watched the names land in him. She saw the moment he understood that the Okonkwo boy was not merely a name to her, that this was not a widow selling a dead man's secrets out of loneliness, that there was architecture here, and that the architecture included the apprentice.

"Those names are not on the sailing list," Bray said carefully.

"No. Which is precisely why I'm offering you the only thing in Vellamouth worth more than the atlas itself—the power to put them there." She leaned forward. "You have access to the ledger. You have access to the clerk who alters it, which we both know he does, because I have eyes, Sergeant, and this town has been watching that ledger like hawks since the day it was nailed shut. You can amend the list. You can do it quietly, in a way that no one will ever question, because who questions a clerk's clerk? And in exchange, I will tell you where Edmund kept the thing you've been turning this town inside out to find."

The silence that followed was long. Outside, a gull screamed over the harbor, and beneath the sound, faint and rhythmic, came the groan of cargo being loaded onto the ship that was supposed to carry the town away and was, by all appearances, preparing to carry nothing but army freight.

"You understand," Bray said at last, "that if I agreed to such a thing, and it were discovered—"

"It won't be discovered. That's the beauty of it. Nobody discovers a bargain that both parties need buried." She let a small, bitter smile touch her mouth. "You think I don't know what you are, Sergeant? You're not a zealot. Zealots don't search linens; zealots burn houses. You're a man who has learned that the empire is a machine, and that the men who feed the machine survive, and the men who argue with it are fed into it instead. You want the atlas because your superiors want the atlas, and you want your superiors to be pleased with you. I am offering you the pleasure of your superiors at the cost of three names on a list. That is the cheapest price anyone will ever ask of you in this whole dirty business."

Something moved behind his eyes. She could not read whether it was contempt or respect, and she understood, with a strange lurch, that it did not matter which. Either one would serve.

"And if I refuse?" he said.

"Then you'll tear my house apart, and you'll find what you always find—dust, linens, and a dead man's spectacles. And you'll go on searching, and the ship will sail, and the atlas will be wherever it is, and you will have to explain to General Vance why a widow with bad knees outlasted the intelligence of the entire garrison." She rose, slowly, and went to the window. The harbor lay below, grey and glittering, the transports riding at anchor like patient beasts. "I am seventy-one years old, Sergeant Bray. I have buried a husband, a child, and every illusion I was born with. You cannot threaten me with anything the world has not already done. But you can bargain with me. And I think you're a man who prefers to bargain."

She heard him stand. She heard the small sounds of a man reassembling his authority, hat in hand, boots finding their stance.

"Three names," he said. "Amended to the sailing list. Quietly." A pause. "And the atlas."

"The atlas, and the truth about how Edmund came to die with it." She turned to face him. "You'll have it tomorrow. Tonight I have to—prepare myself. A widow doesn't hand over her husband's last work without a night to sit with it. Even you must grant me that."

He studied her for a long moment. She let him study. She had nothing left on her face but an old woman's grief, and it was even true—she *was* grieving, though not for the reason he believed.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Noon. If I learn you've deceived me, Mrs. Hollis, the bargain is void, and I promise you the consequences will not be theoretical."

"I should be disappointed in you if they were."

He put on his hat. At the door he paused, and without turning said, "Your husband was a difficult man, Mrs. Hollis."

"He was an honest one. I find the two conditions usually travel together."

The door closed behind him. She stood listening until his boots faded down the lane, and then she stood listening a while longer, until she was certain of the silence, and only then did she allow her hands to shake.

She sat down in Edmund's chair and pressed her palms flat against her knees and breathed, in and out, the way she had once taught him to breathe when his hands trembled over a difficult plate. *The fear is real, Edmund. The fear has always been real. It's only the showing of it that's a choice.*

* * *

The garden door opened at dusk, and Rosalind came in white-faced, having watched the whole exchange from the widow's walk above the conservatory, where she had ordered him to wait with a spyglass and instructions not to move no matter what he saw. He had moved once—when Bray's hand had rested on the door latch a moment too long—and she had seen the curtain twitch from the corner of her eye and had hated herself for the risk and loved the boy for it in the same breath.

"You gave him the atlas," Rosalind said. His voice was raw. "Margaret. You gave him—"

"I gave him a *location*." She took his hands. They were cold. "There is a difference between a thing and the place where a thing was. Edmund taught me that. He kept the real atlas in the one place no soldier would ever think to look, because it was in plain sight, and he kept a decoy—no. No, that's not the truth either." She steadied herself. "Listen to me, Rosalind. Listen as if your life depends on it, because Adaeze's does."

She led him to the parlor and made him sit, and then she knelt—not easily, at her age, but she knelt—so that their eyes were level.

"Tomorrow at noon I will take Bray to the old ice house below the cliff path. There is a strongbox there. Edmund put it there himself, two years ago, when he first understood what was coming. It contains a book. It is bound in the same leather as the atlas. It has the same tooling. It has maps in it—real maps, Edmund's own hand, coastlines and soundings, weeks of work. And every marginal name in it is false. Invented. Edmund wrote them himself, from nothing, names that sound true because he was a careful man even in his lies." She watched the understanding break over Rosalind's face like a slow tide. "Bray will seize it. He will carry it to Vance in triumph. And while they are congratulating themselves on their prize, you will be moving the true atlas out of Vellamouth."

"The ice house—Margaret, if he opens it there, if he examines it—"

"He won't. Not there. A man like Bray does not open a strongbox on a windswept cliff with the prize in his hands; he carries it to his superior sealed, because a sealed prize is worth more than an examined one. Ambition is a kind of blindness, Rosalind. We are counting on it." She gripped his hands harder. "But it will only hold until they open that book and read the names. A day. Perhaps two. No more. Finch must have it before then. Do you understand? There is no second bargain. There is no tomorrow after this one."

Rosalind was silent. She watched him struggle with it—the same struggle she had watched Edmund lose and win by turns, the arithmetic of sacrifice, the terrible ledger of the heart.

"You'll be in that room with him," Rosalind said. "When he realizes—"

"I will be an old woman who was deceived by her dead husband's caution. It is even mostly true. He deceived me for years, God rest him, keeping the true atlas in the lining of my own sewing basket—" She stopped. Her breath caught. "Go. Go now, before you lose your nerve and I lose mine. Take the north path to the marsh. And Rosalind—" She caught his sleeve. "When you give it to Finch, tell him the marginalia is the whole of it. The maps are beautiful, but the names are the weapon. Edmund did not die for coastlines."

Rosalind stood. He bent and kissed her forehead, and his lips were trembling, and she put her hand against his cheek and held it there a moment, this apprentice of her husband's, this boy who had become in the space of one terrible month the son she had buried and the son she had never had.

"Margaret," he said. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong. It will go *expensively*." She smiled, and the smile cost her more than he would ever know. "That is what courage is, my dear. Not the absence of the bill. The willingness to pay it."

He went out through the garden, and the dark took him, and she stood at the window watching until the lane was empty.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in Edmund's chair with his spectacles in her lap and let the night move through the house—the tick of the case clock, the settling of beams, the sea saying its one long word over and over against the cliff. She thought about the sewing basket. She had carried the true atlas in and out of that basket for two years, had stitched linings over it, had taken her mending to the market with a revolution in the bottom of the basket, and no one—not one soldier, not one informant—had ever looked twice at an old woman's sewing.

*They never see us*, she thought. *The old women. We are furniture to them. And furniture, it turns out, is the best hiding place in the world.*

Near dawn she rose, and took from the linen press the bundle she had prepared—her good shawl, Edmund's letters, the small purse of coins that was to have bought her passage—and she dressed for noon the way a woman dresses for her own funeral, with care, and without hurry.

At half past eleven, Sergeant Bray's boots came up the path again.

Margaret Hollis folded her fear one last time, small and flat, and put it away in the drawer where it lived. Then she opened the door, and smiled at the man who had come to be deceived, and said, in the calm voice of a widow who has already won and cannot tell him so:

"You'll want your coat buttoned, Sergeant. It's a cold walk to the ice house—and I imagine you'll want to move quickly, once you see what Edmund left behind."

She picked up her shawl, and stepped out ahead of him onto the path, and led the law of the Crown down toward the cliff with the light, unhurried tread of a woman going to market.
