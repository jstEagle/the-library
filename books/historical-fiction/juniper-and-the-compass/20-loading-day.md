## 20. Loading Day

Adaeze Okonkwo had counted boats her whole life. As a girl she'd sat on the sea-wall with salt drying white on her knuckles and tallied herring smacks for coppers a fishwife paid by the score. She knew the harbor of Vellamouth the way a priest knows his psalter—by heart, by rhythm, by the particular creak each hull made against its mooring. So when she came down the cliff path on the morning of loading day and saw the *Providence* sitting low in the water before a single passenger had set foot on the quay, she stopped walking, and something in her chest went cold and quiet, like a lamp turned down to a blue bead of flame.

A ship bound for rescue should ride high. A ship that rode low was a ship already full.

She stood at the top of the path with her hands in the pockets of her father's old coat and made herself look properly, the way Rosalind looked at maps—systematically, refusing the comfort of glancing away. Three lighters were already alongside the transport, and cranes swung crates from the army warehouses out onto the quay in long, patient arcs. The crates were stenciled with the broad arrow of Crown property. Naval canvas. Shot and shell in wicker cases marked FLUID — her brother had taught her the marking once, in one of his moods of instruction, and she had retained it the way she retained everything practical and nothing ornamental. Powder. The holds of the last ship out of Vellamouth were being filled with powder.

Behind the powder, stacked in tiers and lashed under tarpaulins, were other things. Furniture. Crates stamped with the names of London merchants. Bales of wool. And along the quay's southern edge, roped off behind a line of redcoats, a mountain of trunks and boxes belonging to nobody the army had any use for—belonging, she understood slowly, to the people of Vellamouth, taken up at first light by soldiers "assisting" with the evacuation, and now sitting in a heap like evidence at an inquest.

She had counted boats all her life. This morning she counted soldiers instead, and lost her place twice because her hands were shaking.

* * *

The dockworkers gathered at the customs house by six, as they always did, though there had been no cargo but army cargo for nine days now and no work to speak of since the requisition. They gathered anyway, out of habit and because habit was the only property they had left that couldn't be entered in a ledger. Tam Broadhurst was there with his ruined lung wheezing like a bellows with a split seam. The Pell brothers, who between them owned one coat and shared it by season. Old Nan Ferris, who wasn't a dockworker at all but who came down every morning regardless, saying the quay was the only place in Vellamouth where you could still hear the truth spoken above a whisper.

Adaeze pushed through them to the edge and looked at the lighters, and the lighters looked back at her with their black holds open like mouths.

"They're loading her," she said. It came out flat. She had meant it to come out as a question.

"Aye." Tam spat into the water. "Since four. We asked if we could take a hand at the winches—there's coin in it, they said, army rates—and then when we presented ourselves they told us the berths were full of navy men."

"Navy men." Nan Ferris's laugh had no floor under it. "Half those lads couldn't find Vellamouth on a map with both hands."

"They don't need to find it," Adaeze said. "They're taking it away."

Nobody answered that. The word *away* sat among them like something dropped.

She worked anyway. That was the thing about Adaeze that Rosalind had never understood and Margaret Hollis understood completely: when the world broke, she reached for the pieces nearest to hand. There were crates to be shifted whether or not they deserved shifting—the soldiers were clumsy with shiploads, ignorant of how a hold should be filled so a vessel sat true in the water, and the naval loader, a harried lieutenant with a voice worn down to gravel, eventually waved half of them aboard the lighters just to get the morning moving. Army rates, after all. Coin was coin. And a woman who handled a crate could count what was inside it while she carried it.

So she spent the morning learning the ship by its cargo, and by noon she knew what the *Providence* was, and it was not a rescue.

Four hundred tons of naval stores, give or take—she kept the tally chalked inside her own head, columns rising like a fever chart. Coaling enough for a round voyage and a long stay at anchor besides; nobody coaled a ship for a single crossing and back. Timber. Spars. A disassembled crane. Tents by the hundredweight. And in the deepest hold, under the powder cases, baulks of dressed stone packed in straw—dressed stone, brought *to* Vellamouth, which had stone enough of its own to build a cathedral and no use whatsoever for more unless someone intended building something new.

Barracks, she thought. A depot. A garrison town with the garrison's families brought in behind it and the old families shipped off to whatever port would have them, scattered like chaff across counties that owed them nothing.

She set down the crate she was carrying, straightened her back, and looked at the low-riding ship, and the cold blue bead of flame in her chest began, very slowly, to warm.

* * *

The proclamation was read at two o'clock from the customs house steps, and Adaeze heard it standing in the third row with wet wool smell in her nostrils and another woman's child on her hip because the child's mother had fainted in the crush and been passed forward over heads like something rescued from drowning.

The reading was done by a captain whose name nobody caught, in a parade-ground voice that bounced off the warehouse walls. The embarkation of civilian passengers, it announced, would be postponed. Indefinitely. Owing to requirements of tonnage. Passengers would be notified when accommodation became available. Residents would remain within the designated area, receiving rations at the established stations, until such notification. God save the King.

The crowd received this the way a field receives hail—one breathless moment of stunned stillness, and then the damage began showing everywhere at once.

"*Postponed*—" A voice near Adaeze, a fisherman's widow named Mrs. Cade, saying the word over and over as if repetition might change its meaning. "Postponed till when? Till when, sir? Till when?"

The captain had already turned away. An ensign repeated, mechanically, that notification would be issued through proper channels.

Adaeze stood with the child on her hip—it was the Pell brothers' youngest, a boy of four with his thumb in his mouth—and watched the arithmetic happen in the faces around her. She was good at arithmetic herself; it was the family failing, she and Rosalind both, though he did his with compasses and she did hers with calluses. Postponed indefinitely meant not postponed. It meant never, dressed in a uniform. The ship was full of their furniture and the army's powder, and the people of Vellamouth were to stand in ration lines in their own streets, on their own ground that was no longer theirs, until the paperwork of their disappearance was complete. Every day of postponement was a day the ledger could be amended. A day someone's name could be struck, or moved, or quietly misfiled into the great nowhere where the Crown put things it did not wish to look at.

The child on her hip began, softly, to cry. She didn't shush him. She found she hadn't the authority; some part of her wanted to cry with him and only discipline kept her face from it.

"Put him down, love," said Nan Ferris beside her, gently. "Your arms are shaking."

"My arms are fine."

"They are. But your shoulders are up around your ears, and the boy feels it." Nan took the child and held him against her shawl. "Go home, Adaeze. Eat something. There's no battle here today."

But there was, and Adaeze knew it, and she suspected Nan Ferris knew it too, and that was why the old woman had said *go home*—because battles were for the young, and Nan had seen enough of them to know the shape of one forming. The crowd wasn't dispersing. It was thickening. Word was running up the hill lanes and back down again faster than any official channel could move: *postponed, indefinitely, the ship is full, they've loaded the ship with everything but us.* Men came down from the ropewalk with the tar still on their hands. Women came with aprons still floury. By four o'clock the quay held most of the town's remaining population, pressed against the rope barriers, staring at the ship that was to have saved them and was instead sailing away with their beds in its belly.

And the soldiers along the barrier had fixed bayonets. Nobody remarked on it aloud. Everybody saw it.

* * *

Rosalind found her there at dusk, working by then not at cargo but at the small stubborn labor of re-stacking the confiscated luggage mountain so that the trunks at the bottom wouldn't be crushed—the soldiers had simply hurled things, and Mrs. Cade's sea chest had already cracked a plank, and somebody had to keep the town's belongings from being destroyed by neglect before they were stolen by right of conquest. He stood at the edge of her work with his bandaged hand held against his chest and his face gray with a day's worth of something he clearly wasn't going to tell her about.

"Don't say it," she said, without looking up.

"I haven't said anything."

"You've said it with your eyebrows. Go home, Roz. You shouldn't be down here after the curfew horn. Your name's not on any list worth the paper."

"Neither is yours."

That landed harder than he could have known, and she let a trunk drop the last inch onto the pile rather than answer it gently. "My name's on one list," she said. "One. Out of the whole book of Vellamouth. Do you know what that means, brother of mine? It means when the arithmetic is finally done, I'm supposed to climb aboard alone and watch this quay from the stern rail while you and Margaret wave from behind a rope. That's the Crown's plan for the Okonkwo family. One name, alphabetized, filed, and shipped."

"Adaeze—"

"And now there's no ship at all." She rounded on him, and the words came up out of her like water finding a breach. "Do you understand what happened today? Because I do. I've been carrying their cargo all morning and the cargo talked to me. Coal for a long stay. Stone for building. Tents for four hundred men. That isn't an evacuation, Rosalind. That's a founding. They are founding a new town on top of ours and they mean for us to evaporate—slowly, in ration lines, one amended ledger entry at a time, until nobody's left to say there ever was a Vellamouth of fishing families and rope-walkers and children who could swim before they could write. They postponed us forever. They postponed us the way you postpone a funeral until everyone who loved the dead man has forgotten his face."

Rosalind was quiet a long moment. Around them the quay emptied by degrees, the crowd sullenly draining up the hill lanes under the eyes of the pickets, the lamps coming on one by one along the mole with their old unbothered regularity. Somewhere out in the roadstead the buoy bell clinked, keeping time for a world that didn't care.

"There's a way to fight it," he said at last, very low. "I can't tell you what yet. But it's real, Ade. It has weight and names and it's nearly ready. Can you give me a few more days?"

She looked at her brother—at the ink stains and the bandage and the terrible earnestness he'd inherited from a dead man—and felt the two halves of her life grind against each other: the sister who wanted to shake him until he ran for the hills, and the dockworker who had spent all day moving crates of powder and knew exactly how much force was stored in small sealed things.

"I've given you weeks," she said. "Everyone in this town has been giving somebody a few more days since the redcoats came. But all right. Days. And meanwhile I'm not going home, Rosalind, and you can't make me. Tomorrow morning I'll be on this quay with every worker who'll stand with me, and we're going to be very slow. Slow as syrup in January. Crates go astray. Slings develop faults. Winchmen find their orders confusing. If they want their depot built on our bones, they can build it at our pace—which is no pace at all."

"That's dangerous."

"So is standing still in a ration line waiting to be erased." She picked up the next trunk, and her voice gentled despite itself, because that was the treachery of loving someone. "Days, brother. I'll buy you your days with slow winches and stupid questions. But if your way doesn't open soon, mine will. I can feel it coming up the hill lanes already, same as you can. All these people went home tonight, but they didn't go home to sleep. They went home to talk in kitchens with the shutters closed. And tomorrow there'll be more of them, and fewer of them patient."

* * *

She stayed past curfew—her own quay, her own risk—and walked the length of the mole in the dark to look at the ship one last time before the sentries moved her on.

The *Providence* lay at her anchors with riding lights burning, low and heavy and patient. Beyond her, out in the black roadstead, other lights rode at anchor: the transports that had arrived with the occupation, empty now of troops and waiting, Adaeze had assumed, to carry Vellamouth's people to resettlement. Tonight, watching them, a different thought arrived, the way the worst thoughts always do—plainly, in her own voice, wearing the clothes of common sense.

Those ships weren't waiting to carry anyone anywhere. Those ships were the reason the *Providence* was full. The evacuation had ended the day it began; everything since was theater performed for a town that was not permitted to leave the stage. The postponement was not a delay in the plan. It was the plan, arriving at its final page.

She stood at the end of the mole with the wind pulling strands of hair loose from their pins and the buoy bell clinking its idiot patience, and she let herself feel all of it at once—the despair coming up through the soles of her boots from the planks her grandfather had laid, and the rage rising through it like fire through a floor. Despair was the tide and rage was what floated on it, and tomorrow morning she would bring the rage to work.

Behind her, boots on stone. A patrol, coming to move her along. She turned—

—and stopped, because it wasn't a patrol. It was young Jem Pell, running barefoot down the mole with a shuttered lantern bobbing in his fist and his face white as the foam, and when he reached her he grabbed her arm hard enough to bruise and gasped out the news that was already, even now, running uphill through the dark lanes faster than any soldier could chase it:

The ration stores. The army had seized the ration stores that afternoon—quietly, while the town watched the empty ship—and word was the lists posted at the station gates in the morning would decide who ate and who didn't. Same ledger. Same clerk's hand. Same alphabet that had already decided who sailed.

"Miss Okonkwo," the boy panted, "they're saying come down at first light. Everyone's saying it. Everybody's coming."
