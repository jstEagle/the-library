## 21. The Riot at the Gate

The notice went up on the customs house door at six in the morning, and by seven the whole of Vellamouth had gathered beneath it like iron filings drawn to a lodestone.

*BOARDING POSTPONED*, it said, in the same bland official hand that had decided who lived where for the past three weeks. *THE SHIP WILL TAKE ON CARGO PRIOR TO PASSENGERS. PASSENGERS ARE ADVISED TO REMAIN CALM AND AWAIT FURTHER INSTRUCTION.*

*Await further instruction.* Adaeze Okonkwo read the words twice, her arms crossed against the cold coming off the water, and felt something in her chest go very still and very hard—the stillness she had learned on the dock, the stillness that came before a rope parted under strain or a crate came down wrong. The stillness before a thing broke.

"They're not postponing anything," said Josiah Beck behind her, one of her dockhands, a big man made bigger by fear. "They're loading the army's freight first. I saw the manifests. Gunpowder before grandmothers."

"Of course they are," Adaeze said.

She had counted boats all her life. Boats arriving, boats leaving, boats that rode too high in the water because their holds were empty, boats that sat low and heavy with things worth more than the people who had carried them. She had known, since the night the transports anchored, that to the men in red coats this town was not a town at all. It was cargo awaiting reclassification.

But knowing a thing and standing in front of it were different animals entirely.

The crowd was not a crowd yet. It was four hundred separate griefs pressed shoulder to shoulder, each family clutching its papers, its bundles, its ledger-stamped permission to exist elsewhere. Mrs. Tallow with her canaries. The Priddy brothers, who hadn't spoken in nine years and now could not leave without each other. Old Nan Carrow, ninety if she was a day, wrapped in every garment she owned so as not to abandon any of them. And everywhere children—children asking when they'd board, children asking why the soldiers looked at them like that, children being told *soon* by parents who no longer believed it.

The *Perseverance* sat in the roads beyond the mole, a grey shape in grey light, taking on crates of naval stores while four hundred souls waited to learn whether they would be permitted to continue existing in some other place.

Adaeze turned from the notice. "Josiah. Get the lads together."

"The loading crew?"

"The slowest loading crew you ever saw in your life. Every knot double-tied. Every dolly wheel that squeaks, unsqueaked and then broken. If they want to load gunpowder before people, then gunpowder moves at the speed of our grief, and our grief is heavy."

Something kindled in Josiah's face—a small, dangerous light. He touched his cap and moved off through the crowd, and Adaeze watched him go and understood that she had just crossed a line she could not see behind her anymore.

It did not matter. There was nothing on the far side of the line worth less than what was on this one.

* * *

By noon the quay had become a held breath.

The soldiers had formed a cordon across the harbor gate—two ranks, bayonets fixed, faces the particular blankness of young men who have been ordered to do a thing and have not yet been asked how it feels. Behind them, the customs house. Beyond the customs house, the gangways. Beyond the gangways, the ship.

And between the cordon and the town, the people.

Sergeant Bray walked the line like a man inspecting fence posts. Adaeze knew him by sight now; everyone did. He had the gift of looking at a person the way a clerk looks at an entry he intends to strike. When his eyes passed over her, she met them flatly, and something flickered there—recognition, calculation—and moved on.

"You there." He stopped in front of the dockworkers' stretch of the crowd. "You've been assigned to the cargo crews. Why is nothing moving?"

"Ropes is wet," Josiah called back, and forty dockworkers found sudden fascination with their boots.

Bray's jaw worked. "There will be boarding," he announced, to no one and everyone. "When the ship is ready. Until then, this gate stays closed. Disperse to your quarters."

Nobody dispersed. Where would they go? Their quarters were requisitioned rooms in requisitioned houses in a requisitioned town, temporary by decree, and the only permanent thing left in Vellamouth was the queue of them standing in front of the guns.

The afternoon wore on. The tide turned. Crates went up the gangway—crates marked with the broad arrow, army stores, powder kegs handled with exquisite care by men whose own belongings had been tossed into wagons like offal. A woman near Adaeze began quietly weeping, the small unheroic crying of exhaustion, and her husband put his arm around her and stared at the soldiers as though memorizing their faces for some future reckoning.

It was the child who started it.

Adaeze never learned his name until later. He was perhaps six, the son of a fisherman lost two winters back, and he had been pressed against the front of the crowd all day, watching the ship with the fixed devotion of the very young. When the gangway came down for another load of stores, he simply walked forward—as any reasonable child would walk toward a thing he had been promised—and a corporal caught him by the collar and swung him back, harder than necessary, harder than a grown man needs to swing a six-year-old, and the boy went down on the cobbles and began to scream.

The sound did what four hours of waiting could not. It found the place in every breast where patience had been stretched thin as paper, and it tore.

"*That's a child, you bastard*—"

"He never touched him hard—"

"—her boy, let her through, let his mother through—"

The crowd surged. Not toward the soldiers, whatever the reports would later claim—not at the bayonets, not at the gate—but inward, sideways, four hundred bodies trying simultaneously to reach one fallen boy, and the soldiers, seeing movement, seeing mass, seeing the thing they had been drilled to see, leveled their muskets.

Adaeze was three rows back. She saw the muzzles come down and the world narrowed to a single clear thought: *they will fire, and we are packed so close that no one can fall.*

"HOLD THE LINE!" Bray's voice, cracking with its own alarm.

"DOWN!" Adaeze screamed, and shoved, and the front rows dropped as if a scythe had passed—

The volley cracked over their heads. Over, deliberately over—someone in command had kept just enough humanity to aim at the sky—and the ball-hum of it passed through the crowd like a physical hand, and the crowd did the only thing a terrified mass can do: it detonated in every direction at once.

* * *

Afterward, Adaeze could never assemble those minutes into order. They came back to her always as fragments, bright and wrong:

Mrs. Tallow's birdcage, open, the canaries gone up into the smoke-grey sky like scraps of yellow paper torn from a burning book.

A soldier's face, young under the brim of his shako, mouth open in a silent *oh* as the crowd broke around him—he had fired, he had fired over their heads, and he looked as though he might be sick.

Old Nan Carrow borne along like a bundle of laundry, her many garments streaming, her voice raised not in fear but fury: "*I buried a king's taxes and I'll bury you too—*"

And the boy. Always, afterward, the boy.

He had not gotten up. In the stampede he had been knocked down again—or never fully up at all—and the crowd's panic poured over him like floodwater over a stone, and Adaeze saw him, a small dark shape among the flying legs, and she did not decide anything. Decision was for slower moments. Her body simply left the ground of everything else she had been doing.

She went into the crush the way she had gone into the sea once, at fourteen, after a rope-parted crate that would have crushed Josiah's brother—straight in, elbows out, low, screaming in the voice dockworkers use in gales, a voice built to arrive through chaos: "*MAKE WAY! MAKE WAY, YOU'RE TRAMPLING A CHILD, MAKE WAY OR GOD WILL ACCOUNT IT MURDER!*"

A man's boot caught her shoulder. Someone's knee found her ribs. Hands seized her, pushed her, were pushed away. She got a fist into a coat collar and hauled herself a yard, two yards, and then she was through to the eye of the storm, where the noise fell away into a strange pocket of relative quiet, and the boy lay on the cobbles with blood on his forehead and his eyes wide open and alive, God thank God, alive, blinking up at her.

"Hullo," she said, absurdly. "Hullo, love. Can you hold on to me?"

He could. Small arms around her neck, grip like a drowning sailor's, and she got him up against her chest and stood, and stood, and the crowd saw what she held and the wave of them bent around her like water around a piling.

"It's the Okonkwo woman—"

"She's got the Priddy lad—"

"Make way for her, make way—"

For one moment—one suspended, impossible moment—the riot organized itself around her purpose. Men linked arms to shield her passage. Women pulled children out of her road. She carried the boy through the breaking crowd like something salvaged from a wreck, and the people parted, and for that moment she thought—God help her, she actually thought—*we are going to be all right. Look what we can do when one of us falls. We are going to be all right.*

Then Sergeant Bray's hand closed on her arm.

* * *

"Incitement," he said.

They had cleared a space at the foot of the customs house steps. The crowd, driven back by a second rank of soldiers, milled at bayonet's length, and the boy had been surrendered, sobbing but whole, into his mother's arms—a mother who had fought her way there from three streets off and now looked at Adaeze across the soldiers' shoulders with an expression Adaeze expected never to forget.

"Sedition. Incitement to riot. Obstruction of His Majesty's forces." Bray recited the charges like items read from a menu, and his eyes, close up, were not cruel exactly. They were worse than cruel. They were *administrative*. "You were heard ordering the crowd forward."

"I ordered them *down*," Adaeze said. "You heard me. Half your men heard me. Your own ears heard me."

"What I heard," Bray said, "was a known agitator whose dock crews have sabotaged army loading for a week, shouting in the middle of a stampede. A magistrate will sort out which version goes in the book." He nodded, and two soldiers took her arms.

"Your brother's maps won't save you here," he added, low, and she understood suddenly that this arrest was not only about today—that Bray had wanted her name in his book for weeks, and today had merely provided the page.

"His maps," Adaeze said, "are the least of your troubles, Sergeant."

He smiled without warmth and turned away, and the soldiers marched her up the steps, and the doors closed on the sound of the crowd—not a roar, not yet, but a low continuous murmur like the sea working at a seawall, patient, certain, immense.

* * *

The cell was a converted storeroom in the fort's undercroft: damp stone, a stool, a bucket, a high slit of window that admitted a bar of grey light and the smell of the sea. They took her boots—for fear she'd harm herself, they claimed, a courtesy so insulting she almost laughed—and left her alone with the arithmetic of her situation.

One name on the sailing list, and it was hers. One berth. Rosalind excluded. Margaret excluded. And now the one name struck through by a sergeant's pen, filed under incitement, and the berth presumably reassigned to some alphabetically fortunate stranger while she waited in the dark for a tribunal as summary as a hatchet.

She should be afraid. She was afraid, in the body—in the bruised ribs, in the hands that would not stop their faint tremor. But underneath the fear, in the place where her stillness lived, another thing had settled and would not be moved.

She had seen them part for her. Four hundred terrified strangers, and they had parted, and linked arms, and made a corridor of themselves for a child none of them knew. All these weeks the occupation had taught her that people were ledger entries, that neighbors became competitors the moment names became currency. And then the guns had spoken, and every one of them had remembered, all at once, what the ledger could not record: that they belonged to each other.

*Let them write incitement*, she thought, lying back on the cold stone, one arm over her eyes. *Let them write whatever fills their books.*

Somewhere above, boots crossed the yard. Somewhere beyond the walls, Rosalind was—where? Doing what? Chasing his atlas and his ink and his general's daughter through whatever shadow-war he was fighting, believing he had to save everyone, bless him, bless his foolish cartographer's heart. She had meant to tell him, when she found the words: *you cannot save everyone, little brother. You can only make sure the truth outlives the men who bury it. That is the saving. Everything else is arithmetic.*

Now the words would have to find him some other way.

Footsteps on the stair—two sets, unhurried, deliberate. Keys. The bar of light at the window had gone orange with evening, and Adaeze sat up on the stool, spine straight, hands flat on her knees, and arranged her face into the expression she wore for storms.

The lock turned.

The door opened, and framed against the lamplight of the passage stood a figure she had not expected—tall, in civilian clothes gone travel-worn, marsh-mud dried grey on his boots, holding a satchel the size of a hymnal against his chest as though it were made of glass.

Tobias Finch, the printer. The exile from the marsh.

Behind him, half in shadow, hood up, a second figure—slight, breathing fast, a smear of ink along one wrist that no honest surveyor's hour could account for at this time of day.

"Well," Adaeze said, into the dark. "Someone had better tell me quickly what my brother has done."
