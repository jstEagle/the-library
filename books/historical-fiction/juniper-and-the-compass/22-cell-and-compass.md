## 22. Cell and Compass

The guardhouse smelled of wet stone and tallow, and beneath both, faintly, of the sea—because everything in Vellamouth smelled of the sea now, Rosalind thought, even the places built to keep it out. He stood at the duty desk with his hands flat on the wood the way he had seen honest men stand, though he did not feel honest. He felt like a man carrying a stolen cathedral inside his coat.

"I'm here to see Adaeze Okonkwo," he said. "She's my sister."

The sergeant on duty—a heavy-faced man with a cold sore at the corner of his mouth—did not look up from his ledger. Of course there was a ledger. There was always a ledger now. Names in, names out, names crossed through with a stroke of a pen that had never once paused to consider what a name weighed.

"Prisoner visits are at the discretion of the garrison command," the sergeant said. "Next."

"She was arrested on the quay two days ago. For pulling a child out from under soldiers' boots." He kept his voice level. He had learned that much already: rage was a smell, and they could smell it, and they used it the way butchers used hooks. "I'd like to know whose discretion I should apply to."

That got him a look. The sergeant's eyes were small and patient, like a man who had waited all his life for someone to give him a reason. "Yours is a name that's been looked at before," he said. "Map room boy. You come here asking questions, you're doing arithmetic you can't afford."

"I'm doing addition," Rosalind said quietly. "One sister. One visit."

The silence stretched long enough that Rosalind counted the cracks in the plaster behind the desk—seven of them, branching like river systems he could have named—and then, somewhere down the corridor behind the cells, a door opened and closed, and a woman's voice said something he couldn't make out, and the sergeant sighed as if the whole world were an inconvenience personally arranged to annoy him.

"Ten minutes," he said. "Through there. No touching the bars, no passing anything, and if I hear singing or politics, it ends."

* * *

The cell was not a cell in the way Rosalind had imagined cells. It had been a storeroom once—he could tell by the iron rings where casks had been chained, by the pale rectangle on the wall where a shelf inventory had hung for thirty years and then, abruptly, hadn't. A plank bed. A bucket with a lid, which was a courtesy. One high window, barred, through which gray light fell at the angle of late morning, laying a ladder of shadow against the opposite wall.

Adaeze sat on the plank with her back straight and her wrists resting on her knees, and she was—this was the thing that stopped him in the doorway—*mending*. A soldier's sock, of all things, grey wool, needle flashing. She had found work even in a cell. His sister, who could count boats by ear in the dark, had found the one useful thing to do inside four walls and was doing it.

"You look terrible," she said without looking up.

"You should see the other side of these bars."

"I have. He's got a cold sore and poor posture." She bit the thread, tied it off, and only then raised her head. Her face was bruised along one cheekbone, yellow-green now, the color of old charts, and there was a scrape at her temple that someone had cleaned because it was too neat to have healed that way on its own. "Rosalind."

Just his name. But she said it the way she said *the tide's turning*, and it undid him more than any wailing would have. He crossed the little cell in three steps and forgot the rule about the bars because there were no bars between them, only air, and he knelt and took her hands—the needle-pricked fingers, the rope-callused palms, the hands that had hauled nets and counted transports and pulled a stranger's child out of a crush of boots—and pressed his forehead to them.

"They're saying you incited it," he said. "Sergeant came to the widow's house with paper. Incitement, obstruction, resisting—I read it three times and it doesn't say anything. It's words shaped like charges."

"It is." She freed one hand and put it on the back of his head, the way she had when he was seven and the other boys had broken his first pair of spectacles. "Rosalind. Breathe. I've been in worse rooms than this."

"When? Name one."

"The hold of the ship that brought us," she said, mild as milk. "This one has a window."

He laughed, and the laugh cracked halfway into something else, and he stayed kneeling on the cold flags with his sister's hand on his head until the worst of it passed. Through the high window he could hear the harbor: the groan of cargo being swung aboard the transport, the endless rhythmic shouting of men loading crates that would never feed anyone in Vellamouth.

"Margaret sends—" He stopped. He had brought nothing. They had searched him at the door, patted his ribs and turned his pockets out, taken even his pencil. "Margaret thinks about you constantly. She's recovering. Her hands are bandaged but she made me promise to tell you she'll be able to knead dough again by month's end, and that when you're out we're all having proper bread, none of this requisition flour."

"Requisition flour builds character."

"So does prison, apparently."

Her mouth twitched. Then it settled, and he watched the humor drain out of her face the way water drains from a lock, deliberately, to let something heavier through.

"How is the atlas?" she asked.

The question landed strangely. He had expected *how do we get me out*. "It's—it's safe. Margaret moved it again after the fire. It's somewhere even I don't know."

"Good. Keep it that way." She picked the mending back up, needing her hands busy for what came next; he knew that habit of hers, had catalogued it across twenty years of sharing a life. "Listen to me now, because I've had two days and nothing to do but think, and thinking is the one cargo they didn't confiscate."

"I'm listening."

"You're trying to save everyone." She said it without accusation, which somehow made it land harder. "You've been trying since the proclamation went up. Berths for three, then berths for Margaret, then berths for the dock children, then justice for the whole town, all of it balanced on your shoulders and Elowen Vance's conscience and whatever Finch can print. Brother. You cannot carry a whole town."

"Somebody has to—"

"*Nobody* has to. That's the trap." The needle paused. "Do you know what I did wrong on the quay? Nothing. That's what the charge sheet can't say plainly, so it says everything else. I saw a child going under and I went in after her, and the crowd moved like crowds move, and a corporal decided that was sedition. And sitting here, do you know what I've worked out? If I had saved every soul on that quay, the ledger would still say what it says. The ship would still be loading army pork instead of people. Saving isn't the same as changing. Saving is what love does when it's losing."

Rosalind sat back on his heels. The gray light had shifted; the ladder of shadow on the wall had climbed one rung.

"And changing?" he asked.

"Changing needs the truth to be somewhere they can't reach it. Not hidden—" she jabbed the needle toward him, precisely, like marking a point on a chart— "*published*. Hollis understood that. Why else write down every family, every town, every date? He wasn't keeping a grudge. He was building a case that would still be true after he was dead. And you—" her voice gentled and sharpened at once, the way only hers could— "you keep treating that book like a treasure to be guarded. It's not a treasure, Rosalind. It's a *verdict*. Verdicts don't work in drawers."

"The printer's ready. Finch is ready. But the original has to survive—if they burn it, the pamphlets are just claims—"

"Then stop spending yourself on getting me out." She set the mending down and took his face in both hands, forcing his eyes to hers. Hers were their mother's eyes; he had not let himself notice that in years, and noticing it now felt like a hand on an old wound. "Hear me. I am fed. I am dry. The corporal who arrested me has been reassigned because half the dockworkers suddenly became very slow and very stupid about which crate goes where, and the garrison knows it and can't prove it, and that is *my* work, done from inside this room, by telling the right person the right thing through the guard who brings the water. I am not helpless in here and I will not be helpless waiting. What I cannot survive is you hanging for me. Or drowning with me. Or standing on that quay when it sails, holding nothing but good intentions."

"Adaeze—"

"Finish the atlas." Flat. Final. The voice she used for counting boats in bad weather. "Every plate. Every margin. Every name. Make it so complete, so exact, that when it's printed, no magistrate in three counties can pretend he didn't read it. You're the last hand that touched it that loved it before it loved us back. Do you understand? The book is how we get out. Not the ship. The *book*. Ships go where they're pointed. Truth goes where it's needed."

He stared at her. Something was rearranging itself in him, some load shifting in the hold, and for a moment he couldn't speak because the relief of it was almost humiliating. He had spent weeks framing the problem as arithmetic—one berth, three berths, three hundred berths, a ledger of small lives measured against a ship with finite space—and she had just, in eleven sentences, dissolved the entire equation. The ship was the Crown's escape route. It went where the Crown pointed. But a printed page went anywhere. It fit through keyholes. It survived its author. It could not be requisitioned, because the moment it was read it belonged to whoever held it, and everyone who read it held it.

"They'll want the original," he said slowly. "Bray especially. He'll trade real berths for it."

"Then sell him something," Adaeze said, and smiled, and it was their mother's smile too, and slightly terrifying. "You're a mapmaker, brother. You've been drawing things that aren't quite the world your whole life. Every coast is a decision about what to include."

* * *

He walked out of the guardhouse into a morning he experienced as a changed instrument. Same fog, same fish-stink, same redcoats stamping feeling back into their feet outside the requisitioned customs house—but he heard it differently now, the way a man hears a piece of music after someone shows him the score. Every posted notice was a lie with good penmanship. Every ledger line was a sentence awaiting appeal. And somewhere across the town, in whichever hiding place Margaret had chosen, the verdict sat wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for the last hand that loved it.

Elowen was waiting for him at the corner by the chandler's, hood up, looking for all the world like a woman buying soap. The sight of her produced in him the usual complicated chord—warmth, wariness, guilt about the warmth, resentment about the guilt—and today, beneath it all, something new: he was no longer afraid of what she might cost him. He had a use for her, or rather she had offered herself to a purpose, and purpose simplified everything it touched.

"You were longer than ten minutes," she said.

"My sister is persuasive."

"Is she all right?"

"She's mending a soldier's sock and running a slow-down strike from a storeroom." He found he was smiling. "She's better than all right. She's given me orders."

Elowen studied his face. "Orders I can help with?"

"Maybe." He lowered his voice. The street had ears; it had grown ears overnight two months ago and never shed them. "How soon could you get me into the fort archive again? Properly. With papers. Enough time to work."

"That depends what you'd work on."

"A finished map," Rosalind said. "The most finished map anyone in this county has ever seen."

She looked at him a long moment, and he watched her arrive at the edge of understanding and step past it—she was quick, quicker than the army deserved—and what crossed her face then was not fear. It was the expression of a woman deciding, once again, on which side of the ink she wished to stand.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Survey of the powder magazine. Bray will insist on escorting me, which means Bray will be watching me, which means, Mr. Okonkwo, you will have exactly forty minutes and one chance."

"Forty minutes," Rosalind repeated, and thought of the empty plates waiting at Margaret's hiding place, and of Adaeze's needle flashing in the gray light, sewing even captivity into something useful. "That's enough. It was always enough. I was just measuring with the wrong instrument."

Behind them, out on the gray water, the transport's winches groaned on, loading pork for an army, while the tide—patient, indifferent, faithful—began, at last, to turn.
