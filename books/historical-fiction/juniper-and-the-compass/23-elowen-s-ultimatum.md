## 23. Elowen's Ultimatum

The summons came at an hour designed to feel like a sentence.

Elowen Vance was still in her riding coat, one glove off, when the orderly appeared at the door of her quarters and did not quite salute. He had been told not to salute her, she understood. Someone above her father's rank—or below it, which amounted to the same thing in matters of pride—had decided that a daughter under suspicion should be addressed with the neutral courtesy one extends to a stranger whose name appears on a list.

"The General will see you in his study, Captain."

*Captain.* The word sat strangely on her now, the way a borrowed cloak sits—heavy at the shoulders, wrong at the hem. She had worn the rank for six years without once thinking about how it sounded. Now she heard it in every mouth like an accusation rehearsed.

She followed the orderly through the fort's inner corridor, past the map room where a sentry stood who had not stood there three weeks ago, past the quartermaster's office with its new bars on the windows. The garrison was closing around its own secrets like a fist. She noted each change with the part of her mind that had been trained to note things—the part that had corrected Rosalind Okonkwo's coastlines by candlelight and felt, even then, a shame she could not name—and she kept her face as level as a horizon.

Her father's study smelled of lamp oil and cold tea. General Augustus Vance stood at the window with his back to the door, hands clasped behind him in the posture he assumed for bad news, inspections, and funerals. He did not turn when she entered. He let the silence work on her first, as he had done since she was a girl, as if patience were a weapon he alone had thought to load.

"You went to the marshes on Tuesday last," he said.

Not *good morning*. Not *sit down*. Her father believed in opening with the blade.

"I conducted a hydrographic survey of the eastern channels," she said. "As ordered. The approaches to the quay silt badly after the spring tides; you wanted soundings."

"I have seen your soundings." He turned then. He was not an old man, but the requisition had aged him in ways she suspected were deliberate—a performance of gravity for the benefit of his officers, a stooping into the role of the man history required him to be. On his desk lay a folder, and she knew before he touched it what the folder held. She had known from the moment she passed the barred windows. "Or rather, I have seen a copy of your soundings. In a hand that is not yours, annotated in a manner you did not authorize, found among the papers of a man awaiting trial for sedition."

The floor did not drop away. She had imagined this moment too many times in too many sleepless hours for it to have that power over her. Instead the room simply narrowed, the way a channel narrows before a weir, and she felt herself moving toward the drop with terrible, unhurried certainty.

"Which papers?" she asked. It was the only question left worth asking. If they had taken the decoy atlas—the beautifully wrong thing Rosalind had planted—then the survey she gave him was still hidden. If they had found the real survey, then everything was already lost, and this interview was merely the formality that preceded rope.

"The private survey," her father said, and watched her face as he said it. "The one drawn in my own hand. The one showing the naval depot plans. The permanent disposition." He let the words sit in the air between them like coins on a table. "Found copied out, in your hand, folded inside a chart of the Vellamouth estuary in the possession of Rosalind Okonkwo."

So. Bray's search had gone deeper than she'd calculated, or the boy's hiding place had been less clever than either of them hoped. Her heart performed a single hard knock against her ribs and then went quiet, the way it had gone quiet once before, at her mother's funeral, when grief had been too large to make noise.

"May I see it?"

"You may not."

"Then may I ask how it came into Sergeant Bray's possession? Because I would like to know whether my correspondence is now subject to search, or only—" she paused, choosing the word with care, "—only my associations."

Something moved behind her father's eyes. Disappointment, perhaps. Or the flicker of a man recognizing, unwillingly, his own stubbornness in someone else's face. She had inherited more than his jaw and his habit of standing at windows. She had inherited the refusal to flinch, and both of them knew it, and it made everything between them harder.

"Sit down, Elowen."

She sat. It seemed important, suddenly, to give him nothing else to complain of—not posture, not tone, not the insolence of remaining upright while he arranged whatever it was he had arranged in his mind. She folded her gloved hand over her bare one and waited.

He remained standing. He picked up the folder, weighed it, set it down again. Outside the window the harbor lay grey and flat, the evacuation ship riding at anchor like a patient animal, and beyond it the transports, and beyond those the sea going on to everywhere else in the world that was not this room.

"Do you know," he said, "what I was doing at your age? I was in the Coromandel, surveying rivers that no Englishman had named. I slept in swamps. I buried two sergeants of fever and drew charts through the fevers of three more. And every chart I drew went into a drawer in Calcutta, and every drawer fed a ledger, and every ledger fed the Board, and the Board fed the Admiralty, and I understood—I understood even then, girl, because I am not a fool—that the drawing was never innocent. A map is a claim. Ink is annexation. I have known since I was twenty-six years old exactly what my profession is for."

"Then why did you stay in it?"

"Because it is *for* something." His voice hardened, and she heard under the hardness the other thing, the frightened thing. "Because empires are built by men willing to know what their work means and do it anyway, and undone by sentimentalists who learn the same lesson late and imagine that learning it absolves them. You have learned my lesson thirty years early, Elowen, and you think that makes you better than me. It does not. It makes you *early*. And being early, in matters of conscience, is only another way of being alone."

She thought of Rosalind's hand, bound and bleeding, the night she had knelt on a rooftop and tied the bandage with fingers that would not stop shaking. She had not been alone then. That was the whole of it. That was the entire treason of her heart, reduced to a single fact: she had knelt in the dark with an enemy of the Crown and had felt, for the first time since childhood, that she stood on ground that would bear her weight.

"The survey was leaked," her father said. "That fact is not in dispute. The copy exists; the hand is identified; the chain of custody begins at your desk and ends in the effects of a seditionist. What remains in dispute"—and here he came around the desk at last, and sat opposite her, and leaned forward with his forearms on his knees in the attitude of a man negotiating, which was worse than the window, far worse—"is what we do about it."

"What *we* do," she repeated.

"There are two roads from this room, and I am going to describe both of them, and then you are going to choose, because I have spent four days arranging for there to be a choice at all, and you will not waste my work by refusing to look at it."

She said nothing. Her bare hand had gone cold inside the gloved one.

"Road one." He held up a finger. "Tomorrow morning, you come to me and tell me that you discovered the leak—that you traced it to Okonkwo yourself, that you allowed him access to your materials in order to confirm his guilt, and that your delay in reporting arose from your desire to identify the full extent of his network. You sign a statement to that effect. You repeat it at his tribunal. You say the words *I believed him to be a foreign agent*, or some formulation the advocate will draft for you, and you say them clearly enough that the panel believes you." He lowered the finger slowly, watching her. "In exchange, the matter ends. No court-martial. Your rank intact, your commission intact, your name intact. Okonkwo hangs or transports regardless—you understand that; the decoy atlas has already condemned him in Bray's report, and Bray's report does not unmake itself—but *you* walk out of this clean."

"And road two."

"Road two," her father said, and now his voice dropped, and she heard in it something she had almost never heard there: exhaustion. "You say nothing. You stand mute, or worse, you stand up in that courtroom and do what I know you are imagining doing, Elowen, because I raised you and I know exactly how your mind works at three in the morning. And then the tribunal concludes what the evidence already says: that Captain E. Vance, of the Corps of Royal Engineers, wilfully transmitted classified military intelligence to a person charged with sedition, during a state of requisition, in wartime conditions as defined by the order in council. And you are tried alongside him. Not after. *Alongside.* The same dock, the same panel, the same morning's work. And whatever they give him, they give you a portion of, because you are an officer and officers are judged more severely, not less. You will lose the rank. You will very likely lose the years. And I—" his voice caught, barely, a hairline fracture across stone— "I will have to preside over the finding, because I cannot recuse myself without explaining why, and the explanation is the crime."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere outside, a sentry's boots measured the parade ground, back and forth, back and forth, keeping time like something mechanical, like something that had never once been asked whether it wished to march.

Elowen looked at her father—at the lines around his eyes that she had memorized as a child resting her cheek against his shoulder; at the folder on the desk that contained, folded in her own careful hand, the shape of her ruin—and she found that she was not angry. That was the strangest part. She had expected anger, had armored herself for it, and instead she felt only a vast and weary tenderness, because she could see now what he could not say: that this offer, this ugly forked bargain, was the largest act of love his imagination could perform. He had spent four days building her a door out of a burning house, and he genuinely could not comprehend why she might prefer the fire, if the fire meant she did not have to walk through it carrying a lie.

"Papa," she said quietly, using the word she had not used since she was twelve, and saw him go still. "Do you remember the summer you taught me to take bearings? I was nine. We were on the headland and you put the compass in my hands and you said, *the needle doesn't lie, but the mapmaker can*. Do you remember saying that?"

"That was a lesson about instrument error."

"It was a lesson about everything." She unfolded her hands. "You taught me that the truth is in the instrument, and the lie is in the hand that holds it. And then you built your whole life on the lie, and now you're asking me to hold the compass crooked so that your life stays standing. I can't do it. I'm sorry. I know precisely what it costs you to offer me road one. I know you believe you're saving me. But you taught me to read the needle, and the needle points somewhere you don't want to go."

"It points to a gallows," he said flatly.

"It points north," she said. "What stands north is not my doing."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Through the window the light was failing over the water, the tide turning, the great ship swinging slowly on its moorings until its bows pointed, briefly and meaninglessly, toward the open sea. She thought of the town behind her, the shuttered shops, the ration queue she had walked past that morning with her eyes fixed forward, the names in Hollis's margins that she had read by candlelight in a marsh printer's back room while Margaret Hollis held the lamp with steady old hands. *Every family the Crown has displaced.* Her father's signature was at the bottom of three of those displacement orders. She had looked at it a long time—that confident descending hand, the small flourish after the final *e*—until Margaret had gently covered it with her palm, as though the ink could wound.

"Four days," her father said at last, rising, and the negotiation was over and the sentencing had begun, though neither of them named it. "I have kept this out of Bray's hands for four days, telling him I wished to handle the matter internally, and I can keep it for perhaps one day more. You will give me your answer tomorrow at this hour. If your answer is yes, you will sign the statement and I will burn this folder myself, and we will never speak of any of it again, and in ten years you will thank me. If your answer is no—" He stopped. He straightened his spine with the effort of a man lifting something invisible. "If your answer is no, then you will leave this study knowing that I love you, and that I will sit in that chair tomorrow week anyway, and that neither of us will survive it intact, and that I would rather have lost you to the Coromandel fever than to this."

"I know," she said softly. "That's what makes it unbearable. For both of us."

She rose. She crossed to the door. Her hand was on the latch when he spoke again, and his voice had changed—it was quieter, and older, and it belonged to the man on the headland with a compass in a child's hands rather than to the general at the window.

"He'll hang, Elowen. Whatever you choose. The tribunal has the forged atlas, Bray's testimony, the pattern of association. One captain's word either way won't move that stone." A pause, and then, so low she nearly missed it: "Unless the word is accompanied by paper. Unless it is *numbers*, read aloud, in the room, from a document the panel cannot call forged because it carries my seal and my hand. Then the stone moves. Then everything moves."

She turned. He was facing the window again, his back to her, and she understood suddenly that this entire hour had been a map drawn in mirror-writing—that the ultimatum was real, and the threat was real, and underneath both, in the marginalia where her father hid everything that mattered, he had just told her how to destroy him.

He had shown her the weakness in his own coastline. He had handed his daughter the sounding line and turned his back.

"Good night, Papa," she said.

"Good night, Captain."

The corridor outside was dark and cold. She walked it slowly, her boot heels striking stone in a rhythm like a heartbeat, and by the time she reached the outer door she had stopped shaking and started planning. There was a copy of her father's private survey still hidden beneath the floor of a dead mapmaker's house, guarded by a widow with burned hands. There was a printer in the marshes with ink on his sleeves and a press that had been waiting thirty years for exactly this. And there was a cell in the guardhouse where a young man with an injured hand sat waiting for a tribunal that intended to erase him.

Tomorrow she would give her father her answer, and it would be the answer he had already read in her face tonight, the one he had spent four days trying to prevent and one evening, at the last, teaching her to deliver.

She stepped out into the wind coming off the harbour, and turned—not toward her quarters, but toward the town, walking fast now, a soldier's stride, a traitor's compass bearing—north.
