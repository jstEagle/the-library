## 24. The Forged Chart

The forgery took him four days, and it was the finest work of his life.

That was the bitter joke of it—four days and eleven years of training, all of Hollis's patient instruction in the weight of a line and the honesty of a scale, poured into the creation of one beautiful lie. Rosalind worked on it at Margaret's kitchen table by daylight, when the map room was watched, and again at night behind drawn curtains, and each hour he spent on it he felt like a man teaching his hands to betray everything they knew.

Because that was the trick. A bad forgery was easy; any fool could draw a wrong coastline. But Bray was no fool. If the decoy atlas was to be believed—if Sergeant Bray was to seize it with the triumph of a man who had finally caught the thing he'd been hunting—it had to be *good*. It had to bear Rosalind's own hand unmistakably, because that was half the evidence: apprentice Okonkwo, seditious cartographer, caught with the proof in his possession. And it had to contain errors so carefully placed that only someone who already knew what to look for would ever find them.

He started with paper. The real atlas lived inside the sealed map room still, hidden in its old place beneath the false bottom of the chart drawer that held Hollis's coastal surveys of the northern approaches—the one place in Vellamouth Bray's men had searched three times and found nothing, because soldiers searched for hiding places and never for ordinariness. The real atlas was bound in grey boards, sewn with linen thread, its pages foxed at the margins from twenty years of damp. Rosalind could not match the foxing exactly. He could age paper with tea and smoke and a cold oven, but foxing had its own weather, its own slow biography. So he did not try. Instead he made the new pages *slightly too clean*, just enough that a suspicious man would notice, and then he gave Bray something better than authenticity: certainty.

Certainty, he had learned from watching the clerk alter the census ledger, was what corrupt men bought most eagerly. A forger's greatest ally was not skill but desire.

* * *

The errors were the heart of it.

He began with the displacement figures themselves. In the true atlas, Hollis's marginalia recorded four hundred and eleven families erased across three towns—Thornmere, Ashcombe, and Vellamouth—with dates, acreages, and the names of the commissioners who had signed each order. Rosalind copied the structure faithfully: the same cramped marginal hand, the same abbreviations, even reproducing a blot where Hollis's pen had rested too long on the page in March of 1844. He copied four hundred and eleven families.

Then he made the numbers *wrong*.

Not obviously wrong. Wrong the way a man wrongs things when he is frightened and hurried—which is precisely how Bray would expect a fugitive copyist to be wrong. He inflated the Thornmere figures past credibility, to a number that would invite ridicule rather than outrage. He misdated two of the Ashcombe orders by nearly a decade, an error Elowen—*no*, he corrected himself, an error *any trained surveyor* would catch instantly, because the commission seals on those pages belonged to a board not yet constituted in the year given. And in the Vellamouth section, the section that mattered most, he attached the displaced families' names to the wrong parcels of land—a mistake invisible to anyone who didn't know the town, and glaring to anyone who did. To Bray. To the general. To the syndicate of land-men whose signatures Hollis had traced from refugee testimony and harbor records.

Let them seize it. Let them parade it before the tribunal as evidence of sedition. And let some clever counsel—one of the reformist magistrates Finch had written to, or Elowen herself, if it came to that—turn the decoy over in the light of a courtroom and find every seam showing. The book meant to hang Rosalind Okonkwo would become the instrument that proved the *real* figures existed somewhere, in a truer hand, and that the Crown's men had been fool enough to congratulate themselves on a counterfeit.

A map was an argument, Hollis used to say. You did not argue with a map; you argued *through* it. Rosalind was learning what his master had never taught him: that the same was true of a lie.

* * *

On the fourth night, Margaret sat across from him while he worked the final leaf, her bandaged hands folded on the table. The burns along her forearms had begun to heal into pale ridges, and she refused the laudanum the surgeon had left, saying it made her dreams crowded. She watched him lay the last wash of sepia along a fictitious inlet and said nothing for a long while.

"You've given the Ashcombe seals to the wrong commission," she said at last.

"Yes."

"Deliberately."

"Deliberately." He blew dry the ink. "It has to be findable. If they never discover the flaws, the forgery works too well, and then the real atlas becomes worthless—the moment they have this one, they'll stop looking for it. The flaws are the message. They're a letter to whoever examines it closely enough to ask questions."

Margaret turned one of the loose sheets toward her, studying the marginal hand, the imitation of her husband's cramped script. Her face did something complicated. Grief and admiration and a kind of horror, all passing over her like weather.

"Aldous spent twenty years making his lines true," she said quietly. "And here you are in four days making them beautifully false. I don't know whether to bless you or bar the door against you."

"He'd have done it himself," Rosalind said. "If he'd had time. That's why they killed him—he understood that a map in the wrong hands is a weapon, and he wouldn't let go of it."

"No," Margaret said. "He'd have refused. That was Aldous. He'd have died before he drew one line that wasn't honest." She looked up at him, and her eyes were very steady. "Which is why it falls to you, I suppose. He was a man of truth, Rosalind. You're becoming something rarer. A man of truth who can lie."

He didn't know what to say to that, so he said nothing, and dusted the finished leaf with pounce, and closed the grey boards over it—boards he had stained and scuffed and rubbed with tallow to approximate twenty years of handling—and tied the whole in the oilcloth wrapping he had seen in his mind a hundred times since the night he'd first hidden the atlas away.

* * *

Planting it was the simplest and worst part.

Simplest, because he had walked the map room's shadows since he was thirteen years old and knew the creak of every floorboard the way other men knew their mother's lullabies. Worst, because the room was now guarded, and because the planting required him to do the one thing he had sworn never to do: touch the real atlas and let the two books exist in the same room at once.

He went on a Tuesday, in the grey hour before dawn, using the route through the yard behind the chandler's that Adaeze's dockworkers kept clear of sentries—a debt he intended never to mention aloud, because mentioning it would endanger it. The lock on the map room door had been changed after the fire scare; it took him nine minutes with picks fashioned from clock springs, kneeling in the cold with his breath smoking and every bell in his body tolling *wrong, wrong, wrong.* Nine minutes was too long. Nine minutes was a lifetime, if the patrol came early.

Inside, the room smelled of char still, faintly, under the ink and the linseed. One corner of shelving was blackened where a lantern had burst during the burning of the archive, and the scar ran down the wall like a vein of coal in chalk. His hand remembered the burn blisters on Margaret's palms and he stood very still for a moment, letting the anger settle into something cold and usable.

The real atlas came out from under the false bottom of the northern surveys drawer lighter than he expected—or perhaps his arms had grown stronger with everything they now carried. Grey boards. Foxed margins. Hollis's hand inside, dead these many months, waiting. He wrapped the forgery in the oilcloth and slid it into the hiding place, and for a moment he held both books, one in each arm, and felt like a ferryman between two worlds: the world where the town was erased cleanly, and the world where it wasn't.

*Then neither,* he thought. *Then both.* Then let the state take the beautiful lie and strangle itself with it.

He set the real atlas inside his coat, against his chest, where its corners pressed his ribs with every heartbeat, and he left the map room the way water leaves a basin—through the lowest gap, without a ripple—and locked the door behind him with Bray's new key, which he had waxed a mold of ten days ago and cut himself, and which was, he reflected as he slipped into the alley, the single most criminal object he had ever owned.

* * *

Bray took the bait in thirty-one hours.

Rosalind learned of it the way a playwright learns the audience has laughed—from the gallery, at a distance, unable to see his own work but hearing everything. He was at the ration line on the quay, standing with his tin cup and his daily loaf among the shuffling hundreds, when two redcoats came down Coppergate at a pace that made shopkeepers pull their children in doorways, and the word ran ahead of them the way word always ran in Vellamouth, faster than any horse: *they've found it, they've found it, the sergeant's found the book.*

By noon the whole town knew. By afternoon Rosalind, standing in the crowd that had gathered outside the guardhouse under pretense of waiting for the evening ration announcement, watched them bring the atlas out under escort—an honor guard, practically, four soldiers and a lieutenant and Sergeant Bray walking beside the oilcloth bundle as if it were a coffin at a state funeral. And Bray's face—

Rosalind would remember Bray's face for the rest of his life.

It was not triumph exactly, or not only. It was the look of a man who had carried a splinter in his palm for months and finally gripped it with the tweezers. Relief and hunger together, and beneath them something almost tender, the way a hunter is tender with a trail that has cost him blood. Bray's eyes went over the crowd as he climbed into the waiting cart—over Rosalind, specifically, and lingered, one professional animal acknowledging another across a distance of forty feet—and Bray smiled.

It was not a cruel smile. That was the terrible part. It was the smile of a man doing his job well, and Rosalind understood, watching the cart roll away toward the fort with four hundred and eleven beautifully wrong families inside it, that Sergeant Bray genuinely believed he had just saved the army from a conspiracy, that he was the honest instrument of an honest crown, and that history would record him—if it recorded him at all—as the diligent officer who recovered the seditious atlas before it could poison the public mind.

*History will record you differently,* Rosalind thought. *I'm writing the margins.*

* * *

But fear is not a thing you can forge, and it found him that night.

He sat in Margaret's kitchen with the shutters closed and the fire banked low, and the calm he had worn all day like a borrowed coat slid off him all at once. His hands shook. Not gently—violently, the way they had shaken at fifteen when he'd nearly drowned under the ice off the north spit, the body remembering after the mind had moved on that it had gambled its existence and lost the toss and somehow kept breathing anyway. He had handed Sergeant Bray a book. Bray had taken it. Between those two facts stretched every court-martial in the empire, every cell, every dark transport ship carrying men who displeased the Crown to places with no maps at all.

And worse than the fear was the arithmetic underneath it. The tribunal had been announced for Friday—summary proceedings, three officers, no jury, no appeal. Which meant the decoy had perhaps two days to do its work. Two days for the forged errors to be found, or not found. Two days for Bray to examine the marginal hand he had studied for months and fail to notice—or succeed in noticing—that the blots were right but the rhythm was wrong, that Hollis's pen strokes leaned a hair more to the east, that the ink of the marginalia in the true atlas was iron-gall mixed with gum arabic in a ratio Rosalind could taste on his tongue but had not quite matched, because the true recipe had died with its maker.

There were a dozen threads and any one of them, pulled, unraveled everything.

"They'll cross-examine whoever presents it," Margaret said from the doorway. She had heard his silence, the way she always did. "And examination cuts both ways. Once they start asking questions about dates and seals in open session—"

"—they start asking questions about dates and seals," Rosalind finished. "Yes. Every flaw is a door, and doors open from both sides."

"And if Bray finds the flaws first? Privately?"

"Then I hang for sedition with a forged book as evidence, and the real atlas dies in a drawer." He looked at his shaking hands and made them stop, by will, the way you stop a clock by holding the pendulum. "So we must make sure he doesn't examine it privately. We need him proud. Pride doesn't check its work."

Margaret came and sat down across from him. For a while neither spoke. The banked fire ticked. Somewhere out in the fog-bound streets a patrol's boots went by, unhurried, masters of everything.

"There's one thing you haven't considered," she said at last, and there was something in her voice he couldn't read—a care taken with the words, stones crossed on ice. "The tribunal. They'll want expert testimony on the document. Someone who knows surveying. Someone close to the command who can certify the atlas as genuine Hollis work." She paused. "They'll call Captain Vance."

The name landed in his chest like a dropped anchor.

Elowen. Who had bound his bleeding hand on a rooftop and asked nothing. Who had given him her father's private survey—the true plan, naval depot, town never to be returned—at the cost of everything she was. Whose testimony, summoned to authenticate a forgery built around her father's own crimes, could either bury him or, if she looked at those deliberately wrong seals and understood what she was looking at—

"She'll see it," Rosalind said slowly. "She knows the Ashcombe dates are impossible. She corrected my maps once with exactly that kind of knowledge. If she takes the stand and finds the flaws—"

"Then she has a choice," Margaret said. "Condemn the forgery and destroy you. Or say nothing, let the tribunal build its case on sand—and collapse the whole edifice later, in front of everyone, when it matters most." Margaret's burned hands lay flat on the table between them, pale ridges shining faintly in the firelight. "You've placed your life in the hands of the general's daughter, Rosalind. I hope you know what you've done."

He did know. That was the vertigo of it. The forged chart was out of his hands now, sailing under escort into the fort, and its fate—and his—rested with the one person in Vellamouth who could read a map the way he could, who owed him nothing and risked everything, and whom he had last seen pressing his wounded knuckles closed as though a wound were a thing two people could agree upon.

Outside, the garrison bell rang the hour, and somewhere above the town, in a lit window of the fort, a soldier's daughter was perhaps already bent over a grey-bound book, her finger moving down a column of impossible dates, beginning—to understand.
