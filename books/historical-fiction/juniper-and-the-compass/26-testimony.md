## 26. Testimony

The tribunal convened in the customs house because it was the largest room left standing and the only one with a floor that did not creak when men of rank shifted their weight. Someone had hung the King's arms above the magistrate's bench—Elowen noted the nail was crooked, the plaque tilted two degrees starboard, and some distant, useless part of her mind, the part trained to correct surveyors' errors since she was nine years old, wanted to walk up and straighten it. Everything else in her life had gone crooked. Why not the Crown's lion too.

She sat where her father had placed her: front bench, right side, in uniform, sword at her hip, hands folded in her lap like a woman attending church. The placement was a message and she understood it as one. *Watch what happens to traitors*, the seat said. *Learn your place by watching his.*

Rosalind Okonkwo stood in the dock between two grenadiers who made him look even slighter than he was. His right hand was bandaged—the hand she had bound on a rooftop three nights ago with strips torn from her own sash, the hand whose blood had soaked through the linen while he tried to joke about wasting good bandaging on a cartographer—and he held that hand carefully against his chest, the way a man holds something borrowed that must be returned intact. His coat had been taken from him. They had dressed him in a prisoner's grey that fit him nowhere, and still he stood with the particular uprightness of someone who has spent years bent over a drafting table and has, in the end, a spine anyway.

He looked at her exactly once. He did not look away quickly, which was the mistake an innocent man would have made, nor did he linger, which would have been the mistake a guilty one would make. He looked at her the way he must have looked at coastlines: taking the measurement, accepting what was there.

Then he turned to face the tribunal, and Elowen understood that whatever happened in this room, he had already decided not to spend himself on hope. That frightened her more than anything else had. A man who expects nothing from the law cannot be saved by it.

Her father presided. General Augustus Vance wore full dress uniform for a summary proceeding held in a requisitioned fishing town, which told everyone present that this was not a trial but a coronation—a public execution of an idea, dressed in scarlet and gold. He sat beneath the crooked lion with Sergeant Bray at his shoulder, and before him, on a table draped in the garrison colours, lay the atlas.

The decoy.

Elowen had seen it only once, briefly, in the evidence room—had known within thirty seconds that it was wrong, that the coastlines were subtly, gorgeously falsified, that whoever had drawn it had done so with the specific intention of being believed. It was beautiful work. It was the most eloquent lie she had ever seen committed to paper, and it had been made to be caught. She had said nothing. She owed him that much silence, though she could not have said then what she intended to say aloud.

The charges were read. Sedition. Theft of government property. Conspiracy to incite riot—the riots were his fault now, the firing of muskets over the heads of fishermen's wives was *his* doing, the trampled child on the quay was a consequence of his ink. The clerk's voice went on and on in the flat cadence of paperwork, and Elowen listened to her own heart counting under it, and outside the salted windows the gulls screamed over a harbour full of ships that were supposed to be carrying people to safety and instead carried army ordnance to a naval depot that officially did not exist.

Witnesses were called. Bray testified first, precise and unhurried, describing the missing pages, the night survey, the trap set and sprung. He was good. He was very good. He described Rosalind's escape across the rooftops without embellishment, and somehow the absence of embellishment made it worse—it sounded like what it was, a man fleeing for his life from soldiers in his own town. Then a clerk swore to the ledger alterations as though alterations had never been made before in the history of paperwork. Then a neighbour Elowen did not know gave confused testimony about pamphlets and printers and marsh paths.

Through all of it her father watched her sidelong. She felt his attention the way one feels weather changing.

"Captain Vance." His voice filled the room without effort. "You will take the stand."

There it was. The thing he had promised her in his study four nights ago, with the fire burning low and his orders from London spread out on the desk between them like a hand of cards he expected her not to understand: *You will testify that this man is a spy, or you will stand beside him.* Loyalty is the only mercy you will be permitted, Papa had said. He had believed, she realized, that she would choose the mercy. He had raised her to believe it.

She rose. Her knees worked. That surprised her faintly, as though her body had been consulted after the fact.

The oath was administered. She swore it on the Bible with her palm flat against the cover, and the words tasted like every oath she had ever taken, layered one atop another until they choked the throat: oath to the King, oath to the regiment, oath to the survey, oath to the truth of a measurement honestly taken. Somewhere in the layering, she thought, they had stopped being the same oath. Somewhere along the marsh path to Finch's printing house, carrying her father's own private survey rolled inside her cloak, she had chosen which one remained.

"State your name and commission."

"Elowen Vance. Captain, Corps of Royal Surveyors. Attached to the Vellamouth requisition under General Sir Augustus Vance."

"You are the accused's—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—associate."

"I surveyed the fort with him. He corrected my maps. I corrected his." Let them hear it. Let them hear that she had worked beside him as an equal, that the association began in competence and not conspiracy. "We are colleagues in the same science, sir."

"And do you swear," her father said, and now his voice took on the warmth he used for audiences, for inspectors, for the moment in a review when the guns were about to go off, "that you have examined the evidence before this tribunal, and that you are prepared to speak to the character and conduct of the accused?"

"I have examined the evidence," Elowen said. "All of it."

Bray shifted at the general's shoulder. Something in the sergeant's stillness changed quality—not movement, but the potential for movement, the way water changes before it freezes.

"Then tell the tribunal," her father said, "what you know of this man's treachery."

The room waited. The clerk's pen hovered. Outside, tide slapped the pilings; she could hear it in the pause, patient, indifferent, keeping its own accounts.

"Papa," she said—she used the word deliberately, publicly, and saw the flinch travel through him like current through wire—"I was raised to believe that a map is a form of honesty. You taught me that. You said a surveyor's first loyalty is to the ground itself, because the ground does not flatter and the ground does not lie, and any man who draws it falsely is committing forgery against God's own handiwork." She turned to face the bench fully. "I am going to keep faith with that teaching today. I am going to tell the truth about maps."

"Captain." His voice dropped, private, terrible. "Mind your testimony."

"I am reading," she said, "from your survey."

She had carried it into the customs house that morning inside her greatcoat, rolled against her ribs where a heart would be, which was as accurate a description as any. Now she drew it out—three sheets, sealed with her father's own crest, annotated in his own small fierce hand—and the sight of that handwriting nearly undid her. She had learned her letters copying it. She had learned her profession imitating it. There was a version of her life, she understood, in which this moment had been inevitable since she was six years old and traced her first coastline at her father's knee: he had given her the instrument of his ruin long before he knew there would be a ruin to give it to.

"General Vance's private survey of Vellamouth and district," she said, loud enough to reach the back benches where the townspeople crowded against the doors—they had let them in, her father had insisted, this theatre required an audience—"completed the month before the proclamation of sanitary necessity. Dated. Signed." She unrolled it on the witness table and weighted the corners with the inkwell, the prayer book, her own cap. "Item one. The marsh fever cited in the requisition order infected eleven persons in the autumn of 1847, of whom none died. The survey's own medical annex—" she found the line with her finger, because her hands wanted to shake and giving them a task was the only way to stop them, "—describes conditions as 'within ordinary seasonal range.' Those are the surveyor-general's words. Within ordinary seasonal range."

"Those documents are classified," her father said.

"Then arrest me for reading them, sir, and be quick about it, because I am not finished."

Someone in the back benches made a sound—not laughter, not yet, but the ancestor of laughter.

"Item two." She kept her voice level, kept her finger moving down the columns, because numbers were safer than sentences; numbers had been her first language. "The requisition classifies Vellamouth as unfit for habitation. The survey classifies the harbour—" she read it verbatim, wanting the syllables exact, wanting no one to be able to say later that she had paraphrased, "—as 'deep-water capable, suitable for first-rate deployment, requiring no dredging.' Item three. Under a heading marked *Proposed Improvements*, the survey specifies construction of a naval depot, three powder magazines, a coaling station, and permanent barracks for a garrison of eight hundred. Estimated completion, four years. There is no provision anywhere in this document"—and here her voice finally cracked, and she let it, because the crack was true and everything else was procedure—"for the return of the town. Not one line. Not one word. The people of Vellamouth are listed in this survey exactly once."

She looked up. The whole room was looking at her now. Even the grenadiers in the dock had turned.

"As a clearance figure."

"Captain Vance." Her father rose halfway from the chair. "You will surrender those documents."

"No, sir."

The word fell into the room and detonated silently. *No, sir.* Two syllables, twenty-six years of obedience spent in them. She watched her father's face perform its entire argument in miniature—rage, disbelief, calculation, and beneath all of it, horribly, a flash of something that might have been pride, arriving a decade too late and in the wrong direction.

"The figures," she continued, turning now to the tribunal members, to the clerk, to the crowd at the doors, to the grey-coated man in the dock who had gone very still, "are these. Three towns surveyed and cleared under this programme in eighteen months. Thornmere: two hundred and forty-one families displaced, land resurveyed for pasture syndicate purchase at eleven pounds per acre, sold at forty-two. Ashcombe: one hundred and eighty-eight families, same pattern. Vellamouth: projected three hundred and six families, pending this morning's sailing." She laid the second sheet flat. "Every name is recorded here. Every acre. Every buyer—some of whom, I note, appear also among the subscribers to this expedition. The fever was invented. The ship was real. The depot is the point. And the ledger that decided who sailed and who stayed was altered—I have seen the alterations, I can identify the clerk's hand and the date of the ink—so that certain names would fail and others pass, according to considerations having nothing to do with health, alphabet, or chance."

"How," said one of the tribunal officers, an elderly major with a voice like a door hinge, "does the witness account for her possession of classified material?"

"I stole it from my father's desk," Elowen said, "and gave it to the accused, and would do it again tonight."

The room broke. It did not erupt all at once; it came apart in stages, like ice off a harbour wall. First the townspeople at the doors—a sound that was half gasp and half hymn. Then the clerk's pen stopped scratching, which in a bureaucracy is a kind of scream. Then the major demanding order, and another officer talking over him, and a woman's voice from the back calling out a name—*Thornmere, my sister was at Thornmere*—and the grenadiers in the dock uncertain whether to guard the prisoner or the room, and through all of it, above all of it, her father's voice commanding silence with the authority of thirty years of commands, and the silence coming slower each time, obeyed less each time, the machinery of his rank audibly stripping its gears.

Only Rosalind did not move. In the dock, amid the collapse of the proceeding that was meant to hang him, he stood with his bandaged hand pressed to his chest and looked at her with an expression she will remember on her deathbed if she is granted one: not gratitude, which would have been smaller than what was in his face, and not love, or not only love, but recognition—as if he had drawn her a hundred times in the margins of his work and was seeing, at last, that she matched the drawing.

She looked back at him and finished it.

"I took an oath," she said, and the room quieted to hear it, the way rooms quiet for scripture, "to defend the realm against its enemies. I have been asking myself, these past weeks, who the enemy is. I have my answer. The enemy is not the printer in the marsh. It is not the dockworkers slowing the cargo boats, or the widow who hid a dead man's book, or the apprentice in the dock who never once drew a false line except to save his own neck, and drew it beautifully." She set her hand flat on her father's survey, over the signature, over the name she shared. "The enemy is anyone who writes a town out of existence and calls it sanitation. I am Captain Elowen Vance, I am the general's daughter, I am the last person in this room who should be saying these things, and I am telling you they are all true. Check the figures. Check the buyers. Check the graves."

She stepped down from the stand. No one stopped her. There was no procedure for what she had done; the tribunal sat in it like men in rising water.

Her father descended from the bench. For one suspended moment she thought—he would embrace her, or strike her, or weep, and she braced herself impartially for all three. Instead he leaned close, so close she could smell the pomade in his greying hair, and said in a voice pitched for her alone, low and level as a fuse:

"Those papers leave this room over my body, Elowen. Do you understand what you have started? Do you understand what is coming?"

"Yes, Papa," she said. "I helped carry it."

He straightened. Whatever he saw in her face finished the conversation more thoroughly than any reply. He turned to Bray, and snapped orders—the gallery cleared, the documents seized, the prisoner remanded—and his voice was iron again, entirely iron, and only Elowen saw the tremor in the hand that gathered his fallen authority off the bench.

But it was Bray she watched as the grenadiers moved, Bray whose eyes went not to her, nor to the general, nor to the prisoner, but to the atlas on the evidence table—to the beautiful, wrong, deliberate atlas—with an expression she had last seen on her father's face when the returns from some forgotten colonial posting had come in twenty years ago and the numbers had not been what the numbers were supposed to be.

Sergeant Bray was counting, and discovering that somewhere, somehow, he had been dealt a forged hand.

And Sergeant Bray, she thought, watching the realization move behind his eyes like a lamp behind a shutter, did not seem like a man planning to burn alone.
