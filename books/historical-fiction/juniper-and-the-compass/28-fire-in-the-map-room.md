## 28. Fire in the Map Room

The bell began at a quarter past two in the morning—not the harbor bell, which rang true and rounded, but the garrison bell, thin and frantic, hammered by someone who had run to it rather than been ordered to ring it.

Rosalind was awake before its second stroke. He had learned, in the past weeks, to sleep the way a soldier sleeps: clothed, boots by the door, the atlas's hiding place memorized in the dark behind his closed eyes. He was at the window before the third.

The glow came from the direction of the quay—no, not the quay. Behind it. North of it, where the old customs house stood with the archive annex built onto its flank, the annex where, until ten days ago, the survey records of Vellamouth had been shelved in oiled oak cabinets and the requisition ledgers had lain chained to their desk like sleeping dogs.

The glow deepened. It went from amber to orange to a color he had no honest name for, a color that made his mouth go dry—the color of something that did not intend to stop.

"Margaret." He was already down the stairs, not waiting for an answer, then pausing on the landing because her door stood open and her bed was empty and cold when he put his palm to it. "Margaret!"

"In here." Her voice came from the kitchen, steady as a plumb line. She stood at the hearth in her coat, pulling on her gloves, and beside the door sat the satchel she had carried from the map room on the night of the fire that had not yet happened—the satchel with the flint, the tallow stubs, the roll of linen bandages she had kept stocked since Hollis died. She had known. Not this fire specifically. But some fire. She had lived long enough with the kind of men who burned things to keep a bag packed for it.

"That's the archive," Rosalind said. The words felt absurd, stating it, as if naming it might make it less true.

"I have eyes, boy." She took her stick down from its peg. "And you have your coat half on. Finish the job."

He followed her into the street.

* * *

Vellamouth in those weeks had gone quiet in a way towns are not meant to be quiet—not sleeping-quiet but held-breath-quiet, every window shuttered, every footstep quick. Now the town poured into its streets all at once, and the silence broke into a thousand voices, and none of them were screaming yet. That came later. First there was only the crowd flowing downhill toward the fire the way water finds a drain, and Rosalind and Margaret moving against it, shoulder to shoulder, Margaret's stick jabbing the cobbles like a blind woman's though her eyes were wide open and fixed on the smoke climbing the sky.

They saw the soldiers before they saw the flames clearly. A cordon at the bottom of Quay Lane, red coats black in the firelight, bayonets fixed—and behind them, doing nothing, standing in a loose idle knot exactly as men stand when the work is finished and they are waiting to be told they may go home: a dozen soldiers with buckets at their feet, unfilled.

"They're not fighting it," Rosalind said.

"No," said Margaret. "They're guarding it."

The arithmetic assembled itself in his head with terrible speed, each figure falling into place the way coastlines fall into place when the last triangulation is drawn. Vance had lost at the tribunal—or would lose; Elowen's testimony was three days old now and the whole town had heard the figures read aloud, the displacement counts, the naval depot plans, the names. Bray had turned, was in custody or had fled, and either way his testimony about the land syndicate was being written down by clerks even now. A man watching his own ruin approach does not wait for it. He destroys what can convict him.

And what could convict him lay in the archive annex. The original requisition ledger with its altered pages. The correspondence. The survey drafts.

And—the thought struck him so hard he actually stumbled on the step, and Margaret caught his elbow without looking—"the atlas."

"What?" she said sharply.

"The atlas. I returned it. After Finch printed the pamphlets I put it back in the map room, in the false drawer, because—" because it belonged there, because it was Hollis's, because he had wanted it found in its proper home by whatever inquiry came, proof with clean hands, proof in its right place. He heard himself saying it and heard how it sounded: vanity dressed as principle. "It's inside, Margaret. It's inside the building."

She did not waste breath cursing him, though he saw her jaw set in the firelight, and somehow that was worse than any curse. She turned from the cordon and looked at the burning customs house with a surveyor's eye, reading the geometry of it the way Rosalind read a coastline.

"Annex is separate construction," she said. "Built onto the north wall after the fire of '31—they'd learned their lesson by then, stone and slate, iron door." She was walking as she talked, along the edge of the cordon, forcing him to keep pace. "The customs house will burn. The annex will burn slower. Slower is enough, if we're quick and stupid."

"Quick and stupid," Rosalind repeated.

"It's how most necessary things get done." She stopped. Ahead, where a timber yard ran down to the water behind a wall, a gap yawned where a gate had once hung, and beyond it the rear elevation of the annex rose black against the fire, its high windows beginning to glow at the corners like coals being blown upon. "Hollis showed me that gap. He used it when he didn't want the clerk to know what hours he kept." Her voice did something small and private on the word *Hollis*, and then closed over it like water. "Iron door's at the front. Rear windows are leaded glass in wooden frames. You break one, you climb through, you take what matters, you come out before the roof comes down. Do you understand the order of those instructions?"

"Take what matters. Come out."

"And the atlas first. Everything else second. If it comes to choosing, you choose the book, and you do not choose me." She said it flatly, taking away any room for argument, and then spoiled the flatness entirely by gripping his hand once, hard, her fingers thin and strong as whalebone. "Go."

* * *

The timber yard was full of smoke that had arrived ahead of the fire, drifting low and acrid, tasting of pitch. Rosalind crossed it at a run, found the drainpipe he was praying for, and went up the wall hand over hand, his injured left hand screaming where the knuckles had never properly healed from the rooftop escape—he had learned to favor the right since, and now the right did all the work while the left merely hooked and endured.

The window ledge was hot through his sleeves. The glass, when he drove his elbow into it, burst outward in a spray that fell glittering into the yard below, and the heat that rolled out through the new hole was unlike anything he had imagined heat could be—a physical presence, an animal, shoving him back, roaring softly in the throat of the chimney the building had become. He counted to three, the way he counted when plotting a bearing under pressure, and went through.

The map room.

Even now, even like this, the sight of it stopped him for half a heartbeat. The chart drawers he had escaped through once already, the great table scarred with Hollis's compass points, the shelves of folios going up the wall like the shelves of a cathedral—and above them, along the ceiling, the smoke lying flat and slow as fog on a moor, thickening, descending by inches.

The false drawer was third from the floor, beneath the window, and he went to his knees before it and hauled it open, and his heart stopped.

Empty.

For one full second the world ended. Then his eyes adjusted to the gloom and he saw it—the leather corner, the brass clasp, tucked at the back beneath a folded set of coastal soundings. Not empty. Merely pushed back, deeper than he'd left it, as if someone had rummaged and missed it. Or as if Hollis himself had once hidden it there and the hiding place remembered its habit.

His hands shook as he drew it out. The atlas. *Juniper and the Compass*, Hollis had called it privately, after the tree over the grave of the first name ever written in its margins. Forty towns' worth of erasures in its leaves. Every family. Every date. Every warrant number. In his arms it weighed nothing and everything.

Smoke touched the ceiling an arm's length above his head now, and it had begun to move—no longer lying still but rolling, curling, reaching down like fingers testing.

The folios next. He swept them toward the window with both arms—the displacement correspondence, Vance's private survey draft, the ledger pages he had copied and the originals Bray had hidden and Rosalind had recovered—all of it into a canvas map-carrier he found hanging by the door, and the weight of it nearly buckled him. Too much. He would never fit through the window with both loads.

Behind him, glass shattered somewhere in the body of the customs house, and a sound followed it like a wave arriving, and the temperature in the room leapt.

He slung the carrier across his back. He thrust the atlas inside his coat, against his chest, and felt it there like a second heartbeat.

Then the door of the map room—which he had not opened, which had been shut—began to glow along its seam.

* * *

He got out the window with seconds to spare and knew it was seconds only because of what followed him: a soft rushing exhalation from under the door, the fire finding the air corridor he'd opened and filling its lungs. He dropped from the ledge into the drainpipe's embrace and slid more than climbed, hit the yard, and ran for the gap in the wall—

—and Margaret was not at the gap.

She was in the yard.

She was at the far end of it, near the annex's side door, which stood open—someone had opened it, perhaps soldiers looting, perhaps her, and God help him, he knew it was her—and she was dragging something along the ground, a cabinet drawer, wrenched whole from its case, heavy as sin, hauling it by its iron handle with her whole slight body bent to the work. And around her the fire had come out of the building. It had found the timber yard's stores, and the stacked planking along the east wall had caught, and a lane of flame was walking toward her across the yard with the unhurried confidence of something that has never once been late.

"MARGARET!"

Her head came up. Through the smoke he saw her face, and what was in it was not panic. It was recognition—she had known he would come back out, the way she had known to pack the satchel, the way she always knew—and then, terribly, it was relief, because he was alive and she could stop worrying about one thing in a world full of things to worry about.

"The drawer," she called, and her voice cracked in the middle of it. "Rosalind, the drawer—it's the 'forty-one register—"

He understood then what she had done. While he took the atlas, she had gone for the other evidence—the earliest register, the one whose margins held the first displaced families, the one that proved the pattern predated Vance, proved it was policy, proved it was empire-wide. She had not been able to lift it through the window alone. So she had opened the side door and tried to drag it out herself.

The flame-lane reached the spilled oil from the yard's lamp store, and the yard lit up white-orange, and the heat came at them both like the opening of a furnace door.

He ran into it.

Later, he would not be able to reconstruct those moments in order. Memory gave them back to him as fragments, the way fire gives back wreckage. The atlas pressed against his sternum. The handle of the drawer in his hands alongside hers, her knuckles white inside his, both of them hauling together, the iron runners shrieking on stone. A beam cracking somewhere overhead with a sound like a rifle shot. The smoke going black and coming down to within six feet of the ground, and then five. Margaret coughing—a small, contained cough, then another, then a wet uncontained one. Him shouting at her to leave it, leave it, and her voice coming back out of the dark, maddeningly level: *When we reach the wall, boy, not before.*

The wall. Cool air. Grass. They had come out through the gap into the lane behind the timber yard, and the drawer lay between them on the frozen mud like a beached boat, and Margaret was on her knees beside it.

"Margaret." He turned her toward him, and then he saw.

Her right sleeve was charred from wrist to elbow, the wool fused black, and beneath it the flesh was wrong—shiny, weeping where the fabric had pulled away, the hand beneath it swollen already to twice its size. A live coal had lodged in the fold of her collar and was eating a small red circle into her neck, and he beat it out with his bare palm and barely felt his own skin sear. Her hair on one side was shortened, curled, smoking faintly.

"We have it," she said. Her voice was almost gone, scraped down to the grain. "Both registers. The atlas. All of it." She looked up at him, and her eyes were clear, and that clarity frightened him more than the burns. "We have everything, Rosalind. Everything Hollis—" The cough took her again, doubled her over, and when she straightened she swayed, and he caught her.

He got the drawer's contents into the carrier with shaking hands—the register pages, loose now, he stuffed them in without order—and he got the atlas secure inside his coat, and then he put his shoulder under Margaret's arm and lifted her, this woman who had taught him to hold a pen, who had fed him for seven years, who had just walked into fire for dead men's handwriting, and she weighed nothing, she weighed nothing at all, and that was the moment the grief finally broke through the urgency and flooded him, because the cost had stopped being abstract. It had hands. It had a face. It was leaning on his shoulder and whispering, of all things, *"Mind the corner of the page, it's torn, mind it,"* as if the register were the patient and she merely its escort.

* * *

Finch's print shop smelled of ink and tallow and, that night, of burnt wool.

Margaret lay on the worktable's daybed, and Finch—who had come to the door with a pistol and lowered it only when he saw the carrier—worked over her arm with boiled linen and the goose-fat-and-wax dressing he used on scalds, his big printer's hands impossibly gentle. The burns ran from her wrist halfway to her elbow, and where they crossed the back of her hand they had gone deepest, and the doctor Finch sent his boy running for said what Rosalind already knew and refused to say aloud: that the hand might not serve a pen again, and that the fever to come would decide more than anyone in the room could.

"You should see the other fellow," Margaret said, when the dressing was done. Her voice was a ruin. She was gray to the lips. She smiled anyway, and it was the bravest and most terrible thing Rosalind had ever seen. "The archive's gone. Whatever Vance wanted burned is ash." Her good hand found Rosalind's wrist. "But we're holding the only copies now, boy. Do you understand? Everything else is smoke. What's in that bag is the last truth there is."

He understood. He understood it with a weight that pressed him down into the chair beside her bed—that the fire had not merely been an attack but an argument, and the argument was this: *nothing survives except what people carry.* Institutions burn. Ledgers burn. Archives burn. Truth survives only in the flesh of those willing to house it, and tonight the flesh had been Margaret's forearm and his own scorched palms, and tomorrow it would need to be paper again, hundreds of sheets of it, printed and scattered beyond any torch's reach.

"Finch." His voice came out steadier than he expected. "How fast can you set type?"

Finch looked up from the bandage roll. "For Hollis's book?" He wiped his hands slowly. "I've kept the frames warm for three years, lad. I can start before the sun's up."

"Start with the 'forty-one register. Names first. Names before anything." Rosalind laid his palm flat on the carrier, on the atlas inside his coat, and felt its heartbeat against his own. "Every family. Every town. Let them try to burn the whole county."

Margaret's breathing had gone shallow and even; she was asleep mid-listen, her bandaged hand resting on her breastbone like a knight's gauntlet. Rosalind covered her with the blanket to her chin, and stayed crouched there a long moment, watching her face in the lamplight, learning by heart the stubborn line of a woman who had walked into fire for the dead and come out carrying their words.

Then the shop door burst open, and Finch's apprentice fell in, white-faced, out of breath, snow melting in his hair.

"Sir—master—there's soldiers at the end of the street. A whole column. And they're not going to the garrison." He gulped air. "They're asking after the printer."
