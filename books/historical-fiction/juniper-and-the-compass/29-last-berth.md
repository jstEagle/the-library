## 29. Last Berth

The morning of the sailing came in grey and silver, the fog off the Vellamouth estuary so thick that the ship at anchor seemed less a vessel than a rumor—a suggestion of masts, a smudge of rigging, the intermittent tolling of her bell like something calling across water from another world. Rosalind stood on the quay with his bandaged right hand cradled against his chest, the burn beneath the linen still weeping, and watched the harbor he had drawn a hundred times resolve itself out of nothing, piece by piece, as if his own memory were conjuring it back into being.

*The East Mole. The customs house with its leaning chimney. The fish-curing sheds, tar-black, that Hollis had once measured for a chart no one would ever need because every fisherman born here knew those waters blind.*

He had not slept. None of them had. The archive fire was eleven hours behind him and Margaret lay in the surgeon's house on Chandler's Row with her arms wrapped in grease dressings, refusing laudanum because she said she wanted to remember everything clearly, and when Rosalind had asked her why, she had looked at him with eyes that were old before the burns and older now, and said, *Because someone has to carry it whole, dear boy, and my hands can't hold a pen anymore.*

The town had come down to the quay in its hundreds. That was the first thing that struck him, standing in the crowd: how many of them there still were. Weeks of ledger arithmetic and ration queues and the low grinding terror of the occupation had taught Vellamouth to think of itself as a diminishing quantity—names subtracted, berths denied, families thinned out by deportation and flight until the word *town* felt like an overstatement. But here they all were, pressed shoulder to shoulder between the bollards and the sealed customs house doors: fishermen in their oilskins, shopkeepers in their Sunday coats gone shabby, dockworkers with Adaeze's calluses on their hands, grandmothers, children hoisted onto shoulders so they could see the water. They had come not because they believed any of them would sail but because the ship was leaving and a thing leaving must be witnessed.

A naval cutter patrolled the line of the quay, and redcoats stood in loose formation along the mole with their officer consulting a clipboard, and Sergeant Bray—Bray, who two days ago had held a beautifully wrong atlas in his triumphant hands and was now, as the whole garrison knew by whispers, giving evidence against General Vance in exchange for his own skin—was conspicuously absent from the proceedings. Disgrace, Rosalind had learned, moved quietly. It did not stand on quays with a clipboard.

"Rosalind."

Adaeze came through the crowd the way water comes through a gap in stones—inevitably, without hurry, arriving. Her cheek still bore the bruise from the cell, and her wrists had a rawness about them where the irons had sat, and she was walking like a woman who had decided that her body belonged to herself again and would take some persuading to hand it back. She stopped beside him and followed his gaze out to the ship.

"She's real, then," Adaeze said. "Half of me thought they'd tow her away empty in the night just to watch our faces."

"They've lowered the gangway ladder."

"I see it." She was quiet a moment. Then: "Margaret?"

"Sleeping. The surgeon says the fever's broken." He heard his own voice flatten around the word *fever*, the way it did now whenever he approached the memory of the map room burning, Margaret going back into the smoke for the atlas while he screamed at her, both of them emerging blackened and coughing with the satchel between them like a rescued child.

"And the book?"

"Finch has it. Presses running since midnight."

Adaeze nodded slowly. Out on the water, the fog was beginning to tear into long rags, and sunlight came through the gaps in columns, laying bright moving patches on the estuary. The ship's name emerged from the mist letter by letter: *HMS Providence*. Rosalind almost laughed at it. The Crown had never once in this whole affair chosen an honest name for anything—the requisition had been called *sanitary necessity*, the land-grab had been called *provisional administration*, and now the last ship out of a stolen town was called Providence.

They announced the boarding at eight o'clock. A navy lieutenant climbed onto an upturned crate near the customs house steps, unrolled a paper, and read in the flat voice of a man reciting someone else's sentences: by order of the Admiralty and pending the findings of the Royal Commission, one civilian berth remained available aboard *Providence*, departing on the afternoon tide; eligible claimants would be verified against the master list; persons not appearing on the list were advised that subsequent transport would be arranged *in due course*. In due course. The phrase moved through the crowd like a current under ice, and Rosalind saw faces close around themselves, the way faces had learned to do. In due course meant never. Everyone in Vellamouth understood the grammar of that now.

"One berth," Adaeze said softly. "After everything. One."

"It's yours," Rosalind said. "Your name's on the master list. It always was."

"Yes." She looked at the ship, and he watched something move across her face that he couldn't read—not longing exactly, and not fear. Calculation, maybe, but the generous kind: the arithmetic of a woman who counted boats for a living, who knew the difference between what floats and what sinks. "One berth," she said again, "and three of us, and half the town besides. Well. We always knew the sums didn't work."

* *

The clerk's table was set up on the customs house steps under a navy awning, and the queue before it formed with the terrible docility of people who have learned that lines are the only justice on offer. Rosalind stood with Adaeze at the back of it, and around him he could hear the town doing its hoping out loud—an old man rehearsing the pronunciation of his own surname in case grief had made him stumble on it; a young mother telling her boy, over and over, *if they say our name you walk up the ladder and don't look back, don't look back for me, do you hear*; a fisherman named Cobb whom Rosalind had known all his life explaining to anyone who'd listen that he'd been on the original list, he'd seen it, the clerk Wrenn himself had shown him, before the alterations, before—

"Cobb," Rosalind said, taking his arm. "The commission will reissue the berths. Elowen testified; the requisition's suspended. Your name will be restored."

Cobb looked at him with wet, furious eyes. "Paper doesn't put back what paper took, boy."

And Rosalind, mapmaker, keeper of the margins, had no answer for that, because it was true. He had spent the whole crisis believing that names restored to a record were justice delivered, and standing in that queue he felt the belief crack along a fault line he'd been ignoring for weeks: ink was only ever a beginning. The houses still had to be given back. The deported had to be brought home. The dead—Hollis, and the others whose names filled the atlas's margins—could not be reissued anything.

The queue advanced. The clerk checked names against the ledger with a pencil, ticking, ticking. A family ahead of them was refused—their entry marked *transferred*, which everyone now knew was the euphemism stamped on the papers of the deported—and the mother made no sound at all, which was worse than screaming, and the father stood holding his hat against his chest like a man at a graveside, and then they turned and walked back up through the crowd and the crowd parted for them the way it parts for funerals.

Then it was their turn, and the clerk ran his pencil down the column and stopped.

"Okonkwo," he said. "Adaeze Okonkwo. Dockworker. Present?" And then his pencil moved half an inch further down the page and stopped again, and he looked up, and Rosalind watched him decide not to say the next thing, and say it anyway. "There's... a second notation. Rosalind Okonkwo. Struck through. Sedition charge, withdrawn—" the clerk's voice thinned, careful, "—withdrawn upon dismissal of charges. But the strike-through stands. No berth. My apologies."

"It doesn't matter," Rosalind started, "there's only—"

"There's one berth," the clerk said, not unkindly, "and it belongs to the name not struck through." He set down his pencil and looked past them to the next in line, and that was the whole of it. Arithmetic. The sums didn't work; they had never worked; the entire machinery of the occupation had been built on making small numbers of people believe that small numbers were mercy.

Adaeze didn't move toward the ladder. That was the thing Rosalind noticed first, even through the roaring in his ears—even standing there with his exclusion formalized in graphite, he noticed—that his sister did not step forward. She stood with her feet planted on the wet stones of the quay, arms folded, looking at the gangway as if it were an argument she intended to win.

"Well?" the clerk said.

"In a moment," Adaeze said.

Rosalind took her elbow and drew her aside, under the leaning gable of the net-mender's hut, out of the queue's earshot.

"You have to board," he said.

"Do I."

"Adaeze." His throat was tight. "Listen to me. This is not the moment for your principles. The berth is yours by law, by right, by every list that was ever honest. If you give it up out of stubbornness—"

"Out of *what*?"

"Out of love, then, I know, I know it's love—but it buys nothing. Two of us staying changes no arithmetic. You go, you're safe, you carry the story east to London if Finch's pamphlets don't get there first, you stand in front of the commission yourself and tell them what the docks looked like. That's worth more than your company, and I will miss you every day I live, and you are getting on that ship."

She let him finish. She had always let him finish—it was one of the ways she loved him, the patience of a woman used to watching slow thinkers arrive. And then she said, "Finished?" and when he nodded: "Good. Now hear me, little brother. I spent nine days in a cell for the crime of pulling a child out from under boots. Nine days learning what a person is worth to the Crown when the paperwork goes against them. And you want to know what kept me sane in there?" Her voice dropped low, steady as a keel. "It was knowing you were out here with the book. With *our* dead in the margins. I don't need to be safe, Rosalind. I need us to be *right*, and we are right whether I'm in Vellamouth or in London or at the bottom of the sea. So don't you dare spend your sacrifice on me. Save it for someone who needs saving."

"That's exactly why—" he began, furious and drowning, and that was when the child screamed.

* *

It came from down the quay, near the fish-curing sheds—a thin, high sound that cut through the crowd noise the way a nail goes through cloth. Rosalind turned. Near the waterline, where the tide left its slick green margin on the stones, a knot of people had gathered around something low to the ground, and above the murmur rose a woman's voice, cracked and repeating: *"He fell, he fell, the crates shifted, somebody help us, please, please—"*

Adaeze got there first—she moved through crowds the way she moved through cargo, reading gaps before they opened. Rosalind followed, pushing past shoulders, and came out into the little cleared space by the shed wall and saw.

The child was perhaps six years old. A boy, dark-haired, lying at an angle that legs are not meant to lie at, his shin bone tenting the skin of his trouser leg, and his face was white as paper and shockingly silent now, the scream spent. Beside him crouched a woman Rosalind did not know—young, hollow-cheeked, dressed in traveling clothes worn to the weave, the clothes of someone who had walked a long way. Her hands hovered over the boy, touching nothing, trembling.

"We came from Marlowe Cross," the woman was saying, to no one, to everyone. "We walked. There was nothing left there either—they cleared it two years ago, before Vellamouth, before anyone was watching—we walked four days. I heard there was a ship. I heard they were letting people on."

Marlowe Cross. Rosalind felt the name land somewhere beneath his ribs, because he knew that name. Not from gossip—from *marginalia*. Page forty-one of the atlas, in Hollis's small upright hand, third column from the left: *Marlowe Cross — 212 souls — removed for the reservoir scheme — no record survives.* Hollis had written it down because no one else would. The woman crouching on the wet stones of Vellamouth quay was one of Hollis's names. She was the atlas, walking around with a broken leg beside it.

"He's not on any list," a dockworker muttered—not cruelly, just stating the physics of the situation, the way Adaeze counted boats. "No list, no berth."

"He's six," Adaeze said.

The lieutenant had come down from the customs house steps, drawn by the commotion, and now stood at the edge of the group with his clipboard held against his chest like a breastplate. Rosalind watched him look at the child, and look at his list, and Rosalind saw—clearly, horribly, with the same precision he brought to a coastline—the exact moment the man's compassion lost to his instructions. "I'm sorry," the lieutenant said. "I can't board passengers not on the manifest. If the boy receives treatment at the garrison infirmary—"

"The garrison infirmary," Adaece said, in a voice that could have stripped paint. "The one that's shipping out with the rest of the regiment on that tide."

"The nearest civilian surgeon is forty miles—"

"Then put the mother and boy on the ship."

"I can't—"

"*Then someone puts them on the ship,*" Adaeze said, and her voice cracked at last, all that cell-forged control splitting open, "because I am sick to God of watching this country do arithmetic with children—"

"Adaeze." Rosalind said it quietly, and she stopped, because in twenty-three years he had said her name in that tone perhaps twice, and she knew it.

He looked at the boy. The boy looked back at him—grey-faced, lips bloodless, eyes enormous with the particular courage of children who have decided not to cry because their mothers cannot afford it. Four days walking. Two years displaced. No record survives. He thought of Hollis's hand moving across the marginalia by candlelight, filling the white spaces of the map with the people the map was built to erase. *For those the ledger forgot.* He had not yet understood, until this exact moment, that the dedication was not an epitaph. It was an instruction.

The tide was turning. The *Providence* would sail within the hour, and after her, nothing—no schedule, no promise, only *in due course* stretching out over a lifetime. There was one berth. There was a name entitled to it, and that name was his sister's, and his sister had already told him, in her flat keel-steady voice, not to spend his sacrifice on her.

But she hadn't told him what to do with it instead.

Rosalind turned to the lieutenant. "The berth is mine to assign," he said. "My sister surrenders it. Board the child. Take the mother too—I'll stand surety for the irregularity before the commission myself, in writing, under my own name. Rosalind Okonkwo, apprentice surveyor, late of the Hollis map room. I'm not hard to find. I'm apparently the most documented seditious cartographer in three counties."

The lieutenant blinked. "That's—that's not a regular procedure—"

"Neither was requisitioning a healthy town," Rosalind said. "You've all become very flexible men this season. Be flexible for five more minutes."

"Rosalind." Adaeze's voice, very close. He turned to her and found her face only inches from his, wet-eyed and blazing. "Don't. Don't you do this as some penance—"

"It's not penance." And it wasn't; that was the miracle of it, the thing he would try to explain to her later and never quite manage. All winter he had imagined this choice as a wound, a loss, a door closing—had dreaded it the way you dread your own death in dreams. But standing on the wet stones with the fog lifting off the water, he felt no door closing at all. He felt a compass needle settling. He thought: *history does not happen elsewhere, to other people. It happened here, to us, and I was here for it, and I chose.* "It's a mapping decision," he said, and smiled at her, crooked, his real smile, hers. "Some things belong on the chart, Ade. Some things belong in the margins. I've decided which one I am."

"You're not a margin. You were never a margin."

"Then write me big," he said. "Write me across the whole page."

The sailors brought a stretcher from the ship's boat and lifted the boy gently onto it, and the boy, even now, even grey-lipped, reached up and caught Rosalind's sleeve with a hand hot with fever. "Thank you," the child said, with the grave formality of someone repeating an adult phrase learned quickly, and Rosalind knelt on the wet stone, heedless of the cold soaking through his trousers, and said:

"What's your name?"

"Jonah."

"Jonah. When they ask you, years from now, how you got off that quay—you tell them a mapmaker gave you his seat because your name deserved to be somewhere better than nowhere. Can you remember that?"

Jonah nodded, solemn as an apprentice.

They carried him up the gangway. His mother followed, turning at the top to look back, and she did not wave and did not speak, only pressed her hand flat against her breastbone—the gesture of a woman committing a debt she could never repay—and then the fog swallowed them both, and the bell rang, and the mooring lines went into the water with their heavy slaps.

Adaeze took his hand. Hers was rough with rope-callus and cold, and she gripped his hard enough to hurt, which was how she said everything that mattered.

"Mama would have killed you," she said.

"Mama would have done it first."

That surprised a laugh out of her—a short, torn, real laugh, and she pulled him into her arms and held him on the quay in front of the whole remaining town, and nobody looked away because everybody on that quay had lost something on it and knew exactly what an embrace was worth. "Stay right," she said into his shoulder. "Whatever else. Stay right."

"Always," he said. "Count the boats for me."

The *Providence* warped off the wall at noon, her rails lined with the few, the listed, the lucky—Jonah propped on cushions on the quarterdeck, small and splinted and alive. The fog tore fully open as she cleared the mole, and sun came down on the estuary like a verdict, and Rosalind stood with his sister's hand in his and watched the last ship carry away a child whose name would now survive because his own had been willing not to.

He felt lighter than he had any right to feel. Empty-handed, excluded, exiled-in-place—and light. Somewhere behind him the crowd was dispersing, and somewhere across town Finch's presses were still running, and somewhere in a surgeon's house on Chandler's Row Margaret was sleeping with her bandaged hands above the blanket like a woman who had set down a heavy load at last, and somewhere east, on a ship called Providence, a boy named Jonah was telling his mother about the mapmaker.

"Right," Adaeze said briskly, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, becoming practical the instant grief and grace had finished their business. "If we're staying, we're working. The commission arrives within the week and this town is going to testify like it has never testified before. I want the dockworkers' statements. I want Cobb found. And you—" she jabbed a finger into his chest, "—have an atlas to finish. Don't think I haven't noticed you've been avoiding the last plate."

"I haven't been avoiding it."

"You have. You're afraid to draw Vellamouth whole in case drawing it makes losing it real." She softened the words with her hand, briefly, against his burned one. "Finish it, Rosalind. Whatever happens next. Finish it."

He was still holding her finger-jab in his chest like a benediction when the boy came running up the quay—a printer's devil, ink to the elbows, breathless, waving a fresh pamphlet overhead.

"Mister Okonkwo!" he shouted. "Mister Okonkwo—Finch says come quick—the pamphlets crossed into Ashcombe overnight and there's a magistrate there, a real one, and he's sent word—he wants to meet the man who wrote the margins."

Rosalind looked at Adaeze. Adaeze grinned—the old grin, the wharf-grin, wide as the estuary.

"Go on then, mapmaker," she said. "Your history's asking for you."
