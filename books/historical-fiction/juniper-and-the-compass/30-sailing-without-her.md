## 30. Sailing Without Her

The child's name was Peter. That was all anyone knew of him—Peter, six years old maybe seven, his left arm splinted with a barrel stave and strips of a fisherman's shirt, his mother lost somewhere in the crush at the gate two days past. He sat on an upturned crate at the end of the quay with the terrible patience of children who have learned that questions get no answers, watching the great ship *Providence* take on her passengers the way other children watched a fair being taken down.

Adaeze Okonkwo crouched in front of him and offered the last of a barley biscuit.

He took it with his good hand and ate without ceremony, which she approved of. A child who fussed over food on a quay in Vellamouth in this year of grace 1848 would not survive the week.

"Where is your mother, Peter?"

"Gone." He said it the way he might have said *rain*, or *dark*. A fact of weather.

"Well." She wiped crumb from his chin with her thumb. "Gone isn't always gone forever. Sometimes gone is only gone for now."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Are you gone-for-now too?"

The question went through her like a gaff hook. Behind him, down the length of the quay, the boarding line had begun to move—a ragged procession of families clutching their paper, their stamped pages, their proof that they existed in the eyes of the ledger. One berth remained. The clerk had confirmed it that morning with the flat voice of a man reading figures aloud: one berth remaining, name of Okonkwo, Adaeze, entitled by right of the original sailing list, reinstated by order of the commission pending its inquiry.

One berth. And Rosalind standing beside her with salt wind pulling at his coat, already composing, she could see, whatever gentle speech he had prepared to put her aboard that ship if he had to lie to her, carry her, bribe the mate to do it.

"Almost," she told Peter. "But not yet."

* * *

She had been counting things since she was nine years old. Boats first, then crates, then barrels, then wages, then the years since the fever took their parents, then the number of times she had watched her brother walk into Master Hollis's map room carrying his head full of coastlines while she walked onto the docks carrying fish. Counting was what her hands did when her heart could not bear to look at what they touched.

So she counted now, while the line shuffled forward and the gulls screamed their opinions overhead.

Forty-one souls boarding. Nine soldiers at the gangway, bored now, their rifles shouldered. Three clerks with the ledger open between them like a Bible at a hanging. One harbourmaster drunk by ten in the morning, weeping quietly into his collar because his own name was not on any list and never had been—the commission had found his office bought and sold twice over. One brother beside her, breathing carefully, the way he breathed when he was about to do something noble and stupid.

And one berth.

"You've been quiet," Rosalind said.

"I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"How many ways you're going to try to talk me onto that boat before the tide turns."

He had the grace to look caught. Her brother—her brilliant, infuriating brother, who had once redrawn the whole of Vellamouth harbour from memory after Hollis's charts burned, who had outdrawn Sergeant Bray himself with nothing but penmanship and nerve, who had given away his own berth yesterday as though it were a biscuit and not his life—he looked at her now with his hand half-raised toward the argument he'd rehearsed, and lowered it.

"It's your berth," he said. "By law. By every law that finally works in this town, it's yours."

"The law." She tasted the words and spat them into the water below. "Three weeks ago the law said I didn't exist unless a clerk wrote me down. Two weeks ago the law arrested me for pulling a child out from under soldiers' boots. Yesterday the law reinstated my name because it embarrassed them not to. And now the law wants to give me a berth on a ship sailing for a place where I'd owe my life to the same Crown that emptied this harbour." She shook her head slowly. "Rozzy. You know what I counted this morning, before dawn? I counted the ships' carpenters still working in Marsh Yard. Fourteen men with adzes and no work, because the fishing fleet is impounded and the warehouses are sealed. I counted the widow Osei's shutters—still boarded from the riot. I counted forty-one people getting on that boat and I asked myself: who among them is coming back?"

The question hung between them over the grey water.

"That's not the point of a rescue," Rosalind said quietly.

"No," Adaeze agreed. "It isn't. But it's the point of a home."

* * *

The child Peter had fallen asleep against a bollard, his splinted arm cradled like something precious and broken. It was this, more than anything, that decided her—or perhaps it decided nothing, and merely showed her what she had already chosen in the dark hours before dawn, lying awake in Margaret Hollis's spare room listening to the old woman's burned hands being dressed, listening to her hiss with pain and say, each morning, *well, I'm still here.*

Because here was the truth she had carried since the night of the riot, since the trampling crowd and the rifle-fire over their heads and the cell afterward where she had told her brother to stop trying to save everyone: Adaeze Okonkwo did not believe in rescue. Rescue was a story powerful people told about other people so they wouldn't have to tell the truth about what they'd broken. What she believed in was work. Planks laid true. Rations shared fairly. Names remembered.

A berth on the *Providence* was rescue. Staying was work.

"Peter," she said softly, waking him.

He came up out of sleep all at once, the way dock children did, eyes wide and body ready. When he saw her face he settled, and something in the settling undid her.

"How would you like to go on a big ship?"

His lip trembled with the effort of not hoping. "Am *you* going?"

"I'm staying here. I have work." She took his good hand. "But there's a lady—" she scanned the line, found what she needed: a thin woman in a grey shawl, third family back, whom Adaeze had seen sharing bread in the ration queue with strangers; some people revealed themselves in queues— "a kind lady getting on that boat, and I'm going to ask her to sit with you. And when you land, there will be people whose job is finding mothers. Do you understand? Your mother's name will be written down"—she pressed her thumb gently to his chest—"here, and also on paper, in ink, so they cannot forget her. I know someone very good at writing names down."

Behind her, she heard her brother inhale.

* * *

It took an hour. Of course it took an hour; nothing in Vellamouth happened quickly anymore except rumours and bad news. There was the clerk to satisfy, and the commission's observer with his clipboard, and the woman in the grey shawl to convince—which took less time than expected, because the woman looked at Peter's splinted arm, then at Adaeze's face, and simply opened her arms. There was a soldier who objected, and a sergeant newly arrived and hungry to matter, and then there was Elowen Vance—no longer Captain Vance, stripped of rank but wearing her spine straight as ever—who walked down the quay with her papers in hand and made the objection disappear with four sentences and a signature.

Adaeze watched her brother watch Elowen, and smiled despite everything. Some things even a requisition couldn't redraw.

Then the gangway was pulled in, and the mooring lines splashed into the water, and Peter stood at the rail with his face pressed between the bars, his splinted arm held up—not waving, exactly. Showing her he still had it. Still had the arm, the splint, the proof that someone had cared for him. She raised her own hand and held it there until the ship turned past the mole and the harbour fog took him the way the fog took everything eventually.

She did not cry until the ship was fully gone. Then she let herself have thirty seconds, standing at the rail's-end of the quay, fists tight, jaw tighter, while the gulls wheeled and the tide ran out and her brother stood beside her saying nothing, because he knew—had always known, better than she gave him credit for—that some griefs need witnessing and none need commentary.

"He'll be all right," Rosalind said at last.

"He'd better be." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, done. "Or I'll learn to sail, and I'll find him, and I'll raise him myself on fish and arithmetic. Don't think I won't."

"I don't think anything about you is beyond believing anymore." He turned to face her fully. "You refused it. Your berth. I stood there ready to fight you for it and you just—gave it away like handing over bread."

"It wasn't mine to keep." She looked out at the empty water where the ship had been. "You gave yours to him yesterday. I only finished what you started. That's how it works with us, isn't it? You begin things with ink. I finish them with my hands."

"And what do we call what you've begun now?"

She looked down the quay—at the boarded customs house, at the impounded fleet riding sullenly at anchor, at Widow Osei prying the boards off her shutters with a crowbar and no permission from anyone, at the carpenters drifting toward Marsh Yard because word travelled on the docks faster than any gazette that the Okonkwo girl was back and there was work to be done.

"Rebuilding," she said. "We're going to call it rebuilding. And you're going to draw it, Rozzy. Every roof, every restored name, every plank. Because if the empire taught us one thing, it's that whoever holds the map holds the town. So we'd best hold our own."

* * *

They walked home together through streets that were learning to breathe again.

That was how Adaeze thought of it—streets learning to breathe. Shutters opening like eyes after long sleep. Smoke rising from chimneys that had been cold a fortnight. At the corner of Chandler's Row, Mr. Prewitt the chandler had set out a single tallow candle in his window, and next to it a handwritten card: *Open. God help us all, open.* She laughed for the first time in days, and the laugh surprised her by turning briefly into something else, and Rosalind pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of love.

Margaret Hollis was waiting for them at the door of the old map room, her bandaged hands folded in her apron, her face composed into the particular calm of a woman who has heard everything twice and intends to hear it properly anyway.

"Well?" she said.

"She gave it away," Rosalind reported. "The berth. To the child."

"To Peter," Adaeze corrected. "He has a name. We're done with nameless people in this town."

Margaret looked at her for a long moment—looked at her the way she had once, according to Hollis's letters, looked at difficult charts: patiently, thoroughly, until the thing confessed its truth. Then the old woman nodded, once, and stepped aside to let them in.

"Then you'll want tea," she said. "And you'll want work. Both are inside. I made inquiries this morning while you two were performing heroics on the quay. The commission needs local assessors—people who know every household—to verify claims for restitution. They asked for names. I gave them yours." A pause, dry as old vellum. "I hope you don't intend to give *that* away as well."

"No, ma'am," Adaeze said, and found she was smiling. "That one I'm keeping."

Inside, the map room smelled of smoke still—old smoke, the ghost of the fire that had nearly taken the atlas—and beneath it, the familiar church-and-rain scent of ink and sizing and paper. The real atlas lay open on the great table where Rosalind had left it, its plates weighted down with polished stones, Hollis's marginalia marching along the edges in his cramped, furious, faithful hand. Names. So many names. Families from three towns, erased from every official record, preserved by a dead man's stubbornness and an apprentice's devotion.

Adaeze had seen the atlas a hundred times. But she had never truly read it until now. Now she saw what it was: not a book of coastlines. A book of people. Her master's love letter to everyone the ledgers forgot.

"Sit," Rosalind said, pulling out a stool for her, the way he used to when they were children and he needed somewhere to pile his drawings and she needed somewhere to eat her supper.

She sat. He ground ink. Margaret poured tea three-handed with her wounded hands, refusing help, and outside the window the light shifted as the afternoon came on, and Vellamouth went about the business of existing without anyone's permission.

"There's something else," Rosalind said, after a while. He said it carefully, the way he drew a coastline he wasn't sure of—light strokes first, testing. "The commission. They'll want statements. From both of us. About the riot, the arrests, Bray, everything. And after the inquiry—" he hesitated.

"After the inquiry," Adaeze prompted, because her brother's silences always had contents and she had spent a lifetime unpacking them.

"After the inquiry, I think they'll offer me something. The Crown. A post abroad, mapping new territories. Better pay than I've ever seen." He looked down at the atlas, at the plate of Vellamouth drawn whole, every restored name inscribed in his careful hand. "And I think I'm going to refuse it."

Adaeze set down her cup. For a moment she couldn't speak—for a moment she was nine years old again counting boats, counting losses, counting the years she'd spent braced for her brother to leave her for his maps, his masters, his world of abstraction where history happened to other people. And here he was, telling her, in his oblique cartographer's way, that he was staying. That whatever came, he had chosen the small town and the living sister and the dead man's book over the empire's gold.

"You're sure?" was all she managed.

"I've never been sure of anything," Rosalind said. "But I'm certain of this. Besides—" and now he smiled, that quick crooked smile that had gotten him out of trouble and into more of it, "someone has to survey the harbour properly before the fleet comes home. And I know a dockworker who counts boats better than any man in the admiralty. I'll need a partner. The pay," he added solemnly, "is terrible."

"The pay," Adaeze agreed, "is terrible." She reached across the table and took his inky hand in her rope-hard one, and neither of them let go for a while, and the tea went cold, and Margaret Hollis pretended to read and let them have their moment, because wise women know when to be invisible.

Outside, the tide turned. It always turned. That was the oldest lesson the harbour taught, and Adaeze Okonkwo had learned it young: nothing stays out forever, not the boats, not the storm, not the fog. Everything comes home on the turn of the tide, if the channel is kept clear and someone tends the light.

Someone would have to tend the light.

* * *

She dreamed that night of the quay, but changed. In the dream the customs house windows glowed yellow, and the fleet was in, nets heavy, and the fishwives sang the old unloading song their mother had sung, and Peter stood at the rail of a ship coming home instead of going away, taller, laughing, whole-armed. And in the dream her parents were on the beach with their backs to the water, faces turned toward the town, and she could not see their features but she knew them by their stillness, by the way they stood as if satisfied—as if the accounting had come out right at last, all the numbers balanced, nothing owed.

She woke before dawn with the dream still warm in her chest and lay listening to the house settle: Margaret moving quietly below despite her burns, starting the stove, insisting on the morning routine as though routine itself were a form of defiance—which, Adaeze supposed, by now it was.

Downstairs, a knock at the door. Early—even for the commission's men, early. Margaret answered it, and Adaeze heard voices, low and urgent, and came down the stairs with her heart already climbing into her throat, because in Vellamouth that year, early knocking had only ever meant one thing.

But Margaret turned to her with an expression Adaeze had never seen on the old woman's face—something between disbelief and fierce, unholy delight—and held out a printed sheet, still smelling of fresh ink, the type slightly crooked, the paper cheap, the words unmistakable.

Tobias Finch had worked through the night. The pamphlets were out.

At the top of the page, above the names—every name, three towns' worth of names, the displaced and the dispossessed and the dead—ran a single line in bold:

*Read who they erased. Read who they intended to erase you as.*

And below, smaller, the printer's mark, and beneath that, the first line of the atlas's dedication, set in type for the first time in its long hidden life:

*For those the ledger forgot.*

Adaeze read it twice. Then she read it aloud, because some things needed to be said into the air of her own kitchen, in her own voice, to be believed.

"Finch says," Margaret reported, her burned hands trembling around the pamphlet, "that by noon it will be in every market square in the county. And that the magistrate—*our* magistrate, the sympathetic one—is calling a session for tomorrow. And that a royal commission arrives within the fortnight." Her eyes were bright and wet. "Your brother's book is loose in the world, Miss Okonkwo. God help anyone who tries to put it back."

Adaeze folded the pamphlet with a docker's care and put it inside her coat, over her heart, where names belonged.

"Then we'd best get to work," she said, and reached for her boots.
