## 31. The Printing of Truth

The press woke like an animal.

Rosalind had never heard anything but the small sounds of the map room—the scratch of pen nibs, the whisper of vellum, Hollis's old clock clearing its throat at the quarter hours. Finch's workshop was another country entirely: a cavern of oiled iron and ink-stained beams where the great flatbed press breathed and shuddered through the night, throwing off heat like a forge, its rhythm so steady and so immense that Rosalind felt it in his sternum, in his teeth, in the soles of his feet. *Clunk. Slide. The roll. The bite.* A page born every forty seconds, each one warm.

"Mind the flywheel," Finch said, not looking up from his composing stick. He was a squat man with forearms like mooring posts and ink worked so deep into the creases of his knuckles that his hands seemed drawn rather than fleshed. He set type the way Rosalind drew coastlines—by feel, by memory, by some private conviction about what the world owed him. "And mind your hope, lad. It gets underfoot worse than grease."

It was past midnight. Margaret sat wrapped in Finch's good coat on a stool near the stove, her bandaged hands resting in her lap—she had refused to let the surgeon's boy rewrap them until the first forme was locked—and watched the pages come off the stone with an expression Rosalind could not name. Not triumph. Something older than triumph. The face of a woman watching her husband's voice come back from the dead.

They had been at it since dusk. The atlas itself lay open on the proof table, the real one, salvaged from smoke and water, its burned corner trimmed away with a razor, and beside it the stack of freshly printed sheets grew and grew: *THE VELLAMOUTH REQUISITION: Being a True Register of the Families Displaced in the Counties of Ashmoor, Wend, and Corvelay, As Recorded by the Late Edmund Hollis, Surveyor and Mapmaker, and Now Set Forth for the Perusal of All Honest Subjects.*

Finch had insisted on the full title. "Names sell nothing," he'd grunted. "Wrongs sell everything. But names are what make it *law*, come the inquiry. So we put both."

Three hundred copies before dawn. That had been his promise, made in the marsh hut with the reeds hissing outside: three hundred by first light, and his two apprentices—silent boys with the wary eyes of poachers—would carry them out at first light on the milk cart, north to Wend, east to Corvelay, while a third route went hand to hand through Vellamouth itself, parish to parish, shop to shop, into the hands of men who owed Finch favors going back twenty years.

Rosalind's work was the plates. The pamphlet carried four of them, reduced and recut by his own hand over eight furious hours: the harbor survey showing the naval depot's foundations already staked; the ledger page facsimile, altered entries and all, with the true names restored in red beneath the clerk's cancellations; Elowen's father's private plan, copied line for line; and last, filling the final leaf, the map of Vellamouth entire—with every displaced family's name inscribed in its rightful place, in Hollis's marginal hand, exactly as the master had recorded them across three counties and eleven years.

He had cut those letters into the block himself, weeping without knowing it, until Margaret had put her unbandaged hand briefly on his shoulder and said, "He would have charged you for smudging the proof," and something in him had unlocked enough to breathe.

*Clunk. Slide. The roll. The bite.*

"Read me the Ashmoor list again," Finch said. His apprentices were sleeping against the paper stock, and he wanted a voice to set type against.

Rosalind read. "Pell. Mardling. Okonkwo." His own family's name, entered in Hollis's hand among the dispossessed of a town none of them had ever lived in—his grandfather's name, he had learned only weeks ago, erased from a register in 1831 as if no one named Okonkwo had ever paid rent or buried children or mended nets there. "Cutter. The widow Seagrave. Fenwick and sons."

"Good," Finch said. "Ink's drying on their resurrection now."

* * *

Dawn came gray and salt-smelling, and with it the milk cart, and with the milk cart the beginning of the thing none of them could afterward stop.

Rosalind walked the first hour of the delivery route himself. Finch had argued against it—"Your face is known, and lately known is dangerous"—but Margaret had overridden them both. "Let him see it," she said. "A man who plants in the dark should stand in the field at harvest, even for one morning." Her hands were fresh-bandaged, stiff as a doll's in their white linen, but her voice had recovered its old schoolroom firmness, the one she had used on Hollis when he talked of giving up.

So they walked together through streets still blue with early fog, and Rosalind carried twelve pamphlets inside his coat against his chest like contraband scripture, which in a sense they were.

At the fish market, Denny Prawle took his copy with the suspicious slowness of a man who could not read well and did not like to say so. But then his wife leaned over his shoulder, and Rosalind watched her finger travel down the fourth plate, the map of the town, and stop. "There," she said. "There's my mother's name. On our street. Printed." She looked up at Rosalind with an expression close to anger. "Who put this here? Who told them her name?"

"You did," Rosalind said gently. "All of you did. Master Hollis only wrote it down."

She took a second copy. Then a third, folding them into her basket beneath the mackerel as though hiding candles from a taxman.

By the chandlery, a knot of dockworkers had gathered around Adaeze—of course around Adaeze, who stood on a bollard reading aloud in her clear carrying voice, her finger under each name, pausing where the crowd needed her to pause. She had been released from custody five days earlier with the charges dissolved in the general collapse of Vance's authority, and she wore freedom the way she wore everything, practically and without apology. When she reached the Pell family entry she stopped, looked up, and found Rosalind in the crowd.

"They're listening," she said, simply, as if reporting the weather. "Brother, they're finally *listening*."

*Clunk,* said Rosalind's heart, in time with a press miles distant. *Slide. The roll. The bite.*

* * *

The clergy came first, because the clergy had always kept the registers the Crown preferred to forget.

Reverend Aldous Crane of Saint Brendan's—a narrow, asthmatic man whom Rosalind knew only as a figure who dozed through town meetings—arrived at Finch's door before nine o'clock, breathing hard, holding copy number eleven. "I baptized half these families," he said without preamble. "Mardling. Seagrave. I christened the Fenwick twins in '39. They were drowned in the crossing to Wend, Mr. Finch, and no one has ever told me why their family was moved in the first place." He held up the pamphlet. "I want two hundred more. My parishioners will read them from the pulpit if it comes to that, and let General Vance—or what's left of his command—hear the reading over the wall."

Finch looked at Rosalind. Rosalind looked at the reverend's trembling hands and thought of Hollis's, guiding a pen across vellum. "Two hundred," Finch said. "On credit. God can settle my account after the inquiry."

Then came the merchants, who required less grace and more arithmetic. Harriet Quill of the Quill Shipping Office arrived at ten with her ledgers under her arm and her son behind her carrying more ledgers. She laid the pamphlet flat on Finch's proof table and tapped the harbor survey. "If the depot proceeds, my berths are seized, my contracts void, and my insurance voided with them. If the requisition is rescinded pending inquiry—which the magistrate tells me is now being discussed—then I am a wronged loyal subject due compensation." Her eyes were bright with the purest motive in the world, self-interest honestly declared, and Rosalind found he did not care at all. "I will fund the paper, Mr. Finch. I will fund the carters. I will fund whatever it costs to make sure every customs house from here to Corvelay has this nailed to its door by Friday. Do we understand each other?"

"We understand each other beautifully, madam," said Finch.

But it was the third visitor who changed the weight of the day.

Magistrate Corwin came at noon, alone, in a plain gig, and stood in Finch's doorway with his hat in his hands like a man calling on a sick relative. He was sixty, pink-faced, careful, a man who had spent thirty years ruling on weights and measures and trespass and the small honest disputes of small honest lives—and whose signature had appeared, months ago, at the bottom of Vance's requisition order, because the order had worn the King's seal and the seal had seemed answer enough.

"I have read this," Corwin said, lifting the pamphlet. "Twice. Once as a magistrate." He stepped inside and closed the door behind him, which he had never done in his life, Rosalind suspected. "And once as the man who countersigned the order that started all of it."

Nobody spoke. The press stood cold between them; Finch's apprentices had melted into the back room; Margaret rose slowly from her stool.

"The requisition cited sanitary necessity under statute," Corwin went on, as if delivering a verdict to himself. "I verified the seal. I verified the statute. I did not verify the claim, because the seal was supposed to *be* the verification. That is how the law works, gentlemen. Madam. That is how it has worked for thirty years, and I hid inside it like a man hiding inside his own house." He set the pamphlet down on the proof table, squared its edges precisely, and looked up, and Rosalind saw that behind the pink caution there was steel that had merely never been asked to hold weight. "The royal commission arrives within days—I have had the correspondence. When it does, there will be depositions taken properly, under oath, before witnesses. I intend to reopen every requisition file in my jurisdiction and enter this document"—he touched the pamphlet once, lightly, as one touches a relic—"into evidence. Names, dates, alterations, all of it. I am told the commission includes two commissioners not appointed by the Admiralty." A thin, dry smile. "I intend to find out whether the law still works when one actually reads it."

Margaret crossed the room and took his hand in her bandaged ones. "My husband believed the law would wake up eventually," she said. "He just doubted he'd live to see it."

"Madam," said Corwin, with a small bow, "it has been struck very hard across the face this morning. I heard it wake from three counties away."

* * *

By afternoon the town had transformed.

Rosalind stood at the window of the chandlery loft—Adaeze had commandeered it as a kind of headquarters, and no soldier alive would have argued—and watched Vellamouth behave like a town again. Groups at corners, yes, but talking instead of whispering. Copies of the pamphlet passing hand to hand like coins, folded and refolded until the folds themselves wore soft. Two fishermen nailing a copy to the customs house board, openly, while a marine detail watched from across the quay and did not move—either because their orders had collapsed with their commanding officer's reputation, or because they had read it themselves, or because young soldiers firing on townsfolk over printed names is a thing even sergeants begin to refuse. Down at the water, women were repainting house numbers that the requisition clerks had chalked over, working in pairs, quick and matter-of-fact, restoring addresses as casually as hanging washing.

"It's moving faster than Finch guessed," Adaeze said beside him. She had the map spread on the loft table, and she was marking it—not coastlines, but people: a cross where a pamphlet had landed, a circle where a reader had promised to carry it inland. Hers was a different cartography than his, and Rosalind understood suddenly that it was the older one. Maps began as trails walked and news carried. Everything else was decoration. "Corvelay by tomorrow. Wend tonight. And once it reaches the cathedral town—" she tapped a point far inland— "it stops being news and becomes *history*. Nobody can unprint it, Rosalind. Not even them. Especially not them."

He thought of Hollis's clock in the ruined map room, clearing its throat at the quarter hours. He thought of the master's rule, spoken a hundred times over a drawing board: *A map is a claim, boy. Ask always—who benefits from this version of the world?* For eleven years the Crown's maps had claimed Vellamouth empty, or nearly; claimed its people temporary, transferable, incidental. By nightfall, three hundred copies and their thousand descendants would be claiming otherwise, in the one archive no soldier could burn: memory, multiplied.

There would be a reckoning for it. He was not naive. Commissioners could arrive and be steered; magistrates could be reversed; a government embarrassed in one season grows ruthless in the next. Somewhere in London, men whose names appeared nowhere in Hollis's margins were reading their own versions of events and composing replies. The land syndicate behind the requisition had money, and money had lawyers, and lawyers had patience. Bray's testimony—the testimony he was trading even now to save his own neck—would need corroborating, and corroboration would need Elowen, stripped of rank and standing before the inquiry had even convened, to keep standing in the witness box and telling the truth at personal ruin, day after day.

But that was tomorrow's weather. Today, the tide had turned, and everyone in Vellamouth could feel it under the keels.

Toward evening, Finch called them down to the workshop one last time. The press was cold; the floor was ankle-deep in paper trimmings; the apprentices were scrubbing ink from the forme with spirits and rags. On the proof table, weighted flat with a brass rule, lay a single sheet pulled aside from the run—the final leaf, the map of Vellamouth entire, every restored name standing in black where cancellation marks had once tried to bury them. Beneath the title block, small and precise, Finch had added a printer's line:

*Set forth from the original atlas of E. Hollis, completed by R. Okonkwo, and given freely to the people of three counties.*

"Printers' privilege," Finch said gruffly. "Every book tells you who made it. Usually it's flattery for some lord. This time it isn't." He cleared his throat and looked hard at the window, at the fading light on the water. "Your master's name would have gone there regardless. Yours went there because you finished it. There's a difference, lad, and history keeps the accounts."

Rosalind picked up the sheet. Three hundred identical brothers and sisters of it were loose in the world now, in vicarages and counting houses and galley kitchens, folded into baskets and nailed to doors, traveling north and east through the dark on milk carts and in fishing smacks, multiplying as they went. Somewhere out there, a woman in Wend was running her finger down a column of names and finding her mother's. Somewhere, a man was reading aloud to a room too full for the room.

His hand ached. His master was dead. The town was wounded and the inquiry was coming and nothing that followed would be clean or simple or cheap.

But the names were out. The names were *out*.

He was still holding the sheet when the door burst open and one of Finch's apprentices skidded in from the yard, breathless, mud to the knees, a rider's whip still coiled in his fist where he'd torn it from the messenger's horse.

"Rider from Corvelay," the boy gasped. "For Magistrate Corwin—came through change-of-horses like the devil's behind him. Commission's orders, sealed and shouted all over the yard." He swallowed. "They've recalled the whole inquiry panel, Mr. Finch. Replaced it. New commissioners appointed by the Admiralty, sitting in private, arriving Thursday. And they're summoning witnesses by name." He fumbled a crumpled copy of the notice from his pocket. "First name on the list, top of the page."

Rosalind already knew whose it would be. He unfolded it anyway, and read it in the cold light of the lamp, and felt the tide he'd celebrated all afternoon pull suddenly, treacherously, back out to sea.

*Miss Elowen Vance—to appear before the Commission at Corvelay, Thursday next, to answer charges of theft of official documents and correspondence with persons inimical to the Crown.*
