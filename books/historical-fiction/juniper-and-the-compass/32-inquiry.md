## 32. Inquiry

The commissioners arrived on a grey morning in three carriages, the kind of grey that made even the harbor look administrative. Elowen watched them from her father's study window—her father's, though he had not been permitted to enter it since the recall order came—watching the lacquered doors open and men in black step down onto the wet cobbles of the square. They wore no uniforms. That was the first thing she noticed, and the thing that frightened her most. Uniforms could be read like charts: rank, regiment, allegiance. These men wore only wool and consequence.

She had dressed in what remained to her. The army had taken the commission itself, of course—the letter arriving two days after the tribunal collapsed into farce, requesting—*requesting*, as though courtesy survived such things—the return of her captain's insignia, her sash, her papers. The envelope had been sealed with the broad arrow. She had packed it all into a single drawer and shut it, and found, when she tried to close the drawer properly, that the sash's brass buckle kept it from sitting flush. It gaped an inch, all night, all day, a small silver of light in the furniture of her life. She had not fixed it. Let it gape.

The dress she wore now was civilian grey-blue, high-collared, plain enough to be mistaken for mourning. Perhaps it was not a mistake.

Below, the commissioners were met by the town's remaining officials—what was left of them—and by Sergeant Bray in irons, delivered by his own escort like a parcel being returned to sender. He did not struggle. He looked, if anything, relieved, the way a man looks who has been carrying something heavy in the wrong direction for a very long time and has finally been told he may set it down, even if setting it down means dropping it on his own foot.

Elowen turned from the window. In the hall mirror her reflection paused with her, a woman she was still learning to recognize: not Captain Vance anymore. Not quite daughter, either—that word had died somewhere between the witness stand and her father's face when she read aloud the figures from his own survey. *Four thousand one hundred souls displaced. Three counties. Eleven years.* She had read them in a steady voice, and she had watched him understand, in front of God and the garrison and the whole assembled town, that his daughter was not going to save him.

He had not spoken to her since. There had been no need. His eyes had said everything worth saying, and none of it had been forgiveness.

She took up her gloves and went down to give her testimony.

* * *

The inquiry sat in the church hall, because the town hall still smelled of smoke from the archive fire and the magistrates' court had been judged—rightly, Elowen thought—too small for what was coming. Benches had been carried in from the schoolhouse. The commissioners occupied a raised platform where the altar would have been, three men behind a long table hung in green baize, with a fourth chair standing empty at the end. She learned later the empty chair was for the chairman of the land syndicate, summoned but not yet arrived, and that everyone in the hall understood the empty chair the way sailors understand a clear horizon: as a promise of weather to come.

She gave her name. Not her rank. The clerk asked for her rank and she said, "I have none," and there was a small sound from the benches, half gasp and half approval, and Commissioner Hargreaves—a dry man with ink-stained cuffs and the patience of a man who had spent thirty years reading other people's lies—asked her to begin at the beginning.

"The beginning," Elowen said, "is a map."

She had thought about this in the nights before. How to say it. What order. She had decided against rhetoric; her father had been a rhetorician, and look where rhetoric had brought him—recalled in disgrace, traveling under escort toward London and a hearing of his own. She would do it the way Rosalind did it. Plain lines. Accurate measurements. Let the shape speak.

"My father received orders eleven years ago to survey the Vellamouth coastline for defensive purposes. The surveys were real. The purpose was not. Over eleven years, the Crown's surveyors produced charts whose marginalia recorded displacement figures—families removed, houses requisitioned, common lands enclosed—in towns along the entire coast. My father collected these figures. He believed, I think at first, that he was documenting necessity. Later, I believe, he was simply documenting. And later than that—" she paused, and chose honesty over mercy, "—he was curating. Selecting which figures reached his superiors and which did not."

"Which did not," Hargreaves repeated.

"The requisition of Vellamouth was presented as a sanitary measure. Fever in the low town. I have here—" she produced the folded papers, "—the surgeon's actual reports, obtained from the garrison infirmary. There was fever, yes. Twelve cases. Six deaths. A serious matter for those families, and I do not diminish it. But twelve cases do not require the removal of four thousand people, nor does a fever care whether a town sits above or below the finest deepwater anchorage between here and the capital."

She heard her own voice grow steadier as she went, and understood something she had not expected to learn in this hall: that testimony was a kind of navigation. You fixed your position by the truth and held your heading, and the currents of the room—the murmurs, the commissioners' faces, her own fear—were simply weather. You sailed through weather.

Commissioner Ashworth, the youngest, leaned forward. "You were present at the summary tribunal of Rosalind Okonkwo. You were called as a witness for the Crown."

"I was."

"And instead you read from documents."

"I read from my father's private survey. The true plan for Vellamouth: a naval depot, permanent fortifications, and no provision whatsoever for the return of the displaced population. The town was never to be given back. The ship was never meant to carry them anywhere except away." She let that stand a moment. "Mr. Okonkwo stood accused of sedition for possessing evidence of this. I ask the commission to consider what word remains, when telling the truth about a theft is the crime and the theft itself is policy."

Silence, of the particular quality that follows a blow landed cleanly. Somewhere behind her, in the crowded benches, someone began to clap—a single pair of hands, then more—and Commissioner Hargreaves rapped the table and said order would be maintained, but he did not sound angry. He sounded like a man taking notes.

They asked her about Bray. She described the trap he had set, the forged order, the decoy atlas seized and triumphantly produced at trial as evidence of coded sedition—an atlas, she noted, that was *beautifully wrong*, its coastlines shifted, its annotations invented, drawn by Mr. Okonkwo's hand with such skill that it had fooled a professional intelligence officer until the moment he examined it under oath. Bray, she said, had turned crown's witness within a day of understanding he had been outdrawn, and everything he testified to corroborated her account.

They asked her, at last, the question she had known was coming, the one the whole hall had come to hear: "Captain Vance—you will forgive the title; habits die slowly in this room—why did you do it? You betrayed your general. Your father. Your uniform. Speak to the commission of your reasoning."

Elowen looked at her hands. There was still a faint scar line on the left one, from the night she had bound a bleeding mapmaker's wound in a doorway and understood, with a clarity that had never afterward left her, exactly which side of every future question she would stand on.

"My reasoning," she said slowly, "was a ledger."

The word moved through the hall like wind through wheat. Everyone in Vellamouth knew ledgers now. Everyone had learned, these bitter months, precisely what a ledger could decide.

"There is a census ledger in the town clerk's office that determined who lived and who was transported, by alphabet and accident. There is my father's survey ledger, recording four thousand people erased so that an anchorage could be owned. And there is the atlas—Hollis's atlas, finished by his apprentice—which records in its margins the names. Not numbers. Names. The Okonkwo family. The Finch household. Every family, written down by a man who was murdered for writing them." She lifted her head. "I was trained my whole life to serve the Crown. But I was trained by a father who taught me that a map is a promise: that what is drawn true will hold. When I discovered the Crown had broken its own promise—had drawn false maps, kept false records, told a town it was being rescued while it was being robbed—my training did not tell me to look away. It told me the chart was wrong, and a wrong chart, in my profession, kills people. I corrected it. That is all. That is the whole of my treason: I corrected the chart."

She sat down. Her knees were shaking and her voice had not, and she thought: *Father, I learned the steadiness from you. Only you taught me to point it the other way.*

* * *

They recalled General Sir Aldous Vance on the third day, and Elowen did not attend.

She told herself it was propriety—daughter testimony concluded, proceedings to be conducted without family present—and it was partly true. But she knew, walking the cliff path north of town in a wind that smelled of incoming rain, that the deeper truth was simpler: she could not watch it and remain the person the testimony required. Pity was the enemy now. If she watched her father's ruin, some traitorous fraction of her heart would spend itself on him, and she needed every fraction intact—for the town, for the inquiry, for the years of appeals and inquiries and quiet revenge that institutions wage against those who embarrass them.

So she walked, and let the wind have her coat, and thought about her father as she had not allowed herself to in weeks: the man who had taught her to read contour lines at seven, laughing at her fury when the hills refused to make sense; the man who had once, she remembered suddenly, refused a bribe from a shipping concern when she was twelve, slamming the door of his study, telling her mother that some things were not for sale. When had the door stopped slam-ming? When had the things-not-for-sale list grown so short that eventually it contained only himself, his rank, and the story he told about his own honor?

By the time she came back down, wet to the knees, the town already knew the outcome, because Vellamouth by now could read official carriages the way Adaeze Okonkwo read tides. Vance had been recalled to London. Stripped temporarily of command pending the full tribunal—not the theatrical kind he had favored, the real kind, with counsel and cross-examination and the syndicate's empty chair now ominously occupied. He had left the hall through the side door, they said, and had not looked at the crowd, and the crowd had done something worse than jeer: it had simply watched him go, four thousand grievances keeping a silence that could be heard in the next county.

Elowen found she could not feel triumphant, and she examined this failure the way she'd examine a chart discrepancy—with professional interest and some unease. Justice was arriving. She could see it coming up the road like weather, slow and grey and real. But justice arriving late was a strange cargo. The dead master Hollis was still dead. Margaret Hollis's hands were still bandaged from the archive fire, the burns healing into scars she would carry past any restitution. Rosalind had given away his berth on the last ship and nearly swung for it. A child named Tam Broadstairs, whom no ledger had ever listed, slept warm now in the Hollis house because a mapmaker had done arithmetic with his own life. The berths were reissued; the requisition rescinded *pending inquiry*—that phrase, *pending*, doing quiet work in every clause, the institution leaving itself doors.

Vindication, she decided, walking down through the lanes as lamps were lit, was a coastline seen from the sea: it looked like arrival, and it was really only another shore to negotiate.

At the Hollis house, Margaret was at the table sorting names—always names, the great patient catalog of the restored—and Rosalind sat across from her with pen and ink, redrawing something by candlelight. He looked up when she came in, and his face did the thing it always did, the small involuntary brightening he probably believed he hid.

"Well?" Margaret asked, not looking up. "How was the empire's conscience today?"

"Slow," Elowen said, hanging her wet coat by the fire, "but moving."

"And your father?"

"Gone to London. To answer for it." She warmed her hands. "They'll want me in London too, eventually. The full tribunal. They'll ask me to say it all again, slower, with more paperwork, until it stops meaning anything."

"And will you?"

Elowen looked at Rosalind. He had stopped drawing. The candle flame stood perfectly still between them, the air of the room holding its breath.

"I'll say it again as many times as they ask," she said. "But I intend to be somewhere worth testifying *from*."

Margaret snorted—her version of applause—and returned to her names. Rosalind turned his paper around on the table so she could see it. Not a chart of the coast. Something else: a rough sketch of a building, drawn in his quick confident hand, with rooms marked out and one large room labeled, in careful letters, *school*.

"What's this?" she asked.

"A survey," he said. "Of premises on Fore Street. Available at a reasonable rate from a landlord who owes Margaret a favor. Good light. Room for a press downstairs—" he nodded toward the news of Finch's intentions, already circulating, "—and room upstairs for teaching." He hesitated, and she saw, with the intimate precision she was beginning to trust, that he was nervous. "If I had a partner who knew the coastline. Someone to teach the practical work. Soundings, bearings. Where the compass is honest and where it lies."

Outside, far off in the dark, the tide was turning in the harbor—the long breath of water against the pilings, the town's oldest clock. Elowen picked up the pen. It was warmer where his hand had been.

"Show me," she said, "where we begin."

But the knock that came at the door just then was heavy and official, three strikes, and Margaret rose to answer it, and the boy on the step carried a paper with a seal Elowen knew too well: London's summons, arriving early, requiring her attendance before the full tribunal—departing on the morning coach. Two days hence.
