## 35. The Empty Frame

The map room had been rebuilt the way a broken bone is set—carefully, and with an ache that never quite left it. Rosalind walked the length of it each morning before the apprentices arrived, his boot heels sounding against new floorboards that had not yet learned to creak like the old ones. The walls were fresh-plastered, pale as unmarked paper. The great drafting table at the center was Hollis's own, saved from the fire with one scorched leg, now braced by timber cut from the same oak as the rest. It stood a half-inch higher on that side. Every drawing made upon it drifted, very slightly, toward the window and the light.

Rosalind had come to think of this as correct.

He carried the portrait under his arm, wrapped in sailcloth. Margaret had found it in the wreckage of the old house—a small oil painting, darkened with age, showing a man she described as *too handsome for how he actually looked, which is how you know it's flattery*. Master Aldous Hollis, aged perhaps forty, brush in hand rather than pen, posed before a half-finished coastline. His eyes in the painting were the eyes Rosalind remembered: patient, amused, always appearing to look just past your shoulder at something more interesting than you, which was usually true.

The frame was gone. Burned or shattered or simply lost in the months of ruin—he did not know. What remained was the canvas and a thin border of raw wood, and so Rosalind had built the frame himself over three evenings in the workshop below, planing pine joints by lamplight, his scarred hand slow but steady on the plane. He had thought, more than once, about what kind of frame a man like Hollis would want. Gilded, never. Something plain. Something that would hold him without pretending to improve him.

He hung it on the north wall, above the flat files where the finished plates slept in their paper shrouds. Then he stepped back and found the room wrong.

Empty. Not beside the portrait—the space around it. A room rebuilt but not yet repopulated. Shelves waiting for atlases that did not exist yet. Hooks for instruments not yet purchased. And beneath the portrait, a stretch of blank wall exactly the size and shape of the great map of Vellamouth that had hung there for forty years, the masterwork of Hollis's prime, which had burned with everything else.

Rosalind stood looking at that absence until his chest hurt.

*You are not here,* he thought at the painted face. *But I am. That will have to be the arrangement.*

He heard them before he saw them—the particular thunder of children who have been told to walk quietly. Three sets of feet on the stair, then Adaeze's voice cutting through like a cleaver: *"I said WALK, Tobias—"*

"Not that Tobias," Rosalind called. "The other one."

They came up single file, trying for solemnity and failing in three distinct ways. Adaeze had brought four of them this time: Tam Broadfoot, the harbor-master's boy, twelve years old and already broad as a bollard; Nesta, the cooper's daughter, small and quick-eyed; a girl named Prue whose family had returned from the inquiry camps two months ago with nothing but their names intact; and little Sam Fenwick, seven years old, who had been pulled half-drowned from the harbor when he was five and had spoken rarely ever since, though he watched everything.

Dock children, all of them. Adaeze's harvest. She sent them up from the quay the way other people sent baskets of fish—with a certain insistence and no apology for the smell.

"This is a schoolroom now, is it?" Tam said, eyeing the drafting table with open suspicion. "Only it looks like a church."

"It's neither," Rosalind said. "It's a workshop. Churches want you on your knees. Schools want you silent. Here I want you working. First lesson."

He took down from the shelf a stack of scrap paper—misprints from Finch's press, blank on one side—and laid out pens, ink, rulers. The children arranged themselves around the table with the wary ceremony of cats discovering a shared sunbeam.

"Before you draw anything," Rosalind said, "you're going to tell me where things are. Nesta. Where is north?"

She pointed immediately at the window. Correct, as it happened, but that wasn't the point.

"How do you know?"

Nesta frowned. "The sea's that way. Sea's west-ish. So north is—" she rotated her hand, "—there."

"Good. You navigated by what you know. That's all mapping is." He turned to Tam. "Where does your father keep his boat?"

"East slip. Third from the stairs."

"Draw me the way from this room to his boat without using any words."

Tam stared at him. Then, slowly, he took the pen and began: the door, the stair, the crooked lane, drawn as lines and blocks, the harbor rendered as a long hatched band, and finally a small rectangle among rectangles with a crude X. It was ugly, disproportionate, and entirely legible. Rosalind felt something turn over in his chest, warm and heavy.

"That," he said, "is a map. Everything else we do here is making it honest."

* * *

They came every morning after that, and within a fortnight there were eight of them, and by spring, eleven. Adaeze delivered them like tribute and inspected the results like a foreman, holding their exercises to the light with her dockworker's hands, ink-stained to the second knuckle herself these days—Rosalind had taught her lettering too, in the evenings, because she claimed she wanted to write bills of lading that couldn't be cheated.

"You've got to teach them the ledger as well as the map," she told him one evening, watching Sam Fenwick labor over a chart of the inner harbor. "That's where they get caught. Nobody showed us how the thing worked until it was eating us."

"I know," Rosalind said.

"Do you?" She looked at him across the lamplight—not unkindly, but straight. "You knew maps before you knew what was written round the edges of them. They'll be born knowing both. That's the difference. That's why you're doing this."

He had no answer, only the memory of his own first evening in this room, nine years old, sent by his father to apprentice under a white master who had looked at him a long moment and then simply handed him a pen, as if the question had been settled before anyone raised it. *Hollis never asked if I could learn,* Rosalind thought. *He asked what I could see.* That was the whole inheritance, really. The rest was instruments and ink.

Sam Fenwick spoke perhaps ten words a week, but his maps were extraordinary. He drew the harbor from memory with soundings marked in his own private notation, depths he could not possibly have measured but somehow knew—where the channel shifted after a storm, where the mud gave way to gravel. When Rosalind asked him how, the boy shrugged and said the water talked to him. Rosalind entered it in his notes without comment. Half of cartography was listening to people the ledgers didn't bother to ask.

Prue asked the question he had been dreading in February, when the afternoons were still grey and the stove smoked.

"Mr. Okonkwo. Why'd the general burn the maps? If they were true?"

The room went quiet. Even Tam stopped carving his initials into the bench.

"Because true things are dangerous when enough people can read them," Rosalind said. "A lie only works if it's the only story going. Hollis wrote down the names they wanted forgotten. So they killed him for it, and burned the proof." He paused. "It didn't work. Do you know why?"

Prue shook her head.

"Because he taught me first. And I taught Mrs. Finch's press, and the press taught three counties." He tapped the table. "Knowledge isn't a thing you can burn while it's still walking around inside somebody. That's what this room is for. Making sure it keeps walking."

* * *

Margaret came on Thursdays.

She arrived at first, when the burns on her arms were still wrapped in linen, sitting in the chair by the stove while the children worked, correcting their coastlines in a dry voice that carried surprising authority. She had spent thirty years sharpening quills for a master cartographer; she knew a bad line faster than Rosalind did.

"The child's estuary is fiction," she announced of Tam's third attempt. "Rivers don't widen because the boy got tired of drawing them."

"Yes, Mrs. Hollis."

"And his compass rose has north pointing at the pie shop."

"The pie shop *feels* like north," Tam muttered, and Margaret laughed—one short bark, quickly suppressed, the laugh of a woman still learning that laughing was permitted again—and something eased in the room that had been tight since the fire.

By April she was teaching. Not formally; she would have refused the word. But Thursdays became *her* day, and the children knew it, and brought her their worst work to be savaged and their best work to be quietly praised, and she sat in Hollis's chair by the stove with her scarred hands folded and presided over the whole enterprise like a small, sharp-eyed queen.

One Thursday, near closing, she rose stiffly and went to stand beneath the portrait. She looked at it a long time.

"He'd have hated this," she said. "All these children mauling his instruments."

Then, softer: "He'd have hated it wonderfully."

Rosalind came to stand beside her. In the painting, Hollis gazed past them both toward some coastline neither could see.

"There's an empty frame's worth of wall under him," she said. "What goes there?"

"The town," Rosalind said. "The new map. All the names back where they belong. I've been drawing it since the restitution—I'm slow because I keep adding people." He hesitated. "I want the margins full this time. Not hidden names. Open ones. Every family, every house, inscribed right where everyone can read them. Hollis hid the truth in the margins because he had to. I want to put it in the middle of the page."

Margaret was quiet for a while. Her reflection in the window glass seemed to lean against the painted man.

"He used to say a margin was where the map kept its conscience," she said at last. "Put yours in the middle, then. Let the conscience hold up the whole sheet." She turned away from the portrait. "Now sit down, Mr. Okonkwo, because Nesta's meridian is drifting east and someone ought to say so before it reaches France."

* * *

Summer came to Vellamouth the way it always had—through salt haze and the cries of gulls, the harbor filling with fishing boats instead of transports, the quay loud again with commerce that belonged to it. The royal commission had come and gone, leaving behind subpoenas and a magistrate's clerk who took rooms at the Anchor and asked too many questions in a pleasant voice. Elowen walked the headland most mornings with her surveying chain, mapping the coast for fishermen now, unpaid, unranked, unrepentant; sometimes she came by the workshop in the evening and Rosalind taught her projections by lamplight, and those evenings were the ones he did not describe to anyone, because some things kept better unwritten.

On a bright Tuesday in June, the workshop full and noisy, Rosalind pinned the finished plate to the wall beneath the portrait.

It had taken him four months. Vellamouth, drawn whole: the harbor with its true soundings in Sam Fenwick's careful figures, the lanes with their houses marked and named—Broadfoot, Fenwick, Okonkwo, Hollis—the marsh road, the fort reduced to its actual footprint, labeled plainly *former military works*. And in the center of the sheet, where the title cartouche would traditionally sit, framed in scrollwork Finch had printed from woodcuts: the roll of families. Not hidden, not coded, not marginal. Set in the very heart of the map, in letters any child could read, the names of every household the Crown had displaced, each one followed by the word *restored*.

The children stood before it in silence, which for them was unprecedented.

"It's not finished," Rosalind said. "Maps never are. The day you think a map is finished is the day someone starts erasing from it." He touched the lower corner, where space remained blank. "There'll be more names someday. Good ones. Your names, maybe, on charts you draw yourselves."

Tam raised his hand, an affectation he'd adopted from nobody.

"What's the empty bit for, then? Bottom corner."

"For whatever comes next," Rosalind said. "That's what an empty frame means. It means somebody trusted you to fill it."

* * *

That evening he stayed late alone. The children's work dried on the racks; the stove ticked as it cooled; through the window the tide was turning, the harbor breathing its slow breath. Rosalind took down his pen one last time and stood before the great plate, considering the final line.

Underneath the roll of restored names, beneath even Finch's printed dedication, he wrote in his own smallest, steadiest hand:

*Drawn by R. Okonkwo, in the room of A. Hollis, who began it.*

He blotted it, and stepped back, and felt the grief move through him—not as absence anymore, but as weight properly distributed, load-bearing, the way a keel holds a ship upright by pulling downward.

The knock came at the street door. Two raps, unhurried.

When he opened it, the magistrate's clerk from the Anchor stood on the step with his hat in his hands, and behind him, at the corner of the lane, waited a carriage with government arms on its door and a tall woman descending from it whom Rosalind recognized, with a cold settling in his stomach, from the gallery of the tribunal.

"Mr. Okonkwo," the clerk said pleasantly. "Forgive the hour. The Commission would like a word about your atlas—and about the offer we're instructed to make you."
