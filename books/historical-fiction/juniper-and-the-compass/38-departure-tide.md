## 38. Departure Tide

Adaeze Okonkwo had counted boats her whole life. Boats coming in, boats going out, boats riding at anchor with their lights doubled in the black water like a second town drowned beneath the first. She had learned it as a girl on the quay, her father's hand on her shoulder, teaching her that a dockworker who could not count was a dockworker who could be cheated. Numbers were how the powerless kept their footing. So she stood at the harbour mouth before dawn with her hands jammed in the pockets of her coat—her good coat, the one she'd worn to their mother's funeral—and counted the last transport as it woke.

One ship. That was all the Crown had promised Vellamouth, and one ship was what the Crown had sent: the *Perseverance*, three masts and a hull scarred from troop service, her guns struck belowdecks now because there was no longer anyone left to frighten. Adaeze watched the lamplighters on her deck move like slow insects. Watched the gangway crew unship the boarding planks they would not need. Watched the fog lie along the water like a held breath.

Behind her, the town was also waking, but differently than it had woken for six weeks. No ration queue snaking from the corn store. No redcoats at the corner of Hollis Lane checking stamps against names. The checkpoint at the quay gate stood empty, its ledger table abandoned, a single discarded form skittering across the cobbles in the wind off the water. She bent and picked it up. It was blank. Somehow that seemed right. All those pages of names, all that arithmetic of who deserved to live where—and here was one they'd never gotten to fill.

"You'll wear a hole in the stones," said Tobias Finch beside her. The printer had come up from the marsh two days after the inquiry commissioners arrived, blinking in daylight like a man released from a cellar he'd forgotten he volunteered for. His coat still carried the smell of ink and peat. "Standing in the same spot since before light."

"I'm counting," Adaeze said.

"Counting what? There's only the one."

"That's why I have to count it." She did not smile, but something moved at the corner of her mouth. "Numbers are how you keep your footing, Finch. My father's rule."

"And where is your brother?"

"Where he always is now. The map room. He hasn't slept properly since the fire—since Margaret—" She stopped. The words still caught sometimes, on Margaret's name, on the memory of the old woman's bandaged hands and her stubborn, crooked spine straightening day by day in the front parlour while neighbours brought broth she refused to eat in bed. "She's making him eat something. That's the only reason he isn't here already."

The fog was beginning to lift off the water, and as it thinned, the town behind her came into focus the way towns do at that hour: the fish-market awnings going up, smoke rising crooked from a dozen chimneys, the gulls resuming their ancient argument over the gutting tables. Six weeks ago Adaeze had believed she would never see this again without grief attached to every sight of it. Now the grief was still there—it would always be there; she understood now that some griefs do not leave, they simply learn to walk beside you—but threaded through it ran something else, something she had no better word for than *ours*.

* * *

They began gathering on the quay an hour after dawn, and they gathered the way water finds a level—not called, not organized, just drawn. Adaeze watched them come and knew each arrival by its story.

Old Mrs. Petch came down Fore Street leaning on the arm of the grandson she had almost lost, the boy Rosalind had given his berth to—the child whose injured leg had started all of it, or finished it, depending on how you told the story now. The leg had healed crooked but healing. The boy broke from his grandmother's grip the moment his feet touched the quay and ran to the bollard where Adaeze stood, the way children run when they have decided someone belongs to them.

"Did it come?" he demanded.

"It came," Adaeze said, and took the paper from her pocket. The order of rescission, signed by the royal commission's own clerk, stamped twice, posted on the church door yesterday morning and copied out by Finch's press before the ink on the original was dry. *The requisition of the town of Vellamouth is suspended pending final inquiry. Householders may return to their properties. All sailing lists are void.* She let the boy hold it. He couldn't read more than half the words, but he held it the way other children held marbles or knives, with the gravity of possession.

Down the quay, the dockworkers arrived in a body, as they did everything now. Adaeze had not set out to lead them. She had set out, six weeks ago, to make the loading of army cargo go slowly, and slow loading had become miscounted manifests, and miscounted manifests had become a solidarity so complete that the army had eventually stopped trying to load anything at all. Now Tam Oundle, who had once crossed the street to avoid her because her family were strangers to the town, clapped her shoulder as he passed and said, "Ship won't take our people out, missus. Ship won't get so much as a coil of our rope."

"We don't want their rope," said another voice, and there was laughter, brief and bright as a struck match.

The Widow Hollis came at half past eight, walking slowly, refusing the chair Tam tried to fetch her, because Margaret Hollis had spent seventy years refusing things and saw no reason to reform at the finish. Her hands were still wrapped where the fire had taken skin from her palms and the backs of her fingers, but she had insisted on gloves being cut away at the fingertips so she could feel what she touched. She reached the rail near Adaeze and looked out at the ship for a long moment.

"He'd have drawn her," Margaret said at last. "Edmund. Any vessel in the harbour, he drew her, even the ugly ones. Especially the ugly ones." Her ruined hand made a small motion in the air, the ghost of a pen stroke. "He said everything that floats deserves an honest portrait, because everything that floats can sink."

Adaeze had loved this woman distantly, politely, for years—the mentor's widow, the sad house next to the map room. She loved her now with the specific ferocity the fire had forged, watching her stand at the rail with her burned hands and her unbent back, and she could not speak for a moment. When she could, she said only, "He's finishing the last plate today."

"I know. I sat with him while he inked the coastline. He has your master's patience, that boy, though he'd deny the relationship." Margaret's eyes stayed on the water. "Both of them. Patience and stubbornness in equal measure, which is the only inheritance worth leaving anyone."

* * *

Elowen Vance came down to the quay alone, in civilian clothes, and the crowd parted for her the way it parted now—for her, and not against her. It had taken the town time. Adaeze understood that; she had needed the time herself. There was no forgiving in a straight line. But a woman who read her father's own figures aloud to a tribunal, knowing what it would cost, had paid for her standing on the quay in a currency no one could counterfeit.

She stopped beside Adaeze at the bollard. For a while neither spoke. The *Perseverance* was weighing anchor with the ponderous ceremony of all large reluctant things, capstan clicking, men moving along her decks.

"She sails empty," Elowen said.

"Not empty," Adaeze said. "She sails with nothing of ours on her. That's different."

It had been Rosalind's phrase first, spoken the night before in the map room with ink to his elbows, and Adaeze had taken it without asking, the way siblings take each other's best sentences. She watched the anchor break the water dripping, watched the great hull begin to yield to the tide, and felt the sentence settle into truth. Every name the ledger had weighed was still ashore. Every family the margins had recorded stood on this quay or slept in these streets. The ship that had come to carry Vellamouth away was leaving carrying nothing but its own crew and its own shame.

The fog was lifting off the water properly now, drawing back from the harbour mouth in long grey ribbons, and the town emerged into a clean cold light. Adaeze looked and saw them all: the Petches at the rail; Tam and the dockworkers with their arms folded like a jury; the Reverend Mr. Callow with his prayer book closed under his arm, because today was not, he had announced, a day for prayers but for witness; the chandler's widow; the girls from the net-loft; the schoolmaster who had quietly taught forged-stamp detection to half the youth of the lower town and never once been charged. And scattered through them, faces she had known all her life and faces barely six weeks old, neighbors who had bribed and betrayed in the terrible arithmetic weeks and been forgiven anyway, because Vellamouth had learned in those weeks that survival purchased with betrayal is not survival, and forgiveness is not forgetting but a decision to keep counting together.

Her chest did something complicated. She let it.

"Your brother," Elowen said, softly, "is coming down the hill at a dead run."

Adaeze turned. Rosalind was indeed descending from Hollis Lane at a pace that would have scandalized Master Hollis, coat unbuttoned, one bootlace trailing, a folio case clamped under his arm, and Margaret Hollis following at her own fixed pace calling after him about eating and about running with valuable documents and about the state of his collar. He reached the quay breathless, and the crowd opened for him too, and someone—Tam, probably—started a low sound that might have become applause, and Adaeze saw her brother flush to the roots of his hair and pretend he hadn't.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm exactly—" He caught his breath. "I finished it."

"The atlas?"

"The atlas." He held the folio case against his chest the way the boy held the rescission order, with the gravity of possession. Under his arm, wrapped in oilcloth, was the book that had cost Edmund Hollis his life and Margaret her hands and Rosalind his berth and Adaeze four nights in a cell she did not regret. Somewhere inside it, beneath the plates and the coastlines and the soundings, were printed openly now—all of Finch's night work, all three counties' worth of pamphlet evidence folded back into its source—every displaced name, every erased family, every line of the patterned theft the Crown had dressed as sanitation and rescue.

"What did you write?" Adaeze asked. "At the end. You said you'd added a line of your own."

Rosalind looked at her, then past her at the ship, then at the crowd, at Margaret arriving pink-faced at his elbow, at Elowen standing quiet at the rail, at the boy with the healed-crooked leg. He didn't answer right away, and Adaeze recognized the look: it was the look he'd had at fourteen, hunched over charts, when the answer to a problem of projection came to him and he wanted to hoard it a moment longer before giving it to the world.

"There's a place for it," he said finally. "On the last plate. Where a dedication goes. Hollis left the space blank and told me once that a mapmaker should know which spaces to leave empty." His voice went rough at the edges. "I filled it."

"With what?"

But the ship's horn sounded then, one long note rolling across the water and breaking against the hills, and nobody answered anything, because the *Perseverance* was turning her bow toward the harbour mouth and the whole quay had gone quiet to watch her go.

* * *

She cleared the point at half past ten, and the fog lifted off the water entirely, as if the sea itself had decided to give the town one unobstructed view of its own deliverance. The *Perseverance* grew smaller against the bright water, her canvas taking the wind, and Adaeze counted her—once, twice, a third time, out of habit, out of devotion, out of her father's rule—until counting failed and there was only a shape, and then less than a shape, and then the ordinary glittering emptiness of the open sea.

Nobody cheered. That surprised her at first, and then it didn't. Cheering was for victories, and this was not quite a victory; it was a returning, which is a quieter thing. What happened instead was that the crowd on the quay turned inward, toward each other, and began—to talk. To argue about the fish market reopening. To compare notes on which houses needed re-glazing and which roofs the winter would test. To laugh, tentatively and then not tentatively, at something Tam Oundle said about Sergeant Bray's face when the decoy atlas was proven false. The Reverend Mr. Callow shook hands with the chandler's widow. Mrs. Petch sat down on a bollard and wept comfortably into her shawl while her grandson patted her arm with tremendous condescension. The net-loft girls started a work song just to see if the acoustics of a free harbour had changed. They had.

Adaeze stood in the middle of it with the wind pulling at her coat and felt the exhale move through the town like a tide going out—one vast, collective release of six weeks of held air—and she understood that this, and not any proclamation, was the moment Vellamouth became theirs again. Not granted back. Taken back, by standing on its own stones and talking about fish.

Margaret Hollis found her hand in the crowd and gripped it with burned fingers that did not flinch.

"Go home," the old woman said. "All of you. Go home and live loudly. That's the only revenge worth the trouble." Then, softer, watching Rosalind and Elowen standing a little apart at the rail, heads bent over the folio case: "And those two will want the afternoon light if they mean to start surveying the headland. A man should ask a woman properly before he teaches her theodolites."

"He already asked. She already said yes. They're arguing about tidal tables."

"Naturally." Margaret smiled with her whole ruined, radiant face. "That's how it starts."

The fog was gone. The sun stood clear over the harbour, and the water lay open in every direction, and the town behind them rang with hammers as shutters that had been closed six weeks came open one after another down Fore Street like a slow round of applause.

Rosalind raised his head from the folio and looked at his sister across the crowd, and mouthed one word—*home*—and Adaeze nodded, and turned toward Hollis Lane, and began to walk.

Behind her, at the map room window, the light was already laid out on the drafting table like a gift. There was one plate left to hang, one frame waiting on the wall, and beyond the window, on the bare hill above the churchyard, Adaeze had noticed that morning—a small green shoot, defiant against the season, pushing up through the fresh-turned earth of Edmund Hollis's grave.
