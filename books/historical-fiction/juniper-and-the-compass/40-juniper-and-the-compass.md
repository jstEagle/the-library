## 40. Juniper and the Compass

The schoolhouse at the top of Chandler's Lane had been a sail loft once, and it still smelled faintly of tar and canvas when the sun warmed the roof. Rosalind Okonkwo had kept the beams—low, dark, scarred with a century of hands hauling rope—and hung his charts from them on iron hooks, so that walking into the room was like walking through a fleet at anchor. On spring mornings the light came in off the water and moved across the hanging maps like weather, shadows of coastlines drifting over the heads of the children seated below.

There were nineteen children this term. Three of them Adaeze had sent up from the docks herself: the Ferro girls, whose father mended nets, and a small fierce boy named Tam Wrenn who had been born the winter of the requisition and who, Rosalind suspected, had been born angry and simply never found reason to stop. Tam sat at the front. He always sat at the front. This morning he had his finger stabbed down onto the great chart of Vellamouth Harbour like a man pinning a beetle.

"That mark's wrong," he said. "You drew the old breakwater. It fell down."

"Did it fall," Rosalind said, "or was it pulled down?"

Tam blinked. The other children went quiet, sensing the shift in air that meant Mr. Okonkwo was about to do the thing he did.

"Look again," Rosalind said. He came around the front table slowly, his left hand—the one that had never fully straightened after the rooftops, after Bray's trap, all those years ago now—resting easy against his coat. "This chart is dated. Every chart is dated. That is the first lesson and the last lesson and all the lessons in between. A map is not the world. It is a *claim* about the world, made by someone, for a purpose. So before you trust a single line on it, you ask three questions." He held up the crooked fingers one by one, and the children recited with him, in the bored sing-song of ritual that he loved precisely because they did not yet understand it.

"Who drew it. Who paid for it. Who disappears from it."

"Who disappears from it," Tam repeated, and something in the boy's voice caught, as if the words had snagged on something inside him on their way out.

"Good," Rosalind said. "Now. Who can tell me why this breakwater might have been drawn standing when it had already fallen?"

A girl in the back—Maren Ferro, nine years old and sharp as broken glass—raised her hand. "Because somebody wanted the harbour to look safe. For the ships."

"Somebody wanted the harbour to look *available*," Rosalind agreed. "Now you are reading maps the way your town taught me to read them. The hard way."

He turned back to the chart and let them laugh at some small joke Maren made, and while their laughter filled the room he stood very still in the moving light and felt, as he did most mornings now, the strange double weight of it: grief and peace laid over each other like two transparencies, each making sense of the other.

Twenty-two years. Long enough that the children in front of him thought of the requisition as history, a story told at festivals, its villains named in plays Adaeze still refused to attend because, she said, she had lived the play and preferred the festival food. Long enough that Sergeant Bray had died in prison two winters past and Margaret Hollis had wept at the news—not for him, she said, but for herself, because hatred, once you have carried it that far, becomes a limb, and losing it hurts. Long enough that Elowen's hair, braided back this morning with the same severe practicality she had worn her captain's knots, showed silver at the temples, and Rosalind found the silver more beautiful than he knew how to say, and so said nothing, which she had long ago learned to translate correctly.

* * *

At midday he walked down to the churchyard alone, as he did every Friday, carrying the small tin of oil he used on the gravestone. The path went past the rebuilt quay—wider now, honest in its proportions—and along the low wall where Adaeze had counted boats in another life, and out to where the ground rose gently toward the sea. Hollis lay at the top of the rise, under the juniper.

They had planted it the first spring after the inquiry, a sapling no taller than Tam Wrenn, taken from a cutting Margaret's sister had sent from upcountry. Margaret had chosen the spot herself, standing at the graveside with her arm still bound from the fire, and had said only, "He liked things that grow slowly and keep their shape in bad weather." Twenty-two years later the tree was broader than the stone, its branches dense and blue-green, heavy with berries in the autumn, and the salt wind had bent it seaward without ever managing to break it, which Rosalind had come to think of as the most accurate portrait of the man that anyone had produced.

He wiped the lichen from the carved letters with his sleeve. ALDERN HOLLIS, MASTER OF MAPS. TEACHER OF HANDS AND EYES. Below that, newer, cut three years ago at Margaret's direction when her own strength began to fail: AND HIS SCHOLARS, WHO BECAME MANY.

"He became many," Rosalind told the stone. "You would be insufferable about it."

Above him the juniper moved in the wind with a sound like distant surf, or like pages turning. He stood there the length of time it took the church bell to strike the half hour, and then he went back down the hill, because the afternoon class was the older students, and the older students were beginning the atlas.

* * *

They used the second printing now, of course. Finch's press had run the plates four times over the years—once in desperation by lamplight in a marsh barn with soldiers on the roads; once in triumph after the inquiry, publicly, with a subsidy from the very Crown that had tried to drown it; twice more since, corrected, expanded, Rosalind never quite able to leave a plate alone until the day he died, Elowen teased, and probably not even then. But for teaching he brought out the first edition, the rough one, the one printed overnight on paper that was still damp in places, because the flaws were the lesson. He would set the book open on the front table and let the children find the errors—the smudged soundings off Gull Point, the name OKONKWO misspelled in the plate list—and then he would tell them what those flaws had cost and what they had bought.

Today he opened it to the final plate. Vellamouth, drawn whole. He had finished it twenty-two years ago in the rebuilt map room with the smell of smoke still in the plaster, working by daylight for the first time in his life, no candle, no fear, Margaret asleep in the chair by the window with a blanket over her knees. He had inked the town as Hollis had taught him: every street true to its measure, every house marked, and beneath the houses, in the margin where Hollis had once hidden the names of the displaced in cipher and shorthand, the names printed openly, in rows, in a hand Rosalind had made steadier than his own on purpose, so that it would read clearly for a hundred years.

Below the dedication, one line of his own. He had argued with himself over it for a week. It was too plain, he had thought. It was not clever. It did not sound like an atlas. Then he had remembered that Hollis had never once been clever when simple would do, and he had written it, and Finch had set it in type without a word, and it had outlived everything else on the page.

FOR THOSE THE LEDGER FORGOT.

He read it aloud to the class, because he always read it aloud, and because Tam Wrenn, who could not have explained why, had asked him to.

"What was the ledger?" asked little Jonas Pike, six years old, all elbows and questions.

Rosalind considered how much to give him. He had learned—from Hollis, from Margaret, from Elowen—that the trick was never to decide for children how much truth they could hold. You handed them the whole weight of it and trusted their spines.

"A ledger is a book of accounts," he said. "Money in, money out. The men who took our town kept one, but instead of money they wrote people. Whose house. Whose boat. Whose grandmother. In column, out of column. And the rule of their arithmetic was simple: if your name was not written, you did not exist, and if you did not exist, anything could be done to you." He let that sit. Through the windows behind him the harbour glittered, ordinary and miraculous, fishing boats coming in on the afternoon tide, none of them requisitioned, all of them belonging to themselves. "Your great-great-grandparents' names are in that atlas because a dying man spent his last months writing them down, and an old woman carried the book through a fire, and a soldier tore up her own career to read numbers aloud in a courtroom, and a printer risked the gallows to set them in type. Memory is not free, Jonas. Somebody always pays for it. The least we owe the payers is attention."

Tam Wrenn was staring at the page. Not at the map—at the margin. At the rows of names.

"Sir," he said slowly, "my gran's name is in there. Third row. *Wrenn, Tamsin.*" His voice did something complicated. "She never told me it was in a book."

"It's in several books now," Rosalind said gently. "That was rather the point."

The boy ran his finger down the column, lips moving, and Rosalind watched him and understood—as he understood perhaps once a season, and forgot, and understood again—that the whole architecture of those terrible years had resolved into this: a dock child in a sunlit room, finding his grandmother's name where a government had intended there to be nothing at all.

* * *

Elowen came at dusk, as she always did on Fridays, walking up from the harbour with her instrument case slung across her back and her boots wet to the ankle because she still, after all these years, refused to use the new paved section of the quay. She called it "surveyors' privilege," which meant she liked the way the wet stones looked. They ate bread and cheese and pickled herring at the long table among the sleeping charts, and she told him about her day: the coastal survey, section fourteen, the stretch below Harrow Cliffs where the fishermen had asked her to chart the new shoal the winter storms had shifted. For the almshouse fund, she said. The fishermen wanted fair notice of where the water deepened and where it lied, and they wanted it on paper no admiralty had signed. Her old training ran through everything she did—the precision, the notation, the habit of command—but it pointed now in the opposite direction from the one it had been built for, and she had never once, in twenty years, looked back at what she'd walked away from with anything except relief.

"The commission papers came," she said, partway through the herring, in the tone she used for weather. "Final settlement on the depot lands. Signed, sealed, the whole theatre. Vance's name is nowhere in it, of course. He's in the colonies somewhere, mapping rivers for men who don't ask what the rivers are for."

"Do you hate him still?"

She thought about it honestly, which was one of the thousand reasons. "Less than I pity the rivers," she said, and Rosalind laughed until he had to put down his cup.

Afterward they climbed the narrow stair to the loft where the original atlas lived now, in its glazed case, under lock, with the key on a ribbon around Rosalind's neck. He unlocked it because she asked him to, though neither of them needed to see it—they both knew it plate by plate, the way other couples knew songs. But knowing was not the same as looking, and looking was a kind of keeping faith. The candlelight moved over Hollis's coastlines, over the marginalia in two dead-and-living hands, over the burn shadow along one corner where Margaret's blood and the fire's damage met on the same leaf, and Elowen stood with her shoulder against his shoulder and said, quietly, "It holds."

"It holds," Rosalind said.

That was their whole liturgy, some nights. It held. It held. Twenty-two years of conspirators, magistrates, clergy, printers, widows, sisters, and children, and the book still lay open to the future like a hand.

* * *

Later, unable to sleep, he went down to the map room and lit one candle—he still worked by candlelight sometimes, deliberately, the way other men visited old battlefields—and pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward him.

There was one more map he had been avoiding. He had been avoiding it, if he were honest, for three years, which was to say since Margaret's funeral, since the line of mourners that stretched from the churchyard gate to the harbour road, since Adaeze's hand hard on his shoulder and Elowen's umbrella tilted against the rain over them both. Margaret had left him a letter, and in the letter a request, and the request was simple and enormous: *Finish the county survey Aldern started before Vellamouth. All the erased towns. The three that came before us, and any others the ledgers swallowed. Do for them what you did for us. I have seen how long your memory lasts, and I am betting the rest of mine on it.*

Hollis's original notes for it were here in the drawers—thin, scattered, thirty years old, the work of the years before Vellamouth, before the murder, before everything. Towns whose names had been struck from the county rolls. Parishes where the census simply stopped one year and the fields changed hands. Rosalind had redrawn Vellamouth whole; he had taught children to ask who disappears; but this was larger. This was years of fieldwork, of walking drowned roads and interviewing old women whose grandmothers remembered different village names. This was the rest of his life, laid out on the table like an unrolled chart.

He dipped the pen. His crooked left hand braced the paper the way it always had, the old injury finally revealed as preparation, as though the world had known all along what it would need him for.

At the top of the sheet, in a careful hand, he wrote the title: *An Atlas of the Forgotten Counties.* Beneath it, smaller: *begun by A. Hollis. continued by R. Okonkwo. to be finished by others.*

Then he ruled his first line, north to south, true and clean, and outside the window over the sleeping town the wind came up off the water and moved through the streets and out toward the churchyard on the rise, where the juniper stood bent but unbroken over the master of maps, keeping its shape in the dark, waiting for morning, and for the children, and for whatever else the tide brought in.
