## 1. The Weight of Letters

There is a particular silence that lives inside a type foundry at night, and I know it the way other people know their mothers' voices. It is not an empty silence. It is the sound of forty thousand letters holding their tongues.

I was cutting a lowercase *g* by lamplight, the third one I had ruined that evening, and I was beginning to take it personally.

The trouble with a double-story *g* is the ear — that small enclosed bowl at the top, no wider than a lentil at body size. In punchcutting you work in reverse, always in reverse: you carve the letter mirrored and inverted into steel, and if your hand drifts a hair's breadth to the left, somewhere in the future a thousand printed pages will say *g* when they meant to say *q*, and some clerk in some countinghouse will hang for it, or worse, will merely misfile something. Letters are patient that way. They wait years to betray you.

My father says — my father *said*, back when he still said things aloud at the supper table instead of merely occupying his chair like furniture that had once been spoken to — that a punchcutter must have two souls: one to make the cut and one to forgive it. He has one soul, as far as I can tell, and it is made entirely of lead, and he was asleep upstairs while I ruined good steel over his bench, which he would have objected to on principle, since the bench is his and the steel costs four shillings the bar and sleep, in his accounting, is a form of theft from the day.

The lamp guttered. I trimmed the wick with my fingers, an old habit; the burn never hurts anymore, which Tamsin Crowe once told me was unnatural and I told her was practice, and neither of us believed the other.

Let me tell you what the shop is, since you will need to know it, and since no one else is going to describe it to you accurately — the army's surveyors drew it last spring as "warehouse, disused," which wounded my father more than any insult ever leveled at his daughter.

Underhill & Sons occupies the ground floor of a crooked building on Candlewick Lane, three doors up from the chandler who smells perpetually of tallow and grievance. The front room is the showroom: a counter, a case of specimen type arranged like jewelry, and a small brass bell above the door that rings so softly most customers pretend not to hear it, which suits my father, who regards customers as weather — to be endured, occasionally profitable. Behind the showroom is the casting room, where the furnace squats like a sleeping boar and the matrices sit in their cabinets in ranks, each one a letter reduced to a hollow in lead-tin alloy, each one capable of making ten thousand copies of itself before it dies. Then the finishing room, where the cast type is dressed and sorted into cases — upper, lower, each compartment its own small country. And behind everything, through a door my father keeps locked and I keep mapped in memory, the punch room, where the letters are born backward in steel.

*Underhill & Sons.* There are no sons. There never were sons. There is a dead wife named Miriam whose name appears nowhere on the signage, and there is me, and the sign was painted eleven years before my birth, when my grandfather still hoped, and my father simply never amended it, because amending a sign means talking to a sign painter, and my father would rather die.

So: Cyrus Underhill. That is what the town knows. The foundry's boy.

It is a strange thing, being a fiction you did not write. When I was six, after my mother died, my father stopped calling me Juniper — my mother's choice, her one victory over his imagination; she wanted a name like a flowering thing and he had wanted, I suspect, nothing at all — and began calling me Cyrus. Not gradually. All at once, the way a man turns a coat against weather. *Cyrus, hand me the tongs. Cyrus, mind the furnace. Cyrus, if anyone asks.* If anyone asks what? I asked, aged six, and he said, *Nothing. No one will ask. That is the point.*

He was right. No one asked. Grayharbor looked at the silent child trailing the silent founder and saw what it expected to see: an heir, thin and unsociable, learning a dying trade. Boys learn trades. Girls learn nothing that gets written down. My father understood, earlier than most men, that in this century a name is a kind of clothing, and clothing can be changed, and the changing is legal but the changing back is not.

So I became Cyrus the way iron becomes a tool — under heat, under blows, without much consultation.

And here is the confession I will make only once, here at the beginning, because it is the truest thing I know about myself: I do not miss her. Juniper is a word I know the way I know Latin — studied, unused. What I feel about the name is closer to embarrassment than grief. Somewhere under my skin there may be a girl who answers to it, but if so she has kept very quiet for twelve years, and I have learned not to disturb quiet things. Quiet is a craft, too. I am very good at it.

* *

Grayharbor in October is a town pretending to sleep.

You hear it best around two in the morning, when the fog comes off the water and lies down in the streets like something that intends to stay. Sound carries strangely in fog. Voices arrive from nowhere, belonging to no one. A dog barks on Rope Walk and you cannot say which end of the street owns it. The whole town becomes a rumor of itself.

I finished the *g* at half past two. It was good work — the ear true, the bowl round, the link between them cut fine enough to hold ink without choking on it. I set it on the bench and looked at it for a long time, because that is the other thing about this trade: you spend all night making something no one will ever see. Ten thousand little g's will march across ten thousand pages and every reader will pass them without a glance, and that is the mark of success. Invisible competence. I could think of no better description of my life, and I laughed, quietly, alone, which is the only way I laugh.

Then I heard the footsteps.

Not my father's — his tread has a drag on the left side, an old injury he refuses to discuss, from the mill accident he also refuses to discuss, from the year he refuses to discuss. These steps were lighter and quicker and they passed along Candlewick Lane without pausing, and then, thirty seconds later, they came back the other way. Also without pausing. Nobody walks Candlewick Lane twice at two in the morning unless they are a watchman, and our watchman, Hobbes, walks with the rhythm of a man who has given up on the profession entirely.

I turned down the lamp and stood in the dark listening.

That is another thing fog does: it makes towns nervous. Grayharbor has been nervous for a year now, the way a household is nervous before a bankruptcy. You could read it in small things. The loyalist merchants on Quay Street had stopped dining with the harbor men who run cargo that arrives unlogged and leaves unpaid. Mr. Prynne's coffee house had split, literally split, into two rooms — he had built a partition in September, and now the east room drank government coffee and the west room drank smuggled coffee, and everyone agreed the partition was for warmth. The print circle — the printers, mostly young, mostly hungry, who bought their type from us and printed pamphlets that said careful things in careless tones — those men had begun to pay in coin instead of credit, which tells you they expect accounts to stop mattering.

And there were fewer ships. I will come back to the ships later, because the ships matter, but note it now: fewer ships, and the ones that came in flew colors that took a moment to identify, and the harbormaster's log had gotten thicker with annotations, and Tamsin Crowe, who reads everything her father writes, had gone quiet in a way I recognized because it is my own quiet.

A town that expects soldiers starts doing small arithmetic. My father did his by buying tin. Eleven crates of it, ostensibly for matrix backing, stacked in the punch room. I did mine by memorizing faces. Every stranger on Candlewick Lane got catalogued: height, coat, hands. Hands especially. You can tell a man's trade by his hands if you have spent twelve years looking at hands, because I look at hands the way my father looks at letters — professionally, jealously, noting the flaws.

These footsteps had belonged to someone with soft boots and a walking stick that tapped twice per stride. Not a watchman. Watchmen lean on their sticks; this person carried theirs like an opinion.

The steps faded toward the water. I relit the lamp and went back to work, because fear is a poor craftsman and I had a commission due Thursday: fourteen point body text for Aldous Reed the bookseller, who wanted to print a history of the county that said nothing whatsoever about the county's recent history. Safe work. Dead work. The kind of work that pays.

* *

My father came down at five, as he always does, though the shop does not open until eight, because he believes the two hours before dawn belong to the punches and the hours after belong to commerce, and never shall the twain meet.

He stood in the doorway of the punch room and looked at the bench, and I watched him perform his inventory without moving a muscle of his face. Ruined blank. Finished *g*. Lamp low. My hands on his tools.

"You've cut the ear too deep," he said.

"It holds ink."

"It'll break in the matrix within a thousand casts."

"Then I'll recut it."

He crossed the room and picked up the *g* and held it to the light, turning it, and for a moment — I want to be honest here, because if I am honest nowhere else — for a moment his face did something complicated. Something almost like pride, if pride had been dragged through disappointment and left out in the rain. He set it down.

"The Reed job," he said. "Is it justified?"

"Locked. I proofed it twice."

"Good." And then, because he is my father and affection must be smuggled past customs in both directions: "You should sleep."

"So should you."

"I sleep." He moved to the furnace and began the lighting ritual — damper, coal, patience — a liturgy he has performed perhaps twenty thousand times, and I realized, watching his back, that I could not remember the last time he had addressed me by anything other than a task.

This is the part where a different narrator would tell you she resented him. I have considered resentment. I have examined it the way I examine a flawed matrix, turning it in the light, and I find it doesn't fit. Here is what I know: he took a six-year-old girl and made her into a son to keep her safe, and the price of safety was that she would grow up invisible, and invisibility, practiced daily for twelve years, becomes indistinguishable from loneliness. He knew that price when he paid it. I think he pays it daily too, standing in his own silence across the shop from mine, and I think neither of us knows how to say so in any language we share. We speak in metal. Last month he regrinded all my gravers without being asked, putting a finer edge on them than he uses himself. That is, I believe, the closest thing to *I love you* that has been said aloud in this family since my mother died.

But love conducted entirely in subtext accumulates debts. Remember that. It matters later.

* *

Thursday I delivered the Reed proofs to the docks, and it was on the docks that the town finally said aloud what it had only been thinking.

The proofs went into my satchel wrapped in oilcloth — finished pages, locked, ready for the press Reed rents from a printer who owes us money, which is how half the commerce in Grayharbor works: a web of debts so intricate that if any one thread broke, six businesses would fall over like dominoes, and everyone knows it, and everyone extends credit anyway, because the alternative is admitting how fragile the whole arrangement is. Grayharbor runs on mutual hostage-taking and calls it neighborliness.

The fog was lifting off the harbor in long ropes. I counted masts, an old habit — nine brigs, two snows, a lugger, and at the far end of the quay, riding high and empty, a brig I didn't recognize, black-hulled, with her name painted small, as if embarrassed by it: *Juniper*.

I stopped walking.

You will understand that I had not heard that name spoken in years. My mother named me in a fever of poetry, my father told me once, the only sentence he ever volunteered about her preferences; and the name went into the drawer with everything else of hers, and the town forgot it, and I let them. Seeing it now on a hull, in faded paint, in a harbor full of strangers' ships, produced a sensation I cannot recommend: as if someone had taken a private thing and hung it from a yardarm.

"Cyrus Underhill."

Mr. Reed, receiving his proofs at the tavern door, pressing his thumb into the margins the way merchants do, testing a world that cannot be tested by thumbs. "Fine work. Fine. Though I wonder—" he lowered his voice, though there was no one near, "—whether the county history might want... tempering. Certain passages."

"The passages are as you approved them."

"Yes. Yes, of course." He was not looking at the proofs. He was looking past me, at the black brig, and his face had the particular slackness of a man doing sums he dislikes. "Strange tonnage coming in lately. Strange flags. My wife says there's talk in the market. Soldiers' talk. Requisitions."

"People say many things, Mr. Reed."

"They do." He folded the proofs away. "They say things, and then other things arrive to make the saying unnecessary." He patted the satchel against his ribs like a talisman. "Your father's a careful man, Underhill. Always has been. Tell him — tell him Reed asked after him. Tell him Reed remembers every transaction."

Which is a strange thing to say about type purchases, unless it isn't, unless the ledger in question records not just who bought what weight of fourteen point but who bought it *for*, and Reed has spent thirty years selling books and printing pamphlets and knows exactly which of his customers might someday need forgetting.

I said I would tell him. I did not tell him. My father and I communicate chiefly in tasks not performed.

Walking home along the quay, I passed the black brig again, close enough now to see her boatswain coiling line on deck with the economical motions of a man who has done it ten thousand times and expects to do it ten thousand more. A woman leaned at her rail — dark-skinned, sharp-eyed, wearing the particular stillness of someone counting everything on the dock. She looked at me. I looked at her. Neither of us nodded, which in Grayharbor constitutes an entire negotiation.

Her eyes stayed on me longer than courtesy required, and I had the uncomfortable sensation of being read — of being, somehow, *proofed*, the way I proof a page, looking for errors in the composition. I put my head down and walked on.

* *

That night my father slept, and I did not, and the foundry held its forty thousand tongues, and I sat at his bench cutting letters for a book about a county with no history.

I want to explain what it is like, this competence, because everyone mistakes it for contentment. When I cut, the world narrows to a point of light and a line of steel, and there is no loneliness in that point, none, because loneliness requires an awareness of absence and in the cut there is no absence, there is only the letter arriving backward out of the metal. For hours at a time I am not a girl disguised as a boy disguised as a person. I am simply a hand attached to a graver attached to a purpose. It is the closest thing to peace I have found, and I have searched this town for peace the way other people search for God.

But the cuts end. The steel cools. You lift the punch to the lamp and there it is, finished, perfect, and nobody in the world knows it exists except you, and the knowing curdles immediately into the question that follows every craftsman to bed: *for whom?*

Letters are made to be read. That is their entire nature. And here I was, the finest cutter in Grayharbor — I say it without vanity; my father's hands shake now — producing letters for a county history that no one will read and a town that does not know I exist, in a name that is not mine, for a father who communicates in reground gravers, while outside the window the fog lay in the streets and the footsteps came again, soft boots and tapping stick, up Candlewick Lane and down it, twice, unhurried, like a man taking measurements.

I set down the graver. I listened until the steps faded toward the water.

Then I banked the lamp, and climbed the stairs past my father's door — his breathing slow, his sleep the dreamless sleep of a man who believes his secrets are buried deep enough — and I stood a moment in the dark landing, looking back down at the shop, at the cases and cabinets, at the locked punch-room door behind which eleven crates of tin sat waiting for a purpose my father had not yet named.

A town expects soldiers the way a lung expects winter. But expectations, in my experience, are rarely equal to arrivals.

Three days later, the gray coats came up from the quay in the early morning fog, and the first thing they requisitioned was the printing office on Rope Walk, and the second thing they posted on every corner was a notice, printed — I noted professionally, and with a chill I did not yet understand — in type that could well have come from our own racks.

All business ledgers, the notice said. To be surrendered within ten days.

Every record. Every account. Every name.
