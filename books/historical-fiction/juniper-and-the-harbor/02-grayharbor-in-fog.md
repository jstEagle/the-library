## 2. Grayharbor in Fog

There is a particular quality to Grayharbor fog that makes a town of four hundred souls feel like a conspiracy. Sounds arrive without their owners. Footsteps belong to no one. A voice calls out across the water and you cannot tell whether it's greeting or warning, which in Grayharbor is generally the same thing.

I was out in it before eight o'clock, a Tuesday in the wet season, with a canvas satchel of proof sheets against my chest and my collar turned up against a drizzle that wasn't rain exactly but couldn't be called air either. The proofs were for Mr. Hollis at the chandler's shop — a handbill announcing a shipment of Swedish pitch — harmless as milk. But you learn early in my trade that paper is never only paper. A proof sheet has two sides, and a delivery girl has two faces, and a town like ours has about six, counting the ones it keeps under the floorboards.

My father did not ask me to map Grayharbor. He would have been horrified at the suggestion. But twelve years of walking these streets with type dust under my fingernails taught me the thing he most hoped I'd never learn: who wants what, and from whom, and what they'll pay. My father taught me letters. The town taught me everything else.

Consider the harbor road on a Tuesday morning. Begin at the customs house, where Mr. Pellweather raises the flag whatever the weather, because the flag is not for the weather. Pellweather is a loyalist the way some men are tall — structurally, without effort, and with an unexamined faith that the arrangement will hold him up forever. He buys his type from us, plain stock, nothing fancy, for shipping manifests and notices of seizure that haven't been needed in years. *Not yet needed*, I should say. There was a time when Pellweather's office burned papers by the barrowload, back before I could read, and my father never once asked what they'd said. That silence was the closest thing to affection our family practiced.

Then walk east past the Salt & Anchor, where the loyalist merchants take their breakfast. You can identify them by their conversation: loud enough to be overheard, dull enough to be safe. They discuss wool tariffs the way other men discuss scripture — with intensity and no originality. Old Man Crewe holds court at the corner table. He owns three ships and half the wharf and once paid my father forty pounds for a commemorative typeface honoring the King's birthday, which tells you both his politics and his taste. When I deliver proofs to the Salt & Anchor, I am invisible in the way of girls carrying satchels, and I hear things. Crewe was saying, even then, that the times wanted a firm hand. He said it into his coffee, which is a way of saying it to nobody, which is how the dangerous sentences get said in Grayharbor.

Continue past the cooper's yard, where the smell of oak shavings covers everything including, on some days, the smell of the sea itself. Then down the stairs cut into the rock — sixty-one of them; I counted them as a child during a winter with little else to count — to the lower quay, and here, sirs and madams, we leave the loyalist map and enter another country entirely.

* * *

The lower quay belongs to the smugglers, though everyone above those stairs agrees to call them "the night traders," a fiction so flimsy you could sail a brig through it, which is precisely what happens most nights. The night traders are Grayharbor's honest men, if you want my view: at least they admit to wanting money. Their leader in practice if not in title is a woman named Bree Callum who runs a fish stall by day and, I am fairly certain, runs most of the Atlantic by night. She buys type from us too — small orders, always cash, always for printing things she does not show us. My father prints her invoices without comment. In our shop, the ledger records the transaction; what the type says afterward is between the metal and God.

"Proofs for the chandler?" Bree asked me that morning, though I carried nothing for her. She had a knife in one hand and a mackerel in the other and was performing surgery on both.

"And nothing else," I said, which was true, and she grinned, because *nothing else* is the password of everyone in this town who has ever moved anything worth taxing.

"How's your father's hand these days?" she asked. Meaning: is the foundry still producing? Meaning: I may have work soon. Meaning: keep your lamp burning late, girl.

"Steady," I said, which covered all three meanings, and she went back to her mackerel, and I went on down the quay, cataloguing as I went. The *Merrow* was in, riding low, which meant she'd unloaded already rather than wait for light. The harbormaster's skiff was out among the anchored vessels doing its slow official rounds. And at the far bollard, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, sat a figure I knew better than I knew almost anyone outside my own house, watching the water with the patience of a lighthouse.

Tamsin Perrault. The harbormaster's daughter.

We are not friends. I want that understood. Friendship requires that two people speak to each other, and Tamsin and I have exchanged perhaps four hundred words in six years, mostly about weather and mostly lies. But there is a fellowship among people whose fathers' work consists of writing down the truth about ships, because in Grayharbor the truth about ships is the most valuable and dangerous commodity there is. Her father logs what comes and goes. My father casts the letters that record what is bought and sold. We are both daughters of record-keepers, which means we are both apprentices to the town's memory, and neither of us chose it.

She saw me come down the stairs — she sees everything; it is her single talent and she exercises it relentlessly — and lifted her chin in acknowledgment. I lifted mine. Between us we had conducted an entire conversation, which was three sentences more than usual.

"The fog's thick," she said.

"It's fog."

"My father says it'll burn off by noon." She said this with the flat certainty of someone quoting scripture she privately doubted.

"Your father also said the *Merrow* wouldn't be in till Thursday."

That earned me a look. Not offense — appraisal. Tamsin Perrault appraising you is like a customs officer opening your trunk. She decided something, apparently, because she scooted along the bollard to make room, which she had never done, and I sat, which I had certainly never done, and for a while we watched the water do its gray trick together.

"You walk the docks every week," she said finally. "Proofs. Deliveries. Same route."

"I like routine."

"You count the stairs."

Everyone counts the stairs. "It's sixty-one."

"It's sixty-two since March. They added one after the ice broke the bottom step." She let that sit. "You've been counting wrong for eight months."

I confess I felt a genuine flash of alarm, which is absurd, but there it is: discover that your private arithmetic is public and you wonder what else is. Tamsin watched me arrive at this thought — watching people arrive at thoughts is her second talent — and smiled very slightly, and looked back at the water, and let me keep my dignity, such as it was.

That is all I know of kindness in Grayharbor, and I have never forgotten it.

* * *

Here is the thing about Tamsin Perrault, seen from the outside, which is the only way I saw her in those days.

Her father, Master Perrault, keeps the harbor ledger, and the harbor ledger is the truest document in Grayharbor, because a ship that isn't logged might as well not exist, and a ship that exists unlogged is worth more than one that does. Every morning before dawn, Tamsin rows her father out among the anchorage so he can count hulls by lantern light, and every morning she writes the fair copies while he dictates, because his eyes are going and hers are the sharpest in the family. She is nineteen. She can identify eleven vessels by the sound of their anchor chains alone. She knows which captains bribe and which don't and which lie about cargo and which tell the truth so audaciously that no one believes them.

And she is bored beyond the reach of language.

This is the part nobody understands about the harbormaster's daughter, because they look at her sitting on her bollard and think: quiet girl, waiting for a husband. What they should think is: that girl has memorized the entire maritime economy of a port town and is permitted to do precisely nothing with it. Her father loves her the way strong locks love what they contain. She is kept accurate, kept safe, kept. The boys of the harbor get to be reckless and call it learning the trade. Tamsin gets to copy manifests in a fair hand and be praised for neatness, and every night she goes to sleep knowing the exact tonnage of everything moving through Grayharbor and the precise legal weight of none of it being hers.

That Tuesday, rowing her father through the fog, she was doing her private arithmetic — the same disease I had, just applied to different columns. The *Merrow* in early, riding wrong. Two navy cutters expected within the month, per the customs house gossip that reached her father's table whether he wished it or not. A new clerk at the customs house who asked questions in a soft voice and wrote everything down twice.

"Count again," her father said at the third vessel. "Is that the *Providence* or the *Provident*?"

"*Provident*. The *Providence* lost her bowsprit in September; you logged it yourself."

"Hm." He trusted her corrections more than his own eyes, which was correct of him, and he never once said so, which was also correct of him, and Tamsin rowed on with her face arranged into obedience and her mind running like a press.

From the lower quay she could see the whole theater: Crewe's men taking on pitch at the chandler's, Bree Callum's stall doing brisk imaginary business, and — she noted this, because she noted everything — the foundry girl sitting on the far bollard like a piece of driftwood that had learned to hold a satchel. Cyrus Underhill. Everyone knew the story, or thought they did: Underhill took an apprentice instead of mourning, gave the boy his own name, and the boy grew up odd and silent and brilliant with a burin. Tamsin had watched her for years the way you watch a clock you suspect of keeping better time than the church bell. She had counted the proofs, the routes, the pauses. She knew the foundry girl mapped this town the way she herself did, and she suspected — correctly — that they were the only two people under thirty in Grayharbor who understood that the maps were about to matter.

She didn't know yet why. Neither did I. But fog lifts, in Grayharbor, usually by noon, and it shows you what was there all along.

* * *

I left Tamsin at the bollard and climbed back up — sixty-two steps now, and I resented every one — to make my last deliveries of the day, which were the ones that mattered.

The print circle meets behind the stationer's shop on Candle Lane, in a room that officially stores damaged stock. There are five of them, or six depending on whether you count Mr. Fenwick, who attends faithfully and contributes nothing but excellent cheese. They are printers, mostly — men who bought type from my father over the years and used it for pamphlets that circulated from hand to hand until the paper wore soft as cloth. Essays on representation. Satires. Last spring, something with a woodcut that made the customs house furious enough to search three shops and find nothing, because my father's customers know how to hide type, having learned it from my father, who never taught them anything so directly as by example.

I don't attend their meetings. I deliver. There is a difference, my father would insist, and the difference is roughly the width of a gallows. But I stood in the stationer's back room that Tuesday while Mr. Aldous — no, that name comes later; the stationer was named Mr. Quill, appropriately enough, life being occasionally generous with names — while Mr. Quill read aloud the draft of a new essay, I watched their faces and I understood that these men believed in something larger than Grayharbor, and I envied them with an envy so sharp it embarrassed me.

They talked of the colonies' grievances as if the grievances were theirs. They spoke of rights as if rights were a cargo that could be landed at any friendly port. And when they'd finished, one of them — young Devane, the poet, the reckless one — looked at me standing by the door with my empty satchel and said, "The Underhill girl. Does she read?"

"She sets type," Quill said. "Different thing."

"It is not remotely a different thing," Devane said, "and you know it."

They all looked at me then. It was the longest anyone outside my family had looked *at* me rather than through me in a year, perhaps longer, and I discovered that being seen is a sensation much like being cold: you don't miss it until it arrives, and then you can't stop shaking.

"Cyrus reads," I said. My own voice startled me. "Cyrus reads Greek."

Devane laughed, delighted. The others didn't. Quill said, gently, the way you close a door on a draft, "The girl delivers proofs. Thank you, girl."

I walked home through the thinning fog with my satchel empty and my head dangerously full. Six factions, I counted, climbing the harbor road. The crown's men at the customs house. Crewe's merchants at the Salt & Anchor. Bree's night traders below the stairs. The print circle behind Candle Lane. The army, still an rumor, gathering somewhere across the water like weather. And underneath all of them, holding them together the way a spine holds a book: the silent majority, the neighbors who see and say nothing, my father's true congregation, the church of the closed mouth.

Six factions, and I belonged to none of them. I belonged to the space between — the delivery girl, the counter of stairs, the daughter of the man who cast letters for everyone's convictions and held none of his own, or held them so deep in the metal that even he couldn't read them anymore.

Or so I believed, that Tuesday, climbing toward the foundry with the fog lifting behind me and the harbor showing its true face at last: masts and rigging and small boats and the whole floating architecture of a town built on knowing things and not saying them.

My father was at the door when I came up the lane, which was unusual — he met no one at doors; doors were how drafts got in. He had a strange set to his shoulders, and behind him, through the open shop, I could see the type cases pulled out from the wall in a way I had never seen, as if he'd been looking for something, or hiding something, or both.

"Cyrus," he said. "Come inside. There are notices going up tomorrow, and we should talk before you hear it from the street."

I want to record, honestly, that my first thought was not fear. My first thought was: *he's finally going to explain the second ledger.* I had found the odd blocks months ago — the ones that made no sense, impressions that reversed into nothing legible — and filed the question away the way I filed everything.

He was going to explain nothing. That is not how my father said important things. But the soldiers were coming to Grayharbor, and they would want the ledgers, all of them, and my father stood in his doorway wearing the expression of a man doing arithmetic he cannot make come out even.

I stepped inside, and he closed the door behind me.
