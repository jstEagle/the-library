## 3. Requisition

They came on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Catastrophe ought at least to have the decency to arrive on a Sunday, when a body has time to sit with it. Instead the gray coats marched in through the south gate at midmorning, while I was elbow-deep in a font of ten-point bodoni that had come out of the mold misshapen, and the whole business of the century turning against us was conducted with the efficiency of men delivering flour.

I heard them before I saw them — boots, but not the loose scatter of harbor boots. These were drilled boots, forty pairs striking cobbles in unison, and under that a drum beating something slow and patient, the rhythm of a man who knows he will outlast you.

Father was upstairs in the counting room, doing sums he had already done twice. I put down my burin, wiped my hands on my apron — lead dust does not wipe so much as relocate — and went to the door.

Grayharbor had seen soldiers before, of course. The war down south sent its beggars and deserters through every few months, and once, years ago, a naval press gang had worked the docks for a week until the smugglers arranged for two of its members to fall in the harbor, which the coroner called misadventure and everyone else called Tuesday. But these were different. These men did not stagger. They walked like the town already belonged to them and they were merely arriving to collect what had always been theirs.

An officer rode at their head on a chestnut mare too fine for him, or perhaps he was too fine for the mare; it was difficult to say. He wore gray as well, but cleaner, cut closer, and his face had the particular blankness of a man who has been taught that expressions cost money.

He stopped at the market cross, unfolded a paper with a small crisp gesture, and read.

"By order of the Provisional Authority of the Eastern District," he said, in a voice built for courtyards, "the town of Grayharbor and its port are received into the protection of the revolutionary army." He said *protection* the way a tollhouse says *fee*. "All civil administration is suspended pending reorganization. All businesses shall present complete and truthful accounts of trade — ledgers, books of account, correspondence registers, bills of lading — to the Commission of Inventory, to be seated at the old customs house, within ten days of this notice."

He folded the paper. The drum stopped.

"Failure to comply," he added, almost as an afterthought, the way you might mention rain, "will be construed as concealment. Concealment will be construed as conspiracy."

Then he rolled the paper back up, and the whole mechanism of the army resumed, flowing down Fore Street in that same patient drumbeat, nailing notices to doors as it went — four nails each, corners and center, very thorough, very neat.

* * *

The notices went up faster than gossip could, which in Grayharbor is saying something. Within an hour there was one on the customs house, one on the churchyard wall, one on the cooperage where Mrs. Pennhallow waters down her husband's beer, and one — nailed with particular ceremony, as though the door itself were being knighted — on the front of Underhill & Sons, Type Founders.

I stood in the street and read it three times. The language was the same as what the officer had said, only inkier. *Complete and truthful accounts.* Ten days. *Concealment will be construed as conspiracy.*

Twelve years I had lived above that shop, and in twelve years I had learned exactly one thing about my father's books: they existed, and I was not to touch them. Not forbidden in the loud way — Father does not do loud — but in the way of a door kept closed, a drawer turned casually toward the wall, a ledger carried upstairs after supper and brought down before I woke. I assumed, in the way children assume the geography they're raised in, that whatever those books held was ordinary. Debts. Orders. The price of antimony.

Now the army wanted them, and the army does not want things for sentimental reasons.

Inside, Father was at his bench, examining the bodoni I'd abandoned, turning the sorts over with his lens. He did not look up when I came in. He never looks up quickly; I have a private theory he believes sudden movement attracts fate.

"They posted one on our door," I said.

"I saw. Four nails." He set the lens down. "Thorough men."

"Ten days, they're giving us."

"Ten days is generous. It means they don't know yet what they're looking for." He said this mildly, the way another man might comment on the weather turning, and then went back to sorting type, and I understood that the conversation was concluded, and understood also, with a cold sliding feeling under my ribs, that I had no idea what he meant by it.

* * *

The town spent the afternoon performing innocence, which is Grayharbor's oldest industry.

At Merle's chandlery, three loyalist merchants stood in a row comparing outrage. Old Mr. Quill — who is not a printer, despite the name, but a rope merchant with opinions — declared loudly that he would surrender his ledgers gladly, since honest books fear no inspection, and then went home through the alley behind his own warehouse instead of the front street, which is where honest books go when they fear nothing, I suppose.

Down at the fish market the talk was quieter. The smugglers' end of the harbor — and everyone knows which end it is, we simply agree not to draw it on maps — received the news with the shrug of professionals. Ledgers? They had ledgers the way theater people have names: several, all false, all interchangeable. Tam Osei's people — not the captain, the lesser Oseis, cousins — were reportedly already soaking paper near a stove to age it convincingly backward. There is a craft to everything in this town, including compliance.

And in the print circle — the quiet web of printers, pamphleteers, and one extremely nervous bookseller who bound more than he sold — there was silence of a different texture. I delivered proofs that afternoon because proofs do not stop needing delivery merely because history arrives, and I saw the change in every shop. Mr. Aldery at the Sign of the Compass had taken down his broadsides from the window, all of them, even the almanac. He met me at the side door instead of the front and counted my proofs twice, and his hands shook on the second count, and neither of us remarked on any of it.

"The army in Grayharbor," he said finally, softly, looking past me at the fog coming in off the water. "Your father's shop. They'll want everything, won't they. Everything ever printed."

"Everything bought," I corrected, before I thought. "Type sold leaves a record. What men print with it afterward—" I shrugged, and the shrug was a lie, because I knew precisely what men printed afterward, having set some of it myself at midnight: arguments, appeals, satires with the minister's face on a donkey. The foundry's ledger was a list of customers, and a list of customers is a list of suspects, and everyone on Fore Street knew it even if the notice didn't say so in words.

Aldery paid me and did not invite me in for tea, which he always does, and I walked home through fog thick enough to chew, thinking about drawers turned toward walls.

* * *

Supper that night was mutton, potatoes going soft, and a conversation shaped like a fencing match in which only one party holds a sword.

"They'll come here," I said. "To inventory us. We're not a rope walk, Father, we're a foundry. Every sort we cast is traceable."

"Mm."

"'Mm' is not a plan."

"It is the beginning of one." He ate a little, neatly. My father eats the way he does everything: precisely, without appetite anyone could prove. "The shop's books are in order. Every transaction for twelve years, entered fair and dated. Let them read until their eyes bleed."

"And the customers?"

"What about them?"

"Half of them are going to be standing in front of a commission with their names written in our hand. Aldery. The woman in Brackle who prints hymns that aren't quite hymns. The man from—"

"Cyrus." Just the name, in the voice he used to stop the polishing wheel. Then, quieter: "The books are what they are. A record of honest trade. If men bought type from us for honest purposes and have since turned dishonest, that is between them and their consciences, and now, unfortunately, the army."

It was reasonable. It was entirely reasonable, and I hated it, the way you hate a floor that gives way — not because the floor betrayed you but because you had trusted it to be solid. He sounded exactly like himself. That was the trouble. He sounded like a man with nothing to hide, and I sat across the table from him unable to decide whether that was the truth or the finest performance I had ever witnessed, and the not-knowing sat in my stomach like swallowed lead.

Because here is the thing I did not know yet, the thing I would learn weeks later on my knees among the type cases with my heart going like a drop hammer: I looked at my father across that supper table and believed he had no second ledger, no hidden accounts, no drawer of sins. I believed the shop's silence was just silence, the natural condition of a quiet man in a quiet trade.

He hid nothing, so far as I could see.

That is precisely how well he hid it.

* * *

The soldiers billeted nowhere that first week — they camped in the market square under canvas, gray and orderly, and Grayharbor adjusted around them the way water adjusts around a dropped stone, closing over the interruption without admitting anything had happened. But the Commission of Inventory opened in the customs house on the third day, and then the adjustments got more creative.

I watched the queue form on Friday morning. Shopkeepers with ledgers hugged to their chests like infants, faces arranged into expressions of bland cooperative emptiness — the local specialty. Mr. Quill went early, loudly, so everyone would see how gladly honest men comply, and came out an hour later looking faintly disappointed that honesty had earned him no applause.

What struck me, watching, was the politeness. That is what nobody warns you about subjugation: it doesn't kick the door in. It knocks. It has forms. There was a clerk at a table with a bell, for God's sake, ringing it pleasantly between appointments like a dentist. The dread arrived dressed in its best coat, apologized for intruding, asked you to sign here and initial there, and thanked you for your cooperation, and it was somehow worse than burning would have been. Fire at least lets you feel burned.

Tamsin Corvane was on the customs house steps, taking the harbor register inside to her father, and she caught my eye as I passed with the proofs cart. She made a small face — her entire commentary on the occupation, delivered wordlessly, eloquently — and mouthed something I chose to interpret as *ten days*, and we both kept walking, because the street had ears now. Everyone was learning that quickly. Even the gulls seemed to keep their opinions to themselves.

* * *

That evening Father took the ledgers down from the counting room — three of them, cloth-bound, spines cracked from use — and set them on the workbench under the lamp to review, and I stayed to help, partly from duty and mostly from espionage.

"Entry here," I said, pointing. "Brackle woman, March. Two hundred sorts, pica. You've written 'ornamental stock.' She ordered text."

"She ordered text," Father agreed. "She received text. What she told the carter she was carrying is her affair and the carter's."

"So we're recording what people bought."

"We are recording what people purchased from us." He looked up, and for a moment the lamplight caught his eyes, and something moved behind them — not guilt, quite. Inventory. A man checking his stores. "Cyrus. Ask what you're asking."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, army boots went by in step, four men, maybe six, keeping the town's time now.

"All right," I said. "Is that everything? Those three books, that's the whole of it? Because if there's anything else — anything, Father, a memorandum book, letters, a second set of figures — ten days is not long enough to be clever, and you are not a clever liar. No offense."

Something crossed his face then. I have gone over that moment a hundred times since, and I still cannot tell you what it was. At the time I read it as mild hurt — the honest tradesman insulted by the suggestion. Later I would reread it as something else entirely: a man hearing the right question asked by the wrong person, and deciding, in the space of half a second, that the answer could wait.

"No offense taken," he said. "Three books. Fair copies, complete. That is the whole of Underhill & Sons."

He said it plainly. He met my eyes. And I believed him — God help me, I believed him completely — because I had twelve years of practice believing that my father's silences contained nothing, that a man who spoke so little must therefore have little to say.

"That's the whole of it," I repeated.

"The whole of it." He closed the third ledger and squared its edges with a small tap, the gesture of a compositor locking a finished chase, and I thought nothing of it at all.

* * *

I could not sleep, which was becoming the season's fashion. Through the shutters I could see the square: watch fires, gray sentries, the mare stamping. Somewhere a soldier sang low, a marching song with more homesickness than melody in it, and the fog came up off the harbor and put its arms around the whole ridiculous town.

Ten days. Complete and truthful accounts. Concealment construed as conspiracy.

I lay there cataloguing, the way I catalogue type — by case, by compartment. What did the army think a type foundry was? A supplier. Which meant the ledgers weren't really about us at all; we were just the pen they'd used to write other people down. Forty shops, fifty, a hundred customers across three counties, every dissident pamphlet and smuggled tract traceable backward through our fair entries to the door of a quiet man on Fore Street. Father was right that the books recorded honest trade. He was wrong — or evasive — about what honest trade was worth to men in gray coats. To them, our ledger wasn't an account.

It was a map.

And Father had handed it to me across the supper table and called it complete, and I had looked him in the eye and believed him, and tomorrow or the day after, the polite men with the bell would believe him too, and then forty families would learn what their purchases had purchased them.

Unless.

There was a thought forming, down under the dread, in the place where I keep the things I don't yet dare look at directly. Something about my father's precision. About a man who had spent thirty years cutting letters in metal, who thought in reversals, who taught me that every impression is made by its opposite. Such a man keeps records the way he keeps everything: exactly, completely, and in whatever form serves him best.

Paper burns. Paper is evidence. Paper says your name out loud.

Metal keeps quiet.

I got up in the dark, lit no lamp, and stood in the doorway of the type room where the cases waited in their ranks, thousands upon thousands of little leaden letters, every one of them silent, every one of them holding its face turned inward.

If my father had hidden anything, he would not have hidden it in a drawer. He would have hidden it in plain sight — in the one place in the world no inventory clerk would ever think to read.

I put my hand on the nearest case and stood there a long time, listening to the shop not speak.
