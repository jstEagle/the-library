## 4. What Father Kept

My father slept like a man who had earned it, which was the most dishonest thing about him.

He slept the way he had slept every night of the twelve years I had known him as anything more than a shape in the dark of the stairs — heavily, righteously, with his good conscience folded under his cheek like a second pillow. A man who has done nothing wrong sleeps that way. A man who has done nothing at all sleeps even better.

I did not sleep well. I want that on the record. Whatever else this story contains — and it contains betrayals, several of them mine — let it also contain the fact that I was awake while he snored, and I was working.

The requisition notice had given us ten days. Ten days to produce "all business ledgers, correspondence, customer records, and accounts" for the inspection of the Army of the Interior, whose clerk had signed himself, in a hand so neat it looked ironed, *A. Vane*. Ten days. The army had taken towns in less. It had certainly taken printers in less.

Father's response to all this had been to eat his supper, thank me for the salt pork, and go to bed at his usual hour, saying, "We have nothing to hide, Cyrus."

That sentence should have comforted me. Instead it sat in my chest all evening like a bad casting — smooth on the outside, hollow where the metal hadn't filled. Because I knew my father, and I knew that a man who says *we have nothing to hide* is either a fool or a liar, and Josiah Underhill had never once in his life been a fool.

So: the type cases. There were perhaps four hundred of them in the foundry's back rooms, and the army would want them inventoried, and an inventory meant Vane or one of his gray-coated clerks lifting every tray, tilting every case, running their soft clerk fingers through twelve years of other people's words. If there was anything in those cases that shouldn't be found, I had ten days minus one night to find it first.

I lit the lamp at the sorting bench, turned it low, and began.

* * *

You need to understand what a type foundry keeps, because I didn't, not fully, not until that night. People imagine we make letters, pristine little armies of A's and B's marching into print shops. We do. But we also keep everything a letter ever was. The matrices — the copper molds from which type is struck. The patterns. The damaged casts. The sorts that came out wrong, too thin, pocked with gas-holes, misaligned on their bodies. A foundry's back room is a graveyard of near-words, and my father never threw anything away, because my father was a man who believed that everything might someday be worth something to somebody, which I had always taken for thrift and would come to take for something considerably colder.

Sorting is dull work, and I am good at dull work. That is not false modesty; it is a professional assessment. Give me a jumbled case of mixed pica and long primer at midnight and I will have it ordered by dawn, and I will have enjoyed myself, in the narrow way a person enjoys anything when the alternative is lying awake listening to her father's conscience snoring.

I started with the cases nearest the wall, the old ones, the ones Father hadn't opened since before the war news started coming north. Mostly rubbish. Broken sorts, worn matrices for faces we no longer cast, three dozen asterisks someone had cast crooked and someone else — me, aged nine, by the clumsiness of the filing — had tried to correct with a graver and made worse.

By the third hour my hands had found their rhythm and my mind had gone to that place sorters go, the flat quiet place, and it was there, in the flat quiet, that my thumb noticed what my eyes had missed.

A block that weighed wrong.

Not much. A whisper of difference. But a cutter's hands are educated beyond her education, if you follow me, and mine knew lead the way Tamsin Cole's father knew tide — by feel, without looking, without forgiving. I brought the block up into the lamplight.

It was a standard nonpareil body, or shaped like one. But heavier. And the face was wrong — not a letter at all, when you studied it, but a tangle of strokes that pretended to be ornament. Foundries do cast fleurons and dingbats; there was nothing strange in that. What was strange was that this one had no matrix twin anywhere in the case, and every face in our shop had twins, triplets, whole choirs of itself. Every legitimate face.

Except, I realized, turning it over, the ones that didn't.

I set it down. I told myself it was nothing. Then I went to the next case, and sorted faster now, hands reading rather than eyes, and forty minutes later I found its brother in a case of long primer scrap — same pretense of ornament, same wrong weight, same singularity. And then another. And then, in a dusty case behind the annealing oven, behind a false front of broken matrices that I had to pry forward with a screwdriver and a held breath, an entire tray of them. Sixty-one blocks, each unique, each disguised as decoration, each heavier than it had any right to be.

I lined them up on the bench and looked at them for a long time.

Here is what I did not do: I did not call for my father. Remember that too.

* * *

A word on how letters work, because the discovery makes no sense without it, and because I need a moment before I can write the rest.

Type is cut reversed. Everything on the face of a sort is mirror-writing; the press flips it true. This means a typefounder spends his life handling language backwards, seeing it backwards, thinking it backwards. To us, *mirror* reads *rorrim* and neither form surprises us. And this means — I understood it slowly, sitting there with sixty-one ornaments on the bench — that a clever cutter could hide a message two ways. He could encode it. Or he could simply cut it true instead of reversed, so that when the block was pressed into paper or wax or soft clay, the impression came out mirrored, illegible, dismissible — unless you knew the trick, unless you pressed it again, or read the impression in a mirror, or held the thing up backward to the light the way only someone raised in a foundry would ever think to do.

Or — and this was the thought that made my hands go cold on the bench — unless you took rubbings. Graphite and thin paper, the way we proofed matrices. The impression of a true-cut block comes out reversed. The impression of the impression comes out right again. Two mirrors facing each other, and somewhere between them, the truth standing quietly where nobody looks.

I proofed the first block at half past two in the morning, rubbing a soft pencil across paper laid over the face, then laying a fresh sheet over the graphite ghost and rubbing that, and the name that rose up out of the paper, black and small and perfectly ordinary, was:

**MERRICK, T. — printer, Cordwain Lane. 4 lines roman, 2 lines brevier. Paid in pamphlets.**

I sat down on the stool. My legs had opinions of their own and they expressed them.

Tom Merrick. Tom Merrick who printed the dissenting pamphlets until 1789, who vanished the year the magistrates came for the Corresponding Society, who everyone said had gone to France or America or the bottom of the harbor depending on who was drunk. Tom Merrick had bought type from Underhill & Sons. Of course he had. Where else would a printer in Grayharbor buy type? And my father had sold it to him, wrapped in plain paper, no names in the day-ledger — I knew there were no names, I kept the day-ledger's fair copy myself —

Except there was a ledger. There it was, in sixty-one pieces of lead dressed up as flowers, hidden behind a false tray in the back room, growing entry by entry across exactly the years I had grown across, and I had never seen it though I had sorted these very cases a hundred times.

I proofed more. God forgive me, I couldn't stop.

* * *

By four o'clock I had thirty-one rubbings laid out on the bench in the order I'd found the blocks, which was not the order they'd been cut in, but I could reconstruct that; the faces told me. Early cuts were stiff, over-hatched, the letterforms of a man remembering his craft rather than practicing it. Later ones flowed. Somewhere around entry fifteen, the cutter stopped fighting the disguise and started enjoying it, the ornaments blooming genuinely beautiful around the buried names, and I hated how much I could see of my father in that progression, hated that I knew his hand the way I knew it, hated that pride of all things was among what I felt.

Because here is what the rubbings said, laid end to end:

Every radical printer in Grayharbor for twelve years. Every smuggler who'd needed bills of lading run off. Two customs men — *paid in silence*, one entry said, which I read five times. The schoolmaster who taught me figures and had left town abruptly when I was eleven. The widow Prentice, who sold pies on the quay and had once, I now learned, financed the printing of something called *An Address to the Free Seamen of England*, six hundred copies, paid in coin.

And entries I didn't recognize at all. Names from out of county. Dates going back further than I'd have guessed — the earliest was older than me. A code column of figures beside some names, amounts or debts or degrees of involvement, I couldn't tell. One entry consisted only of a date and the phrase *the Harwich arrangement*, which sounded like nothing and therefore meant everything.

Forty families. Call it forty. Call it forty households who had trusted a quiet typefounder because quiet men are easy to trust, and who had been written down, weighed, measured, and stored in lead against a day my father evidently always believed would come.

I want to be honest about the size of what I felt, because smaller accounts of this winter exist and they are lies.

There was awe in it. I won't pretend otherwise. My father, who fell asleep over the Scriptures on Sundays and apologized to the chair when he knocked it, had spent twelve years cutting a secret ledger into spare type one block at a time, in a shop that soldiers now walked past, keeping faith with a version of events he never spoke aloud. Each block represented hours. Gravers don't hurry. Some of the later ones were masterwork. He had built a cathedral out of scraps and hidden it in a graveyard and never once, in twelve years, not over supper, not on the docks, not in the dark of the stairs, had he smiled the smile of a man with a secret. I had lived three yards from this all my life. I had prided myself — oh, I had *prided* myself — on being the observant one, the quiet one who saw everything in Grayharbor and missed nothing.

And the whole time, the truest thing in the town had been within reach of my elbow, and it was in my father's hand, and it was about everyone but me.

That was the other feeling. I'll name it plainly: betrayal. Not because of what the ledger was. Because of what it proved — that my father was capable of this depth of concealment, this patience, this *art*, and had never once aimed any of it at honesty with me. He used to say names were dangerous currency, that a person should spend hers carefully, that silence was the Underhill inheritance. I thought he meant it as philosophy. He meant it as tradecraft. I was raised inside a cipher and taught to call it character.

And beneath the betrayal, colder and quieter, the question that got me off the stool and back to work:

Why keep it? A man hides evidence of crimes — even borrowed crimes, even other men's — because he expects to need it. Insurance, leverage, a hostage of his own. Which meant my father had looked at the world for twelve years and seen this coming: the notices, the gray coats, the polite clerk with the ironed handwriting. He had seen it coming and told me *we have nothing to hide*.

Which meant either he planned to protect me from knowing.

Or he planned to protect himself, and I was part of the hiding — the daughter named for a son, the innocent face of the shop, the best possible explanation walking around in an apron, dusting type, believing everything.

I proofed the last blocks as the sky behind the shutters went the color of dishwater. Entry fifty-nine was a name I didn't know. Entry sixty was the Harwich arrangement again, with a second date added. Entry sixty-one, the most recent, the finest cut, the one whose ornament was so lovely I almost hated to spoil it with pencil —

I rubbed it twice, certain I'd erred. I got the candle closer. I got it closer still, until the flame bent against the paper.

**UNDERHILL, J. — wife, ELIN. — 1 line pearl, cut fine. Owing.**

My mother's name, cut into lead by my father's hand, filed away among smugglers and informants, marked *owing* — owing whom, owing what, owing whom *what* — in a ledger she had died without knowing existed, four years before the earliest block I could date, except the dates must be wrong, except the earliest block must lie, except my father had cut her into this collection of other people's secrets and I did not know yet whether it was an accusation or a monument or a debt he was still paying and I stood in the cold foundry at dawn holding a rubbing of my dead mother's name and understood precisely nothing, and heard, above me, the floorboard creak of my father waking.

I put out the lamp. I gathered the rubbings into my apron. The blocks went back into their false tray, the false tray back behind its screen of broken matrices, and I had just smoothed the dust with my sleeve when his step reached the third stair from the top, the one that always complained.

Ten days, the notice had said.

I had six hundred and eleven questions, sixty-one blocks of evidence, my mother's name folded in my apron, and a father descending the stairs to breakfast in a house that had never contained so large a lie.

And across town, I would learn soon enough, a clerk named Vane was already sharpening his pencils.

* * *

He came down whistling. Low, tuneless, the way he always whistled when his mind was working on metal and didn't want company. He hung his coat, banked the stove ashes to flame, and said, "You're up early," in the voice of a man who suspected nothing, and I looked at Josiah Underhill across twelve years and one folded piece of paper and said the hardest sentence of my life so far:

"Couldn't sleep. Thought I'd start sorting the back cases. For the inventory."

"Good girl." He poured the water for tea. His hands, I noticed — his hands were steady. Twelve years of secrets and prison-worthy lead and his hands were steady, and mine, holding my own cup, were not, and he looked at them, just for a moment, just at my hands, and something moved behind his face like a fish under ice.

"You'll want to be careful with the old cases," he said. "Some of the trays stick."

"Yes," I said. "I found."

The kettle sang. The fish sank. He carried his tea to the window and watched the fog come off the harbor, and I stood in my own kitchen holding my own cup and understood that we had just exchanged our first lie as equals, and that he knew it, and that neither of us would say so.

Three days later, a man with very clean boots would walk through our door, introduce himself as Aldous Vane, compliment our stock, and begin asking — gently, terribly, one by one — about customers long gone.

And I would stand there in my apron with my father's gift for silence sitting newly in my mouth, and lie.
