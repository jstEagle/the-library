## 5. The Inventory Men

They came on a Tuesday, because bureaucracies are cruel in small ways before they are cruel in large ones, and Tuesday is the day my father sorted his matrices.

There were two of them: a soldier with a face like a shut door and a musket he carried the way other men carry umbrellas — without thought, without affection — and behind him a thin man in a gray coat that had been good five years ago and had decided not to give up on itself. He had spectacles cleaned to a shine that seemed almost aggressive, and he carried a ledger of his own, which I found professionally interesting. Everyone in my life kept ledgers. It was only a question of what they wrote in them.

"Underhill," the thin man said, consulting a paper rather than looking at us. "Underhill & Sons. Proprietor: Josiah Underhill." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Sons."

"My sons are my type," my father said.

It was the sort of answer a man gives when he has rehearsed honesty so long it has worn smooth, like a pebble. The clerk's eyes came up from the paper and rested on me for exactly as long as they needed to, no more. I know how long that was. I counted. Two seconds. Long enough to see an apprentice girl standing where a son should be, and short enough that a man who wanted to miss it could miss it.

"Aldous Vane," he said. "Commissioner of inventory, Southern District. We're here to count your shop, Mr. Underhill. Not you. Not yet." He smiled, and it was a strange thing, that smile, because it contained no warmth whatsoever but also no cruelty. It was simply accurate. "I find that putting people at their ease produces better counts."

He put us at our ease for four days.

* * *

I want to be fair to him, which is a sentence I did not expect to write about any man in a gray coat, and fairness requires me to say this: Aldous Vane was very good at his work. I have spent my life around men who were good at theirs — cutters who could strike a letter so fine the paper wept ink into it, casters whose hands knew the temperature of metal by the color of the air above it — and I know what mastery looks like from three feet away. It looks like ease. It is never ease.

On the first morning he walked the length of the foundry with his hands clasped behind him like a bishop touring a garden, and he admired everything. The casting bench. The dressing room where we finished the sorts. The wall of drawers, each labeled in my father's upright hand — *long s, double pica, canon* — a taxonomy of every sound a mouth can make, filed by size.

"You keep a clean house," Vane said.

"We make things people trust with their names," my father said. "Cleanliness is the trade."

"Mm." Vane ran one finger along the edge of the composing stone and examined it for dust. There wasn't any. He seemed neither pleased nor disappointed, which frightened me more than either would have. "You'll understand that requisition requires documentation. Every case inventoried. Every drawer weighed, sampled, recorded. Your customers' accounts reviewed. The army does not confiscate ignorance, Mr. Underhill. It confiscates *records*. Ignorance it leaves where it lies, as being of no value to anyone."

My father said, "The ledgers are in the office."

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I am allotted, the thing I turned over all that first night while the house ticked and settled above the cold cellar where the metal sleeps: my father said it plainly. No hesitation. No flicker. Because there was nothing to hide — or so I believed then, with the comfortable faith of a daughter who has lived twelve years inside a man's silences without once asking what they were made of. The master ledger sat in the office, honest as a Bible, recording forty years of orders: type sold, type delivered, names taken, money received. Every dissident printer in three counties was in that book under his own name, because my father had never seen a reason to do otherwise. Business, he always said, is conducted in daylight or not at all.

So when Vane asked for the ledger, my father gave it to him, and I stood by the casting bench with my hands in my apron and felt something I could not then name and can now: it was the feeling of watching a man walk past a precipice whistling, certain the ground goes on forever.

* * *

Vane read the way I cut type: completely, and without hurry, and with the understanding that mistakes live in the margins.

By the second afternoon he had filled eleven pages of his own book, and he began to ask questions. That was the phrase I settled on later, telling Tamsin about it over the harbor rail — he didn't *interrogate*, he *asked*, in the tone of a man making conversation at a funeral, when everyone speaks softly because the silence is doing the real work.

"Mr. Underhill. Here's an order, March of '89. Fourteen pounds nonpareil, delivered not to the buyer's address but to the cooper's yard on Tanner's Lane. A Mr. Elias Crane." He looked up. "Coopers rarely print pamphlets."

"Crane printed bills of lading," my father said. "For the cooper. It saved him carriage."

"How industrious. And here — a purchase by a woman giving no surname. 'Mrs. R.,' the entry says. Paid in coin, collected after dark." Vane's pen hovered. "You extended credit terms to no one, I notice. Cash on delivery throughout, excepting six houses. The six houses are the merchant families on Chapel Row." Another smile, accurate as a surveyor's mark. "A cautious tradesman, our Mr. Underhill. Except twice. Once for Mrs. R., once for Crane. Caution has exceptions exactly where curiosity lives."

I was dressing casts ten feet away, and every word came across the room to me with the clarity of struck type. I watched my father's back. His shoulders did not change. His voice, when it came, was the voice he used for quoting prices — level, uninterested, slightly bored, the voice of a man who answers questions all day and finds them all equally small.

"I don't ask buyers what they buy type for. Asking is bad trade. People come to a foundry to be anonymous; anonymity is half the product, like the box a coffin comes in."

"An elegant philosophy."

"It's an expensive one. It cost me the custom of everyone who wanted credit."

Vane laughed. It was a real laugh, brief and genuine, and somehow that was the worst moment of the day, because a man who laughs at your father's jokes while copying his ledger into another ledger is a man who understands exactly what he is doing, and does it anyway, and enjoys the craft of it.

Then he turned to me.

"And this is?"

"Cyrus," my father said. "My apprentice."

"Cyrus." Vane looked at me over his spectacles, and I understood that he was letting my father answer for me, and that the courtesy was a kind of trapdoor standing open in the floor of the conversation. "A family name?"

"On the mother's side," I said.

Here is what happened next, and I have replayed it enough times to describe it honestly. Something opened in me — not courage; courage implies a choice — something more like a door swinging on oiled hinges, a door I had not known was in the wall. And out of it came fluency. My mother's maiden name, which I had never spoken aloud in this shop because it was hers and not mine to spend. A village two counties inland where a cousin had supposedly kept books. A dead uncle Cyrus who had wanted sons. Each detail arrived fully dressed, pressed, and punctual, as though they had been waiting years in a green room for their cue, and perhaps they had — twelve years of watching my father construct a quiet life out of omissions may teach a person the whole grammar of it, even if she'd never been given leave to speak a sentence.

"Your uncle taught you the trade?" Vane asked.

"He died before I was born," I said, regretfully, and believed it myself for almost a full second.

Vane studied me. Not long. Two seconds again; I counted, because counting steadied something in my chest that wanted to sprint. Then he inclined his head — to me, mind, not to my father — as one craftsman acknowledges another across a workshop, and went back to his papers, and I stood there with my heart going like a drop hammer and my face arranged into the mild blankness of a girl who knows nothing except the dressing of type.

That night I lay awake and made myself say it plainly, because I have a horror of flattering myself: *you lied well.* Not well for a first attempt. Well. The details had been specific, the regret had been genuine — I had borrowed real grief for a fake uncle, which is a form of theft I hadn't previously known I was capable of — and most damning of all, I had enjoyed it. Not the fear. The *craft*. The feeling of the lie setting true in its mold, coming out crisp at the edges, no flash, no seam. It had felt like cutting a letter that prints perfectly on the first pull.

Fear, it turns out, has a talent. Mine had just introduced itself.

* * *

On the third day Vane weighed the type.

This is not a figure of speech. He had the soldier — whose name, I learned, was Brant, and who spoke approximately four words per day, all of them monosyllabic — carry cases down to a borrowed scale, and he compared the weights against the ledger's record of stock on hand. Metal does not lie. Metal cannot lie; it is the whole reason I love it. Forty years of purchases against forty years of sales, pound against pound, and somewhere in the arithmetic of it, discrepancies would surface the way drowned things surface, slowly and at their own insistence and always too late.

I helped carry cases. My father watched Vane watch the scales. And I did the sums in my head as we worked, faster than Vane could, because I had done the foundry's sums since I was nine and my father still believed I was copying out alphabets.

We balanced. Almost. Within the tolerance of wear, of dross skimmed from the pot, of sorts lost to breakage and time. Vane frowned at page thirty-one of his own book for a long minute, and I stood behind him holding a case of fourteen-point and thinking, with a calm that alarmed me: *the missing weight is in the cellar, in spare blocks, cut in reverse, and I am the only living soul who can read it.*

He gave up the discrepancy as rounding. Or he pretended to give it up. With Vane I have learned that those are often the same act.

On the fourth morning he returned alone — no Brant, no musket — carrying a small parcel wrapped in oilcloth, and asked, almost shyly, whether the foundry sold keepsakes. Single type blocks, letters mounted on wood, the sort of trinket printers give their children. My father, wary now in the way of a man who has been handled gently for three days and distrusts gentleness on principle, said yes, we had offcasts, what letter did the gentleman require.

"Any," Vane said. "Whatever is at hand."

And I saw it, the way you sometimes see a whole weather system in the behavior of one gull: he wanted a sample of our work. He wanted to hold our metal in his hands and take it home and know it, the way a man learns a face. He chose an ampersand, offcast, thirteen-pound, and paid full price, and thanked us, and at the door he paused with his hand on the frame and said, without turning around:

"Mr. Underhill. When the inventory closes, some shops reopen within the month and some do not. In my experience the difference is rarely the ledger." Now he turned, and the accurate smile was back, and behind the spectacles his eyes were doing arithmetic of a kind no scale could check. "The difference is usually the second ledger. There is always a second ledger. Tradesmen are sentimental creatures; they cannot bear for anything to go unrecorded." He settled his hat. "Good day. I'll want your mark on the final count tomorrow."

The door closed. My father stood very still in the middle of his own foundry, among forty years of letters, and I watched the whistling man finally look down and discover the ground had ended some steps ago.

"There is no second ledger," he said, to the door, or to himself, in the voice of a man saying a prayer he intends to believe.

And I — who had lied beautifully for four days, who had discovered a fluent and willing stranger living just behind my own teeth — I said nothing. Because there *was* a second ledger. I had found it a week before the gray coats came, sorting the spare-case drawer: blocks cut in reverse, impressions that read as names when pulled in ink, entries in a private cipher threaded through with letters I recognized as my father's hand and dated across the whole span of my apprenticeship. I had told no one. I had not decided yet whether it was treasure or evidence or a bomb waiting on a fuse of paper.

Now the decision had a deadline. Tomorrow, the final count. After that, the army's arithmetic and Aldous Vane's patience, and a cellar full of metal that could hang forty families, mine first.

My father went through to the office to prepare the closing papers. I took the lamp and went down into the cellar, where the metal sleeps, and knelt at the spare-case drawer with my heart going soft and quick, and began, for the first time in earnest, to try to read my father's cipher — starting with the one entry I could already decipher, the oldest one, cut in a hand shakier than his present hand, dated the winter I was born.

It was a name. It was not mine.
