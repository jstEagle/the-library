## 7. Checkpoint One: The Bridge

There is a particular way a person walks when she has four stolen pieces of evidence sewn into her coat, and I had practiced eliminating it for three days in the foundry's back room, pacing between the type racks with my father asleep upstairs, teaching my shoulders to forget what my ribs knew. The body wants to confess. That was the first thing the blocks taught me. Lead is honest metal — it takes whatever shape you press into it and holds it forever — but flesh is a coward made of tattletales. My heartbeat wanted to announce itself to every gray coat in Grayharbor. My hands wanted to drift toward the left lining of my coat the way a tongue worries a broken tooth.

So I walked to market. I bought onions. I let a fishwife cheat me out of a farthing without arguing, because arguing draws eyes, and I walked home with my onions like a woman whose entire biography fit inside a string bag.

The bridge over the Alden was the town's throat. Everything going south — grain, gossip, contraband gin, and now, apparently, revolution — passed over its single span, where the army had planted two barrels, a sawhorse barrier, and four soldiers in coats the color of wet ash. I had been watching them for two days from the window of Maren's bakery, which faces the bridge road and sells, in my considered opinion, the worst rolls in the county, a fact I had previously held against the establishment and now counted among its virtues, since nobody loiters over bad rolls.

Two days of watching taught me this much: the soldiers were not hunting. They were bored. Boredom has a rhythm like any other thing, and once you can hear it you can move inside it. In the morning they were diligent and stupid, checking papers with the earnest thoroughness of men who expected to find something and would be disappointed all day until they did. By late afternoon the sergeant — a wide man with the complexion of boiled ham and the eyes of someone who had stopped expecting anything from the world around his twentieth year — sent the youngest one off to fetch his supper, and the checks grew lazy. Papers glanced at, not read. Faces glared at, not remembered.

Faces not remembered. That was the whole art. A face that gets remembered is a face that gets described later, and descriptions travel faster than ships.

I decided on the fourth day, which was a Thursday, because Thursdays brought the coal barges and the coal barges brought dust and dust gave everyone an excuse to be squinting and foul-tempered.

The blocks rode against my left side, sewn into the lining between two layers of my mother's old shawl-cloth, each one wrapped in waxed linen so they wouldn't clack. Four names. Forty families' worth of futures compressed into lead no bigger than my thumb joint. When I pressed my elbow against my ribs I could feel them there, dense and patient as sin.

My father did not know I'd taken them. He knew the second ledger existed — he'd cut it, letter by letter, across twelve years of spare blocks, hiding dissidents' names in the very alphabet he sold — but he believed it was insurance, a thing to sit in the dark and never be needed. He thought armies were weather; you waited them out. But the requisition notices had gone up on Monday, and Vane's inventory men had already been through the shop twice, and I had done arithmetic my father refused to do: if the coded blocks were found in our cases, we would hang alongside everyone whose names we carried. And if they were never moved at all, the forty families would be found anyway, because Vane had fragments from the print circle and a patience I did not like thinking about.

Someone had to carry the letters out. It seemed the sort of work that fell to people who could pass unnoticed, and after twelve years of being no one in particular, I was, if nothing else, qualified.

* * *

Thursday morning came down gray and smelling of snow that couldn't commit. I wore my delivery coat, the one I used hauling proofs to the printers, patched at both elbows because a courier with fine elbows invites questions. Under it, the blocks. In my basket, six printed handbills for a chandler — genuine ones, dull as porridge — because the best place to hide cargo is under boring freight.

The queue at the bridge was short. Coal dust hung in the air like bad news. I took my place behind a cooper's apprentice and rehearsed being unremarkable: I am delivering bills for Hobbs the chandler, no I don't know when I'll be back, yes it's cold, isn't it. Simple sentences. Truth-shaped lies, which are the only kind that hold their casting.

The young soldier — he couldn't have been twenty, with a farm boy's knuckles and a coat a size too hopeful — waved the cooper through and looked at me.

"Papers."

"I'm not carrying papers," I said, and let the irritation come through just enough. "Delivery girl. Hobbs and Sons." I tilted the basket so he could see the handbills. "Unless the army's started taxing advertisements."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not suspicion, exactly. Recognition. He looked at my hands.

I hadn't thought about my hands. I'd thought about my walk, my face, my voice, my basket. But twelve years at the composing stone leaves its signature: ink in the knuckle creases that no lye fully lifts, a certain callus on the right middle finger, small nicks scarred white along the fingertips where the cutting blade had kissed me over the years. My hands looked like what they were. A cutter's hands. And somewhere in the army's instructions, I suspected, was a note that the underground in Grayharbor was a printing underground.

"Those bills," he said slowly. "You set them yourself?"

"My father sets them. I carry them." I shifted the basket on my hip, and the movement pulled my coat tight across my ribs, and I felt every one of those four blocks the way you feel your own name when someone shouts it. "Do you want one? They're for lamp oil. Riveting subject."

He almost smiled. Then the sergeant's voice came down the line like a dropped plank.

"What's the delay, Brandt?"

"Just asking about the bills, Sergeant."

The sergeant — Boiled Ham himself — came over at the pace of a man who had never once hurried for anything he didn't plan to eat later. He looked at my basket. He looked at my face. And then he said the sentence I had been dreading for three days, saying it in the tone of a man commenting on weather:

"Open the coat."

* * *

Here is what terror does, once you've lived with it long enough: it stops screaming and starts doing sums. Some cold clerk deep in my skull began itemizing while the rest of me stood there wearing a face of mild rural offense. Coat lined throughout, single layer at collar and cuffs, doubled only at the left breast and side seam. If he pats, he'll start at the shoulders. If he opens, he'll see the stitching — new thread, wrong color, pale against the shawl-cloth. There was perhaps the space of ten heartbeats in which none of this mattered yet, and I spent nine of them deciding how to spend the tenth.

Then a cart came over the bridge from the far side, fast, and a voice called out ahead of it with all the authority of inherited paperwork:

"Army freight! Make way — quartermaster's manifest, Sergeant Hobb, and if you make me wait in this cold my father will write to your colonel, who owes him money!"

Tamsin Corve.

She sat up beside a driver on a flatbed loaded with casks, wrapped in a coat nearly as gray as the soldiers', holding a leather folio above her head like a bishop hoisting a relic. Her father was the harbormaster, and the harbormaster's daughter possesses the one weapon no sergeant enjoys facing: somebody else's paperwork, signed by somebody else's superior.

Sergeant Hobb's face performed a small civil war between duty and the desire to remain uncomplained-about. The cart was already rolling. Tamsin caught my eye across the sawhorse — one flash of it, quick as a type block dropping into its case — and then she was looking past me as if I were furniture, calling down something about the tide tables to her driver.

"Go on, then," Hobb said to me, already turning toward the manifest. "Mind the gap."

I crossed the bridge with my onions of composure intact and my heart conducting a full riot behind them. On the south bank I did not run. Running is confession. I walked, and I let myself feel it only when I reached the chandler's, delivered the handbills, and stood a moment in the reek of tar with my hand pressed flat over my left side like a woman with a heartache, which was not entirely a lie.

Four names, safe on the south side of the river. Three checkpoints remained between here and the harbor quay, and I had just learned two things at the price of one near-catastrophe: first, that my hands would betray my trade to anyone who knew what to look for. Second, that Tamsin Corve had lied to a sergeant of the occupying army with the effortless grace of a woman pouring tea, and had done it — I was almost sure — for me.

We had never exchanged more than a dozen words in our lives. She was the harbormaster's daughter, orbiting in the town's bright upper ring while I orbited invisibly in the lower dark of the foundry. And she had seen a girl stopped at a bridge and understood in one glance what was in the balance, which meant she had known about the ledger's journey, or guessed it, which meant someone had talked, which meant —

No. Sums again. Later. One terror per day; a body can only cast so much.

* * *

I found her that evening, or rather she permitted herself to be found, standing at the harbor rail where the fishing boats knocked together in the dusk. She didn't look at me when I came alongside.

"You owe me a cartload of quartermaster's salt," she said. "It doesn't exist. By tomorrow it will have been delivered to a warehouse that also doesn't exist. My father's clerks will enter it in the daybook because they enter everything, and the army will count forty casks of salt that no one can produce, and there will be a small, tedious inquiry that goes nowhere. All of that, for you."

"Why?" I asked. It seemed the only question worth spending.

Tamsin watched a lantern swing on the mast of some distant brig. "Because my brother's name is written down somewhere," she said quietly, "in ink or in metal, I don't know which, and if the lists get read out someday, I intend to know about it before the reading. You're the only person in this town who might know what's written where." She turned to me then, and her face in the lamplight was neither friendly nor unfriendly — it was businesslike, which from a harbormaster's daughter is considerably more valuable than friendship. "So we're going to be useful to each other, Cyrus Underhill. Starting with the bridge. Because you'll be crossing it again — don't insult me by denying it, I can smell the lead on you — and next time Hobb won't be the one working the line. Next time it might be the quiet one. Vane's man."

"The quiet one," I repeated. I had noticed him too, on the second day: a thin soldier who stood apart from the boredom and actually read the papers put in front of him, the way a man reads when reading is how he thinks.

"Vane doesn't post fools twice," Tamsin said. "Learn the rhythms faster. You won't get a third Thursday."

She was right, of course, and I hated her pleasantly for it. Walking home through the fog with the lamps coming up yellow along the quay, I made my sums properly at last. Four blocks south. Dozens still hidden in the hollowed cases at home, each one a name, each name a neck. Three checkpoints. One pair of telltale hands. And a soldier at the bridge who had looked at my fingers and asked about the setting of type with the mild, terrible curiosity of a man filing things away for later.

Purpose is a strange alloy. It is mostly terror, I discovered, smelted down with stubbornness until it pours. I climbed the foundry stairs past my sleeping father's door, sat at the cutting stone with the lamp turned low, and picked up the graver — not to cut, but to think, the way some men think with a pipe.

If my hands could betray me, then my hands would learn a new trade. Deliveries, errands, a laborer's calluses built up in wax and pumice over weeks I did not have. Or better: a reason for the hands to look as they looked. Printers' girls were everywhere in Grayharbor still, though the shops were burning. A girl carrying proofs drew less interest than a girl carrying onions, if the proofs were dull enough and the girl too bored to be interesting.

I would need routes. Times. A second and third story, each one true-shaped. I would need Tamsin, and Osei's Juniper waiting at anchor with its captain's careful greed, and more luck than I believed the century owed me.

And downstairs, in the hollowed case beneath the third rack, the rest of the ledger waited in the dark — including, I would discover soon enough, entries my father had never mentioned, cut in a hand steadier than his own.

But that night I knew only this: I had crossed the bridge once with death riding my left side, and I had not been searched, and the world had gone on, and somewhere across the river four names slept safely in waxed linen. Tomorrow I would map the tollhouse road. Tonight I allowed myself five minutes at the window, looking down at Grayharbor's roofs smoking gently into the fog, a town pretending with all its might that nothing was happening.

Then I trimmed the lamp, took up the graver, and got back to work.
