## 8. The Juniper at Anchor

The Juniper sat at the far end of the long quay like a woman who had learned not to make eye contact with the customs house.

I had walked past her a dozen times in my life — every harbor child has a mental inventory of the ships that come and go, the way other children catalog dogs — but I had never looked at her properly until now. She was a brig, modest, black-hulled, her paint gone chalky at the waterline where the sea chewed at it year after year. Her name was painted small on the stern, almost apologetically, as though she'd rather not be identified. I approved of this instinct entirely.

The fog was coming in off the water in long gray ribbons, and the town behind me was still digesting the news of the requisition. Ten days for all business ledgers. The notice had been nailed to the foundry door that morning by a soldier who couldn't have been more than nineteen and who apologized with his eyebrows while he hammered. My father had watched him work with an expression I couldn't read, which was nothing new; my father's face was a locked type case, and I'd only ever been given a few of the sorts.

But I wasn't thinking about my father. I was thinking about four blocks of lead sewn into the lining of my coat, each one heavy as a sin, riding against my ribs with every step down the quay.

* * *

Let me explain how a girl who has spent twelve years being invisible finds a smuggler.

You don't find smugglers. You arrange to be findable. This is one of the many things Grayharbor taught me without ever saying it aloud. I had spent three days doing nothing more suspicious than delivering proofs — legitimate proofs, to legitimate customers, my father's actual errands, which is always the best cover because it requires no lying whatsoever. On the second day I let myself be seen reading the manifest board at the harbormaster's office. On the third day, a boy with a face like a closed shutter fell into step beside me on Coopers' Lane and said, "The captain will see you at evening slack water. Come alone. Come bored."

"Come *bored*?" I'd repeated.

"He doesn't like people who want things," the boy said, and peeled away down an alley as if he were made of fog himself.

So I practiced looking bored all afternoon, which was not difficult. Boredom is my native language. I have spoken it fluently since I was nine years old.

* * *

Evening slack water came, and with it a light rain that couldn't decide whether to be snow. The quay was nearly empty. A customs watchman stood under the eave of the weigh-house, sheltering from the weather and from his own employment in equal measure. He nodded to me as I passed. I nodded back, a girl out walking, bored to death, carrying nothing but errands.

The gangway of the Juniper had a man at the foot of it, mending a net that did not need mending. He looked at me once, thoroughly, the way you'd inspect a coin suspected of being clipped.

"I've a crate of proofs for the captain," I said. "From Underhill's."

That much was true. I carried an actual crate of actual proofs on my hip, because I am my father's daughter and do nothing halfway, including deception.

The net-mender jerked his chin toward the deck. "Mind the third stair. It lies."

"So does everything else in this town," I said, and went up.

* * *

The captain was in the stern cabin, and he was not what I expected, which is a thing I should stop expecting, since Grayharbor has never once delivered what I expected.

I had imagined a smuggler captain as a certain kind of man — scarred, loud, a beard with opinions. Captain Osei was a tall, spare Ghanaian with gray threading his close-cropped hair and reading spectacles pushed up onto his forehead, and when I came in he did not look up from the chart he was annotating. He simply said, "Shut the door. The lamp's got a nervous disposition."

I shut the door. The lamp did look nervous. Everything on the Juniper looked like it had been chosen for its ability to survive bad weather and worse company.

"You're the foundry girl," he said. Still not looking up. His accent had traveled a long way to get here; it carried salt and somewhere else in it, someplace warmer than Grayharbor had ever managed.

"Cyrus Underhill."

"Mm." He finished his annotation, set down his pencil, took the spectacles off his forehead and put them on, and only then looked at me — a long, deliberate look, top to bottom, the kind that makes most people want to apologize for something. I held still. Years of standing behind a counter while strangers stared through me had trained me well. "Sit," he said, indicating a barrel. "You're the girl whose father sells type to people who shouldn't be buying it."

"My father sells type," I said. "What people print with it is their own affair."

"That's a very convenient religion."

"It's the only one I've found that pays."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that a joke had attempted to land nearby. He pulled a bottle and two cups from a shelf that swung gently on gimbals, poured with the economy of a man who resents wasting motion, and pushed one cup across the table at me.

I drank it. It was terrible. I drank all of it anyway, because I sensed we were negotiating, and in negotiations you don't refuse things.

"Now," Osei said, leaning back. "Tell me why a foundry girl climbs aboard my ship at slack water with a crate of paper and a coat that weighs too much on the left side."

The coat. Of course the coat. My heart performed a maneuver I can only describe as falling down stairs, but I kept my face where I'd put it, which was nowhere in particular.

"The coat is padded," I said. "It's winter."

"In 1792? Remarkable. Nobody told me." He folded his hands. "Girl. I've been running this coast since before you could walk. I know what a coat full of contraband looks like from across a harbor. You're lucky it was me who noticed and not the watchman under the weigh-house, who is lazy but not blind, and who I pay precisely enough to be looking the other way on Tuesdays."

"Is it Tuesday?"

"Don't be clever. Cleverness is a candle you burn at both ends and then you're standing in the dark wondering where your fingers went." He let that sit for a moment. Then: "Talk. What do you want carried?"

And there it was — the moment the whole walk down the quay had been building toward, and I found that all my rehearsed openings evaporated, and what came out instead was the truth, or near enough to it that the difference didn't matter:

"There are names," I said. "Names the army wants, cut into metal, in a code they don't have the key to. If they get them, forty families in this town hang or transport. If someone else gets them, those families live. I need them out of Grayharbor before the year turns."

Osei listened without moving. When I finished, he reached over and turned the lamp down a shade, which I understood to mean the conversation had just become more expensive.

"And why does a foundry girl care about forty families?"

Because one of them might have been mine, I did not say. Because I have spent twelve years learning everyone's names except my own. Because my father cut those blocks in secret and never told me, and every night since I found them I have lain awake feeling betrayed and proud in equal measure and I cannot tell which one is winning.

"Because somebody has to," I said.

"Hm." He studied me. "You're a bad liar, foundry girl. Fortunately for you, you're a good enough one for government work."

* * *

We negotiated for the better part of an hour, and I will record honestly that Captain Kwabena Osei out-negotiated me so thoroughly that I left that cabin having agreed to terms I hadn't known existed when I walked in.

He would carry cargo. That much he offered almost freely — cargo was his trade, cargo asked no questions, cargo did not weep at trial. Metal type, sealed, manifested as ballast trimming or ship's fittings, no questions from him and none from me about what else shared his hold. For that: payment in kind. Foundry work. Type cast and cut to his specification, delivered quietly, the sort of custom plates and seals and lettering that a smuggling brig finds useful and a respectable foundry would never admit to producing. He named a figure. It was steep. I haggled him down four percent and realized afterward he'd let me think I'd won something.

"But," he said, and held up one finger, and the finger was the whole rest of the negotiation, "not people. And not causes."

"It's not a cause. It's evidence—"

"Evidence is the most dangerous cause there is." He stood and went to the stern windows, where the rain traced slow lines down the glass and the lights of Grayharbor swam behind it like fish under ice. "You know what I was doing before I ran cargo? I'll tell you briefly, because it explains my prices. I carried people. Refugees, out of the colonies, during the troubles. Good work. Righteous work. Do you know how it ended? A hold full of families, a patrol sloop, and me watching from a longboat while they burned my ship at anchor rather than let anyone prove what she'd been carrying." He turned. "I rebuilt. I run metal, cloth, brandy, papers when the price is right. I do not run people and I do not run causes, because people and causes burn, and I have already been burned once for other men's convictions."

"The cargo is the cause," I said. "The metal *is* the people. Forty families are inside those blocks."

"Then it's fortunate for them," said Osei mildly, "that metal doesn't scream when it burns."

I hated him then, briefly and completely, sitting on his barrel in his low-ceilinged cabin with his terrible brandy and his burned-ship eyes. Hated him for making it arithmetic. Hated him more for being right to.

"When the year turns," I said slowly, "and the army has done whatever the army is going to do to this town — will you still be calling it arithmetic?"

"Yes," he said. "That's why I'm still alive to call it anything." He came back to the table, sat, and refilled both cups, and this time the pour was slightly more generous, which I chose to read as respect. "Here is the whole of my offer, foundry girl, so listen carefully, because I'll say it once. I sail when the ice allows and the manifests allow, likely before the century turns, likely not. Your metal goes in my hold, sealed, quiet, no names attached — not yours, not your father's, not these forty families'. I deliver it where you say, to whom you say, and after that it is not my story anymore. What happens to it ashore is the affair of whoever receives it. In exchange: your work, your silence, and your assurance that no one aboard ever learns what the cargo means. The day my crew starts believing in something, I lose the ship."

"And if I need passage myself someday?"

His eyes went flat and careful. "Then that would be a different negotiation. With different numbers. Numbers I hope you never hear."

* * *

I should have left then. The bargain was struck; the sensible move was up the gangway, past the net-mender, home through the rain to the foundry and my father's locked type-case face.

Instead I heard myself ask: "Why the Juniper?"

He looked at me over the spectacles. "What?"

"The ship. Why name her that?"

For a long moment I thought he wouldn't answer. Then he leaned back and something old surfaced in his face, some current beneath the calm.

"A juniper grows where nothing else will," he said. "Poor soil, thin ground, hard winters. Doesn't grow tall. Doesn't grow fast. But it grows *anywhere*, and it stays green when everything else is bare sticks." He tapped the table once. "I needed a ship that would survive being underestimated. So did the tree. Seemed a fair arrangement." He stood, which was dismissal. "Third stair still lies. Use the second rail."

At the companionway I stopped, because there was one more question and it had been sitting in my chest since the bridge, since the notices went up, since the first block sang under my thumb in the dark of the shop.

"If the army comes to the harbor," I said, not turning around, "the way they came to the town — what happens to ships like yours?"

Behind me, silence, and then, quietly, the sound of a man deciding to answer honestly because the question deserved it:

"Foundry girl. Armies don't come to harbors. Harbors happen to armies. They requisition the quays, impress the crews, burn what won't serve. Whatever is afloat when the soldiers arrive is either useful to them or it's smoke." A pause. "Get your metal to me before the year turns. After that, I promise you nothing, because promising is how honest sailors die liars."

* * *

The rain had thickened while I was below. The watchman had abandoned his post under the weigh-house entirely, and the quay belonged to the fog and to me. The third stair lied, as promised; the second rail held.

Halfway down the gangway, the net-mender spoke without looking up from his useless net.

"Left side of your coat's heavier than when you came aboard."

My hand was on the rail before I knew I'd moved. "It's winter," I said.

"It is," he agreed, and went back to his knots, and let me pass, and I walked the length of the long quay with my heart hammering against four blocks of lead and my face arranged into perfect, practiced boredom, and I thought: *this is what I am now. A girl who negotiates with smugglers and lies to strangers on gangways and calls it errands.*

I could not decide whether the feeling in my chest was terror or freedom. Possibly they are the same metal, stamped from different sides of the same matrix.

* * *

Home. The foundry lamps were out but the forge banked low, my father's chair empty, his door shut, his silence seeping under it like cold. I hung my soaked coat on its peg with the left side carefully toward the wall.

And then, because I could not help it — because Osei's arithmetic had gotten into my blood and started counting — I lit the small lamp at my bench and drew the hidden blocks from their hiding place among the spare cases, and began again the work that had eaten every night since I found them.

Block by block. Name by name. The reversed letters swimming in the lamplight, waiting for me to turn them into the world.

Fourteen names deciphered. Twenty-six to go, and the winter shortening every day like the light itself.

I pressed the fifteenth block into the soft wax I'd melted from a stub of sealing stick, and lifted it, and bent close to read the impression —

— and stopped breathing.

I knew that name. I had known it before I could read.

It was my mother's.
