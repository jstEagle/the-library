## 9. Names in Metal

My father slept like a man who had made peace with his conscience, which is to say loudly and without apology. The snores came through the floorboards in a rhythm I knew as well as I knew the tread of my own feet on the foundry stairs — a rhythm I had been falling asleep to since I was four years old and had never once, in twelve years of sharing a roof with him, thought to question.

It was past midnight when I went down to the shop. I told myself it was the cold keeping me awake, and then that it was Vane's visit still sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone, and then — because lying to the empty room seemed pointless even by my standards — I admitted it was the blocks.

Four of them sat on my workbench where I'd left them, ordinary-looking as any of the ten thousand sorts in the racks behind me. Pica body, well-worn, faces nicked from years of use. They had spent their lives in the jobbing case by the window, the case every compositor in Grayharbor had thumbed a hundred times without a second glance. And all that while they had been carrying names.

I had discovered it three nights ago by accident, the way most truths arrive: clumsily, at the wrong hour, while looking for something else. I'd been proofing a job lot of handbills for the chandler and had grabbed the wrong case, pulled a proof of what should have been gibberish — spare sorts, mixed pi, the kind of jumble that accumulates in any working shop — and there, reversed in the ink, half-formed but unmistakable, was a name. Not a word. A *name*. *Hollis*. With a date after it, and a figure, and a mark I didn't recognize.

Spare type doesn't spell things by accident. Type is set; someone sets it. Someone had stood at a composing stick in this very shop and arranged those letters deliberately, then distributed them back into the general stock like a man hiding a letter in a haystack, one character at a time.

Tonight I meant to find out how much of the haystack was letter.

* * *

The method, once I worked it out, was beautiful in a grim way, and I confess part of me — the part that has always loved the craft more than is strictly safe — admired it even as it frightened me.

You could not simply set the coded blocks in a stick and pull a proof. Anyone could do that; any soldier turning the shop upside down might pull a random proof and strike gold. No. My father — or whoever's hand it was, and I was still telling myself *whoever* with the diligence of a woman rowing away from a waterfall — had been cleverer. The coded names were cut into blocks whose *faces* were ordinary letters. What you saw when you looked at the type was its public self: an *e*, an *r*, a worn-out ampersand good for nothing but padding out a line. But the sides of the shanks — the flanks no one ever looks at, the private faces of metal — carried fine scored marks. Grooves. Notches. A system.

The notches told you where in the case's jumble to look for its fellows. Block bearing the face-letter *k*, three grooves on the left flank: go three cases down, find the sort that pairs with it. It was a map written on the pieces of the map. You assembled the name the way you'd assemble any locked job, following the metal's own instructions, and when you were done you had a line of type that spelled something no one had authorized, dated and numbered and marked with that same strange device — a small circle bisected by a stroke, stamped deep, deliberate as a signature.

I recognized the device after an hour of staring at it, and recognizing it did not comfort me. It was my father's journeyman's mark, the stamp he struck on finished matrices before they went to the hardening furnace. His own sign, hidden in plain sight on every entry, the way a man signs a confession he intends someday to be read.

"Well," I said to the lamp, "that's one mystery solved and forty opened."

The lamp, being a lamp, declined to comment. I pulled my coat tighter — the shop held the cold like a grudge in winter, all that iron and stone drinking the warmth out of the air — and set to work.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about decoding: it is tedious. Glorious fiction would have me racing through revelations while candles guttered dramatically, and indeed the candle did gutter, but mostly what happened was this: I sorted. I read flanks with a jeweler's loupe borrowed from the watch-mender's drawer my father kept for repairs. I built a tally on scrap paper, crossing out false starts, and twice I assembled a line that read backward because I'd misread the groove order and had to break it down and start again, cursing in whispers because the floor above me creaked with every snore-shift of the man whose secrets I was stealing.

By two in the morning I had five names.

*Hollis, 1787.* The old Quaker who ran the stationer's on Cooper's Lane and had died the year I turned eleven, mourned by everyone including, apparently, this ledger.

*Prynn.* A smuggler's name if ever there was one; the Prynns had been running brandy past the customs house since before the harbor had a customs house to run past.

*Wetherell.* That one landed strangely. Wetherell was the *loyalist* shipping agent, the man who dined with the fort commander. If his name was in a ledger of dissidents, either the ledger lied or Wetherell had two faces, and I knew enough about men who dined with forts to bet on the second.

*Anselm the Younger, 1790.*

And the fifth: *Merrick, 1784,* with a notation in the notch-code I couldn't yet parse — a longer sequence, a sentence rather than a name.

Five names out of what the density of marked blocks suggested was a great many more. I sat back and looked at the little pile of sorted metal and felt the shape of the thing settle onto my shoulders. This wasn't a list of customers. Customers were recorded in ledgers, in ink, in the honest open book that Vane had already taken away in its canvas satchel. This was the other book. The one for people who paid in silence instead of coin. Every name here was a name my father had chosen to remember in the only medium he trusted — and chosen to hide in the one place he could control absolutely.

A man who kept records like this wasn't a merchant. He was an archivist of dangerous things. And I was his heir apparent, promoted tonight without consultation.

* * *

I might have stopped there. I want it noted, somewhere in whatever record eventually survives of my life, that I considered stopping. It was nearly three. My father slept directly overhead. The decent, filial, self-preserving thing was to put the blocks back, distribute them into their cases exactly as found, and go to bed a girl who suspected her father of secrets the way everyone suspects their father of secrets — vaguely, forgivably, without evidence.

But there were more grooved blocks in the case. I could see them now that I knew what to look for — a slight extra polish along the flanks, the shine of metal handled carefully by someone who cared about these particular sorts more than the rest. And among the polished ones, near the bottom of the third case, one block whose notching matched nothing I'd yet placed. An orphan. A name waiting for its partners, patient as metal.

I found its companions in the jobbing case itself, tucked under the *q* and *z* sorts where no compositor alive would ever go unless desperate. I set them in the stick. I inked a proof. I pulled it.

And the press gave me my mother.

*Marguerite Underhill, 1780.*

Not Underhill. She hadn't been Underhill yet in 1780 — she'd married my father in '81, a fact I knew the way I knew the tide tables, from repetition rather than memory, since I had been two years old when she died and owned exactly nothing of her but a christening spoon and her name given to me sideways, disguised, converted into *Juniper* and then buried under *Cyrus* before I was old enough to know I'd been renamed.

*Marguerite Delacour.* That was the full entry, and beneath it the circle-and-stroke mark, and beneath *that* a long notch-sequence I couldn't read yet, several lines' worth, the longest entry I'd seen.

I put my hand flat on the proof and left it there a long time.

Here is the arithmetic of my particular orphanhood: I have now lived more years than she did at her death. She was thirty-one. I am twenty-four, and every year from here forward is a year she never got, spent in a shop she never saw, cutting letters she never read. As a child this had felt like a wound. Somewhere in adolescence it had calcified into a fact, the way wounds do when nobody tends them — and nobody had tended mine, because my father's grief was a locked room and I was raised on the corridor outside it.

Now here she was. In lead. In his hand. Cut and stamped and hidden with the smugglers and the Quaker and the double-faced Wetherell, dated six years before he ever needed insurance against soldiers, back when the only danger worth insuring against was grief itself.

He hadn't recorded her as family. He'd recorded her as *entry*. Same mark, same format, same careful numbering. Whatever this ledger was — debt book, protection roll, confession — my mother was in it on equal terms with a brandy-runner, and some cold analytical part of me wanted to know what figure followed her name, and the rest of me wanted to throw the whole case into the furnace.

I did neither. I am, whatever else may be said of me, a professional.

* * *

The sun came up gray over the harbor fog and I was still at the bench, surrounded by proofs laid out like a spread of losing cards. My father came down at seven, stopped on the last stair, and looked at the bench, and at me, and at the proofs, for a long moment.

I watched him decide. It was visible, the deciding — the way his eyes went to the door first (locked), then to the windows (shuttered), then to the proofs, and how his hands opened and closed once at his sides like a man testing whether he could still hold things.

"You found the third case pairing," he said finally. "Most don't. The groove reads ambiguous."

"That's your observation. That I found your secret ledger. Written in metal. Hidden in our walls for twelve years." I kept my voice level, which cost more than the night's work had. "That's what you're leading with."

"Would you prefer I start with an apology?"

"It would be traditional."

He crossed to the stove, fed it a knot of kindling, watched it catch. My father has always needed a fire to talk to. "It's not a rebellion ledger, Cyrus, whatever you're imagining."

"I don't imagine anymore. I've learned better this winter." I gathered the proofs, squared them, kept them in my hand where he couldn't take them without asking. "What is it, then? Because I've read five names so far, and one of them belongs to my mother, and I would very much like to know what business arrangement binds Marguerite Delacour to a brandy-smuggler who died before I was born."

Something moved through his face then — not guilt exactly. Older than guilt. The expression of a man shown a photograph of a house that burned down. "Sit," he said.

"I'll stand."

"Of course you will." He lowered himself onto the stool across from me, and for the first time I noticed properly how old he had gotten this winter, how the requisition notices and Vane's soft gray courtesy had aged him like acid fumes age copper. "When your mother came to Grayharbor, she didn't come alone. She came carrying debts — not hers. Her father's. Debts owed to people in Calais and people here, and the people here were the dangerous ones, because the people here could reach her." He warmed his hands. "I bought her debts. All of them. It was the only honorable way to marry a woman who wouldn't marry a man to escape her creditors. I paid the money, and I kept the record — every payment, every favor the debt bought, every name attached. Because in this town, Cyrus, a debt forgiven is never forgotten by the man who owed it. It sits in him like a splinter. I wanted proof of what we'd paid, in case anyone ever came to collect twice."

"And the others? Hollis. Prynn. Wetherell?"

"Others came to me over the years with the same need. People who needed their obligations *written* — fixed, provable, hidden. Ink burns. Paper can be seized." He gestured at the bench, at the thousand silent cases around us. "Metal endures. And only a cutter can read the flanks. I became a bank, of a kind. A bank of secrets."

"And Mother's entry — the long one I can't decode?"

His hands stilled on the stove's iron lip. For a moment I thought he wouldn't answer, and I was preparing the argument, the leverage, the whole apparatus of persuasion I'd been assembling since dawn.

"The long entries are narratives," he said quietly. "Names are short. Stories take more sorts." He met my eyes. "Your mother's story takes eleven lines. And I will tell it to you, Cyrus, all of it, I swear it — but not tonight. Tonight you need sleep, and I need you to understand one thing before anything else."

"Which is?"

"The army took the ink ledger this morning. Vane's clerk carried it out in that satchel like a dead cat." He stood, joints cracking, and looked down at the proofs in my hands with an expression I couldn't file under any of my usual headings. "Everything in *that* book damns us both. Everything in *these*" — he touched the edge of the topmost proof, lightly, the way a man touches a sleeping animal — "damns forty families. And the army cannot read it. Yet. Understand that *yet*, daughter. Vane is a patient man, and patience plus a type foundry equals a decoder, given time. So the question is no longer whether your father kept secrets from you."

He walked to the stairs, paused on the bottom step.

"The question is how fast you can learn to read the rest of it — and what we do with what you find. Because I cut some of those names in bad years, girl, for reasons that seemed sound at the time, and I promise you—" the stair creaked under his weight as he climbed, "...one of them is going to break your heart."

He went up. The door closed. The stove ticked.

I looked down at the proofs in my hands — my mother's name reversed in black on white, *Marguerite Delacour*, patient as metal — and then, because I was my father's daughter and had apparently inherited both his habits and his talent for finding terrible things at terrible hours, I picked up the loupe and went back to the third case, where a block I hadn't yet placed waited with fresh grooves and a face-letter I now recognized:

*T.*

And beneath the notch-code, when I finally assembled it near noon, a name I knew from the docks, from Tamsin's laughing mouth and Tamsin's fierce loyal family, from the manifests her father signed every week at the harbormaster's office.

Tamsin's brother. Dated 1791. Marked with a symbol I hadn't seen before — and would spend the next three nights learning to dread.
