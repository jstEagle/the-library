## 10. Vane Returns

The trouble with soldiers requisitioning your town is not the boots on the cobbles or the gray coats drying in every doorway. The trouble is that after a fortnight you stop noticing them, which is precisely when they start noticing you.

I had settled into a rhythm by the second week of December: father asleep by eight, me at the bench by nine, the coded blocks spread before me like a card game I was teaching myself to lose at. Two hours of deciphering, one hour of cutting ordinary type to keep my hands honest, then bed by midnight with the blocks sewn back into the false bottom of the lead drawer where Father had kept them for twelve years without my ever once thinking to look.

It was during the honest hour — cutting a run of fourteen-point bodoni for the customs house, of all the ironies, since the customs house had been dissolved and its forms would never see press — that the shop bell rang.

We had kept irregular hours since the requisition. The army wanted ledgers, not letters, and the townsfolk had learned that walking into Underhill & Sons was now an act with consequences attached, like wading into cold water you weren't sure you could stand up in. So when the bell rang at half past ten at night, I did not think *customer*. I thought *finally*.

I put down the graver and turned around slowly, the way you turn toward a sound in an empty house.

Aldous Vane stood inside the door, shaking snow from his hat.

He was out of uniform. That was the first thing. A plain wool coat the color of wet slate, no braid, no insignia, a civilian's muffler wound twice around his neck. He might have been any clerk gone soft around the middle, except that clerks do not stand the way he stood — weight even on both feet, hands visible, head tilted with the particular patience of a man who has decided that everything in the room will eventually tell him what he wants to know if he simply waits.

"Mademoiselle Underhill." He said it carefully, as if the words were foreign coin he'd been assured was genuine. "Forgive the hour."

"The shop is closed, monsieur." French came easier than it used to; twelve years of playing the foundry's daughter had taught me that a voice is a tool like any other, and this one — soft, apologetic, faintly provincial — fit my hand well these days.

"So it appears." He looked past me at the darkened benches, the racks of matrices, the casting machine under its canvas shroud. "I wonder if I might impose anyway. Ten minutes of your time. Perhaps fifteen." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the corners, practiced enough to look unpracticed. "I find I cannot sleep in this town. The fog, I think. It gets into the lungs."

"Fog doesn't get into the lungs, monsieur. It gets into the bones."

"Then it gets into the bones." He removed his hat entirely and held it against his chest, which somehow made him more alarming rather than less. "May I?"

There are moments when refusing is the only move available and also the worst one. I lit the second lamp instead of answering, which in Grayharbor counted as hospitality.

* * *

He walked the shop the way I imagine a man walks a gallery — slowly, hands clasped behind him, touching nothing, missing nothing. He paused at the specimen books. He paused at the wall of punches, each labeled in Father's small upright hand. He paused longest at the casting bench, where my unfinished bodoni sat exactly as I'd left it, graver resting alongside like a spoon beside a half-eaten meal.

"You cut these yourself?" He didn't look at me when he asked. That was deliberate, I understood later. People confess to profiles.

"The house style, yes."

"It's very fine work." He leaned closer without touching. "Very fine. You can tell a cutter's hand the way you can tell a walker by their gait, did you know that? The serifs on the ascenders — there's a hesitation there. Not uncertainty. Deliberation. Whoever cut this thinks about every stroke." He straightened. "Your father's work lacks that quality. His letters are confident. Almost careless."

"My father has thirty years of confidence, monsieur."

"And you have—" He turned, finally, and looked at me with frank appraisal. "Forgive me. How old are you?"

"Old enough to be confident about some things."

That earned a real laugh, brief and genuine, and I hated how pleased I was to have earned it. This is a thing worth writing down somewhere, in case anyone ever wonders how revolutions fail: it is pleasant to be liked by dangerous men. They know so much. They listen so well. Being listened to after a lifetime of being overlooked is a drug, and Aldous Vane dispensed it in measured doses.

"I'll confess why I've come," he said, "since we're both too polite to pretend otherwise for long." He drew a chair from beside the ledger desk — Father's chair, which no one but Father had sat in since Mother died — and lowered himself into it with the sigh of a man whose feet hurt, which was such a human detail that I distrusted it instantly. "I'm off duty. I want you to understand that. What I say tonight, I don't say as the inventory clerk of the Third Brigade. I say it as a man who has spent twenty years around printing houses and knows them the way a shepherd knows sheep."

"We're not a printing house, monsieur. We cast type."

"You cast type." He nodded slowly. "And who buys your type, mademoiselle? Printers. And what do printers keep?" He held up one finger before I could answer, enjoying his own catechism. "Working copies. Every respectable foundry keeps a working copy of every order. Proof sheets. Specimen pulls. Records of what was cast, in what quantity, for whom — because a founder who cannot prove what he sold is a founder who takes the blame for every libel printed from his metal." He let the silence sit. "Your father produced no working copies for my inventory. No proof books. No order files. In thirty years of trade."

"He keeps things in his head."

"At his age? With the quantities your own ledger claims?" He shook his head with something that was almost fondness. "No. I have met many liars, mademoiselle, but very few of them are founders. Founders are the most honest tradesmen alive. Their whole business is making impressions that can be matched, compared, traced. Metal remembers. That's the beauty of the craft, isn't it?" His eyes moved across the shadowed racks. "Metal remembers everything. One only needs to know how to read it."

I thought of the blocks in the lead drawer, forty paces from where he sat, separated from his fingers by one floorboard, one drawer bottom, and whatever remained of my composure.

"Some houses burn their proofs," I said. "Paper is cheap and fire is free. If there were records worth keeping, monsieur, they'd be in the ledger you already have."

"If there were records worth *hiding*," he corrected gently, "they wouldn't be in any ledger at all. They'd be somewhere cleverer." He smiled again, and this time the warmth took longer to arrive, like a lamp turned down to its last thread of wick. "But you know what interests me most? Not the copies. Copies are paperwork, and paperwork is for clerks." He touched his own chest, mock-humbly. "What interests me is the customers. Your ledger lists forty-one buyers over twelve years. I have interviewed nine. Six are dead, moved, or remarkably unwilling to discuss typography. The remaining thirty-two interest me very much, because a man who buys type in wartime is buying a voice, and I should like to know what all those voices were saying."

"They were saying wedding invitations. Playbills. Shipping notices."

"Some of them." He rose from Father's chair, smoothing his coat. "Some of them, certainly. But here is the arithmetic that keeps me awake worse than your fog, mademoiselle: the print circle that was arrested last week bought nothing from your foundry. Nothing. Their presses were stocked with type cast right here in Grayharbor — I examined the fragments myself before the fire finished them, and I would know this house's hand anywhere, because this house's hand is distinctive. Confident, almost careless." He looked at me. "Except now I learn the careless hand belongs to the daughter, and the daughter cuts differently. Which means either the arrested printers cast their own metal — unlikely — or someone sold to them off the books. Or—"

"Or," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and reasonable, "your fire destroyed the evidence, and now anything you remember about it is conveniently unverifiable. With respect, monsieur, that's not arithmetic. That's wishful counting."

The room went very still. Outside, snow ticked against the window glass. Somewhere down the lane a dog gave three barks and thought better of it.

Then Vane laughed — softly, genuinely, with what appeared to be delight — and I understood I had made an error I couldn't yet measure.

"There she is," he said quietly. "You know, I called on the father first because protocol requires it, and because fathers are predictable. But I told Captain Brasseur at the time: watch the child. The child sorted the type cases faster than her father reads them. The child answered my questions before I finished asking them. And the child—" he collected his hat from the bench where he'd set it, turning it once in his hands, "—the child just told me the truth about my evidence problem in a tone no innocent person would risk using with me."

"The tone of a shopkeeper protecting her trade, monsieur."

"The tone of a cutter protecting her hand." He set the hat on his head, adjusted it against the mirror of the specimen case, and paused there, studying his own reflection and, presumably, mine behind it. "I am not your enemy, mademoiselle. I want you to believe that. I am a man who was hired to count, and I have discovered that no one in this town wants to be counted. That offends me professionally. It does not offend me personally." He buttoned his coat. "But I will tell you what does trouble me personally. Your brother."

The word landed in the room like a dropped punch.

"I have no brother," I said.

"No," Vane agreed mildly. "You don't. Odd, that. A woman in the Fish Market row told my corporal last week that old Underhill had two children once. A boy and a girl. The boy died young, she said. Or went to sea. She wasn't sure. Funny, the things people aren't sure of." He opened the door onto the snowed-in street, letting in a blade of cold air and lamplight. "When you're ready to sell me a keepsake, mademoiselle — a type block, a proof, anything with this house's mark on it, as a souvenir of my time in Grayharbor — you send word. I pay generously for souvenirs. And I look at them very closely."

He tipped his hat. "Good night. Mind the fog. It gets into the bones."

The bell rang behind him, a small bright idiotic sound, and then the street was just a street again.

* * *

I stood in the doorway until his footsteps faded toward the quay, mostly to convince my legs they still worked. Snow was falling in that fat, unhurried way it has before a hard freeze. At the corner, two sentries stamped their posts into white mounds, and beyond them the harbor lay invisible, though I could hear the rigging of the moored brigs complaining in the wind like old women at a funeral.

*Metal remembers.* He'd meant it as a threat. It was also, I reflected grimly, the entire plan — the whole reason I was sewing contraband alphabet into my coat linings and learning the patrol rhythms of the bridge checkpoint. Metal remembers. The question was who got to read it first.

I closed the shop and bolted it, which was theater — a bolt would stop a neighbor, not a brigade — and went back to the bench, because the alternative was sitting in Father's vacated chair and thinking, and thinking could wait until my hands were busy.

He knew things he shouldn't know. That was the fact I kept circling. The Fish Market gossip about a brother was town noise — half of Grayharbor still believed Father kept a mistress and the other half that I *was* the mistress — but the rest was not noise. *The child sorted the cases faster than her father reads them.* He had been watching since the first day. Off-duty, hatless, buying nothing, he had come tonight to show me how long he'd been watching, the way you let a mouse see the cat stretch.

And underneath all his gallery-walking and his shepherd's patience, he had told me his actual conclusion, laid out as neat as a forme locked in its chase: the burned printers' type came from this house, and therefore the hidden records exist, and therefore it is only a matter of time and pressure.

Souvenir, he'd said. Send word. I pay generously.

He wasn't buying a trinket. He was offering me a bargain shaped like a purchase: give me a sample of your hand and I'll pretend that's all I wanted. The moment any block left this shop into his fingers, he'd have what he needed to match the burned fragments — serif by serif, hesitation by hesitation, the way you match a walker by their gait. My own letters would testify against me, in reverse, in negative, in the only language I had ever truly spoken.

Father stirred on the cot behind the partition. I heard him shift, cough once, go still again. Twelve years he'd cut names into spare blocks, patient as erosion, and never once — I was nearly sure of it now — never once had he imagined the collector would come wearing civility instead of irons, asking politely, paying generously, learning our gait.

I doused the lamp over the bench but left the window shutter open a finger's width, which I had taken to doing lately, though I'd have denied it under oath. Across the lane, one lamp still burned in the harbormaster's house. Tamsin kept late hours since the requisition — copying manifests, she said, her father's eyes failing. Her window and mine faced each other across the snow like two pages of an open book, and I wondered, not for the first time and with considerably more urgency than before, what a girl who logged every keel in and out of Grayharbor might know about the schedules of soldiers, the habits of clerks, and the price of a blind eye.

Because Vane would be back. Men like Vane always come back; patience is their whole religion. And when he did, I intended to be holding something he wanted more than type.

I blew out the second lamp. Down the lane, Tamsin's window went dark a heartbeat after mine — and that, I thought, was interesting.
