## 11. Tamsin's Bargain

Tamsin Cole did not come to the foundry. That was the first thing I noticed, and the first thing that frightened me.

For three years she had come the way weather comes — without announcement and with consequences. She'd lean in the doorway of the shop with fog in her hair and an opinion about everything, and my father would pretend not to hear her while she talked to me across his silence, and eventually she would leave behind some small object — a whelk shell, a bent nail, once a whole mackerel she claimed had "followed her home" — as if marking territory. She had never once failed to appear when she said she would. It was one of the things I trusted about the world, right up there with lead pouring at exactly the temperature Father taught me and soldiers being stupid in groups.

But for six days after Vane's second visit, nothing came through the door but cold. No Tamsin. No word of her. The harbor fog lay against the windows like something listening, and I sat at my bench cutting letters I no longer believed were innocent, and I waited to find out what shape my luck would take when it finally arrived.

It arrived on the seventh day, wearing a fishmonger's apron and carrying a basket of herring that could not have fooled a child, much less a soldier.

"Delivery," Tamsin said loudly, to the street in general, and then quietly, to me: "Walk with me. Bring nothing."

She didn't wait for an answer. She never did. I told my father I was going to the chandlery for rosin, which was true in the sense that a lie is sometimes true in its clothes, and I followed her out into Grayharbor's particular brand of winter — a damp that got under wool and stayed there, a gray so complete it seemed less like weather than like the town's opinion of itself.

We walked the long way, past the cooperage, past the salt sheds, down to the sea wall where the tide was doing its patient arithmetic against the stones. Nobody followed us. In Grayharbor nobody follows anybody; watching is done from windows, openly, the way cows watch a gate.

"My father keeps the harbor ledger," Tamsin said, without preamble, because preamble was a luxury she had never permitted herself. "Every keel that comes in, every keel that goes out. Cargo manifests, crew lists, port of origin, port of destination. He's kept it for nineteen years. And now—" she made a small, bitter gesture back toward the town, where somewhere a gray-coated clerk was presumably warming his hands over someone else's fire— "the army reads it before he does. Every departure gets copied. Every copy goes to Vane's office."

I looked at the water. The water looked grayly back.

"You're telling me this because?"

"I'm telling you this because you're going to need a ship." She said it the way you'd say *you're going to need an umbrella*. "Don't look at me like that. I've known you since we were eleven, Cyrus Underhill, and you've been walking around these last two weeks like a woman carrying a piano up a staircase in the dark. You don't talk to the gulls anymore. You used to argue with the gulls. Whatever it is you're carrying, it needs to leave by water, and every boat in this harbor now leaves by permission."

The tide hit the wall and fell apart. I thought about the four type blocks sewn into my old coat, hanging behind the shop door like a scabbed-over secret. I thought about the other blocks still hidden in the type cases, the ones I hadn't yet deciphered, the ones whose reversed impressions I read by candlelight at night like a woman reading letters addressed to somebody braver.

"Suppose," I said carefully, "that a person did need a ship."

"Then that person would need her departure recorded accurately." Tamsin picked a herring scale off her sleeve and flicked it into the sea. "And accurate is such a flexible word. My father signs whatever I write for him. His eyes are going. He trusts my hand more than he trusts his own, which between us is the only sensible decision he has made this decade. If I write that a brig took on salt cod and barrel staves for Lisbon, then a brig took on salt cod and barrel staves for Lisbon, and God help any clerk who says otherwise."

"And what," I asked, though I already felt the price coming the way you feel a tooth crack, "does this cost?"

Tamsin turned and looked at me. She has green eyes, Tamsin, the flat gray-green of harbor water in March, and she has never in her life been able to keep a feeling out of them, which is either her greatest flaw or her entire character depending on whom you ask.

"There's a name," she said. "My brother's name."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Tammas Cole before that morning, which was nearly everything I knew about anything outside the foundry: he was four years older than us, he had gone to sea at fifteen, he had come back twice — once with a tattoo nobody would explain and once with a limp nobody would discuss — and then he had gone away a third time, two years ago, and not come back at all. Tamsin spoke of him the way you speak of a room you grew up in that has since been painted a color you hate. Fondly. With reservations. Without opening the door.

"He writes," she said now, to the water rather than to me. "Not to me. To my father. Short letters, no return address, money folded inside sometimes. My father pretends he doesn't know where the letters come from and I pretend I don't know they exist, and that is how the Cole family loves each other: by mutual arrangement of blindness."

"Tamsin—"

"Whatever your list is," she said, and now she did look at me, and her voice went very level, the way it does right before she does something reckless, "whatever names are cut into whatever it is you're carrying around this town like a reliquary — and yes, Cyrus, I know there's something, I've watched you lie to Vane's face twice and you're terrible at it, you blink like a startled owl — if my brother's name is on it, I want it gone. Not crossed out. Gone. Recut. Melted down. Made into a comma or an ampersand or something useful."

The wind came off the water and tried to get between us.

"I don't decide what's on it," I said. "I only read it."

"Then read it," she said. "And tell me honestly whether I should be terrified."

* * *

That night, after my father went to bed, I sat in the back room with the lamp shuttered down to a coin of light and I sorted type.

I should explain how the reading works, because it is the strangest thing I know and I have never been able to say it aloud to anyone who wasn't made of metal. When you cut a letter into steel and strike it into copper and cast type from it, everything reverses. Left becomes right. A name cut forward reads backward in the metal. My father, over twelve years, had cut names into spare blocks — cut them mirrored, so that when you pressed them into paper, or held them to a candle at the correct angle, or simply learned to read lead the way other people read scripture, they resolved. Forty-some names by my count so far, each with figures beside them: dates, sums, ports. Not a customer list. A debt list. Though whose debts, and owed to whom, I had not yet worked out — I only knew that my father had spent twelve years cutting the history of Grayharbor's shadow trade into metal small enough to hide in a drawer of punctuation marks.

I sorted for two hours. I found a *Brennan*, a *Quill & Sons*, a name I didn't recognize with the figure 300 beside it and the letters *LIS* — Lisbon, probably, or Leith.

And then I found *COLE*.

Tammas Cole. Cut in my father's neat, economical hand, the letters tight the way he cuts everything, wasting no metal. Beside the name: a date from eighteen months ago. A sum — 60. And a word I had seen beside three other names and had been deliberately not translating for myself, because some words, once read, cannot be unread:

*SPAKE* — spoken. Spoke to. Talked.

An informant's fee.

I sat there for a long time with the block in my palm. It weighed almost nothing. Lead weighs almost nothing; that's the whole trick of it, that civilizations rise and fall and families drown on a metal you could carry a hundred names' worth of in one coat pocket, and each name weighs less than a sparrow.

Sixty pounds. Tammas Cole had been paid sixty pounds for talking, and his sister was downstairs sleeping under a roof warmed by that money, and she had just offered to forge army documents for me — documents that could see her transported, or worse — in exchange for the removal of a name that deserved, by any honest accounting, to stay exactly where it was.

* * *

I want to be honest about the next part, because this account is for myself as much as anyone, and there's no point lying to the only witness.

I considered not telling her.

I sat up half the night constructing the case for silence, and it was a good case, a well-made case, built by a girl who has spent her whole life learning that information is the only currency that doesn't devalue. She'll forge the manifests regardless, I told myself. She's doing it for you, not for the truth. Knowing will only hurt her. The name can be recut; the harm is in the knowing. Father's voice, actually — it was Father's voice making the argument, Father who taught me that names are dangerous currency, Father who had hidden forty lives in a drawer full of semicolons rather than say anything aloud to anyone.

But that was precisely the thing, wasn't it. I had spent two weeks reading those blocks and learning what silence costs when it compounds over twelve years like interest. Every name my father never spoke had become a weight I was now carrying past checkpoints sewn into my coat. Silence doesn't shrink. It waits, and it grows, and eventually somebody younger than you has to haul it.

And there was this, too, which I admit more reluctantly: she had offered me a partnership. Falsified manifests are complicity — real complicity, gallows-adjacent complicity — and I was going to accept it. You do not build a thing like that on a floor you know is rotten. Not if you plan to stand on it in a storm.

So the next morning I walked to the harbor master's house, and I stood in the cold outside it for the better part of an hour rehearsing, and the gulls watched me with the frank contempt gulls reserve for hesitation, and finally Tamsin came out with a bucket and said, "You look like a woman about to confess to something, and I'm not absolution, so out with it."

We walked to the sea wall again. Our spot now, apparently. Every conspiracy needs a bench.

"Your brother's name is in the ledger," I said.

I watched it land. I watched her face do three things in sequence — refusal, arithmetic, grief — and I watched her hold all three at once, which most people can't do; most people collapse into one.

"Spake?" she said. Just that. She knew the structure somehow, or guessed it — knew there'd be a word beside the name, knew which word it would be.

"Sixty pounds. Eighteen months ago. There's a date and what might be a ship reference."

The bucket hung from her hand. Down the wall the tide kept working, indifferent, professional.

"He sent money home that winter," she said finally. "Twelve pounds. My mother's lungs were bad and we needed charcoal and a doctor from Kingsbridge, and Tammas sent twelve pounds, and my father cried over it, actual tears, and I thought — good. He's all right. He's finding his way." She set the bucket down very carefully on the stones, as if it might break, though it was wooden. "He sold us, and he sent us the proceeds, and we wept with gratitude over them."

"Tamsin—"

"Don't." She held up one hand. She looked out at the water for a long moment, and I did the hardest thing I have done in this whole affair, harder than Vane, harder than checkpoints: I said nothing and let her.

"All right," she said at last. "Here's what I know, and here's what I choose. My brother is my brother. He's also, apparently, a man who sells names for sixty pounds, and one of those names might have been mine someday, or yours, or some printer's with children. Those are both true and I am not going to pick one tonight and pretend the other dissolved." She picked up the bucket. Her knuckles were white around the handle. "You will not erase his name."

I blinked. "You don't want—"

"I wanted him clean. He isn't clean. Recutting the block changes the metal, not the man." Her jaw was set the way it sets before she swims in November, which she does, annually, and calls everyone else a coward for not joining. "The bargain stands. Manifests for passage, whatever you're shipping. But you'll do one thing for me in addition, and it's worse than erasure, so brace yourself."

"Name it."

"If his name appears anywhere else — in anything else, in anything the army recovers, in anything that ever gets printed — you let it appear. You don't warn him. You don't send word through your smuggler captain. You let my brother own what my brother did." She turned to me fully and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "That's my price. Not mercy. Consequences, delivered at market rate."

I put out my hand. We shook on it, there on the sea wall, two girls and a bucket, sealing the sort of compact that in novels gets signed in blood but in life gets sealed with chilblains and herring smell.

"The Juniper," I said. "Captain Osei. New Year's Eve, if the weather allows. I'll need her manifest to show cargo for a buyer in Amsterdam that doesn't exist, and I'll need it flawless."

"Nothing's flawless," Tamsin said. "But I've been copying my father's hand since I was nine, including his flourishes, including his mistake on the number seven — he always crosses it too low. Vane will be looking for forgery. He won't be looking for filial imitation." She almost smiled. It was a terrible almost-smile, the kind that has grief standing directly behind it. "One more thing, Cyrus. Your Captain Osei. Whatever you're paying him, pay it fast and sail the moment you can. Because yesterday morning my father logged a navy cutter into the outer roads, and the men who came off it reported to nobody in the harbor office — they went straight to the gray coats. Something's moving. And it isn't fish."

She picked up her bucket and walked back toward her father's house, shoulders square, a woman carrying a piano down the stairs in broad daylight and daring anyone to comment.

I stood on the sea wall a while longer, doing sums. A cutter in the roads. Soldiers asking after printers. And Tamsin's last words turning over in my head alongside the ones I'd read in lead the night before, until the two threads braided themselves together into a single cold rope and pulled tight.

Because there had been another entry, weeks old, among my father's blocks. Two names beside a print shop on Candle Street. And this morning, walking in, I had passed Candle Street, and seen the soldiers' cart parked outside it, and smelled something on the air that I had told myself was a chimney.

It was not a chimney.
