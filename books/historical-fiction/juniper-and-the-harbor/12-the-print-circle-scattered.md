## 12. The Print Circle Scattered

The news came to us the way news came to Grayharbor in that winter: sideways, in fragments, carried by people who spoke quickly and looked over their shoulders while doing it.

I was at the counter wrapping a consignment of ten-point brevier for a stationer up the coast when Tamsin came through the door without knocking, which she never did. The bell above the frame rang its small bright note and kept ringing in my ears after the door had shut behind her, because of the look on her face.

"They took the Merricks," she said. "Last night. Both of them."

The string I was tying went slack in my hands. "Took them where?"

"Does it matter?" She pulled off her gloves finger by finger, and I noticed her hands were shaking, and that she was watching them shake as if they belonged to someone else. "Soldiers came at dark. No warrant shown, or if there was one, no one read it aloud. They put Josiah and Prue in a cart and drove off toward the fort, and then this morning—" Her voice snagged on something. "This morning they burned the shop."

"Burned it?"

"Set it alight in the street. In daylight, Cyrus. In front of everyone."

I set down the parcel. My hands had gone very steady, which is a thing about me you will learn if you learn nothing else: when something terrible happens, my hands become a printer's hands again — precise, unhurried, fit for nothing but work. It is either composure or cowardice. I have never managed to determine which.

"I have to see it," I said.

"No," said Tamsin, with the flat certainty of someone who has already seen it. "You don't."

But of course I did.

* * *

Smoke still stood over Cooper's Lane when I got there, thinning into the gray sky as though ashamed of itself. The print shop of Josiah and Prudence Merrick — Quill & Anchor, the sign had read, though the sign was now a blackened plank leaning against nothing — was half gone. The front wall remained, holding up its own absence, windows blown out or melted, the interior beyond it a field of wet ash and charred paper gone soft as snowdrift.

They had burned the type too. That was what stopped me, standing in the lane with my collar up and my breath coming out in little flags. Anyone might burn a shop; fire is fire, indiscriminate and dumb. But type does not burn. Lead slumps, brass fittings blacken, but the letters themselves survive flame better than most things in this world. Someone had raked the cases out into the street first. I could see them through the smoke-stink — the long wooden trays, split open, their compartments of metal letters scattered across the cobbles like spilled teeth, some of them already sagged out of true from the heat. A soldier stood near the pile with a pike, not guarding it exactly, just standing near it, the way a man stands near something he has been told matters without being told why.

And around him, the neighbors.

Grayharbor is not a large town. On a normal morning, Cooper's Lane would have been thick with people by nine o'clock — fishwives arguing prices, boys hauling barrels, old Mr. Petchy taking his constitutional at the exact pace of a man who has nowhere to be and wants everyone to know it. This morning there were people, yes. There were perhaps thirty of them, standing at intervals along both sides of the street like fence posts, each one carefully alone, each one looking anywhere but at the ruin.

A woman across the way was scrubbing her doorstep. There was nothing on her doorstep. She scrubbed it anyway, working the brush back and forth with the diligence of the damned, her eyes fixed on the stone as if salvation lived between the cobbles.

Two men stood outside the chandlery speaking about cordage. I knew one of them — Daniel Hobb, who had bought type from my father for years, whose pamphlets we all pretended were about agriculture. He was discussing rope. His voice was too loud and his knots of conversation kept collapsing, and every few sentences his gaze skittered toward the smoking shell of the Merricks' shop and then flinched away again, quick as a tongue finding a broken tooth.

Nobody spoke to anybody else. Nobody crossed the street. Nobody picked up so much as one of those scattered letters off the cold stones and pocketed it, though God knows I wanted to — though I could see in the set of other shoulders that others wanted to. A single piece of Merrick type in your possession would be enough. Everyone understood this without it being said. That is what an occupation teaches first: not obedience, but arithmetic. What a thing costs versus what it weighs.

I stood among them and did exactly what they did. I looked away.

Let me be honest about this, since honesty is supposed to be the one virtue left to me. I want to tell you that I walked to the pile of ruined type and gathered an armful of it while the soldiers watched, that I said something fine and memorable, that the crowd found its spine because I found mine first. That is how such scenes go in novels, and I have written a few such scenes myself, sitting warm at my lamp with the whole cowardly world arranged conveniently before me.

Here is what actually happened. I counted the exits. I noted the positions of four soldiers and calculated whether any of them could describe my face to Aldous Vane, whose name was already becoming a kind of weather in the town — a pressure you felt before you saw him. I established that the woman scrubbing her step could see me clearly if she chose to look up, and I moved out of her line of sight, and then I stood in the lee of the cooper's yard and I watched, and I memorized, and I did nothing.

Because here is the other thing I understood, standing there in the smoke-stink while my town practiced its deafness: the silence my father had raised me in was not a family eccentricity after all. It was not even a choice. It was simply the native language of every person on that street, and the army had done nothing but make us all fluent overnight. Twelve years I had thought myself singular, marked, set apart by my hidden name and my father's careful lessons — *names are currency, Cyrus, spend them slowly* — and now I looked down a lane of thirty people spending nothing, hoarding everything, and recognized every single one of them as kin.

Josiah Merrick had set type in that shop for twenty years. He had printed broadsides under three different false imprints, argued politics till two in the morning at the Anchor, taught half the apprentices in the town their ABCs, including, once, briefly, a girl called something else. And his neighbors stood in the cold discussing cordage.

Prue Merrick used to slip me barley sugar when I was seven and still allowed in public. She called me clever in front of my father, which nobody else ever bothered to do.

I looked away. We all looked away. It was the only concert the town had left.

There was one exception, and it shames me less than the rest because at least it was honest. An old woman — Mother Kessing, who everyone said was simple, though I suspected she was merely past pretending — stood directly in front of the ruin with her hands folded and did not look away once. She just watched the smoke, and occasionally she opened her mouth as though singing, though no sound came out. A soldier told her to move along twice. She didn't. Eventually he gave up, having apparently decided that madness was not on the requisition list.

She was the bravest person on Cooper's Lane that morning, and she likely didn't know the Merricks' names. Bravery and knowledge turn out to be barely acquainted. That was another lesson the street taught me before I left it, and I have paid full price for every one.

I turned for home. As I passed the chandlery, Daniel Hobb's voice dropped mid-sentence about cordage, and he said, very quietly, without looking at me, without pausing in the knot he was failing to tie:

"Third house this month. Up the coast, they say. Falmouth way."

Then he resumed, loudly, "—so twelve fathom at least, or it'll chafe through by spring," and I walked on, carrying the news the way we carried everything now: hidden, weightless, and burning.

* * *

My father heard me come in and did not call out, which meant he knew where I had been, which meant the news had reached him faster than I had, which meant the network of silences was already functioning better than any newspaper in the county.

He was at the casting bench, doing nothing. This requires explanation: my father was incapable of doing nothing at the bench. If his hands were on the lead pot and no work lay before him, he invented work — trimming knuckles that needed no trimming, sorting sorts already sorted. To find him sitting at the bench with clean, idle hands was to find a man who had heard something that had stopped the machinery.

"Tell me," he said.

So I told him. The arrests in the night, the fire in the morning, the raked-out type, the scrubbed doorstep, Hobb's twelve fathom. I told it plainly, the way he'd taught me to report technical faults — no decoration, no emphasis, facts in order. It was only when I described Mother Kessing mouthing her silent song in front of the ashes that my voice betrayed me, a hairline crack right down the middle of a word, and I stopped talking altogether and became extremely interested in the state of the composing stone.

My father let the quiet sit for a while. He was good at quiet the way some men are good at drink.

"You knew them," he said finally. Not a question.

"My whole life."

"Yes." He turned a sort over in his fingers — a lowercase e, worn nearly smooth, older than me. "Josiah bought his first fount from this shop before you were born. Before the name changed. He paid in installments over two years and never missed one." He set the e down with exaggerated care, aligning its foot with the edge of the stone. "He printed the first thing I ever wrote that was worth the paper."

I stared at him. In twelve years — thirteen now — my father had spoken of his past the way he spoke of everything: in inventory. Quantities, weights, prices. I had not known he wrote anything. I had not known there was a *before* with texture to it.

"The army will come here," I said, because someone had to say it and it was evidently going to be me. "Merrick bought from us. Everyone in that circle bought from us. When Vane goes through what's left of Quill & Anchor, he'll find invoices. He'll find our name."

"He'll find invoices," my father agreed. "Invoices are legal documents. We sold printing equipment. That is a lawful trade."

"And the ledger?"

The word dropped between us like a coin down a well. I watched him hear it — the master ledger, surrendered ten days ago into the hands of the crown, containing twelve years of customers' names in my father's cramped upright hand. Every printer. Every pamphleteer. Every smuggler who'd ordered a commemorative plate and a few dissidents who'd ordered rather more than that. I had watched them carry it out the door in a canvas satchel and I had thought, at the time, *it is only business records, only names and dates and sums.* I had learned since then what names weigh when the wrong hands hold them.

"The ledger lists purchases," my father said. "Nothing more. Purchases can be innocent."

"Can they?"

He looked at me then, really looked, and for a moment I thought he was going to say something enormous — that the stillness in his face was the stillness of a man standing at a door he has kept locked for years, with his hand actually on the latch. Something moved behind his eyes, some calculation or confession assembling itself word by word, lead by lead.

What he said was: "Go and check the third case on the wall. Count the eighteen-point capitals. If any are short, we should know before the inventory men count them for us."

It was dismissal dressed as instruction, the uniform he wore for both. I went, because I was fifteen years of obedience deep and one morning of smoke, and because part of me understood — even then, even angry — that whatever door he'd stood at was not yet open, and forcing it might break it off its hinges entirely.

But as I crossed the shop floor, my father spoke once more, so quietly I almost lost it under the sound of the wind at the shutters.

"Cyrus." Not girl. Not boy. The name itself, which he rarely spent. "If anyone asks what you think of the Merricks — and someone will ask, that is what men like Vane do, they ask not because they want answers but because they want to watch you build one — you say they were customers. Good payers. You liked Mrs. Merrick's manner. Nothing else. Grief is evidence now. Do you understand? Even grief. Especially grief."

"I understand," I said.

And I did understand, which was the worst of it. I understood that the lesson of Cooper's Lane was the same lesson of my whole childhood, merely scaled up: that survival in Grayharbor that winter meant amputating pieces of yourself in public and walking on as though the limb had never been yours. The neighbors had amputated their friendship with the Merricks on the cobblestones that morning, briskly, without anesthetic, and the wound would close over clean because that is what wounds do when no one is permitted to mourn them.

At the type case, I counted capitals that didn't need counting and thought about the scattered letters in the lane. Somewhere under the ash and trample, a lowercase e from Josiah Merrick's fount lay in the gutter, worn smooth, orphaned. I resolved — quietly, in the place where I kept everything quietly — that before winter ended, I would go back and find it.

That was the day I stopped believing the silence would protect us.

I hadn't yet started believing anything would. But I had begun, in the dim workshop arithmetic of my heart, to calculate what it would cost to fight — and the figure frightened me less than the scrubbed doorstep, less than the loud talk of cordage, less than my father's face when I said the word *ledger.*

Three nights later, the knock came at a civil hour, which somehow made it worse. Aldous Vane stood in our doorway with snow on his shoulders and courtesy on his face, and asked, in the gentlest voice I had yet heard him use, whether we might discuss a few of our late customers — starting, he said, with poor Mr. Merrick, whom he was given to understand had been a friend of the house.

My father said, "Come in, sir," and I went to stand by the counter, and began, for the second time in my life, to lie with my whole body.
