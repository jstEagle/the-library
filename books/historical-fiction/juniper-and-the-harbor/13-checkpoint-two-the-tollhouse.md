## 13. Checkpoint Two: The Tollhouse

The second cache was heavier than the first, and heavier still for what it held: eleven blocks bearing the names of the harbor's most respectable families — the kind of men who paid their taxes twice and attended church in good wool. Respectability, I had learned by then, was precisely what the army collected. Nobody requisitions a ledger full of smugglers when there are aldermen to be had.

I could not sew eleven blocks into a coat and walk like a woman wearing nothing. The bridge trick worked once; the second time it would be a habit, and habits get searched. So I went to Tam Ruddle.

Tam was a cooper on Anchor Lane with shoulders like a doorframe and the conversational range of one. He owed my father money for type — everyone owed my father money for something — and he had a daughter of his own who did deliveries, a girl named Prue who was, by happy accident, roughly my size and permanently afflicted with an expression of profound boredom. That last part required no acting whatsoever.

For two shillings and a promise to square his account, Tam lent me a barrel — small, oak, freshly hooped, smelling of sawdust and vinegar — and Prue's old cloak, and the story that went with them: I was delivering sample staves to the garrison commissary at the tollhouse road, as the Ruddle yard did every Thursday, army contracts being the only growth industry left in Grayharbor.

Inside the barrel, wedged in a false bottom Tam built without asking why, because coopers understand compartments the way priests understand sin: eleven blocks of lead, wrapped in oilcloth, each one worth a family's neck. On top, a layer of genuine staves. A girl can carry forty pounds if she believes in what she's carrying. Or if she believes in what happens if she doesn't.

* * *

The tollhouse road ran east out of town through the marsh, a causeway barely wider than a cart, with black water on both sides pretending to be fields. In December the fog sat on it like a landlord. You could walk a quarter mile and see nobody, and then round a bend and find a whole garrison materialized out of the gray, which is more or less how armies prefer to appear.

The tollhouse itself was a squat stone building straddling the road, built in some more innocent decade to collect pennies from farmers and now collecting oaths, papers, and occasionally dignity. A barrier of pine plank crossed the road on a pivot. Beside it burned a brazier tended by three soldiers in gray coats, and inside the tollhouse window I could see a fourth, a sergeant, warming his hands at the stove and reading something with the slow lip-moving concentration of a man fighting literacy to a draw.

I had been coached by Tamsin, who knew soldiers the way her father knew tides. *They're bored,* she'd said. *Boredom is your friend and your enemy. Bore them and they wave you through. Let them be interested and you're done.* Her advice for avoiding interest: be slightly annoying. Not rude — rude gets you remembered. Annoying. Chattering. The kind of girl a soldier wants elsewhere.

I am not, by nature, a chatterer. Twelve years in a foundry will cure you of unnecessary words the way winter cures you of swimming. But I had discovered in myself, over these weeks, a talent I did not know I possessed and did not entirely like: the talent for becoming someone. It turned out I had spent my whole life rehearsing for it. If you can be Cyrus Underhill when you are Juniper, you can be Prue Ruddle for the price of a barrel.

I practiced on the walk. *Morning, sirs. Sample staves for the commissary. Tam Ruddle's girl, usual Thursday, don't know why he sent me in this fog, he knows I hate it, but there's wages in it so here I am.* I gave Prue a limp. Then I took the limp away, because limps invite questions, and questions were the enemy. Everything about my performance was calibrated to answer questions before they were asked, which is the whole secret of lying well: never leave a silence where curiosity can put down roots.

The fog thinned as I came up the causeway, and the tollhouse assembled itself out of the murk: brazier, barrier, three soldiers, smoke, the smell of wet wool and woodsmoke and, beneath it, the marsh's own perfume of salt and rot. One of the soldiers was young and kept stamping his feet. One was older and had the immovable quality of furniture. The third stood apart, arms folded, watching the road before I ever reached him, and my stomach performed a small cold maneuver.

He was perhaps thirty, weathered, with a scar through one eyebrow that made him look permanently skeptical. He didn't stamp his feet or read or tend fires. He watched things. Of the three, he was the one Tamsin hadn't warned me about, because Tamsin couldn't have known: every checkpoint has one soldier who chose the work because he was good at it, and everything depends on whether he is bored today.

"Staves," I said, before anyone spoke. "Ruddle's yard. For the commissary." I hefted the barrel against my hip. "Don't suppose one of you gentlemen wants to carry it the last bit, do you? Tam'll dock me a day's wages if I drop it."

Furniture looked at the young one. The young one looked at Furniture. The skeptic looked at me.

"Papers."

I produced them. Tam had gotten genuine chits from the commissary clerk — real paper, real stamps, real boredom behind their issuance — which is vastly better than forged ones, the best forgery being the one somebody official already made for you. The skeptic read them longer than they deserved.

"Prudence," he said. "Ruddle."

"That's me." *God help me.*

"You deliver Thursdays."

"Every Thursday since Michaelmas. You'd think the army would run out of things to put in barrels." A laugh from the young one. The skeptic didn't laugh, but he handed back the papers, which was better than laughing.

"Lift the lid."

There is a moment — I have come to know it intimately, the way a sailor knows the weight before a wave strikes — when the plan either holds or doesn't, and nothing you do in the moment matters, because everything was decided in the preparation. Tam's false bottom was good. Tam's staves were genuine. The vinegar smell was honest work-smell. And yet my heart went off like a dropped hammer, and I heard my own voice keep chattering — *mind the hoops, they're fresh, Tam'll fuss* — while hands I mostly controlled lifted the lid.

The skeptic looked into the barrel. He looked at the staves. And then he said, "Hands out."

I want to record, honestly, that I did not understand at first. Hands? The papers were in order. The barrel was clean. What business had he with my—

But he was looking at them. At my hands, extended palm-up over the open barrel in the light of the brazier, and I understood one full breath too late what he saw, what any man with eyes would see, what I had spent twelve years not seeing because they were simply my hands:

Cuts. Dozens of them, old and new, fine white seams across the fingers and the heels of the palms, the particular callus at the thumb joint, the burn scar on the right forefinger where hot metal once kissed me — the hands of a type cutter. No cooper's daughter had hands like this. Cooper's daughters had rope burns and splinter scars and knuckles thickened from hoop-driving. Mine were a cutter's hands, precise and scarred by steel and lead, and they hung in the firelight confessing everything.

"Those aren't barrel hands," the skeptic said.

The world narrowed to the width of a barrier plank. Behind me, gray road and fog and a mile of marsh with nowhere to run. Ahead, the tollhouse, the sergeant inside, and somewhere beyond, the commissary, where a man might reasonably ask Prudence Ruddle why Tam had sent a stranger with a foundry worker's fingers.

"They're my mother's tools," I said, and heard myself say it, that other self stepping forward smoothly while the real one stood very still. "She cut horn. Combs, spoons, buttons. She taught me before she died and the trade died with her, near enough. I keep my hand in. Cut buttons for the neighborhood." I turned my palms as if showing them off. "You get the scars young in horn-work. It's the boiling."

"Horn," the skeptic said.

"Horn," I agreed. "Smells dreadful. Worse than this fog, and that's saying something."

The young soldier laughed again. Furniture examined his boots. And the skeptic — this is the part I turn over even now — the skeptic looked at my hands another long moment, and something moved behind his eyes that was not suspicion. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who has his own trade, whatever it is, and respects another's marks upon a body. Or the look of a man filing something away.

"Go on, then," he said finally. "Mind the barrel."

I did not run. Running is for the guilty, and I had decided, in those three seconds, to be a bored girl with a heavy load and a dead mother's trade. I hitched the barrel up, nodded, muttered something about the commissary hours, and walked through onto the causeway with my heart going like a trip-hammer and my legs performing their duties under protest.

I did not look back. Looking back is for amateurs.

It wasn't until the fog closed around me a quarter mile on that I stopped, set the barrel down on the wet planks, and discovered my hands were shaking so hard the staves rattled. I stood in the marsh fog and shook, and laughed, a horrible little sound, and thought: *he saw. He knew. And he let me go.*

Which meant I owed my life to a soldier's whim, or his patience, or his arithmetic — and I could not begin to guess which.

* * *

The commissary at the far end was a barn full of barrels and one harried corporal who signed Tam's chit without looking at me, because to him I was Thursday. I left the barrel with the sample staves, all aboveboard, all exactly what the papers said, and started back along the causeway lighter by eleven blocks' worth of terror.

No. That's wrong, and I should tell it true. I carried the blocks *to* the commissary, yes — but the cache didn't stay there. That was the day's second deception, the one layered inside the first: the barrel went to the commissary, was logged, was stacked among fifty identical barrels, and by evening three of those barrels would be loaded on a dray belonging to a fish merchant who supplied the garrison and whose route home passed the harbor. Tamsin had arranged it. Tamsin arranged everything now, with her father's manifests and her calm dock-side arithmetic, and neither of us talked about what we were building between us, because talking about it might break it.

So the blocks traveled on, hidden in plain sight inside the army's own supply chain, which struck me as a joke my father would have appreciated and never told.

I walked back slower than I'd come. The fog was lifting in rags, and the marsh showed itself in patches — black mud, pale grass, a heron standing in the shallows with the patience of a saint and the intentions of a sinner. My hands had steadied. My mind had not.

Because here is what I kept returning to, walking that causeway with the water flat as pewter on either side: *horn-carver's daughter.* I had invented a mother, a trade, a death, an apprenticeship, in the space of a breath, and delivered it flawlessly to a man trained to catch exactly such inventions. Where had she come from, this mother? I had a real one, dead these fourteen years, and I had not used her. I had reached past her for a fiction, as if the truth of my own family were too dangerous to spend — or as if spending the truth had become unthinkable, the way you stop counting your own blood as currency.

My father says — used to say — that names are dangerous coin. He taught me that lesson so thoroughly that now, when the bill came due, I found I would rather invent a dead mother than speak the living one's name aloud to a stranger in a gray coat. I didn't know whether that was wisdom or damage. Probably both. Probably everything worth having in this century was both.

And underneath that thought, colder: the skeptic had known. *Barrel hands*, he said, and he was right, and he let me pass anyway. A man like that does not let things pass for free. Either he had decided a girl with horn-carver's fictions was harmless — possible, but he didn't strike me as a man who believed convenient things — or he wanted something, later, and was investing. Soldiers at checkpoints meet ten thousand people and forget them all. He would not forget mine. Some corner of the future had just acquired a shape I couldn't see yet, and it had scarred eyebrows and patient eyes.

I got home after dark. The foundry windows glowed the way they always did, my father's lamp behind the frosted panes making the shop look, from the street, like any honest business ending an honest day. I came in the alley door. He was at the bench, sorting type with those same scarred hands — my hands, his hands, the family inheritance written in cuts and callus — and he looked up when I entered, and he read my face the way he reads a galley proof, quickly and completely.

"It went badly," he said.

"It went perfectly," I said. "That's worse."

I told him all of it. The barrel, the chit, the chatter, the lie about the horn-carving mother. I told it lightly, the way you tell a story that has already ended safely, and I watched his face while I did, and I saw something cross it when I mentioned the hands — not fear exactly. Shame. Old shame, arriving punctually, like a debt collector who knows the house.

He set down the type he was holding, block by block, very carefully, as if the small orderliness of it could postpone what needed saying.

"Cyrus," he said. "Sit down. There's something I should have told you before you ever carried the first block out of this shop. About why I cut that ledger at all."

The lamp guttered. Outside, snow had begun again, soft against the glass, and my father — who had faced requisition, arrest, and ruin with the same mild expression he used for pricing — looked suddenly like a man standing at the edge of deep water.
