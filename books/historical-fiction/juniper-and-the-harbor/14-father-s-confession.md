## 14. Father's Confession

My father had exactly two modes of speech: the instruction and the silence. The instruction was short, precise, and concerned itself with metal. *Mind the bevel. Cooler hands. Again.* The silence was everything else — every grief, every fear, every year of my mother's absence, all of it packed away behind his sternum like lead in a case too heavy to lift.

So when he set down his supper spoon halfway through the gray gruel that passed for our evenings now, folded his hands, and said, "Cyrus, we should talk about the blocks," I knew before he said another word that something in him had finally broken loose.

It was the ninth of December, or thereabouts. The army had been in Grayharbor long enough that we'd stopped flinching at boots on the stair outside the foundry and started flinching again only when they stopped. Two soldiers were billeted in the front room where customers once waited among specimen books. They snored in rotation, which I had begun to think of as a kind of clockwork. My father and I shared the back room: one bed apiece, one lamp between us, and between us also a silence twelve years wide.

"I've cut suppers," I said, meaning to deflect. A joke, even a bad one, was my usual armor. Wit is what you develop when you can't say anything true.

He didn't smile. He never smiled at the work jokes; he considered them a form of blasphemy against the craft, which was the closest thing to religion he kept. "The blocks you found. Don't pretend you didn't find them. You've been carrying them wrong ever since."

"Carrying them wrong," I repeated.

"The way you move. Your father taught you to walk, girl. I'd know that guilty shuffle anywhere — I invented it." He looked at his hands. They are cutter's hands, like mine, scarred across the pads with a lifetime of slips, and for once they weren't steady. "Sit down."

I sat. The lamp guttered and steadied. Somewhere out front, Emmerich turned over in his sleep and muttered something in a language of pure exhaustion.

"You found them three weeks ago," he said. "You've been moving them since. Four blocks, then six. Sewn into that coat your mother would have hated." He raised his eyes to mine. "I want to know how much you've guessed, and then I'm going to tell you how much you've got wrong."

I have lied to an army clerk. I have forged a cooper's delivery note with a fluency that frightened me. But sitting across from my father under one lamp, I found I could produce nothing but the truth, and only part of it. "I know they're a ledger. Names, reversed. Customers, debts, dates. I thought — I assumed you were keeping accounts for the print circle. That you were one of them. That all these years you'd been funding sedition from the back bench and hiding it in plain type."

"And there it is," he said softly. "That's what you wanted me to be."

"That's what would have made sense."

"No." He said it the way he said everything about metal: flat, final, true. "No, Cyrus. It isn't rebellion. It was never rebellion. God forgive me, it's worse than that. It's insurance."

* * *

He talked for a long time, and I mostly let him, because I understood that this — the talking itself — was costing him more than any confession at the gate ever could.

"When your mother died," he began, "I did the arithmetic every widower does. Who will raise the child. How will the shop survive. What happens when I die and someone comes through that door wanting to know what Underhill & Sons really sold, all those years, to all those men." He rubbed the scar on his left palm, an old habit I'd watched ten thousand times without once wondering what it meant. "This trade puts you in rooms with dangerous people, Cyrus. Printers who print the wrong things. Smugglers moving pamphlets inside barrels of herring. Gentlemen in the capital who buy Greek type for reasons that have nothing to do with Greek. You sell to them all, because selling to everyone is how you stay alive. But every sale is a thread, and every thread runs back to your door."

"So you recorded them."

"I recorded *everyone*. Every name, every order, every odd request. Not to give anyone to the authorities — I want that understood, whatever else you take from tonight. I never intended to hand a single soul over." His voice cracked on *intended*, and he pressed on before it could finish breaking. "I cut those blocks so that if ruin ever came to this shop — if I were arrested, if I were accused, if some magistrate decided Underhill was a traitor and needed hanging — I would have something to trade. Forty names, Cyrus. Merchants, captains, printers, two aldermen, a colonel's brother. Men whose liberty depends on no one ever reading that ledger." He looked up. "You don't hang a man who holds forty hostages."

The lamp flickered. Outside, snow ticked against the shutters like a slow telegraph.

"Insurance," I said. "You cut a dozen years of other men's secrets into metal so you could ransom yourself."

"Yes."

"And you never told me."

"How could I?" And here his voice did break, entirely, and he let it, which frightened me more than anything else he'd said. "Telling you meant making you guilty of knowing. This town survives on not-knowing, girl. You learned that before you learned your letters — I made sure of it. I gave you a boy's name and a man's trade and taught you to walk past trouble like it owed you money. I thought if I never spoke the ledger aloud, if it existed only as impressions in spare type buried in the case, then it could never touch you." He laughed, a terrible dry sound. "And then the army came, and requisitioned my shop, and demanded my ledgers, and I sat there like an honest fool while they inventoried my life's work — and my daughter went digging in the type cases at night."

"You couldn't stop me."

"I could have burned them," he said. "Any night these six weeks. One trip to the furnace while you slept. Do you know why I didn't?"

I didn't answer. I suspected I knew, and I didn't want to hear it.

"Because they're still worth something," he said. "Because a coward's first instinct, when the house is on fire, is to save the gold." He put his scarred hand flat on the table, as if taking an oath, or bracing against a wind only he felt. "That's the confession, Cyrus, if you want it in full. Not that I kept a ledger. That when you started carrying those blocks toward the harbor, risking the wall and the rope, I *let* you. I told myself you were saving the families in it. I told myself it was noble. But some low cellar-door part of me was thinking: the girl gets the evidence out, and if the worst comes, her father still has leverage. I used you. I watched you sew metal into your coat and I thought about my own neck."

The room was very quiet. Emmerich's snoring had stopped. I remember hoping he was merely asleep on his other side.

"Do you want to know what I thought," I said, "when I first decoded those blocks? I thought: so he trusted me after all. All those years of silence, and here was this whole hidden country inside the shop, and he'd built it where only I would find it. I thought it was an inheritance. I thought—" I stopped, because my eyes were doing something embarrassing, and Villalobos help me, I was not going to cry at my own table. "I thought I'd finally been let in."

My father looked at me for a long moment. Then he did something he had not done since I was small enough to sit on the casting bench: he reached across the table and took my hand, cutter's hand to cutter's hand, scar against scar.

"You were always let in," he said. "That was the failure. I loved you by keeping you outside of everything — the grief, the danger, the truth. I thought silence was shelter. It was just silence. And now you're carrying my sins to the harbor in your mother's coat, and I can't even claim I didn't know." He squeezed my fingers once, hard, and let go. "I never meant you to carry it. Understand that if you understand nothing else. Whatever happens — whatever Vane does, whatever they find — I never meant you to carry it."

"Well," I said, when I could trust my voice. "That makes two of us who planned my life badly. At least I'm consistent."

And because he was my father, and because grief in our family has always worn the mask of instruction, he actually smiled — briefly, crookedly, like type set a half-point off true. "Your mother joked like that."

"My mother," I said, "joked better than that."

* * *

He slept afterward, truly slept, the first deep sleep I'd seen him get since the requisition. I sat up with the lamp and did not decode anything. I just thought.

Here is what I settled, somewhere between the second guttering of the lamp and the first gray suggestion of morning: I forgave him. Not because he deserved it yet, but because I could see the shape of what he'd done. He was a man who had lost his wife and concluded that love was a liability, and who had therefore spent twelve years expressing devotion in the only currency he trusted — concealment. He had hidden me inside a boy's name the way he'd hidden forty names inside spare type. Everything he loved, he encrypted. It was a monstrous way to treat a family, and it was the only language he had, and both of those things were true at once, and I would spend the rest of my life holding them together.

But forgiveness, I decided, did not mean obedience.

Because here was the flaw in his insurance, the flaw he was too honest to see and too frightened to admit: leverage only works against a rational man. Aldous Vane was not irrational, exactly — he was worse. He was patient. A patient man doesn't ransom hostages; he waits for the price to drop. And every block I carried toward the Juniper lowered my father's price, until the day came when the ledger was gone and Vane held nothing but an old cutter with scarred hands and a shop full of incriminating silence.

I could stop carrying. I could leave the remaining blocks in their cases, preserve my father's ransom, keep his neck attached to his body a little longer. That was the loving thing. That was the thing he'd never ask for but was praying for anyway — I heard it in his breathing upstairs, the particular rhythm of a man asleep only because he'd finally confessed.

Or I could finish it. Get every name out of Grayharbor before the century turned, burn the power Vane wanted, and accept that my father had built his insurance policy out of the one material that doesn't survive fire.

I looked at the coat hanging on its peg, lumpy with hidden metal, and I thought about Tamsin waiting on the docks with her father's manifest book, and Osei anchored out past the chain, and forty families sleeping soundly because they didn't know they needed saving.

Then boots on the stair. Not the patrol rhythm — slower, softer, deliberate. A knock at our door at four in the morning, courteous as a knife being unwrapped.

Emmerich answered it, blinking, candle in hand, and I heard a voice I knew — smooth, unhurried, terribly polite — ask whether Master Underhill might receive a visitor regarding certain irregularities in the inventory.

Aldous Vane had come to the shop. Off-duty, alone, smiling.

I reached for the coat.
