## 15. The Price List

Captain Osei did not ask me to sit down, because there was nothing to sit on. The *Juniper*'s cabin was a box of cedar and shadow barely tall enough for a man of ordinary ambition, and Osei was not a man of ordinary ambition. He filled the little room the way a ledger fills a drawer — completely, and with an implication of more beneath.

"So," he said. "The girl from Underhill's."

"The woman from Underhill's," I said.

He looked at me for a long moment, the way my father looked at a piece of type that had come out of the mold with a burr on it — not with disapproval, exactly, but with an assessment of how much work it would take to make me right. "The woman from Underhill's," he conceded. "You've brought me something to read, you said. In your letter you were very careful not to say what."

I had written that letter three times before I sent it by a harbor boy who could be trusted to be too lazy to read it. *I have cargo worth carrying. Not brandy. Not guns. Names.* I had signed no name and given no direction, which meant either the boy was honest or the whole scheme had drowned in the harbor before it started. It had not drowned. Here I was, in a cabin that smelled of tar and cloves, being assessed.

I unwrapped the oilcloth on his desk and set out four type blocks. They looked like nothing — spare sorts, misstruck, the kind of thing any foundry sweeps into a bin at week's end. Then I turned them face up and laid them in a row, and I took the sheet of paper I'd made rubbings on the night before, pressed soft lead over the reversed letters until they spoke forward instead of backward.

Osei read. He read the way soldiers march, I thought — steadily, without hurry, but covering ground that would bury you if you tried to match it. When he finished he sat back, and the bench complained under him like a gull.

"Names," he said. "Printers. Chandlers. Two aldermen." He tapped the rubbing with one blunt finger. "And this is four blocks out of how many?"

"Enough to fill a strongbox. The rest is hidden where the army won't find it, because the army has already looked once."

"And what do you want for it?"

"Passage. For the whole ledger. Off this coast, to printers who'll use it."

"To use it *how*?" His voice didn't rise. It lowered, if anything, which was worse. "There are two things a man can do with names, woman from Underhill's. He can sell them or he can print them. One of those makes him rich and the other makes him dead, and in my experience most men mean the first when they say the second."

"I mean print them."

"You mean it now. In this cabin, with the fog outside and my crew pretending to work the deck above us, you mean it. Ask yourself whether you'll mean it in March, when an army clerk offers you your father's life for a single page."

"My father isn't arrested," I said. "Yet."

"Ah." Something moved behind his eyes — not sympathy; I don't think Captain Osei spent sympathy, he invested it. "So there is a father. And there is a *yet*. You're learning to talk like a smuggler already. That's a pity. Or a qualification. I haven't decided."

He stood — crouched, rather, under the deck beams — and poured two fingers of something from a bottle I hadn't seen until he wanted it seen. He pushed one cup across the desk. I don't drink; my father's apprentice years had taught me that a steady hand is a career, and I said so.

"Good," Osei said, and drank mine too. "Now. Passage I can give you. That part is easy; I run cargo past gray coats twice a season, and half my cargo is less interesting than yours. But passage costs money, and money is only paper that everyone has agreed to believe in, and lately the army prints better paper than anyone." He set the cup down with the deliberation of a judge setting down a gavel. "So let me tell you about the *Juniper*'s debts."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Captain Emmanuel Osei before that night, gathered the way Grayharbor gathers everything, through dockside murmur and my father's occasional remarks over supper: that he was born somewhere south of everywhere, that he'd sailed as mate on a slaver out of Bristol and walked off it at Montserrat and never explained himself and never needed to, that he'd owned the *Juniper* outright for six years and carried anything across any water so long as it wasn't people, and that the harbor customs men hated him with the special hatred of officials who cannot catch a man doing anything except precisely what he does openly.

What I learned that night was the rest of it. Three years ago, the *Juniper* had taken a contract from the army itself — military freight, powder and pay-chests, the dull honest work that keeps a smuggling hull respectable. Halfway through the second run, the contracts stopped. The payments didn't. The army's quartermaster's office held paper saying the *Juniper* owed it for goods received and never delivered — four hundred pounds, which is a number I will tell you plainly because I have never learned to whisper arithmetic — and the harbor admiralty court, such as it was under occupation, agreed. A ship with a judgment against her cannot clear port legally. She cannot insure her cargo. She cannot even, technically, exist, though she goes on doing it anyway, the way my father went on being a widower and I went on being a boy named Cyrus.

"They don't want the money," Osei said. "They want the debt alive. A debt is a leash. Every time I sail, I sail as a fugitive instead of a merchant, and a fugitive pays higher fees to every hand between here and the Channel. Your army invented that leash. I've been looking for a pair of scissors for three years."

"You want me to cut it."

"I want you to forge the release."

He said it the way Vane asked questions — flatly, courteously, giving me all the time in the world to understand exactly what I was agreeing to. I want to record here, honestly, that I did not flinch, because flinching is a luxury and also because I am writing this myself and a narrator may be forgiven one vanity. But my hands, which had been resting easy on the desk, arranged themselves flat, and Osei noticed, because noticing is his trade.

"Understand the shape of it," he went on. "Not a copied pass. Not a faked countersign, which any decent engraver can do and any clerk can catch. A full discharge of record. Quartermaster's seal, comptroller's signature, registry number, wax impression, and the entry copied fair into the style their clerks use — the abbreviations, the flourishes, the little slovenly habits that make handwriting look lived-in instead of drawn. The army knows its own papers the way a mother knows her child's cry. Get one note wrong and it isn't a fine we're talking about."

"It's the gallows."

"For forgery of military instruments? For the man who did it, certainly. For the woman—" He shrugged, a small precise motion. "Transportation, probably. Seven years in a place with better weather and worse company. Though they've been inventive lately. I wouldn't count on the old mercies."

"And if it's done well?"

"Then the leash is cut. The *Juniper* clears port as a free vessel, carries your cargo wherever you say, and owes nobody. That's my price, woman from Underhill's. Passage plus scissors. Both or neither."

* * *

I walked home along the quay because the fog had come in thick enough to hide a person from herself, and walking in fog is nearly as private as thinking, and considerably warmer than either deserves.

Let me be honest about what I felt, since this account will likely survive me and I would hate to be remembered braver than I was. I felt the cold arrive in my stomach first, then spread outward, the way fear actually works — not the theatrical chill of the stage but a slow administrative dread, itemized. The gallows risk I could hold at arm's length; I had been carrying gallows risk sewn into my coat lining for weeks, and a body grows accustomed to its own weight. What frightened me was smaller and sharper: *he asked me to be good at lying forever.* Everything until now had been finite. Carry the blocks. Cross the bridge. Survive the checkpoint. But forgery was not an errand; it was a practice. It would require me to study the army's hand the way I had studied my father's, hour upon hour, until deception sat in my fingers deeper than the alphabet.

My father cuts type because he believes letters should be exact. I was being asked to make letters exact in service of a lie, and some traitorous corner of my mind — the corner I was beginning to suspect was the largest corner — had already begun to look forward to it.

Because here is the thing I understood in the fog, walking past the customs house where a sentry's lantern swam like a fish in milk: Osei had not merely named a price. He had told me a truth about the world, gratis. Survival is quantified. Everyone in Grayharbor was paying something — Tamsin paid in manifests falsified by lamplight, her father paid in signatures on them, my father paid in silence, the arrested printers had paid in blood and sawdust. The revolutionaries in their capital paid in ideals while their soldiers collected ledgers in the provinces. Nobody survived for free anymore. The century was ending and the whole town had become a shop with everything marked down.

Very well, I thought. Then let me know my prices precisely. Let me write the list.

Passage for the ledger: one forged discharge. My father's safety: however much evidence remains unspent. Tamsin's friendship: her brother's name, which I had found in the coded entries and had not yet found the courage to mention. My own name: apparently negotiable, though the rate kept rising.

I reached the foundry and stood a moment in the alley, watching the light in our front window. We were billeting soldiers now — three of them slept in the drying loft, and their boots lined the hall like a row of small corpses — but the workshop lamp still burned past midnight, because my father still worked past midnight, cutting type for customers who no longer came, keeping his hands honest while everything else went crooked. From outside, through the fogged glass, his shadow bent over his case, and he looked exactly as he had looked every night of my childhood: a man making letters for a world that had stopped reading.

I could refuse Osei. I want it recorded that the door was open. I could walk in, wake my father, confess everything, and spend the winter hiding blocks in herring barrels until one of us was caught or the war moved on. There were versions of honor available. They simply all ended with the ledger burned or seized, forty families condemned on false records, and my mother's name — yes, her name was in those blocks; I had found it by then, cut in my father's steadiest hand — erased by men with gray coats and inventory lists.

Or I could learn to lie in lead.

* * *

Osei had given me three days to answer, and a token of intent besides. Before I left the cabin he handed me a packet wrapped in sailcloth, saying only, "Study material," with the dry smile of a man who has just made someone else complicit and enjoyed it.

Inside, when I opened it at my workbench with the lamp shuttered to a slit, I found what he must have spent years assembling: scraps. Corners of army requisitions bought from sailors and clerks. A complete freight manifest, water-damaged and worthless as paper, priceless as pattern. Three different impressions of the quartermaster's seal, each in a different state of wear, because seals age like faces and a forger who copies only the fresh die hangs herself. And a letter — an actual letter in the comptroller's own hand, acquired God knew how, thanking some functionary for services and signed with a flourish that looped and doubled back like a path through marshland.

I laid them all out under the lamp and did what I had been trained to do since I was six years old. I looked at letters.

The comptroller, I saw within the hour, had a tremor in his downstrokes — age, or drink, or both — but only in his descenders; his capitals were confident and slightly too large, the writing of a man who signs all day and resents it. The quartermaster's seal had been re-cut sometime in the past year; the older impressions showed a finer line in the laurel border, and anyone forging from a recent example would produce a seal *better* than the current one, which is precisely the sort of perfection that betrays. The clerks used a long-s abbreviation in "quartermaster" that had gone out of fashion everywhere else, a fossil habit, and their ink sat heavier on the upstrokes because they dipped carelessly and hurried.

An hour in, I caught myself smiling at a dead clerk's careless dips. Another hour in, I chose a piece of spare brass from the scrap bin and began scribing the seal's border onto it, working at half scale first, the way my father taught me — *learn the letter small, and the large one will contain you.*

That was when my father's footsteps stopped outside the workshop door.

I want to explain the geometry of that moment. The door was closed. Between us lay twelve years of agreed silence, a treaty older and more carefully maintained than any in Europe. He knew I stayed up late; I knew he knew; neither of us had ever asked why, because the answers were dangerous currency and we had both been taught the exchange rate. But the lamp was shuttered to a slit, and on my bench lay the comptroller's signature, and my hand held a graver against brass, and for the first time in my life the silence between us was not protection but exposure.

His boots waited in the hall. Ten seconds. Twenty. I did not move. I did not breathe, very nearly. Somewhere in the house a billeted soldier rolled over and his cot complained, and under cover of that sound my father's boots went on — past the workshop door, up the stairs, along the landing, and then the small click of his own door closing, gently, the way a man closes a door when he has decided not to know something.

He had seen the light, I understood then. Perhaps he saw it every night. And he had chosen, again and again, not to open the door — not out of ignorance, but out of the same terrible arithmetic I was learning, the price list every adult in Grayharbor kept folded somewhere near the heart.

I worked until the candle failed. By then the border was true at full scale, and I had traced the comptroller's signature eleven times, and on the twelfth I did not trace it. I wrote it. Freehand, tremor and flourish and all, and set my pen down, and looked at the two names side by side — his real, mine counterfeit — and could not immediately say which was which.

That is the moment I will point to, when they ask when it began. Not the bridge, not the cells, not the quay at midnight with bells ringing. A workbench, a bad candle, a stolen letter. I forged a dead man's confidence and found it fit my hand.

Three days remained before I owed Osei my answer, but we both knew I would say yes; he had known it in the cabin, watching my hands go flat. The question was never whether. It was only ever the fee, and the fee was now counted, and I had paid the first installment without noticing the transaction.

I banked the lamp, wrapped the study materials in the sailcloth, and slid them into the hollowed type case behind the third drawer — where Emmerich had searched twice and would search again, poor boy, and find nothing but type. On my way to bed I paused at the foot of the stairs, where the fog had followed me in and hung in the hallway like something waiting.

Above me, my father's floor creaked once. Still awake, then. Still counting his own prices.

And below the stairs, pinned to the board where notices accumulated like snowdrifts, I saw by the last of the banked light a fresh paper I had not noticed coming in — army stock, wide margins, the comptroller's office stamp in the corner. A notice of review. *All commercial inventories subject to verification by the commissary of records,* it began, in the long-s and careless dips I now knew like a psalm, *beginning the first of the month.*

The commissary of records. I knew that office only by its reputation and its seal, which I had spent the evening learning to counterfeit.

Its chief clerk, the notice said at the bottom, in a signature I recognized from a stolen letter: *A. Vane.*
