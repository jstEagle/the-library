## 16. Learning to Lie in Lead

The trouble with cutting a counterfeit seal is that the real thing is badly made.

That was the first thing I learned, and it offended me more than the gallows did. The army's shipping seal — the one pressed into wax on every manifest leaving Grayharbor — featured a lion rampant that no lion had ever resembled, a crown with seven points when heraldry allowed five, and a letterer's *R* for the King's initial that slumped like a drunk leaning on a fence post. Some clerk in the customs office had commissioned it forty years ago from some cutter who didn't care, and every honest merchant in the port had been pressing that sloppy lion into honest wax ever since, and nobody had ever noticed, because nobody looks closely at what authority stamps down.

I looked closely. I couldn't help it. I had spent twelve years learning to see letters the way a surgeon sees flesh — as structures, as things that hold weight or fail — and once I saw the seal's flaws I could not unsee them. The lion's forepaw ended in a blob where there should have been separation between paw and chest. The seventh crown point drifted left. The *R* had a bowl too large for its leg.

Which meant, I realized somewhere around the second night of work, that my forgery would be better than the original. And that if anyone ever examined my papers closely enough, they would catch me not because my lion was wrong but because my lion was *right*.

I sat back from the bench and laughed, alone, at two in the morning, in a shop where laughter had never been an ingredient. It was that or weep, and I'd done my weeping in chapters fourteen and fifteen, so to speak. There is only so much grief a girl can spend before she starts billing herself for it.

Osei wanted three documents: a coastal trading pass under the flag of a neutral merchant house, a cargo manifest consistent with it, and a customs clearance bearing the harbor collector's countersignature. He brought me specimens — genuine papers lifted from ships that no longer needed them, their owners beyond caring — and he spread them on my father's proof table the way a gambler spreads cards he intends to win with.

"You understand," he'd said, "that if they hang you, they'll hang me after, and probably Tamsin Corl before either of us."

"I understand the order of operations," I said. "It's flattering to be first."

"It's not flattery. It's arithmetic." He had rolled the papers up again and left, and his arithmetic had sat in my chest ever since like a swallowed coin.

So. Nights. My father slept badly since his confession — since telling me that twelve years of coded type had been cut not for any cause but against the day the debts came due, insurance laid down grain by grain by a frightened man who never meant his daughter to carry it — and I could not risk him waking and finding me at this work. Not because he would stop me. Because he would try to do it himself, and do it worse, and get us all killed faster.

That was the second thing I learned about deception: it begins as necessity and becomes a judgment. Every lie I cut into lead that winter carried a small cold note attached: *you do this because you are better at it than he is.* I did not enjoy the note. I read it anyway.

* * *

The seals were the craft part; the signatures were the art.

A seal is geometry. A signature is a biography. Collector Amos Renwick — whose name I now knew better than my own mother's maiden hand — had signed perhaps sixty thousand documents in thirty years of service, and his hand told the story: firm early strokes degenerating over decades into a hurried scrawl, the *A* opening up, the flourish after *Renwick* shortening year by year until it was less a flourish than a shrug. On current papers he barely finished the final *k*. The man had grown tired of his own name.

To forge him properly I had to become tired with him. I practiced on scrap zinc until my wrist learned his weariness, until I could feel in my own tendons the specific boredom of a man who has stamped lions and slumped his *R* through four administrations and two wars and has stopped believing, somewhere deep, that the papers mean anything at all.

There is something obscene about how well I came to know him. By the fourth night I could have written Renwick's biography from his pen alone: desk job always, bad knee by the way he lifted the pen between words rather than dragging it, a small tremor in the downstrokes that said drink or age or both. I knew his signature more intimately than his wife did. I forged his tiredness so faithfully that some evenings I felt tired in his particular way, and had to put down the graver and go stand in the yard and remember which fatigue was mine.

Mine, incidentally, was also considerable. But mine had a shape. His was just weather.

My father, I should say, could never have done this. I don't mean his hands were less skilled — they were not, they were finer, he could cut a six-point body face smaller than a lentil and keep the serifs true. I mean he lacked the appetite. When Father cut his hidden ledger into spare blocks, he encoded everything, buried every name under cipher and reversal, and even then — I saw this now, holding my new knowledge up against the old — even then his cuts were hesitant. Guilt lives in the hand. Every letter he ever cut in secret carries a flinch. You can read his fear in the metal the way you can read Renwick's boredom: the strokes start strong and apologize halfway through.

My strokes did not apologize. That was the discovery of that fortnight, the thing I kept finding and losing and finding again like a coin under furniture: when I lied in lead, I cut clean. No tremor. No guilt in the downstroke. I forged the customs seal and the lion looked *more* rampant than the rampant lion. I forged Renwick's signature and it bore his exhaustion with a conviction the living man had lost.

I am better at this than my father ever was.

I want to be careful how I say that, because I have had a long time since to think about what it means, and because a younger reader might take it for pride. It is not pride. It is closer to the moment a cutter discovers a flaw in the punch she has used for years — the recognition that everything made with it was subtly wrong, and that she herself, her eye, her judgment, contains the error as naturally as it contains her skill. My father taught me to cut truth beautifully. It turned out the same hand, the same eye, the same discipline, served lies without modification. Nothing needed to change. That was the horror. Deception required none of my invention. It only required my competence pointed in a different direction, like a lamp turned from one shelf to another.

And beneath that horror, quieter, worse: a kind of relief. All those years of hiding — the name, the boy's coat, the silence maintained so perfectly that even I sometimes lost the girl inside it — I had thought of them as costs. Sacrifices. But sitting at the bench at midnight, pressing wax onto a forged document and stamping my lion into it, I understood that I had not merely survived the disguise. I had *excelled* at it. Twelve years of being someone I wasn't, and the being-someone-else had gone into me like oil into leather. I was waterproof in falsehood. It slid off me.

Every gift is a diagnosis, if you look at it long enough.

* * *

On the sixth night, my father caught me anyway.

Not at the work — I heard his stair groan in time and had the wax scraped and the blanks covered before his door opened — but at the evidence. He came down for the reasons old men come down at night: water, pain, the habit of worry. And he stood in the doorway of the workshop in his nightshirt looking at the bench where I had, in my haste, failed to turn over one sheet of practice zinc. Renwick's signature sat on it, eleven times in a column, marching from stiff to weary like a life lived in miniature.

He picked it up. I watched his eyes travel down the column. I watched him arrive at the bottom of it, where the signatures were best, and I watched his face do something complicated — pride and grief and a third thing I had no name for then and have only partly named since.

"You've got his hand," Father said. "The tiredness. Most forgers copy the letters. You've copied the man."

"Is that a compliment or an accusation?"

"Yes," he said.

He set the zinc down with great care, the way you set down something that might go off. Then he stood there in the lamplight, and I could see him wanting to tell me to stop, and I could see him remembering — as I was remembering — that he had no standing to say it. This was his harvest. He had planted fear in this shop for twelve years, block by block, and the crop had come up in me.

"When your mother died," he said finally, "I couldn't sign the register."

I knew this story. Everyone in Grayharbor above a certain age knew some version of it — the widow Underhill, dead of the fever, and a husband so undone he had the neighbor make his mark. But I let him tell it, because I understood he was not telling it to inform me.

"My hand wouldn't do it. Her name — writing her name, knowing she'd never sign anything again. My hand refused." He flexed his fingers, looking at them as if checking whether they'd betrayed him again recently. "You'd have signed it. That night. Steady as stone."

"Someone has to be steady," I said. It came out harder than I intended, and truer.

"Yes," he said. "God help whoever it is." And he went back up the stairs, and the shop was silent in its old way, except that now I knew the silence had been training me all along.

* * *

I finished the first full set on the ninth night: pass, manifest, clearance, each aged with tea and handled — this mattered, Osei had been specific — with the greasy confidence of a document that lives in a captain's cabin rather than a glass case. I rubbed salt into the fold lines. I let candle smoke kiss one corner. I forged a wine stain of respectable vintage near the manifest's lower edge, and then, in a fit of thoroughness I can only describe as professional vanity, I forged Renwick's small correcting tick beside the tonnage figure, the little checkmark customs men make to show they noticed something and permitted it anyway.

Then I sat with the completed packet in front of me and did the thing I had been not-doing for nine nights: I looked at what I was.

Forty families. That was the freight, ultimately — the ledger in code, the names in metal, the whole hidden architecture of my father's fear, moving toward the Juniper piece by piece on forged paper that I had made *better than the government's*. The army's own sloppiness was my ladder. Their lion was wrong and my lion was right, and the rightness of my lion would carry the wrongness of their war past their own gates. There was a justice in it so neat it felt designed, and I distrusted neat justice the way sailors distrust calm water. Still. It held me up.

I should record, honestly, the other thing I felt, because this account is worth nothing if it flatters me. Underneath the purpose, underneath the forty families and the righteous freight, there was a small hot coal of pure enjoyment. The work was *good*. It was the best work I had ever done — better than the type faces, better than the ledger decipherment, because it demanded everything at once: eye, hand, nerve, invention, performance. For nine nights I had been fully awake in a way that twelve years of hidden virtue had never made me. Excellence in service of a lie had woken me up, and honesty, my long honesty, my costly and dutiful honesty, had mostly made me tired.

I turned the coal over in my mind and looked at it plainly. *This is what you are for,* it said. Not the foundry. Not the filial silences. *You are the weapon the circumstances made.* And weapons that know what they are begin to want to be used.

I wrapped the documents in oilcloth. I hid them in the hollowed case behind the proof press — third row, under the fourteen-point nonpareils, a location I had chosen precisely because it was boring. And then, because the hour was late and the coal was hot and I had perhaps one more good hour in my hands, I took out a fresh blank and began the hardest piece yet: the gate pass. Countersigned, sealed, and requiring a hand I had not yet studied — Major Hessler's own, broad and brutal-looking on the specimen Osei had procured, a signature with no tiredness in it at all, a signature written by a man who believed completely in what he signed.

That would be a different kind of forgery. You cannot copy conviction by imitating weariness. I would have to find where his certainty lived in his strokes and reproduce *that*.

I was three letters into studying it — bent close to the specimen, lamplight pooling on the metal, the whole shop gone soft around me — when I heard the sound that did not belong to midnight in our street: boots. Many boots, in step, coming down Copper Alley with the unhurried confidence of men who had decided the town was theirs and were no longer bothering to pretend otherwise.

I pinched the candle out and stood in the dark listening. The boots stopped. Not passing, then. Arriving.

Through the shutters came the light of lanterns, and a voice — official, bored, already rehearsing its announcement — said words that rearranged my entire week: *"By requisition of the Northern Army, these premises are designated for quartering effective immediately—"*

They were not stopping at our door.

They were stopping at every door.

## * * *

I stood in the black shop with Hessler's unbroken signature in my hand and counted doors down the alley. Eight. Nine. Ours was tenth. I had perhaps ninety seconds to decide what a foundry looks like when soldiers live in it — which benches get covered, which cases get locked, which hollowed block of nonpareil must vanish tonight into the frozen yard or not exist at all by morning.

Somewhere down the alley, the knocking began.
