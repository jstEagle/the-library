## 17. Winter Quarters

The army took the foundry on a Tuesday, which felt fitting — Tuesdays were always the day the world decided Cyrus Underhill needed rearranging.

They came at noon, six men in gray coats with a corporal who read the requisition order aloud as though he expected the building to argue. *By authority of the Provisional Command, all structures of manufacture adjacent to the harbor road shall be made available for the winter quartering of troops.* The paper had an official seal on it, and I remember thinking, with the part of my mind that never stops appraising letterforms, that the seal was badly cut. The eagle looked like a duck.

"Manufacture," my father repeated, in the tone of a man tasting something to determine whether it was poison or merely unpleasant.

"You make letters," the corporal said. "Letters are manufactured. You'll do."

There was no arguing with that logic, mostly because there was no logic in it. By evening the casting room held four straw pallets, a card table missing one leg propped on a copybook, and the particular smell of soldiers — wet wool, pipe smoke, and the boiled-meat melancholy that follows armies the way gulls follow fishing boats.

I want to be honest here, since honesty has become something of a professional habit: my first thought was not for my father's safety or the ledger hidden in the type cases. My first thought was that they would put their boots on the composing stone, and that I had spent eleven years keeping that stone level to the width of a hair, and that some privates from the hinterlands were going to stand on it while picking their teeth.

Grief takes many forms. Mine has always been territorial.

* * *

You would think hiding contraband beneath the noses of an occupying force would require cunning. It requires only that soldiers believe thoroughly in their own importance and not at all in yours. To a gray coat, a type foundry is a room full of small metal boxes. Nobody opens the boxes. Opening the boxes would suggest the boxes mattered, and nothing that belongs to a conquered town matters — that is the whole theology of occupation, printed in no book but practiced everywhere.

Still, I prepared. I had learned by then that preparation is fear wearing its work clothes.

The coded blocks — the secret ledger my father had cut into spare type over twelve years, forty families' worth of names and debts and dealings rendered in reverse impressions readable only under raking light — numbered nineteen. Nineteen blocks, each no larger than a knuckle. Too many for a coat now that coats were searched at whim and my body had become a piece of suspicious cargo. So I did what any foundryman would do. I gave them a home among their own kind.

I selected nine type cases — the deep California-style cases along the north wall, the ones whose compartments run two layers if you know to lift the tray. Into the space beneath the trays, between the divisions where the lead sorts rattled loose anyway, I bedded the blocks in wool waste. Then I scattered genuine sorts over the trays' seams — a compartment of quads here, a run of long-s there — so that each case looked exactly as boring as every other case in the shop. Boredom, I have come to understand, is the finest lock ever devised. No man picks what puts him to sleep.

I labeled the deception with my father's own system of worn brass tags, moved three cases to positions of maximum visibility, and left a spill of dried ink on the shelf above them, because soldiers do not touch things that look like work.

Then Corporal Hesse's men moved in, and the true test began: living with your secrets at a distance of four feet, through meals, through the night, through the small hours when sleeping men talk and hiding women lie awake doing arithmetic about rope and gallows and the weight of a door bar.

* * *

They were not monsters. That was the trouble.

I had spent months imagining the army as a single animal — a gray beast with many boots. What billeted itself upon us was instead four tired human beings plus a corporal, and human beings, even uniformed ones, shiver and joke and burn their porridge. There was Brant, heavy and slow, who wrote letters home every Sunday with his tongue out in concentration like a boy. There was Kessel, who had a cough he treated with rum and denial. There was little Mauve — actually Mauvet, but the army had sanded him down — seventeen at most, who was frightened of my father and therefore of me, and pretended both of us didn't exist with the total commitment of the truly terrified.

And there was Emmerich.

Emmerich was twenty-two or twenty-three, tall in the way of a fence post rather than a tree, with a face that seemed permanently on the verge of asking permission. He had been a clerk in a wool merchant's house in the capital before the war conscripted him, and he retained the clerk's habit of apologizing before touching anything. On the second morning he knocked on the frame of the casting-room door before entering a room that now legally belonged to him.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Is the stove meant to smoke like that?"

"It's meant to draw," I said. "Someone has shut the flue."

"Ah." He looked at the flue, then at me, then back at the flue, with the expression of a man navigating between two truths he suspected were at war. He opened it. The smoke went up the chimney, where smoke goes when the world permits it.

That was all, that first exchange. But you can learn more from a man opening a flue than from most sermons. He had noticed the problem. He had asked instead of commanded. And he had said *sorry* to a foundry girl whose town his army had swallowed — sorry as reflex, the way some men say *God keep you.*

I filed him away the way I file everything: carefully, in reverse, against the day I might need to read him backward.

* * *

The weeks of December turned the foundry into something I cannot fully explain without embarrassment: a home with wolves in it, and the wolves turned out to keep regular hours.

They rose at six. They drilled on the harbor road until ten. They ate at noon and again at dusk and complained with great seriousness about both meals, which my father received in silence and I received with a fury I disguised as indifference, because we ate less than they did and had before the army arrived too. At nine they lit the stove one last time and slept, except for whoever drew the watch, which rotated nightly, and except Emmerich, who read.

He read by rushlight after the others snored — cheap pamphlets, a battered book of sermons, once, incredibly, a volume of parliamentary debates that must have cost him a month's pay on the gray market. He read the way I cut type: totally, with his shoulders gone quiet.

"What's the argument about?" I asked him one night, because the fire was low and my caution was low with it, and because twelve years of enforced silence will do strange things to a person's tongue after dark.

He started, then held up the book. "Whether property may be seized for the public welfare without compensation."

"And what does Parliament say?"

"That it may," he said. "At length. With many examples." A pause. "Parliament owns property, so it says it very comfortably."

I should have laughed politely and withdrawn. Instead I heard myself say, "Do you believe them?"

And Emmerich — good God, the man considered the question. He looked at the stove, at his hands, at the row of type cases along the north wall behind which nineteen small treasons slept in wool. He looked everywhere, in fact, but through me, which is why I let myself ask such questions near him; a man who won't meet your eyes while thinking is safe, but a man who looks *around* while thinking is honest, and honest men are the most dangerous and most valuable things in an occupied town.

"I believe," he said finally, "that I was told this would be a short war of liberation, and instead I am sleeping in a building full of little metal letters I'm not allowed to touch, in a town where nobody will look at me. So either the war is lying or I am bad at wars." He closed the book. "Good night, mistress Underhill."

Nobody had called me *mistress* anything since autumn. It sat strangely warm in my chest, and I distrusted it immediately and completely. Sentiment toward the enemy is a leak in the hull; it starts the width of a kind word.

* * *

The hollowed cases passed their inspections because inspections in that foundry were theater. Vane's clerks came twice more with their inventories, and I walked before them narrating the shop like a museum docent leading children past the exhibits she doesn't want touched. *Composing stone, original. Casting bench, my late grandfather's. Type cases, ordinary, mixed faces, mostly worn.* The word *ordinary* is a wonderful tool. Use it often enough and even the examiner yawns.

Once, Kessel, drunk on his medicinal rum, knocked a case off the shelf — one of mine, one of the nine, the third from the left — and I felt my heart perform a maneuver I have no anatomy to describe. The case hit the floor and broke open and hundreds of sorts skittered across the boards like startled beetles, and beneath the loosened tray, visible for one half-second in the rushlight, lay the corner of a wool-wrapped block.

Brant laughed. Kessel swore. Emmerich knelt down first.

He gathered the sorts with those clerk's hands, quick and orderly, and he lifted the tray back into place, and — I watched him do it — his thumb pressed the wool flat and tucked the wrapping's corner down out of sight in a single motion, casual as breathing, before he slid the tray home. He never changed his expression. He never looked at me. He handed the case up and said, "These things are heavier than they look," and went back to his pamphlet.

I did not sleep that night. Not from terror. From the far worse problem of not knowing what I felt.

He knew. Or he suspected, or he had seen and chosen blindness — I could not determine which, and the not-knowing gnawed at me worse than discovery would have. Discovery I could fight. Kindness I could only owe.

* * *

My father, meanwhile, was fading into the walls. Occupation suited him the way damp suits paper. He spoke less, ate less, and spent his evenings at the window watching the harbor with the fixed attention of a man reading a document everyone else finds blank. Twice I caught him with his hand flat against the wall of cases, not searching — resting. As though the ledger were a heartbeat he checked.

"We should move them," I whispered once, when the soldiers were at drill.

"Where?" he said. "There is nowhere in this town the army isn't. Better hidden among their beds than in any hole they might dig for." Which was my own reasoning returned to me in his voice, and I hated how much it pleased me, that we thought alike, that his mind and mine had worn matching grooves across twelve silent years.

"The young one," my father said, after a while. "The reader. Watch him."

"I do."

"Not the way you watch Vane," he said. "Vane you watch like a blade. Watch that boy like weather. Weather decides things without meaning to."

Which was, characteristically, exactly right, and exactly useless, and exactly my father.

* * *

The decency came due on the twentieth of December, in the ordinary way that decency arrives: without announcement, attached to an inconvenience.

Mauvet — the frightened one, the seventeen-year-old — fell ill. A fever, coughing blood-flecked, shaking on his pallet in the casting room while his comrades stood around him with the helpless aggression of men who can fix wagons but not boys. The army surgeon was at the fort across the river; the roads were snow-choked; the corporal could not leave his post to fetch help without orders, and orders required paper, and paper required the commissary, and the commissary was closed for a feast day I have mercifully forgotten.

I should have stayed in my corner. Every calculation said stay in your corner, girl; their fever is not your fever; pity is how complicity begins.

But Mauvet had once spent an entire afternoon sweeping the floor around me without once demanding I move, which in a soldier is practically a love letter, and besides — I was my father's daughter, raised in a trade whose whole principle is that small things, set in order, make large things possible. Fever is a sort of misprint. Sometimes it can be corrected.

Feverfew and willow bark I had. Boneset, from Mrs. Adair at the chandler's, traded for a promise of printed handbills come spring. I brewed what my mother's recipe book called *the poor man's cure*, and I carried it into a room full of enemy soldiers, and I sat by their sick boy and dosed him through the night while Kessel wept quietly into his rum and Brant held the boy's legs still during the worst shivering.

Emmerich sat across from me. Toward dawn, Mauvet's fever broke and the boy slept like the dead but warmer, and Emmerich said, without looking up:

"They told us in training that people here would poison us if they could."

"People tell themselves stories about strangers," I said. "It saves the trouble of learning them."

"Yes," he said. "We were also told *we* were the story. That we'd be welcomed." He turned the rushlight up a fraction, and its glow crossed the space between us and lay on the type cases along the wall — my cases, my buried nineteen — and he looked at that light on that wood and said, in a voice of complete mildness:

"It's remarkable, isn't it. How much a town can hold inside ordinary-looking boxes."

The fire ticked. Somewhere Kessel snored. My pulse performed its maneuvers.

"Remarkable," I agreed.

"I'd best check the watch," Emmerich said, and rose, and took his coat, and went out into the snow, and neither of us said another word, and everything had been said.

* * *

Afterward I stood a long time in the cold corner of the casting room, doing the arithmetic I am always doing. If Emmerich knew, why hadn't he reported? If he didn't know, what had his words meant? And the largest figure in the sum, the one I kept circling: why did the thought of his knowing not frighten me as much as it should?

Because he had opened the flue. Because he had apologized to furniture. Because he had tucked the wool corner down with his thumb and gone back to his pamphlet. Because he asked what the arguments were about. Because he was a clerk conscripted into a wolf's coat, and wolves are not born, they are issued, and somewhere under Emmerich's gray cloth was a man who owned a book of parliamentary debates and doubted every word in it.

My father warned me: watch him like weather. But I have spent my life among letters, and letters teach you their true lesson eventually — that meaning lives not in the thing itself but in its arrangement, and that the same characters, differently set, spell either mercy or murder. Emmerich was type. I could not yet read the line he'd been set in. I only knew the font.

On the twenty-third of December, the corporal posted a new notice on the foundry door, and I read it by lamplight with my breath smoking, and the badly-cut duck of an eagle stamped at its head suddenly did not seem funny at all.

*BY ORDER OF COMMISSARY ALDOUS VANE:* it began, and my stomach dropped through the floorboards before I reached the rest — *a full inventory audit of all requisitioned premises shall commence in the new year, including itemized inspection of stock, stores, and containers of every description.*

Containers of every description.

Boxes, the notice said, in its stiff official hand. All the boxes. Even the boring ones.

Even, said the notice, in its polite gray way, the ones full of little metal letters nobody is supposed to care about.

Behind me, inside the warm foundry, four soldiers slept, and one of them had tucked a wool corner down with his thumb and never said a word, and I stood in the snow understanding that whatever Emmerich was — weather, mercy, trap — I was about to learn it under Vane's own eyes.
