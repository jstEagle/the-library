## 18. The Brother's Name

The trouble with a secret ledger cut into type is that it insists on being read. It sits there in its drawer of hollowed cases, patient as lead, and every night I tell myself I will only check the two blocks I need for Osei, and every night my fingers find another entry and my eyes do what eyes do.

That was how I found him.

I had been sorting the western tier of the cache — the blocks Father had cut in the years before the war made secrecy fashionable rather than necessary — when my thumb snagged on a burr of metal that hadn't been dressed down properly. Sloppy work. My father is many things, most of them careful, but he cut this ledger at midnight after twelve-hour days at the casting bench, and even master hands shake at midnight. I turned the block to the lamp to see how bad the burr was, and the reversed letters caught the light like frost.

*J. MERROW.*

I knew the name before I knew I knew it. It went off somewhere behind my ribs, small and cold, like a coin dropped down a well.

Below it, in the compressed cipher Father used for the sensitive entries — not names alone but dispositions, services rendered, accounts settled — ran a line I have now read so many times I could cut it from memory: *harbormaster's office. Information regarding cargo movements. Paid quarterly. 1789–present.*

Present. As in this year. As in while Tamsin Merrow was teaching me which soldiers took bribes and which took pride, her brother — or her father; the entry did not distinguish, and God, I hoped it didn't — was selling everything that moved through that harbor to the same army burning print shops up the coast.

I set the block down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

Then I picked it up again, because I am my father's daughter, and copied the cipher into my memory with the same thoroughness I'd once given to ligatures.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic of it, done honestly, because I promised myself I would do this one sum without flinching.

Tamsin has falsified manifests for me. She has risked transportation — the polite word for being shipped across an ocean to die somewhere warmer — for a girl she met over smuggled tea and proofs of a pamphlet neither of us will admit to printing. She has done this because she believes we are on the same side, and because she trusts me, and because she told me once, in the dark of the customs loft, laughing: "At least my family's clean, Cy. Somebody in this town has to be."

And here is the block of lead that says otherwise, with a burr on it.

Against that: if I tell her, and she is the wrong kind of shattered, she stops helping. The Juniper does not sail clean. The evidence dies in a requisitioned foundry along with, quite possibly, everyone who touched it. Forty families burn because I couldn't hold my tongue.

And against *that*: if I don't tell her, and Vane finds it first — and Vane finds things, it is his whole talent — then Tamsin learns her brother's name from an interrogator instead of a friend, and learns at the same moment that I knew and said nothing, and whatever we were to each other becomes one more thing this winter ate.

Truth as a weapon. That's what it felt like, sitting there under the lamp with the block in my hand. Not a weapon against Vane. Against the one person in Grayharbor who'd never aimed anything at me.

I put the block back in its case and blew out the lamp and lay in the dark above the shop listening to the billeted soldiers snore one floor down, and conducted the further argument that the dark always wins.

She deserves to know. She'd want to know. Knowing is what she does — her father logs ships, she logs everything else, Tamsin Merrow has never in her life let a fact escape unexamined.

But knowing about your own family is different. I would know. There are entries in that ledger with my mother's name in them, and I have not slept properly since I found them, and I love the man who cut them, and some mornings loving him feels like a decision I keep having to make again.

So it wasn't ignorance arguing for silence. It was cowardice wearing ignorance's coat, and I have spent enough winters in this town to recognize borrowed clothes.

* * *

I lasted four days.

Four days of meeting Tamsin at the customs loft or the back step of the cooperage, taking her manifests, trading whispers, watching her laugh with her whole face the way she does — mouth first, then eyes, then everything — and hearing under every word: *paid quarterly.* Four days of her asking, on the third one, "Cy, what's wrong? You've got your cutting face on," and me saying *nothing, tired,* which was the first lie I'd ever told her and slid out so smoothly it scared me more than any soldier has.

On the fourth day the army arrested a fishwife at the gate for carrying a letter, and held her six hours, and let her go, and the whole town pretended it hadn't happened, and I stood in the crowd watching Tamsin watch it, and saw her jaw set the way it sets when she's decided something, and thought: *she is deciding to keep trusting me. Right now. In front of me.*

That decided it. Not honor. Fear of watching her decide otherwise later, on worse terms.

I came to the customs loft after dusk, when her father was at the harbor office and the lamplight through the slanted window made the place look like the inside of a struck bell. She was copying manifests — legitimate ones, for once, or as legitimate as anything gets in Grayharbor — and she looked up with that grin starting and said, "You've got news, you walk like someone with news—"

"Tamsin."

One word, and the grin died, because she's not a fool, which is the whole problem.

"There's an entry in the ledger," I said. "A coded one. Names and services. It goes back years."

"I know what it is. You've told me. It's why we're doing any of this." She set her pen down. Slowly. People only put things down slowly when they're bracing.

"The names aren't just printers and buyers. There are informants in it too. People who sold things to the army. Cargo movements. Ship schedules."

The loft got very quiet. Somewhere below, a gull said something ugly and flew off.

"How far back?" she asked, and her voice was perfectly level, which is how I knew.

"Seventeen eighty-nine."

"To present," she said. Not a question. She'd done the same sum I had, instantly, the way she does sums.

"Tamsin—"

"It's initialled J.M., isn't it." She wasn't looking at me anymore. She was looking at the manifest in front of her, at all those honest columns of tonnage and destination, as if they'd betrayed her personally. "Not my father. My father can't spell 'quarterly' without swearing. He barely writes. It's Joss."

Joss. The name landed and I understood, with a lurch, that I hadn't known it — the ledger gave initials, and I had built a stranger out of that letter, and the stranger was easier to hate than a boy who'd grown up three doors from her, whose handwriting I was looking at right now on these manifests, looping and confident.

"He runs messages for the harbor office," she said, still in that flat, level voice, reading facts off the inside of her own skull. "He has access to everything. Sailing times, cargo, manifests. He's been — he's twenty, Cy. He started when he was seventeen. Selling us."

"Tamsin, listen—"

"No." And then, terribly, her voice cracked open on the next word: "*No.* Because I made jokes. I said my family was clean, I said—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth, hard, like holding a seam shut. "Does it say amounts? What he was paid?"

"Yes."

"Don't tell me." A breath. "No. Tell me. No." She laughed, and there was nothing in it, and it was the worst sound I'd heard all winter, and this winter has had competition. "God. You sat on this four days. I can tell, you know. You've had your cutting face on since Tuesday."

"Three days," I said, because apparently honesty arrives in installments. "And I sat on it because telling you felt like using a knife, and not telling you felt like using a worse one, and I couldn't find a version where I wasn't holding a weapon in this room."

She looked at me then. Eyes red, jaw set, absolutely present.

"You could have not told me at all," she said. "It would've been safer for your ship."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me anyway, mid-operation, with my brother's treason sitting in your pocket next to my forged manifests." She wiped her face with both hands, briskly, businesslike, wringing herself out. "You realize that's the worst possible logistics. You realize that."

"I realize that."

"Good." She stood up. Walked to the window. Stood there with her back to me while the lamplight put her shadow halfway across the room toward me, which felt about right. "Then here's what happens now. You give me his entries. All of them, copied out exact. If Vane ever gets that ledger, my family's name doesn't get to be a surprise to me. I'll know everything the army knows, ahead of them, and I will spend the time between building him a story that keeps him alive."

"And the manifests?"

She turned around. Her face was wrecked and her voice was iron, and I have never admired anyone more or been more afraid of anyone less.

"The manifests don't change. He sold the harbor; I'm not going to help him sell you too. Joss made his entries." A pause, and something moved through her face — grief, fury, and underneath them both a terrible stubborn love, the kind that isn't a feeling but a labor, and I watched her begin it. "I'm making mine."

* * *

I walked home through the snow with the copies sewn into my coat lining, next to the blocks, and I confess I did not feel noble. I felt like a woman who had fired a shot and hit exactly what she aimed at and discovered that aiming well is no comfort at all.

Because here is what I couldn't stop turning over, boot after boot in the fresh-falling white:

Father kept that ledger as insurance, leverage, a weapon he never meant to fire. And tonight I fired it into the one friendship I had. And tomorrow night I'll sort the cache again, by lamplight, and my fingers will find more blocks, and my eyes will do what eyes do, and there are forty families' worth of names left unread in that drawer.

Somewhere in them, sooner or later, will be someone else Tamsin loves. Someone else I do. The ledger doesn't care. Lead never does — it just waits for the hand that strikes it, and takes the shape of whatever that hand is honest enough to admit.

I reached the foundry door and stopped. One floor up, behind the frosted glass, a candle was moving in the front room — one of the soldiers, awake past midnight, pacing. Emmerich, probably; he paced when he couldn't sleep, and he couldn't sleep most nights lately, and had begun looking at me like a man composing a question.

I stood in the snow and watched his shadow cross and recross the glass, and thought: *everyone in this town is holding something they haven't decided whether to use.*

The candle went out. I went in.
