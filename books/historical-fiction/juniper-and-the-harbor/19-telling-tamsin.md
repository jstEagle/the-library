## 19. Telling Tamsin

There is a particular cruelty in knowing a thing about someone you love, and I had been carrying mine for three days now, folded up inside me like the type blocks sewn into my coat — small, hard, and heavy far beyond their weight.

I had rehearsed the telling perhaps forty times. In the rehearsal versions, I was gentle. I was wise. I led Tamsin to the truth the way you lead a horse across ice, one careful step at a time. In none of the rehearsals did I simply blurt it out over cold tea in the harbormaster's back room while her father's boots thundered overhead and the fog pressed against the window like something eavesdropping.

"Your brother's name is in the ledger," I said. "Aldous Renner. Listed as an informant. Four entries."

The tea in her cup did not move. Her hands were that steady. It was her face that came apart — slowly at first, like ice on the harbor in March, cracking along lines no one could see until the whole surface gave way.

* * *

I should go back. I should tell it properly, because the way a thing is told matters almost as much as the thing itself, and my father taught me that too, though he never knew he was teaching it. Every name he cut into those blocks, every reversed letter waiting for ink to make it legible — he was a man who believed that how you record a thing determines what the thing becomes. A list can be a memorial or an indictment depending on who holds the paper.

So: three days earlier. The tollhouse behind us, my hands still smelling of cooper's pitch from the disguise, and Tamsin waiting for me in the back room with a falsified manifest folded into her apron and a question in her eyes that I had been dodging since the bridge checkpoint.

She wanted to know what else was in the ledger. She had earned the right to ask; she'd forged her father's hand twice by then, and discovery of either forgery would mean transportation at best. She had bought her knowledge honestly, with risk. That's what made the telling so unforgivable — not that I would hurt her, but that I owed her so much already and the debt was about to grow teeth.

"The whole ledger," she said. "You've read all of it by now. Every block."

"Most of it."

"Then you know if there are people in Grayharbor who talked." She said it lightly, the way you'd ask about weather in another county. "Informants. Collaborators. Whatever we're calling them this winter."

And I looked at her — this girl who had crossed a frozen town at night to warn me off the bridge, who kept two sets of books under her father's nose, who laughed at my jokes even when they weren't good — and I thought: *she already knows.* Sisters always half-know. Some part of her had been standing at that door for years, listening, and she wanted me to tell her she was wrong.

She wasn't wrong.

* * *

"I need to hear it plainly," she said now, in the present, her voice coming from somewhere very far away. "Not coded. Not in your foundry language. Plainly."

"Aldous passed information to the army liaison office. Dates of arrivals. Names associated with certain shipments. There are four entries spanning eighteen months, ending—" I checked the memory against the block I'd decoded at midnight, holding the metal to lamplight until the reversed letters swam upright. "Ending last February. The last entry has a payment notation beside it."

"How much?"

"Tamsin—"

"*How much?*"

"Eleven pounds," I said. "Ten shillings."

She set down the cup. She set it down very carefully, the way our father used to set down type cases, as though the world had become something that could shatter if handled carelessly. Then she stood and walked to the window and stood there with her back to me, and I counted her breaths because counting was the only useful thing left in the room.

Eighteen months of entries. Eighteen months before that, of Aldous coming to dinner, of him teaching me knots, of him laughing at Father's stories. Eighteen months during which Tamsin had written to him twice and received one reply. And underneath all of it, the arithmetic I couldn't escape: I had known for three days. Three days of choosing my comfort over her right to the truth, and calling it *timing.*

"You're certain it's him," she said to the window. "Not someone with the same name."

"There's a description in the margin. *Harbormaster's son. Stutters when lying.*"

That got a sound out of her that might have been a laugh in some other century, in some other town where brothers didn't sell names for eleven pounds ten.

"He stuttered worse after Mother died," she said. "He worked at it for years. Practiced in the mirror. Did you know that? He'd stand there for an hour some nights, saying the hard words over and over until they came out smooth." She turned around. Her eyes were dry, which frightened me more than tears would have. "He made himself smooth, Cyrus. For *this.*"

"I don't know why he did it. Whether they pressured him, or—"

"Don't." One word, quiet as a knife going into a sheath. "Don't build excuses for him. I'll do that work myself if I choose to, later, when I've earned it. But you don't get to do it for me, and neither does he."

I have thought about that sentence many times since. *When I've earned it.* As though forgiveness were a wage and not a gift — as though it were something you labored toward, shift by shift, like a cutter at her bench. I thought forgiveness was supposed to be a feeling that arrived, warm and complete, like spring. Tamsin taught me it is closer to a trade. You learn it the way I learned letters: badly at first, then by rote, then by heart, and even then only if you practice it daily for the rest of your life.

"All right," I said. "No excuses."

"Good." She sat down again. Picked up the cup. Her hands had begun to shake now that the steadiness wasn't needed for performance, and she watched them shake with a kind of clinical interest, as if they belonged to a stranger. "Now tell me the rest. There's a rest. You've been sitting on something else for three days, and I want all of it, because apparently we're doing honesty this winter, God help us."

The rest was this: his name appeared not once but in connection with the print circle's movements. The two arrested printers — Hale and Marlowe, whose shop was ash and whose fate nobody spoke above a whisper — had changed locations three times in the autumn. Each change was recorded in the ledger, each preceded by an entry in Aldous's hand-code. He hadn't merely informed. He had tracked them.

"He didn't know about the fire," Tamsin said flatly.

"No. Almost certainly not. Informers are told as little as possible. That's standard—"

"Don't tell me what's *standard.*" She closed her eyes. When she opened them again, something had rearranged itself behind them — not acceptance, nothing so gentle, but a kind of triage. I watched her sort her brother into a different category in real time, the way a harbormaster sorts cargo: this stays, this goes, this must be weighed separately.

"Here is what happens now," she said. "I am not going to stop helping you. The manifests, the passes, all of it. That continues, and you will never thank me for it again, because thanks would make it a favor and I need it to be arithmetic. Do you understand? It has to be numbers. If it's feelings I will drown in it."

"Understood."

"Second. My brother is not my brother anymore. He is a man I share a father with, and a history, and possibly a grave someday, but the word *brother* is suspended until further notice. I am not striking him from any record. I'm not erasing him. I'm just..." She searched for the word and I watched her find it, watched it cost her. "*Quarantining* him. Setting him aside so his rot doesn't spread to everything else I hold."

"That seems—"

"It doesn't matter how it seems." She drank the cold tea as if it were medicine. "Third, and this is the part you're going to argue with. I want to see the entry. The actual block. Tonight."

"Why?"

"Because I have spent three days being lied to by omission, and I will not spend one more hour taking anyone's word, including yours, including his, including God's, about what Aldous Renner did. I want to hold the evidence myself. I want to read his crime in metal, the way you read everything, and I want to know it in my hands." She looked at me. "That's what you do, isn't it? That's what cutting type teaches. Truth isn't true until it's fixed in a form that can't take it back."

It is exactly what cutting type teaches. I had simply never heard it said aloud.

* * *

We went to the foundry after midnight. This was madness, of course — soldiers billeted in the front rooms, Emmerich asleep somewhere with his rifle near his hand, Vane's men walking rounds past the windows on a schedule I knew down to my own pulse. But there was no arguing with her, and I had discovered by then that most of what I called prudence was actually fear wearing a respectable coat, and I was trying, that winter, to learn the difference.

I lit the lamp in the sorting room with its shuttered window and brought out the case of suspect blocks. Tamsin stood over the bench like a surgeon over a table.

"Show me how it works."

I laid four blocks in a composing stick, mirror-wise, dusted them with lampblack, pressed paper down with the roller. The impressions rose up reversed and then, in the glass, reversed again to rightness: dates, sums, initials, and there, rendered in my father's careful, merciless hand —

*RENNER, A. — harbormaster's son — 3 Feb. — manifest, brig* Lark *— 11£ 10s.*

Tamsin read it. Read it twice. Then she did the thing I will remember until I die: she took off her glove and put her bare fingers on the block, on the raised metal of his name, and held them there until I feared she'd mark herself.

"Cold," she said. "It's just cold metal. All that, and it's just cold metal."

"Yes."

"My father logs every ship that leaves this harbor. Every one, for six years, into the army's book, because they pay him and because refusing would be suspicious and because it's easier." Her thumb moved across the letters. "Is he in your ledger too?"

I hesitated one breath too long, and she saw it.

"He is," she said. "But not as an informant. As a *source*, isn't he? Your father recorded that the harbormaster's logs exist, that they could be had. That's different. That's potential, not betrayal."

"Yes."

"And my brother converted. From potential to practice." She replaced her glove, finger by finger, unhurried. "One decision apart, the two of them. Just one decision, made in a warm room instead of a cold one."

We stood there in the lamplight among twelve years of silent metal, and I understood that I was watching her do labor — actual physical-seeming labor, the muscles of her face working, her breath deliberately slowed — the labor of building a wall inside herself between the boy who taught her knots and the man who sold printers to the army. Forgiveness, I saw then, was not even the right word for what she was attempting. What she was doing was harder. She was refusing hatred without granting absolution. She was keeping love and judgment in the same house without letting either evict the other.

"I'm going to keep forging the manifests," she said finally. "Because forty families shouldn't burn for what my family helped light. And when this is over — whatever 'over' means — I'm going to find my brother, and I'm going to ask him one question. Just one. Not *why*. Why is a coward's question. I'm going to ask him whether he ever once, in eighteen months, thought of warning even one person." She picked up her cup of nothing, remembered it was empty, set it down again. "And then I'll decide what he gets to be to me. Not before."

"And until then?"

"Until then he's a stranger who knows where I sleep." She turned to the door, then stopped. "Cyrus. One more thing, and this is a condition, not a request. You don't carry this for me anymore. No more three-day silences. No more deciding what I can bear. We're past that, you and I. Whatever you learn — whoever it damns — I hear it within a day, or our arrangement ends and you can walk your blocks to the harbor yourself."

"Within a day," I agreed.

"Say it as if you mean it."

"I mean it."

"Good." At the door she paused again, and for a moment the old Tasmance flickered through — dry, wry, unquenchable. "Eleven pounds ten. He always was a poor negotiator. Father haggles the fishmongers down harder than that."

And then she was gone into the dark, and I sat alone with my lamp and my ledger of sins, and I felt — not relief, nothing so clean — but the specific exhaustion that follows honest work done badly late and paid for in advance.

* * *

Two days later she brought me the new manifest for the Juniper, flawless, and slid it across the bench without comment. But beneath it, in her own hand, in ink, she had written a single line:

*For the record: I know what he did, and I am here anyway.*

I looked up. She was watching the soldiers in the yard, her face composed, her jaw set like a woman hauling on a rope that would not come in.

"They'll search departures soon," she said quietly. "Vane's clerks have been copying my father's departure logs all week. Every ship, every cargo. He's looking for something, Cyrus, and I don't think it's contraband." She met my eyes. "I think he's counting ships against cargoes, and the Juniper doesn't balance. Whatever you're planning for New Year's Eve — you should plan faster."

Outside, snow began to fall on Grayharbor, softening the rooftops, filling the tracks of every boot that had ever crossed the yard, erasing nothing.
