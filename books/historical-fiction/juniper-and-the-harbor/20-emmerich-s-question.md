## 20. Emmerich's Question

There is a particular hour in a requisitioned house when the soldiers who sleep in your father's workshop have all stopped snoring, and the one on the stairs has stopped coughing, and the house belongs to you again — or to whatever version of you has learned to move without sound across floorboards that used to creak under your own feet when you were nine and careless.

I had come to think of that hour as mine by right of conquest. The army had taken the foundry; I had taken three in the morning. It seemed a fair trade, or would have been if trades were ever fair.

That night I was working on the seal.

The seal was the hardest thing I had ever cut, and I had once cut a lowercase g so fine it made a printer in Kingsport weep into his apron. A seal is not type. Type is honest work: letters are letters, reversed for pressing but true in their shapes. A seal is a lie with a border around it. It must be wrong in exactly the way the real one is wrong, because every official stamp carries its little imperfections like moles on a face, and anyone who knows the face will know a stranger wearing it.

I had borrowed the impression from a shipping manifest Tamsin had lifted from her father's office — pressed in wax gone brittle with age, but legible enough. For six nights I had cut away at a block of soft pewter, working by the light of a shuttered lamp, holding my breath through the parts that required breath-holding, which was most of them.

I was perhaps four minutes from finishing when the stair board sang.

Not creaked — sang. Emmerich's tread had a note to it I'd learned the way you learn a neighbor's dog: the third step from the bottom always gave a low complaint, and only Emmerich came down the stairs heavy-footed and guilty about it, the way young men do when they're sneaking to the privy or stealing bread. Everyone else in the billet moved like they'd been taught moving was a crime.

I had perhaps eight seconds. Eight seconds to close the lamp, sweep the pewter block and graver into the hollowed case beneath the sorting bench, and arrange myself into a portrait of a daughter unable to sleep, sitting at her dead trade out of grief and habit. I had practiced the arrangement before. Grief and habit are excellent cover stories because no one can check them.

He came around the bench with his candle raised, and I saw his face do the thing faces do when they find something they weren't looking for — the small collapse, the recalibration. He looked at me. He looked at the lamp, which I had closed too neatly, its shutter aligned, its warmth still rising off the glass like a confession.

"You're burning oil," he said. "At this hour."

"My eyes ache," I said, "unless I'm working. It's a cutter's affliction. My father has it too." This was true, which is the best kind of lie to build a house on. Father did have the affliction. He simply hadn't been permitted to indulge it since men in gray coats nailed a notice to our door.

Emmerich set his candle down on the bench, which was either politeness or a decision to stay, and looked at the sorting cases — at the one case, I noticed with a lurch, whose lid sat a half-finger proud of its neighbors, because in my hurry I had not seated it flush.

He saw it too. I watched him see it. His eyes went there, stayed a moment, and then — and this is the part I still turn over like a stone to see what's underneath — he looked away. Deliberately. The way a man looks away from a beggar he means to give nothing to, except that what he was declining to see was me.

"The third step," I said, because silence felt more dangerous than talk. "It announces you every time."

"I know." He almost smiled. "I've started taking it as practice. Someday someone will be listening who matters, and I'll want to be warned early."

"And am I someone who matters?"

He considered this with a seriousness that would have been funny in anyone else and was merely disarming in him. "You're someone who works at three in the morning," he said, "in a shop full of metal that makes words. In my experience, those people matter more than the ones giving orders."

I should have ended the conversation there. Instead I reached for the lamp — a mistake, an instinct, a woman tidying — and his hand came down over mine on the shutter.

"May I?" he asked, and opened it himself.

The lamplight fell across the bench, across my hands, across the tray where I kept my gravers lined up like surgical instruments, and across — God help me — the faint dust of pewter filings I had not swept, glittering in the seam between two boards like frost.

Emmerich looked at the filings for a long time.

I want to record here what I felt, because later I could never quite reconstruct it. Not fear, exactly. Fear is a clean animal thing; it runs or it freezes. What I felt was closer to the sensation of standing on ice and hearing it speak — that long, patient groan that tells you the water below has opinions about your weight. I had lied to Vane, lied to checkpoints, lied fluently and well, and each lie had cost me something I couldn't name, a small coin paid out from a purse I couldn't inventory. I had thought the purse bottomless. Standing there with pewter dust on my fingers and a boy soldier's hand an inch from evidence enough to hang us both, I discovered the purse had a bottom after all, and I could feel it.

"You're wondering," Emmerich said quietly, "whether I'm going to report this."

"The thought had crossed."

"I've been posted here eleven weeks. Do you know how many smugglers I've caught?"

"None," I guessed.

"One," he said. "A boy of sixteen running brandy in a fish cart. I reported him because it was my duty, and they took him to the tollhouse yard, and a sergeant broke his fingers with a musket stock while I stood at attention, because that was also duty." He turned the candle in his hands, watching the wax shiver. "Since then I have developed a professional blindness. It's remarkable how much a man can fail to notice when failing to notice is the kind thing. The army calls it negligence. I've started calling it mercy, and I sleep better than the sergeant does."

The filings sat between us, glittering, unremarked-upon.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked.

And here he surprised me, though I suppose by then he had earned the right to. He didn't answer right away. He pulled the stool from beneath the bench — my stool, my father's stool, worn to the shape of Underhill spines for forty years — and sat down in it like a man entering a church he wasn't sure welcomed him.

"Because I've been wanting to ask someone a question," he said, "and everyone I could ask would give me the answer they'd been issued."

"What question?"

He looked at the type wall — at the hundred shallow drawers of letters that had been my whole childhood, each compartment labeled in my father's hand, each one a small wooden grave for thousands of identical souls waiting to be arranged into sense.

"What is it for?" he said. "The war. The occupation. All of it. I marched here behind a drum in September believing I knew, and now it's nearly February and I stand at a gate checking papers for men who print pamphlets, and I break boys' fingers over brandy, and I sleep in a house full of beautiful machines that make sentences, and I cannot — " He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Nobody asks *why* anymore. They ask *who*. Who's loyal, who's suspect, who needs watching. Nobody asks what any of it is for."

I should tell you what I wanted to say. What I wanted to say was: *It is for power, Emmerich. It is for the convenience of men who mistake their ledgers for the world. It is for nothing, the way a fire is for nothing; it burns because fuel and air got together and nobody put it out.* I wanted to say: *Your army seized a town of fishermen and typesetters because we are near a harbor and far from anyone who loves us.* I wanted to say all of it, the whole sermon, and watch him carry it back to the barracks like a lit fuse.

But I had learned something in twelve years of being a name that wasn't mine: that the truths people can hear are smaller than the truths you want to say, and a truth sized correctly does more work than a truth shouted.

So instead I said: "Can I show you something?"

He nodded. I rose, careful of my hands, careful of the case with its proud lid, and took from the wall drawer marked *Q* a handful of sorts — the letter Q, a dozen of them, dull leaden things. I poured them into his palm.

"Hold them," I said.

He held them. "They're heavy."

"They're lead. Everything worth saying is heavy; that's why we make it out of the densest metal we can afford." I took one sort back and held it up. "Now. This letter has never been in a sentence yet. It's just a Q. Worthless. Worth maybe a farthing in raw metal." I set it down and picked up another. "This one was in the word *quality* in a broadside advertising herring, last spring. Still nearly worthless." Third sort. "This one was in a pamphlet. *No taxation without representation* — that phrase passed through this shop in October. The man who bought the type was hanged in November for printing it."

Emmerich looked at the little gray letter in my fingers as if it might go off.

"Same piece of lead," I said. "Same weight, same shape, same metal. The only difference is what sentence somebody put it in. That's the whole craft, Emmerich. Letters don't mean anything until they're arranged, and then they mean everything. And the war — " I set the sort gently back in his palm and folded his fingers over it. "The war is a very large sentence. Somebody powerful arranged it. And everybody in it thinks they're the meaning."

He stared at his closed fist.

"That's not an answer," he said finally.

"No," I agreed. "It's better than one. Answers end conversations. That will keep you asking for the rest of your life, which is what questions are for."

For a moment neither of us moved, and the lamp hissed softly, and somewhere above us a soldier rolled over in his blankets, and the whole enormous machine of the occupation ticked on around two people sitting in a type foundry sharing a stolen hour and a stolen metaphor.

Then Emmerich opened his fist, looked at the Q, and — I will swear to this on anything you like — slipped it into his breast pocket.

"For the sentence I haven't found yet," he said.

"It'll find you," I said. "They usually do. Usually at inconvenient hours."

"Like three in the morning?"

"Exactly like three in the morning."

He stood, took up his candle, and paused at the edge of the lamplight. "Your lamp," he said. "I noticed nothing tonight. Oil allowance in this quarter is generous; no one counts a widow's-waster like yours." Then, awkwardly, formally, the way he'd probably been taught to address officers: "Good night, Miss Underhill."

"Cyrus," I said, before I could stop myself.

He blinked. "Sir?"

"My father is Mr. Underhill. I'm — " And here I did something I hadn't done aloud in years, offered the name to a stranger like a coin across a counter. "Call me Cyrus. Everyone does."

"Cyrus," he repeated, tasting it, finding it strange, accepting it anyway. "Good night, Cyrus."

His tread went back up the stairs. The third step sang. This time I found I liked hearing it.

* * *

I did not finish the seal that night. I sat for a long while with my hands flat on the bench, feeling the wood's cold come up through my palms like a message from the building itself.

Here is what troubled me, and I have tried to be honest in this account even where honesty costs: Emmerich had seen. Not suspected — *seen*. The proud lid, the filings, the lamp burning at the hour when liars work. He had assembled it in eight seconds, quicker than Vane would have, quicker than any checkpoint soldier, and he had chosen — freely, without bargain or bribe — to un-see it. I had spent weeks learning to buy passage: a name erased for Tamsin here, a forged manifest there, every kindness invoiced. And this boy had handed me mercy off the books, gratis, and asked for nothing but a question answered.

It unsettled me more than a search would have. A search you can survive with skill. Generosity you have to survive with character, and I had no inventory of that.

I thought of my father in the cells across the harbor, and of the cipher key I still hadn't cracked — the last blocks, the ones whose impressions refused all my substitutions, as if the final lock of the ledger had been built to open only from a side of the door I hadn't found yet. Father had taught me everything about letters except the one lesson left, and he was behind bars, and the century was turning whether the Juniper sailed or not.

And I thought about what Emmerich had said — *nobody asks what it's for, they ask who* — and I understood, sitting alone in the dark among ten thousand patient letters, that I had given him the truest sentence I owned. We are all sorts in a case, waiting to be arranged. The only freedom any of us gets is choosing, sometimes, against every instruction, which sentence we'll agree to stand in.

I swept the pewter filings into my palm and scattered them out the window into the snow, where by morning they'd be indistinguishable from everything else that fell on Grayharbor.

Two days later, the notices went up on the harbor gate: all departures suspended pending inspection, passes to bear countersigns issued weekly, no exceptions. Tamsin came to the shop with the paper crushed in her fist and her face the color of the fog outside, and she said only, "Cyrus. They've changed the locks on us. Every one of us."

Behind her, at the gate ledger I could see through the window, a clerk in a gray coat was writing — and beside him, hatless in the cold, stood Aldous Vane, reading the harbor traffic like a man reading a book he had written.
