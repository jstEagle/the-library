## 21. Checkpoint Three: The Gate

There is a particular sound a plan makes when it begins to die. It is not dramatic. No trumpet, no crack of ice. It is quieter than that — the sound of a countersign you cannot forge being read aloud by a soldier who does not know he is reading your future.

I heard it on a Tuesday, standing in the fish-market crowd with a basket of herring I had no intention of eating, watching a gray-coated corporal at the harbor gate turn over a cooper's pass three times, frown at it, and call for his sergeant.

The cooper was a man named Pell who had done nothing wrong in his life except be in the wrong queue. He stood with his cap in his hands while the sergeant compared the pass against a sheet of paper pinned to a board — a new sheet, I noted, a *new* sheet, and my stomach performed a slow revolution like a barrel rolling downhill.

"Countersign's changed," the sergeant said, not unkindly. Cooper Pell looked as though he'd been told the sea had changed.

"It was 'harbor' last week," Pell said. "My brother-in-law told me—"

"Your brother-in-law can come tell me himself." The sergeant tore the pass in half, wrote out a new one, and sent Pell through with a look of apology that was almost worse than cruelty would have been. Then he turned back to his board and pasted up another notice beside the countersign schedule: **BY ORDER OF COLONEL HALLOWAY, ALL GATE PASSES REQUIRE DAILY COUNTERSIGN, CHANGED AT DAWN, KNOWN ONLY TO GATE OFFICERS AND THE PROVOST'S CLERK.**

*The provost's clerk.* Aldous Vane. Of course. The man had the patience of a spider and roughly the same attitude toward flies.

I bought the herring anyway. It seemed wise to have a reason for standing there.

* * *

Here is what had happened, pieced together over the following two days from Tamsin, from gossip, from the particular quality of silence soldiers wear when something has gone wrong for them: four nights ago, a man named Ansel Roake — a fisherman, a smuggler of modest ambitions, known to every dockside child as a source of tobacco and terrible jokes — had tried to leave Grayharbor by water rather than by gate. He had bribed a pilot, loaded his boat at the north stairs after dark, and gotten perhaps two hundred yards off the mole before the army's new patrol cutter found him.

They did not hang him. That was the frightening part. Hanging would have been simple grief. Instead they made a lesson of him: three days in the stocks in Market Square, then transportation, and his boat burned on the beach where everyone could watch the tar catch. And because an escape attempt is never treated as one man's folly but as evidence of a system — of *sympathizers*, of a network — the army tightened everything. Gate hours shortened. Searches doubled. And the passes, which had been annoying, became impossible.

A pass could be forged. I know; I had forged several, and I will say without false modesty that they were good. A name, a purpose, a seal, a scrawl — all of that lives in metal and ink, and I could make any of it sing. But a countersign that changes every dawn, written fresh each morning by an officer's hand and known to perhaps six men alive, cannot be cut into type. There is no lead thick enough for that kind of ignorance.

"You look like your dog died," Tamsin said when I came to the harbormaster's office that evening. Her father was at supper; we had twenty minutes and a fire that did more smoking than burning.

"Worse. My plan died. Dogs come back."

She listened while I laid it out. She has a way of listening that makes you more honest than you intended — she doesn't nod encouragingly or frown sympathetically, she just watches, and so you find yourself telling her true things in self-defense.

"So the gate is shut," she said finally.

"The gate is shut unless someone reads those countersigns before dawn and carries them to me."

"Someone would hang."

"Yes."

We let the fire pop. Somewhere below us a winch complained about a cargo it didn't like.

"My father writes them down," Tamsin said at last, very quietly, in the voice of a woman stepping off a ledge to see what happens. "The daily countersigns. He logs every departure, every pass number, every word the garrison sends him. It's all in the harbor book, Cyrus. In his own hand. On his own desk." She looked at me. "You understand what you're asking me to do."

"I'm not asking—"

"You're sitting there with your face asking." She rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. "Copying a countersign is forgery next to what my brother's done, and I helped you already, so I suppose my soul stopped being negotiable some weeks ago and nobody told me." She stood. "Come back Thursday. My father drinks on Thursdays. He sleeps like ballast."

And that should have been the end of the problem — walls opening instead of closing, thanks to a friend who measured her courage in ounces and paid in pounds. But this is Grayharbor, in the year of our Lord 1792, and nothing here finishes cleanly.

* * *

Thursday did not come quickly enough.

In between, the foundry had its own troubles, which is to say it had Emmerich and three other soldiers billeted behind the type racks, and it had my father, or the space where my father used to be, which the army still called a prisoner and I still called Papa when the lamps were low and no one could tax me for softness.

It also had visitors. Word travels fast in a small town that a shop is under suspicion, and faster still among men whose trade is suspicion. Twice that week a clerk in army gray appeared at the door with questions dressed as small talk. How many apprentices did the shop keep? Had we ever supplied the print circle on Chandler's Lane? Did we cast matrices to order, and might we show the process?

I lied to both of them. I have become, I am sorry to report, quite good at it. There is a version of myself that appears when a gray coat walks in — pleasant, dull, slightly tired, a tradesman's daughter with ink under her nails and no thoughts deeper than the price of tin. She talks about alloy ratios. She complains charmingly about her late hours. She is, I suspect, unforgettable in precisely the wrong way, which is why I ration her.

But on Friday evening the third visitor came, and he did not ask questions at all.

He was not Vane. He was younger, a captain by his cuffs, with the clean scrubbed look of a man who has been transferred to a cold posting and intends to resent it efficiently. He stood in the doorway of the foundry while Emmerich straightened to attention behind him, and he surveyed our racks of type the way a man surveys livestock he has been ordered to count but suspects are lying about their number.

"Underhill & Sons," he read from a paper. "One son?"

"One apprentice, sir," I said. "Me. Cyrus."

"Mm." He did not ask about the discrepancy, which meant he knew about the discrepancy, which meant somewhere in an office a file about my family was growing thicker while I slept. "Your ledgers were seized in October. Your premises are inventoried weekly. And yet." He folded the paper with great care. "Yet ships keep leaving this port with cargo that matches no manifest. Printers in other towns keep receiving type with a familiar hand in its serifs. And a certain gentleman of the provost's office believes that answers are still walking around inside these walls wearing an apron."

"I wouldn't know anything about that, sir. I cut letters."

"Yes," he said softly. "So you do." He set a folded square of paper on my workbench and opened it. Inside lay a broken shard of type — a fragment, cracked across its body, the face intact. A capital letter, reversed, waiting to bite paper. I knew it the way you know your own reflection in a dark window: it was from the coded ledger. One of the blocks. Not one of mine — one of my father's, old, the alloy grayer, cut in some winter before I was trusted with the graver.

Where he got it I could guess. The burned print shop. The ashes had been sifted; Vane had recovered fragments once before, and now the fragments had multiplied.

"This came out of a printer's ruin," the captain said. "It doesn't spell anything. We've pressed impressions of it forty ways and it reads as gibberish — or as cipher. Your opinion? You're a professional."

My heart was doing honest work behind a dishonest face. "Looks like damaged sort, sir. Bad casting. We melt failures down."

"Do you." He watched me for a long moment, and I gave him back the dull tradesman's daughter, and eventually — because he was young and cold and wanted his supper more than he wanted my ruin — he believed me, or decided to pretend to. He left the fragment on the bench.

"Colonel Halloway wants the shops inventoried again. All of them. New Year's Eve, when the whole town will be drunk in the streets and unable to complain about inconvenience." He pulled his gloves on. "Happy century, Master Underhill."

When the door closed, Emmerich looked at me. He has a face that has not yet learned to hide things, and it was hiding nothing: he had heard every word, and he was afraid, and the fear was not for himself.

"He knows," Emmerich said quietly.

"He suspects. There's a difference, and the difference is proof, and he hasn't got it."

"He'll get it."

"Then I'll have to be faster than he is." I pocketed the fragment when the other soldiers weren't looking. Later, in the dark of my room, I held it to the lamp and read its impression against the cipher key my father built into my own name, and what I found there made the room colder: the block was not from the cache I had been moving. It was older. It was one of the *first* entries, cut twelve years ago, in the winter my mother died.

Which meant either my father had scattered more of the ledger than I knew —

— or someone else had carried a piece out of this shop, long ago, and lost it in a fire, and that piece was now sitting in the provost's office teaching Aldous Vane how to read my family's handwriting.

* * *

Thursday came at last. Tamsin delivered.

I will not write here what her hands looked like when she passed me the copied countersigns, folded inside a laundry list, or how carefully casual her voice was, or how she laughed too loudly at nothing on her way out the door. Some debts are better kept unwritten; writing them makes them real, and real debts can be called. But she gave me dawn words for seven days running, and with them the gate opened — in theory.

In practice, theory is where plans go to be humiliated.

I tested the first countersign myself, on a small errand, as a cooper's delivery girl with flour on my sleeves and a pass signed by a dead man whose signature I cut better than he ever wrote it. The gate took me. The second day, same. I began — foolishly, wickedly — to believe.

On the third morning, the sergeant at the gate held my pass an extra moment.

"Cooper's girl," he said. "You've been through twice this week."

"Flour doesn't deliver itself, sir."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "It doesn't. And now it'll wait while you step aside, because orders changed this morning too. Every repeat traveler gets searched. Every one. No exceptions below the rank of major." He waved me to the side shed with the courtesy of a man who has done this a hundred times and will do it a hundred more, and I spent eleven minutes — I counted — being patted down by a bored soldier while my flour, my pass, my dead man's signature, and my heartbeat sat in a pile on a table.

They found nothing. There was nothing to find that trip; I had been careful, carrying only the test itself. They waved me through, and I walked out onto the quay with my legs shaking and my face arranged into mild boredom, and I understood the shape of the trap entirely.

The countersigns were solvable. Tamsin had solved them. But the searches were now random, frequent, and thorough, and no amount of cleverness moves sewn lead through a stranger's hands eleven minutes at a time, week after week. The ledger was too heavy to carry in one piece and too dangerous to split carelessly. Every passage risked the whole war on one bored soldier's fingertips.

The plan hadn't stalled all at once. It had stalled the way a tide goes out — imperceptibly, then undeniably, leaving the hull sitting in mud.

* * *

That night I climbed to the loft above the casting room, where the hollowed type cases live beneath a false floor that Emmerich walks over twice a day without knowing he is walking over the only thing in Grayharbor that matters. I sat among them with the lamp turned low and did arithmetic.

New Year's Eve was nineteen days away. Osei sailed on New Year's Eve or not at all — the tides, the patrols, the window of celebration when the garrison would be half-drunk. Nineteen days to move the rest of the ledger past a gate that now searched repeat travelers, demanded countersigns that lived and died at dawn, and would, per Captain Clean-Cuffs, be conducting a full inventory of every shop in town on the very night I needed to move.

Including this one. Including the loft. Including the false floor Emmerich steps over.

Unless the ledger wasn't here anymore by then. Unless it moved in pieces so ordinary — crates, freight, laundry, coal — that no search would trouble itself. Unless someone else carried half of it out through the harbor book and someone else's hands, and I carried the rest myself, through the gate, on the night, wearing whatever mask fit.

Half here, half gone. If either failed, the other survived.

It was a cold thought and I warmed my hands on it.

Below me, one of the soldiers laughed at something in a letter from home. Above me, snow had begun again, the soft gray kind that erases the town by morning. Somewhere across the roofs the cells held my father, and somewhere along the quay the Juniper rode at anchor, patient as a debt.

Nineteen days. And then, as if the year itself had been listening and wished to inform me that walls close from all sides at once, boots on the stair — two pairs, unhurried, official — and Emmerich's voice, pitched carefully flat, calling up to me through the floor:

"Cyrus. Someone's here to see you. It's the clerk. It's Vane."
