## 22. Vane Closes In

The morning the soldiers came for my father, I was upstairs mending a coat seam that had four blocks of lead sewn into its lining, and I did not hear the knock. This is the detail I have never forgiven myself for: not the arrest, not the confession, not even the look on his face when they walked him past me — but the seam. My hands were full of thread while history knocked at our door, and it had to knock twice.

Tamsin brought the warning first, breathless, her cap askew in a way her father would have boxed her ears for.

"Vane's on his way to you," she said. "He's got men with him. Four. And a box."

"What kind of box?"

"The kind you carry things in when you mean to keep them." She gripped my wrist. "Cyrus. They pulled fragments out of the Ashworth print shop ashes. Charred type. He's been matching pieces all week — I heard him tell my father so over supper two nights ago. He's been *matching* them."

Matching. Of course he had been. Aldous Vane was the sort of clerk who counted other people's letters for pleasure. I thought of the burned shop on Cooper's Lane, the way the crowd had stood and watched it go up and then turned home to their suppers as though fire were simply weather. I had watched too. I had thought: there but for a ledger's thickness.

"You should run," Tamsin said.

"Where? The gate needs countersigns I can't forge yet. If I run today, they take me today." I tucked the last block deeper into the coat lining and felt it press against my ribs like a second heart. "If I stay, maybe they only take him."

I said *maybe*. God forgive me the arithmetic of that word.

* * *

Vane arrived the way winter arrives — no drama, just a gradual chilling of the room he stood in. He had removed his hat, which I had learned was his signature courtesy, the thing he did before doing damage. His gray coat was brushed. His gloves were folded over one arm. Behind him, four soldiers arranged themselves in our workshop with the unconscious tact of furniture being moved in.

"Mistress Underhill," he said. We had settled long ago into the fiction that I was the master's daughter and nothing more; Vane had never once, in all his visits, let his gaze linger on my hands the way the tollhouse soldier had. Whether this was blindness or patience I still do not know. With Vane it was always impossible to tell whether you were being spared or saved for later.

"Master Vane."

"Is your father at home?"

"He's at the bench."

"Good. Then we needn't send for him." He set the box on my father's worktable — a plain pine box, unremarkable except that everyone in the room now orbited it without looking at it directly, the way sailors orbit a storm chart. "I regret the hour. I know how tradesmen guard their mornings."

My father came out from behind the sorting racks wiping his hands on his apron, and I watched something happen in his face that I have spent years trying to name. It was not fear. It was recognition — the expression of a man who has kept a debt ledger all his life and finally sees the collector coming up the walk. Relief and ruin in the same breath.

"Master Vane," my father said. "You've found us again."

"I find everyone eventually, Mr. Underhill. It is the whole of my profession, and I confess I love it poorly some days." Vane opened the box. Inside, nested in straw, lay perhaps thirty fragments of type — scorched, split, some fused into little metal grapes where the heat had melted a dozen sorts together. Even damaged, I could read them. That was the horror of my education: I could read anything cut in lead, including its burning.

"We recovered these from the Ashworth premises," Vane said. "Or what remained of it. Fire is thorough but not infallible. Metal survives flame better than paper does — a fact I imagine your trade knows better than most." He lifted one fragment with two fingers, delicate as a man handling an egg. "Now. Here is my difficulty. These sorts bear reversed impressions. Names. Dates. A cipher of some ingenuity, cut by someone who understood letterforms down to the beard and serif. Someone trained." He set the fragment back. "The Ashworths bought their type from this foundry for eleven years. So either they cut this ledger themselves — unlikely, as neither brother could spell *harbor* with the card in front of him — or the cutting was done elsewhere, by hands that knew what they were doing, and sold or given to them already finished."

The stove ticked. A soldier shifted his weight. Somewhere outside a cart went by, ordinary as bread, and I hated it for that.

"That's an interesting theory," my father said.

"It is not a theory, Mr. Underhill. It is an inventory." Vane's voice stayed gentle, which was somehow worse than shouting. "What I have is a theory is this: that somewhere in this building, or once in this building, exists a fuller version of this coded record. More names. More entries. Perhaps the master copy itself." He closed the box lid softly. "I do not want that master copy for myself, you understand. I am not a thief. I want it for the army, which is a different and more respectable appetite entirely."

"And if I told you no such thing exists?"

"Then I would believe you were protecting someone, and I would search anyway, and we would both have wasted a morning." Vane put his gloves back on, finger by finger. "Mr. Underhill. You are a man of some age who has spent his life among furnaces and hot metal. You know what happens to material under pressure. I am here to apply pressure. What shape the material takes afterward is — truly — up to the material."

I looked at my father. I looked at him the way you look at a lighthouse when your ship is already breaking on the rocks, wanting instruction, wanting rescue, wanting the one thing the light cannot do, which is reach down and hold you.

And my father looked back at me for exactly one second — one second, I have counted it ten thousand times — and in that second he did something I did not understand until much later. He smiled. Not warmly. It was the smile of a man setting type: precise, final, each character locked into its chase.

"There is no master copy," my father said, turning to Vane. "There is only me."

* * *

I want to render what followed honestly, because dishonesty about it would dishonor the thing itself, and because I have noticed that people tend to gild sacrifice after the fact, polishing it until you'd never guess how clumsy and human it was.

My father confessed. There is no better word and no softer one. He told Vane that the coded ledger was his own work, begun — he said — eleven years prior, cut sort by sort in the slack hours between commissions, maintained as insurance against creditors and, later, against precisely the kind of government that had come to Grayharbor wearing gray coats. He said he had supplied finished coded blocks to certain customers, the Ashworth brothers among them, as a service. He said the whole scheme was his: his idea, his hand, his guilt. He said, in a voice like a man reading a shipping manifest, that no member of his household had knowledge of it, that his apprentice — he called me his apprentice, though I had been that and everything else for twelve years — was a dull, honest girl who could cut a fair roman face and nothing more, and who believed her master spent his evenings on private commissions for collectors.

A dull, honest girl. I have been insulted by professionals since, and none of them ever managed anything so perfectly wounding.

Vane listened with his head tilted, the way a man listens for a false note in a bell. Twice he asked questions — whether any copies had left town by sea; whether the foundry had done work for the Juniper or any vessel of that class — and twice my father said no with such flatness that even I almost believed him. And then Vane asked the question I had been dreading for a month, the question I'd rehearsed answers to in the dark.

"The cipher, Mr. Underhill. Every cipher has a key. Where is yours kept?"

"In my head," my father said. "Where keys are best kept."

"You'll give it to me."

"No."

"No?"

"No." My father unknotted his apron, folded it, laid it on the bench with the unhurried care of a man closing shop for the night. "You can have my confession. You can have my person. You cannot have forty families' worth of names to hang in the town square, Master Vane, and I will tell you why, since you seem to appreciate reasons: because in five years, ten, whatever this madness turns out to be, someone will write the history of this winter, and I intend to be a footnote in it, not a chapter of denunciations. A man should leave behind the sort of record he can bear to have read aloud."

Something moved across Vane's face then — quickly, gone before it could be named, but I saw it, and I have wondered about it since. It might have been respect. In Aldous Vane I believe those two things were neighbors sharing a wall.

"You understand," Vane said quietly, "that refusing the key means I must treat you as a hostile asset rather than a cooperative subject. The cells are not comfortable in January."

"I have spent twelve years in a room with a furnace," my father said. "I expect the contrast will be instructive."

They took him at half past ten in the morning. He shook Vane's hand first — shook it! — and then he paused beside me on his way to the door, and he put his hand on the crown of my head the way he used to when I was small and he wanted me to feel blessed rather than bossed, and he said, in the voice he used for correcting my kerning:

"Mind the shop, Cyrus. Sorts don't distribute themselves."

That was all. Mind the shop. Twelve years of lessons compressed into six words, and every one of them a key he trusted me to find.

I minded the shop. I stood in the doorway and watched them walk him down Fish Street through the dirty snow, his hands bound in front of him, his back straight as a composing stick, and I minded the shop. The neighbors came out to watch, and every one of them looked at me and then away, away, away, the great synchronized aversion of Grayharbor, and I understood that I was watching my inheritance pass to me in real time: the silence, the looking-away, the survival. He had practiced it alone for twelve years so that I could practice it now, in public, without flinching.

I did not flinch. I wish I could say that pride was the feeling. It was closer to frostbite.

* * *

Vane stayed behind. Of course he did. The soldiers took my father; Vane took the workshop, walking it slowly with his gloved hands clasped behind him, pausing at the casting bench, the sorting racks, the bank of type cases along the north wall — the wall behind which, fourteen feet of floorboard and one false case away, my hollowed cases sat with their cargo of names.

"You'll want to close for a few days," he said. "People will talk less if the shutters are up."

"People will talk regardless. It's the chief industry of this town."

That startled a sound out of him — brief, rusty, possibly a laugh in its funeral clothes. He stopped at the window and looked out at the street where my father had just vanished around the corner.

"Do you know what I expected to find in this shop, Mistress Underhill? Years of imagining it, on the bad nights, when the work curdles — I built this place in my head. A den. Seditious presses in the cellar. Traitors' names filed alphabetically." He turned. "It's a foundry. It smells of smoke and pitch. Your father drinks tea. It is disappointingly like every other honest business in Grayharbor, and that disappointment, I promise you, is entirely his design."

"Will they hang him?" My voice came out level. I have rarely hated my own competence more.

"He refuses the key, so he goes to the cells until he doesn't. Armies are patient with old men in winter; it costs nothing." Vane collected his hat from the table. At the door he stopped, and did not quite look at me. "Mistress Underhill. When a craftsman takes the entire debt onto himself — every entry, every customer, every crime, real or invented — it usually means he is hiding something whose loss he cannot calculate. Something that outweighs a life." His eyes came to mine at last, mild as milk. "I have not yet decided what that is. But I have narrowed it down to this shop, or to you."

He put on his hat and left. The door clicked with the softness of a type slug seating in its stick.

I stood in the empty workshop a long while. The furnace ticked as it cooled. My father's apron lay folded on the bench, and his last sort was still locked in the composing stick where he'd left it that morning — I crossed the room and read it upside down, the way you read type, the way he'd taught me before I could read anything else.

It was the letter J. A single capital, cut in his own hand, sitting in the stick as if he'd meant to print something and changed his mind.

Or as if he'd left me a message the only way he knew how — in metal, reversed, waiting for the right pair of eyes.

J. Juniper. The ship. The deadline. New Year's Eve, nine days off, with the harbor gates sealed and the man who could have carried my father's freedom now carrying my father instead.

I took off my coat, unwrapped the four blocks from its lining, and began, with steady hands and a heart like a dropped anvil, to sort out exactly what a daughter inherits when her father leaves her nothing but silence and a deadline.

The answer, it turned out, was: everything.
