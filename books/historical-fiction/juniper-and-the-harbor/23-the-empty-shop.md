## 23. The Empty Shop

The soldiers took my father at ten in the morning, and by noon the army had taken everything else it wanted, and by two o'clock I was alone in a building I had lived in for twelve years that no longer belonged to anyone but the Republic, which is to say it belonged to nobody, which is to say it belonged to me in the way that a ghost belongs to a house.

I locked the door anyway. Habit. Spite. The lock was mine to turn even if nothing behind it was.

The foundry in winter afternoon light is a thing I could draw blind — and had, in a sense, drawn blind for twelve years, my hands knowing every case and rack and quoin without the trouble of looking. Now I looked. That was the difference. For twelve years I had moved through this shop the way water moves through a pipe: useful, contained, unseen. Now the pipe stood empty and the water had nowhere to be except wherever I was standing, which was the middle of the floor, holding a candle I did not need lit yet, watching dust come down through the light like slow gray snow.

They had left the furnace. They had left the type, most of it, because Aldous Vane's inventory men had catalogued it over three days in their beautiful clerk's hand and requisitioned it as *war material*, which would have been funny if it weren't so exactly true. Type *is* war material. My father taught me that before he taught me to read whole sentences. Every letter is a small soldier waiting in its case; give them to the right printer and you raise an army of ten thousand identical voices overnight. Vane understood it too. That was the whole horror of him. He was not a butcher playing at clerk. He was a clerk who understood that he was the dangerous one.

They had taken the ledgers, both real and imagined — the master ledger from the office, and three days ago, in shackles, my father's confession of having kept another. They had taken the correspondence drawer, the customer files, the bundle of proofs tied in string going back to '84. They had taken the portrait of my mother off the office wall, examined the back of it for hidden documents, found none, and left it face-down on the desk like a bird they'd decided not to pocket.

They had left me. Because I was, in the words of the requisition order, *the minor dependent of the household*, seventeen being still a minor when it was convenient to the army and fully grown and culpable whenever Vane should decide otherwise.

I set the candle down on the casting bench and turned the portrait right side up. My mother looked at me with that expression she has worn in oil for twenty years — half amusement, half appraisal, the look of a woman deciding whether you were worth her time. She had never once decided I wasn't. Everyone else in my life had managed to decide it without asking her.

"Well," I said to her. "Here we are."

The shop answered with the particular silence of a foundry at rest: not quiet but *held* breath, the whole room full of metal waiting to speak. People think a type foundry is silent. It isn't. It is a room where silence is manufactured, ingot by ingot, and stored in cases, and sold. Twelve years I had been the shop's finest product.

Here is what I mean by that, since we have time and the light is failing anyway.

My mother died in June of 1780, of a fever that came up the coast on a grain ship and went through Grayharbor like fire through dry grass. I was five. What I remember of her is mostly other people's remembering — my father's stories, told rarely and always the same way, worn smooth like sea glass. But I remember one thing myself, and I have checked it against my own memory so many times I can no longer tell whether it's a memory or a prayer: I remember her calling me Juniper. Not *little juniper*, not an endearment. My name. She was braiding my hair at the window over the harbor and she said, *Juniper, hold still,* and the name fit me the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

Six weeks later my father buried her and changed the key.

Not gradually. Not with explanations. One morning I was Juniper and by supper I was Cyrus, and when I asked — I must have asked, I must have cried about it, though mercifully that part has been sanded away — he crouched down to my height on this very floor, in this very light probably, and told me the only true thing he ever told me all at once instead of in pieces:

*Names are currency, Juniper. And we are poor now. So we will spend a false one, and keep the real one safe, and when you are grown you may spend your own.*

I waited twelve years for the growing. I cut type. I became Cyrus in the streets and Cyrus in the church register and Cyrus to Tamsin, who has known me six years and never once heard the other name, because my father's rule was absolute: the name lives in the house or it doesn't live. Even the print circle, who trusted us with their pamphlets and their necks, knew me as the Underhills' apprentice boy. I let them believe it. I corrected nobody. Do you know what it costs, correcting nobody? It costs nothing per day. It compounds.

And here is the thing I discovered standing in my empty inheritance at seventeen, talking to a painting: my father never meant any of it as cruelty. That's the terrible part. He built the silence the way he built anything — carefully, to last, out of love and lead. The name was armor. The invisibility was armor. The second ledger, cut into spare blocks where no clerk would think to look, was armor. He armored me so thoroughly that when the enemy finally came through the door, they took him and walked past me, exactly as designed.

It worked. God help me, it worked perfectly. And I have never in my life felt so abandoned by a success.

* *

I did not cry. I want that on the record, for my own record, since no one else is keeping one. I stood in the middle of the shop until the candle had burned down a thumb's width, and I did not cry, because crying is loud and I had been trained out of loud the way other girls are trained into it, and also because — fine, because I was afraid that if I started I would not stop, and there was work to do, and grief is a luxury that arrives with safety and I had scheduled none.

Instead I did what I do. I worked.

I began at the casting bench because it was nearest, and because touching the tools was the closest thing to touching him. His apron hung on its peg, stiff with old metal dust, and I did not touch that, because some things are for later. But his burnisher, his tongs, the little brass square he used to check my kerns when I was eight — *your E's lean like drunkards, Cyrus* — those I gathered up and laid in order, and my hands steadied as they worked, and I hated them a little for steadying.

Then I walked the racks.

The matrix cabinets first, the drawers of copper faces, each one a font in embryo. Then the type cases, seventy-three of them, labeled in my father's small upright hand: *long primer, brevier, pica, nonpareil*. The army had gone through these twice. Vane's men had gone through them three times, the third time at my suggestion — I had offered the third search myself, do you understand, volunteered it, pointed at the cases most likely to hide things, because the best place to hide a lie is inside an invitation. They had found nothing. They were not supposed to. The ledger blocks were already gone from here — divided, sewn, crated, distributed, half of them by Tamsin's hands into shipping crates marked *cooperage, empty*, half of them against my ribs right now under my shift, four flat squares of lead antimony that I had bathed and slept beside and spoken to in my head like a lunatic.

But there were other blocks. Small ones. The ones that don't matter to the army and matter entirely to me.

In the case marked *spare sorts, damaged* — the case every foundry keeps and no inventory man ever lingers over, because who covets broken letters? — I found what I was looking for. My practice pieces. Twelve years of them. The first letters I ever cut, at six years old, standing on a stool: a wobbly **J**, because even then, even coached to cut a **C**, my hand had wanted the letter with the descender, the one that hangs below the line where respectable letters don't go. My father had let me keep it. Had kept it himself, rather. Filed it among the damaged sorts like contraband.

He had known, all along, exactly what he was storing in this shop besides type.

I sat down on the casting stool with that crooked **J** in my palm and I finally understood the shape of my childhood, the way you understand a knot only once someone has pulled the wrong end and made it worse. My father had spent twelve years cutting names into metal — informants, printers, smugglers, debts, insurance — a secret ledger of everyone in Grayharbor who had ever touched dangerous ink, including, in cipher, himself, and my mother, and me. He had spent those same twelve years erasing the one name that mattered most, scrubbing me out of registers and conversations and the future. He recorded strangers in lead and unrecorded his daughter. Both acts were the same act. Both were love, in the grammar of a man who had watched his wife die in a town that remembered everything and wrote down half of it.

And the army came for the records and took the man, and the girl the records forgot was the only person alive who could read them.

That's not tragedy. Tragedy is when it ends there. This — sitting in the dust and the held breath with a crooked letter in my hand — this is just the space between the second act and the third, and I have read enough bad plays to know the third act belongs to whoever gets up off the floor first.

* *

The light went. I lit a second candle off the first, and then a lamp off the second, and I lit the shop the way we lit it for rush jobs: lamp at the casting bench, lamp at the composing stone, candle in the window — no, not in the window. Nothing in the windows anymore. Windows in occupied towns are mouths you learn to keep shut.

I heated the furnace. Not to cast — I hadn't the metal sorted, and the chimney would smoke and smoke draws neighbors, and neighbors in January of 1792 are informants with chilblains. I heated it low, just enough that the shop remembered what it was for. The iron ticked and settled. The smell came back, hot metal and oil and the ghost of every workday of my life, and I stood in it and let it do what my father's shop has always done, which is make the world hold still long enough to be worked on.

Because here is what I decided, somewhere between lighting the lamps and lying to the furnace, and I am writing it here in my head in capital letters so that future Cyrus, the one who will be frightened next week on some checkpoint road, can find it again:

*Silence ends with me.*

Not the town's silence — the town will whisper and look away and survive, God forgive us all, and I watched two printers' shops burn to learn how that works and I will not pretend to be braver than the neighbors whose doors stayed shut while the Ash & Bell went up. Not my father's silence either; his bought him a cell and bought me time, and I intend to spend both purchases wisely, but the debt is his and I won't inherit it as guilt. I'll inherit it as skill. There is a difference, and surviving will be the proof of it.

I mean *my* silence. The one I was cast in. Twelve years of being a name that wasn't mine, a boy that wasn't, an apprentice-shaped hole in the conversation. Twelve years of *hold still, Cyrus* when what was meant was *hold still, Juniper, until the world is safe.* The world is not going to be safe. I have read Vane's courteous inventory lists; I know the world better than my father did at my age. If I wait for safe, I will die spelled correctly and utterly forgotten, and the ledger in my coat will rot with me, and forty families will hang on the strength of paper the army already believes.

No. The name comes out of the case. Not yet — I'm not a fool, whatever Vane's reports will eventually say — not on gate passes I haven't cut yet, not in rooms where courtesy carries pistols. But I know now what I am carrying south to the harbor, and it was never four blocks of lead. Lead is just the medium. I am carrying the end of a twelve-year silence, and I am going to deliver it to a ship named for the girl I used to be, and when the last of it is off this coast I am going to stand somewhere and say my own name out loud and see if the sky falls.

(It doesn't fall. I've since checked.)

That night I did something I had never done in the shop: I worked on something of my own. I cleared the composing stone, chose a case, and began setting type by lamplight — not a proof, not a job for a customer, not coded blocks for a smuggler's hold. Words. Mine. I won't tell you what they said; the stick is still locked, wrapped in oilcloth, under a floorboard my father never knew about because I found it after he stopped teaching me things. Some silences you keep. The difference between me and my father — the difference I am choosing, tonight, in the cold, with the army billeted forty feet away snoring through the partition wall — is that I get to pick which ones.

Toward midnight there were boots in the lane. Two of them, unhurried, stopping outside the shutter. A pause I measured by heartbeats — nine, maybe ten — and then moved on. Vane's men walk in fours when they mean business. Twos means checking. Threes means arrest. I blew out one lamp and sat still in the dark with my hand on the floorboard until the boots faded, and my heart did not race, and I noted that fact with something adjacent to pride.

When they were gone I relit the lamp and finished setting the line.

At dawn I put out the flames, banked the furnace, hung my father's apron back on its peg where the requisition men had ignored it, and buttoned my coat over the four blocks riding against my ribs. In the office mirror — a cheap thing, spotted, bought for customers to check their wigs — I looked at the apprentice boy the town saw, and behind him, faintly, at the girl the mirror had been keeping all along.

"Three days," I told her. "Then the harbor."

There was a knock at the front door. Light. Twice, then a pause, then once more — Tamsin's knock, our old signal from the days when our secrets were only about stolen plums and forged excuses.

Through the glass, pale in the blue morning, her face — and she was not alone.
