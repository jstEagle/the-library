## 24. Bargaining with Osei

Captain Osei kept a cabin the size of a confessional and furnished it like a man who expected to be chased out of any room he occupied. A chart, unrolled and weighted at the corners with cannonballs too small for the guns they'd been taken from. A lamp turned low enough that the flame looked embarrassed. And himself, wedged behind a table no wider than a pulpit's, watching me come down the companionway as though I were weather he'd been promised but had hoped to avoid.

"You've brought nothing," he said.

"I've brought an offer."

"You've brought your hands and your nerve. The first I've seen. The second is doing the work of cargo." He gestured at the stool bolted beside the table — the only seat — and did not invite me to it, which in his language was a kind of courtesy. He liked people standing. Standing people negotiated faster.

Outside, the *Juniper* creaked against her lines like an old woman muttering about stairs. Snow had been falling since noon, the fat, unhurried flakes of a winter settling in for a long argument, and through the stern glass I could see the harbor wearing its white coat over all its sins: the gray-coated soldiers on the quay, the customs skiff riding at its mooring with a lantern swinging from the gaff, the other ships packed hull to hull like conspirators pretending to be strangers.

"New Year's Eve," I said. "You sail on New Year's Eve."

Osei leaned back. His chair complained; he ignored it, the way he seemed to ignore everything that could be outlasted. "I sail when the tide and the profit agree. They rarely agree on anything, but they have never both agreed on New Year's Eve."

"They will tonight."

"Will they." Not a question. He let it sit between us while he poured something dark from a jug into two cups, and pushed one across the table toward me, which was the actual invitation. I sat.

"Here is what I know," he said. "A girl from the type foundry has spent a month making my acquaintance in small doses. Proof sheets. Repairs to my manifest book — don't look surprised, girl, I can read handwriting the way you read faces. She asks careful questions about tides and gates and which of my crew drinks and which prays. Now she comes aboard in a snowstorm without asking whether my bosun would sell her to Vane for the price of a warm dinner. That means she is desperate. Desperate cargo rides cheap. So." He folded his hands. "Make your offer. I'll tell you what it costs."

I had rehearsed this on the walk down. Rehearsed it twice more on the quay while a sergeant of the watch stamped feeling on frozen feet not six yards from where Tamsin's crate-ladgers moved my father's boxes into the *Juniper*'s hold under bills of lading so dull no clerk alive could finish reading them. But rehearsal is to negotiation what type is to printing: the metal is ready, and still the impression surprises you.

"The master ledger," I said. "The whole of it. Every name my father cut in twelve years. Printers, smugglers, factors, informants — who bought what type, when, and what they printed with it. Including the entries the army doesn't know exist. Including," and here I made myself slow down, because this was the coin with real weight, "including the names of men currently on the army's own payroll who sold it paper, powder, and silence."

Something shifted behind Osei's face. It was very small, and he was very good, but I had spent twelve years learning to read impressions in reverse, and I saw it: the flicker of a man doing arithmetic he hadn't expected to enjoy.

"That ledger," he said carefully, "is worth more than my ship."

"It's worth more than every ship in this harbor."

"And you want me to carry it out on a single night, through a gate Vane is already sniffing at, because—"

"Because after New Year's Day it's worth nothing. Vane gets his full inventory. My father rots. The names get sorted into lists — the ones to hang, the ones to tax, the ones to turn. Leverage spoils, Captain. It isn't brandy."

He drank. I drank. The rum was terrible and warming and I suspected deliberately so.

"Your father," Osei said. "They took him."

"Nine days ago."

"And you're selling his life's work out from under the army to buy—" He stopped. Recalculated. "No. You're not buying him. The amnesty talk says he walks free regardless, when the wind shifts in the capital. You're buying something else."

It stung, mostly because he was right. "I'm buying forty families their necks. My father's case of evidence goes to printers abroad who'll use it before Vane can burn it. After that, the ledger stops being a weapon pointed at Grayharbor and becomes one pointed back."

"And you trust these foreign printers."

"No," I said. "But I trust distance, and time, and the fact that Vane can't requisition Amsterdam."

For a long moment the only sound was the ship working at her moorings and, distantly, a soldier's laughter blown in from the quay — thin and wrong-sounding, the way celebration sounds when it's compulsory. The century was four days from turning and the town had been ordered to enjoy it.

Osei set down his cup.

"Girl. Cyrus." He used my name deliberately; we'd settled its pronunciation weeks ago, and hearing it now in his accent felt like seeing a familiar letter set in an unfamiliar face. "I have run this coast since before you cut your first 'a.' I have watched armies take harbors before. Listen to me, because I will only say this once, and then I will either take your money or throw you off my ship."

I listened.

"An army does not occupy a harbor. It digests one." He tapped the chart, where Grayharbor sat drawn in faded ink, the soundings marked in some dead hydrographer's careful hand. "First it takes the ledgers — you know this part, you're living it. Then it takes the manifests. Then it takes the men who understood the ledgers, because clerks are cheap and understanding is expensive. And then, when the town has been emptied of everyone who knew how it worked, the army discovers it owns a machine it cannot operate. The fishing fails because the fish move and no one left reads water. The trade dies because the correspondents abroad stop writing to addresses that have soldiers at the door. In ten years Grayharbor will be a garrison with a beach attached, and the children of the men now posting those notices will stand on the quay and ask their fathers why anyone ever built a town here."

"You've seen it."

"Bissau," he said, simply. "Saint-Malo, half of it. A river port on the Guinea coast whose name I won't give you because there are men living who'd deny it and men dead who couldn't correct them." He refilled his cup. "So when you come to me with treasure and ask me to sail it out on the one night the whole army will be watching the water — understand that I am not refusing because I doubt the cargo. I'm weighing how much a harbor is worth, and who pays when I guess wrong."

"Vane announces searches for New Year's Eve," I said. "Every departure. Full inspection."

"So I heard."

"Then here's the arithmetic." I set my hands flat on his table, and I confess I chose the pose deliberately; a woman who works metal learns that hands are testimony. "If you sail on any ordinary night, you are one smuggling brig among the traffic, and if Vane's boats stop you, they find cargo, papers, and a captain with a talent for looking bored. If you sail New Year's Eve, you sail among a dozen hulls going out with the bells, all of them inspected, all of them delayed — and every delay is cover for the last one through. He can't search us all closely. Search takes hours and New Year's Eve gives him one."

"And if he decides to search mine closely?"

"Then it matters enormously what's in the crates under my father's boxes, and I have arranged for it to be cooperage stock bound for Lisbon, and I have arranged the cooper."

"Tamsin Harrow." He said it flatly, and I kept my face still, though it cost me; I hadn't told him her name and didn't enjoy learning how short his list of sources was. "Whose father logs every keel that leaves this harbor for the army's pleasure."

"Yes."

"You're building your escape out of the enemy's own furniture. That's bold. Bold sinks ships." He was quiet a moment, turning his cup. "What happens to the ledger afterward? Truly. Not the speech."

"Truly? Half of it goes out with you. Half stays sewn in my coat until I know yours arrived. If either half fails, the other survives."

His eyebrows rose perhaps a quarter inch, which from Osei was a standing ovation. "Splitting the evidence. Who taught you that?"

"A man who hid a second ledger inside a first profession for twelve years," I said. "Deceit is the family trade. We just never charged enough."

That earned me something rare: a short laugh, real, quickly put away.

He rose then, and went to the chart, and stood over it the way my father stood over a case of type — not reading it so much as listening to it. When he spoke again his voice had changed register, captain to captain, or whatever he considered me.

"Terms," he said. "One. I take the case, not the cause. Whatever those printers do with it afterward is between them and their consciences. Two. My fee doubles, payable in the coin we discussed — I'll want cut plates within the week, seals and signatures, the quality only you can make, and I won't ask what for and you won't insult me by lying well enough to make me believe you. Three." He turned. "New Year's Eve, yes. But not at the bells. At slack water after. The bells draw every eye ashore; slack water draws none afloat. Your father's case goes over the side in Tamsin's crates with the last of the cooperage, my boat puts out under oars quiet as sin, and we're past the point before the fireworks start. If the wind holds. If the wind does not hold, I sit at anchor and look festive, and you keep your ledger and your coat, and we are all of us very lucky the century turns without us."

"Agreed."

"On all?"

"On all. Though I notice you named a fee for forgery and never once asked whether I'd do it."

"I assumed," Osei said, "that a girl who offers me a fortune to smuggle her own inheritance past a man like Aldous Vane stopped asking permission from herself some weeks ago." He came around the table and, for the first time in our acquaintance, offered his hand. It was broad and scarred across the palm in the pattern rope leaves. "One more thing, and it is not a term. It is advice, which is dearer."

"Go on."

"When this is done — when the ship sails and the bells ring and you're standing in the crowd with your half still against your ribs, feeling clever — remember what I told you about digestion. Because the wind is shifting in the capital, they say, and one day the army leaves, and the merchants will come back to their ledgers, and the print circle will come back to its presses, and everyone will be very warm and forgiving and no one will ask what it cost the ones who carried things in the dark." His grip tightened briefly. "Grayharbor will eat its heroes along with everything else, girl. Don't be on the plate."

"I'll remember," I said, which was a lie, or a promise, or the thing that looks like both and behaves like neither.

I climbed back up into the snow. The deck glowed under a lantern swaying at the shrouds, and forward, two of his men were tarping the hatch over my father's case with the unhurried competence of men who had hidden many things and intended to go on being paid for it. Beyond the rail, Grayharbor lay white and lamplit and full of soldiers, breathing smoke, keeping its appointments with fear.

Four days. Slack water after the bells. A forged pass with a dead man's name on it waiting in my coat for the courage and the graver it needed.

And then, from the darkness of the quay, a lantern lifted — once, twice, three times — in the signal Tamsin and I had agreed meant trouble, and only trouble, and come quickly.

* * *

She was waiting in the lee of the customs house, hood up, breath smoking, and I knew before she spoke that the news was bad, because she had chosen a spot with no lamp and no witnesses, and Tamsin chose comfort whenever trouble allowed it.

"Vane posted new orders an hour ago," she said. "Every departure searched on New Year's Eve. Not sampled. Searched — crews mustered below, hatches off, the whole ceremony." She swallowed. "And Cyrus — he's requisitioned the harbor gate registers. All of them. My father's logs. He has them in his office at the fort, and he's been reading them since supper."

The snow fell between us, patient as anything patient and stupid.

"He's going to find the *Juniper*," Tamsin said. "In my father's hand, in black ink, dated New Year's Eve. He's going to find it before we ever sail."
