## 25. The Counterfeit Pass

The army had a genius for paperwork that I confess I admired, in the way one admires a well-made trap.

Every pass leaving Grayharbor now required three elements: the garrison seal, a countersign that changed weekly, and the signature of an officer whose hand I could not copy because I had never seen it. The seal I could manage — Vane himself had taught me its geometry without meaning to, pressing his letters flat on our counter while he inventoried us a second time, patient as a man counting another man's coffin nails. The countersign was a matter of listening; Tamsin's father logged departures, and logs get read over shoulders by daughters with quiet feet.

But the signature. There was the wall.

I sat in the foundry at two in the morning with a stolen impression of a garrison pass drying between my fingers, and I studied that signature until my eyes watered. *R. Callum, Capt.* The R was a flourish with pretensions. The Callum slanted like a fence leaning into wind. It was confident handwriting, bureaucratic handwriting, the hand of a man who had signed a thousand deaths and never once lost sleep over the ink.

I could not fake it because I did not know it. And then, folding the paper to put it away, I realized I did not need to know it. I needed only to know a *dead* one.

* * *

Here is a thing about a type foundry: we are the gravekeepers of other people's words. Every shop in town that ever printed a notice, a bill of lading, a wedding announcement — some fragment of their orders passed through Underhill & Sons, and my father kept everything, because men who deal in secrets keep receipts. Somewhere in the long drawers of specimen sheets was every hand that had ever bought type from us.

I went looking for a ghost and found him before dawn.

Elias Crane, stationer, deceased eight months of a fever that took him kindly in his sleep, according to the funeral sermon I myself had set in ten-point Baskerville. In life he had ordered engraved invitation stock from the coast and always, always insisted on signing his own proofs — a vanity, my father had noted with contempt, of a man who trusted no one else's pen. His signature lay on three specimen pages in our files, each more florid than the last.

More usefully, Elias Crane had once been granted — through some cousin's patronage I never learned the details of — a legitimate commercial pass from the harbor office, back when passes were merely commerce instead of confession. Tamsin had found me the format. The format had changed since; the *authority* cited at the bottom had not.

A dead loyalist stationer with a documented right to ship goods. A signature I had three specimens of. A seal I could cut in lead finer than any forger's wax imitation, because I would cut it in metal and strike it true.

I laid the pieces out on my workbench like a surgery, and I felt something I want to be honest about, since honesty is supposed to be the theme of this account: I felt *delighted*. Not frightened, not guilty. Delighted, the way I had felt at nine years old the first time I cut a letter that cast clean on the first pour. My father spent twelve years teaching me that names were dangerous currency. He never once mentioned they were also *materials*, and that I had been apprenticed to work them.

* * *

Cutting a signature in steel is not writing. It is the opposite of writing. You carve away everything that is not the stroke, which is a fair description of most of what I have done in my life.

I worked three days. By day I kept the shop's fiction alive — polishing brass, sorting cases under Emmerich's incurable young gaze, answering Vane's occasional courteous nothing-questions with the smooth fluency that still alarmed me when I caught myself mid-lie, so I made a practice of never catching myself. By night I worked the steel under a shuttered lamp, breath held, hands steady in the way that only comes when the rest of you has quietly left the room and only the craft remains.

The countersign for the last week of December was *MERIDIAN*. Tamsin whispered it to me across a crate of herring she pretended to sell me and I pretended to buy, and we both kept our faces stupid, and I loved her in that moment with the fierce specific love of conspirators, which is the purest kind and the least useful for anything else.

The seal took longest. Wax seals are forgiving things — press soft stuff into soft stuff and call it authority — but the new passes used a stamped ink device, an eagle-and-anchor crest with a dotted border, and a dotted border will forgive nothing. Eleven dots per curve. I counted them off a borrowed impression eleven times. Then I cut the die in brass rather than lead, because lead strikes soft and soft strikes *wrong*, and a clerk's eye might forgive a shaky signature but never a blurry crown.

On the fourth night I struck the first complete pass.

Paper aged with weak tea and dried near the forge. Ink matched by mixing oak gall with a trace of the iron-rich rust water from our casting pits — our own water, our own town, betraying itself on the page. Signature copied from a dead man's vanity. Seal struck so cleanly that when I closed my eyes and ran a thumb across it, I could feel the crisp bite of the border and I thought, absurdly, *my father would be proud*, and then I thought, *that is exactly the problem.*

But I was not done. That came after, and it came without plan.

I held the finished pass up to the lamp — *Permission is granted to ELIAS CRANE, stationer, to convey goods of paper and print to the brig Juniper, Osei, master* — and it was perfect, flawless, anonymous, and something in me rebelled. Every name on it belonged to someone else. Dead Elias. Living Osei. Even the fictional clerk of the harbor office had more presence on that page than I did. I had built a document that proved, in its whole construction, that Cyrus Underhill did not exist.

I took my finest graver — the one my father called the needle, reserved for kerning, for the smallest corrections — and in the lower left corner, inside the flourish of the anchor's chain where the eye slides past and never lands, I cut one letter into the die before the final strike.

*C.*

Not Cyrus. Not even a name. Just a claim. A mark the size of a pinhead, invisible unless you knew to look, illegible unless you already knew what you were looking at. The kind of mark a cutter leaves on her work. The kind of mark I had been leaving in my own mind for twelve years, in a place where no one ever looked.

I re-inked the die and struck the final pass, and there it was: my initial, riding out of the harbor under a dead man's name, tucked into the chain of an anchor on an army's own paper.

My father taught me that names are currency. He was wrong, or half wrong. Names are *type*. They get set, they get printed, they get worn down and recast. And a woman who has spent her life cutting letters into metal understands what no clerk or captain ever will: the smallest character carries the same weight as the largest. Ask any compositor. Ask any gallows.

I wrapped the pass in oilcloth and hid it inside the hollowed case marked CASLON — SMALL PILCROWS, because no soldier in history has gone looking for pilcrows, small or otherwise. Then I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark listening to the billeted soldiers snore one floor up, and I laughed, silently, into my own hands, until the laughing turned into something else and stayed that way for a while.

* * *

Morning brought Tamsin, which morning usually did now; she had invented a standing errand to the cooper's that happened to route past our door, and neither of us insulted the other by pretending otherwise.

She read the pass twice. Once fast, once slow, her lips moving on the second pass the way her father's did over manifests.

"The chain," she said finally, tapping the anchor. "There's a mark in the chain."

"There is."

"Whose?"

"Mine," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment, and I braced for the lecture — risk, discipline, all the reasons small vanities kill conspiracies. Instead she said, "Good," and handed it back, and I understood I had misjudged her, which was becoming a habit. She wasn't warning me against the mark. She was keeping a record of it. Tamsin filed things the way her father filed ships: precisely, and forever, and against future need.

"It'll sail New Year's Eve," she said. "Osei's manifest is drafted. I've entered the Juniper as coastal trade, grain outbound to Kingsport, no passengers declared." She paused, and I watched her choose whether to say the next thing. She said it. "There's a problem, Cyrus. Vane posted new orders yesterday evening. Effective immediately — every departing vessel gets a boarding inspection, every crewman named, every shore party's papers checked at the gate. Personally checked." She looked at me. "By Vane. Himself."

* * *

I told her it changed nothing, which was a lie, and she let me tell it, which was its own kindness. After she left I stood at the workbench with the CASLON case open under my hand and did honest arithmetic for the first time in weeks.

A gate pass got a body through the gate. It did not get a body past Aldous Vane, who had spent four months learning the foundry the way I had learned it — drawer by drawer, block by block. Who had asked me, in his gentle clerical voice, whether we kept working copies. Who had taken my father away in irons on evidence recovered from a burned print shop, evidence my father had claimed entire with the single selfless lie of his life.

Vane inspected papers the way I cut steel: removing everything that was not the stroke. He would find the pass perfect, because it was perfect. And then he would look up from the paper at my face, because that was the final element no forger could strike in metal, and he would decide.

I could plan for checkpoints. I could plan for rhythms, for boredom, for tea-browned paper and dead men's hands. What I could not plan for was being *known*. Everything I had built — the name, the silence, the fluent lies, the little C hiding in the anchor chain — rested on being nobody, and Vane's whole talent was for making nobodies into somebodies, briefly, in an interview room, just long enough to hang them.

So I did the only thing left to a craftsman whose materials are exhausted: I prepared my tools and waited for the job. I rehearsed my answers. I practiced the foundry daughter's dull modesty in the polished plate of the press bed — the downcast eyes, the stammer placed like a kern, the hands that had sorted type for twelve years and could not possibly have cut anything but type. I rehearsed until the mask sat easy, then rehearsed further, until it sat so easy it stopped feeling like a mask.

That should have frightened me. I noted that it should have frightened me, filed the note away, and kept rehearsing.

Three days to New Year's Eve. The storm had boxed Osei's fleet in port like flies under a glass, and every day of delay was a day for Vane to sharpen. But the century would turn regardless — centuries are punctual that way — and somewhere across the water printers were waiting for forty families' worth of names, and my father was sitting in a cell owing his freedom to a girl with a graver and a dead stationer's hand.

On the third night, as I banked the forge, a folded paper came under the door. No knock. No footsteps that I heard, and I had learned to hear everything in that building.

It was an army summons, correct in every particular, bearing the garrison seal — the true one; I checked it against my die with a professional's envy — and a single line:

*Mistress Underhill is requested to attend the garrison office at ten o'clock, Thursday, regarding certain discrepancies in the foundry inventory. — A. Vane.*

*Discrepancies.* A beautiful word. A word with eleven dots per curve of consequence.

I set the paper down on the bench next to my tools, side by side, the summons and the graver, the accusation and the craft, and I looked at the pair of them for a long time.

Ten o'clock Thursday. Two days before the tide.

He was ready to look at my face. I would have to be ready to show him one.
