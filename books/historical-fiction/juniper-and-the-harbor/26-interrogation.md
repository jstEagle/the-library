## 26. Interrogation

The summons came the way winter comes to Grayharbor — not announced so much as simply present one morning, sitting in the doorway of the foundry with its hands folded and its breath steaming.

"Cyrus Underhill." The corporal didn't make it a question. He held a paper at arm's length as though it might be damp. "You're requested at the customs house. Ten o'clock. Questions regarding inventory."

"Requested," I said. Not summoned. Not ordered. Requested, as though the army were a gentleman caller and I a lady with a calling card dish. I had learned by then that the gray coats used the softest words for their hardest business. Requisition. Request. Inquiry. Each one a small upholstered door with iron behind it.

"I'll be there," I said.

He looked faintly disappointed, the way men do when you don't give them the pleasure of reluctance. He left the paper on my bench — I noticed he did not hand it to me, exactly; he set it down as if handing things to women was against some regulation he couldn't quite recall — and crunched away through the snow.

I read the paper twice. Then I did what any sensible person would do with three hours' notice: I sat down and thought about everything Vane might know.

The discrepancies would be type weight. There was no help for that; I had been shaving the inventory for months, moving pounds of lead out the door in Tamsin's crates and into my own coat linings, and lead is honest stuff — it weighs what it weighs no matter how sweetly you ask it to lie. A clerk who was good at his arithmetic could not fail to notice forty pounds of missing matrices, and Aldous Vane was very good at his arithmetic. It was, I suspected, the only part of him that slept soundly.

What I had to decide in those three hours was this: how much ignorance to wear, and of what cut.

There are ignorances the way there are coats. There is the coarse wool ignorance of the peasant girl who cannot read the summons handed her — useless on me; Vane had watched me work a press. There is the nervous ignorance of the apprentice who suspects his master kept secrets but knows nothing of them — better; fear reads as innocence in a shop where fear has always lived. And there is the finest cloth of all: the ignorance of a daughter grieving a father taken from her, a girl so thoroughly deceived by her own household that she has become, in her own mind, the army's most useful witness against it.

That last one fit me like a glove made to my hand. Which frightened me, a little, even before I put it on.

* * *

The customs house had been a merchant's home once, back when Grayharbor still pretended its wealth came from shipping rather than from whatever moved through the shipping unmentioned. The requisitioned parlor still had its wallpaper — faded shepherds and shepherdesses, disapproving of the military map now tacked over them. Someone had built a fire too well; the room was hot enough to make a liar drowsy, and I understood that this was deliberate. Comfort loosens tongues. Warmth is cheaper than pincers and works nearly as often.

Vane received me himself. No table between us this time, no ledger open like a shield. Two chairs set close by the fire, a decanter of sherry on the sideboard, and Vane standing at the window with his hands clasped behind him, watching the harbor through frost-ferned glass as though the ships might do something interesting if only he stared long enough.

"Miss Underhill." He turned. He always said *Miss*, though the town called me by my father's name, and I had never decided whether it was courtesy or a test. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. Sherry?"

"No, thank you, sir."

"You're wise. It's the garrison's sherry and tastes of the barrel it was requisitioned from." He smiled — the small, precise smile he kept for occasions when he wished to demonstrate that he knew exactly what requisition meant to the people on the losing end of it. "Sit."

I sat. I let my shoulders curve the way they do after twelve years bent over a casting bench, let my hands settle in my lap with the calluses showing, thumb and forefinger, the two fingers a cutter wears like a confession. I had spent an hour before leaving the shop deciding which version of myself to bring, and I had settled on the truth, mostly, told badly — hesitant, incomplete, frightened. Nothing disarms a man like watching a woman frighten herself.

"You'll wonder why you're here," Vane said, taking the other chair.

"Yes, sir."

"Then I'll spare you the wondering. Your father's inventory — the one we took in October — lists the foundry's holdings at four hundred and eleven pounds of type metal, cast and in pigs, exclusive of the working cases. We have completed a second accounting. Do you know what a second accounting is for?"

"To find mistakes in the first," I said. My voice came out smaller than I'd planned. Good, some cold practical corner of me observed. Smaller is better.

"To find thefts dressed up as mistakes in the first," Vane corrected gently. "The difference is thirty-one pounds, Miss Underhill. Thirty-one pounds of metal that walked out of your father's shop sometime between October and this week. Now. You will understand that I am not much interested in the metal itself. Lead is lead. What interests me is what it was cast *into* before it left."

I let the silence sit. Not the practiced silence of a liar holding her ground — the wet-eyed silence of a girl gathering courage around a grief. When I spoke, my voice cracked on the first word, and I confess I was proud of it even as I was appalled.

"My father never stole anything in his life, sir. Whatever's missing — someone took it when the soldiers were billeted in the workshop, or miscounted, or—" I stopped. Bit my lip. "I'm sorry. I don't know how these things are counted. I only sweep the floor and sort the cases."

Vane studied me. He had a way of studying that felt less like looking at a person than reading proof sheets — hunting for the broken letter, the reversed impression, the flaw in the form. I kept my eyes on the middle button of his coat and let him look. Behind my face, in the dark where he could not reach, I laid out the inventory of myself: *thirty-one pounds*. So they had not found the hollowed cases under the soldiers' bunks. They had not found Emmerich's clean report. They knew something was gone but not what, and not where, and — crucially — not why. A thief steals value. I had stolen testimony. If Vane understood the difference, I was already at the gate with a rope's shadow on my neck.

"Your father," Vane said, "kept meticulous books."

"He did."

"Twelve years of meticulous books, and in all that time, by his own ledger, the foundry sold type to precisely the customers a respectable foundry sells to. Almanacs. Broadside ballads. Shipping notices. And yet." He rose, crossed to the sideboard, poured himself the sherry he had offered me, and did not drink it. "And yet I have in my possession fragments of type recovered from the Ashworth print shop — you'll recall the fire — bearing names that appear nowhere in your father's ledger. Names of men who bought type that your father, by his own accounting, never sold."

"That must be very confusing, sir," I said softly.

"It is *interesting*," Vane said. "Confusion is for lesser clerks."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us a soldier's boots crossed the floor, unhurried, and the shepherds on the wallpaper went on tending their impossible flocks. I let my eyes fill. It is a skill, weeping on request; my mother taught it to me without meaning to, in the year I learned that tears made the neighbors gentler with the widow's child. One tear, mind. Two is theatrical. One tear, allowed to sit at the lash line without falling, is worth more than a speech.

"Sir," I said, "if you think my father sold type to rebels — I've wondered myself, since they took him. I've lain awake wondering. He was secret about things. He was secret about money, and about my mother, and there were nights I heard the lathe going when I should have been asleep, and I never asked, because you don't ask, in a house like ours you learn not to ask—" I let the sentence die of natural causes and pressed my knuckles to my mouth.

Every word true. That is the trick nobody warns you about. The best lie is a row of truths set in an order they were never meant to keep. My father *was* secret. I *had* heard the lathe at night. I *had* learned not to ask. I had merely rearranged the furniture of my life so that it pointed at innocence the way a compass points north, and I stood there watching Aldous Vane read it, and I felt — God forgive me — nothing. No flutter of guilt. No sweat at the hairline. Just the mild professional satisfaction of a cutter checking her work against the proof.

That was the moment the mask fit too well. Because Vane believed me — I could see it arrive in him, the slow softening of a man who has been offered the answer he half hoped for — and instead of relief I felt a kind of vertigo, standing inside a self I had built so quickly and so well that I had lost sight of its edges. If he believed me, then the girl in the chair with the wet eyes and the broken sentences was, for every purpose that mattered in that room, real. And some small cold clerk in my own chest was already taking notes on her performance for next time.

"There," Vane said quietly, almost kindly. "You see? You know more than you think you do. The lathe at night. Tell me about the nights."

"They were mostly in winter." True. "After Mother died." True. "I thought he was mourning. Men mourn with their hands, don't they? He'd work till the candles guttered." All true. All arranged like knives in a pie.

Vane sat down again, closer this time. "Miss Underhill. I am going to tell you something I tell very few people, because it costs me nothing and may gain me a great deal." He turned his glass by the stem. "I do not believe your father was a rebel. Rebels burn their ledgers. Your father *kept* his — kept two, I suspect, though I cannot yet prove the shape of the second. A man who keeps records wants them found eventually. Insurance, or leverage, or simple vanity — the motive hardly matters. What matters is that somewhere in your shop there exists a catalogue of every dangerous name in this harbor, and that whoever holds it when the century turns holds this town by the throat." He looked at me over the rim of the glass. "If such a thing exists, and if you found it, and if you gave it to me — no one would ever need to know the Underhills had cooperated. I can be generous to the cooperative. It is one of my few extravagances."

*I have no idea what you mean, sir,* said the girl in the chair, trembling politely.

*I know exactly what you mean,* said the clerk behind her face, *and I would sooner set the whole thing in type and drop it in the harbor than hand forty families to you in alphabetical order.*

What I actually said was: "If Papa hid something, he never trusted me with it. I think that's what hurts most, sir. Everyone keeps assuming I'm part of his secrets, and the truth is I was just — the apprentice. The hands. He never once treated me as though I could understand the rest."

The last sentence was the most dangerous thing I said all day, because it was the truest thing in the room, and Vane flinched — barely, a hairline crack in the glaze — and I understood with a lurch that I had wounded him. Some old wound of his own, some history of being the competent hands excluded from the confident councils of better-named men. For one heartbeat the customs house was empty of army and army business and held only two people who had spent their lives being underestimated, and I hated him, and I pitied him, and I wanted him to stop looking at me before the pity showed.

He stopped. He gathered himself the way men do, straightening an internal collar, and when he spoke again the clerk had returned to his voice.

"The inventory will be conducted monthly from now on. You will be given three days' notice each time. Any further discrepancy will be treated as evidence of conspiracy — yours, not your father's. He is in the cells; conspiracies require free hands, and yours are, at present, free." He stood, and I stood, and he walked me to the door with that terrible courtesy of his, and paused with his hand on the latch. "One more thing. The pass you carry for the cooper's deliveries — you'll surrender it at the desk downstairs. New ones are being issued. With countersigns."

My stomach performed a slow, silent revolution. The pass. The pass I had already copied, seal and signature and all, and improved upon, and hidden inside a hollowed quire of rag paper not four feet from where soldiers slept. Every hour of my night work, rendered obsolete by a stroke of a clerk's pen.

"Of course, sir," I said, and gave it up with fingers that shook — genuinely, this time; no art required — because fear is the one performance that needs no rehearsal.

At the door he said, without turning: "Your father cut beautiful letters, Miss Underhill. I've examined hundreds of founders' work. His matrices have a particular finish at the counter — a maker's habit, invisible to anyone who hasn't spent years reading metal." Now he turned, and his face was perfectly blank, and blanker than blank. "It's remarkable, the family resemblance."

He closed the door on me.

I walked home through the snow with the shepherds' wallpaper still floating behind my eyes and my heart going like a press at full pull. I had won — I saw that clearly enough. He had nothing; suspicion was all he owned, and suspicion cannot be entered in a ledger. I had lied to a professional liar's face and watched him sign off on it.

And underneath the winning, cold as harbor water, lay the thing I could not say to anyone: that I had frightened myself. Not with the danger — the danger was ordinary now, a familiar animal sleeping in my coat. With the ease. The girl with the cracking voice had come out of me fully formed, no drafts, no false starts, the way my father pulled a perfect letter from the matrix on the first try when the metal was right. Twelve years of learning to be invisible had not been wasted time. They had been apprenticeship. I was a master now, of something, and there was no guild, no shop sign, no honest name for what I had just been made a master of.

Tamsin was waiting in the doorway of the foundry, stamping her feet, her breath silvering the air. She read my face in the way of people who have shared too many risks to need speeches.

"Well?"

"Well," I said, "we need new papers. All of them. By New Year's Eve." I pulled off my gloves, and my hands, I noticed, were perfectly steady. "Tamsin. How good are you at forgery?"

She laughed, thinking I was joking.

Behind her, through the frosted window of the shop, lamplight fell across the bench where a dead man's name waited on my cutting plate, half finished — and beside it, small and patient, a blank block of lead the exact size of a single initial letter.
