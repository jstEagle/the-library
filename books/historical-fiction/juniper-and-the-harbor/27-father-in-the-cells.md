## 27. Father in the Cells

The cells beneath the customs house had been built, I suspect, by an optimist. The stones were damp but sound, the door hinges recently oiled, and a barred window at street level let in a gray ribbon of January-adjacent light — as if someone had imagined prisoners might want to read, or pray, or take a modest interest in the weather. My father had been in that room four days when they finally let me see him, and he had done what my father always did with any space given him: he had organized it.

His blanket was folded into a precise square in one corner. His tin cup sat at an exact right angle to the wall. Someone had left him a stub of candle, and I could tell from the wax drips on the floor that he'd been using it not for light but for something else — he had arranged the fallen drops in little rows, like type sorts laid out on a composing stone, waiting to be made into sentences.

"You look terrible," I said, because it was true, and because we were a family that expressed tenderness through inventory.

"And you've been crying," he said. "Which is wasteful. Salt water's worth nothing on the market this winter."

I sat down on the cold flagstones across from his cell, close enough that I could have reached through the bars and touched him, which I did not do yet. There is a discipline to grief, too. You learn it young in a foundry: don't grab at hot metal.

"They feed you?"

"Twice a day, allegedly." He smiled, and the smile cost him something; I watched him pay for it. "The bread is mostly sawdust, which offends me professionally. At least make it good sawdust."

I laughed. It came out wrong, half a sob wearing a laugh's coat, and he heard it for what it was.

"Cyrus." He said my name carefully, the way he handled a cracked matrix. "Sit properly. We're going to work."

* * *

I should explain what I had come prepared to do, which was everything except work.

I had rehearsed speeches on the walk down from the foundry. I would confess the whole scheme — the ledger, the blocks sewn into my coat hem, Tamsin's manifests, Osei, the counterfeit pass drying somewhere under my mattress with its dead man's name and my own small initial hidden in the corner where no clerk would think to squint. I would tell him all of it and ask his forgiveness for finishing what he never meant me to start, or perhaps ask his blessing, I hadn't decided which. Fathers are supposed to give one or the other and I genuinely did not know which mine owed me.

Instead he looked at my hands.

They were red at the knuckles from cold and from the file work I'd been doing three nights running, cutting the gate pass by candlelight with my coat thrown over the window to hide the flame. A cutter's hands. Vane had nearly caught me once over those hands, back at the tollhouse road, and I had learned since then to keep them still around soldiers, to fold them like a merchant's wife's.

But there was lead in the creases of my palms that no amount of washing ever fully lifted. Twelve years in the trade. You can scrub until your skin peels; the metal keeps a little of you, and you keep a little of it.

"You've been cutting," he said. "Fine work, from the look of the file wear. Small faces."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Yes you do." He leaned forward, and the candle stub between us threw his shadow up the cellar wall, enormous, misshapen — the shadow of a much larger man than the one who sat shivering behind the bars in a prisoner's shirt. "And I'm not going to ask you what. Do you understand? I am never going to ask you. If they interrogate me again, and they will, I need to be able to say truthfully that my daughter tells me nothing. Ignorance is a kind of armor, Cyrus. Let me wear it."

"That armor didn't fit you very well," I said. "You're sitting in it."

"My armor failed because I built it badly." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "I cut that ledger for insurance, not for war. Insurance you keep in a drawer. War you carry in your body, past checkpoints, at night, in the cold, at your age, alone—" His voice snagged on something and he mastered it the way he mastered everything, by force of habit. "I never meant you to inherit this. That's the confession I owe you and I've been composing it for four days and it comes out worse every time."

"It comes out fine," I said. "It's the same confession you already gave me in the kitchen. Insurance, leverage, debts. I remember."

"There's more."

* * *

Here is what I knew of the ledger's cipher, before that morning: that certain spare type blocks carried coded impressions, reversed names cut in a private shorthand of my father's devising; that I had learned to read most of them through two winters of patient decipherment; and that a handful of entries resisted me completely. Nine blocks, maybe ten, whose impressions followed some logic I couldn't find. I had turned them every way a mirror turns. I had tried reversing them, inverting them, reading them against the master order of the type case, against dates, against prices. They kept their secret the way my father kept everything.

I told him about them now, because if I couldn't give him my confession I could at least give him a problem, and problems were our love language. I recited the stubborn entries from memory — I know them still, God help me, the way other people know psalms.

He listened with his eyes closed. When I finished, he was quiet a long moment, and then he did something I had seen him do perhaps twice in my life.

He laughed.

Not bitterly. Not the dry click he used instead of sighing. A real laugh, small and rusty, like a press after a long idleness turning over at last.

"The ninth block," he said. "The one with the anchor mark above the crossbar. Tell me what you tried."

"Mirror reversal. Case position. Date offsets. Price keys. I even tried reading it as French, in case you'd borrowed a foreign hand."

"And none of it opened?"

"Not a word."

"Because you were looking for a key outside yourself." He opened his eyes and looked at me, and there was something in his face I had never seen there before — not pride exactly, pride implies distance, admiration of a thing achieved. This was closer to surrender. "Cyrus. What did you try for the name key?"

"The name key?"

"Every cipher I ever built, I built the same way. One key system, applied throughout. Consistency is the whole virtue of a code — anyone who has to consult his notes to read his own ledger deserves to hang by it." He rubbed his face; his beard had gone fully gray in four days of prison, or perhaps I had simply never looked closely enough before. "The first layer, the one you broke, uses the case order — you found that, well done, that took your mother three weeks. The second layer uses dates. But the deep entries, the dangerous ones, the ones I cut last, after your mother died, when I understood what kind of town Grayharbor really was and what kind of neighbors we had—"

He stopped. The ribbon of light from the window had crawled along the floor toward his folded blanket, and I watched him watch it, buying time, an old compositor setting his type one word at a time.

"After Mother died," I prompted.

"After your mother died," he said, "I changed the key. Because a key based on the shop — on cases and dates and prices — is a key any clever cutter could break, and you were eleven years old and clever, and frightened, and I was afraid for you. So I moved the key somewhere no one would ever look for it. Somewhere I could always find it and no one could ever guess it."

"Where?"

"Juniper," said my father.

The word landed in the cell and sat there. Our old name. Her name — the name my mother chose for me before she was two weeks gone, the name that lasted twelve days in the parish register before my father paid to have it struck out and replaced, because a girl named Juniper Underhill would grow up marked, legible, a question everyone in a garrison town could answer at a glance. Names are dangerous currency. He taught me that before he taught me my letters, practically.

"What about it?" I asked, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted.

"Count it," he said. "The way you'd count any word set in type. Six characters. J-U-N-I-P-E-R. Now assign each character its place in the alphabet — the first lesson I ever taught you, do you remember? A is one, B is two—"

"Ten. Fourteen. Fourteen. Nine. Sixteen. Five. Eighteen." I said it automatically, the sums rising in me like water finding a level, and even as I spoke them I felt the shape of the thing assembling itself, the lock tumbling. "Ten-fourteen-fourteen-nine-sixteen-five-eighteen."

"Now go back to your nine stubborn blocks. Take the impressions in their case order. Apply those numbers as offsets — first character shifted ten, second fourteen, and so on, cycling through. Read what comes out."

I closed my eyes. And there in a freezing cell beneath the customs house, with my father watching through bars, I read the deep ledger in my head for the first time.

Names. The ones I had known were there and could not reach. Aldous Vane's predecessor in the customs service, bought years before the army came. Two harbor merchants whose loyalism was a costume worn over smuggling partnerships. A printer whose arrest I had witnessed — his own name was in the deep ledger, entered months before the soldiers burned his shop, entered not as dissident but as *paid*. And beside one entry, dated the spring I turned fifteen, a name I had been carrying in my heart through all of this like contraband: Tamsin's brother.

I opened my eyes.

"You recorded informants," I said. "People who sold names. You kept them in the deepest part of the code, where only family could reach."

"Where only *you* could reach," he said. "That's the point I've been failing to make for four days, so let me fail it plainly now. The key is your name. Your real one. The one we buried. I built the deepest truths of this town into a lock that opens only for a girl who doesn't exist anymore — because I thought that girl was dead, Cyrus. I thought I had killed her to save you, and I grieved her, and then I hid her inside the metal the way you hide a message inside a message, because I could not bear to lose her entirely." He pressed his forehead briefly against the bars, and the iron left a faint line on his skin, a printer's mark. "And then you walked in here today having broken half my cipher without the key, through sheer stubbornness, and I understood that I never killed her at all. I just didn't know where I'd put her."

* * *

We sat with that a while. The light moved. Somewhere overhead, boots crossed the customs house floor, and we both went silent until they faded — old reflex, shared silence, the family dialect.

"I have questions," I said finally.

"I imagine."

"Tamsin's brother. He's in the deep ledger as an informant. She's falsifying manifests for me. She deserves to know whether she's helping a traitor or an informer's sister."

"Tell her," he said at once. "Truth between allies is ballast. It weighs, but it keeps you upright." Then, softer: "And forgive him if you can, or her if you must, but remember that men inform out of fear more often than malice. Fear is cheap currency. Everyone in Grayharbor has been paid in it."

"Why did you never teach me the key? All those nights deciphering — you watched me struggle. You let me think I'd failed."

"Because the day you needed it would be the day I couldn't help you," he said simply. "A key you're handed is a debt. A key you earn is an inheritance. I wanted—" He stopped, restarted, the way he did when a sentence wouldn't justify. "Your mother taught me the first layer of the cipher. Did you know that? Not the other way round. She was the better cutter. She designed the case-order system, and when she died I added the date layer myself, and then the name layer last, and each generation of the lock got deeper and more personal and less useful to anyone but us. That's not a ledger anymore, Cyrus. That's a memoir written in a language of one reader. I suppose I built it so that someday, if everything fell on me, the last thing of mine you'd have to find would be yourself."

I reached through the bars then. I had been saving it, the way you save the last of the good paper, and I spent it now: I took his hands, both of them, cutter's hands like mine, lead in the creases, cold as the flagstones, and I held on.

"I'm getting you out," I said. "There's an amnesty coming, or a shift in the wind, or Osei's leverage, or something. I haven't got the mechanism yet. But I've got a ship and a forged pass and forty families' worth of reasons, and I am going to finish this."

"I know," said my father. "I read your hands, didn't I?"

"And one more thing." I made myself say it steadily. "When it's done — when the ledger sails and the amnesty comes and the shop reopens — people will talk about restoring my legal name. Making me Juniper again in the register, proper and correct."

"Don't," he said.

"You don't even want to hear—"

"Cyrus." He smiled, tired and entire. "I named you Juniper and then I renamed you Cyrus to keep you alive, and both times I chose for you, which is the great liberty fathers take. You're the only person alive who holds the key to the deepest things I ever cut. So the name is yours to spend however you like. That's the inheritance. That was always the inheritance. The rest is just metal."

* * *

The turnkey's footsteps came down the stair on a schedule I had already learned — the visit was ending, visits end, everything in occupied towns runs on timetables. I stood, brushed off my skirts, put my hands away in their merchant's-wife fold.

At the top of the steps I stopped, because there was one more thing, and it had been forming in me the whole visit, the way wax forms under a steady flame.

"Papa. The ninth block. The anchor mark. Whose entry is it?"

He was quiet long enough that I turned back. In the dimness his face was unreadable — or rather it was perfectly readable, and I refused the reading.

"Cut it open tonight," he said. "You have the key now. But Cyrus — hear me. Some of what's in the deep ledger will cost you to know. That's why I buried it so far down. Knowledge in wartime isn't treasure. It's cargo, and cargo is heavy, and you must decide before you load it whether your ship can carry it."

"I'm carrying half the ledger sewn into my coat already," I said. "How much heavier can it get?"

I asked it lightly. Even then, even there, I asked it lightly, because wryness is the last tool on the bench and you reach for it when everything else is taken.

He didn't smile. He looked at me through the bars with the expression of a man watching his daughter pick up a piece of metal he knows is still cooling.

"Heavier," he said.

The turnkey appeared at my elbow, coughed, and showed me up into the gray morning, where the snow had begun again over Grayharbor, soft and patient, falling on the harbor, the foundry, the forged pass under my mattress — and all the way home I turned the anchor block over in my mind, counting letters, and by the time I reached the shop door I had almost finished decoding it, and I already knew, from the length of the entry and the size of the sum, that whatever it held would change what I had to do before New Year's Eve.
