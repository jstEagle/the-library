## 28. Tamsin's Manifest

Tamsin Cole wrote a better hand than any clerk in Grayharbor, which was the sort of joke God liked to make — give the forger the gift and the daughter of the harbormaster the conscience to go with it.

She'd spread the papers across my workbench in the back room of the foundry, weighted at the corners with type cases because we had no paperweights worth the name, and she was copying the harbor office's manifest format from memory. Not from a copy. From memory. I had spent twelve years learning to cut letters in metal, and I still felt like an apprentice watching her draw a *J* with that little flourish at the tail, the one Harbor Officialdom used on everything from wharfage receipts to notices of seizure.

"Where did you learn that?" I asked. "The flourish."

"Darning my father's collars while he read manifests aloud over supper." She didn't look up. "He thinks I don't listen. He thinks I embroider."

"Do you?"

"Badly. It's why I'm good at this. A person who can't stitch a straight seam learns to compensate elsewhere."

Outside, the foundry floor belonged to six soldiers of the garrison who had been billeted on us since Winter Quarters began, and their boots creaked overhead where they'd claimed the drying loft. Emmerich was out — posted to the gate until evening — and the others had gone to the taverns to spend their pay before the century ended and someone invented new ways to tax it. We had perhaps two hours of privacy, which sounds generous until you understand that two hours is exactly long enough to commit a crime thoroughly or badly, and not nearly long enough to do both.

"You understand what this is," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question, but she wanted me to answer it anyway. People who are about to do something unforgivable like to be witnessed doing it correctly.

"It's the Juniper's departure manifest," I said. "Dated the thirty-first. Cargo listed as cooperage staves, salt fish, and household goods bound for the northern ports. Cleared by your father's office, countersigned by the duty officer."

"And if anyone compares it to the original book in my father's office—"

"They won't until she's past the point."

"They might," Tamsin said. "That's the part everyone forgets about forgery. The ink dries. The lie doesn't. It stays wet forever, and you walk around with it on your hands for the rest of your life, waiting for someone to ask you to shake theirs."

I let that sit. Wry commentary has its place, and there's a version of me — the one who talks to type cases at three in the morning — that would have made a joke about lead poisoning. But Tamsin wasn't being clever. She was counting the cost out loud so that it would be paid in front of a witness, and the least I owed her was silence while she counted.

"Discovery means transportation," she went on, in the flat voice of a girl reciting scripture. "Not the gallows, usually, for a woman. They send us to the colonies instead. Seven years, fourteen, life, depending on the magistrate's digestion. My father would die of it before the ship cleared the sound. Not the shame — the actual dying. His heart is bad. Everyone knows except him."

"Tamsin—"

"I'm not arguing." She set down the pen and flexed her fingers, and I saw they were steady. Steadier than mine. "I'm pricing it. You taught me that. Everything has a price list now; Osei made sure of that. So here's mine. My brother's name came off whatever lists exist, and you got that done. This is the other half of the bargain, and I'm paying it, and you're going to let me, and neither of us will ever speak of the price again once the ship sails. Agreed?"

"Agreed," I said, and hated how easily it came, and understood for the first time in my life what my father had meant when he said names are dangerous currency. Every agreement signs you. Every signature costs.

* * *

We worked.

The difficulty with forging an official document is not the letters. Letters are honest; they sit where you put them and mean what they say, and after twelve years among them I trust metal more than I trust most people. The difficulty is the error of perfection. A real manifest is written by a tired man in a cold office between other tasks. It hesitates. The ink pools where his pen rests too long at the end of a stroke. He crosses a seven because he's superstitious, or doesn't, because he isn't. A document with no errors announces itself the way a painted whore announces modesty.

So Tamsin built errors in, deliberately, and this was the part that frightened me most — not the risk but the craft of it, the way she'd pause over a word she'd written perfectly and then spoil it on purpose with a small tremor of the wrist.

"There," she said. "My father always blots the second line. Always. Twenty years of manifests and he never once kept a clean second line. If the forgery doesn't blot, he'll know it isn't his."

"Does he check his own work?"

"He checks everything since the requisition. He's frightened, Cyrus. Frightened men audit."

I took the finished sheet and held it near the lamp, tilting it so the light moved across the surface the way I'd learned to inspect cast type for hairline flaws. The paper was right — salvaged from a damaged shipment of harbor forms, aged under the stove with tea and smoke until it matched the office stock. The ink was ground from the same oak-gall recipe the town offices bought from the chandler. The seal was my contribution, pressed from a block I'd cut over four nights while pretending to trim ornamental sorts for a bookseller, and it was the finest piece of deception I have ever produced, and I try not to think about how proud that made me.

"Say it back to me," Tamsin said.

"The Juniper, brig, ninety tons, Captain E. Osei, clears harbor the thirty-first at the evening tide. Cargo: staves, salted herring in forty barrels, household effects of passengers repatriating north. No arms. No printed matter. Inspected at the gate per standing order and passed."

"'Inspected at the gate,'" she repeated. "That's the line that hangs us. That's you claiming the inspection already happened, so that when Vane's men look for the record of it, there's our paper saying it happened, and the absence of any other record becomes a clerical omission rather than a fraud. It only works if nobody asks the gate sergeant directly before midnight on the thirty-first."

"Nobody will ask. Nobody asks anything on New Year's Eve except where the rum's gone."

"Cyrus." She looked at me across the lamp, and her face did something I hadn't seen it do before — it aged, ten years in a breath, and I realized I was looking at the woman she'd be if we survived, and that woman was tired. "Vane will ask. You know that better than I do. He's been circling the foundry like a gull round a fish cart for two months. He announced the searches himself. Whatever he's waiting for, he'll come looking on the thirty-first precisely because everyone believes nobody works that night."

I put down the manifest. My hands wanted something to hold and there was nothing in the room that would help.

"Then we make the thirty-first boring," I said. "Boring enough to survive inspection. The Juniper carries half the evidence in your cooperage crates, sealed and manifested as staves, and if Vane opens them he finds exactly what the paper says he'll find, because I've packed staves myself, top layer, real ones, with the hollow barrels beneath. Boring is a craft, Tamsin. I've been apprenticed to it my whole life. Nobody ever suspected the quiet girl in the type shop of anything, and that was never an accident. That was design."

* * *

We hid the manifest that night inside a hollowed type case in the composing stone's cabinet — the same trick I'd used on the soldiers' cache, because the best hiding place is the one you've already survived a search with. Tamsin left through the yard gate at half past nine, walking the long way home along the seawall, and I stood in the doorway watching her go and thought about the word *accomplice*, which comes from the same root as *complicated*, or should.

Then I sat at my bench and did what I always do when the fear gets loud: I cut letters.

I was working on the counterfeit gate passes — chapter and verse of a dead man's authority, rendered in copperplate small enough to sew into a hem — when Emmerich came in early, stamping snow off his boots, and stopped dead at the smell of fresh ink.

"You've been busy," he said.

"Orders for the bookseller. New Year broadsides." I turned the composing stick toward him with a half-finished form set in display type: *A NEW CENTURY, A NEW MARKET — GRAYHARBOR PROVISIONERS.* It was even true, in the way that most of my lies were true these days, which is a condition I recommend to no one.

"Century," he said, tasting the word. "They say the old one ended badly."

"The old one ended the way they all do. Somebody kept records."

He laughed — a short, surprised laugh — and hung his coat on the peg by the stove, and asked nothing else, and I have wondered since whether that was decency or exhaustion or the particular mercy of a young man who has decided not to know things. With Emmerich I never did find out which. Perhaps he didn't either.

* * *

Two days before sailing, Tamsin came to the foundry gray-faced, with sleet freezing in her hair, and I knew before she spoke that something had gone wrong with the arithmetic of our plan.

"My father's been summoned," she said. "To the commandant. All shipping officers, all manifests, full review of every departure scheduled for the turn of the year." She pulled off her gloves finger by finger, and her hands, those steady forger's hands, were shaking now. "Cyrus, they know something's going to sail. They don't know what or when exactly, but they've narrowed it to the harbor and the holiday, and my father is going to be sitting in that room with every legitimate paper in his book while ours sits forged in a type case across town."

"Then we move the date."

"We can't. Osei won't sail early — he told you why. Tide and cargo and the patrol schedule, and besides, a brig leaving ahead of schedule is a confession with sails."

"Then we hold," I said. "We hold and we bore them. Your father reviews his manifests. Ours says staves and herring. Let him review it."

Tamsin sat down slowly on the casting bench, and for a moment she looked very young, younger than the crimes we'd both committed, and she said, quietly, "Do you know what transportation actually is? I looked it up in the justice manual in my father's office. It's not a voyage. It's being erased. They take your name off the town rolls. My mother died in the old country; there'd be no one to write to. If they take me, there won't be a Tamsin Cole anymore anywhere that matters. There'll just be a number on a colonial ledger, filed under convicts."

The lamp hissed. Upstairs, a soldier dropped a boot.

"Then we make sure the number never gets written," I said. And because she had priced her sacrifice honestly, I gave her the only thing I had left to give, which was the truth about myself — not the whole of it, but the shape of it. "I was born with another name, Tamsin. My father changed it to keep me alive, and for twelve years I thought that erasure was the worst thing that ever happened to anybody. But here's what I've learned cutting this winter: a name survives anywhere you can cut it. In metal. In a friend's memory. On a manifest signed by a girl whose father thinks she can't embroider. They can transport the body. They can't transport what we've already given away to each other."

Tamsin looked at me a long moment, and then she laughed — wet-eyed, furious laughter, the kind that keeps a person upright — and said, "That is the worst comfort anyone has ever offered me, and I'm going to keep it anyway."

* * *

On the last night of December's first week of waiting — for the plan held, and the days dragged, and every bell seemed determined to ring twice — we finished the final copies together: the manifest, the clearance paper, the inspection certificate, each in its duplicate, each aged and blotted and flawed in all the correct places. Tamsin signed her father's name eight times, and by the eighth she had stopped flinching at it.

"How does it feel?" I asked. "Signing a man who doesn't know?"

"Like cutting type in reverse," she said. "You make the letters, and then you unmake yourself a little to fit them." She blew the last signature dry and slid the papers into their oilcloth wrapper. Then, quieter: "When this is done — when the Juniper's past the point and my brother's debt is settled — I want it all back. The name. The clean hands. The whole dull honest life."

"You'll get it," I said. "That's what all this is for."

But even as I said it, I was looking past her, through the frosted window, at the harbor road, where lanterns were moving in a pattern I didn't like — too many, too orderly, the pattern of men searching something systematically rather than stumbling over it. And far out past the mole, where the Juniper rode at anchor, the wind had come around hard from the northeast, and the water in the roadstead had gone the color of wet slate.

The barometer at the chandlery had been falling all day. Old sailors say you can hear a gale coming before it arrives, a low note under the wind, like a bell rung underwater.

I heard it that night, and I thought: *three more days. We only need three more days.*

The sea, as I would shortly learn, had made other arrangements.
