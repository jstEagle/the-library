## 29. The Storm Delay

The wind arrived on the twenty-ninth of December like a creditor who had finally located our address.

I heard it first in the type racks — a low hum through the lead, as though the letters themselves were shivering — and then the shutters began their clattering argument with the frames, and then came a sound I had never heard the harbor make before: not water against the quay but water *slapping* it, furious, over and over, like someone slapping a table mid-quarrel.

By morning the fleet was still in port. All of it. The Juniper sat at her mooring three hundred yards from my window, tugging at her lines like a dog that has smelled dinner two streets away and cannot understand why everyone else remains seated.

Three more days. The century would turn without us.

* * *

I went down to the quay at dawn, or what passed for dawn under that sky — a gray so thick it seemed less like weather than like an opinion the heavens had formed about Grayharbor. The gale had scattered the fishing boats up onto the strand where they lay on their sides like beached whales with opinions of their own. Sailors moved along the docks with the particular hunched walk of men arguing with God and losing.

Tamsin was already at the harbor office when I got there, wrapped in her father's old coat, watching the anemometer — a cup-and-spoke contraption her brother had built before he became the sort of person who built things for the army instead — spin itself into a blur.

"Ninety miles an hour at the capes," she said, without turning around. "Maybe more. No captain with sense puts out in this."

"Osei isn't known for sense."

"He's known for living. That's better." She turned then, and I saw she hadn't slept, which made two of us, except that Tamsin's sleeplessness looked like competence while mine, I suspect, looks like a woman who has been staring at the ceiling composing her own epitaph. "The manifests are done, Cyrus. Dated for tomorrow night, the thirty-first. If the ship sails on the second instead, I have to re-cut them, and if the army extends its search—"

"I know."

"You don't know. You know type and you know lying. I know paper. Paper is unforgiving. Every date is a confession waiting to be read."

There was something almost religious in how she said it. I have noticed that people become mystics about their own trades. My father speaks of molten lead alloy the way bishops speak of transubstantiation, and I once caught myself explaining kerning to a soldier with the fervor of a woman describing the face of a saint.

"Then we sail tomorrow," I said. "The wind will turn."

"The wind," Tamsin said, "does not take requests."

* * *

I found Captain Osei aboard the Juniper, lashed to the rail by the simple expedient of holding on with both hands and looking annoyed about it. He was shorter than I remembered from our first meeting, though I suspect this was the storm's doing; there are men the wind makes smaller out of spite.

"You've heard," I said.

"I've heard the sky rehearsing its grievances all night, Miss Underhill. Yes." He gestured at the harbor with his chin. "Look at that anchorage. Half those captains will drag anchor before morning. If I put out now, I'm a wreck, and wrecks carry no ledgers anywhere except to the bottom, where the fish can read them at their leisure. Fish are poor archivists."

"So we wait."

"We wait." He said it heavily, the way other men say *we bleed*. "Three days is not merely three days, Miss Underhill. It is three days of Vane walking his checkpoints, three days of sailors drinking their wages and saying too much, three days of that boy Emmerich standing post trying to look like he sees nothing. Time does not pass neutrally in an occupied town. It accumulates. Like debt. Like rust."

I wanted to argue. Instead I watched a wave come over the breakwater and detonate against a bollard ten feet from us, spraying salt across my face, and decided the sea had joined the argument on Osei's side.

"What do we do?" I asked.

"We do what cargo does," he said. "We stay dry, we stay hidden, and we hold very still."

* * *

Holding still, as it happens, is the one thing I have never been good at. Give me a graver and a block of type-metal and I can achieve a stillness monks would envy, but ask me to sit in a requisitioned foundry with half the evidence of forty families' innocence sewn into my coat lining and nothing to do but wait, and I begin to come apart at the seams — some of them literal, since I had been sleeping in the coat and it was starting to complain.

The soldiers billeted in the shop felt the delay too. Storms make soldiers superstitious. They gathered around the stove in the evenings and told stories about drownings with the relish of men who had never met one, and every time the wind hit the north wall hard enough to shift the type cases on their shelves, someone would cross himself, and someone else would laugh at him for it, and both of them would glance at the dark windows as if the wind were taking notes.

Emmerich did not tell stories. He sat near me — not *near* me, near the stove, with the accident of geography placing him within conversational distance — and mended a boot sole with a patience I envied.

"Your harbor's angry," he said at last.

"It's not my harbor."

"Everything's somebody's harbor," he said, which was either philosophy or homesickness, and with Emmerich the two had never been distinguishable.

* * *

On the thirtieth the gale eased enough to let the harbor master's men check the moorings, and I used the lull to do what I had been avoiding: I went to see Tamsin about the manifests, and found her not at the office but at home, sitting at a table covered in shipping papers, holding one of her brother's letters.

She didn't explain why. She just pushed the letter toward me across the table, the way you'd push a dish you couldn't eat from.

It was short. It was warm. It asked after their father's cough and mentioned, in passing, that the writer hoped for a posting away from Grayharbor soon because "the work here sits poorly." That was all. It could have been any young man's letter to any sister, and that was precisely the problem. Informants do not sound like informants. They sound like brothers.

"He doesn't know what he's listed as," Tamsin said. "In the ledger. Does he?"

"No. It's coded, and the key—" I stopped myself. Some truths are still mine to ration. "He may never know."

"But it'll be published. Abroad. And anyone with the key—" She folded the letter along its existing creases, over and over, until it was a small hard square she could have used as a nail. "When I falsify these manifests, Cyrus, I'm committing a crime for the sake of forty families and one informant I'd rather see transported than hanged, and I cannot even explain to myself which part of that is mercy and which part is just being unable to stop loving someone inconvenient."

"I don't think you have to explain it," I said. "Love rarely files its paperwork."

That startled a laugh out of her — short, wet, real. Then she squared her shoulders, pulled a fresh sheet of manifest paper toward her, and said, "Thirty-first. If the wind turns, the Juniper sails tomorrow night and these papers had better be perfect."

"They will be."

"Of course they will," she said grimly. "I'm making them."

* * *

That night the wind dropped altogether, which was somehow worse. A gale at least announces itself. The silence that fell over Grayharbor on the eve of the century's turning was the silence of held breath, and I lay awake in it listening to the foundry tick and settle around me, thinking about my father in the cells across town, and about Vane, who had not come to the shop in nine days, and about the peculiar mathematics of hope — how three days of delay had subtracted nothing from our plan and yet somehow made it feel halved.

Near midnight I gave up on sleep and went down to the workshop, lit the lamp, and pulled out the remaining cache — the blocks sewn into my coat, laid out on the bench like a surgeon laying out instruments. Forty families. Names in reverse, cut by my father's hand over twelve years, readable only in impression, only by someone who knew the cipher, only by me.

I had learned the last key from him through the bars. *Your name*, he'd said. *Juniper. The tree that survives everything, that grows in cracks, that no one plants and no one can pull.* The cipher had been under my nose my whole life, spelled out in what everyone assumed was simply a girl's odd christening.

I was checking the impressions against my memorized list — again, for the fourth time, because repetition is what fear does instead of praying — when I heard the front door.

Not forced. Unlocked. Which meant either my father returned in the night by means available to no prisoner in Grayharbor, or someone had a key, and only the army had keys now.

I killed the lamp and stood in the dark with a type block in my fist, feeling deeply ridiculous: armed, if it came to it, with a single letter *Q*.

Footsteps crossed the shop. Stopped at the threshold of the workshop. And then a match flared, and Aldous Vane stood in the doorway holding a candle he had lit from our own stove coals, courteous as ever, terrible as ever, looking at the bench where my lamp had just gone out.

"Miss Underhill," he said mildly. "Working late. Or perhaps working early — it is, after all, nearly a new century." His eyes moved across the bench, the scattered cases, the coat draped over the chair with its suspicious weight. "Forgive the intrusion. I've taken the liberty of a key. These are uncertain times for unlocked buildings."

He smiled. I smiled back, and somewhere behind my ribs I felt the whole winter tilt, three days of held breath threatening to go out all at once.

"I couldn't sleep," I said. "The quiet after a storm always wakes me."

"Yes," Vane said, stepping into the workshop, bringing his candle toward the bench. "It has that effect on thoughtful people."
