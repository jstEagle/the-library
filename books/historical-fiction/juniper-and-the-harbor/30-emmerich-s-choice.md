## 30. Emmerich's Choice

The soldiers had been billeted in the foundry for six weeks by then, and I had learned to read their moods the way I read the grain of a type-high block — by touch, by the small telltale flaws. Sergeant Brauer's mood was always *hungry*. Corporal Fenn's was always *put upon*. And Emmerich's mood, that winter, was a thing I could not name for a long while, because I had never before seen a man carry guilt about something he had not yet done.

He did his rounds faithfully. Every third morning he walked the shop from the casting bench to the type racks with his musket slung and his ledger book open, counting cases of type against Vane's inventory lists, and every third morning he wrote down exactly what he saw, because Emmerich did not know how to write down anything but what he saw. This was the quality that made him dangerous and decent in equal measure. Honesty is a virtue in men; in occupying soldiers it is a public hazard.

I had hidden the blocks well. Twelve years of cutting metal teaches you where a secret can live: inside the hollowed cases beneath the third-floor eaves, behind a false back in the drawer of German pica, wrapped in oiled cloth that smelled of the tallow we used for the molds. The room itself was unremarkable — a storeroom of misfit furniture, broken galleys, the great iron frame of a press my grandfather had bought secondhand and never assembled. If you went looking for contraband, your eye slid off that room like water off waxed paper.

But Vane had ordered a full inventory of the upper floors. Vane, who noticed everything, had begun to notice the shape of what he *didn't* notice, which is the most frightening kind of noticing there is.

* * *

It happened on a Tuesday, if the army's calendar can be trusted, which is doubtful — they had declared two Sundays in one week in December to catch smugglers at their devotions. Snow came down in the flat, businesslike way of Grayharbor snow, the kind that means to stay until spring whether anyone likes it or not.

Emmerich climbed the stairs at nine o'clock. I heard him pass the casting floor, heard his boots on the third landing, and then I heard them stop.

I was downstairs at the furnace, ostensibly skimming dross, actually listening so hard that my whole body had become an ear. My father was across the shop pretending to sort quoins, which meant he was standing very still over a box of wooden wedges with his hands folded, the posture of a man praying to a god of small carpentry.

The boots moved again. Slow. Pausing. The creak of the false-backed drawer — I heard that too, the little sigh of wood that I had waxed precisely so it would *not* speak, and which spoke anyway, because wood keeps its own counsel.

Then nothing. A long nothing. Long enough for me to count my own heartbeat up to forty and lose faith at thirty-one.

When Emmerich came down the stairs, he was carrying his ledger open, and he stopped at the foot of the stairs and looked at me over the top of it. He had a young face doing an old man's work — twenty-two at the outside, with a farm boy's sunburn long since surrendered to harbor pallor, and hands that were too careful for a soldier's. He held his pencil the way my father held a graver.

"Frau Underhill," he said. He had never managed to settle on what I was — *Fräulein* seemed to embarrass him, *frau* seemed inaccurate, and *Cyrus* he considered too intimate for a billeted man to use. "Your third floor. It is storage only?"

"Storage," I said. "Furniture nobody wants. My grandfather's taste in purchases was worse than his eyesight."

"Yes." He looked at his ledger. Looked at me. There was a particular silence available to him in that moment — the silence of a man deciding — and I stood inside it and felt it close over my head like harbor water.

"Clean," he said, and wrote something. I have wondered since what word he actually set down on that line. *Clean*, I suppose. Or perhaps nothing at all — just the date, and a blank where a finding should go, which is its own kind of testimony.

He clicked his heels — not a salute, exactly; Emmerich's heel-clicks had a way of being apologies — and went out into the snow.

* * *

You will want to know why. So did I. For three days I turned it over like a coin I suspected of being counterfeit, and found no seam.

The obvious answer was that he hadn't found anything. Men miss things; rooms are good at hiding; the oiled cloth and the hollowed cases had been built by a frightened, meticulous girl and her own twelve years of practice at being invisible. Possible. But I had heard the drawer sigh, and I had heard the boots stop moving for the length of time it takes a man to unwrap something and understand what he is holding. Coded names cut into type blocks do not explain themselves to a farm boy from the interior, but they don't need to. Suspicion is enough. Suspicion is all Vane had ever needed.

So: he had found them, or near enough, and said nothing.

I tested him after that, the way you test ice at the edge of a pond. Small things. I let him see me watching the stair door. I left the third-floor room unlocked when protocol demanded it locked, and reported it myself, blushing at my negligence — a lie handed to him gift-wrapped, which he accepted without comment, which was itself an answer. Once, passing him his supper ration, I said, "My grandfather's old press frame upstairs — should we break it up for firewood? It's dead iron." And Emmerich, spooning gray soup, said, "No. Leave it. Rooms remember their purpose," and ate his soup and would not look at me, and I understood that we were having a conversation in a language with no words in it, conducted entirely in the pauses between ordinary sentences.

* * *

He came to the counter on the evening of the fourth day, after lights-out, when the only honest light left in the shop was the banked glow of the furnace. He wasn't in uniform — well, he was, but without the crossbelt and the bayonet, which among these men was the equivalent of arriving naked. He stood with his cap in his hands, turning it, and I thought: *here it comes.* Either the arrest or the bargain. With soldiers those are usually the same thing wearing different coats.

"I've put in for transfer," he said.

I kept my hands on the counter, where he could see them, though there was nothing in them but lamp oil smell. "Transfer."

"East. The river garrison. They need men for the ice patrols." He turned the cap another quarter turn. "Sergeant Brauer says no one volunteers for the ice patrols. He asked me why."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I'm a fool from the interior who's never seen a proper frozen river." He almost smiled. Almost. "It has the advantage of being true."

"And the other advantage," I said, because I am not a generous woman and never have been, "of putting forty miles between yourself and this shop."

The cap stopped turning.

"Frau Underhill." He said it carefully, as if setting the word in type, checking it wouldn't print crooked. "In the storeroom. Behind the German pica. There is a case with a false—"

"Don't," I said. Not loudly. But the furnace chose that moment to shift its coals, and we both watched the orange light move on his face, and neither of us finished anything.

"—is very dusty," he completed, softly. "Rooms should be dusted more often."

We stood in the ruin of our mutual euphemism, two people pretending very hard that no sentence had occurred. Then Emmerich straightened, and I saw him gather himself the way a man gathers himself before cold water.

"My family are farmers," he said. "Near Aldenmoor. We are not political. When the requisition came through our valley, they took the autumn store and shot two of my father's oxen for the commissary, and the officer apologized. That is the part I think about. He *apologized*. Very courteously. He had a nice voice." He looked at the furnace. "I enlisted because a recruiter told me the war would be over by spring and the pay was real money. It is four winters now. I have learned that courtesy and cruelty can share a coat, and I have learned that I am not made for this work. Some men aren't. Nobody warns you that discovering this is also a kind of wound."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're going to ask me why I didn't report the room, and I wanted you to hear the reason from me instead of inventing it." He put his cap on, finally, which somehow made him smaller. "I didn't report it because I opened that case and found names. Dozens of names. Cut in metal, backwards, patient as prayer. And I understood that whoever cut them had spent years — *years* — making something small enough to hide and important enough to kill for, and had done it one letter at a time, at night, alone." His jaw worked. "My mother preserved fruit that way. One jar at a time. All summer. Against a winter she couldn't name yet. When I saw your blocks, Frau Underhill, I did not see rebellion. I saw somebody's mother, putting food by."

"They aren't my mother's," I said, which was true, and also, in the way that mattered to a man like Emmerich, completely false.

"No," he agreed. "But somebody's."

He clicked his heels. At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"The transfer will come through within the week. After that, whatever inspections come here will be Vane's men, not mine. Vane sees everything." He was quiet a moment. "I want you to understand — I'm not brave. Brave men stay and fight for rooms like yours. I'm asking to be posted elsewhere. That's all. I just—" The latch clicked under his fingers. "I'd rather freeze honestly on a river than thaw comfortably in a lie."

"You'll do both," I said. "That's what winter is."

He laughed — once, short, surprised at himself — and went out into the snow, and I listened to his footsteps diminish down Lantern Row until the fog took them, and then I stood a long time in the red light of the furnace with my heart going like a trip-hammer.

Complicity, I have since decided, is passed like a candle. Not thrown, not seized — *handed*, carefully, flame to wick, each person shielding it with a cupped palm and both of them aware that the wind is everywhere and the dark is hungry. Emmerich had lit me and gone. I stood holding his small fire and understood that I now owed a debt I could never repay to a man whose surname I had never learned.

* * *

My father found me still at the furnace past midnight. He did that sometimes now — came down in his nightshirt and greatcoat, ghosting through the shop he no longer owned, touching things. Since his arrest had become a matter of *when* rather than *if* — since Vane's fragments from the burned print shop had started appearing in his dreams, he said — he had taken to wandering the foundry like a man taking leave of a dying relative.

"Emmerich," I said. "He found the room."

My father's hand stilled on the rack of long primer. For a moment he was entirely silent, and in that silence I heard everything the town sounded like at night: rigging ticking against masts, a dog somewhere by the fish market, the army's patrol boots keeping their stupid perfect time.

"He found it," I repeated, "and he asked for a transfer, and he told me why, and then he left. Father. *Father.* He's gone and he said nothing and I don't know whether to be grateful or terrified."

"Both," my father said. "Gratitude and terror are sisters. They visit together or not at all." He lowered himself onto the casting bench with the groan of a man twice his age, and looked into the coals. "Did he say what he saw?"

"Names. He said it looked like someone's mother putting food by."

My father made a sound that might have been a laugh if grief hadn't gotten to it first. "A farmer's son. Of course. Only a farmer would recognize the work for what it is — everyone else would see treason and miss the labor entirely." He rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands. "He could have bought his sergeantcy with that find, do you know? A promotion. A purse. Officers get purses for finds like that. Instead he asked to stand on a frozen river." He shook his head slowly. "There are more decent men in that army than the army knows what to do with. That's the tragedy of armies, Cyrus. They are mostly good people, arranged badly."

"Vane isn't."

"No," my father said, with the mildness of absolute certainty. "Vane is what happens when a clever man decides that seeing clearly entitles him to be cruel clearly. He thinks he is the only honest man in Grayharbor." He looked at me then, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen since my mother died — a kind of urgent tenderness, a man memorizing. "And Vane is close, love. You feel it. Fragments surfacing. Inspections tightening. A man like that doesn't knock on the door when he's ready — he brings the door down and apologizes to the hinges."

"Then we move faster."

"We?" He smiled sadly. "You keep saying *we*. There is no *we*, Cyrus. There is you, and there is an old man waiting to be arrested, and the only question left in my case is how much of the sentence I take onto myself before it lands on you."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly how it works," he said gently. "Listen to me. Emmerich gave you days, not weeks. When he's gone, Vane puts his own men on these floors — quiet ones, thorough ones, men who dust." He reached out and took my hand, and his fingers were ink-stained and furnace-scarred and shaking very slightly, and I let him hold them because he was my father and because he was afraid. "Whatever remains to be done, do it as though tomorrow morning the soldiers arrive with the door. Because someday soon, that morning comes."

He went back up to bed. I stayed by the furnace until the coals went from orange to gray, and then I climbed to the third floor with a lamp and unwrapped the blocks one more time, just to count them, just to feel their weight in my hands — my mother's winter store, my father's insurance, my inheritance in lead.

Forty families. Three checkpoints. Nine days to the century's turn.

And below me, faint through the floorboards, I heard the gate bell ring out of hours — the specific, doubled toll that meant a rider had arrived at night with dispatches — and heard, a few minutes later, boots in the street below stopping before our door, and a fist beginning to knock.

Not Vane's knock. Not yet. But the night had teeth in it now, and everyone in Grayharbor could hear them.
