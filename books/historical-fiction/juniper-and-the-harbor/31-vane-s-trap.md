## 31. Vane's Trap

The notice went up on the foundry door on the morning of the twenty-ninth of December, and I read it three times before my hands stopped being mine.

*By order of the Provisional Authority of the Harbor District: in consequence of irregularities observed in coastal traffic, a complete inspection of all vessels departing the port shall commence on the thirty-first day of December. All cargo, manifests, passengers, and persons shall be subject to search without exception. By command of Inspector Aldous Vane.*

*Without exception.* Two words doing the work of forty.

I had learned to read army paper the way one learns to read weather — not the words themselves but what they meant for the day ahead. This was not weather. This was the sky falling with excellent penmanship.

My father stood at the case beside me, pulling a proof of a cooper's invoice — legitimate work, we still did some, the army having discovered that even occupation requires receipts — and he read it over my shoulder the way he read everything now, slightly sideways, as if looking at things straight-on might cost him.

"He knows," I said quietly.

"He suspects," my father corrected. "There is a difference, and it is the only difference you have left."

"Then it is a very thin difference."

"All the important ones are." He set his composing stick down. "Cyrus. If he knew, we would already be in the cells beside me — or rather, I would be back in them and you would be joining me. Vane does not announce what he knows. He announces what he wants frightened people to reveal."

I thought about that. It was, in its way, the most useful thing anyone had said to me all winter, and my father delivered it in the flat tone of a man discussing ink viscosity, because that was how he delivered everything now — the arrest had used him up in some way the amnesty hadn't refilled, and what remained was precision without warmth, a lamp burning low but steady.

Except this was before the arrest. Let me keep my winters in order. This was the twenty-ninth, and Father still slept in his own bed above the shop, and Emmerich still came down the stairs each morning with the guilty carefulness of a boy billeted in a stranger's house, and the storm had blown itself out two days prior leaving the harbor glazed and glittering like something a jeweler would charge too much for, and the *Juniper* still rode at anchor with half my family history sewn into her hold.

Three days. The gale had stolen three days from me, and now Vane wanted to take the rest.

* * *

Tamsin came at noon, under cover of picking up her father's order of numbering type — a plausible errand, since the harbormaster's office did genuinely consume numbers the way other offices consumed paper. She came in flushed from the cold and from something else, and she did not wait until we were alone in the back room before she gripped my sleeve, which told me everything, because Tamsin Grieg touched people in public exactly never.

"The harbor office," she said. "He came this morning. Vane himself."

"And?"

"And he read every manifest for December. Every one. Then he sat down at my father's desk — *sat down*, Cyrus, in my father's own chair, drank my father's terrible coffee and complimented it, which is somehow worse than insulting it — and he said, 'Curious thing about ships, Miss Grieg. They are like promises. The ones that matter most are the ones most often delayed.'"

I felt the floor do something unreliable. "He knows about the delay."

"He knows a ship delayed. There were eleven of them, thanks to the storm. But then—" she glanced toward the front of the shop, where a soldier's boots were audibly pretending to examine font samples, and lowered her voice to nearly nothing, "—then he asked which of the eleven had taken on fresh water and provisions *after* the storm cleared. Which is only done by ships planning to leave soon. Only four did that, and one of them is the *Juniper*."

"Can your father alter the manifest again? Postpone her officially?"

"To when? The century turns in two days. Osei won't stay past it — he told you so himself, and a man like Osei doesn't say a thing twice." Tamsin pulled me back behind the type racks, into the smell of lead dust and lamp oil that I had lately realized was the smell of my entire life. "Listen to me. He also posted extra watches at the gate. Doubled them. And there's a new countersign as of this morning — my father has it, and I've copied it, but Cyrus, the countersign changes *again* on the thirty-first. At midnight. With everything else."

Of course it did. The century would turn and the army would turn with it, all its locks re-keyed, all its passwords reborn, so that any document forged before the new year would be worthless after it and anything forged for after could not be tested beforehand. It was elegant. I hated how elegant it was.

"So the pass must be perfect," I said, "and it must be used on the thirty-first, at the one checkpoint where Vane himself may be standing."

"Yes."

"And the crates?"

"Loaded tomorrow night. My cousin drives the cart, same as planned. He doesn't know what's in them and he can't know, so don't look at me like that when he stammers at the soldiers — he stammers at everyone, it's practically his trade." She paused, and the humor drained out of her face the way color drains out of a sunset. "Cyrus. He's not doing this because he deduced things. Deduction is work. Vane doesn't do work he can avoid. Someone told him a ship sails on New Year's Eve."

The word *someone* sat between us like a fourth person.

"Who?" I asked. "Who could know?"

"You know who could know. Everyone who knows is standing within ten feet of you right now, plus one captain with a crew of twenty-six, plus my cousin, plus—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening. "No. No, I won't do this. We don't eat each other. That's what he wants, the suspicion, everyone chewing on everyone until the circle eats itself. He said something like that once, did you know that? In my father's office, drinking the terrible coffee. He said, 'A town betrays itself, Miss Grieg. An inspector merely holds the mirror.' I wanted to pour the pot over his head."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because my brother works for these people," she said simply, "and I have learned what it costs to be the person who pours the pot."

* * *

Emmerich came to the back room that evening, ostensibly to report that his sergeant required the billet register signed, actually, I think, because he had been carrying a question around for days the way one carries a stone in a shoe.

"They posted the notice at the barracks too," he said. "Full inspection. Everyone's talking about it."

"Are they."

"Some of the men are pleased. Search duty means picking through cargo, and picking through cargo means..." He didn't finish, and didn't need to. Confiscation was the army's second-favorite word after requisition, and they meant nearly the same thing depending on whose pockets opened.

"And you?" I asked. "Are you pleased?"

He looked at the type cases, the great silent walls of them, thousands upon thousands of letters that had never said a word aloud. "I drew gate duty for the thirty-first," he said. "That's why I'm telling you. Not — I'm not telling you anything. There's nothing to tell. Just that I'll be there, at the gate, from dusk onward, and Sergeant Brandt will be there, and Brandt searches like he's owed money by whatever he's searching."

"Thank you," I said.

"For what? I told you, there's nothing—"

"For the nothing," I said, and had the strange pleasure of watching him almost smile before he remembered his face was supposed to belong to an occupying army.

When he'd gone, I sat a long while with the lamp turned low. Emmerich at the gate. That was either the worst news of the winter or the best, and I genuinely could not tell which, and the fact that I could not tell which — that I now evaluated a decent young man's presence at a checkpoint by calculating how his conscience might serve me — said something about this year that I decided I was not yet ready to hear.

* * *

Vane came to the foundry himself on the thirtieth.

He did not come in uniform. He came in a gray civilian coat with a fur collar, carrying a wrapped parcel under one arm like a man calling on a neighbor to borrow sugar, and he stood in the doorway of the workshop breathing in the smell of molten lead with an expression I can only call appreciation, and I hated him a little for appreciating it, because it was *my* smell, and he had no right to love the thing he intended to destroy.

"Mistress Underhill," he said. He had called me that since autumn, always with the faintest stress on *Mistress*, a small knife he thought I didn't notice. "Master Underhill. Don't rise, please. I've come about the inspection — courtesy, nothing more. We find that tradesmen comply more readily when the inconvenience is explained face to face."

"We are a foundry, Inspector," my father said. "We ship type, not cargo."

"Type is cargo, Master Underhill. Everything is cargo; that is rather the discovery of the age." He unwrapped the parcel on our workbench with the deliberation of a surgeon laying out instruments, and inside was type. Loose type, perhaps two hundred pieces, jumbled, scorched at the edges.

I knew it at once. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting. The burned print shop — Marlowe and Finch's shop, arrested in November, burned the same night while the neighborhood watched its windows glow and studied its shoes. Some of that metal had gone into the fire and some into evidence bags, and here was a fistful of the evidence, sitting on our bench.

"Fragments recovered from the fire," Vane said pleasantly. "Mixed type, mostly ordinary, but you'll observe some of it bears unusual marks. Punches cut off-register. Private marks. The sort of thing a foundry might recognize and a layman might not." He turned his pale eyes on my father. "I wonder — could you tell me which founder made these?"

My father picked up a piece. His hands, which had shaken for weeks after the arrest and had only lately steadied, were steady now. He turned the type over, held it to the light, set it down.

"Cheap work," he said. "Whoever cut it rushed the serifs. Not ours."

"Not yours." Vane smiled. "You see, this is what fascinates me about your profession. Every letter in the district was born in one of three foundries, and yet none of you ever remembers making anything. It is as if the whole town writes in invisible ink." He began wrapping the parcel again, unhurried. "Tomorrow evening we begin the inspections. Every vessel. Every crate. Every passenger. I mention it to you especially, Mistress Underhill, because I understand your establishment sometimes sends out deliveries, and deliveries require passes, and passes will be examined with a thoroughness that may surprise even honest folk." He tied the string in a small neat bow. "One tries to assume good faith. One is usually rewarded, eventually. Eventually is the operative word — it always arrives, though rarely on the schedule anyone prefers."

At the door he paused, half-turned, and said, without any particular emphasis: "Your shop, Master Underhill, sits closer to the harbor than any other foundry in Grayharbor. Curious geography, for a business whose customers are printers. Almost as curious as a ship taking on water after a storm." And then he tipped his hat, wished us a happy new century, and was gone into the fog, leaving the parcel behind.

My father stared at it. So did I.

"He left it," I said. "Why would he leave it?"

"Because it isn't evidence anymore," my father said slowly. "It's bait. He wants to see whether we dispose of it. A foundry receiving suspicious type would melt it down immediately — destroy it, bury it, make it vanish. If that type disappears by morning, he learns the Underhills had reason to fear it." He sat down heavily on his stool. "If it stays on that bench, we're merely honest tradesmen baffled by a gift. God help us. He has built a trap out of *politeness*."

"Then it stays," I said.

"It stays," my father agreed. "And Cyrus—" he caught my arm, and his grip was stronger than I expected, "—whatever leaves this shop tomorrow night must not be traceable to this shop. Not the crates, not the papers, not you. Do you understand me? He has narrowed his world to this street. He lacks proof, so he will manufacture it, and the first careless thing we do becomes his proof."

"I understand."

"I don't think you do." For a moment something moved behind his eyes that was not the diminished man of recent months but the old one, the one who had renamed a daughter and buried a wife and cut twelve years of secrets into spare blocks, waiting for exactly this winter. "Vane is not stupid, and he is not cruel, which makes him twice as dangerous as both together. Stupid men make mistakes you can use. Cruel men want things you can buy. Vane wants only to be *right*, and a man who wants to be right cannot be bought and cannot be fooled forever — only outrun."

* * *

I did not sleep that night, or rather I slept the way a sentry sleeps, in shallow installments, and somewhere past two in the morning I gave it up and went down to the workshop with the parcel still sitting on the bench like a sleeping animal I didn't trust.

I turned the lamp low and took out my own work instead — the pass, my pass, the third version, cut and recut until the counterfeited signature flowed the way real signatures flow, with the tiny hesitation a tired clerk makes at the crossbar. Tomorrow night it would carry me through the gate, or hang me at it. I added the last refinement now: a smudge of genuine grime along one edge, because documents too clean confess themselves.

And then, because I could not help myself, I took one block from the parcel — just one, a battered piece with a private mark cut off-register — and pressed it into my proofing pad, and pulled a proof, and looked at the reversed impression in the lamplight.

It was not code. It was worse. It was a maker's mark, a cutter's signature, and I recognized the hand the way Tamsin would recognize her brother's step on the stairs. Old Finch — old half-deaf Finch who had bought type from us for fifteen years and complained about the price every single time. These were *our* blocks, sold to him years ago, and Vane knew it, and the question wasn't whether my father remembered making them.

The question was how long Vane had been holding them, and what answer he was waiting for the town to give.

I set the block back in the parcel exactly where it had lain, adjusted two of its neighbors by a hair to match my memory of the arrangement, and stood in the cold workshop listening to the harbor bells count three, and understood, finally, the shape of the thing.

Vane did not need to find the ledger. He needed only to make sure that nothing sailed tomorrow night — no ship, no crates, no girl with sewn seams in her coat. He would stand at the mouth of the harbor with the whole machinery of the army behind him and let the clock run, because the century was turning whether I liked it or not, and Osei would not wait past it, and every hour that passed tightened the net one mesh further.

He lacked proof. But he had time, and soldiers, and patience, and me — one girl, one night, one gate.

Outside, snow began again, soft and indifferent, covering the streets of Grayharbor the way fog always had: gently, thoroughly, hiding everything and revealing nothing, and I put out the lamp and climbed the stairs to lie awake beside the last hours of the old century, rehearsing the walk to the gate.
