## 32. Dividing the Ledger

There is a particular arithmetic that belongs to people who have spent their lives around type. Most arithmetic is about abundance — how much more, how much gained. Ours was always about scarcity. How many ems fit in a case, how many lines on a page, how many days until the army did the thing armies do. And now: how much evidence can one girl carry sewn into a wool coat without walking like a quilt.

The answer, I had determined by candlelight on the last night of December, was eleven pounds if I wanted to keep my shoulders, fourteen if I was willing to walk like a woman who had recently lost an argument with a horse.

I had laid everything out on the composing stone the way my father lays out tools before a job: every coded block, every cut name, twelve years of my father's secret handwriting in metal, sorted into piles by my own system — the families in most danger, the names already burned, the entries that implicated men still breathing and still powerful. The lamp guttered and steadied. Snow ticked against the shutters like a patient clerk.

The trouble with dividing a thing is that you must first admit it cannot be kept whole.

* * *

Tamsin came at midnight, as arranged, letting herself in through the yard door with the ease of someone who had been sneaking into this foundry since we were both small enough to hide in the paper bins. She brought cold air, a loaf of bread she claimed was for me but which we both knew was for her nerves, and her father's second-best crate stencil.

"Your father's crates," she said, setting it down. "Fish oil. Nobody opens fish oil crates unless they're paid to."

"Nobody opens fish oil crates at all," I said. "That's the whole genius of fish."

She looked at the composing stone, at the little metal city of names I'd built there, and something in her face went carefully blank. I had watched her do that more and more lately — arrange her features into neutrality the way a printer arranges sorts into a stick. We were learning each other's trades.

"That's all of them?" she asked.

"Half of them." I told her the plan while she tore bread she didn't eat. The Juniper would sail New Year's Eve, God and Osei willing, and in her hold would go Tamsin's crates — six crates of "fish oil" for a buyer in Falmouth who did not exist, each crate lined with type blocks packed in sawdust. Twenty-one blocks. Eleven names. The families in the deepest water, the evidence against Vane's own superiors, the entries that would hang a man if read aloud in a courtroom and free forty households if read aloud in a newspaper abroad.

"And you take the rest," Tamsin said.

"I take the rest."

"On your body."

"In my coat. Which has become a very serious coat."

"Cyrus." She put the bread down. That was how I knew it was bad; Tamsin abandoning food was like the tide abandoning the harbor — a sign of something fundamentally wrong. "If they search the crates and find nothing, you've lost half. If they search you—"

"They won't search both of us the same way. That's the point. A crate gets opened with a knife. A body gets searched with hands, and hands need a reason, and reasons need suspicion, and I have spent twelve years learning how to be a person nobody bothers to suspect." I heard my own voice come out flat and certain, and I realized I had rehearsed this speech. I had rehearsed it in the dark, to the lamp, to my father's empty chair. "But if I'm taken — or if Osei's ship turns tail at the first navy pennant, which he'd deny but might do — then what's on me survives. And if I'm searched and they somehow miss it, what's in the crates survives. Two halves. Neither knows about the other. Whichever half lives, the ledger lives."

Tamsin was quiet for a moment. Outside, a patrol went by on the cobbles, boots keeping time, and we both did the thing everyone in Grayharbor did now, which was stop talking until the boots faded.

"You've made yourself divisible," she said finally.

"I've made the truth divisible. There's a difference." Though even as I said it, I wasn't sure there was. My mother's name was in the half going to the ship. My father's confession — his own account of why he'd cut the ledger, written in cipher in the margins of three blocks — was in the half staying with me. I had divided myself along the same seams and called it strategy.

"It's good strategy," Tamsin said grudgingly, because she was honest even when honesty annoyed her. "It's terrible for you."

"Yes," I said. "Most good strategy is."

* * *

We worked. There is a strange intimacy in packing secrets into a fish oil crate with another girl at midnight, both of you kneeling in sawdust in your dead mother's foundry while soldiers sleep two rooms away, snoring in shifts. We wrapped each block in wool waste, nested them in the crates, and Tamsin stenciled FALMOUTH — PICKERING & SONS over each lid in her father's hand, which she could imitate better than her father could, a fact neither of us said aloud because some truths are load-bearing and shouldn't be jostled.

Block by block, I told her what she was carrying. Not the cipher — she didn't need the key and keys are dangerous currency, my father taught me that before I could write — but the weight of it. The Merriweather family, whose name sat in a block no bigger than my thumb, who ran the chandlery and had fed us pies every Michaelmas for a decade. The Harrow schoolmaster. The two widows on Cordwainer Lane who were almost certainly guilty of nothing except buying type from a shop the army had decided was interesting.

"So if the Juniper makes it," Tamsin said, pressing down the sawdust with both hands like she was tucking children into bed, "these people just... stay alive. Because some metal went to sea."

"That's the theory."

"And nobody will ever know it was us."

"Nobody will ever know it was anybody. That's rather the point of me." I meant it lightly. It didn't come out lightly. Twelve years of being nobody's daughter in public, Cyrus Underhill who was not quite a son and never once a Juniper, and here I was at the end of it, proposing to save forty families through the simple application of being unremarkable. It should have been bitter. Instead it felt like finding out the tool I'd been given was actually the tool I needed.

Tamsin wiped her hands on her apron and looked at me across the crates.

"When it's done," she said, "when the century turns and the ship is gone and your father's home — what happens to Cyrus? Does Juniper come back?"

The question landed harder than she could have known. Or perhaps she knew exactly; Tamsin's great talent has always been asking questions from the far side of a wall and pretending she's on this side of it.

"Cyrus stays," I said. "He chose the name to keep me. I keep it to choose him back. Or—" I stopped, because the true shape of it surprised me. "Or maybe I keep both. Maybe a person can be two names deep. Printers do it all the time; a letter is one thing reversed and another thing printed."

She considered that the way she considers weather. "You're very strange, Cyrus Underhill."

"I'm a typefounder. We're all strange. It's the lead fumes."

* * *

The sewing took the rest of the night, and this part I did alone, because there are things a person should do by hand and alone or not do at all.

Fourteen blocks. I had unpicked the lining of my old gray coat — the coat I'd worn to deliveries since I was thirteen, the coat that knew the docks — and built pockets between lining and wool, each block wrapped in oiled silk stolen from the proofing room, each pocket positioned over ribs rather than belly, because blocks pressed against the stomach show, and blocks against the ribs read as a corset's boning to anyone whose hands are moving quickly and hoping to be done.

Eleven pounds. I put the coat on and walked the length of the shop, and then walked it again with my delivery-girl slouch, shoulders forward, chin down, hands loose. In the blackened window my reflection moved like a tired errand girl, slightly thick through the middle, entirely forgettable. Good. Forgettable was armor too.

What I had not prepared for was the sound. Lead against lead, faintly, when I moved wrong — a whisper of metal meeting metal, quiet as a secret told through a wall. I stood in the dark listening to myself chime and thought: so this is what Father carried. All those years at the case beside me, cutting names into spare blocks by lamplight while I thought he was practicing serifs. He wore a coat full of other people's danger for twelve winters and never once let it ring.

I sat down on the composing stone steps and, for a moment — a short one, I'll insist — I put my face in my hands.

Because here is the thing about strategy that no one says: it is devotion wearing practical clothes. Every block I divided tonight was a decision about who I could bear to lose. The ship might sink. The gate might close. Vane might be standing at it with his terrible courteous eyes, reading passes the way he reads inventories. I could keep nothing whole — not the ledger, not the plan, not the family — so I broke everything deliberately, along chosen lines, and called the breaking wisdom. My father had done the same thing twelve years ago. He had broken our family along a chosen line — buried the daughter, raised the apprentice — and called it love. I understood him completely now. That was the worst part. I forgave him completely, too, and forgiveness turned out to be heavier than eleven pounds of type.

I got up. Blew my nose on a rag that had previously wiped ink and therefore improved my character. Went back to work.

* * *

Near dawn, Emmerich knocked at the yard door — the soft double-tap he used, different from the army's fist — and stood there in the snow with his breath smoking, young and tired and holding a requisition form.

"Inventory count tomorrow," he said quietly. "Vane's doing it himself. Full recount of the foundry stock. They're saying—" He glanced past me into the dark shop, and lowered his voice further. "They're saying every departure on New Year's Eve gets searched stem to stern. He posted the order at midnight. Whatever you're doing—" His ears went red. "I don't know what anyone's doing. But the counting starts at nine, and Vane counts twice when he's suspicious."

The cold came through the coat, through the lead, through everything, and settled somewhere behind my sternum where the blocks couldn't cushion it.

"Thank you," I said.

"I didn't say anything."

"No," I agreed. "You didn't."

He hesitated on the step, snow gathering on his shoulders, and for a heartbeat I thought he would ask his question again — the one about what the war was for, which I had answered cheaply once and had regretted answering cheaply ever since. But he only touched his cap, turned, and walked back through the falling snow toward the barracks, leaving footprints that the snow was already filling in, patient as a clerk erasing an entry.

I closed the door and stood with my back against it.

Twice, Vane counts twice. Every departure searched. Every crate at the quay opened with knives, every passenger patted down by hands looking for reasons. My beautiful divisible ledger, split precisely down the seam — and Vane about to run his fingers down the same seam.

I looked across the dark shop at the six stenciled crates sitting neat as churchgoers in the corner, and at the coat hanging on its hook, gently misshapen, breathing faintly metal.

One of us was going to make it through the gate, I told the room. One of us had to.

Then I picked up the graver, because if the net was drawing tight, the pass inside my coat would simply have to be the finest forgery Aldous Vane had ever held — and I had three hours until my father's shop needed me, and a dead man's signature to practice.
