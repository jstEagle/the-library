## 33. New Year's Eve: The Harbor

The snow began at dusk, which I took first for an omen and then, having no time for omens, for cover.

It came down in that fat, unhurried way of a harbor snow — flakes wide as thumbnails, catching the lantern light and holding it, so that the whole town seemed to be burning softly without heat. Grayharbor had not looked like this in twelve years. It had not looked *like anything* in twelve years. And now the century was dying, and the town had decided to see it off with noise.

They'd built bonfires along the Fish Quarter — three great pyres of driftwood and broken crates that threw sparks a hundred feet up into the falling white. There were fiddlers on the customs house steps. There were soldiers drinking at every corner, their gray coats dusted pale, muskets stacked against walls like umbrellas nobody expected to need. Somebody's uncle was ringing the church bell out of turn, just to hear it, and nobody stopped him. The century was ending, and even an occupation could not quite stand in the way of arithmetic.

*Good,* I thought, pulling my coat tight over the half-ledger sewn against my ribs. *Be loud. Be drunk. Look at the fire.*

I had left the foundry an hour after full dark, when Emmerich's watch rotation put him at the north door with his back to the lane. Forty-one type blocks rode against my body in two rows, each one wrapped in oilcloth, each one sewn into a coat lining my father would have called extravagant and I called necessary. They pressed against me with every step like a second heartbeat — or like a fist, depending on how frightened I let myself be. I was trying very hard not to be frightened. I was mostly failing, but I had gotten good at failing quietly.

Tamsin was waiting where she said she'd be: under the old whale-oil lamp at the head of Cooper's Stair, wearing her father's spare boat-cloak and an expression I recognized from mirrors. She had spent three days forging departure manifests in a hand that was not hers, for a ship that officially did not exist, and she had every right to look like a woman carrying her own gallows under her arm.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm careful."

"You're late *and* careful. One of those is a virtue." She looked past me at the snow, then up at the sky as if she could read something there besides weather. "Gale blew itself out around noon. Osei's boys have been warping her toward the quay since four. If the wind holds from the northwest, she sails on the bell."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then we're all standing on a cold quay looking foolish, and Vane searches every hull at dawn." She said it lightly. Neither of us believed lightness. We had used up our supply of it somewhere around the arrest of my father, and what we'd been living on since was something cheaper and more bitter.

"Crates are already aboard?" I asked.

"Loaded as cooperage. Staves and hoops. Nobody questions staves." She paused. "Nobody questions anything tonight. That's the whole point of a century, apparently — everyone's too busy being historic to do their jobs."

We went down together through the Fish Quarter, and I will try to describe what it was like to move through that crowd with forty names against my ribs, because I don't want to forget it and I suspect you don't either.

It was like walking through a dream other people were having.

Bonfirelight turned every face copper. Children ran between legs with sparklers made of pine pitch, trailing little comets. A man had set up a table and was selling roasted chestnuts to soldiers and smugglers indiscriminately, commerce being the one loyalty Grayharbor had never once betrayed. Two women were arguing about whether the new century began this year or the next — a dispute I found suddenly, unbearably funny, given that I was carrying evidence worth forty families' lives in a coat my dead mother's curtain fabric had been cut to make, and the entire question of whether history turned tonight or next winter depended on which calendar some clerk in the capital preferred.

I bought chestnuts. This is true. I stood in line behind a sergeant of the 44th, and he held the paper cone for me while I counted out coppers, and he said "evening, miss," and I said "evening," and both of us lied completely and neither of us knew it.

That is what fear does after enough months. It stops feeling like falling and starts feeling like walking. You simply go where you're going, and everything ordinary around you becomes a kind of conspiracy — the chestnut man, the sergeant, the snow itself, all of them unknowingly employed in getting Cyrus Underhill to the water.

* * *

The quay was worse than the town. The quay was where hope got practical.

The *Juniper* lay at the long berth below the customs shed, and I heard her before I saw her — that particular sound of a brig making ready, halyards slatting, canvas being bent on with the hurried competence of people who expect to be interrupted. Her riding lamps were lit but hooded, throwing only thin blades of light across the deck. Men moved along her spars like the idea of sailors rather than sailors themselves, quick and half-seen.

Captain Osei stood at the foot of her gangway, and he was not watching his ship. He was watching the gate.

"The wind holds," he said, by way of greeting. He did not say hello. Osei did not believe in spending words on things that weren't scarce.

"Tamsin says the same."

"Tamsin is a better forger than she is a weatherwoman. The glass has been twitchy since noon." He turned and looked at me properly — that flat, weighing look he had, the one that made you feel like cargo being assessed for freight damage. "Half your evidence goes with me. Half stays on you. That's still the shape of it?"

"That's the shape."

"Then here's the last of it, and I'll only say this once because it's nearly midnight and I'm sentimental." He stepped closer, under the gangway's shadow, and lowered his voice to something meant for me alone. "Armies do not leave harbors gracefully. When they go — and they'll go, the winds are shifting, even Vane must feel it — they burn what they can't carry and break what they can't burn. Whatever you're planning to do with your half after tonight, understand that Grayharbor may look less like Grayharbor when it matters."

"I know."

"You know the way a girl knows, or the way a sailor knows? I've seen ports after. The gulls eat first and ask nothing."

I made myself hold his eyes. "My father is in their cells. My shop is in their hands. Everything I know about armies I learned this winter, Captain, and I learned it close."

Something shifted in his face — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look one professional gives another.

"All right," he said. "Then one more thing, and then I'm done being your oracle. The gate count changed at sundown. They've doubled the pickets, and they're checking passes against a list now — a *countersign* list, not just a seal. Tamsin's papers are good. They may not be good enough."

"We knew that risk."

"We knew it existed. Knowing it's real is different." He glanced up at the hooded lamps, at his men in the rigging, and I saw him do the arithmetic every captain does — crew, cargo, weather, chance — except that tonight the cargo column read *forty families' lives* in a hand only I could read. "Board or don't board, Underhill. But decide before the bell. Once I cast off, I am a rumor, and rumors don't come back for stragglers."

"And if I don't make it?"

"Then the staves and hoops get delivered to a printer in Calais who owes me money, and you become a story I tell badly." He almost smiled. "Go on. You've a gate to lie through yet, and I've a century to sail out of."

I wanted to say something large to him. Something worthy of the winter. What came out instead was:

"Thank you for not asking again what's in the blocks."

"I never asked because I always knew," Osei said. "Lead and names. It's always lead and names, in the end. Go."

* * *

Tamsin and I parted at the customs shed, because her path ran through her father's office above the harbor master's walk — one last pass to correct, one last manifest to initial — and mine ran through the worst hundred yards in Grayharbor: the harbor gate.

I want to be honest about what I felt standing at the edge of that lantern-glow with the crowd pressing at my back. Not brave. Not calm. I felt *thin*. I felt like a piece of stock run through a rolling mill one time too many — all my edges true, all my thickness reduced, until what remained of Cyrus Underhill was exactly enough metal to make one impression and no more.

The gate itself was simple enough: two posts, a bar, a brazier, six soldiers where there should have been three. Beyond it, the quay opened onto the last stretch of cobblestones and the black water and the ship. Every soul passing was stopped. Papers out. Names checked against a ledger a clerk kept warm under his own coat.

In my inner pocket lay a gate pass I had cut myself — or rather, cut and printed and aged and handled until it was no longer mine but some dead excise officer's, creased the way a real pass creases, stained where a real pass stains, signed in a hand I had practiced for eleven nights until my dreaming self wrote it too. In the lower corner, so small you'd take it for a flaw in the paper, I had punched a single letter. My initial. Mine. No examiner would ever find it. It wasn't there for examiners.

I joined the queue of revelers funneled toward the checkpoint, and the crowd carried me the way a river carries a leaf — without malice, without mercy, entirely indifferent to whether the leaf arrived.

Ahead of me a fishwife argued her way through with a basket of salt herring and a tongue that could have stripped paint. Behind me two young men sang something about the death of kings, badly and beautifully. A soldier by the brazier warmed his hands and looked bored in the specific way of a man whose orders have doubled his work on the one night his friends are all drinking. Snow gathered on his shako and he didn't brush it off.

Ten yards. Five. The clerk under the lantern licked his thumb and turned a page.

And that was when the bells began.

Not the midnight bells — not yet, the sky was still black as wet ink — but the practice peals, or celebration peals, or simply madness; every tower in Grayharbor cutting loose at once, bronze on bronze on bronze, the whole town's accumulated metal ringing out the old year early because nobody could wait. The crowd surged. Soldiers turned their heads. The clerk shouted something at the fishwife that even the bells couldn't drown —

— and I walked through the gate inside that wall of sound, papers presented, papers glanced at, a bored nod, a wave-on, my heart going so hard I could feel it in my teeth, my forged signature disappearing into the noise like a single drop into the sea —

— and nobody stopped me.

I was through. The bar came down behind me. The quay stretched ahead, lamplit and snow-thicked and empty of everything but me and the ship and the last hundred steps of a winter's work.

I did not run. Some part of me understood, with the cold clarity the foundry had trained into me, that running is the one thing a body cannot disguise. I walked the way apprentices walk on errands. I walked past the mooring bollards furred with ice. I walked past the customs shed where Tamsin's lamp burned in the upper window. I walked until the gangway of the *Juniper* rose in front of me, and a sailor reached down for the case under my arm —

— and that is when I saw Aldous Vane coming down the quay.

He was alone. No escort, no lantern even, just his gray coat silvered by snow and his unhurried clerk's stride, and he was looking directly at me — not searching the crowd, not scanning the ship. At me. As if the whole roaring, burning, bell-broken night had narrowed to a single point, and the point was a girl with a case of cooper's samples standing at the foot of a gangway as the first stroke of midnight finally, truly began.

The bells crashed down over the harbor. Somewhere behind me Osei shouted an order. The lines came off the bollards one by one, heavy and final as verdicts —

— and Vane kept walking, and he was close enough now that I could see the snow melting in his hair, and he did not stop.
