## 34. Checkpoint Three Again

There is a particular quality to fear that arrives on time. It does not sneak up on you, does not ambush you in an alley or startle you out of sleep. It simply appears at the appointed hour, punctual as a debt collector, and waits with you while the clock crawls. I had feared many things in twelve years — the press-crushed finger, the father's cough, the long dark between lamplight and dawn — but I had never feared anything so promptly as I feared five o'clock on New Year's Eve, when the harbor gate would open and Aldous Vane would be standing behind its table.

I knew he would be there. Tamsin had told me at noon, breathless under the fish-market awning, her hands doing the talking because her mouth could not risk it: *Vane's taken personal charge of the gate. He's inspecting every paper himself.* She had said it the way you'd say *the tide has turned against you* — a fact of nature, not a choice anyone made.

Every paper. Himself.

My pass was good. I want to be clear about that, because pride is the last coin a frightened person gets to keep, and I have spent mine freely ever since. The pass was flawless. I had cut it over three nights from soft lead blanks, working at a scale I'd never attempted — letters smaller than my smallest body type, seals rendered in cross-hatched strokes fine as insect legs. I had aged the paper with tea and smoke and a careful fold worn into a careful crease. The name on it belonged to a cooper's man dead two years, a name lifted from a delivery ledger no one living had reason to consult. The countersign matched the pattern Emmerich had described without knowing he was describing it — he had complained, one billet-night over his thin soup, about the new passes requiring *that ridiculous double-stroke on the harbor cipher*, and I had smiled into my own soup like a woman listening to a lock describe its keyhole.

The pass was flawless. Vane was not a flaw-finder anyway. He was something worse. He was a reader of people who happened to read papers as a secondary language.

"You'll wear the coat," I told myself in the glass-less shine of the foundry window, "and you will be a girl delivering her master's documents, and you will be bored."

Boredom, I had learned, is the great camouflage. Guilt makes people still. Fear makes them quick. But boredom — boredom makes them forgettable, and forgettable is the only safe thing to be in Grayharbor that winter. So I practiced boredom the way I practiced letters: over and over, until the hand did it without instruction.

* *

The century was dying loudly. Word had gone round that midnight would be marked with bells and bonfires despite — or perhaps because of — the occupation, and the town had decided, with the particular stubbornness of port people, to celebrate the turning whether the gray coats approved or not. Lanterns swung along Harbor Row. Snow came down in fat, unhurried flakes that caught the light and held it. Somewhere a fiddle was being murdered by an enthusiastic amateur, and somewhere else a crowd sang a song whose words were mostly laughter.

It was good cover. It was also terrible cover, because crowds mean soldiers, and soldiers on festival nights are twice as likely to stop you — once for duty and once for entertainment.

I went to the gate early, before full dark, carrying a crate of type cases I had no intention of surrendering. The half-ledger rode against my ribs, sewn into the coat lining where I had carried its brothers for weeks until the metal had learned the shape of me. Forty blocks. Forty names, or near enough — names of families who would hang if the army ever learned what the spare type in the Underhill foundry could say. The other forty were already aboard the Juniper, packed in Tamsin's mislabeled crockery, if Tamsin's nerve had held, and I believed it had, because Tamsin's nerve was the kind that held precisely because it hurt to hold.

The queue at the gate moved like cold molasses. Ahead of me: a fishwife with a permit to sell within the walls; two drovers with a wagon of winter turnips and the facial expressions of men who regretted turnips entirely; a clerk from the provost's office who waved himself through with the smugness of a man wearing his own signature. And behind the table, wrapped in a gray officer's cloak with a ledger open before him, sat Aldous Vane.

He looked exactly as he always looked, which is to say he looked like nothing at all until you looked properly, and then he looked like everything. A thin man, neat, with the patient eyes of someone who has spent a career being underestimated and has decided to find it useful rather than insulting. He handled each paper as though it were a specimen — tilting it toward the lantern, running his thumb along the fold, holding it at arm's length and then suddenly close, the way a jeweler checks for paste.

Turnips. Fishwife. A sailor with shore leave stamped in fading ink. Each one passed through those long fingers and came out the other side stamped and dismissed, and each dismissal taught me the rhythm, and each beat of the rhythm taught my heart to knock a little harder, which was the wrong lesson entirely, and I made it unlearn it. Bored. I am bored. I have stood in queues all my life. Queues are the biography of ordinary people.

Then the soldier ahead of me finished stamping his leave paper, and there was no one between us, and Vane looked up.

"Evening," he said. Mild as milk.

"Evening." My voice came out exactly as rehearsed: flat, slightly nasal with the cold, the voice of a girl who wanted to be home. I set the crate on the table. "Delivery for the cooperage on Quay Lane. Master says the papers go with the goods, and the goods don't move till the papers do, so." I shrugged, and the shrug was true, and truth is the best material for lying, as any founder knows — you cast your counterfeit in real metal or it never takes an impression.

Vane did not look at the crate. He looked at the pass.

I watched him read it, and here is what I know about watching a man read your forgery: it is the longest three seconds available for sale in this world, and they cost everything you have. His thumb found the crease — the one I had worn deliberately, folded and refolded forty times until the fiber gave up pretending to be new. His eye traveled the countersign. At the double-stroke on the harbor cipher I felt something in my chest attempt to exit through my spine, but the stroke was right, it was *right*, Emmerich's soup-complaint had been accurate to the letter, and Vane's thumb moved on.

He turned the paper over. He held it to the lantern, and the light came through the fibers, and I thought, absurdly, of my father's hands sorting type in the dark, of all the hours of my life measured in the small sounds of metal meeting metal, and I thought: *this is what twelve years bought me. Three seconds of lead.*

"Cooperage on Quay Lane," Vane said.

"That's right."

"And you're their girl."

"I'm the master's errand tonight, sir. Everyone else is at the bonfire." I let a sliver of resentment into it, the exact sliver a tired girl would let slip. "Somebody has to haul the crate."

"Mm." He set the pass down on the table between us, flat, square, deliberate, and looked at me.

Not at the crate. Not at the coat. At my face.

I have been looked at very few times in my life. That is the honest arithmetic of being a person invented to avoid looking. Shopkeepers look through you. Soldiers look past you. Even my father, who loved me the way bedrock loves a river — constantly, invisibly, destructively over time — even he mostly looked at what I could do rather than what I was. But Aldous Vane looked *at* me, fully, the way he had looked at the pass, tilting, checking for paste.

And here is the confession this chapter exists to carry: I recognized the look. Because it was mine. It was the look I gave type when I suspected a recut, the look I gave a customer's proof when the impression was too clean for the age of the mold. It was the look of a craftsman meeting craft, and I understood, in one cold drop sliding down between my shoulder blades, that he was not searching me for contraband.

He was admiring the work.

"The ink is good on this," he said conversationally, picking the pass back up. "Better than the provost's office manages. They buy their ink from a chandler down on Rope Walk who waters it like beer." His eyes came up again over the paper's edge. "You can tell a lot about a document from its ink."

"Can you, sir." Bored. God, I was bored. I was so bored I nearly yawned, and the near-yawn was the single greatest performance of my life.

"Where did the cooper get this drawn up?"

"His clerk does his paperwork. Older gentleman. Half-blind." A detail volunteered too specifically to be a lie — the oldest trick, and it worked, because specificity is a kind of exhaustion and exhaustion is believable. "He presses hard enough to go through three sheets. You can feel it on the back."

Vane turned the pass over. There was, in fact, an impression on the back, because I had made it, pressing a matching pen-line through from the reverse with a blunt stylus, the way a heavy-handed old clerk genuinely would. He felt it with his thumb. He was silent for a moment, and the lantern hissed, and somewhere behind him the crowd roared at some joke or firework, and the snow kept falling on the gray coats like ash falling upward.

"You have steady hands," he said.

"They shake when they're cold," I said. "Everything shakes when it's cold."

Something moved across his face then — not a smile, nothing so generous, but the shadow where a smile would live in a better-built man. He took up his stamp, inked it, and brought it down on the pass with a sound like a small verdict.

"Happy New Year," he said, and passed it back to me, and did not call after me, and did not look again at the crate, and let me walk through the harbor gate of Grayharbor carrying enough evidence to hang forty families and myself and, if the army had been thorough, several of the soldiers guarding me.

I did not run. Running is for the guilty. I walked, bored to the bone, hauling my crate through the snow and the lantern-light and the singing, until the gate was a glow behind me and the masts of the Juniper stood up against the dark like stitches closing a wound.

Only then did my hands begin to shake. Everything shakes when it's cold.

* *

Osei found me at the foot of the quay, where the crates were going aboard under the supervision of two sailors whose drunkenness was expertly simulated. He looked neither at me nor at the crate; we had agreed on that. He simply fell into step beside me, a broad shadow in a good coat, and spoke quietly toward the water.

"He let you through."

"He let me through."

"Vane himself."

"Vane himself."

Captain Osei was quiet for the length of three waves against the pilings. Then he said, "That man does not miss things. I've traded against his customs seizures in three ports. He once impounded a cargo of Bibles for incorrect pagination. He *lets nothing through*."

"Then he let me through on purpose."

"Or he intends to follow you through." Osei's voice stayed level, which was how I knew it mattered. "Either way, girl, you should not stand still anywhere in this town tonight."

"I don't intend to stand still anywhere in this town ever again."

He almost laughed. Almost. The Juniper's boat crew were lowering the last of Tamsin's crockery into the tender, and the ship herself strained at her lines like a dog smelling rain, and the bells of Grayharbor were twenty minutes from midnight and the end of a hundred years.

"The case goes aboard now?" Osei said. "Your father's evidence. The half you kept?"

I put my hand flat against the crate. Through the wood and the straw and the false bottom I could feel the faint chill of the lead, the way you can feel a grave through grass. Forty names. My mother among them. My father's whole silent insurance policy against a world that had come to collect after all.

"In a minute," I said. "There's something I have to see first."

And that was true, though I did not yet know what it was — only that the quay behind me had gone quiet in a way quays do not go quiet, and that the crowd's song had faltered on its chorus, and that when I turned around, lantern in hand, snow settling on my shoulders like a hand, Aldous Vane was standing at the far end of the pier with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the Juniper load her cargo, and he was not alone.
