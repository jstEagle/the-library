## 36. Sailing

The century ended the way most things end in Grayharbor: in fog, with someone ringing a bell about it.

I had imagined this night a hundred times since October, and in every imagining I was alone on the quay, breath held, heart hammering out some private drumroll as the *Juniper* slipped her lines. What I had not imagined was the crowd. The army had declared a festival — officially to mark the turning of the year, unofficially, I suspected, because Vane wanted every soul in town standing in one place where he could look at them — and Grayharbor, being Grayharbor, had taken the license and run. Lanterns swung from the fish-market beams. Somebody's uncle was selling roasted chestnuts at an extortionate price and doing brisk trade. Children ran shrieking between legs with pinwheels, and soldiers in their gray coats stood among the merrymakers looking like crows invited to a wedding and unsure whether they were guests or the omen.

Into all of this I walked, carrying four pounds of lead sewn into my coat lining like a second conscience.

That is the thing no one tells you about smuggling evidence through an occupied town: once you have done it often enough, it stops feeling like peril and starts feeling like luggage. My shoulder had grown accustomed to the drag of it. The blocks sat against my ribs under the left arm, wrapped in oilcloth, padded with a folded shift, arranged so that if anyone pressed me there they would find only the soft give of wool and a girl who looked bored. Boredom, I had learned, was the great disguise. Fear draws the eye. So does piety, and so does beauty, and so does anything at all that suggests you are worth a second glance. But a tired young woman edging through a festival crowd with her chin tucked against the cold? The eye slides off her like rain off waxed canvas.

I had left the foundry at half past ten. Father was still in the cells — the amnesty talks were "progressing," Tamsin said, which was the kind of phrase officials use when they mean *nothing has happened yet but we would prefer you stopped asking*. Emmerich had been posted elsewhere, at his own request, three days after he found my hollowed cases and reported the room clean; I thought of him sometimes the way you think of a coin you gave away without knowing why. And Vane —

Vane was somewhere. He was always somewhere. That was the trouble with Aldous Vane: he did not need proof to be dangerous, only proximity, and Grayharbor was not large enough to lose him in. Tamsin had seen him at the harbor office at dusk, going over departure manifests with her father, and she had come to the cooper's yard where I was pretending to inspect barrel staves and told me in six flat words: *"He knows tonight. He doesn't know what."*

Which was, all things considered, the best hand I could hope to be dealt.

* * *

Tamsin found me by the chestnut seller's brazier at eleven, as arranged. She wore her good blue coat and the expression of a girl out to enjoy a festival, which fooled everyone except me, and possibly not even me — there was a tightness at the corners of her mouth that hadn't been there in autumn.

"Manifests cleared," she said quietly, eyes on the fire, lips barely moving. "Osei's papers say grain for Falmouth. My father signed them himself." A small, bitter triumph in that. Her father, the harbormaster, logging every departing vessel for the army for two years, had put his own hand to the paper that would carry forty families' survival out of the harbor. I wondered if he knew. I decided he probably suspected, and had chosen, like so many people that winter, to look slightly away.

"And the search?"

"Line boats at the channel mouth. They're boarding everything that crosses it." She turned a chestnut over in her gloved fingers without eating it. "Cyrus. If they take her —"

"They won't."

"You don't know that."

"No," I admitted. "But I know Osei, and I know the tide turns at twenty past eleven, and I know that a man who has run this coast since before either of us could walk does not get caught by line boats he can see from a mile off. Trust the captain. It's the only part of this we don't have to carry."

She looked at me then, really looked, the way she had that first fog-bound morning on the docks when I was a ghost delivering proofs and she was a girl watching a world she couldn't enter. Two months had changed us both in opposite directions: I had learned to be seen, and she had learned what seeing costs.

"Your half," she said. "You're sure you won't put it in the crates with the rest? Split cargo is mad. If yours goes and mine stays, you've lost everything anyway."

"If mine goes and hers stays, she's lost everything," I corrected. "That's rather the point. Half the ledger sails tonight. Half stays sewn into my coat until I know she cleared the channel. If Vane takes the *Juniper*, he finds enough names to hang half the print circle — but not the half that matters most, and not the cipher key that makes sense of it. And if he searches me instead, then at least the ship goes out clean and the families are safe." I shrugged, and felt the blocks shift against my ribs, familiar as my own heartbeat now. "Redundancy, Tamsin. It's the whole art of the ledger. Father taught me that without ever meaning to."

"Your father," she said, "taught you a great many things without meaning to."

"Yes," I said. "He did."

The bells began just before midnight.

* * *

I will tell you what I expected to feel when the *Juniper* cleared the harbor wall, and then I will tell you what I actually felt, because the difference between those two things is most of what I have learned about myself this winter.

I expected triumph. Clean, hot, singular — the feeling of a type block seating perfectly into its chase, the click of metal finding its place. Twelve years of silence carried past three checkpoints, out through the gate, across the water, done. I had earned triumph. I had forged passes and seals and shipping papers; I had lied to Aldous Vane twice to his face; I had watched my father be taken away in irons for crimes that were, in the strictest sense, mine, and had performed grief so convincingly that I frightened myself in the doing. If any soul in Grayharbor had earned one clean moment of victory, it was the person standing at the back of that crowd in a borrowed coat with four pounds of contraband against her ribs.

What I felt instead, as the bells rang out the old century, was the weight of the lead.

The crowd counted down from ten, because counting down is what crowds do, and the harbor answered with horns and whistles and somebody firing a musket straight up into the snowless black. The *Juniper* stood out from her berth at two minutes to twelve, dark-sailed, running lights doused, sliding through the lantern-glitter on the ebb tide like a rumor leaving town. On the quay a hundred strangers cheered the new century, and I stood among them cheering nothing, invisible, holding my breath while the brig made for the channel mouth where the army's line boats hung like a drawn curtain.

She crossed.

I saw it happen — saw the little boat swing alongside, saw the lantern signals exchange, saw the gray-coated boarders go up her side and come down again after an interval that lasted approximately one of my lifetimes. Then the *Juniper* was through, and the wind caught her, and she heeled and gathered and became, in the space of a minute, a shape, and then a suggestion, and then a darkness indistinguishable from the sea.

The bells finished. The crowd sighed and began to break apart, drifting toward homes and fires and the ordinary business of being governed. Tamsin's hand found my elbow.

"She's out," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, very level. "She's out, and the ledger's with her, and in five weeks some printer in another country will set those names in honest type and publish them to the world, and forty families in this town will never know how close they came."

"You could tell them," Tamsin said. "Someday."

"Someday I'll be able to." I watched the place where the ship had been. "It's strange. I thought this would feel like an ending. It feels like — do you know the moment when you're setting a page, and you've placed every letter, and the form is perfect, and then you have to lift the whole thing into the press? Everything balanced, everything ready, and none of it printed yet. That's what this feels like. Not finished. Committed."

"That's a terrible metaphor and you're a foundry brat through and through."

"I contain multitudes," I said, "most of them in reverse."

She laughed — the short, startled laugh that meant she was keeping the bigger feeling down where it wouldn't show — and squeezed my arm once, hard, and went to rejoin her father before his manifest-counting turned into daughter-counting. I stayed at the rail a moment longer. Somewhere out on that black water, Captain Osei was steering my mother's name toward printers who would make it mean something. Somewhere behind me in the cells, my father was sleeping or not sleeping, unaware that his insurance policy had finally paid out. And here, against my ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, rode the other half: the entries that mattered most, the key, the truth entire, waiting for the day the world was safe enough to hold it.

Triumph, I decided, was overrated. Commitment was better. Commitment kept you walking.

I turned from the rail and started home through the thinning crowd, and I was three streets from the quay when I understood that someone was following me.

* * *

I want it noted, for the record I am building in my head, that I did not panic. Two months ago I would have panicked. Two months ago, a footfall behind me on a dark street would have sent my heart into my throat and my feet into a run, and running is the one thing a courier must never do, because running confesses. Instead I did what I had practiced at three checkpoints and a dozen smaller tests: I slowed my walk by exactly nothing, I catalogued the sound — one set of boots, unhurried, matching mine — and I considered my assets.

Assets: the blocks against my ribs, unfindable without cutting the coat off me. A town full of festival stragglers, which cut both ways. And the near-certainty, arriving cold in my stomach even as I named it, that whoever walked behind me had let me reach three streets' distance on purpose. Vane's people did not stumble onto things. They arranged them.

The boots closed the last gap at the corner of Cooper's Row, where the lantern light thinned and the fog came up off the water to meet it.

"Cyrus Underhill."

Not Vane. The voice was wrong for Vane — younger, rougher, uncertain of itself in a way Vane had never been uncertain of anything. I turned.

A soldier. Gray coat, festival leave, a clay pipe unlit in his fingers. Young — Emmerich's age, perhaps, though I had not seen him before. He stood in the fog at a respectful distance and looked, of all things, embarrassed.

"You're the cutter," he said. "At the foundry. I've seen your hands."

For one absurd moment I almost laughed. *A soldier recognizes her hands as a cutter's* — the tollhouse road, November, an eternity ago. It seemed the army had only the one trick and was determined to keep performing it.

"I handle type, yes," I said carefully. "It's the family trade."

"It's not that." He shifted, worked the unlit pipe in his fingers. "It's — look. I'm not here for the patrol. I'm here because I was at the harbor office tonight. Unofficially. Carrying messages." His eyes came up to mine, and what was in them was not suspicion. It was something much worse. It was appeal. "There's a clerk. Vane. He's ordered a full inventory of the foundry at first light. Sealed, this time. Soldiers inside until it's done. He told the officer of the watch it's routine."

Routine. The word sat between us in the fog.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked, and my voice was steady, and I marveled at it the way you marvel at a well-cut letter — look how it holds its shape under pressure.

"Because I stood watch on that shop for a month," he said, "and I saw what billeting does to it, and I heard what they say about your father in the cells, and I've been asking myself since autumn what exactly it is we're guarding all these printers from." He put the unlit pipe between his teeth, took it out again. "I don't know what you've got hidden in that coat and I don't want to know. But if I were you, miss, I wouldn't be home at first light."

He touched his cap, awkward as a boy at a dance, and turned back up Cooper's Row into the fog, and I stood alone in the street with the bells' echo dying off the water and four pounds of my father's twelve silent years pressed against my heart.

First light. Six hours. A sealed inventory of a shop I had already emptied — except that I hadn't emptied it of everything, because there were the hollowed cases, and there was Father's private desk with its false back, and there was the lesson itself, the great coded alphabet of our lives, cut into spare blocks in the sorting room because I had never believed Vane would move that fast.

He hadn't moved fast. He had moved precisely, the way he always did — waiting until the ship sailed, until I thought the work was done, until I was standing at a rail feeling committed instead of afraid.

I pulled my collar up and walked home through the last hour of the old century, past the dying lanterns and the chestnut seller packing his brazier, past the harbor office where a single window still burned yellow above the dark water. The fog came in off the sea to meet me, thick and familiar, the same fog I had walked through as a ghost delivering proofs, a lifetime and about eleven weeks ago.

Let him inventory the foundry, I thought, and found that I meant it. Let him turn over every case and open every drawer and seal the doors behind him. The ledger was at sea. The key was in my coat. And whatever Aldous Vane thought he was going to find at first light in that silent shop full of letters —

— he was going to find that the girl who cut them had already learned to read him better than he read her.

The fog closed behind me, and somewhere out past the harbor wall, in the dark, the *Juniper* ran on.
