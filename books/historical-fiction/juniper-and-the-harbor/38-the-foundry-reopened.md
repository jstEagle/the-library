## 38. The Foundry Reopened

The army left Grayharbor the way an unwelcome guest leaves a house: loudly, with apologies that cost them nothing, taking more than they brought.

They went in February, on a gray Tuesday when the harbor ice had begun to mutter and shift. A lieutenant came to the foundry door with a paper I did not read — I had spent a winter learning what papers could be made to say, and I no longer believed any of them at first sight — and informed us that by order of the Provisional Authority, all requisitions were rescinded and all prisoners held under the old commissions were to be released under general amnesty. He said *amnesty* as if it were a gift he had wrapped himself. Perhaps, for him, it was. Men who have done nothing wrong find mercy easy to give.

"Your father," he said, consulting his list, "is discharged from custody at the county cells. You may collect him Thursday."

"You may collect him," I repeated. As though my father were a barrel of pitch that had been stored at public expense.

The lieutenant did not notice. That was the whole of the revolution in Grayharbor: the men who had noticed nothing all winter packed up their noticing and carried it away.

* * *

I walked to the cells on Thursday through snow gone gray and trampled, wearing my good coat — the one with four type blocks no longer sewn into its lining, though my shoulder still hunched around their absence like a scar remembering the wound. The jailer was a different man than the one who had taken my father in. This one was cheerful, which told me everything about how the wind had turned.

My father came down the corridor slowly, one hand trailing the wall as if the wall owed him money. Twelve weeks of custody had written themselves into him: he was thinner, grayer at the temple where there had been only silver before, and he moved like a man who had learned in prison that haste is a luxury and had decided to renounce it permanently. But his eyes found me across that filthy room the way a compass needle finds north, and I confess I stood very still until they arrived.

"Cyrus," he said.

"Father."

He looked at me a long moment. In that look was the whole of the winter — the arrest, Vane's fragments of coded type laid out on a table like a dealer's hand, my father saying *mine, all mine, the girl knows nothing of letters beyond her trade* — and beneath it, twelve further years of silence, and beneath even that, a woman's name cut into lead where no clerk would ever think to look.

"You came," he said, foolishly, as though there had been some chance I wouldn't.

"You'll want hot soup," I said, because we are Underhills, and tenderness in our family is expressed exclusively through logistics. "And you'll want it in the shop, because the house is cold and I've been keeping the stove lit below."

He nodded. Then, at the door, he stopped and looked back at the cell with its straw and its bucket and its window the size of a hymnal, and he said, quietly, "It wasn't so bad." And then, honestly: "It was worse."

We walked home.

* * *

Reopening the foundry took the better part of three weeks, because the soldiers had used it as barracks and had treated it the way soldiers treat anything they have not bled for. The floor needed scrubbing where boots had been. Someone had burned half our kindling stock and a chair leg apiece from every stool in the composing room. There were initials carved into the casting bench — *H.E.*, in a careful hand I recognized, and beside it a smaller, rougher mark that might have been an apology or might have been Emmerich's idea of a joke; with Emmerich it was never possible to say.

But the furnace still drew. The molds still seated true. And the type — ah, the type had waited for us the way lead waits, which is to say patiently, without opinion, ready to become whatever word was asked of it. I ran my hands down the cases the first morning back and felt something in my chest unclench that had been clenched since October. Twelve years I had worked this room. It had never once occurred to me that I loved it until I thought I'd lost it.

Father pottered. That is the only honest word. He pottered about the shop adjusting things a quarter-inch and standing before the furnace with his hands behind his back like a general reviewing troops that had long since disbanded. He was diminished — I had seen it the first hour, and I did not insult him by pretending otherwise. Prison had not broken him, but it had folded him smaller, the way cloth folds when it has been stored badly, and I suspected some of the creases would never press out.

On the fourth morning I caught him trying to lift the matrix box one-handed, out of pure stubbornness, and failing, and pretending he hadn't failed.

"Two hands," I said. "You raised me to respect the metal. Respect your own ribs."

"My ribs," he said, lowering the box with dignity, "are nobody's business."

"They're mine now," I said. "Inherited. Along with the ledgers and the debts."

He laughed — a short bark, surprised out of him — and something eased between us that had been tight since the night I'd found the hidden ledger and understood that my entire childhood had a second, coded text running beneath it.

* * *

He made the offer on a Sunday, over the mending pile.

This requires explanation: my father mends his own clothes, badly, because my mother died when I was six and he decided sometime after that asking for help was a form of dying slightly ahead of schedule. So there we sat of an evening, he with a shirt he was murdering by degrees and I with a stocking I was actually repairing, and the stove ticking, and the fog pressing at the shutters the way it always does in Grayharbor, politely, endlessly, like a creditor who means well.

"When the courts sit again," he said, to the shirt rather than to me, "there will be papers to file. Reclaiming the business. The deed, the licenses." A pause with weight in it. "And your name."

I kept winding wool around my finger. "Cyrus is filed already. Has been since I was six."

"Juniper," he said.

The name sat in the room between us. It had been sitting in rooms between us my whole life, mostly unspoken, sometimes shouted, once — the worst night of the winter — whispered by him through bars while teaching me a cipher hidden inside it.

"It would be simple," he went on, still addressing the shirt, needling it with the concentration of a man defusing something. "A declaration before the magistrate. Your mother's family had standing; there are cousins in Kingsport who'd swear to you. The war's over here, whatever they're doing elsewhere. No one is collecting names anymore." He looked up. "You could have it back. Legally. Properly. Juniper Underhill, in the register, where she should have been all along."

And there it was: my father's apology, dressed in paperwork, offered the only way he knew how to offer anything — as a transaction that could be executed, witnessed, sealed. Twelve years of guilt converted to a filing fee.

I let the silence stretch, because silences were our family's first language and I am fluent.

"Say what you actually mean," I said at last. "You're sorry. You took my name off me when I was six and gave me a boy's name to keep me alive, and it worked, except for the part where it nearly got me hanged anyway, and now the danger's passed and you want to give back what you took. Yes?"

The shirt received another wound. "Yes," he said.

"All right." I set down the stocking. "Here is what I mean, and I want you to hear all of it, because I've been composing this speech since November and it deserves an audience."

He put down the shirt.

"For six years I was Juniper, and Juniper belonged to Mother, and when she died, Juniper died too, or I couldn't tell the difference. Then you handed me Cyrus like a coat two sizes too large and said *grow into it*, and I hated it, Father. For years. Every time someone called me Cyrus across the yard I flinched. It was a lie I lived in."

"I know."

"Do you?" I said. "Because here's what you don't know, what you couldn't know, because you weren't the one living inside the lie. Somewhere along the way — I couldn't tell you when, maybe cutting type at fourteen, maybe walking the docks at sixteen, certainly last winter with four blocks of evidence sewn into this coat — Cyrus stopped being the lie. Juniper became the lie. Or not a lie — a memory. Mother's name. And I love her, and I carry her, but I won't wear her." I picked the stocking back up, because my hands wanted occupation. "You didn't steal my name, Father. You lent me one. And I have paid it off. It's mine. Chosen, not assigned. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole of it."

He was quiet for a long time. The stove ticked. Outside, the harbor muttered its ice.

"I taught you," he said finally, "that names are dangerous currency."

"You taught me that names are currency," I said. "Last winter I learned they can also be coin you mint yourself. There's a difference, and—" 

"—and the difference is the whole of it," he finished, with the ghost of a smile. "You sound like me."

"I spent twelve years listening to you. Some of it stuck."

He set the ruined shirt aside — mercy upon it — and looked at me across the mending pile with an expression I had never seen on him before, or perhaps had seen a hundred times and never let myself read: pride without fear riding on top of it. Plain pride. Uncoded.

"Cyrus Underhill," he said, testing it. "Set solid. Well then." He cleared his throat, which is what Underhill men do instead of weeping. "The register keeps Juniper for the historians. The shop will know you as you are."

"The shop," I said, "has known me as I am for years. It was only ever the town that was confused."

That earned me the laugh again, and this time it went on longer, and I counted it among the best sounds of my life.

* * *

We hung the sign the following week.

It was the old sign — UNDERHILL & SONS, the paint touched up more times than either of us could count — because replacing it would have cost money neither of us had, and because, as my father said with unexpected dryness, "Sons plural was always a fiction. One daughter and one liar. We'll leave the lettering; it's honest enough at a distance."

But I did one thing to it. Late, after he'd gone up to bed, I took a fine graver and a scrap of practice brass and cut a small new character — my own design, a face I'd been sketching in my head all winter, sturdy serifs, an open counter — and I did not change the sign at all. I only practiced. On brass, on spare lead, on the margins of proof sheets. A letterform of my own making, the first thing in this trade I would own outright, not inherited, not lent.

The first customer came on a Wednesday: Tamsin, naturally, who else, wanting two hundred calling cards printed for reasons she declined to explain and I declined to inquire into, because friendship in Grayharbor runs on mutually agreed blindness. She stood in the doorway of the reopened shop with snow in her dark hair and looked around at the scrubbed floor and the ordered cases and said, "It smells like your father's shop again," and then corrected herself, quietly, "It smells like yours."

She stayed for tea we drank out of solder-mending cups, and she did not mention her brother, who was by then a fortnight gone over the water ahead of the published ledger, and I did not ask, and we were both of us grateful for the mercy of the unasked question. Before she left she paused at the door and said, "Your father seems smaller."

"He is smaller," I said. "Some of him is still in those cells. But what's left is real."

"That's more than most of us can say," said Tamsin, and went out into the fog.

* * *

That evening my father called me to the casting bench, where he had laid out the tools with a formality that immediately suspicious. The good graver. The matrix box. A bar of the clean lead he'd been hoarding against some occasion.

"You'll want your own punch," he said. "For the new face. A cutter doesn't truly have a typeface until she can strike it herself, and you've been drawing that letter of yours on every scrap in the building. Don't think I haven't seen the margins."

"You said margins were waste."

"I said that to apprentices," he said. "I'm speaking to a master founder now. Or I will be, once the guild sits and stamps the paper, which they will, because Aldous Vane wrote them a letter."

I stared at him. "Vane."

"Vane," my father said, with an expression I still cannot fully parse — somewhere between distaste and a very reluctant professional respect. "It seems that when a man spends a winter hunting a forger and finds one, he feels obliged to report the finding, even to his own disadvantage. His letter to the guild says — I have it here — 'the younger Underhill cuts a hand I have examined closely, and I attest it is the finest I have seen in the province, including my own.' Which is damning praise from that man, and the guild being the guild, they value nothing so much as a compliment with malice in it."

I sat down slowly on the casting stool. "He saw the pass. At the gate. He read it and let me walk."

"He read it," my father said, "and recognized the work. Whatever else that man is — and I would not drink with him, Cyrus, do not mistake me — he is a connoisseur. They are the most dangerous audience and the only honest ones." He pushed the lead bar across the bench toward me. "Strike your letter. The shop reopens properly when the founder's mark goes on the door."

I took the bar. It was cold and heavy and full of everything, the way metal always is.

"And afterward," my father added, settling onto his stool with the care of a man whose ribs remained nobody's business, "we should talk about the commission that came today. From Kingsport. They want five thousand broadsides set by spring, and they've heard—" he paused, and something moved behind his eyes, some mixture of amusement and dread, "—they've heard there's a cutter in Grayharbor whose work can pass any inspection in the province."

I looked at my father. My father looked at the commission slip. Outside, the harbor ice cracked and boomed like a bell, and the fog was lifting off the water for the first time in a season, and somewhere under it lay a road south to Kingsport where a printer's daughter with a borrowed-then-boughten name was about to discover whether her talent for deception could be put to honest work.

"Well," I said, picking up the graver. "Let's find out."
