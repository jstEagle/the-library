## 40. Juniper and the Harbor

The first letter I ever cut was a lowercase e, and my father told me it looked like a snail drowning. He was not wrong. I kept that block for years anyway, buried in a drawer of misfits and mistakes, because there is something indecent about throwing away your beginnings, even the ones that embarrassed you.

The letter I cut in January of 1793 was also an e — though nobody would have called it drowned. It stood upright and open, its counter carved wide as a doorway, and when I held it to the window light I thought: *there*. That is the shape of standing in the open and not flinching.

I had been designing the face since November, back when the army still squatted in our shop like an unpaid tenant, back when every stroke of the graver had to stop whenever boots crossed the floor. I drew the letters first on scraps of wrapping paper, then on the backs of proofs nobody wanted, then — when the drawings got serious enough to frighten me — on a clean sheet I hid inside the cover of my mother's psalter. A roman face, but not any roman I had been taught. My father's letters all leaned slightly backward, as if braced against a wind only he could feel. Twelve years of cutting under his eye gave my hand his habits, and it took the better part of a winter to file those habits off the steel. These letters leaned forward. Just a degree or two. Enough to suggest they were going somewhere.

"They look eager," my father said over my shoulder one evening, in the tone he reserved for opinions he wasn't sure he was entitled to anymore. He had been home three weeks by then. The cells at Fort Anne had taken something out of him — not the tremor in his hands, which came and went, but something quieter, a certainty he had carried so long he'd mistaken it for a spine. He moved through the foundry now like a guest, asking before he touched things.

"Eager," I repeated.

"Letters that want to be read." He picked up the e and turned it in the lamplight. "Mine always wanted to be left alone."

"That's one philosophy of typography."

"It's one philosophy of living." He set the block down gently, the way he set down everything now, as if the world might break if he forgot himself. "I never asked you, Cyrus. Whether you wanted this trade, or it wanted you."

"It wanted me," I said. "But I've decided to want it back. There's a difference."

He nodded slowly, and I understood that this was the whole of our reconciliation — not the grand speech, not the tearing of garments, just two people agreeing on the direction of a lean.

* * *

Cutting a full alphabet takes what it takes. Punches, matrices, casting — each capital a week of evenings, each lowercase three days if the serifs behaved and five if they didn't. I worked at the bench by the north window because the light was honest there, and honest light is the only kind worth cutting by; flattery will get a letter as far as the first proofing and no further. My hands knew the work so well that my mind was free to wander, and it wandered mostly to the harbor.

Word of the *Juniper* had come back in pieces, the way news travels when it has to outrun several governments. First a letter in a merchant's hand, passed to Tamsin's father, passed to Tamsin, passed to me folded inside a recipe for pickled herring, which Tamsin insisted was genuine tradecraft and I insisted was her mother's actual recipe. The brig had made Falmouth in nineteen days, beating a gale that sank two army transports behind her, which Osei reportedly called "the sea taking an interest." From Falmouth the ledger went inland to a printer whose name I will keep behind my teeth even now, because some debts of gratitude are best paid in silence. And from that printer, six weeks later, the pamphlets came back across the water like swallows.

I will not pretend I read them calmly. I stood in the alley behind the cooperage where Tamsin worked the accounts, and she stood next to me, and we read the printed names of forty families in an order that meant nothing to the typesetter who arranged them and everything to the two of us. The Harrowgate chandlers, cleared. The widow Prue, cleared. The men of the print circle, their arrests named plainly as arrests, their "crimes" quoted back at the tribunal that had invented them. And there, halfway down the second page, an informant's list — names given to Major Vane's clerks in exchange for remission of duties, one of them belonging to a young man currently supposed to be dead in Bristol but who was, according to the pamphlet and the ship's manifest it cited, very much alive and very much exposed.

Tamsin read her brother's name and did not cry. She had done her crying in December, and what was left after crying was something harder and more useful. "He'll run," she said.

"He already has," I said. "Osei's letter mentioned a passenger booked under a false name, paid in coin, headed for the Low Countries. The captain didn't say who."

"He wouldn't." She folded the pamphlet with the neatness of a woman putting something in its proper drawer. "It's strange, Cyrus. I wanted him punished and I wanted him safe, and both happened, and I don't know where to put the leftover wanting."

"You put it in the work," I said, because I had learned that from my father without either of us meaning to teach it. "There's always more work."

* * *

February. The fog lifted on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate; fogs and governments both prefer to dissolve on unremarkable days. One morning the harbor was a gray rumor and the next it was simply there — masts and water and the brown-green line of the shipping lanes, the fort looking smaller than it had any right to look, its gray coats gone south in the general withdrawal like frost leaving a window. The political winds had shifted in Paris and London and a dozen rooms where nobody from Grayharbor was permitted to sit, and the amnesty had come down like weather, sweeping out the cells without apologizing for them.

My father walked to the water that morning and stood a long while. When he came back he said only, "I counted the ships," and did not tell me the number, and I did not ask, and we were both content with the arrangement.

The foundry reopened the way a wound closes — slowly, tenderly, with the same furniture in the same places and everything somehow rearranged. Some customers never returned; others arrived sheepish, having looked away during the bad months, and bought their type with too many apologies. We let them apologize. Commerce has always been forgiveness with a ledger attached.

It was in March, sorting the recovered cases — the army had left our stock scattered like a burglar with poor aim — that my father made his offer. He did it while wiping down the casting table, eyes on the rag.

"When the records are amended," he said, "I'll petition to restore your legal name. Juniper Underhill. It should never have been buried. It was cowardice, and I've answered for it, and—" He stopped wiping. "You should have your name back."

"I have a name," I said.

"Cyrus was a disguise."

"Cyrus was a coat I wore so long it grew to fit. And then I walked a winter in it, past three checkpoints and a man named Vane, and every stitch of it is mine now. You can't give me back something I've already earned twice over." I took his hand, rag and all. "Juniper sails. Juniper is the ship and the secret and the girl who died of fever in 'eighty. Let her rest. I'm the one still cutting."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him do the arithmetic fathers do — subtracting the child he'd ordered from the sum of the person standing before him — and finding the remainder larger than expected. "Set by Cyrus Underhill," he said, trying the phrase aloud.

"Something like that," I said.

* * *

The broadside was finished in April. One sheet, my new face throughout, the ink still smelling faintly of the pine pitch Tamsin swore by and I tolerated out of loyalty. What it announced hardly mattered — the reopening of Underhill & Sons, revised to *Underhill & Daughter* in my father's hand before I'd seen the proof, a small amendment that cost him more than any confession he made in the cells. Below the announcement, a specimen line: *All manner of printing executed with care. Type cut to order.* And below that, plain as a signature on a contract:

*Set by Cyrus Underhill.*

I pulled the first proof myself, on the old press my grandfather had shipped from Amsterdam, and hung it to dry in the draft from the north window, and stood watching the ink go from wet to certain. There is a moment in printing — every printer knows it — when the sheet stops being an experiment and becomes an object in the world, something that will outlast the day's opinions of it. I had printed thousands of sheets under other arrangements: my father's designs, my father's silence, my name hidden in the colophon like contraband. This one said what it said. Anyone could read it. Everyone would.

Tamsin came by that afternoon, ostensibly to collect an order of billing forms, actually to stand in the shop and breathe the ink. She read the signature and smiled the particular smile of someone who had sewn four blocks of contraband lead into a crate of herring barrels and never once been thanked on paper.

"It leans forward," she observed.

"So I'm told."

"It suits you. You were always walking somewhere." She turned the proof toward the light. "My father wants to know if you'll print the harbor manifests this year. Proper ones," she added, "with nothing hidden in them. He says he's forgotten how."

"Tell him hidden things are extra," I said, and we laughed, and the laugh echoed in the foundry the way laughter never quite had before — not because the room had changed, but because we had stopped being careful in it.

That evening my father locked up early, which he never did, and we walked down to the quay together with no errand at all, which was itself a kind of revolution. The tide was coming in. Somewhere past the point, a ship's bell rang the hour, unhurried, certain the sound would be heard. The fog had stayed away for nine straight days, and the townspeople spoke about it the way farmers speak about rain after drought — superstitiously, gratefully, knocking wood.

"Do you ever think about her?" my father asked, at the rail. "Your mother. What she'd make of all this."

"Constantly," I said. "I think she'd say the letters lean correctly."

He laughed — really laughed, the first time since the arrest — and the sound went out over the water, and I let it.

Later, alone at my bench, I cut one last punch before bed. Not for the specimen book. Not for any customer. A single character, small, to be placed where only a compositor would think to look: on the future title page of whatever I printed next, tucked into the tailpiece, my mark. The cutter's mark I had signed into Vane's forged pass on a December midnight, the initial I added impulsively where no one would check — *C*, for the name I chose, descended from the name I was given, carrying both.

I held the steel up to the lamp. The letter caught the flame and threw it back, sharp and forward-leaning, going somewhere.

Outside, the harbor lay open and clear to the horizon, and for the first time in my life I looked at all that water — the roads, the channels, the far gray line where the sea becomes the century — and felt no distance between it and me. I had spent twelve years learning to be invisible in this town. I had spent one winter learning that invisibility is a skill, not a fate, and that the hand which can hide can also, when it chooses, declare itself.

Tomorrow there would be orders to fill, matrices to justify, a town still healing and pages still needed for the healing. Tomorrow I would begin the book I had been drafting in my head since the bridge — an account of one winter, names changed, names kept, printed under my own imprint on paper worth the ink.

Tonight I set the last letter in the forme, brushed the type clean, and left the press open to the dark and the salt air, ready for morning.

*Set by Cyrus Underhill.*

Every word of it mine.
