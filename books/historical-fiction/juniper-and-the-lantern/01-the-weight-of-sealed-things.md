## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

There is an art to being invisible, and Hazel Villalobos had mastered it the way other women mastered embroidery—stitch by patient stitch, until no one could see the seams.

She began before dawn, as she always did, in the narrow room behind the Marchmont garage where the ceiling sloped so low she could touch it standing on her bed. Rain worked at the window in fits and starts, the kind of October rain that didn't fall so much as seep, and the single gas lamp on her wall threw a circle of light no wider than a dinner plate. That circle was her study. Her library. Her confessional.

On the table sat the morning's post: eleven letters, ten of them ordinary.

Hazel did not open them. That was the first rule, the one that made everything else possible. She held each envelope to the lamp, tilted it, breathed on the seal until the gum softened just enough—not enough to disturb the flap's crisp edge, never that—and lifted with a bone folder she'd bought for six cents at a shop on Hanover Street. The trick was patience. The trick was wanting the words more than you wanted to hurry.

*Madam Marchmont,* began the first letter, from a Mrs. Ashford of Beacon Hill, *I shall attend your rally Thursday if my husband remains ignorant of it, which I trust to your discretion...*

Hazel read it twice, smiling at *remains ignorant*, then folded it along its original creases—the creases always remembered, if you let them—and sealed it with a breath and the flat of her thumb. The second letter concerned subscriptions. The third was a bill. The fourth, she saw at once, mattered: a suffrage association circular listing which aldermen could be moved and which were stone. She committed the names to memory the way other people memorized prayers, then resealed it so cleanly that Edith Marchmont herself could not have told it had been opened.

This was the ritual. This was the weight of sealed things: that they passed through her hands, every one of them, and she alone knew what the household carried.

She told herself, as she told herself every morning, that it was protection. A servant who knew what was coming—a visit from a hostile cousin, a creditor's threat, a newspaperman sniffing for scandal—could stand one step ahead of trouble. She had smoothed over crises the family never learned existed. She had drafted, in her own head, answers to letters that would have cut Mrs. Marchmont deeply, so that when the mistress read them at breakfast her face stayed composed, because some part of Hazel had already grieved for her.

That was the story Hazel believed about herself, mostly.

The truth underneath was smaller and hungrier: the letters were the only place anyone told her anything. Nobody explained things to the chauffeur. Nobody said *Hazel, we're worried about money* or *Hazel, my brother wrote again.* But the mail said it. The mail trusted her with everything.

She dressed in her uniform—gray wool, plain collar, hair pinned so severely it was practically architecture—and checked her reflection in the little mirror. What looked back was precisely what she wished: a woman of no particular age, no particular beauty, no particular anything. Unremarkable was a coat she wore so long she'd forgotten she was cold in it.

* * *

By seven-thirty the Pierce-Arrow stood in the mews with rain beading on its black lacquer, and Hazel had already wiped the mud from the running boards and laid a lap rug on the seat because Mrs. Marchmont would never complain of the cold but would sit very straight and pale and say nothing, which was worse.

"You've outdone yourself, Villalobos," Edith Marchmont said, coming down the steps under an umbrella held by the housemaid. She was fifty-one and built like a mast, with a voice trained—everyone assumed—to carry across lecture halls, though she used it, most days, at half that power. "You look like a woman who has defeated the weather personally."

"I've only arranged for it to fall elsewhere, ma'am."

"One of your many talents." Edith settled into the car, arranged her skirts, and glanced at the leather satchel Hazel set on the floor between their feet. "The correspondence?"

"Eleven pieces, ma'am. All accounted for."

All accounted for. It was true, technically. Hazel had learned to live inside technical truths the way spiders lived inside walls.

They pulled out of the mews into streets turned to hammered tin by the rain. Boston at that hour was all umbrellas and horse steam and delivery boys shouting, and Hazel drove through it with the concentration of a surgeon, hands light on the wheel, eyes flicking constantly to the wet cobblestones where a cart wheel or a stray child might appear. Behind her, she felt Mrs. Marchmont reading papers, the soft shuffling sound punctuated now and again by a small exhale through the nose—her only commentary on the world.

"Mrs. Ashford will come Thursday," Edith said at length. "Her husband believes she takes a water cure in Nahant."

"Yes, ma'am," said Hazel, who knew this already, who had known it since lamplight.

"She is braver than she thinks. Most people are." A pause; pages turned. "Most women, certainly."

Hazel kept her eyes on the street. There was a whole conversation happening here, the kind Mrs. Marchmont conducted entirely on her side of the dashboard, using Hazel less as an audience than as a sounding wall. It was, Hazel understood, a strange kind of intimacy: the mistress thinking aloud before a person she had never once asked a personal question of in three years of service. Not her age. Not her family. Not whether *Villalobos* was a married name or a father's or invented entire. Edith Marchmont knew Hazel drove beautifully, kept silence perfectly, and drank her tea without sugar. Everything else about her was unexamined furniture.

And yet.

And yet there were mornings like this one, when the rain came down and the city smelled of horses and coal smoke, when Hazel felt something dangerously close to being *included*—admitted to the interior of another person's life, even if only from the driver's seat. Trusted with the satchel. Trusted with the silences. Trusted, God help her, with everything except being seen.

It was not nothing. She knew it was not nothing, because she had had jobs where it *was* nothing, where she'd been a pair of hands around a house like a broom that walked. Here she was a fixture, nearly—an institution. Cook saved her the heel of the bread because Hazel had once mentioned, three years ago, that she liked it. The housemaid left the gas lamp in Hazel's room burning later than the rules allowed. These small mercies added up to something a person could almost live inside, the way you could live inside a warm coat in cold weather, provided you never took it off and never asked whose coat it was.

Almost.

* * *

Faneuil Hall was filling when they arrived, and the rain had done nothing to thin the crowd—women came anyway, hundreds of them, umbrellas overlapping like scales on some enormous patient fish. Hazel brought the car around to the side entrance and helped Mrs. Marchmont down, and watched her transform in the space of ten steps: the composure gathering itself into purpose, the chin lifting, the half-power voice climbing toward full.

"Stay with the motor," Edith said, which was unnecessary—Hazel always stayed with the motor—but she said it gently, with a hand briefly on Hazel's arm, and that brief pressure was the coin Hazel hoarded against all the dark hours.

Then Mrs. Marchmont went inside, and the hall swallowed her, and a minute later Hazel heard her voice rise up through the windows—*the vote is not a gift to be bestowed, madam, it is a wage to be collected*—and the crowd roared, and Hazel stood beside the motorcar in the rain and felt, absurdly, proud. As though the roaring crowd were applauding her too. As though the letters in the satchel had built those words, brick by brick, in her lamplit room.

She permitted herself exactly one minute of this feeling. Then she became invisible again, because a policeman was drifting toward the parked cars, and a policeman's eye slid right off a plain woman in gray wool standing unobtrusively beside a machine, but snagged badly on anyone who seemed to be waiting for someone.

He drifted past without looking at her. Good.

She was retrieving the lap rug from the seat when she noticed the envelope.

It lay on the floorboards where the satchel had rested—white, thick stock, hand-delivered, no stamp. Someone had pressed it into Mrs. Marchmont's hand inside the hall, then, and she'd set it aside unread. Hazel should leave it. Hazel *always* left what hadn't yet gone through the ritual, because order mattered, because rules were the skeleton that kept the whole strange arrangement upright.

But she had seen the handwriting on the front. A man's hand, slashing and impatient, and the name above it:

*Mrs. E. Marchmont.* And beneath, in smaller letters, as though the writer couldn't help himself: *from your brother.*

Hazel stood in the rain with the envelope in her hands and her heart going oddly hard against her ribs. She had read three years of this household's mail. She knew every correspondent, every tone, every grudge and tenderness. And she knew that in three years, not one letter had ever come from a brother—because as far as any letter had ever shown, Mrs. Marchmont had no brother at all.

The crowd inside roared again. The rain came down. Hazel looked at the sealed thing in her hands and felt the old hunger wake in her like a dog hearing its name.

Not tonight, she told herself. Tonight it goes on the desk, unopened, in its proper place.

She slipped it beneath the others in the satchel, and told herself that.

She almost meant it.
