## 2. What the Mistress Doesn't Say

There is an art to opening a letter so that no one ever knows you have opened it, and I confess I did not invent it. I perfected it. The trick is patience—steam, not force; a hairpin, not a knife—and the further trick of doing it in such a way that your hands never tremble. Hands are honest creatures. They want to confess everything.

That Tuesday morning in October, I sat in my narrow room at the top of the Marchmont house with four letters fanned across my knees and the radiator clanking like a machine with grievances. Rain had come in off the harbor around midnight and settled in for a long visit, the kind of rain that makes Boston feel as though the whole city is being slowly dissolved and reissued. Downstairs, the household was still asleep, or performing sleep, which in a house like this one is nearly the same thing.

Three of the letters were ordinary. A circular from the Massachusetts Woman Suffrage Association, which Edith would read twice and annotate in pencil until it looked like a battlefield map. A bill from a milliner whose hats, in my private opinion, could be seen from low-flying birds. And a note from a Mrs. Alcott-Pierce—hyphenated, always hyphenated, as though one name were not sufficient burden—thanking Mrs. Marchmont for her hospitality and mentioning, in a postscript that took up more room than the letter itself, that her husband had opinions about the vote which he hoped to share "at his convenience."

The fourth letter stopped me.

It was addressed in a hand I had learned to recognize the way one learns to recognize a face seen once at a funeral: Mr. Ambrose Marchmont, of the firm Hollis & Marchmont, importers. Edith's estranged brother. In two years of reading this household's mail, I had seen perhaps five letters from him, and every one had been opened by me with the particular care one reserves for things that might go off.

* * *

I ought to explain myself here, because a person who steams open other women's letters by lamplight owes somebody an explanation, even if it is only the radiator.

I had not been born a snoop. I had been born, rather worse, a nobody. Hazel Villalobos, of nowhere in particular, orphaned at fourteen, employed at sixteen by a series of households that regarded servants as a kind of upholstering—present, silent, and only noticed when stained. The Marchmont house was different in one respect only, but it was the respect that mattered: Edith Marchmont trusted her people. She handed me sealed letters to deliver and never once checked my pockets. She told me things in the motorcar she told no one else, because she believed—I understood this gradually, the way you understand water is wet—that I was furniture with good hearing.

Being trusted is a warm thing. Being trusted *because you are invisible* is a colder warmth, like standing near a window in winter. I took the comfort where I found it, and I read the letters, and I told myself a story about protection: that a woman who knows what is coming can stand slightly in front of it. That silence, properly arranged, is a kind of shield you hold over other people while they sleep.

Whether that story was true, or merely comfortable, was a question I kept in a drawer with the hairpins and did not often take out.

I held Ambrose Marchmont's letter over the kettle I kept for exactly this purpose, watched the gum surrender, and lifted the flap with the hairpin I wore in my hair for appearances and used for crimes.

*My dear Edith,* it began, and then spent a paragraph on weather and a second paragraph on their mother's grave, both of which I skimmed with the disrespect they deserved. Then:

*I am given to understand certain parties have taken an interest in the papers stored at the warehouse on the tide. I would not think much of it—men of that stripe enjoy appearing to know things—but I would think more of it if I were you. There has been talk at the club that ought not to have been talk. If I were a man who believed in coincidence, I would say this is one. I am not such a man. Burn this.*

*Your brother,*
*A.M.*

I read it three times.

The first time, I thought: *the warehouse on the tide.* Not a name. Not *our Pier 9 facility* or *the Concord Street storehouse*, which is how businessmen write when they mean to be understood. A phrase like something out of a child's riddle. Either Ambrose Marchmont did not know what his own warehouse was called—which seemed unlikely in a man who signed his letters with initials as crisp as ledger entries—or he was writing so carefully that even the address had been disguised.

The second time, I noticed what was missing. No explanation of *certain parties*. No names. No request for a meeting, no offer of help, no family feeling beyond a paragraph about a grave. This was not a letter between estranged siblings. This was a telegram wearing a letter's clothes.

The third time, I read *Burn this* and felt the small cold finger touch the back of my neck.

Because here is the thing about a man who writes *burn this*: he assumes his sister will burn it. He does not know, cannot know, that the letter will pass through the hands of a girl at the top of the house who has made a religion of other people's paper. He does not know that I exist. Nobody writes letters for me. I am the reason envelopes have seals.

And yet some part of him—the part that wrote *I am not such a man*—had hedged. Had written it strangely enough that even a stranger, even furniture with good hearing, might smell the smoke underneath.

* * *

I delivered the letters at half past eight, unopened-looking, and watched.

You can learn a great deal from watching a woman receive mail. Edith stood in the hall in her gray walking suit, sorting the day's paper with the efficiency of a bank teller, and when she came to her brother's letter she did what she always did: carried it into the morning room and set it on the mantel, unopened, face down, where it would sit for a day or two or a week, being punished.

She had done this with every letter he had ever sent. She would read it eventually—I knew because I read them after she did—but the delay itself was a sentence being served. And yet this morning, something differed. She paused. Her thumb moved along the flap as if checking it, the way you check a door you believe you locked. Then she turned it face down on the mantel anyway and rang for coffee.

I filed that away. It went into the drawer with the hairpins, next to the question about whether my silences protected anyone, including myself.

* * *

Dinner that evening was a study in what people do not say, and I have rarely seen a table work so hard at it.

Present: Mrs. Marchmont; her daughter Juniper, nineteen and gloriously unsubtle, home from Radcliffe for the weekend; Mr. Calloway, Edith's solicitor, a dry, precise man who ate as though food were a legal formality; and the new under-cook's assistant, whose presence at the table I will explain presently and briefly, because she was dismissed within the week and deserves her single line of history.

The conversation began with suffrage—safe ground, well-plowed—and then Juniper, who possessed her mother's courage without her mother's discretion, said: "Uncle Ambrose called on Grandmother's lawyer Tuesday. Nellie saw him from the tram."

The silence that followed had texture. It had grain, like good wood.

"Did she," said Edith, and passed the potatoes.

Mr. Calloway, who had been reaching for the salt, reached instead for nothing at all. His hand made a small detour, the way a man's hand does when he has remembered mid-gesture that he is not supposed to know something. I had seen that exact detour before, or its cousins, in a hundred households: the butler who does not look at the broken vase; the maid who stops humming when the master enters. Silence has a choreography, and Mr. Calloway danced it badly, because he was not by nature a dancing man.

"The warehouse business," Juniper pressed, mercifully oblivious, "Nellie says there were men looking at it last week. With papers."

"Men look at many things," said Edith, "with papers. It is practically the definition of the modern age."

Everyone laughed, including me, at the proper servant's interval, which is two beats after the family begins. But I had already begun my map, you see, and I logged the laugh too. Because Edith Marchmont had just made a joke to close a door, and Edith Marchmont did not close doors. She was a woman who nailed windows open on principle. Something behind that door had made her, for one evening only, cautious.

* * *

Over the following days, I drew the map properly. Not on paper—paper can be found—but in the place where I keep everything, which is nowhere, which is everywhere.

Item: the third-floor corridor door, locked now, which had been unlocked all summer. The key hung in the butler's pantry, and Cook claimed not to know why, and the butler claimed not to have been asked, and neither of them looked at the other when they said it, which is itself information.

Item: Mr. Calloway had visited twice in one week without a corresponding legal occasion. Houses like ours have rhythms; a solicitor comes quarterly, or upon deaths, or upon scandals, and there had been no death unless you counted the potato dish, which Cook had taken hard.

Item: Edith had burned papers in her study grate on Thursday night—actual burning, not the figurative kind her brother prescribed—and had done it herself rather than hand the task to me, which was either an insult or a mercy, and I had not yet decided which stung more.

Item: the men from the tram. Nellie, our laundress, was a source of civic intelligence rivaled only by the police blotter, and by Saturday she could produce, for the price of sympathy and a cup of tea, a full description: two gentlemen, well-dressed, one with a beard "like a prophet or a tax attorney," who had walked the length of the Concord Street storehouse—Edith's late father's, now leased out, holding God knew what in the way of old papers—consulting documents, taking measurements, and generally behaving like men who intended to become very familiar with the premises.

Concord Street. On the tide. I sat with those two phrases side by side in my head for most of Sunday, and Sunday evening they clicked together with an almost audible sound. The warehouse on the tide. The warehouse that flooded at spring high water, which the dockmen called by the nickname and everyone else called by the street. Ambrose Marchmont had not disguised the address out of paranoia. He had disguised it so that a letter gone astray—a letter intercepted, a letter read—would point nowhere.

Which meant he expected it to be read.

Which meant the danger was real enough to write carefully about, and serious enough that he trusted his sister's caution more than the post office.

* * *

Monday morning brought rain again, and with the rain, a letter.

Not to Edith. To Mr. Calloway, hand-delivered, no stamp, which in itself was unusual enough to notice. I collected it from the hall tray at seven, as was my duty, and I stood in the hall for a long moment with the thing in my hands, weighing it.

It was thin. Two pages, maybe. Sealed with plain wax, no monogram, the wax applied by someone in a hurry or someone pretending haste. And beneath my fingers, through the paper, I could feel the faint ridgeline of something enclosed—heavier stock, folded smaller than the pages around it. An enclosure. A clipping, perhaps. Or a copy of something.

Upstairs, the kettle waited. The hairpin waited. The drawer with the uncomfortable question in it waited.

I want to be able to say I hesitated out of conscience. The truth is I hesitated because the hall clock was striking, and Mrs. Feeney from next door has ears like a bat with a grievance. When the last chime faded I went up, and I steamed, and I lifted, and I read.

The enclosure was a copied page of a shipping manifest, dated the previous spring, listing "assorted stored goods, one lot" removed from the Concord Street storehouse at night, by men whose names were given only as initials—and one of the sets of initials belonged, unless my memory of two years' worth of dinner-party seating cards had failed me entirely, to a man who had sat at Edith Marchmont's right hand not six months ago and complimented her soup.

The covering letter said only: *They mean to make it look like yours. Move what matters before it is too late.*

I sat on the edge of my bed with the radiator clanking its grievances, and I thought about fire. About how a warehouse full of old papers—an old scandal, dead these fifteen years, papers that could clear a name or bury one—was not really safe behind locks, because locks keep out thieves and fire keeps no appointments at all. And I thought about men who measure buildings in the rain, consulting documents, and what sort of documents a man consults before a building burns.

Somewhere across town, in a house I had never entered, Ambrose Marchmont was waiting to hear whether his strange little letter had been burned in time.

Downstairs, Edith rang for me. Twice—quick, sharp, the signal we used for the motor, no delay.

I put the letter back together, put the hairpin back in my hair, and went down to drive her into whatever was coming, carrying in my pocket the whole of it, like a lantern I did not yet know how to light.

We were three streets from the house when the sky over the harbor turned the color of a struck match.
