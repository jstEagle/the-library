## 4. Three Checkpoints

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for mending and marketing and the small administrative sorrows of a household—the butcher's bill, the coal delivery, the question of whether the upstairs maid had been drinking again (she had not; she was merely in love, which produced similar symptoms). Nothing so dramatic as arson was supposed to arrive on a Tuesday.

Hazel brought it up from the hall on a lacquered tray, because Edith Marchmont's correspondence traveled by tray or not at all, and set it beside the tea things without comment. Then she went downstairs and stood in the kitchen and made herself useful to Mrs. Quill for eleven minutes, which was precisely as long as she judged Edith would need to open her post alone.

She had learned this arithmetic years ago. Letters that mattered were opened alone. Letters that didn't were read aloud over toast. And in the past week there had been no letters read aloud at all—only a stream of heavy cream envelopes, sealed with wax instead of gum, arriving at odd hours from addresses that made the postman squint.

"Hazel," Mrs. Quill said, "you've polished that spoon within an inch of its life."

"Sorry, Mrs. Quill."

"You're not sorry. You're thinking. There's a difference, and it shows on your face." The cook took the spoon away with the firmness of a woman confiscating a toy. "Go see if Her Ladyship wants anything."

Hazel went. She climbed the stairs slowly, arranging her expression into the pleasant blankness she wore the way other women wore hats, and knocked.

"Come in, Hazel," said Edith—which was itself information, because Edith knew her footsteps and did not usually invite them before they knocked.

Edith sat at her writing desk with the morning's letters fanned before her like a losing hand of cards. She was not reading any of them. She was looking out the window at the rain, and her hands were folded in her lap with a stillness that Hazel recognized as effortful. Composure, in Edith Marchmont, was not a resting state. It was a performance, and Hazel had seen enough performances to know when an actress had forgotten her lines.

"The post is heavy this morning, ma'am," Hazel said carefully.

"It is." Edith turned from the window. For a long moment she studied Hazel—not the way employers studied servants, cataloguing and dismissing, but the way one studies a locked door when one has finally decided one needs what's inside it. "Hazel. Do you drive well at night?"

"I drive well at any hour, ma'am. You've said so yourself."

"I say many flattering things I don't expect to be tested on." Edith rose and crossed to the desk, and then—Hazel would remember this afterward, would turn it over like a coin in the dark—she picked up one of the cream envelopes, held it a moment, and set it back down unopened. "There will be errands in the next few days. Errands I may not explain fully. You may refuse them. I want you to understand that refusing is permitted."

"Ma'am," Hazel said, "in ten years of service, has anyone ever explained anything to me fully?"

Something flickered across Edith's face—surprise, and beneath it, guilt—and Hazel realized too late that she had let the mask slip. Ten years of being trusted-but-unseen, and here she'd gone and shown she could count.

"No," Edith said quietly. "No, they haven't. That is rather the point, isn't it."

* * *

The explanation, such as it was, came that evening in the motor car.

They were ostensibly driving home from a meeting of the Massachusetts Woman Suffrage Association, where Edith had spoken for forty minutes about taxation without representation while three men in the back row took notes that had nothing to do with taxation. Hazel watched those men in the rearview mirror the way she watched everything: continuously, without appearing to.

"Take the long way," Edith said as they pulled away from the hall. "By the harbor. I want to look at the water."

The long way by the harbor was not, in fact, a way home by any reasonable geography, but Hazel had stopped expecting reason from directions years ago. She turned down Commercial Street, where the warehouses stood shoulder to shoulder along the water like old men waiting for a boat that wasn't coming.

"Do you see the Corvina?" Edith asked. She nodded toward the pier, where a gray-hulled steamer rode low with cargo, funnels breathing smoke into the wet air. "Sails on the morning tide tomorrow. Liverpool first, then Rotterdam."

"A passenger ship?"

"Passengers, mail, and freight." Edith's voice was light in the way that ice is smooth. "Her lawyer, Mr. Ashford, sails with her. He's been... consulting abroad. On our behalf."

Hazel kept her eyes on the road and her voice on the level. "Consulting on the warehouse, ma'am?"

The silence that followed lasted half a block. When Edith spoke again, all the lightness was gone from her voice, and what remained underneath was something rawer and younger than Hazel had ever heard her use.

"You know about the warehouse."

"I know the harbor fire wasn't in the papers past Tuesday, ma'am, and fires on Commercial Street always are. Somebody paid to keep it quiet." She risked a glance sideways. "I read the letters, ma'am. I'm sorry. I've read them for ten years, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise now, not when pretending might get somebody hurt."

Another silence. Rain ticked against the windshield. Ahead, a lamplighter moved down the street with his pole, touching flame to glass, and Hazel thought—not for the first time—that his was an honest trade. He made light and everyone could see him doing it.

"You should be furious with me," Edith said at last. "Or I with you. One of us ought to be."

"Yes, ma'am. Neither of us has time."

That startled a laugh out of her—a short, cracked thing, quickly smothered, but real. Edith pressed two fingers to her mouth as though pushing the laughter somewhere it couldn't be used against her, and then she looked at Hazel with an expression that Hazel would spend the rest of her life failing to describe: recognition, perhaps. Two women discovering they had been standing in the same room for a decade.

"There is a ledger," Edith said. "In the warehouse that burned—or there was. Ashford has a copy. Names, dates, payments. Everything that proves my brother's partners set the fire themselves and meant to hang it on us." Her hands tightened in her lap. "It's coming to him tonight, packed inside correspondence. Ordinary-looking letters. He'll carry it aboard the Corvina and take it beyond the reach of anyone in this city who'd prefer it burned."

"And someone means to stop it reaching the ship."

"Someone has already tried twice." Edith turned to look out her own window, at the black water sliding past. "Tomorrow morning, between this house and that pier, there are three checkpoints. Police posts. Men who have been reassigned to those corners in the last week by people with the authority to reassign them, and whose interest in private correspondence will be sudden and intense."

Hazel drove another block, doing sums. Three checkpoints. One letter. One driver.

"Why tell me this, ma'am? Why now?"

"Because tomorrow morning," Edith said, "I am going to ask you to drive through all three of them, and I find I cannot issue that order to a woman I've kept blind. It would be—" she searched for the word and landed on it with visible discomfort— "*indecent.*"

* * *

Hazel did not sleep. This was not unusual; sleep and Hazel had maintained a distant professional courtesy for years, nodding to each other across the room without ever properly meeting. But tonight the wakefulness had a different texture. Usually her nights were full of other people's words—the letters replaying behind her eyes, the small betrayals filed neatly under protection. Tonight the only words were her own, and they kept arranging themselves into a plan.

She sat at the little table in her attic room and drew the route on the back of a laundry list, because one plans with what one has.

Checkpoint one: the corner of Hanover and Richmond, where the city detective named Brogan had taken to leaning against a lamppost every evening at dusk with the studied idleness of a man being paid to be idle. Hazel had driven past him fourteen times. He never looked at the car. He looked at *her*—two seconds each pass, no more, and then away, as if filing something. Fourteen files' worth of something.

Checkpoint two: the customs shed near Long Wharf, where a sergeant with a waxed mustache and a reputation for finding contraband in places contraband had never thought to hide. Mrs. Quill called him Mustache Merriweather behind his back, which was not clever, but Mrs. Quill's cleverness had never been required to survive contact with him.

Checkpoint three: the pier itself, the gangplank, the last twenty yards of open ground where anything could happen and everything would be witnessed.

Three gates. And between them, one woman in a chauffeur's coat carrying letters that could hang her, if not literally, then in every way that mattered to a woman without family, without money, without anyone to ask inconvenient questions if she vanished into a cell or worse.

Hazel looked at the map a long time. Then she did the thing she had done every night for ten years, the thing that had started as curiosity and become compulsion and become creed: she asked herself what the letters would say.

Because that was the whole of her method, if she was honest. People wrote what they could not say aloud, and the truth lived in the gaps—the brother who signed himself "your affectionate J" but whose letters smelled of panic; the partner who wrote too warmly; the lawyer whose replies came from hotels instead of offices. Read enough sealed things and you learned that silence had a shape, and fear had a handwriting, and both could be tracked like weather.

And the weather, tonight, was foul.

She thought about refusing. Edith had offered it, genuinely, and there was a version of Hazel—the one she'd spent ten years constructing, the invisible one, the safe one—who would have taken it. That woman would have driven Edith to the corner, pleaded a sick mother, gone home to her attic, and spent the rest of her life listening to the rain and telling herself it was better than drowning.

But that woman had been built out of a failure. Out of a letter she hadn't read, once, ten years ago, because she'd been tired and careful and wanted very much not to know. And the woman who hadn't known had stood at a funeral in the rain, holding flowers she couldn't afford, learning the exact weight of a thing left sealed.

Hazel blew out her candle, folded the laundry-list map into her coat pocket, and lay down in the dark with her boots on.

Resolve, she discovered, felt remarkably like terror. Same quick heart, same cold hands. The only difference was direction—one ran away from the thing, and one walked toward it—and direction, unlike courage, could be decided in advance.

At four-thirty, the knock came at her door, soft as a moth. She was already dressed.

Downstairs, the house was dark except for the hall lamp, and beneath it stood Edith Marchmont in a traveling coat, holding a stack of ordinary-looking letters bound in twine. Among them, plain as a stone in a loaf of bread, was the one that mattered.

"They sail on the tide," Edith said. "Mr. Ashford boards at six. Hazel—" She stopped. Whatever speech she had prepared, she abandoned it, and simply held out the letters. "Drive well."

"Ma'am," Hazel said, taking them, feeling their weight settle into her palms heavier than paper had any right to be, "I always do."

Outside, the rain had stopped, and the sky over the harbor was turning the color of gunmetal. Somewhere between here and the water, three men were waking early to stop her.

Hazel buttoned her coat, went out to the motor car, and cranked the engine awake.
