## 5. The Lantern in the Hall

The house slept the way old houses do—not all at once, but in stages, room by room surrendering its light until only the hall lantern kept its post above the stair. I had learned its sleeping rhythm my first winter: Mrs. Penhale's door went dark at nine sharp, the kitchen fire was banked by half past ten, and Mr. Osei the butler made his final rounds at eleven, his footsteps so regular you could have set a watch by them and blamed the watch when it wandered.

It was now well past eleven, and Mr. Osei's footsteps had made their last circuit. Which meant it was time for me to commit my second crime of the evening.

My first had been smaller—letting myself into the house through the scullery door instead of the front, shaking the rain off my coat like a stray dog, though no dog ever felt the particular guilt I felt coming through that door with a lie already warming itself in my chest. *I turned in early, ma'am.* I had said nothing of the kind. I had sat on a crate behind the livery stable for two hours with my heart going like a loom, turning over what I'd read that afternoon.

*They mean to burn what's left. The warehouse on the tide. Nothing that can be traced to us—the girl who drives for her will carry whatever needs carrying, she carries anything.*

That last line had been sitting in me for hours, heavy as wet wool. *She carries anything.* They had counted me the way one counts a doorframe or a lamppost. Part of the architecture of the Marchmont household. Useful. Unseeing.

Well. Tonight the architecture intended to see very much indeed.

Edith's study stood at the end of the hall, and I knew without looking that the letter would still be there. She had come home from her brother's dinner pale as candle tallow, had kissed my cheek—a thing she did perhaps four times a year—and gone up to bed without touching her supper. People think mistresses lock their secrets away. In truth, most of them leave them lying about precisely because no one servant ever looks. The trust is the lock. The invisibility is the safe.

And I had been picking that safe for ten years.

I took the lantern down from its bracket. It was brass, older than me, with a handle worn to shine by hands whose names nobody remembered, and I want to tell you I hesitated outside that study door. I did not. That is the terrible part. My hand went to the latch steady as a surgeon's, because I had done this a hundred times before, and because every single one of those hundred times had been paid for in advance.

* * *

Ten years ago I was seventeen years old and employed as a maid-of-all-work in the West Newton house of Dr. Aldous Fenn, a physician with a soft voice, a failing practice, and a correspondence I never should have touched.

I had been in service eight months. Eight months of being called by my surname only, of eating alone in the kitchen, of learning that if I moved quietly enough the family would sometimes forget I was a person at all—which stung, at first, and then became a kind of freedom. A forgotten person hears everything. And a lonely person, a person whose own name hadn't been spoken aloud in weeks, will do almost anything to touch other people's lives, even secondhand, even folded in an envelope.

So I read the doctor's letters. Every one. I steamed them over the kettle and pressed them dry under the flatiron and put them back with such care that even now I could seal an envelope better than any stationer in Boston. I told myself it was harmless. Curiosity, nothing more. Reading other people's words was like standing at a lit window on a cold street—you weren't inside, but you were warm for a moment, and no one was harmed by your looking.

Then came the letter from the apothecary on Washington Street.

I nearly didn't open it. It looked dull—a bill, most likely, or a complaint about payment. But I had developed rules by then, little ceremonies of the guilty: always check the handwriting, always note the date, always look to see whether the letter had been opened and resealed. This one had been resealed badly, which meant someone else had read it first and thought themselves cleverer than they were.

Inside was a warning. The ink had smudged, but I made out enough: a shipment mislabeled, a preparation sold under the wrong strength, three children in Charlestown ill and one not expected to see the spring. The letter asked—begged—that the doctor recall the bottles immediately, before another dose went out.

I am not going to pretend I understood it all. I was seventeen and tired and the language of pharmacy was Latin to me in more than the usual sense. What I understood was this: the letter frightened somebody, because somebody had opened it first. And a frightened somebody, I reasoned, was handling the matter. It was not my place. I refolded the letter, sealed it, returned it to the tray, and told myself the lie that has kept me awake on certain nights ever since: that it was none of my business.

Two days later a fourth child sickened. The recall came too late, and it came quietly, and the apothecary's clerk who had written the warning lost his position for writing it. The child was a girl named Prudence Whitlow. I never saw her. I only ever saw the notice of her funeral, printed small, while I polished silver that belonged to people who would never know her name either.

Here is what I learned, kneeling there with a fork in my hand and a dead child's name going around my head like a wheel: silence is not neutral. Silence is not a wall you hide behind; it is a stone you carry, and it grows heavier every day you choose not to set it down. I had read a hundred letters out of loneliness and been harmed by none of them. I failed to act on one letter out of caution, and a girl died of my restraint.

So I made myself a promise, there over the polishing cloth, and it was not a comfortable one. If I was going to read what I shouldn't—and I knew myself well enough by then to know that I would, that I needed to, that some hungry part of me would never stop pressing its face to lit windows—then I owed a debt for every word I took. If I found poison in a letter, I would say so. If I found danger, I would stand in front of it. The reading was theft. The acting was repayment.

When I came to the Marchmonts three years later, Edith hired me because I could drive and keep my mouth shut, and she got both bargains exactly as advertised. She never guessed that the mouth stayed shut because it was full of everything I'd swallowed. Ten years of letters. Ten years of debts, all paid so far, thank God, in smaller coin than Prudence Whitlow's.

Until tonight.

* * *

The study smelled of her—lavender water and ink and the faint coal-smoke of long evenings at the desk. I set the lantern on the blotter's edge and let its light pool over the papers, and there it was, third stack from the left, exactly where she'd left it after reading it a second and a third time. I knew she'd read it thrice; I'd seen the crease marks deepen between dinner and bedtime.

A thick cream envelope, sealed in red wax, addressed in a hand I recognized from the household files: **Mr. Ambrose Kettleby, Esq., c/o Purser's Office, SS Corvina, Pier 4, sailing morning tide.**

Her lawyer. Her brother's lawyer, once—Ambrose Kettleby had untangled the Marchmont shipping interests back when the scandal was fresh, back before the brother went abroad and the name went quiet. And now, on the eve of sailing, he was carrying a sealed letter out of the country on a ship departing before the city woke.

If it held only good wishes, Edith would have posted it days ago. Letters that wait for ships are letters that fear the mails.

I held the envelope over the kettle I'd brought up in silence—I had planned this down to the kettle, God help me, down to the number of steps from the scullery to the study—and watched the wax soften the way it had softened a thousand times before. My hands were not shaking. That surprised me. Perhaps terror and habit use the same muscles.

The letter inside was written in Edith's hand, but not her usual hand—this was cramped, hurried, the script of a woman who had finally stopped composing herself mid-sentence.

*Mr. Kettleby—You were right to distrust Halloran & Voss. Enclosed you will find pages copied tonight from the ledger my brother left in my keeping, which names each man and each payment touching the warehouse matter. I believe they intend the fire on the harbor to finish what the scandal began, and I believe they intend worse besides if these names reach no one. You are known to the federal prosecutors and clean of their influence, which no man in this city now is but yourself. Get this aboard the Corvina and out of Boston before Tuesday. Do not wire ahead. Trust no one in the customs service, no one at the pier, and above all no officer of the police, three of whom I can now name as being in Halloran's pay.*

*If this does not reach you, then let whoever finds it know that Ruth Marchmont's children were honest, and I will have spent everything I have to prove it.*

*I am, faithfully yours,
E. Marchmont*

Beneath the letter lay the copied pages themselves—four sheets in a fair, careful copyist's hand, columns of dates and figures, and beside each figure a name. I knew two of the names from the society pages. One of them I knew from the police gazette Mr. Osei liked to read aloud on Sundays, scandalizing nobody, since we were all already scandalized and merely enjoying the company.

And one of them—Halloran himself—had appeared, six days ago, in a letter I had read on this very desk and returned to this very drawer and said *nothing* about, because at the time it had seemed only business rivalry, the ordinary venom of men who wanted what the Marchmonts had.

The stone in my chest grew several pounds heavier. Even faithfulness has lapses. Even a lantern leaves corners dark.

I copied nothing. There was no time and no need—the originals would do their work if they could only travel. I sealed the wax again with the heated knife blade, pressed it smooth, breathed on it until it took its old shape, and set the envelope back in the third stack exactly where it had lain. Then I sat down on Edith's chaise with the lantern at my feet, like a cat settling over a hearth, and made myself think.

The Corvina sailed on the morning tide. The letter could not go by ordinary post—Edith plainly meant to send it by hand to the pier, which meant she would ask me, because she always asked me, because *she carries anything.* And Halloran's people knew it. They had written it themselves, in a letter meant for other eyes: *the girl who drives for her will carry whatever needs carrying.*

Which meant they would be watching the road to Pier 4. Which meant checkpoints—I had heard Brogan's men had set up "customs inspections" along Atlantic Avenue, three of them, official-looking, entirely unofficial. They would stop every carriage. They would search every bag. They would be looking, with particular patience, for a woman driver with a plain face and quiet hands.

I looked at the letter on the desk. I looked at the lantern, its flame standing straight and patient in the glass, asking nothing, lighting everything it could reach.

"Prudence Whitlow," I said aloud, very softly, to the empty study. "This one I read. This one I carry."

There would be no steaming kettles tomorrow, no sealed envelopes passed quietly at dawn. Tomorrow I would walk up to armed men in broad daylight with contraband against my body, and I could not be invisible, because invisible was exactly what they were watching for. Everything I had practiced for ten years—stillness, plainness, the art of being furniture—would have to be spent in a single morning, spent like coins, spent all at once.

I lifted the lantern and went back down the hall to return it to its bracket, and as I reached it, a floorboard sighed above me. Edith's room. Then silence again—or not quite silence; the small sound of a woman pacing, stopping, pacing, keeping her own kind of vigil one floor above mine.

Neither of us would sleep tonight. Only one of us knew why.

I hung the lantern up and stood a moment in the dark, doing sums. Three checkpoints. One pier. Four hours until the tide.

And somewhere on Atlantic Avenue, a detective named Brogan who recognized eyes, not faces, was waiting for the girl who carries anything.

He had no idea how much.
