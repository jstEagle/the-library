## 6. A Man Who Counts Doors

I had a theory about being forgotten, and I had tested it the way other people test recipes: carefully, repeatedly, and with notes.

The theory was this — no one remembers a woman in a gray coat driving a gray motorcar at a gray hour of the morning, provided she gives them nothing to catch against. Not her eyes. Not her hands. Certainly not her name. A person's memory is a hook-rack by the door, and if you hand them nothing with a loop on it, they hang nothing up. They will swear afterward they saw no one at all. I had been swearing myself into nonexistence for ten years, and until this week it had never once failed me.

So when the police wagon rolled out of the mews behind me just as I turned onto Beacon Street, lamp lit though the sky was going pale, I did what I always did. I became furniture.

Furniture does not hurry. Furniture does not glance in mirrors. Furniture drives at seventeen miles an hour with both hands on the wheel and an expression like a Tuesday.

* * *

They had built the checkpoint out of sawhorses and two bored-looking officers and one man who was neither bored nor looking. He stood beneath the gas lamp at the corner of Charles Street with his hands in the pockets of a coat too good for a constable and not good enough for the man I suspected he was. Heavyset. Gray at the temples in the particular way that suggests worry rather than age. And he was counting.

That sounds strange, I know. But you learn to see it, once you've spent years learning what not to be seen doing. Most men watch faces. This man watched doors — the doors of houses, the doors of the motorcars, the doors of the street itself. His eyes went carriage door, house door, servant's door, my door, in the unhurried rhythm of a man tallying. He wasn't looking for anyone. He was taking inventory of everyone, and somewhere in a notebook in that too-good coat there was a page with our house on it, and I was willing to bet my last dollar that my face was already written there in pencil.

Brogan. I knew the name the way you know a sound in the night — from Edith's letters, from the way her brother's handwriting had gone small and hard around it. *If Brogan asks, tell him nothing; he counts everything.* I had thought it was a figure of speech. It was not. It was a warning label.

There were three checkpoints between the house on Chestnut Street and the pier where the *Corvina* sat taking on her dawn tide. This was the first. There was no way around it that didn't take longer than the tide would wait. So I did what furniture does. I slowed, I stopped, and I waited to be waved through.

He did not wave me through.

* * *

"Morning." He came around to my side of the motorcar at the pace of a man who has never once been late for anything, because he has never once hurried. Up close he smelled of tobacco and wet wool. "Where are we off to so early?"

"We, sir?" I said, and gave him the voice. The voice was a whole instrument in itself — flat, deferential, faintly dull, the voice of a woman whose thoughts rarely rose above the weather and the carburetor. "Just down to the wharf. Errand for my mistress."

"Errand." He said the word as though tasting it. "At half past four."

"Ship sails with the tide," I said. "Letters don't keep."

"No," said Brogan. "They certainly don't."

He walked around the car slowly. I kept my hands on the wheel and my breathing in the low, even gear that ten years of practice had taught me, and I looked straight ahead at a point on the brickwork slightly left of his shoulder, which is where servants look — near enough to seem attentive, far enough to seem empty.

"Whose motorcar?"

"Mrs. Marchmont's, sir."

"Edith Marchmont." He let the name sit in the cold air between us. "You've driven for her long?"

"Three years, sir."

"Like it?"

"It pays," I said, which was true, and dull, and precisely the sort of thing a certain kind of woman says, and I felt him file it away — *pays, no love lost, no loyalty worth bribing or breaking* — exactly as I intended him to.

But here is what nobody tells you about being invisible: you must maintain it every single second, and the man questioning you only has to be right once. My heart was going like a bird in a box. Inside the coat across my lap lay the sealed letter, and inside the letter lay the ledger pages that would hang four respectable men, and between those pages and the harbor stood this gray-templed man who counted doors, and I could not run, because furniture does not run.

"Open the coat, please."

"I beg your pardon?"

"The coat across your lap. Open it." He said it mildly, the way a man asks you to pass the salt. Two constables had drifted nearer without appearing to move. One of them examined a wheel of my motorcar with tremendous professional interest.

"My mistress's correspondence," I said. "Sealed. She'd have my position for it, sir, and I've got a mother sick abed—" The lie came out worn smooth, an old coin passed a thousand times. Mothers sick abed are the counterfeit currency of service; every policeman in Boston has a drawer full of them.

"Mm," said Brogan. "Let's have a look at your face while you're about it."

And he stepped around into my lamplight, and he looked at me.

* * *

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the one luxury I have never allowed myself and I find, at my time of life, I resent it. What I felt when Brogan looked at me was not fear of being caught. It was something older and stupider. It was the fear of being *seen* — the specific vertigo of a woman who has spent a decade perfecting the art of not being looked at, feeling eyes land on her and stay.

I gave him the face. You know the face. Every maid in Boston knows the face: pleasant, vacant, faintly apologetic for existing. Eyes lowered at the proper intervals. A little breathlessness, as though thinking were effortful.

Brogan studied me the way he studied doors.

"You'll think it strange," he said at last, "a man my age asking after faces. But I make a study of them." He took out a notebook. My stomach dropped through the floorboard of the world. "Been watching that house on Chestnut Street three weeks now. I know every soul that goes in and out of it. The cook — big woman, Tuesday market, argues with the fishmonger. The gardener's boy — smokes where he shouldn't. Mrs. Marchmont — gray dress on Sundays, blue the rest of the week, doesn't know it." He turned a page. "And the driver. Quiet one. Reads."

The word went through me like a nail.

"Reads," I repeated, in the flat voice, but the flat voice had developed, I feared, a crack in it visible only to experts, and I suspected I was in the presence of an expert.

"When she waits outside the post office. Ten minutes, sometimes fifteen. Always reading something. Newspaper, mostly." He closed the notebook. He hadn't written anything in it. He had wanted to see what I did when he opened it, and now I understood that everything since the sawhorses had been that — a door held open to see who would look through. "Funny thing about readers. They think nobody notices them reading. Like thinking nobody notices a lighthouse."

"Sir, I don't—"

"Look up here."

Against every instinct that had kept me alive and unnoticed for ten years, I raised my eyes to his.

That was my mistake, and later I would understand it was also the only honest thing I did all morning. Because a face can lie all day — I could teach a seminar in lying faces — but the eyes are terrible conspirators, always confessing. And Brogan looked into mine and whatever he found there, it was not the cook or the gardener's boy or a woman worried about a sick mother in South Boston.

Something shifted behind his own eyes. Recognition — but recognition of the wrong thing, or maybe the right thing, I couldn't yet tell. Not *I know your face.* Rather: *I know what kind of face this is. I've seen it in a mirror.*

"Your mistress's letter," he said quietly.

"Sir, I couldn't—"

"Miss Villalobos."

The name landed on the cobbles between us like a dropped plate.

Nobody had called me that aloud in years. Edith never used it; to her I was Hazel, or *dear girl* when the port was in her. The household used nothing at all — I was the driver, the same way the motorcar was the motorcar. My name lived only in ledgers and on the wage envelope, spelled correctly, which is more than most employers manage. Hearing it in a stranger's mouth, in a policeman's mouth, at half past four in the morning beside two constables and a sawhorse — I promise you the electric chair holds no terrors beyond that moment.

"How do you know my—" I began, and then stopped, because continuing was confession.

"Because I count," said Brogan simply. "Wage books are public records if a man's determined enough, and I am a very determined man. Hazel Villalobos, engaged March 1909. No family local. No church. No sweetheart." He paused. "No one who'd miss you if you went to jail for carrying what I suspect you're carrying."

The constables had drifted back toward their wagon, whether by design or boredom I couldn't say. The wind came up the avenue off the water, smelling of tar and the incoming tide, and somewhere down toward the piers a ship's horn spoke once, low and impatient. The *Corvina*. The tide would not negotiate.

"Here is what happens now," Brogan said. "In about ninety seconds, I'm going to walk around the front of this motorcar, and I'm going to be looking at my notebook, and I'm going to wave you through, because my orders are to stop any courier leaving that house, and I will report — truthfully — that none passed this checkpoint." His jaw worked, as though the next words were heavy freight he had to load alone. "But you should understand what that means. It means the second gate will be warned. It means they'll open everything at the customs shed, seals and all, and they'll be *looking* for a woman in a gray coat who reads."

"Why?" My voice came out smaller than I meant it to. "Why warn me at all?"

He put the notebook away and looked down the avenue toward the dark water, and for a moment the counting man looked tired enough to be counted among the ruined.

"Because whoever's behind this burned those papers to bury a name," he said, "and twenty years ago they buried mine the same way, and I stood on a pier much like yours and let the ship go." He turned back, and his eyes went briefly, unbearably gentle. "Drive fast, Miss Villalobos. And drive better than fast — drive *someone else*. Because they won't remember your face."

He tapped the roof of the motorcar twice, walked around the front with his nose in his unwritten notebook, and waved me past the sawhorses without so much as glancing up.

I drove. Seventeen miles an hour, both hands on the wheel, expression like a Tuesday, while my heart tried to climb out through my teeth. Behind me the checkpoint receded. Ahead, past the warehouses, the customs shed waited with its lamps already burning, and men already waiting, and somewhere among them — I would learn this within the hour, though the telling belongs to the next chapter — a telegraph boy who had arrived not ten minutes before me, carrying word of a gray-coated courier expected from Chestnut Street.

Brogan had lied to his superiors for me. That much I understood even then.

What I did not understand — what I would not understand until a customs officer asked for my papers and a familiar voice cut through the fog behind him saying *those are my letters, sir, and you will regret touching them* — was that I had not escaped the checkpoints at all.

I had merely traded the first one for a far more dangerous thing.

Edith Marchmont was coming to the pier herself.
