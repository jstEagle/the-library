## 7. The Second Gate

Hazel had rehearsed the customs shed the way other people rehearsed prayers.

*Eyes down. Hands loose. A girl delivering letters, nothing more.* The shed sat at the mouth of Pier 9 like a gray wooden tongue, and in front of it stood a customs officer built along the lines of a wardrobe—broad, immovable, with a mustache that seemed to have opinions of its own. Behind him, a second man lounged against the doorframe with a letter knife held casually, the way a bored man holds anything, and Hazel's stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution.

She stepped down from the motorcar.

"Delivery for the *Corvina*," she said, in the flat, apologetic voice she kept for such occasions—the voice of a person who had never once been interesting. "Ship's correspondence. They sail on the tide."

The wardrobe-man extended a hand like a ham. "Letters come through inspection, miss. Wartime rules."

"It isn't wartime," Hazel said, before she could stop herself.

"Rules got ready early." He wiggled his fingers. Hand them over, said the fingers.

*It's fine,* she told herself, and it was not fine, because the letter was in her coat's inner pocket and it was sealed twice and it weighed, in that moment, approximately as much as an anvil. She thought of Brogan at the first checkpoint, his eyes lingering half a second too long. She thought of the second gate—a police sergeant who had waved her through with suspicious courtesy, as though she were expected. Two checkpoints survived. The third was this shed, this knife, this hand.

She produced the letter.

It looked so ordinary lying there. Cream paper, wax seal, Edith Marchmont's distinctive upright script: *Mr. Ambrose Calloway, esq., to be delivered into his own hand aboard SS Corvina.* Inside it, folded small, the ledger pages that would hang four respectable men.

The officer turned it over. Frowned at the seal. Reached for the knife.

Hazel's mind went white and empty, and into that emptiness poured a single absurd thought: *ten years of reading other people's letters, and now I am going to die of irony.*

"Hazel."

The voice came from behind her, and it was impossible, and it was Edith Marchmont's.

* * *

Edith had never driven herself anywhere in eleven years, and she discovered within the first hundred yards why Hazel made it look easy.

The motorcar bucked. It stalled at the corner of Commercial Street while a fish cart honked its horse's opinion of her. Her hat—her good hat, the one with the pheasant feather—caught on the windscreen and she ripped it free with a sound like tearing linen. Somewhere in the back of her mind, a voice that sounded remarkably like her mother observed that ladies of her station did not drive, did not tear hats, and certainly did not chase their maids across Boston at six in the morning.

Edith informed the voice, mentally, that her maid was currently carrying the last evidence of a conspiracy that had already burned one warehouse and would not hesitate, she suspected, to burn a woman.

She had known, of course. Not everything—but enough. She had watched Hazel for years the way one watches a clock one has grown fond of: trusting it without examining its workings. She knew Hazel read the letters. She had known since the second year, when Hazel began handing them back with the seals restored so precisely that only a woman who had licked those same seals herself would ever notice the difference—and Edith had noticed, and had said nothing, because a housekeeper who read your correspondence and told no one was worth ten who read nothing and repeated everything.

But this morning, watching Hazel slip out the side door with a letter pressed to her ribs like a wounded bird, Edith had understood something else entirely: that Hazel was going to die for her family, politely, invisibly, at a customs shed on Pier 9, and that she had planned to do it without telling anyone.

That, Edith decided, wrenching the motorcar around a lamplighter still stooped over his pole, was intolerable.

The pier came into view through the windshield's crack. Gray water, gray sky, the black hull of the Corvina breathing against its ropes. And there, at the customs shed, a large officer turning Hazel's letter over in his hands while a thinner man unfolded a knife.

Edith stopped the motorcar by the simple method of driving it onto the curb and leaving it there.

She heard later that she crossed the pier at a run. She did not remember running. She remembered the cold coming up through the boards, and the feather from her ruined hat streaming out behind her like a banner, and the thought, clear as a bell: *Not this one. You will not have this one.*

"Hazel," she said again, arriving in a whirlwind of wool and fury, "you have let the engine roll backward. I shall thank you to attend to it directly."

Hazel stared at her. Edith had never once seen her stare. There was a first time for everything, apparently, including insubordination.

Then—blessedly, gloriously—Hazel caught on.

"Yes, ma'am," Hazel said, and reached for the letter.

The customs officer was quicker. "This stays with me until it's opened, miss." His eyes moved to Edith's hat, her torn hem, her unmistakable face. Something in him straightened; he recognized her, then, or recognized what she was worth. "You're the Marchmont woman."

"I am the Marchmont woman," Edith agreed pleasantly, "and that is my private correspondence, which you are holding without warrant, without cause, and without—" she leaned forward just slightly, "—discretion."

"Ship's mail gets inspected."

"That is not ship's mail. That is a letter from me to my family's solicitor concerning matters of a personal nature, and if you open it, Officer—" she glanced at his badge, "—Doyle, you will be opening it in front of a magistrate, because I will have you summoned before the ink dries. My family's name has been in the newspapers quite enough lately, wouldn't you say?"

Doyle hesitated. It was a small hesitation but a real one, and Edith pounced on it the way she had once pounced on debating opponents at Bryn Mawr.

"Unless," she said, softer now, and here she lowered her voice to the register she reserved for auction rooms and blackmail, "your instructions come from someone particular. Someone who would very much like that letter opened. Someone whose name, I imagine, appears nowhere official." She let the pause stretch. "Because if they do—if this is private enterprise dressed as public duty—then I wonder whether you've considered what happens to men like you when the men above you need someone small to blame."

The man with the knife had gone very still by the doorframe.

"Doyle," Edith continued, almost kindly now, "is it? I want you to think about Tuesday. Tuesday is when the prosecutors receive certain documents. Tuesday is when names start being read aloud in courtrooms with reporters taking shorthand. And Tuesday is when the question gets asked: who tried to stop the mail reaching Mr. Calloway?" She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "I should hate for that question to be answered with your name, Doyle. I imagine your wife hates it more."

"How do you know about—" Doyle began, and stopped himself, too late.

"The ledger?" Edith said sweetly. "Oh, Doyle. Everyone knows about the ledger soon."

It was a bluff. It was ninety percent bluff, and ten percent faith in a chauffeur she had never once caught in a lie. But Doyle didn't know that, and the knife-man didn't either, and between them they performed a silent negotiation conducted entirely in eyebrows, at the conclusion of which Doyle held out the letter with the expression of a man passing a hot coal.

"Private correspondence," he said heavily. "No inspection required for household staff carrying the mistress's personal mail. If that's what this is."

"That is what this is," said Edith.

"That is what this is," said Hazel, beside her, steady as a metronome.

They walked past him together. Edith kept her chin high and her heart hammering, and it was not until they had passed the shed, passed the knife-man, and gained the open boards of the pier itself, with the Corvina rising gray and enormous ahead of them, that she felt Hazel's hand close briefly, fiercely, around her sleeve.

"You drove," Hazel said. It came out somewhere between accusation and awe.

"I drove," Edith confirmed. "Appallingly. We'll never speak of it."

"You could have been seen. Followed. Those men know your face better than mine."

"And yours they know by heart already, apparently, or hadn't you noticed the sergeant at the second gate waving you through like an old friend?" Edith stopped then, and turned, and took both of Hazel's cold hands in hers, right there on the pier where anyone might see, and found that she did not care. "Ten years you have carried things for this family without dropping one. Don't you dare be insulted that I finally carried something for you."

Hazel's eyes did something complicated. Edith, who had catalogued every expression that careful face could produce, had never seen this one before. It looked, she thought, like a window being opened after a long winter.

"They nearly opened it," Hazel said quietly. "If you'd come two minutes later—"

"Then it's fortunate," said Edith, "that I have always been quick. Now. Where is Calloway?"

"Third gangway. He boards before the tide."

"Then we finish this together, and quickly, because I count at least two gentlemen near that gangway who are wearing their coats as though hiding something under them, and I don't believe they're smuggling brandy."

They walked toward the ship side by side—mistress and maid, though the word *maid* had, in the space of five minutes on a windy pier, quietly ceased to fit. Ahead of them, separating from the shadows near the third gangway with the unhurried gait of a man who had all morning and a score to settle, came Detective Brogan.

He tipped his hat to them both.

"Mrs. Marchmont," he said. "Miss Villalobos. Funny thing—" his eyes flicked to the letter in Hazel's pocket, then to the two men by the gangway, then back, and there was something in his face Edith couldn't read, some door opening or closing, "—I've been ordered to search this pier. Every bag, every coat, every soul."

Hazel went white. Edith's grip tightened on her arm.

"Funny thing," Brogan repeated, and stepped aside. "But orders change. Seems there's a stowaway reported on the lower deck—needs seeing to right away. All of us do, probably. Terrible shame about the next ten minutes. Nobody'll remember a thing."

And he walked off toward the gangway, leaving Hazel staring after him with the letter burning against her ribs and three minutes—perhaps less—before the Corvina cast off for good.
