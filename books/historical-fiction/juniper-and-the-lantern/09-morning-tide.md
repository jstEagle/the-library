## 9. Morning Tide

The Corvina did not so much leave the harbor as shrug it off.

Hazel stood at the rail of the pier with her hands folded—hands that forty minutes ago had been shaking so badly she'd had to press them flat against her coat to make them stop—and watched the ship gather itself against the gray water. The gulls wheeled and complained overhead, the way gulls do, as if they had opinions about maritime law.

Behind her, on the cobbles, came the sound she had been dreading: motorcars. Two of them, moving fast, doors slamming before the engines had properly finished dying.

"Too late," Brogan said beside her. He said it the way a man comments on weather. "The tide waited for nobody. Imagine that."

Hazel did not look at him. Looking at him felt dangerous still, like touching a stove to confirm it was hot. "You let them believe they might catch us."

"I let them *hope*," Brogan corrected. "Hope makes men run. Running makes them sweat." He tucked his hands into his pockets and regarded the arriving officials—the district attorney's man, a police captain whose face Hazel knew from the society pages, two clerks clutching folders like life preservers—as they skidded to a halt at the pier's end and beheld nothing but open water and a woman in a chauffeur's coat.

One of them looked at her. Looked harder. Started forward.

Brogan stepped—not in front of her, exactly, but into the space between, the way a man adjusts his stance without appearing to move at all. "Miss Villalobos is with me," he said. "Material witness. She's been cooperative since Tuesday."

She had not been cooperative since Tuesday. She had been terrified since Tuesday. But she kept her eyes down and her mouth soft and unremarkable, and after a moment the official's attention slid off her the way water slides off oilcloth. Ten years of practice, spent in a single glance.

*He recognized my eyes,* she thought, remembering the first checkpoint, and could not decide whether the skill was failing or finally being used for something.

The Corvina's horn sounded, long and low, absurdly cheerful. Somewhere aboard her, Mr. Aldous Finch, attorney at law, sat with a sealed letter in his breast pocket and, Hazel hoped, a strong cup of coffee. By Friday he would be in New York. By the following Wednesday, prosecutors who had spent months claiming ignorance would discover, with great public surprise, a ledger naming three city aldermen, one police superintendent, and a warehouse company that existed mostly on letterhead.

But all of that was Friday's business. This morning there was only the tide going out, and Hazel standing on the pier, and the strange lightness in her chest where dread used to live—a lightness so unfamiliar she mistook it at first for illness.

"You should eat something," Brogan said. "Witnesses who faint are less persuasive."

"That is the least comforting sentence anyone has ever said to me."

"I've got others." He touched the brim of his hat. "Go home, Miss Villalobos. Or go wherever it is people like us go."

People like us. She turned the phrase over the whole ride back, the motorcar humming under her hands, the streets waking around her—milk wagons, shuttered shops opening their eyes, a boy sweeping a doorstep with the vigor of someone being paid by the stroke. She had delivered the letter. She had done the thing. And yet some part of her kept waiting for the other shoe, because Hazel Villalobos had learned early that good mornings were merely the interval between shoes.

She simply didn't know yet how large this one would be.

* * *

The ledger reached the district attorney on Thursday, as promised. By Saturday the newspapers had gotten hold of it, and the word *conspiracy* appeared above the fold in type so large Edith Marchmont remarked over breakfast that one could read it from a passing tram.

"They'll resign," Edith said, not looking up from the paper. "Two of them. The third will give an interview blaming clerical error and then resign by month's end, mark me." She folded the paper with the crisp violence of a woman who had waited eleven years to fold it. "Alderman Pike once told my brother that the Marchmont name would be a debt paid in full if only we knew which side of the ledger we stood on. I wonder which side he thinks he stands on now."

"He stands in a great deal of trouble," Hazel offered.

Edith smiled—the sharp, private smile she showed almost no one—and for a moment the kitchen was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the stove. Hazel poured the coffee. It was, she thought, the closest thing to triumph she had ever tasted, and it came in the shape of an ordinary Tuesday morning, eggs and toast and a mistress who hummed while she buttered her bread.

Then, on Monday, the lawyers came.

* * *

They arrived in twos and threes through the week—young men from the prosecutor's office with notebooks and earnest faces, taking Hazel's account of the checkpoints, the pier, the letter. They were kind enough, in the way of young men who have been instructed to be kind to witnesses. It was the older one, a Mr. Hollis with spectacles and a memory like flypaper, who asked the question that stopped the room.

"And you became aware of the letter's contents," he said, "how, precisely?"

The clock on the mantel ticked. Somewhere upstairs a door closed. Hazel heard herself say, "I read the mail."

She had rehearsed a dozen versions of this confession in the dark hours—*I glanced at the address, I was given reason to suspect tampering, the seal was already broken*—and in the end she had used none of them, because Mr. Hollis had the sort of face that made lying feel like insulting a dog that had done nothing wrong.

"You read the household correspondence," Hollis repeated, making a note. "As routine."

"For ten years, sir. Yes."

The young men exchanged glances. One of them wrote something down very fast, the way people write when they think they've found the interesting part. Hazel sat very still and let them finish, and answered what was asked, and when they left she stood in the hall and listened to the motorcar carry her secret down the drive like a stone dropped in a pond, rippling outward toward a house where Edith Marchmont was arranging flowers and did not yet know a thing about her driver except everything she thought she knew.

There was, Hazel reflected, no graceful way to do this. There was only the doing of it.

She found Edith in the morning room, up to her wrists in chrysanthemums.

"The lawyers asked about the letter," Hazel began. "How I knew its contents."

Edith trimmed a stem. "And you told them?"

"I read your mail, ma'am." Her voice held. That surprised her; she'd expected it to crack like an egg. "All of it. Every letter, these ten years. Bills, invitations, your brother's letters, the ones marked private. I steamed nothing—I learned to open and reseal so cleanly you'd never find the seam, though I expect you will now go looking, and you'll find it, and I'm sorry for that most of all."

The scissors stopped.

For a long moment Edith did not turn around. The chrysanthemums trembled very slightly in her grip, orange and furious against the window light, and Hazel counted her own heartbeats and thought, absurdly, of the boy sweeping the doorstep, paid by the stroke.

"Ten years," Edith said at last.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Every—" She set the flowers down with exaggerated care, the way one does when the alternative is throwing them. When she turned, her face was unreadable, which was worse than anger. Anger, at least, would have been familiar. "The letter from my sister-in-law. After the baby died."

Hazel's throat closed. She made herself nod.

"You knew before I read it aloud at dinner. You knew, and you handed me the tea I take with bad news, and you said nothing." Edith's voice did not rise; it went quieter, which was more terrible. "The letter from the bank. From Peter's solicitor. From—" She stopped, and something moved behind her eyes, some rapid inventory of ten years of confidences, griefs, small shames, all of it passed through other hands first. "Good God, Hazel. You've been sitting in my pocket for a decade."

"Yes, ma'am."

"*Stop* calling me ma'am. You've earned the right to insult me properly."

"I don't want to insult you. I want—" Hazel's voice did crack then, finally, at the seam. "I want you to understand it wasn't idle. My first year in service, before you, I was in the Calloway house on Pinckney Street. I didn't read the letters then. I was taught it wasn't my place, and I was proud of knowing my place, if you can believe such a foolish thing." She swallowed. "There was a girl there, the scullery maid. Brigid. A letter came for her, and I delivered it unopened, because that was correct. It was from her sister, warning her off a man in the neighborhood. She never opened it herself—she couldn't read well and was ashamed to ask—and she married him inside four months, and inside fourteen months she was dead. A stairwell. An accident, they called it." The room was very quiet. "I have read every letter since. Every one. Because silence can protect, Mrs. Marchmont, but only sometimes, and only if you know when to break it. I broke mine too late for Brigid, and I swore I would always know exactly what I was silent about."

Edith stared at her.

"You spied on me to protect me," she said slowly, "out of guilt over a girl I have never heard of."

"When you say it in that tone it sounds insane."

"It sounds *exactly* like something you would do." And here Edith did something Hazel had never seen her do: she laughed and pressed her hand to her mouth at the same time, half sob, half astonishment. "Ten years. You wretched, faithful creature. You knew about the warehouse letters. You knew what my brother was mixed in before I did."

"You figured it out faster than I expected, ma'am."

"*Ma'am.*"

"...Edith."

The name sat strangely in Hazel's mouth, new-minted, heavy. Edith picked up the scissors and went back to the chrysanthemums, though her cuts had lost their violence; they were careful now, almost tender.

"The testimony," she said, snipping. "They'll ask you about it publicly. Reading the mail. In front of reporters, if Hollis has his way—he strikes me as thorough. Your name in the papers beside the words 'household espionage.'" Snip. "It may cost you references for the rest of your working life."

"I know."

"You'll be the woman who read Edith Marchmont's letters. Some fool will write a song."

"There are worse songs to be in."

Snip. Silence. Then, without turning: "Did you ever once use anything you read against me? Pass it along? Trade on it?"

"No. Never. Not once, and I'd swear it on anything you like, up to and including the chrysanthemums."

"Then I suppose," Edith said, and her voice was steady but her shoulders were not, "that I shall have to testify alongside a woman who knows every embarrassing thing I have ever put in writing. Heaven help us both. You'll be the most informed witness in the history of this Commonwealth." She set down the last stem and finally, finally turned fully around. "I am angry with you, Hazel. I expect to be angry for some days yet, possibly weeks. But you will not pack a bag tonight. Is that understood? If you sneak off in the dark like a thief I will hunt you down myself and be *far* less forgiving than I am being at this moment."

Something in Hazel's chest, wound tight for ten years, loosened one careful click.

"I don't own a bag worth sneaking off with," she said.

"No," Edith agreed. "You really must let me remedy that. We're about to become famous, you and I."

* * *

That night Hazel climbed the attic stairs to her narrow room and sat a long while at the window, watching the harbor lights. Below, the house breathed and settled. Somewhere in it lay a woman who knew her name—all of it, the whole shape of her—and had not turned her out.

On Monday she would stand in a courtroom and say *my name is Hazel Villalobos*, out loud, on the record, forever. The thought did not feel like falling. It felt like the moment at the top of a dive when you stop resisting and realize the water has been waiting for you all along.

She slept, and dreamed of a ship going out on the morning tide, and woke on Sunday to sunlight and the smell of coffee and, on the tray outside her door—left by a hand she knew as well as her own—a note, sealed, with her own name written across it in Edith Marchmont's unmistakable hand.

Hazel held it a moment in both palms, feeling its weight, all the weight of sealed things.

Then she opened it.
