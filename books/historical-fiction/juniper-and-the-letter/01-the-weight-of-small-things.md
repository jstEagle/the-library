## 1. The Weight of Small Things

Wren Nakamura had learned to count in the language of weight.

Not the weight of things as the mainlanders measured it — hundredweights, tons, the gross tonnage of ships she could hear but no longer see — but the truer weight, the weight a thing carried when you had to carry it yourself. A gallon of lamp oil: eight pounds. A box of matches, sealed in waxed paper against the damp: half a pound, and worth more than half a pound of anything else on Juniper Point. Her daughter Noor, asleep upstairs with one arm flung over the edge of the cot: thirty-seven pounds last spring, and gaining, which was its own kind of wealth.

She kept the ledger in her head because paper could be lost. Paper burned, soaked, blew off cliffs. But the mind, if you fed it well and did not let it starve on grief, would hold what you asked of it for years.

*Oil:* forty-one gallons in the tower cistern, twenty-two in the reserve cans lashed under the dwelling's eaves. *Matches:* nine boxes, plus the tin of sixty-three loose ones she'd counted twice by candlelight and once by daylight to be sure. *Bread flour:* enough for five weeks if she stretched it with the cornmeal. *Salt pork:* the slab from Pell's boat, eleven pounds. *Lamp wicks:* fourteen, cut to length, rolled and stored dry. *Rope:* the good hemp coil in the boathouse, and the older coil that would hold a body but not trust one.

And shoes. She went to the shelf above the stove where Noor's shoes stood in a row like small boats at their moorings, each pair stuffed with newspaper against the damp, each pair outgrown or about to be. The red pair from two winters back. The brown pair with the split seam she kept meaning to mend. And the new ones — new to Noor, secondhand from the missionary crate — leather still stiff, soles thick enough for rock.

Wren picked up the brown pair and turned them over in her hands. Mending meant waxed thread. Waxed thread meant the sewing kit, which meant the drawer, which meant the whole inventory again, and she made herself do it, item by item, because the ritual was the point. Needles: seven. Thread: black, brown, sailcloth white. Thimble: one, brass, Toma's mother's.

Toma's mother's thimble fit her thumb badly and she used it anyway.

Outside, the wind had begun to speak in the low register that meant business. Not the shriek of a gust finding a gap in the shingles — she knew every one of those gaps by name, had stuffed them herself with oakum and wool — but the long note underneath, the sound of air moving across ninety miles of open water with nothing to slow it and everything behind it. The barometer in the hallway had been falling since Tuesday. It stood now at 28.9 and the needle seemed to lean, like a man at a rail watching something come.

The great storms. The old keepers had called them that before her, and their wives before them, and there were names carved into the doorframe of the lamp room she'd never bothered to read all the way through, dates going back into the last century, each one a night somebody hadn't expected to survive.

Wren set the shoes down and wrote in the log.

*Nov. 14, 1934. Wind SW freshening. Barometer falling fast. All stores inventoried. Lamp trimmed. Expect weather.*

Expect weather. Four words doing the work of four hundred. That was the style of the service, and though she was technically no service at all — an assistant keeper's widow who had simply refused to leave, whose appointment papers existed only in Crane's ledgers as a line item he never failed to mention when it suited him — she kept the log the way the manuals prescribed, because the manuals were right. A log was not for you. A log was for whoever came after, standing where you stood, reading your hand and trying to understand your night.

She thought sometimes about what someone would make of these pages. Thousands of entries, and almost none of them true in the way that mattered. *No vessels missing. All quiet.* The sea told her otherwise; the sea washed things up below the light with terrible regularity, and she recorded the driftwood and the hatch covers and once, two winters ago, a ship's bell with no name on it, and the official version of events remained what Crane's office sent down on the ferry: *no casualties reported, Juniper station.*

She had stopped writing letters about it. There had been three letters, then a visit from Halvard, smiling on the ferry dock with his cap in his hands, and after that she had learned what her neighbors already knew: that Aldous Crane owned the cannery, the cannery owned the boats, the boats insured themselves against loss, and loss was therefore a thing that happened to other people, on paper, nowhere at all.

She blew out the candle in the kitchen and climbed.

* * *

The tower stairs were ninety-six. She counted them the way she counted everything, without deciding to, her hand on the rope rail, her boots finding the worn centers of each tread. At the top, the lamp room smelled of brass polish and burning mineral oil and, beneath both, the cold stone breath of the sea. The great lens stood in its cage of gears, prisms stacked like a honeycomb cut by a careful giant, and inside it the flame sat steady while the clockwork turned the whole assembly around it, flash every ten seconds, flash every ten seconds, all night, every night, whether anyone watched or not.

Most nights nobody watched. Most nights the water between Juniper Point and the shipping lane held nothing but fish and cold. But the nights it mattered, it mattered absolutely, and that arithmetic justified her whole existence here, and she knew it, and some mornings the knowledge was the only reason she got up before dawn.

Tonight she checked the rotation, oiled the pivot the manual said didn't need oiling, wiped salt haze from the storm panes with a cloth kept for nothing else. Through the glass, the ocean ran dark to the west, but along the horizon there was a different darkness, a thickness, the way the sky looked before snow except this was water being lifted and moved and thrown. Somewhere out under that wall of weather, the last boats of the season were running for home. The *Pelican*. The *Adelaide B*. The *Marianne* —

— the *Juniper Maid*, which had gone out in November four years ago and was now a name on a list, *lost with all hands*, insured for more than her hull was worth, and Wren stood at the rail of her own light and looked at the horizon and felt the old arithmetic try to assert itself. *Husband: 148 pounds. Coat: 6. Boots: 4. What can be carried: nothing.*

She had gotten very good at not carrying it. That was the trick nobody told you about grief. Everyone warned you it was heavy. Nobody warned you that you'd get strong, and that the strength would feel like betrayal.

Flash. Ten seconds. Flash.

Down the stairs, ninety-six, her hand on the rope. In the kitchen she banked the stove and stood a moment listening to the house — the tick of cooling iron, the rattle of a window she'd have to wedge again, Noor breathing through the floorboards overhead in the particular rhythm that meant deeply asleep, not dreaming, safe for now.

Then she took the inventory book — the real one, hers, kept in pencil in a school composition notebook Noor had brought home from the mission summer two years past — and added a line.

*Storm coming. Everything ready.*

It wasn't quite a lie. Nothing is ever quite ready. But the oil was topped, the matches dry, the water barrels full against the freezing of the pump, the boat tied down with double lines, the storm shutters hung on the seaward windows. If the bridge closed — and with a barometer like this, the tidal bridge would close, the causeway road going under twice a day and staying under, until the crossings became impossible and the ferry stopped and Juniper Point became what it always secretly was: an island wearing a road like a costume.

If the bridge closed, they would be alone with the winter. Eleven families, one cannery, one light, and whatever came ashore.

Wren put the notebook away and allowed herself, as a last indulgence of the evening, to stand at the kitchen window and look north along the point to where the bridge began. Even in the dark she could see the spray going over it now, white ropes of it, whipping across the roadway. By morning the timbers would sing. By tomorrow night, likely, the crossing would be suicide.

She thought: I should have taken the ferry to the mainland Tuesday when Marta begged me to, bought the shoes a size large, laid in sugar, argued with the draper about flannel. She thought: there is never a Tuesday like the Tuesday you didn't take.

She thought: we will manage. We always manage. Managing is the one thing I do better than anything on this earth, and I hate that it's true, and I will do it anyway.

She banked the fire, took the candle, and went up.

* * *

Noor woke once in the night, and Wren heard it from the landing — the small change in breathing, the rustle — and stood outside her door with her hand raised to the latch, not entering, just confirming. Six years old. Sharp as a gull and twice as watchful. She had her father's habit of counting things too; Wren had found her at the window last week tallying the fishing boats out, lips moving. *Four, five, six.* Counting them home.

That was the inheritance, then. Not the thimble, not the dark Nakamura eyes. The counting. The refusal to let a number go unverified, because numbers were how you kept people, in a world where keeping was all you got to do.

The wind rose through the small hours in stages, like a tide of air, and by three o'clock the tower was humming, the whole column of it, a note she felt in her teeth. She went up twice to check the mechanism and both times the flame burned true, the lens turned, the light went out over the water and came back and went out again. Whatever was out there in the dark — and the sound of this sea said *something* was out there, something big moving with the storm — it would see Juniper Point. Whatever else failed tonight, that would not.

She was in the kitchen at first light, boiling water for coffee she stretched with chicory, when she saw it.

Not with her eyes at first. With the dog — there was no dog, but the instinct operated anyway, some old sentinel part of her brain lifting its head. The storm had backed off around dawn into hard gray squalls with clean gaps between, and out one such gap she could see the base of the point below the light, the black shelf of rock where the sea deposited its accounts. Driftwood usually. Kelp. Once, the bell.

This morning there was a shape.

She set the kettle down without deciding to. Got the glasses from the shelf — the naval binoculars, Toma's, one strap riveted where he'd cracked it — and worked the focus with cold thumbs.

A man. Face down at the tideline, one arm thrown up over the rocks as if hailing someone, as if the sea had finally delivered him to the address he'd been asking for. Dark hair matted. A fisherman's oilskins, torn at the shoulder. And beyond him, scattered down the reef line like punctuation, the debris: planking, a hatch cover, a coil of net — wreckage in a quantity that had a name, and the name was *boat*, and the boat had a name too, probably, and she knew without knowing how that whatever it was called, the cannery office had already written it down differently.

*Lost with all hands,* they would say. Or, if the insurance preferred it: *no vessel reported missing.*

Wren lowered the glasses. Her heart was doing something complicated, grief and duty braided together so tightly she couldn't have said which was pulling her down the stairs and out the door and along the cliff path with the wind trying to take her hat and the spray coming up through the gaps in the rock. Thirty-seven pounds of daughter asleep upstairs. Forty-one gallons of oil in the cistern. Nine boxes of matches, dry.

And now, at the bottom of her own stairs, delivered by the worst night in three winters: one man, maybe alive, definitely trouble. You could see trouble even at a distance. It had a shape, like everything. It had a weight.

She reached him as the next squall came in, dropped to her knees on the wet rock, and put two fingers under the hinge of his jaw.

There was a pulse. Faint, stubborn, irregular — but there.

His eyes opened halfway, found her face, and focused on it with an effort that seemed to cost him everything he had left. His mouth moved. Salt wind tore the words away, and she bent closer, shielding him with her body, and heard:

"They'll say — " A cough, wracking him. His hand came up, clawlike, and gripped her coat with the desperation of a drowning man even though he was, for the moment, no longer drowning. "They'll say there were no casualties."

Below them, the sea shoved another plank against the rocks, and another, patient as a clerk delivering mail.
