## 2. A Man Against the Rocks

The storm had been working the point all night, and by morning it left its receipts scattered across the tideline: a hatch cover, half a dory seat, rope in yellowed coils, and — furthest out, wedged against the black rocks below the light — what Wren first took for another piece of wreckage.

She knew better within three steps. Wreckage didn't arrange itself face-down with one arm hooked over stone.

She went down the cliff path the way she always did, sideways, one hand on the rock, Noor's voice in her head saying *you promised*, because she had promised, after Toma, that she'd stop going down to the water like grief was a door she could walk through. But the light-keeper's duty book said otherwise. *Assistance rendered:* a line item she could fill in instead of the other line items, the ones with no official box.

*Assistance rendered.* She said it under her breath like an incantation against hope.

The man was breathing. That surprised her more than anything else about him — the shallow, sand-scraped rhythm of a body that had decided, without consulting anyone, to keep on. His oilskins were cannery issue, the cheap kind, split at both shoulders. One boot gone. His hands were torn up so badly she couldn't tell his age from them, though his face said thirty, maybe less, weathered early the way fishermen aged here, like driftwood.

She got her shoulder under his arm and hauled. He weighed what drowning men weigh — everything, and nothing that can help you. Twice he stirred and muttered something, and both times it sounded like *no*, and she thought: fair enough. She'd said it herself plenty, to this sea, this point, this life.

By the time she got him onto the flat rock above the spray line, her coat was soaked through and her hair had come loose and there was blood on her sleeves that wasn't hers, and she knelt there panting and thought, with a clarity that frightened her: *I know how to do this part. I hate that I know how to do this part.*

She'd done it for Toma, or tried to. Three years ago, on a beach forty miles south of this one, kneeling over a man already gone, doing compressions on a chest the sea had emptied of everything worth saving. The doctor had said afterward, gently, that it would have made no difference if she'd started an hour sooner. He'd meant it kindly. It had lodged in her anyway, like a splinter: all that effort spent on a closed account.

This one wasn't closed. This one was breathing.

She worked him over with the competence she'd built out of ruin — cleared his airway, stripped the wet wool, wrapped him in the blanket she'd had the sense to bring, which was itself a kind of inventory: she had learned to carry blankets. To carry matches in waxed paper. To carry, always, a small flask of spirits because shock killed as surely as water. She got a measure of it into him and he coughed, a terrible racking sound, and then his eyes opened.

They were gray, unremarkable eyes, except that they went from blank to afraid so fast it was like watching ice crack.

"No," he said, clearly this time. "No — you have to say—" His hand came up, grabbed her wrist with a strength that startled her, a dying animal's strength. "You have to log it."

"Log what?" she said. "You're alive. I'm logging that."

He stared at her. Then at the lighthouse above, white tower against the running gray sky, and something in his face did a strange thing — relief and terror at once, arriving together, inseparable.

"The light," he said. "You keep the light."

"Six years."

"Then you'll have seen." His grip tightened. "Last night. The *Merrilee*. You'll have logged her going down."

Wren felt the morning shift under her, the way ice shifts when weight comes onto it.

"There was no *Merrilee*," she said slowly. "There was no wreck logged. I've had the lamp burning since dusk. Nobody signaled. Nothing came through from the cannery office. The log says—" She stopped, because his face had changed again, and not for the better.

"The log says nothing," he whispered. "That's right. That's exactly right."

* * *

Elias Voss had counted eleven waves between himself and the boat, and then he had stopped counting, because counting implied there would be an after in which the number mattered.

Now there was an after, and it smelled like iodine and lamp oil, and the ceiling above him was the underside of a light.

He had been below when the *Merrilee* hit. That was the fact he kept returning to, turning it over like a coin worn smooth: he had gone below to warm his hands, and Petter had taken the wheel, and when the water came it came into the engine room first and took Petter and Soren and the Brandt brothers before Elias even understood the ship was lost. He had come up into black water and blacker sky and swum without deciding to swim, the way you do things in dreams, and somewhere in it there had been the light — Juniper Point, steady, four seconds on, two off, the most reliable thing in his entire life — and he had steered for it the way a man steers for anything when everything else is gone.

Eleven men aboard. Four in the water with him at the start. He remembered shouting. He remembered someone answering, once.

And now he lay in a strange bed in a stranger's house, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of cedar, listening to a woman move around the room below with the economy of someone who has learned exactly how much effort every task costs, and he thought: *the log will say no casualties. Because that's what the logs say.*

He had known about the reports for eight months. Not guessed — known. He ran the tally books for the *Merrilee*'s owner, and a tally book is an honest instrument; it only records what actually happened. What happened and what got reported were two different ledgers, and the space between them was where Aldous Crane's money lived. A boat "lost" with full insurance papers in order. A crew signed on in one port, written off in another. And the families — God, the families, paid off in wages owed and warned quiet, and mostly they stayed quiet, because Crane owned the winter, and you don't argue with the man who owns your winter.

Elias had copied things. He wasn't sure why, at first — some instinct older than courage, the same instinct that makes rats leave a ship. Pages in his own hand, dates, tonnage, the names of crews who officially never existed or officially never died. He'd hidden them inside the lining of his sea bag and told himself he was waiting for the right moment, and the right moment kept not coming, because the right moment is a luxury and men like him don't get luxuries.

Then Crane put him on the *Merrilee*. Put him — the one man who knew — on a run through the worst water on the coast, in November, with a forecast nobody sane would sail on.

He hadn't understood it until the wave came. He understood it now, lying in the keeper's spare bed with his lungs full of salt and his hands bandaged in strips of bedsheet. Crane hadn't needed proof of a wreck. Crane had needed a wreck. And a man who knows too much is the cheapest ballast to throw overboard.

Footsteps on the stair. The woman came in — dark-haired, mid-thirties, a face like a coastline itself, weathered and carved down to essentials. He'd seen her before, years back, at the cannery dock. Nakamura. The widow. The whole point knew her story; the whole point pretended it didn't.

"You're awake," she said.

"Barely."

"Your name."

"Elias. Voss." Each word cost him. "Yours I know. Wren."

"Good. Saves time." She set a cup of something hot on the stool beside him. Broth, from the smell. "Tell me about the *Merrilee*."

So he told her. The outbound run, the cargo manifests he'd flagged, the weather warnings ignored — no, worse than ignored, *countermanded*, the order coming down by wireless relay from Crane's office itself: *sail tonight.* Eleven men. He gave her the names because the names were the point, were always the point; numbers were how Crane thought and names were how everyone else should.

When he finished, she was very still, looking at the window where the rain ran down in silver ropes.

"My husband," she said, "was lost on the *Annelise*. Three years ago. October."

Elias closed his eyes. He knew the *Annelise* file. He had copied parts of it.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it, and knew it wasn't nearly enough for what he was about to do to her.

* * *

Wren fed the fire, checked the lamp mechanism though it was hours from needing her, and made herself go through the logbook again, page by page, the way you check a lock twice when something feels wrong.

October 14th. Weather entries, barometer readings, the lamp's hours. Clean lines in her own small handwriting. Nothing about a *Merrilee*. Nothing about anything.

But she remembered the night now that he'd named it. The wind had come around to the southeast past midnight with a violence she'd noted in the weather column as *gale, veering SE, severe*. She remembered standing in the gallery thinking: *someone's out in this.* You always thought that. It was the curse of the trade — every storm was full of somebody.

And no signal. No flare, no gun, no boat light rounding the point. Which meant the *Merrilee* had gone down fast, or far, or both — and it also meant something uglier, something she turned over while Elias slept and Noor napped and the house held its breath around the stranger in the guest bed:

It meant no distress call had gone out. And boats in trouble called. Boats always called, unless the radio was silent on purpose, unless the wreck was supposed to happen exactly where it happened, unwitnessed, unlogged, unwitnessable.

She stood at the window a long time, watching the sea work itself against the rocks, and thought about the phrase *no casualties*, and how a word like *casualties* needs bodies to be real, and how a sea that keeps what it takes makes denial easy. The ocean was Crane's best accomplice. It never testified. It just swallowed, and moved on, and left the shore to make sense of the debris.

Downstairs, the fisherman was awake again, watching her from the bed with those cracked-ice eyes.

"There's a packet," he said. "In my sea bag. If my bag came ashore with me — it didn't, did it. I don't remember reaching shore."

"It didn't come ashore with you."

He absorbed that. Then, quietly, with the terrible patience of a man spending his last currency: "Then I need you to listen to everything I say next, Keeper Nakamura, and I need you to remember it even if you decide to hate me. Because I don't think I'm going to be the one who carries it."

"What is it?" she asked, though some part of her, the part that had inventoried her whole life down to what could be carried, already knew: it was heavy. Whatever it was, it was heavy, and it was hers now, because everything that washed up at Juniper Point belonged to Juniper Point.

"Proof," Elias Voss said. "Of what happened to your husband's boat. Of whose order sent her out. It's in my head, and half of it's on paper, and the paper is at the bottom of the channel unless—" He broke off, coughing, and when he could speak again his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Unless the tide gives it back. It might. The current sets north along this shore. Things from the wreck site come ashore below your light. Everything comes ashore below your light eventually."

Outside, the gale pressed its whole weight against the glass, and Wren thought of the tideline that morning — the hatch cover, the rope, the shape in the rocks she had mistaken for wreckage.

Everything comes ashore eventually.

She picked up the lamp key from its hook, out of habit, out of ritual, out of whatever you call the thing that remains when grief has been worn down into competence. Dusk was five hours off. The light would burn. That much she controlled.

What the tide brought in — that was another matter entirely.
