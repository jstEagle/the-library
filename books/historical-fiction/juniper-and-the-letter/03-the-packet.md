## 3. The Packet

Wren had carried men before. Not like this — not the whole dead weight of one, breathing in wet rags — but she had hauled dories up a slipway with her shoulder, had dragged Toma's sea chest down from the loft alone, and she knew how to let her legs do the work her arms refused. She got Elias under the arms and walked him backward up the rocks, one stone at a time, his boots leaving twin dark smears on the lichen.

The storm had spent itself sometime before dawn, and what remained was the aftermath: a sky scoured to the color of old tin, kelp strewn along the tideline like entrails, and the light above them turning slow on its greased track because Wren had wound it at four o'clock as though it were any other morning, as though there were not a man bleeding out on her gallery floor.

She got him to the stove first. That was the order of things in a keeper's house — heat, then water, then questions. The room was small and smelled of lamp oil and yesterday's coffee, and when she lowered him into Toma's chair he made a sound that was almost a laugh.

"That's his seat," Elias said. His voice was gravel dragged through salt water.

"You knew my husband."

"Everyone knew Toma Nakamura." He coughed, and the cough bent him double, and when he straightened there was blood on his lips, bright and thin as berry juice. "Knew him better than most."

Wren put her hand flat against his chest without asking permission. She'd dressed wounds on fishermen since she was seventeen; the coast taught you triage before it taught you anything else. Ribs cracked on the left, maybe the right too. Something wrong deeper down — she could feel it in the way his breathing hitched, two rhythms where there should have been one. Water in the lung, or blood. Either way, neither of them could fix it, and the bridge to the mainland was already closing for the season, and Dr. Prather was forty miles of gray water away.

"How long were you in the water?" she asked.

"Long enough." He looked past her, toward the window where the sea lay flattened and innocent, and something in his face made her turn to look too, as if the water might still be standing there holding what it had taken. "The *Maren B* went down off Sable Tooth. Eleven of us. You'll read it was three."

"What?"

"You'll read it was three," he said again, patiently, like a man explaining arithmetic to a child. "Because that's what Crane's paper says. Three lost, vessel unsalvageable, claim filed. But eleven of us went out Tuesday night, Mrs. Nakamura, in weather Crane's own man told us was fine. Halvard stood on the dock and said it himself. Fine weather, full hold, don't dawdle."

Wren took her hand off his chest. Her fingers had gone strange, distant, as if they belonged to someone across the room.

"The *Maren B* wasn't yours," she said. "The *Maren B*'s crew—"

"Is seven men short in the ground ledger and none short at all in the flesh. Four of us made the Tooth before she broke. I'm the only one who made shore." His eyes came around to hers and held there. They were pale eyes, nearly colorless, the kind that always looked startled. "You keep this light. You know what went out Tuesday night. You counted."

She had. She'd stood in the lantern room and watched a running light fight the swells off Sable Tooth and vanish between one wave-crest and the next, and she'd written *vessel unknown, course south by east* in her own log, and she had thought, briefly, coldly, *another one*, the way you think it about weather, about seasons. Another one. As though the losing of boats had become simply a fact of Juniper Point, like fog.

"I counted," she said.

"Then you're the second witness I've got. Me being the first, and me—" he gestured at himself, at all of it, the ruined chest, the blue lips, "—not expected to last."

* * *

He lasted through the morning. She gave him brandy cut with hot water and he waved it off after one sip, saying his accounts should be kept clear, which was such an accountant's thing to say that she almost smiled, until she understood that was exactly what he'd been aboard the *Maren B* to be. A tally man. Crane sent one on every trip now, ever since the *Cormorant*, ever since crews started grumbling that their shares shrank while their risks grew. Elias Thorne had been a fisherman for thirty years before he became a set of eyes, and he said the shame of it sat in him like swallowed hook.

"Shouldn't have taken his coin," he said. He'd slid lower in the chair and she kept propping him up with cushions, building him a nest the way you do for the dying because there is nothing else left to build. "But my girl needed shoes same as anyone's."

"Mine too," Wren said.

"Right. Right." He closed his eyes. Opened them. "Mrs. Nakamura. There's a thing I need you to do, and I need you to say yes before you hear what it is, or you won't."

"No," Wren said. "I won't agree blind. Nobody should."

Something moved across his face that might have been respect. "Fair. Fair enough." He fumbled at his shirtfront, and she helped him — his hands had stopped obeying sometime in the last hour — and together they got the oilskin packet out from against his skin, where it had been tied with waxed cord and worn so long the cord had worn a groove in him. It was smaller than she expected. Flat. Heavy in the way paper gets heavy when there are many sheets.

"Letters," he said. "Copies. Originals went to a marine lawyer in Port Anselm in October, but the originals never arrived — or they did and they're sitting in Crane's safe by now, bought back or buried. So I made copies. Every manifest Pell signed that doesn't match the wreck report. Every insurance filing. Names of the dead that weren't dead. Payments to widows marked *wages owed* so nobody would ask why a company pays a drowned man's family full share plus silence money." His breath caught and held and released. "And a letter from Crane himself. Dated March. To his underwriters. Listing vessels *concluded uneconomical to maintain*. You want to hear the names?"

"Tell me."

"*Cormorant. Sweet Alice. Petrel.*" He stopped. He looked at her with those pale startled eyes and she watched him decide, in real time, whether to say the last one, and she watched him decide to say it anyway, because he was dying and dying men don't flinch. "*Gannet.*"

The room did not move. Wren did not move. Outside, the light turned and turned on its track, patient as it had been for sixty years, and somewhere below the cliff the tide was coming back in over the place where she had found this man, filling his footprints one by one.

The *Gannet* went down in November of 1929 with five hands. One of them was Toma Nakamura, master.

"That's a lie," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded fine. Reasonable. That frightened her more than screaming would have. "Crane wouldn't have written that. Toma was his best captain. Toma brought in catches when nobody else—"

"Brought them in at cost," Elias said gently. "Brought them in without running the risk loads. Told other captains not to go out in flagged weather. Filed honest reports." A pause. "Made Crane's fraud expensive."

"He drowned. There was a storm. I know the storm, I lived through the storm, I—"

"There was a storm," Elias agreed. "There was also a letter, dated the week before, in that packet, in Crane's hand, instructing the *Gannet* to sail on a date certain regardless of conditions. Insurance had just been raised on her hull. Read it yourself. I didn't write it. I only carried it."

She did not take the packet. She stood with her hands at her sides and looked at it lying on the table among the mugs and the match tins and the ordinary debris of an ordinary morning, and it looked exactly like what it was — papers, ink, nothing — and it weighed on the room like a stone dropped in a well.

"If that's true," she said finally, "then why give it to me? Give it to the marine board. Give it to a newspaper. I'm a widow who winds a lamp."

"Because the board sits on the mainland and the mainland is closing." He said it slowly, spacing the words out against his failing breath. "Storms close that bridge tomorrow or the day after. Everything on this island belongs to Crane until spring — the dock, the boats, the wages, the debts. Every outbound thing gets searched at his cannery. Except a keeper. Nobody searches a keeper. Keepers are federal, near enough. And because—" the cough took him then, hard, and Wren moved to him and he gripped her wrist with a strength that surprised them both, "—because your husband found these numbers first. Before me. I found his notes in the company office, copied in his hand. He was going to carry them across himself."

The floor seemed to tilt. Or perhaps she tilted. She sat down across from him, in Noor's school chair, her knees folding without consulting her.

"Toma was going to cross?"

"In November. Two years back." Elias's voice was going quieter, being rationed out like the brandy. "Weather turned. You know how that goes." 

She knew. She knew precisely how that went.

"So it falls to whoever's left," he said. "It usually does. That's the whole of history, Mrs. Nakamura. It falls to whoever's left."

* * *

He died a little after two in the afternoon, with the tide halfway up the rocks and Noor due home from the Kesslers' within the hour, and Wren was grateful — was sick with gratitude — that the child would miss it.

At the end he asked her to open the window, though the cold came in like a blade, and she did, and he looked at the sea for a while and said, "Four of ours are still out there. Under the Tooth. And the paper will say three, and it will say the *Maren B* broke up accidental, and some clerk in Port Anselm will stamp it and go to his lunch." Then he said, "Don't let him stamp it," and then he said nothing else.

She closed his eyes and covered him with the quilt off her own bed, the good one, the one with Marta's stitching on the hem, and then she went to the table and untied the waxed cord with fingers that shook only now, now that no one was watching.

Seven documents. She counted them twice. Manifests in Mr. Pell's careful copperplate, each with a line of figures that did not agree with the wreck reports clipped beside them. An insurance schedule. Two affidavits from crewmen whose names she half-knew, men she might have passed on the dock road a hundred times, nodding to. And last, folded once, the letter in Aldous Crane's hand — she recognized the writing from the pay envelopes, that tall confident slant — listing vessels and dates and figures, and there, third from the top:

*Gannet — Nov. 1929 — sail order attached — hull value recovered £1,840.*

*Sail order attached.* Attached to what? The page ended there. Somewhere in the world there was another piece of paper with Toma's sailing written on it like a sentence passed, and Aldous Crane had signed it, and she had sold the cannery's fish and taken its wage and bowed her head at its hall for four years thinking the sea had widowed her.

The sea had only buried the body. The rest was murder done in an office, in tall confident ink.

She heard Noor's boots on the path below, quick and light, and the sound of her singing something tuneless, and Wren stood in the middle of her kitchen with a dead man cooling in her bed and her husband's death warrant folded under a mug, and she had perhaps ninety seconds to decide where in a lighthouse a thing could live that must not be found.

She wrapped the packet in oilcloth, knotted it, and pushed it deep into the flour crock, down under a week's worth of flour, and smoothed the top, and put the lid on, and was ladling soup into Noor's bowl when the door opened and cold air and her daughter came in together.

"Mama, Kessler's dog followed me half the— Mama, whose coat is that on the peg?"

"A fisherman's," Wren said. "He was wrecked in the night. Come eat your soup."

Noor hung up her own coat beside the dead man's, chattering about the dog, about the ice forming on the trough, about nothing, and ate, and Wren watched her over the steam and thought: *everything can be carried. That has always been the lesson. The question is only what it costs the carrier.*

After the dishes she climbed to the lantern room, because the lamp required it, because ritual holds, and while the great lens turned she looked north toward the mainland, where the tidal causeway still showed its dark spine at low water — for now. Tomorrow's gale would shut it. Everyone said so. The barometer falling all day, the gulls gone quiet, the whole coast holding its breath.

Which meant whatever she was going to do with seven pieces of paper and one dead man, she would do it sealed inside a country owned by the man who had killed her husband.

Below, in the yard, Noor was drawing letters in the frost on the water barrel with one bare finger. Wren couldn't see them from up here, but she knew her daughter's hand. She knew it the way she knew the light's turning: without looking.

* * *

The knock came at dusk.

Two heavy blows, unhurried, the kind that assume the door will open. Wren wiped her hands on her apron, checked — without meaning to, already the habit of hours — that the flour crock sat square and undisturbed, and opened the door onto Halvard, the cannery foreman, standing in the last gray light with his cap in his hands and his men behind him in the yard.

"Mrs. Nakamura." His voice was mild. It was always mild; that was the worst of him. "Storm's coming. We're posting notice at every house. Bridge closes tomorrow's low water till spring." He paused, and his pale eyes went past her shoulder, into the warm lit room, to the quilt-wrapped shape on her bed. "We heard a man washed ashore below your light. Company's obliged to collect its dead."

"He isn't company," Wren said. "He died in my house. He'll be buried from my house."

Halvard considered this. Behind him, one of the men shifted, and the wind moved through the yard, lifting the frost-dust like smoke.

"Company insures its crews," he said mildly. "There'll be paperwork."

"There's always paperwork," said Wren, and stepped back to let them carry Elias out into the dark.
