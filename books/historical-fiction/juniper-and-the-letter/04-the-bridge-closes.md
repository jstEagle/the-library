## 4. The Bridge Closes

The tide did not close the bridge all at once. It closed it the way a hand closes — fingers first, then palm.

Wren stood at the north end of it on the morning of the third storm, watching the channel fill. The Juniper Narrows ran between the point and the mainland like a held breath, and twice a day the tidal flats drained far enough that the old timber span could be walked, driven over, mended. Twice a day it drowned again, and everyone waited. That was the arrangement. That had always been the arrangement.

But the storm season changed the arithmetic. When the equinox gales came up from the south and stacked the water against the headland, the flats stopped draining entirely. The channel stayed full for days, then weeks. The bridge became a rumor under gray water, its planking visible only in the rare slack hours, dark and streaming, too warped to trust. Sometime in November, every year, the narrows shut — and Juniper Point became an island of two hundred souls until spring.

This year it shut early.

She knew it before she saw it. She knew it from the sound: the water through the pilings had a different voice that morning, deeper, unhurried, the voice of a channel that no longer intended to give anything back. She walked down anyway, Noor's hand in hers, and stood at the end of the planks where the sea came to within four feet of the last board and stayed there, breathing.

"Is it closed?" Noor asked. She was seven. She knew the answer; she wanted it said aloud by someone whose word held.

"It's closed," Wren said.

"For how long?"

"Until the storms are done."

Noor considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything now, a gravity she hadn't had before her father died, as if she'd learned that grown people's answers could break if you looked at them too hard. "Then Elias can't go to a doctor."

"No," Wren said. "He can't."

They walked back up the path, and Wren felt it begin — the thing she would carry all winter along with the packet of letters sewn into her coat lining. Not fear exactly. Fear was for moments. This was slower and more total: the sensation of walls being built around her while she stood still, each wall made of water, and behind the last wall, on the mainland, men who wanted what she carried to stop existing.

* * *

Elias was worse. She'd known he would be when she saw the color of him that morning — a gray-yellow she remembered from Toma's last winter, from the hospital car that had come out once and never come again. The fisherman lay on the cot she'd made up in the lamp room's lower chamber because the stairs were easier to heat than the whole house, propped on pillows, his ruined chest rising in shallow pulls.

He watched her come in the way a man watches weather.

"You went to the bridge," he said. His voice was gravel poured slow.

"This morning. It's gone under. It won't open again before March, maybe April."

He closed his eyes. For a long moment she thought he might simply let go then, slide out on that sentence, and part of her — the part she didn't forgive herself for — wished he would, because a dead man's letters were only paper and grief, while a living man's letters were a conspiracy.

"That's why Crane does his work in winter," Elias said. "The bridge. Whatever happens out here stays out here till spring. By then the manifests are signed, the insurance is paid, and the dead are just dead." He turned his head with effort. "You should have burned them, Mrs. Nakamura."

"I know what you told me."

"And you didn't."

"And I didn't."

He studied her. There was something in his face beyond pain — a kind of bitter relief, she thought, at not being the only one carrying it anymore. He'd been carrying it alone since the *Merrow*, since the night the boat he should have been on went down with six men and the papers called it weather.

"Toma tried to take them across," Elias said. "You know that."

The room went very quiet. Below them, the lamp mechanism ticked through its rotation, patient as a heart.

"He never told me," Wren said.

"Men don't." A cough took him, long and wet, and she held the cup to his mouth and hated how gently her hands did it. When it passed he said, "He asked me to go with him. I said wait for calm weather. There's always calm weather after, if you live to see it." His mouth twisted. "I'm a coward who outlived a brave man. Now look at us. Both stuck on this rock with the same papers and no way off it."

"The papers stay hidden," Wren said. "That's all. Nobody knows they exist except you and me."

"And whoever killed your husband."

She didn't answer that. She trimmed the wick instead, though it needed nothing, because her hands required occupation or they would shake.

* * *

That afternoon she made her inventory.

It was the ritual she fell into whenever the world grew large and unmanageable — the counting of small things, the measuring of what could actually be held. Her mother had done it during the war years, walking the house with a pencil stub, saying names aloud like a prayer: flour, oil, salt, candles. What you could name, you could keep. What you could keep, you could survive on.

She stood in the store room off the kitchen with her ledger and worked down the shelves. Lamp oil: forty-one gallons, enough for eleven weeks at winter burn, twelve if she rationed the harbor-side light darker, which she would not do. Coal: a ton and a half in the shed. Flour, beans, salt pork, the last of Toma's potato crop in its sand box. Matches — she counted the boxes twice, an old superstition, matches being the difference between light and its absence. Noor's shoes: one pair fitting, one pair nearly not, one pair in reserve three sizes up, because you planned past the season or you didn't plan at all.

What she did not write in the ledger: one packet of letters, nineteen pages, wrapped in oilcloth, currently inside the false bottom of the flour bin and due to move again tonight, because a thing that stayed anywhere longer than three nights was a thing that could be found.

And beneath that, uncounted: one logbook, her husband's, recovered from the wreck flotsam in October and not yet read past its first pages because she was a coward about some things too.

She put the ledger away and stood in the cold store room listening to the house creak against the wind, and thought: *I am sealed in.* Two hundred neighbors. One road, drowned. One cannery owner whose foreman had already begun asking questions at the dock, Marta said. And somewhere in that circle of water and timber, the proof that Aldous Crane had sent men to their deaths for insurance money — including, quite possibly, the proof of how her own husband had died, and the unbearable possibility that he had died knowing things he never told her.

The walls of the store room felt closer than they were.

She went out into the wind deliberately, stood on the gallery walk above the light, and let the gale push at her until the claustrophobia broke apart into something cleaner. The horizon was a wall of slate. The sea came at the point like a debt collector. But wind, at least, moved. Water, at least, went somewhere.

Papers in a drawer went nowhere at all.

* * *

Marta came up the path at dusk with a jar of rendered fish oil and a face that pretended it was a social call. Marta's face had many settings and Wren knew them all; this one meant the children had been left with her sister and there were things to say that couldn't be said across a fence.

They sat in the kitchen. Noor was in the parlor corner doing sums on a slate, close enough to be within sight, far enough — Wren hoped — to be outside earshot, though she had learned to underestimate that at her peril.

"The dock was busy today," Marta said, low. "Halvard had men going through every dory that came in. Every crate, every hold. He told the fishermen it was about smuggled tobacco." She turned the jar of oil in her hands without opening it. "Nobody believed that. Halvard doesn't care about tobacco."

"They're looking for something."

"They're looking for *papers*. That's the word I heard. Papers missing from the office." Marta's eyes came up. "Wren. Tell me you don't—"

"Don't what?"

"Don't make me finish the question."

Wren got up and put another stick in the stove, mostly to have her back turned. The firelight moved on the wall. Through the window the first flurries of the night's snow were coming down sideways, and beyond them, invisible, the narrows roared over the buried bridge.

"There's a man dying in my lamp room," Wren said at last, "who pulled himself out of the sea after the *Merrow* went down. You want me to tell you what he gave me. And if I tell you, then you know, and if you know, then your children know danger, and I won't do that to you."

Marta was silent so long that the stove ticked and shifted.

"You think I don't already know danger?" she said finally. "Owe Crane sixty dollars, do I. My brother owes ninety. The Halls owe two hundred between them. Half this point owes its winter to that man, and every one of us has done the arithmetic you're doing — what's a truth worth against six months of wages?" She set the jar down hard. "I'm not asking you what he gave you. I'm telling you what happens if Halvard finds it. He won't go to the magistrate. There's no magistrate till spring either. He'll come at night with two friends and a boat, and whatever he takes will be taken, and whatever happens after will happen, and nobody ashore will hear a word of it until the ice goes out. That's what the bridge is, Wren. It isn't a road. It's a lid."

"Then help me think instead of scaring me."

"I am thinking. Here's my thinking: burn it. Burn it tonight and let the sea keep its secrets, because the sea keeps them better than we do." Marta's voice cracked on the last of it, and Wren saw that underneath the warning was terror — terror for Wren, for Noor, for the whole fragile economy of survival they'd all built on top of Crane's money. "Spring will come. Spring always comes. Be alive in it."

"And the next boat that sinks with a falsified report? The next widow who gets 'lost in weather' for insurance money? Spring comes for her too, Marta, and she'll stand exactly where I'm standing with exactly nothing."

"That's not your weight."

"It became my weight the minute Elias washed up under my light."

From the parlor, the small scratch of Noor's slate had stopped. Wren caught her daughter's eye in the doorway — watchful, too watchful — and Noor bent back to her sums with elaborate innocence, and Wren filed the moment away in the place where she kept all her fears about what children absorb.

Marta left after dark, refusing escort down the cliff path. At the door she paused, snow gathering on her shoulders.

"If you won't burn them," she said, "at least move them every few days. Halvard's not clever, but he's patient, and patience is worse." Then she was gone into the whiteness, and the door shut, and Wren stood with her back against it feeling the house around her like a fist.

* * *

Night. The storm arrived in full, the season announcing itself, and Wren climbed the tower to check the light because the storm was precisely when the light mattered and also precisely when she least wanted to leave the warm floor.

The lamp burned steady in its great glass belly. Beyond the lens the world was chaos — rain turning to snow, spume torn off the crests below, the narrows a white roar. From the gallery rail she could just make out, when the clouds tore, the black line of the bridge's far end vanishing into water that had swallowed the middle entirely.

She thought about lids. About islands. About the fact that in the morning she would wake to the same four rooms, the same two miles of shore, the same faces, and that every one of those faces might include, by spring, an empty chair — and that the only thing standing between Crane and another quiet winter of drowning men was nineteen pages of paper hidden in a flour bin on a rock at the edge of the map.

Her coat hung by the lamp room door. Inside its lining, flat against the wool where she'd sewn the pocket herself that afternoon, the oilcloth packet rode silently. She'd moved it from the flour bin at dusk, as promised. Tomorrow it would go into the lens housing's maintenance gap, or under the coal, or nowhere good enough, because there was nowhere good enough. That was the discovery of the day, arriving cold and complete: there is no safe place on an island. There is only moving, and hiding, and waiting for the water to drop.

Except the water would not drop. Not for five months.

She heard a sound below — the lower chamber door, the shuffle of slippers, and then Noor's voice floating up the stairwell, small and sleep-thick: "Mama? Is Elias going to die like Papa?"

Wren closed her eyes against the lamplight.

"Not like Papa," she said, and started down the stairs toward her daughter, and toward the lie or the truth — she honestly did not yet know which one she was about to choose.
