## 5. What the Neighbors Owe

Wren counted debts the way she counted oil: by the light they gave off and the dark they left behind.

She walked into the village on the morning after the third storm, boots sinking in the churned mud of the coast road, Noor left with a biscuit and instructions to mind the lamp log — not that the girl needed instructions. The village of Juniper Hollow sat below the headland like something the tide had left behind and hadn't yet decided to reclaim. Forty-one houses, thirty-two occupied since the season began. Wren knew each one the way she knew the steps to the lamp room, by number and by need.

The Okafor house first, because it was closest and because Ruth Okafor had waved from the window, one hand pressed to the glass. Inside, the stove was lit but low, and Ruth's youngest was turning a single dried fish over the coals as if watching it long enough might make two.

"You're well?" Wren asked, though she could see the answer in the pantry door standing open, in the shelves Ruth didn't try to hide anymore.

"Well enough." Ruth smiled the way people smiled when smiling cost them. "Crane's tally man came Tuesday. Sat right there at my table and wrote figures while I made him tea. Eleven dollars for the winter's flour. Nine for the oil he sold us himself at his own price." She wiped her hands on her apron, an old gesture with nothing clean to accomplish. "He says come spring, the boats go out and we work it off. He always says come spring."

"He'll pay fair wages this year," Wren said, and heard the lie sit flat between them, unclaimed by either of them.

Ruth laughed softly. "Wren Nakamura, you keep a light for strangers on the water and you can't keep one lit for your own neighbors." But there was no bite in it. There never was. "Go on. I know you've got your rounds."

At the Lindqvist place, the situation was worse and better hidden. Anselm Lindqvist had been a proud man once, before the *Maren* went down with his brother aboard and Crane's company advanced him against the insurance settlement that never came. Now the pride showed only in how carefully the yard was swept, how the mended nets hung in perfect rows even though there'd be no boats going out under them until the bridge reopened.

"Wren." He nodded from the fence, not opening the gate. Some men couldn't be seen receiving charity; she respected the arithmetic of that. "The keeper walks the village. What's she looking for?"

"Nothing. Checking on folk."

"We're all checking on each other. It's what's left to do." He glanced past her, up the coast road toward the cannery, where the chimney stood against the gray sky like a finger raised in warning. "You hear anything from across the water?"

"The bridge is closed. Nothing crosses till spring."

"That's what I heard too." His voice did something complicated on the last word, and Wren understood, suddenly and completely, that Anselm Lindqvist had already done his own arithmetic about the closed bridge, about wages owed, about what could be said safely inside those numbers and what could not. "Mind yourself, Keeper. It's a long winter when a man owes money to the only man with a boat."

She walked on. The Vasquez boys were mending a roof, their mother below holding the ladder, and all three of them straightened when Wren passed — the particular straightening of people who wanted to seem busy and solvent. At the Halloran cottage, the curtains stayed drawn. Old Mrs. Halloran had buried a son in Crane's service and taken the company's settlement money rather than the company's silence, which made her, in the village's quiet ledger, either a saint or a fool depending on who told it.

By the time Wren reached the harbor side of the village, she had stopped pretending to herself that she was doing anything but counting.

Thirty-two households. Nineteen of them owed Crane money — not abstract debt, not the polite kind that lived in banks across the water, but the immediate, breathing kind: flour advanced against future wages, oil bought on credit at prices set by the seller, boat repairs paid for by the company with liens filed neat as stitches. Nineteen families whose winters ended when Crane said they ended, whose boats sailed when his tally men tapped their ledgers, whose children ate according to the generosity of a man none of them had ever seen laugh.

The letters sat against Wren's ribs like a coal sewn into her coat.

*Half the village,* she thought, climbing the path toward Marta's gate. *I carry papers that would burn half the village's bread out of their mouths.*

* * *

Marta Reyes met her at the door with flour on her hands and a look on her face that meant she'd been waiting.

"Don't stand in the cold," Marta said, pulling her inside. The Reyes house was warm in the particular fierce way of a woman who fed people when she was frightened. Bread rising under cloth, a pot of something with beans in it already exhaling steam, and Marta herself moving through it all like weather. "Sit. Eat. You look like you've been walking the whole coastline."

"I've been walking the village."

"Same thing, in this weather." Marta set bread and butter in front of her and then, instead of sitting, stood at the window with her back to the room. That was wrong. Marta Reyes looked at people when she talked to them; it was her weapon and her flaw. "Nineteen houses," she said to the glass. "That's what you counted. I counted twenty before you got out of bed."

"Twenty?"

"The Petrossians. They owe for the boy's doctoring last year. I forgot them because I wanted to." She turned around. Her face was doing the thing Wren had loved about her for fifteen years and feared about her for exactly six days: showing everything. "Sit down, Wren."

"I'm sitting."

"*Listen*, then." Marta came and sat across from her, took both her hands, and held them hard enough that the flour transferred between them like a treaty being signed. "Elias Brandt washed up under your light four nights ago. Everyone knows it. Halvard's men came asking yesterday whether you'd buried him or sheltered him, and Tomás told them we hadn't seen him, and now I'm lying to Crane's foreman before breakfast, which is a thing I have never once done in my life."

Wren kept her face still. "Tomás has a good heart."

"Tomás has a good heart and bad judgment and he's mine, so I'll worry about him. My point—" Marta's grip tightened— "my point is that whatever Elias gave you, whatever he said with his last dry breath, you need to put it in the stove. Right now. This minute, before you walk home."

"Marta—"

"No. Hear me first." And here was the terrible thing: Marta's eyes were wet, and Marta Reyes did not cry in front of anyone, not at her father's funeral, not when the *Cormorant* took her cousin. "Twenty houses, Wren. Twenty families who eat because Aldous Crane advances them flour against a spring fishing season that may or may not exist. You burn those papers, and Crane keeps paying out his credit and his contempt, and everybody's children eat thin but they *eat*. You don't burn them — you carry them to some mainland board of men in good coats — and what happens? Nothing happens fast. Men like Crane don't fall; they settle, they reorganize, they wait out the weather like everyone else on this coast. But the ledgers close *immediately*. The credit dries *immediately*. Halvard comes through this village within the week asking questions, and every family that owes him finds out exactly what their loyalty costs when it isn't for sale."

"Marta, what's in those papers—"

"I don't want to know!" It came out cracked, half a shout, and Marta dropped her voice instantly, glancing at the ceiling where the children slept. "Don't tell me. Do you understand? The moment I know, I'm a witness, and witnesses on this island have short winters." She pressed her palms flat to the table, steadying herself or the table or the world. "I've carried your secrets for fifteen years. I carried you through Toma's funeral with my own arms when your legs wouldn't hold. I am telling you, as the person who loves you most on this godforsaken rock, that some truths are heavier than the people carrying them. Burn them. Burn them and let me make you bread and let us both live to be old women arguing about whose biscuits are better."

The fire ticked in the stove. Somewhere above them a child turned over in sleep, the small thump of it coming through the ceiling like a heartbeat.

"Marta," Wren said quietly, "one of those letters names Toma's boat."

The words landed in the warm kitchen and changed its temperature entirely. Marta went still — not calm stillness but the stillness of prey, of a woman listening for how close the danger was.

"What does that mean," she said. Not a question.

"It means the night Toma went out, someone had already written down what was going to happen to him. It means his drowning might not have been weather." Wren heard her own voice from far away, level and precise, the voice she used for logging storms. "It means I have spent three years grieving an accident, and I might be holding proof that grief was misdirected."

For a long moment Marta didn't move. Then she released Wren's hands, stood, and walked to the stove. She stood there with her back turned, one hand on the iron, and Wren watched her friend's shoulders rise and fall twice, three times, the way a person breathes when they are deciding something enormous and hate every part of it.

"If I help you keep them," Marta said finally, still facing the stove, "and Crane learns it, my children don't eat either. You understand that. Tomás works his dock. Our debt is eleven dollars smaller than Ruth's and no less real."

"I know."

"And I'm going to help you anyway, aren't I. Because I know myself, and I know that if I let you burn them I'll spend the rest of my life wondering what my silence cooked into the bread I fed my babies." She turned around, and her face was terrible and steady, scrubbed of tears, resolved into something harder than grief. "So here's what happens. Nobody knows. Not Ruth, not Anselm, not Pell, not Tomás. You don't come here again for weeks. If I hear Halvard's men are searching houses, I send my boy running to the light with a basket of fish and you read the warning in how many fish there are — odd number, you run; even, you stay. We don't speak of this again until the ice breaks. Swear it."

"I swear."

"Swear it properly, Wren. Like you mean it. Like Toma's name means something."

Wren swallowed. "On Toma's name. On Noor's life. Nobody knows."

Marta nodded once, sharp, business concluded — and then crossed the room and pulled Wren into her arms, and for a moment neither of them was a keeper or a conspirator, just two women holding on in a warm kitchen while the wind found the gaps in the walls and tested them, patiently, the way it tested everything on this coast.

"Take the bread," Marta said into her shoulder. "And Wren — if it comes to choosing between the papers and Noor—"

"It won't."

"Promise me anyway."

But Wren said nothing, because outside the window, far up the coast road, a figure in a company coat had stopped at the top of Marta's lane and stood looking down at the village — at the roofs, at the harbor, at the smoke rising from forty-one chimneys he could extinguish with a word.

Halvard stood there a long moment. Then he turned, unhurried, and walked on toward the lighthouse.

Toward home.
