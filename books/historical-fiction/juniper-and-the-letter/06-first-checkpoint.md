## 6. First Checkpoint

The dory slid out of the boathouse before dawn, and Wren let it.

That was the trick of it — to look like a woman doing nothing worth watching. She had spent two days making herself unremarkable. She mended nets in full view of the harbor. She carried her fish barrels down to the tideline at the usual hours. She let old Mrs. Tegg count her herring and complain about the price, twice, loudly, so the whole village would know Wren Nakamura's winter was shaping up to be as thin as everyone else's.

A woman with a thin winter did not carry papers worth killing for.

The packet sat where it had sat since the night Elias died: wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining of the cushion she knelt on at the tiller. Six inches of wool and burlap between the letters and the sea, and if it came to it, between the letters and Halvard's hands. She had tested the seam until her fingers ached. Pressed hard, it read as nothing. A lumpy cushion. A poor woman's comfort.

Noor had helped her stitch it, not knowing what she stitched. That was the part Wren kept turning over like a stone in her pocket — that she had used her daughter's neat small stitches as camouflage, and Noor had hummed while she worked, pleased to be useful.

*Later,* Wren told herself, the way she told herself everything now. *Later you can be honest with her. Later, if there is one.*

The cannery dock lay two miles south, around the shoulder of Juniper Point, and every outbound boat on this stretch of coast had to pass it. That was new. That was Crane's answer to a storm season he seemed to have expected — a checkpoint dressed up as bookkeeping. A notice had gone up on the village board in Halvard's blocky hand: ALL VESSELS DEPARTING FOR MAINLAND MUST REPORT TO CANNERY DOCK FOR CARGO INSPECTION. BY ORDER OF THE OWNER, IN THE INTEREST OF SAFETY.

*In the interest of safety.* Wren had read it four times, looking for the joke. There wasn't one. Crane owned the cannery, the dock, the company store, the wages of half the families on the point, and now, apparently, the tide itself.

She was going because she had to be seen going. Marta had said it plain, two nights ago, over the last of the coffee: "If you never show your face at that dock, he'll know there's something under your floorboards. Go. Buy flour you don't need. Let him look at you being ordinary."

So she went to buy flour she didn't need.

The fog was doing its winter work when she rounded the point, thick enough that the cannery came to her in pieces — first the smell, always, the sweet-rot stink of brine and oil and fish blood soaked into wood for forty years; then the red silhouette of the drying racks; then the dock itself, strung with lanterns against the murk, and the shapes of men moving along it slow and deliberate as ants who have found something to dismantle.

Halvard had set up at the narrowest part of the planking. He'd built a kind of gate out of sawhorses and rope, and behind it a table, and on the table a ledger, a lantern, and a short gaff hook whose purpose nobody was invited to ask about. Two of Crane's men flanked him, big ones, the kind Crane hired from the mainland in autumn and never asked questions about in spring. Beyond them, three fishing boats already sat searched, their gear pulled apart and piled on the deck in wet tangles, their skippers standing by with their hats in their hands.

Wren counted heads. Twelve men waiting in line ahead of her, stamping their boots against the cold, not talking. That was the thing that made her skin prickle. Fishermen waiting in a queue will talk about anything — prices, weather, each other's wives. This silence had weight. This silence was fear wearing work clothes.

She brought the dory alongside the lower steps and made fast, and took her time about it. Her hands were steady. She noticed them being steady and was grateful, the way you're grateful for a wall when the ground shakes.

"Morning, Mrs. Nakamura." Halvard didn't look up from the ledger. His voice was flat as slate, neither friendly nor unfriendly, which was somehow worse than either. "You're not on the outbound list."

"I'm buying flour." She climbed the steps. "Unless the company's rationing that too."

Someone down the line breathed something that might have been a laugh, quickly swallowed.

Halvard looked up then. He had a face built for doorways — wide, blunt, hard to get past — and eyes that did none of the work of the face. The eyes just measured. She felt them go over her the way they'd go over a crate: weight, dimensions, likelihood of concealing contraband.

"Flour." He said it like he was entering it in evidence. "Company store's open. You can buy your flour after."

"After?"

"After inspection." He tipped his chin toward the boats. "Everyone goes through. Everyone and everything. New policy."

"I'm not cargo, Mr. Halvard. I'm a customer."

"Everything's cargo." And there it was — not a threat, exactly, but a statement of the world as he understood it, which amounted to the same thing. He gestured her toward the gate. "Bag and person both."

*Person.* The word dropped through her stomach like a stone through cold water.

She had planned for a search of the dory. She had planned for questions about where she was bound. She had not planned for his hands on her coat, and the cushion stayed in the boat, and God — God and Toma and whoever else kept books on such things — the cushion stayed in the boat. It would take him thirty seconds to lift it out and split the lining with that gaff hook, and everything would end here on a fish-smelling dock in the fog, and Noor would grow up visiting her mother wherever they sent women who carried dead men's letters.

She kept her face doing what faces do. Bored. Cold. A widow with errands.

The line moved. Halvard worked methodically, without hurry or malice, which made it more frightening, because malice you could predict and this you couldn't. He opened flour sacks and pushed a rod down through the meal. He pulled up floorboards in the sloop *Gannet* while her owner watched with his jaw working and no words coming out. He found nothing on anyone, and the finding-nothing seemed not to disappoint him but to confirm some private arithmetic. Whatever he wanted, today's haul hadn't produced it.

Then a boat came through the fog that changed the temperature of the whole dock.

It was one of Crane's own tenders, back early from the mainland run, and the man steering it wasn't a fisherman. He wore a city coat. He came up the steps shaking fog off his shoulders, nodded to Halvard like a foreman to an engine, and handed him a folded paper without a word passing between them.

Halvard read it. Wren stood eight feet away, next in line, invisible in the way women become on working docks, and she watched his face while he read. Nothing moved in it. But his thumb pressed the paper flat, and kept pressing, as if the information could be squeezed out through sheer pressure.

He looked up. Not at her. At the water. At the line of boats. Then, briefly, at the point — north, toward Juniper Light, though she couldn't have sworn to it under oath.

"That all?" he said.

"That's all," said the man in the city coat, and went up the plank path toward the cannery offices, and the fog closed behind him like a curtain.

Wren's heart was doing something arrhythmic and stupid. She made herself breathe the way she breathed when the lamp mechanism jammed in a gale: slow, counted, hands still. *Panic later. Panic has a schedule. It is not now.*

"Next," Halvard said.

She stepped through the gate.

He didn't search her. That was the first wrong thing. He glanced at her once, waved the second man past her toward the dory, and stood there with the ledger open while the other man went down the steps — and Wren understood, with a clarity that arrived cold and complete, that the dory search was theater. For her benefit. So she'd see it done and believe herself safe.

The man turned the dory over thoroughly. He lifted the thwart. He ran his hand along the keel. He picked up the stern cushions —

— and Wren's whole life narrowed to the space between his fingers and the seam.

He weighed the kneeling cushion in one hand. Set it back down. Picked up the other. Set it down. He was looking for something with edges. Something boxed. Something you could feel through any wrapping if you knew to press.

The cushion went back on the boards, lump and all, and the man climbed up and shook his head at Halvard.

"Clean," he said.

"Clean," Halvard agreed, and wrote something in the ledger, and looked at Wren with those measuring eyes, and for a moment she thought — this is it, he knows, the whole performance was to see what my face did while they handled my boat —

But all he said was: "Flour's fifty cents the sack this month. Company policy."

"Fifty." Last month it had been thirty-five. "Crane's generous."

"Crane carries this town through the winter," Halvard said, without heat, closing the ledger. "Man might remember that." He paused. The fog dripped off the lantern above them, ticking like a clock. "You keep to the light, Mrs. Nakamura. Out here past the point, alone with the girl. Must be things you see. Boats that come through. Papers that wash up."

The dock went very quiet, or she imagined it did.

"Papers," she said. Her voice came out level, and she marveled at it from somewhere outside herself.

"Wreckage." He shrugged, a small economical movement. "Storm season. Things drift ashore at the light. If you find anything — documents, mailbags, ship's records — company policy is they come to the cannery office. Salvage law. Crane handles the claims." He met her eyes, and for the first time that morning something almost human crossed his face, and it was worse than the measuring. It was reluctance. "You'd be compensated."

"I'll keep it in mind."

"Do more than keep it in mind." The reluctance was gone again, shuttered away, and the slate voice came back. "There's a specific item. A packet. Letters, tied in oilcloth. Could be oilskin. Man-sized fist, maybe this long." His hands sketched a shape in the air, six inches by four, and Wren's knees understood the dimensions before her mind allowed itself to. "Last boat out of the channel before the storm. Went down with all hands, official record says, and whatever it was carrying went down with it. Except paper floats, Mrs. Nakamura. Paper floats a long way."

"Does it," she said.

"It does. And Mr. Crane wants it back for the families. Sentimental reasons." He held her gaze one beat longer than the sentence needed. Two beats. Three. "Anything washing up around your light, you bring it to me personally. Not the harbor master. Not the mail boat. Me."

"I mostly find herring and old boots."

"Mostly," Halvard agreed. He stepped aside and unlatched the rope gate. "Go buy your flour."

She walked the plank path to the company store on legs that belonged to someone else. Inside, the shelves were half empty and the prices chalked high, and Marta's brother-in-law waited on her with red hands and tired eyes, and she bought her unnecessary sack of flour and paid fifty cents for it and did not let her hands shake until she was outside again, alone, with the fog pressing in.

Fifty cents. A warning price. The whole transaction had been a message written in grocery figures: *we can squeeze this town however we like, and everyone will still line up.*

They knew about the packet. They knew its size, its wrapping, roughly. Which meant Elias had told someone before he died — or Crane had known what Elias carried before the storm ever took his boat, and had been hunting it since. Either way, the hunt had a shape now, and the shape was Halvard's hands sketching six inches by four in the fog.

And he had looked north. Toward the light.

Wren stood at the lower steps and made herself untie the painter with ordinary movements, an ordinary woman, going home. The cushion sat in the stern where the searcher had left it, innocent as bread. Fifty yards out, the fog swallowed the dock behind her, and only then did she let her breath come ragged.

He had said *bring it to me personally.* He had said *not the harbor master.*

Not Pell. Which meant Pell was already suspect — or already bought, or already afraid — and the one man on this coast with authority independent of Crane had just been cut out of the circuit like a bad splice.

She rowed north with the flood tide, and the current fought her, and she let it, because fighting gave her arms something to do besides tremble. Juniper Light rose out of the fog by degrees, gray stone, white gallery, Noor a small figure on the gallery walk waving with one arm and holding the rail with the other, and Wren looked up at her daughter and felt the fear rearrange itself, as it always did, into something harder and more usable.

They weren't searching blindly anymore. They were searching for a packet, oilskin-wrapped, six inches by four — and they had told her so to her face, politely, with a reward attached, which meant the asking was over soon. Next would come the not-asking.

The dory grounded on the shingle. Noor came clattering down the stairs in her too-big boots to take the bowline, chattering about the fog, about Mrs. Tegg's dog, about nothing, everything.

"Mama, you're white as the tower."

"Long row." Wren lifted out the flour, then the cushion, and stood holding it a moment too long. "Noor. Go start the stove, sweetheart. I want to check the lamp room before the weather turns."

Her daughter ran up the stairs. Wren stood on the shingle with the packet in her arms and the fog dripping off the rocks, and understood that the hiding place was spent. They had touched the cushion now. They would touch it again.

She looked up at the tower — at the great glass lens turning inside it, patient and blind, the one thing on this point no man would ever think to open — and began, slowly, to climb.

* * *

That night, long after Noor slept, Wren sat at the kitchen table with Toma's logbook open under the lamp, reading it for the fourth time since Elias washed ashore — and stopped, this time, at an entry she had read past before because grief had made her skim.

October 12th. Her husband's hand, cramped and hurried, the pencil pressed hard enough to tear the page:

*Found discrepancies in the wreck manifests. Crane's losses don't match the board's records. Three crews listed lost that came home through Pel. Going to the mainland when the weather lifts. If anything happens to me, the proof is with E.*

*The proof is with E.*

Elias. It had been with Elias all along — and Toma had known, and had been three days dead before she ever pulled the man from the rocks, and the two halves of her winter clicked together with a sound she would hear for the rest of her life.

Wren closed the logbook. Upstairs, the lamp turned. Outside, the wind was rising again from the southeast, and somewhere south of the point, Halvard was writing names in a ledger.

Hers, she had no doubt, was already in it.
