## 7. Her Husband's Logbook

The logbook came back to her the way drowned things come back — sideways, on an ugly tide, when she had stopped watching for it.

Marta brought it. That was the strange part. Wren had spent three years believing the sea had kept everything of Toma's, and then one gray morning in late December Marta climbed the lighthouse stairs with a bundle under her arm wrapped in oilcloth, and her face was doing something complicated.

"Halvard's men dragged the cove after the wreck," Marta said. "Salvage. They were pulling up anything with the cannery's brand on it — nets, dory planking, that sort of thing. One of the boys found this jammed under a rock shelf, wedged so tight they nearly left it." She held out the bundle without quite meeting Wren's eyes. "It's his. I'd know the handwriting anywhere, God help me, from all those notes he used to leave on my kitchen counter when you two were courting."

Wren took it and did not open it. The oilcloth was stiff with salt. It weighed almost nothing and it weighed more than the lamp.

"You could burn it," Marta said quietly. "Some widows would."

"Some widows are smarter than I am."

Marta stayed for tea she didn't drink, talked about her sister in Pelton Sound and the price of flour and whether the ice on the cistern had cracked the boards, all of it the ordinary music of a woman filling a silence she didn't want to leave alone. Then she went down the stairs and Wren was alone with the bundle and the wind, and Noor was at school across the point with the Hendersons' girls, and there was nothing in the world left to do but open it.

* * *

She did not open it at first. She made herself useful. She trimmed the wick. She counted the oil remaining and wrote the figure in her own ledger with a hand that shook only slightly. She ate half a piece of bread standing at the window looking at the water where her husband had died, and then, because she was who she was — a woman who inventoried everything, who believed that any burden could be carried if it was divided into small enough pieces — she sat down at the table and unwrapped the oilcloth as if it were a delivery of matches.

Toma's logbook. Same make as hers, the canvas-bound kind sold at the chandlery on the mainland, the spine broken in the same place his always broke because he opened it flat and pressed too hard with his pencil. His name was stamped inside the cover in fading gold: TOMA NAKAMURA. Below it, in his square, patient hand, the boat: *Kestrel*. The date he'd registered her. The date the registration lapsed, because the sea had taken her, and the man who wrote in her had gone with her, or near enough.

Wren turned pages. She read the way you walk through a house you once lived in — knowing every room before you reach it.

Fishing entries. Weather entries. The little marginal poems of a working sailor: *gulls mean bait, not weather. Halvard shorted us on twine again. Noor lost a tooth, wants it buried in the garden so a new one grows like wheat.* Wren had forgotten that. Or she had not forgotten it; she had put it somewhere it couldn't reach her, and now here it was reaching her anyway, in her dead husband's handwriting, about their daughter's tooth.

She read for an hour. Then, forty pages from the end, the handwriting changed.

Not much. A stranger would never have seen it. But Wren had read ten thousand pages of Toma Nakamura, and she knew what it meant when his letters got smaller and closer together. It meant he was writing fast. It meant he was afraid.

*Oct. 9.* Crane called a meeting of the skippers. Says losses are up, insurance is the difference between winter wages and no wages. Fine. But Pell showed me the manifest for the *Adelie*, lost in March. Four crew, the manifest says. I fished beside the *Adelie* my whole life and she never carried more than three. And the cargo listed — 900 cases? She was a small boat. You could not have stacked 900 cases on her deck if you tried.*

Wren stopped. Put the book face-down on the table like something that might bite. Got up, walked to the window, walked back.

*Oct. 11.* Asked Pell about it, careful-like. He went white as gull-belly and told me to mind my lamp. That's an answer. A man with clean books says what do you mean. A man with dirty ones says get out.*

*Oct. 14.* Checked against the wreck reports filed with the mainland office. The *Cormorant*, lost '31. Report says six souls, full cargo. I KNEW THE CORMORANT. She went down with two men aboard and half-empty, and both of them got off alive — Joe Ferro is living in Pelton Sound right now, I'd swear it on anything. Six souls on paper. Insurance paid on six.*

There it was. Laid out in her husband's cramped, hurried hand, dated fourteen months before Elias ever washed up below her light: the same fraud, the same arithmetic of dead men who weren't dead and boats sunk on purpose. Crane had been sinking his own ships and inventing his own corpses, collecting on crews that existed only in manifests, and the money had built the cannery wing that employed half the village, and the wages that fed them had come out of policies written on phantom drownings.

And one real drowning. Wren sat very still. The room ticked with cooling metal. Outside, the sea did its endless dull work against the point.

She turned the page.

*Oct. 16.* Copied everything. Manifests, reports, dates, amounts. Made two sets. One stays hidden. One goes with me to the mainland office of the Marine Board when the bridge opens in spring. I know what this costs. Half the village eats because of Crane. But he is eating because of us, and some of what he is eating is people.*

Two sets. Two sets. Her hands were flat on the table as if steadying it. Elias had come to her with a packet — one set, presumably, the set Toma had hidden and Elias had found or been given, the set that had cost Elias his boat and nearly his life. Where was the other? Had it gone down in the *Kestrel*? Had Crane found it?

But none of that was the worst of it. The worst of it was the next entry, dated the twenty-second of October, eleven days before Toma died.

*Crane ordered the Kestrel out Thursday. Storm coming — everyone on the water can feel it, barometer falling since Monday, and old Pell said as much at the dock and got laughed at. Crane says the herring run won't wait and neither will the contract. Halvard gave the order with Crane standing right there on the dock looking at me. Not at the sky. At me. Like he was reading something off my face.*

*I think he knows what I've copied.*

*I'm going anyway. If I refuse he'll wonder why, and wondering leads to searching, and if he finds the papers before I get them to the mainland it was all for nothing. Better I sail the trip, come back safe, and cross the bridge in spring with everything in order. Noor has shoes coming for her birthday. Wren will worry but she always worries and she is always right to and somehow we are still here, both of us, still here.*

He was going to tell her. Wren understood that with a pain that had no name in any language she spoke. He had planned to sit her down at this same table — maybe this same table — after the season closed, and show her what he'd found, and say the words out loud: *Crane killed nobody yet but he's betting on funerals, and I mean to stop him.* He had planned a whole future in which the two of them carried this together, and instead the storm came early, and the *Kestrel* went out heavy-laden into weather no competent skipper would have chosen, and the official report called it an act of God.

Act of God. She almost laughed. An act of Aldous Crane, more like — a man who sent a suspecting skipper into a rising storm rather than let him reach the mainland with copies in his sea bag. She had no proof. Elias's letters proved the fraud; nothing on earth proved what she suddenly, coldly believed: that Toma's last voyage had not been greed or accident but murder wearing the weather as a mask.

But belief was enough. Belief was fuel.

* * *

She read the last entries four times.

*Oct. 30.* Wind north-northeast and building. Halvard says sail. I am sailing. Papers are hidden where only I can find them, and if I don't come back, whoever reads this — and I suppose someday someone will, logs outlive their writers — the second set is with the truth. Find it.*

That was all. The next page was blank. The page after that was blank. Three years of blankness, and then salt water had found the book anyway and carried it back to her like a dog retrieving a stick, and she did not know whether to thank the sea or curse it.

She put the logbook down and stood at the window a long time.

Here is what she had believed for three years, the belief she had built her survival on, plank by careful plank: that Toma died because the sea is the sea, because herring runs don't wait, because men go out and sometimes don't come back and there is no why worth asking. She had ground that belief smooth like a stone in her pocket. She touched it every day. It was how she got up in the morning and lit the lamp and measured out oil and mended Noor's shoes: not peace, exactly, but the absence of a question.

Now the question was back, and it had teeth.

He had known. He had known what Crane was, months before Elias ever staggered out of the surf with his packet of letters, and he had chosen — deliberately, with his eyes open, with their daughter's birthday shoes noted in the same breath — to keep it from her. To carry it alone. To be, in secret, exactly what she had become by accident: a courier of the truth toward a mainland that didn't want it.

Kinship. That was the word, and it was terrible, because kinship meant he had done the brave thing and it had killed him, and she was now walking the same road with the same man's dogs on the same trail. Halvard searching boats. Pell looking away. Crane reading faces on docks. Every mile of it already mapped in her husband's hand like a chart drawn by the first man to die on the reef.

And underneath the grief, another feeling, one she was ashamed of and could not put down: anger. Not at Crane — at Crane later; Crane would get his share. At Toma. At him, her gentle, patient husband, who had sat across from her at this table through a whole autumn, letting her count matches and oil while he carried a copy of the truth hidden somewhere within reach of her hands. She had thought they had no secrets bigger than a birthday present. He had been planning testimony the way other men plan escape, and he had let her believe the world was simple.

*You were protecting me,* she said aloud to the empty room, and heard how young it sounded, and hated it, and forgave him in the same breath, because that was marriage, even the part of it that continued past death.

* * *

Noor came home at four with cold cheeks and one glove. Wren fed her bread and canned peaches — Wednesday was peach day, an institution older than the republic as far as Noor was concerned — and watched her daughter talk about the Hendersons' dog and a fight between two boys over a slingshot and whether ice on the tide flats would hold a person this year, and thought: *he wrote about your tooth. He's in this book eight feet from your elbow and you don't know it.*

"Can I go see if the cove is frozen?" Noor asked.

"To the knee, not past. Straight back before the light."

"I know, Mama." But she lingered at the door, and Wren saw her looking at the logbook lying on the table, and saw something flicker there — the particular stillness of a child deciding not to ask. Noor had been three when Toma died. She remembered his voice the way you remember a song from someone else's humming. Wren wondered, not for the first time and not for the last, what a child owed the dead, and what the dead owed the child.

After she'd gone, Wren wrapped the logbook back in its oilcloth. Then she unwrapped it. Then she sat down and did what she should have done hours ago: she thought.

Elias's packet proved Crane falsified reports on the *Cormorant*, the *Adelie*, and others — Elias had said so, gasping, before he said much else. Toma's log proved the fraud independently, from the inside, in the hand of a man Crane had reason to want silent, dated long before Elias's wreck. Together they stopped being a fisherman's dying accusation and became corroboration. Pattern. The thing marine boards and insurers and courts require. One witness is a grievance. Two is a case.

And somewhere — *find it*, he had written, *the second set is with the truth* — somewhere there was a second set of copies, hidden by a drowning man who trusted the future to find it.

Where does a careful man hide things? Wren stood in the middle of the lighthouse and looked at it with new eyes, this tower she had lived in for six years, this tower Toma had helped her provision and repair and loved without ever quite explaining why. Under the floorboards of the storeroom — no, Halvard had pried those up during the salvage sweep in November, hunting for cannery property. In the oil room? Behind the loose brick in the fireplace she'd been meaning to fix since summer?

Or with the boat. With the *Kestrel*'s effects. With whatever the sea gave back.

Her heart was going hard now. Because there was one place, wasn't there — one place a fisherman would trust above all others, one place tied to the *Kestrel* that had come back to Juniper Point and sat, all this time, fifty feet from where she slept.

The dory. Toma's spare dory, salvaged the spring after he drowned, ransacked superficially and then left to rot under canvas in the boat shed because Wren could not bear to look at it and could not bear to sell it. He'd have known she'd keep it. He'd have counted on it.

She pulled on her coat and boots and went down the stairs two at a time with the lamp swinging wild behind her, out into the blue-black cold, across the frozen grass to the shed whose door she hadn't opened since October.

The canvas came away stiff as bark. The dory sat in the dark exactly as grief had left it, and Wren knelt on the frozen ground with her lantern raised, breathing fog, running her hands along the gunwale her husband's hands had shaped, looking for the seam, the hollow, the loose plank — anywhere a careful man would hide the truth from Aldous Crane and leave it for his wife.

Behind her, out on the water, the wind changed.
