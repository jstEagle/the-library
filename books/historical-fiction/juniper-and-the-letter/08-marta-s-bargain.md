## 8. Marta's Bargain

Marta came at dusk, the way she always did when there was something to say that couldn't survive daylight. She let herself in without knocking — the privilege of twenty years of friendship — and stood dripping in the doorway with a covered dish she had no intention of leaving.

"You're thin," she said. "Both of you."

"We eat."

"You cook like a grieving woman. Which you are, but Noor needs more than salt cod and sorrow." She set the dish on the table anyway, a stew heavy with the smell of bay leaf and the last of someone's carrots. Then she took off her wet coat, hung it on the peg as if this were her own house, and sat down across from Wren like a woman settling in for weather.

Noor was at the window seat with a book, pretending to read. Wren knew the signs of her daughter's pretending — the stillness was too complete, the page never turned. The girl had been listening for weeks now, to everything, the way children do when they sense the floor has shifted beneath a house.

"Mama," Marta said pleasantly, not looking at the child, "would you take that stew down to old Sef? He won't come up for it himself and I've been meaning all day."

It was a transparent errand. There was no stew for Sef; the dish was staying here. But Noor took her coat and went out into the dark without protest, which meant the girl understood the transaction too. That was the part that sat worst in Wren's chest — how fluent her daughter had become in the language of things unsaid.

The door closed. The wind worried the shingles. Marta waited until Noor's footsteps faded down the path before she spoke again, and when she did, her voice had changed — stripped of its banter, laid bare the way you'd strip a hull before patching it.

"Crane holds my paper."

Wren kept her hands still around her cup. "I know about the boats."

"Not just the boats." Marta's fingers found a knot in the tabletop and worked at it. "The house. Henrik's father signed with Crane eleven years ago — boat and house together, one instrument, when the cannery was new and everyone thought he was a benefactor. When Henrik died, the debt didn't die with him. It passed to me. It passes to Anni after me, if we're still here to inherit anything." She looked up. "You know what he charges. You know what winter interest does to a number."

Wren knew. Half the village knew their own version of it. But knowing the arithmetic of a thing was different from hearing it named in a friend's kitchen.

"How much?"

"Does it matter?" Marta laughed, short and ugly. "It matters. That's the trick of it. It matters so much I've spent nine years being exactly what Aldous Crane wants his debtors to be. Grateful. Quiet. At the Christmas gathering, I stand near the front and I clap." Her jaw tightened. "I have clapped for that man while he stood on the cannery steps talking about family, about community, and three rows behind me sit women whose husbands went into the water on his orders and never came back, and I clapped."

The lamp above them ticked softly as the oil moved, keeping its small faithful time. Wren thought of Elias, three days mending in her back room, fevered and muttering, alive by fractions. She thought of the packet under the floorboard, wrapped in oilcloth, patient as a seed.

"Why are you telling me this now?"

Marta didn't answer right away. She rose, crossed to the window, checked the yard — an old habit, checking for watchers — and came back looking older than her forty years.

"Because Halvard searched my fish house yesterday."

The words landed cold. "What for?"

"He didn't say. He went through my barrels. He looked under the ice. He asked Henrik's brother whether I'd taken any trips to the mainland lately, whether I'd been seen at night on the north shore." Marta sat again, heavily. "Wren. He's not searching anymore. He's narrowing."

* * *

They talked until the fire burned low, and what emerged was not friendship, exactly. Wren understood that with a clarity that hurt worse than betrayal would have. What emerged was a contract.

"I can get word where Halvard can't," Marta said. "I sell him his herring. His cook buys from me direct. I'm invisible because I'm useful and because I've been harmless for nine years and a man doesn't fear what he's clapped for." She counted it off on her fingers like manifest lines. "I can carry messages in the fish. I can tell you who comes and goes at the cannery. I can put food in your back room for a man who officially drowned, and no one will think twice about the Montrose cart going up the hill."

"And in exchange."

Marta met her eyes. In the lamplight her face was all planes and honesty, finally. "In exchange, when this is over — however it ends — you never speak my name. Not to the board, not to the newspapermen who'll come, not to Anni, not to God. If anyone asks how you got off this rock, you did it alone. You rowed. You were desperate and lucky. Marta Voss knew nothing."

"That's it? Silence?"

"That's everything." Marta's voice cracked and she mastered it. "Wren, if it comes out that I helped, Crane takes the house inside a month. Anni goes to an aunt in Tofino I can barely stand, and I go to work in his cannery at whatever wage he sets for a widow who crossed him. I watched him do it to the Kellermans. I helped pack their crates when they left." She pressed her palm flat against the table as if steadying herself against swell. "So yes. I'll help you bury him. And then you help me disappear from the story."

Wren looked at her friend — her oldest friend, the woman who had sat with her through Toma's wake, who had brought bread every third day for a year after, who had held Noor on her hip at the funeral while the wind tried to take the umbrellas — and felt something shift in the architecture of her heart. Not collapse. Settlement. A wall finding its true load.

"You could just trust me," Wren said quietly. "Twenty years, Marta. You could just ask."

"Trust is what got the Kellermans on the boat." Marta stood, gathered her wet coat, her hands unsteady now that the bargain was struck. "Trust is a fine thing in calm water. I've got two children and Crane's signature in my cupboard. So no. I don't want your trust. Trust is a feeling. I want your silence, which is a thing you can keep."

* * *

After she left, Wren climbed to the back room. Elias was awake, propped against the wall, his color better, his breathing still wet. She brought him some of Marta's stew and he ate slowly, methodically, a man relearning appetite.

"The Voss woman," he said. "She knows?"

"She knows enough. She's offered help."

Elias set down the spoon. For a long moment he studied her, and Wren realized he was doing arithmetic too — counting what she'd told, weighing what remained hidden.

"Everyone who helps you will want something," he said. "The ones who want nothing are the dangerous ones. They're the ones working some angle you haven't imagined yet." He wiped his mouth. "The letters — you've read the one with your husband's boat in it."

"Yes."

"Then you know Crane doesn't just falsify reports. He arranges outcomes. Men who find things out get sent into weather." His eyes held hers, gentle and merciless. "Your friend Marta wants silence. Fine. Pay it. Silence is cheap compared to what Crane charges. Take the deal, keeper, and stop waiting for a clean hand to reach out of this mess. There isn't one. There's only hands, and what they choose to hold."

* * *

She went up the tower afterward, more from habit than duty, and stood beside the lens while it turned its slow white arm over the black water. The packet was below her feet, and now it had a second weight: a promise of silence, owed in advance, to a woman she loved and did not entirely recognize tonight.

Trust can survive transaction, she thought. It just comes out of the water changed — salt-stiffened, smaller, real.

Below, far down the dark road toward the village, she saw a lantern moving. One light, swinging quick and purposeful, climbing the hill road toward Juniper Point. Too fast for a neighbor. Too steady for a drunk.

It was coming to her door.

She killed the stair lamp and stood in the dark of the gallery, watching the light grow.
