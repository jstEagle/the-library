## 9. The Second Checkpoint

The harbor master's office sat at the end of the long pier like a barnacle on a ship's hull — squat, salt-scoured, impossible to dislodge. Wren had walked that pier a thousand times. She had walked it as a bride with Toma's hand around hers, walked it with Noor in a sling against her chest, walked it in every weather the point could devise. But she had never walked it the way she walked it now: with nothing in her pockets and everything in her posture, which was to say, carefully arranged.

She carried no papers. The packet stayed home, wrapped in oilcloth inside the cold stove, under a layer of ash she had laid down like a blessing. Today she carried only her ration chit, a list of supplies, and the small hard knot of intention that had been growing in her chest since the bridge closed: *find out what they know.*

Because Halvard was looking for something specific. She'd seen it in his face at the cannery dock, in the way his hands went through a fish crate — not searching for contraband generally, not performing diligence for Crane's benefit, but hunting. A man who knows the shape of what he's lost.

If Halvard knew papers existed, then someone at the water knew too. And all outbound traffic, every dory and skiff and salmon boat that wanted to leave this harbor for the mainland, passed one pair of eyes first.

Mr. Pell's.

* * *

The old man was mending a net when she came up the pier, which surprised her until it didn't. Pell had been harbor master for thirty years, and in all that time she had never once seen him idle. His hands needed work the way other men needed tobacco. He didn't look up right away, and when he did, she watched something move across his face — quick as a fish under ice — before it settled into the flat professional courtesy he wore for everyone.

"Mrs. Nakamura."

"Mr. Pell."

"Cold morning for the pier."

"Every morning is a cold morning for the pier." She set her supply list on the rail beside him. "I need diesel, lamp wicks, and whatever coffee Marta hasn't hoarded."

He glanced at the list. Didn't pick it up. "Bridge being closed, you'll be wanting to order through the cannery store."

"I'll be wanting to order through you, Mr. Pell, because the cannery store charges Crane prices, and we both know you can get it off the supply tender direct."

A muscle worked in his jaw. Out past the breakwater the sea ran gray and ridged, the storm season still growling somewhere over the horizon, not done with them yet. "Tender doesn't run regular in this weather."

"It ran Tuesday. I watched it."

For a moment neither of them said anything. A gull screamed somewhere behind the cannery roofs. Pell went back to his net, pushing the shuttle through with fingers knotted like tree roots, and Wren understood that the net was an excuse, the way her own inventory ritual was an excuse — hands busy so the mind could go where it needed to.

Three years ago, in October, Noor had gone through the ice at the mill pond. Wren had been forty feet away and useless, frozen in a horror so complete it had stopped her breath, her heart, time itself. It was Toma who went in — of course it was Toma — but it was Pell who had been walking the pond road, Pell who brought the ropes and the lantern and the dry coat, Pell who held Noor upside down and smacked the water out of her until the child gasped like something newly born. He had refused thanks then. "Any man would've," he'd said, gruffly, already walking away. But Wren had seen him cry afterward, alone by his skiff, thinking no one watched.

She did not invoke that day now. You didn't spend a debt like that out loud; spending it cheapened it. You just let it sit between you, heavy and warm, and hoped it counted for something when the counting came.

"The diesel," Pell said at last. "I can manage that. Wicks I'll see about."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." He tied off the net and finally looked at her straight, and his eyes were old and tired and afraid, and that fear frightened her more than anything Halvard had done. "Mrs. Nakamura. You'll pardon an old man speaking plain?"

"You always have."

"There's a new manifest requirement. All outbound vessels, any size, any purpose — inspected at this dock before clearing the breakwater. Including tenders. Including dories." He said it evenly, like reading weather. "Including yours, if it ever were to go out."

"Is that so."

"That's so." He folded his hands on the net. "And I'll tell you something else for free, because free advice is all I've got left. There's a description going around. Paper, they're saying. Documents. Official-looking. Man named Halvard has offered a winter's wages to anyone who turns such paper in, and worse than wages to anyone found holding it back."

The wind came up the pier and cut between them. Wren kept her face still. Somewhere behind her sternum her heart was working like a pump in a flood.

"A winter's wages," she said. "Crane's generous this year."

"Crane is scared," Pell said quietly. "First time in thirty years. Mark what that does to a man like him, and plan your winter accordingly."

* * *

She loaded what she'd come for two days later, when the supply tender made its irregular call, and if her dory rode a little low with diesel tins and a bolt of flannel and three sacks of flour, well, that was winter provisioning, same as any widow's. She rowed out from the lighthouse landing in a thin bright cold, Noor left with Marta on the pretext of bread-baking, and steered for the inspection float below Pell's office.

Her stomach was steady and her hands were steady and both of those things surprised her, because there was nothing in the boat. Nothing but diesel and flour and a woman whose guilt had weight anyway, who felt the absent letters pressing against her ribs like a second heartbeat, who had spent half the night rehearsing innocence and the other half despising herself for needing rehearsal.

Pell stood on the float with his ledger book and a clipboard of the new forms. Behind him, unexpectedly, sat one of Halvard's men — a young Swede named Lindqvist with a rifle slung on his shoulder like he wasn't sure it was allowed there. So. Crane was watching even the watcher. Or trusting no one, which amounted to the same arithmetic.

"Mrs. Nakamura." Pell climbed down into the dory without asking, which was protocol now apparently, and began his inventory aloud for Lindqvist's benefit. "Diesel, four tins. Flour, three sacks. Flannel. Lamp parts. Rope." He lifted each item and set it back down. His hands were slow and thorough and his voice never varied, and Wren realized, with a sensation like the floor tilting, that he was performing the most rigorous inspection of his career — and that it was for her protection. A search this visible, this complete, witnessed by Halvard's man, would satisfy anyone. Nobody could later say Pell had gone easy.

Except he hadn't searched at all. Not really. A man who wanted to find something checks the hollow of a kick-up rudder. Checks the false bottom of a bait box, the space inside a rolled sail, the tarred gap under a thwart. Pell checked none of it. He lifted the flour sacks and set them down gently, like a man patting a sleeping child.

"That's the lot," he said, and signed the form, and handed it to her, and for one moment his eyes met hers and there was everything in them — the ice pond, the rope, the lantern, the crying by the skiff, and underneath all of it something else, something that looked terribly like apology.

"Clear," he called up to the float. "Cast off when ready."

Lindqvist shrugged and looked away, bored, and Wren shipped her oars, and pulled for open water she had no intention of crossing. Not today. Today she only wanted the shape of the passage in her body — the distance, the timing, the sight lines. Who could see the dory from the cannery windows. How long the breakwater hid her from shore. Where the inspection float sat relative to the channel, and how a small boat might leave it at dusk rather than noon, when Lindqvist's shift changed and the light went soft and gray.

Behind her, Pell's voice floated across the water, pitched exactly loud enough to carry: "Nothing in that one. Widow woman hauling her winter stores, same as every year. Write it down."

*Saw nothing,* the words said, in the language men like Pell used. *Saw nothing, and will say so again.*

* * *

The second word came the next evening, wrapped in a twist of brown paper inside a sack of coffee beans Marta delivered — Marta who raised one eyebrow and asked no questions, which was itself a message, and not a comfortable one.

Wren waited until Noor was asleep before she unwrapped it. Inside the paper, in pencil, in Pell's crabbed sailor's hand:

*Tender due Friday, weather holding. Manifests signed in duplicate now. One copy stays with me. One goes to C. Don't know why. Thought you should know. Saw nothing yesterday. Will see nothing Friday. After that I can't say. P.*

Wren read it twice by lamplight, then fed it to the stove and watched it curl.

*Manifests in duplicate.* Her mind turned it over the way her tongue turned a loose tooth. Why would Crane want copies of routine manifests? Unless the manifests weren't routine. Unless there was something on them — or missing from them — that Crane needed to control. Insurance again. Wrecks reported as cargo losses, losses reported as wrecks, numbers shuffled like cards, and here was the paper trail, sitting in a drawer sixty feet from the sea, kept by a man who had been signing his name to lies for years and was only now beginning to wonder what happened to men who knew too much.

*After that I can't say.*

He was frightened, and he was telling her he was frightened, and beneath both messages ran a third, unspoken, that she could feel like a current under a calm sea: Pell was working himself toward something. Toward her, or away from her. Toward confession, or toward the safe quiet surrender of an old man who wanted his last winters warm.

She thought of him mending the net. Of the ice pond and the rope. Of the sound Noor had made when she first breathed again, that thin astonishing cry, and how Pell had wept and denied it ever after.

Then she blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the sea work at the rocks below, and did not hear — because no one could have heard — the small careful sounds from the loft, where a girl who should have been sleeping sat cross-legged in the dark with a letter she had found, sounding out, syllable by patient syllable, words no nine-year-old should have to carry.

Downstairs, Wren banked the fire, believing everything in her house was exactly where she had left it.
