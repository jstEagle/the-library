## 10. Daughter of the Light

The storm gave them a morning of stillness, which was its own kind of warning. Wren had learned that much in eleven winters at Juniper Point: the sea never rested without a reason.

She spent it the way she spent all quiet hours — inventorying. Oil enough for six weeks if she rationed the night watch down to four hours of burn. Matches in three tins, two dry, one suspect. Flour gone soft at the edges but sound in the middle. Salt fish wrapped in oilcloth under the bed. Noor's shoes, toes worn through again, stuffed with wool against the day they'd need resoling and there was no one on the mainland to sole them because the bridge was closed and would stay closed until spring.

That was the arithmetic she could not stop doing. Not money — she had stopped thinking in money years ago — but weight. What could be carried. What could be left behind. What, if the worst came, would fit into one canvas bag and one small girl's arms.

She was counting candles when she heard Noor's footsteps stop on the stair.

There was a rhythm to her daughter now, eight years old and quick as a gull, and Wren knew every variation: the drumming descent when there was food, the slow trudge when there was schoolwork, the light scamper of a child who wanted something. This was none of these. This was a pause. The careful, measured step of someone who had learned — somewhere, recently — that footsteps could tell on you.

Wren set down the candle she was holding.

"Noor," she called. "Come help me with the wicks."

* * *

They sat at the kitchen table with the lamp gutted open between them, brass parts laid out like surgery. Noor trimmed wicks better than most keepers' wives; her fingers were small and patient. For a long while neither spoke. Wind moved through the gap under the door and made the candle flames lean east.

"Ma," Noor said. "What's insurance?"

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass.

"Where did you hear that word?"

Noor's jaw set. Wren watched her daughter weigh a lie and find it too heavy. She had that face from Toma — the stubborn line of him, the way honesty cost him visibly but he paid it anyway.

"There was a letter," Noor said. "In your sewing basket. Under the buttons."

The sewing basket sat on the shelf above the mending pile, ordinary as bread. Wren had put the packet there because it was the last place anyone would look — Halvard had searched the house once already, before the bridge closed, and had lifted the mattress and run his hand along the bookshelf and even opened the flour bin, but he had not touched the sewing basket, because men like Halvard did not imagine that a woman's mending held anything but mending.

"I didn't take it out of the house," Noor said quickly. "I just read it. One. Then I put it back."

"One." Wren kept her voice level. Inside her, something was swinging like the lamp in a gale. *Which one.* There were nineteen letters in the packet, and she had read only seven of them herself, because reading them felt like standing too close to an edge, and some nights the edge won and she folded the packet shut and put the buttons back over it and sewed nothing at all.

"It was about a boat," Noor said. "The *Merry Alice*. It said — it said the *Merry Alice* wasn't lost. It said Mr. Crane wrote down that it was lost so he could get money for it. But the boat was fine. Only—"

Noor stopped. Her hands had gone still on the wick scissors.

"Only what?" Wren asked, though she already knew. She had read that letter. She had read it twice, kneeling by the stove with her back to the room, as if the words needed her body between them and her daughter.

"Only the crew didn't know," Noor finished, very quietly. "It said the crew didn't know the boat was reported lost until they didn't come home."

*Until they didn't come home.* A child's paraphrase, wrong in the grammar and exact in the horror. Wren reached across the table and took the scissors from her daughter's hands and set them down, because she wanted both of those hands in hers.

"How long ago did you read it?"

"Nine days." Nine days. Her daughter had been carrying this for nine days — through supper, through lessons, through the long dark watches when the two of them sat in the same room with the same lamp and Noor had said nothing. Nine days of holding a word like *insurance* in her mouth.

"Why didn't you ask me sooner?"

Noor looked at the table. Then, with the terrible directness of children, she looked up. "Because I thought maybe you were hiding it from me to keep me safe. And then I thought, if I ask, you'll have to decide whether to lie. And I didn't want to make you lie."

Wren had prepared, in nine days' worth of her own silence, a dozen deflections. It's complicated. It's grown-up business. It's nothing for you to worry your head over. They were good lies, kind ones, the sort mothers traded in like small coins. And here was her daughter refusing to spend them.

So she told the truth. Some of it.

"You know Mr. Crane owns the cannery. You know half the village works for him." Noor nodded. "Those letters say that Mr. Crane has been telling stories about boats. Telling the insurance men that boats sank when they didn't, or telling them crews were lost when—" Wren paused, chose. "When he was the reason they were lost. And being paid money for the stories."

"And Papa's boat is in the letters."

The room tilted. Wren gripped the table's edge.

"The letter I read had another name in it too," Noor went on, before Wren could find her voice. "I looked. I looked for Papa's boat in every line. It wasn't there. But I thought — Ma, is that why you keep them? Because of Papa?"

There it was, then. Eight years old, and she had walked straight through the maze her mother had been lost in for weeks and come out the other side asking the only question that mattered.

"Yes," Wren said. "Partly."

"Is Papa in them?"

"I don't know yet. I haven't read them all."

"Why not?" Noor asked, and it wasn't a challenge; it was genuine puzzlement, the clean logic of a child who had been taught since birth that a keeper's first duty was the truth written plainly in the log. *You don't leave pages unread. Unread pages sink ships.*

"Because reading them costs something," Wren said. "And I have to decide how much I can pay."

* * *

Afterward they worked, because work was how the women of Juniper Point held conversations that couldn't be spoken. They polished the lens housing's brass skirt. They aired the blankets in the thin sun. Noor recited her multiplication tables on the gallery stairs while Wren scraped salt rime from the railing, and the numbers rose and fell with the wind like a strange liturgy.

At noon Wren made tea and fried the last of the winter apples, and they ate at the window watching the water, flat and gray and patient.

"Can I ask something else?" Noor said.

"You can always ask."

"Marta's ma came by yesterday when you were at Pell's. She talked to me in the yard. She asked if we had any books she could borrow." Noor turned her apple slice over on her plate. "We don't lend books. We've never lent books. I said no, and she looked at me a long time and then she said, 'Good girl,' and gave me a boiled sweet."

The sweetness of it curdled in Wren's stomach. Marta's mother, asking after books. Marta, who two nights ago had stood at Wren's stove and said *burn them, Wren, burn them tonight* — Marta sending her mother now, or her mother acting alone, or the whole village talking in the coded language of borrowed things. Everyone measuring everyone. Everyone wondering who knew what, and what knowing was worth.

"Did you give her anything?" Wren asked.

"No." Noor hesitated. "Should I have?"

"No, love. You did exactly right."

"But people are supposed to share," Noor said, frowning. "You taught me that."

"And I'm teaching you this now, and it's harder: some things in our house right now belong only to us. If anyone asks about papers, or letters, or books — you smile and you say no and you change the subject to the weather. Weather talk is safe. Can you do that?"

"Because of the man with the gray coat," Noor said. "Halvard."

"You remember him."

"He looked at my shoes," Noor said, with the unforgiving memory of children. "When he was here before. He crouched down and looked at my shoes and asked where I got them. Nobody asks that about shoes." She ate her apple slice. "I didn't like him."

"Neither do I," Wren said. "But he may come again, and if he does, you'll be brave and polite, and you'll let me do the talking. Agreed?"

"Agreed." Then, quieter, staring into her teacup: "Ma. If the letters say Papa knew — if he found out about Mr. Crane before the boat went down — will you tell me?"

Wren's chest closed like a fist. Because that was precisely the question, wasn't it — the one she circled every night with the packet under the buttons. Whether Toma had known. Whether he had sailed out with warnings ignored and knowledge burning in his pocket, whether his drowning had been carelessness or silencing. And whether the truth would be a gift to give her daughter or a stone.

"When I know the whole of it," Wren said, "you and I will sit at this table, and I will tell you everything I know about your father. All of it, good and hard. That's a promise. But I only make promises I can keep, so it has to wait until I know."

Noor considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything now, as if childhood were a coat she was already growing out of.

"That's fair," she said at last. "Unfair things aren't worth promising."

And Wren laughed — really laughed, for the first time since Elias had washed up beneath the light like driftwood with a dying message — because her daughter sounded exactly like her grandmother Nakamura, and grief loosened its grip for one whole breath.

* * *

They spent the afternoon in the lamp room, Noor doing sums while Wren logged the weather. Barometer steady. Wind northwest, light. Visibility high. In the margin, in her small tight hand, Wren wrote what she never said aloud: *Bridge closed 34 days. Village quiet in the wrong way.*

Below them, Juniper harbor lay exposed by the low tide, mudflats gleaming, boats resting on their keels like sleeping animals. From this height she could see the whole geometry of her captivity: the tidal bridge to the south, its causeway drowned twice daily and now gated outright, Crane's padlock on the chain. The cannery dock to the north where Halvard's men inspected every hull. The harbor master's shack, smoke rising thin from Pell's stove. A map of checkpoints, drawn in geography instead of ink.

"Ma, look," Noor said suddenly, pointing down the coast. "Someone's on the flats."

Wren followed her finger. Far down the shoreline, past the point where the village ended and the basalt began, a figure moved along the tideline — bent, unhurried, gathering something. Driftwood, probably. People gathered driftwood.

But people who gathered driftwood did not walk with their heads up, checking sightlines to the lighthouse. And they did not, when the figure paused at the mouth of the little ravine below the headland — the ravine Wren had been studying for days, the one Elias had mumbled about in his fever, *the smugglers' cut, passable at low water if the wind holds off* — they did not stand at the mouth of such a ravine for a full minute, looking in, looking out, looking up at the light.

The figure turned and walked back toward the village.

"Probably just firewood," Noor said, but she had followed her mother's stillness and gone quiet herself.

"Probably," Wren agreed.

She watched until the coast was empty. Then she wrote the hour in the log, closed the book, and took her daughter's cold hands in hers.

"Come down, love. It's getting toward evening. We'll want the kettle warm and the door barred."

"For who?" Noor asked, halfway down the stairs.

For no one, Wren almost said. For the wind. But the wind had a knock in it tonight, she could feel it in the stair rail — a vibration, faint and rhythmic, a boat hull bumping a dock somewhere down the coast, or boots on the cliff path, taking the stones slowly, the way a man walks when he has decided to search a house properly this time.

"Ma," Noor said from below. "Someone's coming up the hill."
