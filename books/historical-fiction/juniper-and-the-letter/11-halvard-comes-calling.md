## 11. Halvard Comes Calling

Wren saw the boat first — Crane's launch, the one with the brass rail and the name *Prosper* painted on the bow in letters meant to be read from a distance. It came around the point at half past two in the afternoon, riding flat and heavy in the water the way boats do when they carry men who have no business being on it.

She was mending Noor's second pair of stockings by the south window, needle paused mid-stitch. Below her, the tide was making against the rocks in long gray shelves of water. The storm had eased overnight into that lying sort of calm the coast does before it changes its mind.

"Halvard," she said aloud, though no one could hear her, because there was no other reason for that boat to come north.

She had rehearsed this without knowing she was rehearsing it. Every morning since Elias had pressed the packet into her hands, she had walked through the lighthouse room by room and asked herself: if a man came through that door looking, where would he look first? The answer was everywhere. A lighthouse is a tower full of hiding places built by someone who never imagined needing one — the oil locker, the coal bin, the space under the stairs, the old fog-signal room with its rusted trumpet. She had considered them all and rejected them all, one by one, the way you reject a lie you know will not hold.

The trouble with every hiding place in a house is that a searching man searches houses.

So the letters were not in the house.

They were ninety feet up, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with tarred twine, wedged in the housing of the great lens itself — tucked behind the pedestal's lower ring where the brass met the iron framework, in a gap no wider than two fingers, reachable only by lying flat under the lens assembly with your cheek against the cold plate glass mount. It was not clever. It was desperate. But it had one virtue: nobody looks inside the light. The lamp was the one thing on the point everyone needed left alone. Even Halvard, she told herself, even Halvard would not take apart the instrument that kept his master's ships alive.

Even Halvard. She heard the phrase again in her own voice and did not believe it.

The launch put in at the landing below the tower, and two figures climbed the cut steps. Halvard in front, collar up, hands jammed in his pockets like a man who resented the walk. Behind him a younger man she didn't know — cannery shirt, soft hands, carrying a leather satchel that looked heavier than paper should be.

Noor was at school down the village path, forty minutes' walk among other people's mothers. Wren counted that as the day's one mercy.

She had twenty minutes to make tea and become a woman with nothing in her house worth finding.

* * *

Halvard knocked the way he did everything — twice, hard, then waited as though the waiting were your fault.

"Mrs. Nakamura." He filled her doorway, bringing the smell of diesel and wet wool. "Crane wants an inventory."

"An inventory." She stepped back to let him in, because refusing would be a story, and stories had to be maintained, and she was tired before the day began. "Of what?"

"Lighthouse stores. Oil, wicks, signal equipment." His eyes went past her shoulder into the kitchen, moving with the flat professional sweep of a man pricing things. "There's been irregularities at the cannery. Missing manifests. Crane's having everything on the point counted."

"And you brought an accountant." She nodded at the young man, who stood at the threshold as if unsure whether he was permitted to be indoors.

"Clerk. Mr. Fenn." Halvard pulled off one glove, then the other, finger by finger, taking his time. "You'll want to put the kettle on. This'll take a while."

It was, she understood, a courtesy — him telling her what she already knew. He intended to search her home, and he wanted her to watch him do it with her consent wrapped around it like a napkin. She put the kettle on. Her hands were steadier than she expected them to be. Grief had taught her this years ago: the body learns to work while the mind stands apart and watches, taking notes for later.

"You'll excuse the state of the place," she said. "Storm season. Everything smells of paraffin."

"It does." Halvard was already at the shelf above the stove, running one thick finger along the spines of her books — Toma's books, mostly, navigation tables and a water-stained Byron she could not bring herself to shelve. He tilted one toward the light. *Nautical Almanac, 1931.* Put it back.

Fenn set the satchel on the kitchen table, unbuckled it, and began laying out forms in neat rows. Inventory sheets. Carbon copies. An ink bottle with a screw cap. He worked like a man building a wall out of paper.

"I'll start with the storeroom," Halvard said. It wasn't a question.

"The oil locker's locked. I'll get the key."

"No need." He held up his own key ring, and among the cannery keys hung one stamped with the Lighthouse Service eagle, and she felt the floor tilt slightly under her, because of course they had copied it, of course they had; the government lent its lights to the trade that depended on them, and the trade had made itself a spare.

"When did—" she began, and stopped, because finishing the question would tell him how much it mattered.

"Two years back," Halvard said pleasantly. "Standard practice after your husband's accident. Safety review." He said the word *accident* the way a man sets down a card face-up to see if you flinch.

She did not flinch. She poured the water.

* * *

They worked through the tower bottom to top, which was wrong — she had expected them to go up first, straight to the lantern, the way desire climbs. Instead Halvard was methodical, patient in the way that frightened her more than haste would have. Storeroom: oil drums counted, tapped, dipsticked. Coal bin: he got into it, actually climbed down into it with a lantern and turned the coal over with a shovel while she stood at the top holding nothing. Under the stairs: he lay on the floor and reached into the dark with a boat hook he'd brought for exactly that purpose, and dragged out a coil of rope, a crate of candles, and three years of dust.

Fenn wrote everything down. *Candles, 4 doz. Rope, manila, 20 fthm.* His pen scratched steadily. Somewhere in that satchel, Wren knew, was the real list — the short one, the one with only one item on it.

"Tea's going over," she said, when the kettle had screamed twice and settled.

"In a minute." Halvard wiped his hands on his coat.

She carried two cups up anyway, and set them on the second-landing table, and watched Halvard pass them without looking at her, and thought: he has done houses before. Many houses. There is a rhythm to it and he has the rhythm in his shoulders.

The rain started while they were on the third landing — a fine, needling rain that came against the windows in gusts, and the tower creaked its old complaint, and Wren used the sound to cover the sound of her own breathing.

"Your daughter at school?" Halvard asked suddenly, halfway up the fourth flight, not turning around.

"Yes."

"Good school, that. Crane gave the bell."

"He mentioned."

"He likes giving things." Halvard climbed another step. "Makes it hard, afterward, to say he took anything."

She let that sit between them, unanswered, and followed him up.

* * *

The watch room below the lantern smelled of brass polish and cold glass. Wren lit the small stove there out of habit and stood with her back to it, and realized too late that standing between Halvard and the ladder to the lantern was a thing a woman with nothing to hide would not do. She moved aside. Smoothly, she hoped. He noticed — she saw it land somewhere behind his eyes — but he only nodded and went up.

She counted to ten. Then she followed.

The lantern room was the highest, brightest, emptiest place in her world. Rain streaked the glass on all sides. The lens stood in its center like something grown rather than built — the great crystal barrel of it, six feet tall, panels and prisms and bull's-eye rings, all of it floating in its carriage so that one hand on the crank could send it turning. Beneath it, the pedestal: iron and brass, bolted to the floor, holding the clockwork and the weight shafts.

And in the gap where the pedestal's base ring met the frame, two fingers deep, wrapped in oilcloth — everything. Elias's letters. Pell's name in Elias's hand. The page that named the *Aldern*, which was Toma's boat, which was supposed to have been lost to weather, which had been lost instead to arithmetic.

Halvard walked around the lens slowly, once, the way men walk around horses they intend to buy.

"Beautiful piece of work," he said. "French glass, they tell me. Cost more than the tower." He stopped at the door of the lamp service cabinet, opened it. Burners, spare chimneys, a cloth. Closed it. "You clean this yourself?"

"Every week."

"Alone?"

"With my daughter, sometimes." She said it lightly. "She likes the turning part."

"Mm." He crouched. And now he was looking underneath — at the pedestal, at the bolts, at the shadowed underside of the carriage where dust collected in gray felted ridges. He took out a pocket flashlight, though the room was bright enough to read by, and lay the beam along the base of the lens assembly, inch by inch.

Wren discovered that she could hear her own pulse in her ears.

He worked counterclockwise. That was all she knew about what was happening, and it was enough to stop her breath: he was working counterclockwise, panel by panel, and the gap — the two-finger gap, the oilcloth, the tarred twine — was four panels ahead of him, maybe five, and each panel took him perhaps thirty seconds because there was nothing to find, because there was nothing anywhere, except where there was.

Think. Not panic — think.

"Mr. Halvard." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Your tea's gone cold on the landing."

"In a minute."

"You said inventory. You're inspecting. There's a difference, and I'd like to know which one I'm hosting."

He didn't look up. Third panel now. The flashlight beam slid along brass. "Crane counts everything, Mrs. Nakamura. That's the whole of it. A man loses two crews and a boat in one season, he gets particular about his property."

"Three boats," she said. "In three years."

The flashlight stopped moving.

She had not meant to say it. It had come out of her like a splinter surfacing — and now Halvard rose slowly from his crouch and turned, and his face was doing something careful.

"That right?" he said. "Who's been counting for you?"

"Nobody counts for me, Mr. Halvard. That's rather the point of me." She gestured at the log book open on the watch desk below, visible through the floor hatch. "I count wrecks. Weather. Oil. It's the work. Three boats in three years, and every report says weather, and every winter somebody else's window has a black cloth in it. A woman notices. It isn't arithmetic. It's just neighbors."

Silence. The rain ticked against the glass. Down on the landing, faintly, Fenn's pen was still scratching, filling in numbers for a world that made sense.

"Fourth panel," Halvard said at last, quietly, to himself, and knelt again.

She had perhaps two minutes. Maybe less. She thought of pulling the crank — spinning the lens, the whole glittering mass of it whirling up with a shriek of clockwork, forcing him back from it, forcing the conversation elsewhere. Too obvious. She thought of dropping a cup. Where was a cup? Upstairs. Useless.

Then she thought of tea.

Not the tea upstairs — cold, forgotten, a prop. She thought of the ritual of it, the long performance of it, the way a woman on this coast was allowed to delay any man for any length of time provided she was feeding him while she did it.

"Mr. Fenn," she called down the hatch, sweet and clear. "How do you take yours? I'm making a fresh pot — this wind goes through a person."

"White, please, ma'am," came the prompt, grateful reply, and she heard him set down his pen.

She caught Halvard's eye. "Excuse me a moment."

"Door stays—"

"—in sight, yes. The stove's right here." She pointed at the little watch-room stove, three steps away, entirely within his view, and busied her hands with the kettle and the tin and the two cups, and made the loudest tea ever brewed in a lighthouse — the rattle of the lid, the clink of the spoon, the deliberate scrape of the kettle onto its plate — and through all of it she watched Halvard's flashlight beam crawl across the fourth panel and the fifth, and reach the sixth, and stop.

Because the sixth panel was where the gap was.

He leaned in. He lay the light flat along the base ring, close, closer than he had looked at anything yet, and his shoulder blocked her view, and she stood with the kettle in one hand and her heart going like a bird in a closed room, and calculated distances: nine steps to the ladder, twelve pounds of kettle, nowhere to run on a rock in a storm with the bridge shut and the sea the color of dishwater.

Halvard reached into the gap.

His hand came back out.

Holding nothing — holding a dead moth, a curl of it, papery, which he turned over once between his fingers with an expression she could not read at all, and flicked away.

The oilcloth bundle sat two inches deeper than his fingers had gone. She had packed it deep. She had packed it the night she hid it, pressing it in until the twine bit her palms, because some animal part of her had known that depth might matter someday.

"Mrs. Nakamura." Halvard stood, pocketing the flashlight. His voice had changed — flattened out, professional again, or performing professionalism. "When did you last correspond with the district inspector?"

The question arrived like weather from an unexpected quarter.

"The inspector? August. Routine lamp report." True. Entirely true. She let the truth stand there wearing nothing, because a naked truth is harder to accuse than a dressed-up lie. "Why?"

"No reason." He picked up his gloves. "We're done up here."

* * *

Downstairs, Fenn totaled his forms while Halvard stood at the kitchen window with his untouched tea, watching the rain come in off the water. Wren signed the inventory at the bottom — *all stores correct and accounted, W. Nakamura, keeper* — and Fenn blotted it and packed the satchel, and for a moment the three of them stood in her kitchen like people at a funeral waiting for someone to speak first.

At the door, Halvard put his gloves back on, finger by finger.

"Mr. Crane will be glad the count came clean," he said.

"I'm glad he'll be glad."

"Aye." He stepped out onto the wet stone, then turned, half into the rain. "Funny thing, though. Moths. In a lens housing. Sealed unit, French glass, best there is." He looked at her, and for the first time all afternoon there was nothing careful in his face at all. "Must have got in somehow."

The launch pulled away from the landing at four o'clock and beat south against the freshening wind, and Wren stood at the gallery rail until it was gone, her hands white on the rail.

Then she climbed the ninety feet, hauled herself flat beneath the lens, and drew out the bundle, and weighed it in her hands, and knew with a certainty that settled in her bones like the cold coming off the glass: Halvard had touched the moth and understood exactly what it meant. He hadn't found the letters. He had found proof that they were near. He had come as a searcher, and he would come again as something else, and next time there would be no tea slow enough.

She repacked the bundle deeper, wired the ring down over it, and went below to the log book, and sat with her pen uncapped over the blank line.

That was when she heard the knock — soft, twice, familiar — and opened the door on Marta, soaked through, no lantern, no umbrella, standing on her step with a face like a woman carrying news she couldn't put down fast enough.

"Wren," Marta said. "He's given me till Sunday. And I have to tell you what I promised him."
