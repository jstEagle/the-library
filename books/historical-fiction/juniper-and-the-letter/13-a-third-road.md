## 13. A Third Road

Elias slept in the oil room, on the narrow cot where spare wicks used to dry, and for the first week Wren had believed he would die there. His fever had come and gone like weather. Now, in the third week, it had settled into something quieter and more stubborn — a low burn that left him gray by afternoon and lucid by evening, when he would prop himself against the wall with his bandaged ribs and watch Noor do her sums at the table as if she were the most remarkable thing the coast had ever produced.

He was lucid now. That was the trouble.

"You're still keeping them in the lens housing," he said.

Wren looked up from the logbook she was pretending to read. "You're supposed to be sleeping."

"I sleep eleven hours a day. I dream about Halvard's hands." He shifted, winced, kept his eyes on her. "I know what I asked of you. I know you didn't ask for any of it. But if we're going to do this properly, we have to stop pretending the storm will do our thinking for us."

Outside, the wind did exactly that — a long moan around the gallery rail, the old sound, the one that meant another low pressure system crawling down from the north. The barometer had not risen above twenty-nine inches since the bridge closed. Wren had stopped marking the glass twice a day because reading it had become a form of grief.

"The packet stays where it is," she said. "It's the only place Halvard didn't look."

"Because he ran out of nerve, not because he ran out of ideas." Elias's voice had a rasp in it that hadn't been there before the wreck, and would probably never leave. "Next time he comes, he'll bring a warrant, or something that looks like one. He'll bring Crane's own paper. And then your clever hiding place becomes a coffin for the truth."

Wren closed the logbook. "Then tell me the alternative. You've had three weeks to think of one, and every morning you wake up and say *not yet* like a man praying."

Elias was quiet for a while. The stove ticked. Below them, the sea worked at the rocks with its patient, mindless industry.

"There's a road," he said finally. "Not a road. A path."

* * *

He told it slowly, in pieces, the way men tell things they half-want to take back. When he was nineteen — before the cannery owned him, before Crane's boats were the only boats — Elias had run contraband for a man named Ostrey who kept a still somewhere in the spruce above Saltee Cove. Nothing dramatic. Whiskey, tobacco, once a crate of unmarked rifles that Ostrey never explained and Elias never asked about. The revenue cutters patrolled the harbor mouth and the tidal flats, so Ostrey's runners went overland instead — around the headland itself, below the cliffs, along a shelf of rock that showed itself for perhaps two hours on either side of low tide and vanished under six feet of green water the rest of the day.

"The locals called it the Kist," Elias said. "Old word. Chest. Because it opens and shuts." He drew it on the table with a blunt finger, tracing through spilled tea: the point, the cliffs, the cove beyond. "Starts at the gully below the old signal station. Ends at a shingle beach on the far side of Gannet Head, maybe four miles as the tide runs. There's a hauled-out spot halfway where you can wait if the water catches you, but I wouldn't recommend finding out how generous it is."

"And from the shingle?"

"Deep water. You could signal a boat. Or you could keep going — there's broken ground all the way to Miller's Landing on the mainland, but that part's a swimmer's route, and I wasn't ever a swimmer." He looked at his own hands, still bearing the yellow-brown of the salt water he'd nearly died in. "I'm less of one now."

Wren studied the tea-map. Four miles of rock shelf, exposed twice a day, usable only when the sea allowed it. She thought about the winter so far: three weeks of storm, the swell never below eight feet, spray coming over the lantern room some nights like rain falling upward.

"How long since you've walked it?"

"Fifteen years." He smiled without humor. "And I was nineteen and stupid and carrying forty pounds of whiskey."

"It's not a road, Elias. It's a rumor with a tide table attached."

"It's a third road," he said. "The bridge is Crane's. The harbor is Crane's. This one belongs to nobody, because nobody remembers it except me and a dead bootlegger. That makes it the only road on this coast worth anything."

She wanted to argue. She found she couldn't, because underneath her skepticism something small and dangerous had already lit — the same thing that lit every time she checked the lamp, every time the light swept out over black water and ships passed safely by. The feeling of a job that could be done if you refused to think about the odds.

"When's the next low tide?" she asked.

"Half past two in the morning." Elias tilted his head toward the window, toward the sound of the wind. "And you can hear what the weather thinks of the idea."

* * *

She started checking anyway. It was the inventory habit, the same muscle — measure what you have, count what you lack, don't lie to yourself about either. Every morning she stood at the gallery rail with the tide tables open on her knee and watched the water drop away from the headland, and every morning the shelf stayed drowned. The swell came in long sets from the southeast, six feet, eight, ten, breaking white across the place where the Kist should have been, hammering the cliff base hard enough that she felt it through the stone under her boots. Twice the water came so far up the gully below the signal station that the path's entrance was a churned cauldron of foam and drifting kelp.

On the fourth morning Noor came out to stand beside her, coat too big, breath steaming.

"Are we going somewhere?"

"No."

"You're looking at the water like Papa used to."

The sentence landed harder than the girl could have known. Wren put an arm around her shoulders and said nothing, and Noor, who had learned to read her mother's silences better than her words, did not push. But at breakfast she pushed anyway, in the way of children, sideways.

"Elias talks in his sleep," she said. "About tides. About a chest opening."

"He dreams loud," Wren said. "Eat your oats."

"Marta says the bridge might not open till April. Marta says Crane's men are writing down who goes to whose house." Noor stirred her bowl without eating. "Mama. If there's a way off this rock, I want to know before you go. Not after. After is what happened with Papa."

Wren set down her spoon. Outside, the gulls were flying backward, which meant the wind had topped thirty knots and the morning's low tide was a joke the sea was telling at their expense.

"If there's a way," she said carefully, "you'll know first. I promise you that. But knowing means doing your sums and keeping your mouth shut at school, and it means not saying a word to Marta's girls, even the nice one. Can you carry that?"

Noor considered it the way she considered everything now, with a gravity that made Wren ache. "I carried the letters' secret for nine days."

"I know you did."

"Then I can carry this."

* * *

Elias mended the way the coast mended — grudgingly, and only when no one was watching him do it. By the second week he was making it up the tower stairs one landing at a time, resting between turns, swearing softly at his ribs. By the third week he could stand at the rail beside her and read the water with a fisherman's eye, and what he read he reported honestly, which was worse than lying.

"Not today," he'd say, or, "Another week of this swell and the shelf won't matter anyway — it'll have silted over," or, worst, nothing at all, just a slow shake of the head while the breakers wrote their answer across the headland.

The tide tables gave them windows. The weather took them away. It became a private arithmetic: a low tide at dawn meant leaving in the dark; a calm meant the fog might roll in instead and make the shelf a trap of wet black rock you couldn't see the end of. Once — one morning in the third week — the wind dropped before sunrise to almost nothing, and Wren actually got as far as pulling the oilskins from the peg, before the barometer fell so fast it seemed personal, and by noon the horizon had gone the color of a bruise and the sea stood up on its hind legs again.

That night she sat with Elias in the oil room and let herself say the thing she'd been rationing.

"We could wait forever. The storms don't owe us a window. Toma waited for weather his whole life and it killed him anyway, on a night everyone said was fine." Her hands were flat on her knees. "Maybe this is what Crane understands and I don't. Maybe waiting is just losing politely."

Elias took his time. She had learned that about him; the fever had burned hurry out of him.

"When I was in the water," he said, "after the *Alcyone* went down, I hung on to a hatch cover and I made myself a rule. One wave at a time. Don't look at the shore, because looking makes you count the distance, and counting makes you despair, and despairing men let go." He looked at her. "But I'll tell you the other half of it, the part I didn't understand until now. You also can't look only at the waves. You have to lift your head sometimes and check the shore. Otherwise you survive for nothing."

"So which are we doing?"

"Both. We watch the tide tables and we watch the sky, and when the sea gives us two hours, we spend them like misers." He reached into his coat, hanging by the cot, and brought out a folded scrap — a page torn from an almanac, marked in pencil. "I've been keeping my own reckoning. Three days from now there's a spring tide — lowest of the month, near enough. And there's a pattern to these systems. They come in fives and sixes. We're on the fifth."

"That's superstition."

"That's thirty years of watching this coast," Elias said. "Superstition is just data that hasn't confessed yet."

* * *

Three days later, an hour before dawn, Wren woke to a silence so complete it frightened her.

She lay listening for the wind the way other people listen for breathing in a sickroom. Nothing. The lamp turned overhead with its steady clockwork whisper, and beneath that — beneath that, the sea. She knew its voices: the storm voice, the working voice, the long lazy grind of a heavy swell. This was none of them. This was small water lapping at rock, apologetic, almost warm.

She dressed in the dark and climbed to the gallery.

The stars were out. All of them, hard and close, in a sky scrubbed clean. The headland stood up black against them, and at its base, where the water had hammered for a month, the tide had drawn back like a held breath released, and there — pale in the starlight, slick and narrow and utterly real — was the shelf. The Kist. Running out along the cliff foot toward Gannet Head, gleaming wet, open for business.

Her heart was going like a bird in a box.

Behind her, the door opened. Elias, wrapped in a blanket, moving carefully, stood in the doorway and followed her gaze.

"There's your third road," he said quietly.

Wren did not move. Because she was a keeper's daughter in her bones, she looked not at the path but at the west, where the horizon lay clean and low — and where, right at its edge, faint as a scar, sat a line of cloud. Thin. High. Moving fast against the stars.

"Elias," she said. "How long do we have?"

He squinted into the west for a long moment, and when he answered, his voice had gone flat and professional, the voice of a man reading his own death sentence and finding the arithmetic fair.

"Less than you'd hope," he said. "Get the girl. Get the papers. We walk at first light — and pray the chest doesn't shut with us inside it."
