## 16. Refusal

The note came by gull-wing, as everything came in those days — carried, dropped at her door, gone again before she could hold it long. Crane's hand was small and precise, the hand of a man who signed other men's lives in tidy columns.

*The cannery hall. Thursday evening. A matter concerning the welfare of Juniper village.*

No *please*. No threat either, which was its own kind of threat. Wren read it twice by lamplight while Noor slept, then folded it into the tin with the letters — no. Not with the letters. The letters stayed in the lens housing where they had lived for a month now, wrapped in oilcloth, closer to the light than anything else on the point. She put the note under the sugar tin instead, among ordinary things, because that was where lies belonged.

She thought about not going.

She spent most of Wednesday not going. She inventoried it the way she inventoried everything: what would be carried, what would be lost. If she stayed away, Crane would know why. He would come to her, or send Halvard, and the negotiation would move into her kitchen where Noor could hear it through the wall, and it would become what he wanted it to be — a private transaction between a keeper and an owner, settled quietly, like everything else in this village had been settled for thirty years.

If she went, she could refuse him in front of the only audience he had ever feared: his own payroll.

Marta came at dusk, snow in her hair, her face doing the thing it did when she'd made up her mind and hated it.

"You'll go," Marta said.

"I'll go."

"Then listen to me." She sat down without being asked, which was how you knew it was serious; Marta had too much pride to sit uninvited even after twenty years of friendship. "He'll offer you the keepership. Everyone knows it already. Halvard told Sorensen, Sorensen told his wife, his wife told the whole waterfront by noon. Keepership for life, debts forgiven. Maybe more."

"It won't be more. It doesn't need to be more."

"Wren." Marta took her hands. Her fingers were cold and cracked from the cannery line, and they held on harder than they needed to. "Say no carefully. That's all I'm asking. There's saying no, and there's saying no *loudly*, and one of them lets you keep living here."

"He killed Toma."

"I know what he did." Her voice didn't rise but something under it did. "I've known longer than you have. I've known since before you did, and I said nothing, and I will carry that until I die, so don't you dare lecture me about volume." She let go of Wren's hands and stood, smoothing her apron, restoring herself piece by piece. "Thursday. Say no. Just — measure it first."

After she left, Wren sat a long time watching the lamp turn. Measuring.

But some things didn't weigh. Some things only burned.

* * *

The cannery hall smelled of diesel and fish meal and forty years of spilled coffee, and it was fuller than any meeting Wren could remember. Crane had made sure of that — he wanted witnesses to her surrender, and instead, God help him, he would get witnesses to something else. Villagers packed the benches: cannery families, boat crews, the widows of two boats whose names appeared in Elias's letters. They sat the way people sit when they already know bad news and are waiting only to learn how bad.

Crane stood at the front beneath the company banner, a small man made smaller by his own portrait hanging behind him. Halvard flanked him with his arms crossed, and near the door stood two men Wren didn't recognize — mainland men, in good coats, with the stillness of hired patience.

"Lady of the light," Crane said, warm as a ledger. "Thank you for coming. I'll keep this brief, out of respect for everyone's evening."

He kept it brief. He spoke about the storms, the sacrifice of the fishing fleet, the burden of ownership — *this village is my family* — and then he turned to her with the expression of a man bestowing a great kindness, and he offered her the world.

Keepership for life. A salary from the company, doubled. Every debt on his books forgiven — and he named them, family by family, watching faces around the hall as each name landed like a coin. The Sorensens. The Pell cousins. The Okabe brothers. "And of course," he finished, "Mrs. Nakamura has recently suffered certain... losses. Difficulties. I want to put all of that behind us. All of it." He spread his hands. "All I ask is help with a small matter of misplaced papers. Rumors. Nothing of consequence — just tidiness. A village should be tidy."

Silence. Forty faces pretending not to look at her.

Wren walked to the front. Her boots sounded very loud. She did not stand beside him; she stopped three feet short, so that everyone could see there was daylight between them.

"My husband drowned on the twelfth of November," she said. Her voice carried better than she expected — the hall had been built for exactly this, voices raised over machinery, and it took her words and gave them back to her bigger. "The log says weather. The manifest says weather. The insurance claim says weather. I have read all three, and none of them say that Aldous Crane ordered the *Kestrel* out against the warnings, with the barometer falling, because a full hold was worth more than a crew."

It went through the room like cold water through a hull. She watched it hit the widows. She watched it hit the Pell cousins. She watched Halvard's arms uncross.

"There are no misplaced papers," she said. "There are true ones. And I am going to deliver them to the marine board on the mainland, every page, including the manifests Mr. Pell has been signing under your instruction for nine years."

"Wren—" Marta, from the third bench, half-rising.

"No." Crane's voice was quiet, and the quiet was worse than shouting. He came down off the platform slowly, straightening his cuffs, and smiled at the hall as if nothing had changed. "Grief does strange things," he said to the room. "We're all very sorry for Mrs. Nakamura. But we don't act on a widow's fancies, do we." Then, close to her, low enough that only she and Halvard could hear: "Give me the packet tonight and none of this ever happened. Refuse, and I will close this village so tight you'll beg me for the bridge by February. No boats out. No wages. No mail. Your choice, keeper. You've always been good at choices."

"That's the difference between us," Wren said. "I make mine once."

She turned back to the hall. "You heard what he just offered you. Debts forgiven if I kneel. Now hear what happens because I won't. He's going to punish all of you for me. I'm sorry for that. I have thought about it every hour for a month, and I am sorry, and I am doing it anyway — because the men on those wreck reports were real, and their names were written down as *no casualties*, and someone owes them the truth."

She looked at the widows. One of them, old Mrs. Vasquez, whose husband had gone down on the *Marigold*, nodded once — a small nod, almost nothing, that Wren would remember for the rest of her life.

Then she walked out of the cannery hall into the snow, and behind her she heard Crane's voice, no longer warm, giving orders in the tone of a man done pretending.

* * *

By morning the village was sealed.

It was done with terrible efficiency, the way Crane did everything. The cannery whistles did not sound. Boats were chained at their moorings — chained, with padlocks, company locks. Halvard's men stood at the head of the tidal causeway with a written order nailed to a post: *CLOSED BY ORDER OF JUNIPER PACKING CO. UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE*. The mail skiff turned back from the mainland side. The store opened for two hours a day, credit suspended.

Wren climbed to the gallery and counted, because counting was what she did when she couldn't act. Eleven boats chained. Fourteen men standing idle on the dock with their hands in their pockets. One village learning, house by house, what her refusal had cost.

Noor stood beside her, breath fogging the glass.

"Did we do something wrong?" she asked.

Wren considered lying and found she had used up her whole supply. "No. We did something true, and true things cost money before they pay out. That's how it works."

"Marta says the store's out of flour already."

"Marta says a lot of things." Wren looked down the hill toward the village, and saw Marta herself coming up the path — fast, coat open, not her usual careful walk.

Wren met her at the door, and knew before Marta spoke.

"They took Elias," Marta said, breathless. "An hour ago. Halvard and the mainland men. Out of Sorensen's back room where he's been mending. Put him in the launch and took him to the cannery — Wren, they took him *out of the village*, past the checkpoint, and nobody stopped it, and Crane's telling people he's a sick man being sent for care." Her eyes were wet and furious. "He knows Elias can testify. And next he'll come for anyone who helped him, and I carried food to that back room twice a week, so it's me, isn't it. I'm next."

"Not if—"

"Don't." Marta held up a hand. "Don't protect me. I'm done being protected into silence — it's how he's run this place for thirty years, everyone protecting everyone into silence." She pulled something from inside her coat: a folded sheet, cheap paper, dense handwriting. "This is the debt ledger. My copy — I've kept records of every payment Crane's bookkeepers 'adjusted' since my husband died. Names, amounts, dates. It proves the debts are manufactured, Wren. Half the village isn't in hock to him at all; he wrote them there."

Wren took it. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

"You understand what this makes us," Marta said quietly. "Not witnesses. Traitors. In Crane's books, from this morning, we're traitors together."

Outside, the lamp turned. Down in the village, a whistle finally sounded — one long blast, Crane's launch putting out from the cannery dock toward the mainland, breaking his own seal before the ink dried on it. Going to town, Wren would understand later. Going ahead of her. Getting his story there first.

Marta watched it go, then turned back. "Elias told me about the smugglers' path. Before they took him. He said the window would come — a calm night, a low tide — and when it comes it'll come fast, and you'll have maybe four hours."

"The storms haven't given us a calm night in six weeks."

"Then pray," Marta said. "And pack."

That night Wren wrote in the lighthouse log, in her careful keeper's hand, the entry she had been saving:

*Village sealed by order of A. Crane. Witness: E. Hara removed against his will. Weather: falling glass, wind rising SE.*

Below it, smaller:

*We are leaving.*

She lashed the oilcloth packet to her body with the same knots Toma had taught her, and lay awake listening to the wind, waiting for it to change its mind.
