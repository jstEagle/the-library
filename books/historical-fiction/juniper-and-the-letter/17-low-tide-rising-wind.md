## 17. Low Tide, Rising Wind

The calm came at dawn like a held breath.

Wren knew it before she opened her eyes — the silence in the stones. For six weeks the lighthouse had spoken a single language, wind against glass, sea against rock, and she had learned to sleep inside it the way sailors sleep inside engine noise. Now the quiet woke her, and she lay still in the narrow bed listening to it the way you'd listen to a stranger in the house.

She was at the east window before Noor stirred, counting what she saw. Low cloud, high and ragged, moving but not racing. The water in the cove lying flat as poured lead. The horizon visible — actually visible — for the first time since the bridge closed. And out past the headland, where the smugglers' path began, the tide drawing back off the ledges, exposing the black spine of rock that only showed itself twice a day and only then when the sea allowed.

Elias had said: *You'll know it when you see it. The rocks come up like bones. But the window's short — an hour, maybe less — and if the wind turns while you're on them, there is no path anymore, only teeth.*

The wind was not turning. Not yet.

Wren dressed in the dark by touch, because that was faster and because she did not want to see the room yet — the room with its emptied shelves, its bare hooks, everything she had spent six years building reduced again to what could be carried. She had done this inventory once before, the winter Toma died, standing over a trunk with his coat in her hands deciding what to keep of a man. She had thought then that she would never make such a list again. Life had taught her otherwise. Life kept teaching her otherwise.

Oil for the lamp — no. That stayed. The lamp would go dark tonight and let the dark stand for itself; there were no ships to warn through this weather anyway, and a cold lamp told Crane nothing about where they'd gone until morning. Matches, half a tin. Bread wrapped in oilcloth, dried fish, the small pot of honey Marta had pressed into Wren's hands three nights ago without a word about what it cost her. Water in two corked jugs. The manifest book from Pell, wrapped in sailcloth and bound with cord. Elias's packet. Toma's logbook.

And the letters. The letters went against her body.

She had learned this trick from the crossing ahead, not behind her — from thinking, night after night, about the moment the dory might swamp, about what a drowning woman can hold and what she cannot. Hands must stay free. Arms must stay free. Anything in a bag is anything the sea can take. So she had sewn a canvas harness, wide straps crossing at her back, the packet flat against her sternum beneath her sweater and coat, the manifest book against her belly, tied down with the same knots Toma used to secure cargo. If the water took her, it would have to take the papers too, and the papers would be found on her body, and her body would be found, because Wren Nakamura had decided long ago that if she drowned she would do it within sight of people who owed her the courtesy of dragging the shore.

It was not a comforting thought. It was a load-bearing one.

Noor was awake when Wren turned around. Sitting up in her bed with her hair loose and her eyes very steady, already dressed, because Wren had told her last night to dress in the dark too, and the girl had understood — children always understood more than the words said aloud — that today was not a school-and-chore day.

"You're wearing the rope knots," Noor said.

"I am."

"Like Papa's."

"Like Papa's." Wren sat on the edge of the bed and pulled Noor's boots toward her. They were the good boots, the ones she'd inventoried that first winter as *shoes, one pair, growing room left*, and there was no growing room left now, which meant the boots were right and the winter had been long. "Listen to me once, all the way through, and then I won't say it again. We walk the rocks. You walk where I step, exactly where I step, no looking around, no stopping. If I say *down*, you get flat and hold onto something black. If we reach the boat and the boat goes into the water, you hold the thwart and you don't let go even if I tell you to. Especially if I tell you to." She laced the second boot. "Can you carry that?"

"Yes."

"Say it back."

Noor said it back, every word, in her flat serious voice that was so much like Toma's that Wren had to look away and busy her hands with the jug.

* * *

They went down through the village in the gray half-light, and the village pretended not to watch them.

That was the arrangement, made in whispers across three kitchens. Marta had walked with Wren the evening before along the harbor road, arm in arm like women taking the air, while between them they counted eyes. Pell at the harbor shed, who would see nothing, twice. Old Sif at the smokehouse, who would leave her door unlatched in case they needed shelter on the north side. The Kessler boys, who would take their skiff out to the far grounds this morning and be conspicuously visible doing it, so that Halvard's watchers would spend their attention on fishermen fishing.

And Marta herself, who would stand at her own gate at eight o'clock and have a loud, audible quarrel with Wren Nakamura in front of God and the cannery row, ending with Wren storming off toward the lighthouse and Marta slamming indoors — after which Wren would slip out the back way through the smokehouse yard and down to the tide path, and anyone asked would swear the keeper hadn't left the point at all.

"It has to be loud," Marta had said. "I'm sorry. It has to be ugly. He'll ask me, after, whether I helped you, and I'll have to swear I didn't, and the uglier it looks, the truer my swearing sounds."

"And after?" Wren had asked. "When the board comes, when it's over?"

Marta had looked out at the flat gray water for a long time. "After," she said finally, "you'll owe me the truth of what you say about me. Nothing else. Just — remember what I did and why, and tell it plain, whichever way it cuts." She'd squeezed Wren's arm once, hard, and gone inside.

Now, passing the smokehouse, the door stood unlatched and the smell of alder smoke covered the sound of their footsteps, and Sif was in her doorway with her back turned, gutting fish, seeing nothing.

At Marta's gate, precisely on time, the performance began. Marta's voice carried the length of the row — something about debts, about pride, about *people who think they're better than the rest of us* — and Wren gave as good as she got, and somewhere in the middle of it her own anger stopped being performed, because it was never really performed, it just had a place to go now. She stormed off up the lane. Behind her, a door slammed hard enough to rattle a shutter.

Then the smokehouse yard, the gap in the fence, the goat track, the drop.

* * *

The headland path was worse than Elias had made it sound, and he had made it sound like a graveyard.

It wasn't a path so much as a sequence of arguments between rock and sea, won provisionally by the rock at low water and lost entirely at any other time. Black ledges shelved down into weed-slick gullies; boulders the size of cottages leaned together leaving crawl-spaces underneath; and everywhere the wet gleamed treacherously, green and gold and brown, beautiful the way poison is beautiful. The exposed tide pools held their own weather — little clouds moved in them, little rains fell upward when a fish flicked.

Wren went first. She tested each foothold with her staff — Toma's old boat hook, cut down — before she trusted it, and called the holds back to Noor in a low running litany. "*Left foot on the white scar. Hand on the crack, not the edge. Jump, don't reach.*"

Noor followed exactly where she stepped, exactly as instructed, face pale and set, and did not look around, and did not stop, and Wren loved her with a ferocity that felt dangerous to carry on slippery ground.

Halfway along the headland, the path narrowed to a ledge barely a boot wide, with the sea working below in a slow heavy swell. The calm offshore hadn't reached here; the ocean remembered its storms longer than the sky did. Wren stood at the start of the ledge and made herself breathe.

"Mama." Noor's voice, very small.

"I know. Watch my feet. Three steps and then it widens."

Three steps. The ledge ran under an overhang furred with dead barnacles, and Wren turned sideways and shuffled, the packet pressing against her ribs with every breath like a second heartbeat. Below her the water rose and fell against the rock with a sound like a great animal shifting in its sleep. She thought — not for the first time — of Toma's logbook entry, the last full page he ever wrote: *Crane wants the *Petrel* out tomorrow. Weather says no. I told him no myself. He said the insurance doesn't care about weather.* She had read those lines forty times now and they still landed like a blow, because the sentence contained the whole architecture of the crime — the boats sent out to die on schedule, the wrecks reported honestly enough to the grieving and falsely to the insurers, the crews written off as weather when they were arithmetic.

Toma had said no. Then he had gone out anyway, because a man who refuses the cannery owner in a company town stops being a fisherman and starts being a warning to others, and he had a wife and a daughter, and warnings get visited on families.

He had gone out, and Crane had collected, and the official log at Juniper Point — written in Wren's own careful hand, transcribed from the harbor office's false report — recorded *no casualties*.

She shuffled the last foot of the ledge and stepped onto the widening rock and stood shaking, and pulled Noor across, and held her a moment longer than the schedule allowed.

* * *

They cleared the headland as the sun came up properly, and for twenty minutes the world was almost kind.

The far side of the point sloped gentler, a stair of terraces descending toward the hidden cove where the smugglers' dory waited — Elias had arranged it through channels he wouldn't name, payment promised to a man neither of them would meet, and Wren had stopped asking questions whose answers she couldn't afford. The wind still slept. The light came low and gold across the water and lit the ledges behind them so that Wren could see the whole route they'd survived, black and gleaming, and ahead the open channel, and beyond the channel the mainland hills standing up blue out of the haze.

Mainland. It looked close. It always looked close.

"We're going to make it," Noor said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't quite a statement either. It was a child testing a plank.

"We're going to try well," Wren said. "Making it isn't ours to promise. Trying well is." She heard how it sounded — the same thing she'd been saying to herself for weeks — and put her hand briefly on Noor's head. "But yes. I think we make it."

They came down the last terrace to the cove at half past nine by the lighthouse clock Wren carried in her head, and the dory lay where it was supposed to lie, tucked under the overhang of the final shelf, dry-hulled on the shingle, oars shipped, a scrap of red cloth on the stem marking it as theirs. The tide was fully out. The window, by Elias's reckoning, had perhaps forty minutes left in it — time to launch, row the channel while it stayed shallow-calm, and round the far marker before the flood tide came back over the ledges and closed the path behind them forever, or nearly forever, or at least long enough to matter.

Wren stood on the shingle and did the last inventory. Child: present. Documents: against her body, cords checked twice this morning and once again now with her fingers under her coat. Food, water, matches: in the dory's stern locker. Boat: sound. Wind: —

Wind: stirring.

Not rising. Not yet. But the flat lead of the cove had begun to move, small cat's-paws chasing each other toward shore, and the ragged high cloud had thickened in the west, and somewhere beyond the edge of seeing the weather had remembered them.

Elias had said it plainly, the night he drew the route in spilled tea on the kitchen table: *The calm is borrowed. Storms like these, they don't stop — they circle. This one's been sitting out past the banks feeding on cold water, and when it comes back it'll come back all at once, no politeness about it. Your job is to be gone before it arrives.*

Wren pushed the dory down the shingle. The hull ground on the pebbles and then floated, and the float felt like a sentence finishing. She handed Noor in over the bow and took the oars.

They were a hundred yards off the shingle when the wind arrived.

It came down off the western hills in one long exhale, flattening the cat's-paws, combing the water into long gray lines, and Wren felt it hit her back like a hand — not hostile yet, just vast, just early. Forty minutes. Maybe thirty. The channel ahead still lay in shadowed calm, but the whitecaps were coming, marching down the wind like outriders.

"Row," she said, and Noor, who had been taught to row since she was six, took the second pair without being told, and they pulled.

The dory leapt. It was a good boat, light and true, and it ran down the channel with the following wind pushing them faster than Wren liked, because fast was fine on flat water and the channel would not stay flat. She steered by feel and by memory of Elias's instructions — *keep the black rock on your left shoulder until it lines up with the notch in the hills, then turn south* — and behind them, when she permitted herself one glance, the headland stood black against the brightening west, and above it the cloud had begun to organize itself into something with intent.

Halfway down the channel the first real gusts found them, ragged and cold, shoving the bow. Noor's arms pumped steadily, her jaw set, her eyes forward, and Wren thought: *she is her father's daughter, she rows like him, she holds like him,* and then made herself stop thinking anything at all except the next stroke.

They rounded the black rock. The notch in the hills lined up. Wren turned them south, and the open water opened ahead of them — two miles of it, then five, then the mainland landing where Pell's cousin would supposedly be waiting with a cart and a silence of his own. Supposedly. Everything now was supposedly. Everything now was borrowed time and other people's courage, and the only thing that was hers, irrevocably hers, was strapped against her chest, riding under her heart like a second child.

The wind steadied and strengthened, a long sustained push from the northwest, and Wren realized with a cold clarity that it was both enemy and ally — it would raise the sea, but it was also driving them toward the mainland, filling their sails if they'd had sails, hurrying them home. She shipped the steering oar and set the blade and rode it.

Behind them, Juniper Point grew smaller. She could see the lighthouse still, white against the darkening headland, dark-eyed, lampless, telling the truth by its silence. Somewhere under it, in a house with empty shelves, the life she'd built was packed into a few bags and a decision.

"Hold on," she said to Noor, as the first true sea began to build under the hull — long, gray-backed, rolling down from the open Pacific with the storm's signature already in it. "Hold the thwart. Don't let go even if I tell you to."

"Especially if you tell me to," Noor said, and gripped.

Ahead, the mainland hills stood in the haze, and between them and the hills the sea rose and rose, and behind them the storm came on like a door closing, and Wren bent her back and pulled.
