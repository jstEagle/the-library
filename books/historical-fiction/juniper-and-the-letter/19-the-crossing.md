## 19. The Crossing

The wind found them a mile off the headland.

Wren felt it before she heard it — a change in the dory's motion, the way the hull began to lift and drop instead of slide. The water beneath them went from gray to a deeper, meaner color, like cloth darkening where it has been soaked through.

"Sit low," she said to Noor. "Hold the thwart with both hands."

Noor did not ask why. She was eleven years old and had grown up on this water; she knew what a change in the sea's voice meant the way town children knew a change in their mother's tone. She slid down off the midship bench onto the floorboards, wrapped her arms around her knees, and pressed her back against the ballast of wrapped documents they had spent three days waterproofing in oilcloth and tar.

That was the arrangement, and it had seemed sound at dawn: the packet lashed inside the boat, the girl lashed inside the packet's shadow, the woman at the oars holding all of it against the weather.

But an hour before they launched, alone in the lantern room while Noor slept, Wren had made a second arrangement, one she had not spoken aloud even to herself until now. She had taken the letters — Elias's packet, Toma's logbook pages, Pell's original manifests, every sheet of it — and bound them flat against her body with the canvas belt she wore under her coat, the one that held up the trousers Toma had left too big for her. The papers sat against her ribs like a second sternum. If the boat failed, the boat would fail first.

She had told herself it was prudence. Papers kept closest are papers best guarded. But there was a colder arithmetic underneath, and she had let it stand without examination: things lashed to a swimmer float. Things stowed in a hull sink.

The wind arrived properly then, a long shove from the southwest that turned the chop into short steep seas with white shredding off their crests. The dory took the first one on the quarter and slewed. Wren braced her feet against the floorboards and pulled, matching the oars to the waves rather than fighting them, the way Toma had taught her before he taught her anything else about boats: *You can't argue with a sea, Wren. You can only agree with it faster than it does.*

"Mama." Noor's voice, small but level, from down among the wrappings. "It's coming."

Wren looked over her shoulder. Behind them, across two miles of blackening water, the storm stood up out of the southwest like a wall being built by enormous hands — a curtain of rain with a smothered brightness inside it, lightning working somewhere deep in its throat. The calm window that had opened at low tide, that Elias had sworn would hold until noon, was closing four hours early.

There was no turning back. The path around the headland flooded within minutes of the tide's turn; they had watched the smugglers' road go underwater behind them as they rowed away from it. There was only forward, and forward meant the open crossing to the mainland landing, three miles of nothing between Juniper Point and the marine board's jurisdiction.

"Hold on to something," Wren said. "Not the wrappings. The boat."

Noor's hands found the gunwale above her head. Wren saw the knuckles whiten and hated herself for every mile of water she had put between her daughter and dry land, and knew also that dry land held Halvard, and Crane, and a village sealed tighter than the bridge, and that there had been no safe choice since October. There had only been choices you could live with afterward, or ones you couldn't.

The first real wave hit them abeam.

* * *

The dory was eighteen feet of cedar over oak, built by Toma's father, patched by Toma, patched again by Wren each spring with whatever the winter had left her time to use. She knew its sounds the way she knew Noor's breathing at night. So when the hull spoke — a high wooden groan from below the waterline, forward of the boards where Noor crouched — Wren heard it as clearly as a scream.

She looked down. Water was coming through the seams. Not gushing; seeping, rising, finding the joints the cold had opened. The storm's sudden pressure had worked the planking like a man working a loose tooth.

"Bail," she said. "The tin pail, by your knee. Bail like I taught you, small fast scoops, don't try to throw it far."

Noor found the pail and began. The rhythm of it steadied them both for a while — scoop, toss, scoop, toss — and Wren pulled for the mainland with everything her shoulders had left, and the sea came up to meet them, and the wall of weather behind them came down like something deciding.

Rain hit first, horizontal, hard enough to sting. Then the world contracted to twenty feet of visibility: gray water, gray air, the bow of her own boat, the sound of her own breath. Somewhere beyond the rain the lightning moved closer, and each flash showed her the same picture — steep seas, no land in any direction, her daughter bailing in the bottom of a boat that was filling faster than a child could empty it.

"Wren Nakamura," she said aloud, to no one, to the water, to the version of herself that had once believed survival was the hard part and everything after would be simple. "Look at your life."

The wave that ended it was not the biggest. That was the insult of it. It was a middle-sized sea, one of a hundred they had already taken, but it caught the dory beam-on as she misread its timing in the rain-hiss, and the boat went over sideways with a slowness that felt almost courteous, and the cold closed over Wren's head like a door.

* * *

Underwater, everything was quiet and green-gray and full of her own hair.

The belt pulled at her ribs. The oars were gone from her hands — she didn't remember letting go of them — and above her, dimly, the overturned hull glowed like the underside of a lamp. *The light,* some old reflexive part of her thought, absurdly. *Follow the light.* And then her boots found the bottom, shallow here, sandbar shoals running out from the mainland shore, and she pushed, and broke the surface gasping into rain.

"DORY," she shouted. "NOOR."

The hull lay forty feet away, keel up, turning slowly in the troughs. Clinging to it, one arm hooked over the keel, hair streaming black across her face, was Noor.

"I have the pail," Noor called back, and the absurdity of it — the child announcing her salvage, the tin pail still hooked over her wrist like a toy — cracked something open in Wren's chest that was half terror and half a fierce, scalding love.

"Kick," Wren called. "Don't climb, it'll roll. Kick toward me and hold on."

She swam. The water was the kind of cold that stops being pain after a minute and becomes something worse, a general slowing, a tax collected from every muscle. The documents against her ribs rode high and light, exactly as she had known they would, and she was suddenly grateful and ashamed in the same breath: she had made herself the life vest for paper. Her daughter had been given a pail.

They met at the hull. Wren got Noor's arms repositioned, got her own arm over the keel beside her, and for a moment they just hung there in the rain, cheek to wet cheek, the capsized boat between them and the worst of the seas.

"The papers," Noor said into her ear. "Are they—"

"On my body. Under my coat. They're fine."

"Good," Noor said, with a child's flat practicality, and then began, very quietly, to cry — not from fear, Wren understood, watching her, but from the sheer effort of having been brave in the correct order. First the pail, then the hull, then the crying. She had raised her well. God help her, she had raised her well.

The dory was swamped and would never be righted in this sea; they both knew it without saying. But it floated, keel-up, and a floating thing can be pushed. The sandbar shoals ran toward the mainland landing, and the landing light — a low harbor beacon, nothing like hers, but a light — burned somewhere ahead through the rain, if Pell's reports were true and the beacon hadn't gone dark with everything else Crane had let rot.

"Kick," Wren said. "We push it home."

* * *

It took them an hour to cover the last half mile.

Wren measured it later in small units of endurance, because the whole was unbearable: one more kick, one more push of the hull, one more wave that lifted them hopefully toward the shore and set them down again in the same gray nowhere. The cold stopped being a sensation and became a fact of her body, like age. Her fingers stopped reporting. Twice she caught herself dreaming with her eyes open — the lantern room, warm; Toma at the table with his logbook, writing something, always writing — and hauled herself back by reciting the manifest numbers she'd memorized, dates and tonnages and the names of dead men, because the names were the cargo now and cargo must be counted.

Once, Noor slipped. Just her hand, numb past feeling, sliding off the wet keel, and Wren caught her wrist and held it in a grip she would not be able to close for two days afterward, and said, very calmly, in the voice she used for the logbook entries:

"No. Not today. Today we arrive."

Noor nodded and kicked.

The sandbar found their feet before they found the shore — a sudden firmness underfoot, then shallows, then the long dragging walk up through the eelgrass with the swamped dory grinding behind them, and then the shingle of the mainland landing, honest gravel, and Wren's knees giving out onto it with the girl still gripped against her side.

They lay there a moment, the two of them, tangled and streaming, while the storm walked ashore behind them and began tearing at the spruce line like a creditor going through drawers.

Then Wren sat up, because there was a figure coming down the landing ramp against the wind, lantern swinging, and for one lurching instant she thought — Halvard, he guessed, he's come to meet the boat — and she reached for Noor and started to rise.

But the figure stopped ten feet off and lifted the lantern, and the light fell on a face seamed and sagging and familiar: Mr. Pell, harbor master, in a slicker two sizes too large, staring at the wreck of them washed up on his gravel with an expression Wren had never seen him wear — not guilt, not pity, but something older than either. Recognition. As if he had been standing at this landing for hours, in weather no sane man stood in, waiting for precisely this arrival, and was ashamed to find he wasn't surprised.

He looked at the two of them. He looked out at the capsized dory grinding on the shingle. Then his eyes went, unmistakably, deliberately, to the shape under Wren's coat — the flat rigid band of oilcloth and canvas riding against her ribs, soaked but sealed, buoyant, intact.

"You carried it," Pell said. His voice cracked on the second word. "Through all that. You carried it on your own body."

"It carried me," Wren said, which was closer to the truth, and could not explain it further, and did not try.

Pell took off his slicker, knelt, and put it around Noor's shoulders. When he straightened, the old harbor master was gone from his face and something harder had taken its place — the man who had signed false manifests for nine years and had evidently decided, sometime during this storm, at this gravel landing, that the account was closing.

"The marine board sits tomorrow morning," he said. "Eight o'clock, the customs house. Crane's lawyer arrived on yesterday's steamer. He thinks the board is his." Pell picked up Wren's arm and put it over his shoulder, taking her weight without asking, and began walking her up the ramp toward the lights of the town. "Come and show him what he's wrong about."

Behind them the storm took the landing sign off its hinges, and the rain came down on the whole coast at once, and Wren Nakamura — keeper of the Juniper Point light, widow, courier, witness — walked out of the sea carrying the truth of eleven drowned men against her ribs, into the last night before testimony.
