## 20. Testimony

The marine board met in the customs house at Port Anselm, a building that had been built to look older than it was—granite columns, brass rails, a dome with a patina of green that made the whole thing seem like something the sea had already claimed and given back. Wren had passed it a hundred times in her life without once going inside. Now she came up the steps with salt still stiff in the seams of her coat and a canvas satchel pressed to her hip like a child she was afraid someone might ask her to hand over.

They had taken the packet from her at the door—a clerk with kind eyes and ink-stained fingers—and for a moment she nearly fought him for it. Nineteen days she had carried those letters. Lashed to her ribs in the swells. Dried against a stove while Noor slept. Read so many times she could have recited them blind. To let go of them now felt like letting go of Toma all over again.

"They'll be returned," the clerk said gently. "Entered as evidence, then returned."

"Then I'll wait," Wren said. "I've gotten good at waiting."

He looked at her a moment longer—at the wind-burned face, the hands that wouldn't quite unclench—and nodded, and gave her a receipt written out in triplicate, and told her the hearing would begin at nine.

It was eight minutes past nine when they called her name.

* * *

The board sat five men behind a raised bench, and none of them were from the coast. She could tell by their shoes. They had the clean, unhurried look of people whose weather happened indoors. The chairman introduced himself as Magistrate Corren, and asked her to state her name and station for the record.

"Wren Nakamura," she said. "Keeper of Juniper Point light."

There was a small stirring in the gallery at that—the word *keeper* carrying differently here than it did at home—and Corren leaned forward over his notes.

"Mistress Nakamura. You've come a long way in poor season to make an accusation."

"I've come a long way in poor season because no one else could come at all," Wren said. "That's part of what you're going to hear about. So I'd rather not waste your time being sorry for the inconvenience."

Someone in the gallery made a sound that might have been a laugh bitten in half. Corren's mouth tightened—not displeasure, exactly. Recalculation.

"Proceed," he said.

So she proceeded. She laid it out plainly, the way she'd write it in the log: the wreck on the ninth of November, the boat the cannery's manifest called *lost with all hands*, and Elias Brandt alive below her light with broken ribs and a story. The letters he carried—correspondence between Aldous Crane's office and an insurance adjustor in Port Anselm, dates altered, crew lists rewritten after the fact, wrecks reported as accidents when the boats had been sent out overloaded and unseaworthy into weather every other captain refused. And underneath all of it, one letter naming the *Cormorant*—Toma's boat—in a hand she had hoped never to see again outside her own memory.

She read that one aloud herself. Her voice held. She had expected it not to, and when it did hold she felt nothing about it at all, which was its own strange discovery—that there are things too heavy even for grief to lift.

When she finished, the room was very quiet. One of the board members, a thin man with spectacles, asked how she had come into possession of the documents.

"A dying man put them in my hands," Wren said, "and asked me to carry them past three checkpoints, and I did. Two of the men who let me pass will answer for it themselves if this board is willing to hear them. The third—" she paused "—the third tried to stop me."

"You're referring to Halvard Senn, foreman at the Crane cannery."

"He's under arrest," the thin man said. "As of yesterday morning."

Wren hadn't known that. She kept it off her face. There would be time later to decide what she felt about Halvard—about a man who had searched her dory with cold thoroughness and then, at the far landing, stood with his lantern lowered and said only, *I never saw you. Get gone.* Men were rarely one thing. That was the whole trouble with the world.

Corren consulted his papers. "The board has also received testimony by wire from Harbor Master Ambrose Pell, regarding falsified manifests signed under duress over a period of eleven years. And we have received—" he glanced toward the doors "—apparently, several dozen more witnesses than we scheduled for."

* * *

They had come overnight, the families. Word traveled faster than any dory: a fishwife's network, a cousin's cousin, a letter slipped into a supply crate. When the doors opened for the afternoon session, the gallery filled with faces Wren knew from funerals—from casseroles left on doorsteps, from the particular silence of a house where a chair would never be sat in again.

Sigrid Holm came first. Her husband had been on the *Maren Lee*, two winters back, the loss Wren had watched from the gallery rail of her own lighthouse—the flare she'd seen, the boat she'd been too far and too slow to reach. Sigrid testified that the *Maren Lee* had gone out that morning with a cracked hull plank patched in tar, that she had begged Crane's dock boss to delay the run, and been told the schedule didn't bend for weather or wives. She said it flatly, without tears, the way women on the coast said true things, and Wren sat in the witness row with her fists in her coat pockets and thought: *this is what my voice sounds like from the outside.*

Then the Anders boy, seventeen now, who had survived the *Petrel* because he'd been left ashore with fever, and who described the insurance paperwork his mother had been shown afterward—a list of crew names, his father among them, dated in a month the *Petrel* had been tied up at the dock. A clerk's error, Crane's office had called it. A clerical error, repeated across four wrecks in six years.

One by one they stood. Some wept. Some shook. One old man simply read his son's name into the record and sat down, and the reading of it—one name, nothing more—did more damage than an hour of argument could have.

Crane's lawyers objected often. Overloaded cargo was standard practice, they said; the sea was dangerous; grief sought scapegoats. But objections need somewhere solid to stand, and the letters kept taking the ground out from under them. By mid-afternoon the board had subpoenaed Crane's ledgers, his manifests, his adjustor's file. By late afternoon, word came down through the gallery like current through a tide pool: the bank in Port Anselm had opened Crane's accounts at the board's request, and the accounts had stopped answering questions.

* * *

Wren saw him only once. Late in the day, between sessions, in the corridor outside the hearing room. He stood near the window with two lawyers murmuring at either shoulder, and he looked—smaller. That was the thing she hadn't predicted. On Juniper Point, Aldous Crane had been weather itself: wages, debt, the price of fish, whether a family ate. Here, in gray daylight, with his collar slightly crooked, he was just a heavyset man of sixty whose hands were soft and whose eyes went around the corridor measuring exits.

He saw her. Crossed the floor. His lawyers trailed him like gulls after a boat.

"Mistress Nakamura." His voice was still the voice that had filled the cannery hall, pitched lower now, meant only for her. "Whatever you think you've done here—"

"I know what I've done," Wren said. "I carried mail."

"You'll ruin this town." He said it quietly, reasonably, as if confiding arithmetic. "Half of Anselm County eats because of me. You'll burn down the village to warm your own hands."

"The village has been burning for years," Wren said. "You just owned the matches."

His face went through something then—she would remember it long after, try to name it during sleepless hours and finally settle on *recognition*. Not of her. Of the truth. For one unguarded second Aldous Crane heard what she said and knew it was accurate, the way a captain knows a reef is where the chart says it is.

Then the mask reassembled, and he turned away, and one of his lawyers said something about proceedings and slander and appeals, and Wren let it wash past her. She had stood in storms before. This was only another kind of noise.

* * *

The verdicts did not come that day, or the next. These things moved like glaciers, Corren warned her—slowly, and leaving the landscape changed behind them. But smaller things broke loose daily. Crane's fishing fleet was impounded pending inspection. Two of his clerks came forward voluntarily, each blaming the other. The insurance company filed suit. And on the fourth morning, the *Anselm Courier* ran the story above the fold with the letters quoted at length, and the headline was simply a list of ship names, and beneath the names, the number of dead, and beneath the numbers, one line: *All preventable. All profitable.*

Wren read it standing in the boarding house kitchen with tea going cold in her hand, and Noor beside her on a stool, and Marta across the table with her arms folded tight, as if holding herself together at the seams.

"It doesn't feel like I thought it would," Wren said finally.

"How did you think it would feel?"

"Like setting a bone. Something clicking right." She set the paper down. "It feels like opening a wall and finding rot all the way through. Necessary. But you can't unknow what the house looks like inside."

Marta was quiet a long moment. Outside, the harbor bells rang the half hour, and gulls argued over something on the pier. "Elias is asking for you," she said at last. "The hospital sent word this morning. He's sitting up. He wants to know if the letters got through."

Wren looked out the window toward the hospital roof, gray against the gray sky.

"Tell him they got through," she said. Then, correcting herself, because she had spent a season learning the cost of softened words: "No. I'll tell him myself. He earned more than a message."

* * *

The board recalled her one final time—not for testimony, but for the return of her evidence, and for a statement Corren read aloud with visible discomfort, as though praise were a procedural irregularity. The board wished to note, he said, the extraordinary service rendered by the keeper of Juniper Point light; the matter of the false casualty reports would be referred for criminal prosecution; and the keeper should understand that her lamp's logbook had been entered into evidence as well—her own records, kept faithfully through years of wrecks, matching or contradicting every official manifest. Her handwriting had helped convict a fleet.

"Your log recorded casualties the manifests denied," Corren said. "Flares seen. Boats overdue. You wrote it all down, year after year, knowing it matched nothing anyone official cared to check. May I ask why?"

Wren considered lying, or at least decorating. She found she didn't want to.

"Because a log is a promise," she said. "You write down what the light saw, whether anyone reads it or not. Someday somebody might need it to be true. It turns out someday had a name, but I couldn't know that when I was writing it."

Corren wrote something down. Whatever it was, it stayed in his notes instead of the record, and she decided she didn't mind. Truth didn't need her name attached to work.

* * *

They stayed through the week's end. On Saturday, Sigrid Holm found her on the boarding house steps and sat down beside her without asking, the way coastal women do, and they watched the harbor traffic together for a while.

"They're saying Crane will stand trial by summer," Sigrid said. "And that the fleet crews will get back wages out of the seized accounts, if the lawyers leave anything in the pot."

"And the boats?"

"Gone, mostly. Sold or rotten." Sigrid turned her ring around her finger—an old wedding band worn thin as wire. "You know what nobody's saying? What happens to us now. Sixty widows in this county, and the man who made most of them is finished, and it feels—" she hunted for it "—it feels like the end of a storm. Everything quiet and wrong-shaped and scattered on the beach."

Wren nodded slowly. "Vindication isn't the same as putting things back," she said. "I keep thinking about the village. About the families who hated me for months and were right to be afraid, and were also wrong, and both of those are going to sit at the same tables with me when the bridge opens."

"Will you go back?" Sigrid asked. "To the Point?"

"The light needs keeping," Wren said, as if that answered everything, and maybe it did, because Sigrid smiled for the first time in Wren's memory and squeezed her hand hard and left.

Afterward Wren sat alone in the cold until Noor came out with a blanket and wrapped it around them both, and asked the question children always arrive at eventually:

"Is it over?"

Wren thought about it honestly, because Noor had earned honest answers at cost.

"The trial isn't. The mending won't be, not for years. But the carrying part—" she pulled her daughter close, feeling the small weight of her, the one inventory item that mattered above all others "—the carrying part is done. We got it across."

From the north, over the water, came the first fair wind in weeks. It smelled of ice going soft. Somewhere beyond the headland, the tidal bridge was waiting for spring, and a village was waiting for its reckoning, and a dark tower waited at the edge of it all with an empty lamp and a blank page.

"We'll know we're truly home," Wren said, "when we can see our own light again."

Noor laughed. "Ours doesn't shine yet. Papa's repairs—"

"Then we'll fix that," Wren said, and stood, and went in to pack.
