## 22. Juniper Light

The lamp had been dark for eleven days.

Wren felt the darkness the way other people felt a fever — as heat behind the eyes, an itch in the hands. Eleven days since the workmen from the mainland had come over the reopened bridge with their crates of new brass and their new glass and their city manners, eleven days of scaffolding in the lantern room and canvas tarps snapping like flags of some foreign country. Eleven nights of Noor asking at bedtime whether the ships could see them, and Wren answering honestly, because she had promised herself she would never again answer any question dishonestly: *No, love. Not yet.*

But tonight was the night.

She climbed the stairs at half past four, before the light went out of the sky, because relighting was not a thing you rushed. It was a ritual, and rituals were what she had left of Toma that no board of inquiry or insurance man or newspaper reporter could take from her. She carried the things she always carried, measured out in her two hands: the matches in their tin, the oil can filled to its line, a rag for the lens. The inventory of small things. She had learned it as a bride, standing in this same stairwell with her arms full and her heart full and no idea what either would cost her.

Now she knew. Now she carried it anyway.

The workmen had done honest work; she'd checked every seam herself, twice, because trusting things to have been done right was a luxury the sea had cured her of long ago. The new lens sat in its housing like a great clear eye, and through it the harbor spread out below — gray water going silver at the edges, the tidal bridge open for traffic again, the first trucks in five months crossing it with their headlamps on even in daylight, as if they didn't trust the crossing either.

And there — she stopped with her hand on the lens frame. There was the cannery.

It stood empty. That was still a thing she had to look at twice. The windows were dark, the dock bare of boats, and the crane — Aldous Crane's own machine, the great iron arm he had been so proud of — stood folded against the sky like a sleeping bird. The marine board had sealed it in February pending trial. Some mornings, when the fog came in off the point, the whole complex looked less like a factory than like something being slowly returned to the weather.

She did not feel triumph looking at it. She had expected to, back in those weeks when rage was the only coal in her stove. What she felt instead was closer to the way she felt about wrecks themselves: sobered, attentive, unwilling to look away. Men would go to trial in the spring because of papers she had carried across open water lashed to her body. Some of those men were neighbors. One of them had eaten supper at her table, once, when Toma was alive. Truth did not sort itself into clean piles. It came up in nets tangled with everything else, and you sat on the dock picking it apart until your fingers bled, and then you did it again tomorrow.

*Enough*, she told herself, and turned to the work.

* * *

Noor came up at five, as she always did now, without being called. Nine years old and she climbed the tower stairs like she'd been born to it, which in a sense she had — born two rooms below this light, in the winter her father's boat first went out under Crane's flag. She had her coat buttoned wrong. Wren rebuttoned it without comment, the way her own mother had done, and Noor let her, which was its own kind of treaty between them.

"Did you bring it?" Noor asked.

"I brought it."

"The good matches?"

"There are no bad matches."

"Papa said there were." Noor took the tin anyway and held it against her chest with both hands, solemn as an acolyte. "He said some matches were damp and liars."

Wren laughed before she could stop herself, and the laugh cracked something open in her chest that had been healing all spring — not a wound reopening but a wound *airing*. Toma's voice coming out of their daughter's mouth, unbidden, accurate. He had said exactly that. He had been particular about matches the way only a man who has stood in wet boots waiting for dry ones can be.

"Then we'll use the liars first," Wren said, "and save the honest ones for the wick."

They prepared the lamp together. This too had become ritual, divided along lines neither of them had ever discussed: Wren filled the reservoir and checked the pressure gauge while Noor trimmed the new wick square with the little brass cutters, tongue between her teeth, taking three times longer than necessary because precision was her offering. Outside the glass the sky was doing its evening arithmetic, subtracting blue, adding rose. Far out, past the point, Wren could see a trawler working the banks — one of Marta's brother's boats, running again now that the crews weren't afraid of what might be written down about their voyages afterward.

Marta. Wren set that name down inside herself, gently, where she kept it these days. They spoke twice a week now instead of every day. Some debts were paid in distance. Marta had kept her bargain — silence after the testimony, and then, when the subpoenas started landing on doorsteps all down the row, something harder: she had stood up in the hearing hall and answered every question they asked her, though her family's debt died unpaid on Crane's ruined books. *You chose your side slow*, Wren had told her once, angry. *I chose it at all*, Marta had answered, and that was the ledger they lived on now, and it balanced, more or less, the way human ledgers do.

"Ready," Noor said.

"Together."

They struck the match together, Noor's hand over Wren's, and touched the flame to the wick. It caught. The mantle bloomed white, and the great lens gathered the light and threw it out over the water in one long turning breath, and Juniper Point was a light again.

Wren watched the beam swing out and around and come back, patient as a heartbeat. Somewhere out there the trawler would see it and correct her course by it, and the skipper would never know whose hands had lit it, or what those hands had carried, or what they had refused. That was right. A light wasn't supposed to be a story. It was just supposed to be true.

* * *

Later, when the room was warm and the lamp ran steady and Noor had gone down the stairs to bed protesting only mildly, Wren opened the log.

The old logbook lay on the desk beside the new blank one — Toma's, recovered from the wreck file in March when the board released what evidence it no longer needed, his handwriting slanting across pages he had written while planning, all unknown to her, the voyage that killed him. She had read it four times now. Each time she found something new. This time, thumbing through, she found a margin note she had missed: a date, and beside it, in his cramped hand, *told H. we don't sail in this — he says sail or the debt sails instead.*

H. Halvard. Who had spent the spring giving testimony of his own, gray-faced, methodical, naming dates and orders and sums. She did not forgive him. She was not sure forgiveness was hers to give — that belonged to the widows of three crews, and some of them had made it plain they would carry their anger to their graves. But she understood him now, and understanding was its own uncomfortable weight, and she had learned to carry those too.

She dipped the pen. Opened the new log to the first page. And began.

*Juniper Point Light, April 14th. Relit this evening after repairs, 5:47 p.m., fair visibility, wind northwest light. Keeper Wren Nakamura. Assistant keeper Noor Nakamura, aged nine, who trimmed the wick square and true.*

Noor would find that in twenty years and pretend to be embarrassed. Wren wrote it anyway.

Then she turned to the matter that had waited all winter to be written, the reason the new log had sat blank for eleven days while she composed sentences in the dark behind her eyes. The board had published its findings in March — falsified wreck reports, insurance claims on vessels ordered out in known weather, the names of the lost corrected at last in government ink. But government ink was not the same as memory. Memory needed a keeper. That was, when you stripped everything else away, the whole of the job.

*Being a true account of the losses of the winter of 1934–35,* she wrote, *as witnessed from this station, correcting the record previously kept by others.*

She wrote the *Marigold*, lost December third with six souls, reported by the cannery as lost in international waters to dodge the liability, actually lost two miles off Juniper Head within sight of this light. She wrote the *Cormorant*, and the night Elias came ashore below the tower with the packet sewn into his coat, and how the official log of that same week had recorded, in someone else's hand, *no casualties.* She wrote the *Prosper J.*, which had been Toma's boat, and she wrote the truth of it plainly — ordered out against warnings, insured above her worth, her loss claimed before her crew was even given up as drowned — and her pen did not shake until the last line, and even then only a little.

*I knew him,* she wrote of Toma. *He would have brought the truth in himself if the sea had let him reach the mainland. I finished his voyage. Keeper's note: the fault was not in the boat, nor in the man, nor in the sea alone.*

She sat back. The lamp turned overhead, steady, unhurried. Down below, the bridge lamps burned in a string across the channel like a rosary counted out, and somewhere among the village lights Marta was putting her children to bed, and Pell — who had retired the week after testifying, spending his last act of authority on his own resignation letter — was doing whatever old men do when they finally put down a weight they've carried thirty years. Probably fishing. Probably badly, on purpose.

Elias was in the city, testifying still; his lungs had mended enough for courtrooms if not for decks. He wrote her every few weeks, short letters with long silences between lines, and last month he had asked, carefully, whether there would be work at a light station for a man who owed her his life and meant to spend some years paying it. She had not answered yet. She thought she would write yes.

The wind shifted a point, as wind does. Wren checked the pressure, wiped a fingerprint from the lens glass with her rag, and stood a moment in the turning light with her eyes closed, listening to the machinery of the place breathe.

There would be hard years yet. The trial would drag into summer; the village would stay poor longer than anyone deserved; there were men in town who still wouldn't meet her eye, and might never. Noor would grow up knowing things children shouldn't have to know, and Wren would spend the rest of her life negotiating that border, country by country, year by year, the way they had begun to do already on winter evenings over cocoa and one letter at a time.

But the light was lit. The record was true. The page in front of her held the worst of it, written out at last in her own steady hand, and beneath it the book stayed open — hundreds of blank pages, whole seasons of weather nobody had seen yet, ships not built, storms not imagined, all of it waiting to be told straight.

Wren Nakamura capped the ink, banked the lamp glass against the draft, and went down the stairs to sleep, leaving the light behind her turning, turning, keeping its faithful watch over the honest dark.
