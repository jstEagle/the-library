## 1. The Weight of Small Things

Everything Freya Coldharbour owned could be carried in two trips.

She had counted, the way other women counted their years or their prayers. One trip for the tools: three files of graduated cut, a pair of pliers with jaws worn to a whisper, the burnisher, the pin-vise, six screwdrivers ground down from nails by her own hand when her master declared that a clockmaker who could not make her own tools had no business touching anyone's clocks. The second trip was for everything that was not steel. Her mother's thimble, brass, dented at the crown where a horse had once stepped on it and spared the hand inside it. A psalter she never read but could not discard, because the margin held her mother's handwriting, which was the only proof remaining on earth that her mother had ever held a pen. And the papers.

The papers were the heaviest thing she owned, though they weighed less than the thimble.

They lived beneath a loose floorboard by the hearth, wrapped in oilcloth: journeyman's papers certifying one *Frederick Coldharbour*, clock-finisher, seven years apprenticed to one *Master Hobb of Clerkenwell*, who did not exist, in a workshop that had never employed him. She had paid a man in Whitechapel four months' wages for those pages, and she had checked every letter of them twice, because a woman who wished to work at benches rather than bedsides needed to be someone else, and being someone else required precision above all things. A forged hand was like an escapement—one flaw in the rhythm and the whole mechanism stopped.

She was thinking about this, kneeling on the cold boards with the oilcloth half unwrapped, not because she meant to flee but because it was Tuesday, and Tuesdays she took the papers out and read them, the way a sinner reads scripture, reminding herself what she had chosen to become and what it had cost. Outside the window, London went about its wet November business. Carts groaned through the mire of Cowcross Street. Somewhere a pie-seller sang her wares off-key. The workshop behind Freya smelled of oil and brass filings and the tallow candle burning low because Master Pettifer begrudged every farthing that did not go into his own pocket.

*Frederick Coldharbour*, she read, in a hand that mimicked hers so well it frightened her sometimes. *Duly bound and discharged.* Duly. There was a word that had never once applied to any hour of her life.

The knock came at the street door—three raps, unhurried, the knock of a man paid by the delivery and not by the haste.

She was alone in the shop. Master Pettifer had gone to Guildhall to argue about an unpaid invoice for a town-house regulator, and would return drunk on other men's claret and self-importance, if he returned at all. Freya slid the floorboard back into place, rubbed the dust of the gap across her knuckles so her hands looked like working hands, which they were, and opened the door a hand's width.

The courier was a boy with a face full of freckles and rain. He thrust out a packet wrapped in brown paper and tied with string.

"Frederick Coldharbour?" he said.

"That's me," said Freya, in the flat voice she kept for the name.

"Then you're to sign."

* * *

She signed. She always signed; that was the joke at the center of her life, small enough to carry. The boy left, and she stood in the doorway of the shop with the parcel in both hands, telling herself it was work—some commission forwarded from Pettifer, a church clock gone erratic, a gentleman's repeater with a cracked balance spring. Work came by post often enough. Work was safe. Work was what she wanted.

She carried it to the bench anyway, set it under the lamp, and cut the string with the smallest of her knives.

Inside was a single sheet, folded twice, and a second paper bearing a seal she recognized without ever having seen one up close: the anchor and star of Thornfield Observatory, pressed in red wax thick as a coin. The sealed paper was formal enough—a summons, written in a secretary's copperplate, requiring *the bearer, journeyman clock-finisher Frederick Coldharbour, whose competence has been represented to us by persons of standing, to attend without delay at Thornfield Observatory, there to undertake the restoration of the great astronomical clock, wages and lodging to be provided for the duration of the works.*

It was the kind of letter that changed lives, and it was not the letter she was reading.

The letter she was reading was the folded sheet beneath it, unsigned, unsealed, written in a hand she knew better than her mother's.

*The escapement is lying. Come and see.*

That was all. Five words, if you did not count the small cipher beneath them—a row of numerals and tick-marks that meant nothing to a stranger and everything to anyone Elias Vane had taught. He had invented it for his apprentices, a private shorthand for gear ratios and pendulum lengths, so that their notebooks, if stolen, would read as arithmetic nonsense. Every apprentice had learned it at the same bench, in the same week, chalking examples on slate until the logic lived in the fingers.

Freya had been the last apprentice to learn it. That had been nine years ago, in another workshop, before the fire.

She sat down without meaning to. The stool caught the bench edge and stopped her.

Elias Vane was dead. It was official; it was printed; she had read it herself in the Gazette two years past, taking tea she then let go cold: *drowned in the Thames near Greenwich, the celebrated horologist, keeper of the great clock at Thornfield Observatory.* A waterman had identified the body. There had been no family to claim it. She remembered reading the notice three times, hunting between the lines for some error, some misprint, some clerical accident that would give her back the man—and finding nothing, because the world did not make such mistakes, only clocks did, and clocks could be corrected.

She had grieved him the way she grieved everything: privately, thoroughly, and while continuing to file brass. Grief, like everything else in her life, had been made portable. Folded small. Put away beneath the floorboards of her heart with the thimble and the false papers.

Now a dead man's hand had reached out of the Gazette and delivered five words to her master's shop.

*The escapement is lying.*

An escapement could not lie. That was the first law of the craft, older than guilds, older than any charter. A wheel train might be badly cut, poorly oiled, crudely assembled—but it could not lie, because it did not know how. Steel and brass had no ambitions. When a clock told the wrong time, the fault lay with a human hand, and the hand could always be found if one traced the wheels backward, tooth by tooth, to the moment of interference.

Which meant the sentence was not about horology at all. It was a message in the only language Elias had trusted absolutely—the language of mechanisms—which was to say, a warning dressed as a lesson, exactly as he had dressed every lesson of her apprenticeship. *Why does this wheel run hot, Freya? Because someone wanted it to. Nothing in a machine is accidental. Find the intention and you have found the criminal.*

Come and see.

Her hands, she noticed, were steady. Her heart was not.

She should burn the note. That was the sensible course, the invisible course, the course the four months' wages in Whitechapel had been spent to purchase. A summons from Thornfield was one thing—an honest commission, wages, a roof, a chance to see the famous clock whose maker's name she had heard Elias speak with something close to worship. But a secret letter in a dead man's hand was evidence. If Elias Vane was alive—if he had faked a drowning and let the world, let *her*, believe him dead for two years—then whatever he wanted, he wanted it through intermediaries and ciphers, from the shadows, and people who worked from the shadows eventually pulled others into them. She had built her whole existence on the principle that shadows were survivable only if you never stepped into them voluntarily.

She held the note over the candle.

The paper began to curl. The wax light caught the cipher at the bottom, the little row of figures, and she saw—in imagination so vivid it might have been memory—Elias's forefinger tapping the slate, tap-tap-tap, saying *check your work, girl, check it again; the sky forgives nothing.*

She snuffed the flame between thumb and forefinger, the way her mother must once have done over a stove, and the note survived, singed at one corner, smelling of tallow.

"Damn you," she said quietly, to a man two years drowned. "Damn you for knowing I'd come."

Because that was the truth underneath everything, and it deserved naming even if only to the empty room. She could refuse the summons. She could remain Frederick Coldharbour, finisher of other men's clocks, invisible as dust in a watch case, safe as anything that has already given up moving. But she had read the notice of his death three times and let her tea go cold, and somewhere in those cold minutes she had promised herself that if the world had made a mistake—if there were errors anywhere in the great mechanism of things—she wanted to be the one holding the tweezers.

And there was more, a smaller and more shameful truth: she was angry. Anger was restless; anger did not sit still under floorboards. Two years he had let her mourn. Whatever game of corpses and ciphers he was playing, he owed her the sight of his living face, and she intended to collect the debt in person, at the bench, under lamplight, where debts between horologists were properly settled.

She repacked the oilcloth, replaced the board, and stood a long moment in the middle of the cramped shop, listening to London breathe its wet breath against the window.

Then she took down her tool roll and began packing it—not hurriedly, not fearfully, but with the deliberation of a woman who had always known, in some chamber of herself, that everything she owned could be carried in two trips, and that someday she would carry it.

The sealed summons she tucked into her coat. The note, singed corner and all, she folded into the lining beside it, next to the thimble, where the heaviest things rode.

Thornfield Observatory lay forty miles north. The mail coach left Bishopsgate at six.

By the time Master Pettifer staggered home smelling of claret and grievance, the shop was dark, the floorboard hid nothing but dust, and the stool at the bench sat empty, still warm, beside a lamp burned down to its final quarter inch of tallow.

On the bench, weighted under a scrap of failed brass, lay a single line in Frederick Coldharbour's careful, borrowed hand:

*Gone to see about a clock.*
