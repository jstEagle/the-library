## 2. A Clock That Lies About the Sky

Thornfield Observatory did not look like a place where lies were told. It looked like a place where the truth had been embalmed.

Freya saw it first from the carrier's cart, cresting the last hill of the North Road: a great stone drum crowned with its copper dome gone green as pond water, the whole of it sitting in a parkland of clipped yews like a bishop's mitre dropped in a field. Windows everywhere. Rows and rows of them, glazed to catch the light, each one a promise that someone inside was watching the heavens with clean eyes and honest instruments.

She had watched it for the better part of an hour before the cart reached the gate, because watching was what she did. She counted the servants moving between the outbuildings — seven, all male, none armed. She counted the horses in the stable yard — four, which meant at most four riders could be dispatched quickly, and none of them saddled. She noted that the gardeners worked with shears but no billhooks, that the lodge gate stood open rather than barred, that nobody challenged the carrier's cart until it had nearly rolled past the door.

A place expecting no trouble. A place that had already finished whatever trouble it required.

"You're the clock-mender, then?" The porter who met her at the steps was broad and uncurious, with flour still dusting one sleeve, as though he'd been pulled from the kitchens to answer the door. "Sir Aldous said to bring you straight up. He's been asking after you twice this morning."

*Twice.* Someone wanted the clock mended quickly. Freya filed that away with everything else and said only, "Then I shan't keep him waiting."

Her trunk went up four flights of stairs on the shoulders of a boy who breathed through his mouth, and Freya climbed behind it with her tool roll slung across her back like a musket. The corridor at the top smelled of tallow and lamp oil and, beneath both, something fainter — the dry mineral smell of brass filings freshly cut. Someone had been working metal here. Recently.

The clock room opened off the dome chamber, and when the boy swung the door wide and stood aside, Freya forgot for a moment to be careful.

The master clock filled the far wall like a cathedral organ laid on its side. It stood eleven feet in her estimation, perhaps twelve, in a case of black walnut so dark it seemed carved from night itself, and behind its long glass door hung the whole glittering architecture of the heavens: wheel upon wheel upon wheel, dials nested inside dials, a lunar track silvered with real moonlight-colored paint, annular rings for the planets, a star field pierced and figured in the great sidereal face so that at any hour of any night the clock would show you precisely what stood above Thornfield's roof. It was the sort of machine a maker spent thirty years building and a lifetime defending. It was the sort of machine she had seen exactly once before, drawn in a plate in Elias Vane's copy of *De Horologiis*, with a note in his hand along the margin: *Whoever made this believed a clock could be a kind of scripture.*

"Ah — my clock-mender."

Sir Aldous Renwick came forward with his hand already extended, and Freya took it because refusing would have been memorable. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples in a way that looked arranged, dressed well but not ostentatiously, with the easy warmth of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice to be obeyed. His grip was dry and precise. Everything about him was precise without being cold, which was its own kind of skill.

"Freya Coldharbour," he said, reading her papers again though he must have read them thrice already. "Journeyman of London. Your master speaks well of you."

"My master is dead, sir," Freya said. "He speaks nothing anymore."

"Ah." A small pause, expertly placed. "Forgive me. Well — you come recommended by your work, which is better than recommendation by tongue. You'll want to see the patient."

"I've been looking at it since I walked in, sir."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite displeasure; approval, performed as displeasure, or displeasure performed as approval. It was impossible to say which, and Freya suspected the ambiguity was deliberate. Renwick gestured to the case with the air of a physician inviting a colleague to consult.

"The striking train stopped entirely nine days ago," he said. "The sidereal face has run slow by some minutes since spring, though we correct it weekly against our transit observations. The planetary rings drag. My keeper before me maintained it beautifully, I'm told, but he is dead now, and machines decay when no one loves them." He smiled. "You'll find nothing beyond ordinary wear, I expect."

"I'll find what there is," Freya said.

"Of course you will." He moved toward the door, then paused, half-turned, and gave her a look of frank, disarming candor. "Miss Coldharbour. One confidence, between people of the same trade. The Observatory's work is of consequence to the Crown — navigation, longitude, the safety of English ships. There are men in London who would seize on any irregularity here, however small, to make difficulties. So if you discover anything *strange* in the mechanism — anything that might be twisted into scandal — I'd take it kindly if you brought it to me before you brought it to anyone else. A courtesy among professionals."

"Of course, sir," Freya said.

"Good girl." And he was gone, whistling something light and tuneless down the stair, leaving Freya alone with the clock and the smell of fresh brass filings.

* * *

She did not open the case for an hour. First she walked around it, and looked, and listened, and wrote nothing down because writing things down was how invisible people became visible.

The stopping of the striking train had not been caused by wear. Wear makes itself known gradually — a softening of sound, a hesitation, a growing reluctance. This clock had been *stopped*. And the sidereal face running slow by minutes, corrected weekly — corrected, always corrected, against observations that themselves required checking —

Freya unlocked the case door with the key the porter provided and put her hands into the machine.

Clockwork does not lie, she had learned at her master's bench before she was twelve years old. Clockwork cannot lie. A gear either meshes or it does not; a ratio is either true or it is false; and if a clock tells a wrong story, then somewhere in its wheels a man has written it. Her fingertips knew this truth before her mind assembled it. Three hours into the going train she found the first of it: a wheel replaced. Not repaired — *replaced*, and recently, its brass brighter than its neighbors by several shades, its teeth cut a half-degree coarser in profile. Whoever cut it had been competent. Competent was the trouble. An amateur leaves damage. A professional leaves intent.

The substituted wheel altered the train's ratio by a fraction so small that no ear would catch it and no eye would see it — a drift of seconds per day, accumulating into minutes per season. The clock would run slow against the true stars, forever, and every correction applied against the Observatory's own published tables would fold that error neatly away, because the tables had been built from the same falseness. The clock and the charts lied in chorus. Each covered the other.

By lamplight, working through the night as she had promised no one she would, Freya found the rest. A pinion in the striking train, filed and shimmed so that the strike would fail after a fixed number of days — the stoppage Renwick had reported was not a failure but a *schedule*. A cam on the planetary ring assembly, ground down and re-soldered, bending Mars's apparent path by degrees. And in the heart of it, behind a plate that should have been sealed by the maker and never opened, a second substitution: the escapement itself, the very soul of the machine, swapped for a near-copy whose depth of engagement differed from the original by less than the width of a hair.

Someone had rewritten the sky, and they had done it from the inside, with love, over many months.

Freya sat back on the floor of the clock case, tools spread around her like surgical instruments, and let herself feel what she felt, which was not fear, or not only fear. It was the cold particular fury of a craftswoman confronting bad work done well. Every alteration had been made to survive casual inspection. Every alteration served the same end: to make the clock disagree, gently, invisibly, with the actual heavens, and to make the disagreement confirm the printed charts that issued from this observatory under Sir Aldous Renwick's name — charts upon which, if the summons' talk of royal monopolies held any truth, the Admiralty would stake the navigation of every ship in England.

Ships would follow these figures. Ships would trust them. And somewhere in the difference between where the stars truly stood and where this beautiful lying machine insisted they stood, sailors would die, quietly, in weathers they could not have predicted, on rocks their charts swore did not exist.

She thought of the margin note in a dead man's book. *Whoever made this believed a clock could be a kind of scripture.* Then whoever altered it believed scripture could be forged.

Freya began to work. Not to repair — not yet. Repair would erase the evidence, and she was no longer certain the sabotage should be erased. Instead she did what invisible people do best: she memorized. She measured the substituted wheel and committed its tooth count to memory. She took wax impressions of file marks. In her room that night she drew the whole falsified train from recall in a coded shorthand of her own devising, then burned the drawing and kept the knowledge, folded tight, the way she carried everything else she owned.

Three nights later, near midnight, she sat in the dome chamber with the shuttered telescope's shadow falling across her knees, consulting the Observatory's own published star catalogue against the sky itself. The clock said Arcturus stood where the catalogue said Arcturus stood. The sky disagreed. The sky, patiently, magnificently, disagreed with both of them.

And below the dome, in the frozen garden, a laborer who was supposed to be illiterate stood looking upward — and his lips were moving, counting stars that were not in any catalogue, correcting the heavens under his breath.

Freya went very still.

She knew the set of those shoulders. She had known it across a workshop for six years, bent over a bench, murmuring numbers exactly like that, exactly so.
