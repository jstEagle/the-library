## 3. The Laborer Who Reads Starlight

The third night, Freya learned the observatory's true architecture was not stone.

It was silence. Thornfield by day rang with the industry of Sir Aldous Renwick's household—maids with their pails, under-cooks shouting across courtyards, clerks scratching in the record room like a nest of contented beetles. But after the last candle was snuffed, the great house emptied of its pretenses, and what remained was the clock: the master mechanism in its tower chamber, ticking its altered heart against her ear, and Freya alone beneath it with her lamps and her files and the sky through the eastern shutters going slowly, honestly wrong.

She had established a ritual by then. At dusk she descended from the clock room to take her supper in the kitchen passage, where the staff left her alone—a woman with tools and a foreign accent was best addressed never. Then she climbed back up as the stars came out, opened the shutters, and set a brass transit level on the sill so she could sight Polaris while she worked. It was not strictly necessary for repairing an escapement. It was necessary for her peace of mind. A clockmaker who could not look at the real sky felt like a surgeon operating inside a lie.

And every night, the lie grew more legible.

She had spent two evenings mapping which wheels had been touched. Sabotage, done well, is indistinguishable at first glance from wear; it announces itself only in ratios. The escape wheel's teeth had been cut two parts in ninety-nine shallower than the original pattern—not enough to stop the clock, only enough to make its sidereal day run long by eleven seconds. Eleven seconds, multiplied across a season, would drift the whole mechanism off the heavens so gradually that no one would blame the clock. They would blame the sky. Observers would "correct" their instruments to match the clock, because Renwick's clock was the authority, and the corrected observations would go into the charts, and the charts would go to the Admiralty, and somewhere in that chain of obedient paper the actual stars would quietly cease to exist.

Freya sat back on her stool the third night, file idle in her hand, and admitted it plainly to herself: whoever had done this work understood gearing better than most men who called themselves masters. He had even left tool marks that imitated age. That took patience. That took *intent*.

The thought should have frightened her. What she felt instead, looking at those false-cut teeth, was something closer to kinship—and that frightened her more.

* * *

It was near midnight when she first noticed the groundskeeper.

She heard him before she saw him: the scrape of a barrow wheel on the gravel path below the eastern shutter, unhurried, the sound of a man whose business outdoors at midnight was nobody's question. Groundskeepers on a great estate keep strange hours; frost, fallen boughs, foxes in the kitchen midden—all plausible reasons. She noted the sound, dismissed it, returned to her work.

Then the barrow stopped. And a voice began to murmur.

At first she took it for prayer. Laborers muttered prayers constantly—against weather, against wages, against each other. But this had cadence she knew, a rising-falling rhythm like a man counting, and she leaned toward the shutter slit despite herself.

"—two degrees forty-one minutes north of the celestial equator, not *two thirty*, you great lying—" A pause. The sound of a man shifting his weight. "And Regulus sits at one hundred fifty-one degrees along, not one hundred fifty-five, whoever ground your lens should be flogged—"

Freya went very still.

The voice was low, rural, roughened at the edges—the voice of a man who had trained it into coarseness. But underneath the deliberate burr ran something else: precision. The flat *a* of a northern grammar school beaten out of a boy decades ago and now deliberately beaten back in. Nobody who could not read could count declination in his head to the arc-minute while pushing a barrow of weeds.

She watched him through the slit. He was big—broader than a scholar ought to be—with his cap pulled low and his sleeves rolled over forearms scarred by some old burn. He stood facing the open sky the way other men faced altars, and he was *angry* at it, or angry on its behalf, correcting the stars under his breath as though the stars were an apprentice who had been given bad figures by a superior they dared not name.

He moved on. The barrow wheel scraped away toward the compost yard.

Freya sat in the dark for a long time with her heart doing something unprofessional.

* * *

Over the following nights she confirmed it. The groundskeeper passed the eastern shutter twice, sometimes three times a week, always between midnight and two, always pausing. Always the murmuring. Once she heard him say, in a voice cracked with exhaustion and something worse than exhaustion, "*They've moved Arcturus half a degree and no one will say so,*" and she bit her knuckle to keep from laughing or weeping, she couldn't tell which, because half a degree of Arcturus was exactly the error the falsified charts carried, and here was a laborer grieving over it like a widower at a graveside.

An illiterate man did not grieve over Arcturus.

So she set her trap.

It was simple, and it was cruel, and she was ashamed of how much she enjoyed designing it. On the fourth night of her watching, she left the eastern shutter open a hand's width wider than usual, and she worked aloud. Not loudly. Just loudly enough for a man standing in the dark below a window to hear a tired craftsman talking to herself, as craftsmen do.

She checked the altitude of a star she had chosen carefully—Deneb, bright, unmistakable—and she announced its position to the empty room with a deliberate error in declination. Not a small error; a small error might be her own slip, forgivable, ignorable. She gave Deneb a full quarter-degree too far north, stated confidently, twice, the second time with a note in her voice of having written it down. Then she held her breath and waited.

Below the window, silence. The barrow stood still on the path so long she thought he would simply move on, and doubt began its cold work in her—*you have imagined it, girl, you have been alone too long in a tower with a broken clock and you have started hearing ghosts in gardeners*—

Then, quiet as a confession dragged out of a man at gunpoint:

"Quarter degree south of that, miss. You'll want to mark it south."

Freya's hands stopped on the file.

The voice was roughened, rural, pitched down into the dirt where voices of laborers lived. But she had spent six years of her childhood falling asleep to that voice reading almanacs aloud in a London workshop while she pretended to be occupied with her pliers. She knew the shape of its sentences before she knew their meaning, the way you know a bell from its first strike. There was a slight catch on the *th* of *south* that she had heard ten thousand times, that she had mocked gently once and been told, with a smile, *an accent is just a map of everywhere a man has been, and mine has been everywhere worth going.*

Elias Vane was dead. Elias Vane had been dead for two years. There had been a fire, a body identified by his own papers, a grave in Stepney that she had not been permitted to visit because apprentices of no property and no name were not welcome at such things. She had mourned him with the only mourning allowed to people like her: she had gone on working, and she had hated him a little for dying without leaving her anything but his tools and his debts.

"Did you hear me, miss?" the voice said, still roughened, still careful. "South."

Freya crossed the clock room on legs she did not fully control and pushed the shutter wide.

He was already moving. Of course he was—he had heard the shutter, and a man does not survive two years in the belly of the thing hunting him by standing in lit doorways. But the moon was high that night, three days off full, and it caught him full in the face as he turned with the barrow handles in his grip.

Older. Greyer at the temples. A scar through one eyebrow that hadn't been there before, and twenty pounds of borrowed laborer's flesh over the same stubborn frame. But it was Elias Vane. It was Elias Vane standing in the gravel in a groundsman's smock with dirt to the elbows, looking up at her with an expression she had seen on his face exactly once before—the day the bailiffs came for the workshop and he hid her in the coal cellar with her hand over her own mouth.

He looked terrified.

"You're dead," Freya said. Her voice came out very small and very steady, which was how her voice behaved when everything else had come loose. "I mourned you. I carried your best file for two years like a relic. I—" The words were arriving faster than her discipline could sort them. "You let them bury *someone*. In your coat. With your papers. And I found out from a parish notice, Elias, a *notice*, pinned to a wall between a lost dog and an auction—"

"*Quiet.*" It came out of him sharp and old and unmistakably the master's voice, the workshop voice, and it silenced her the way it always had—for exactly one breath, before the fury arrived behind it.

"Don't you quiet me." She was gripping the shutter frame hard enough to hurt her palms. "Do you know what it is to be the apprentice of a dead man? No guild will touch me. No master will sign my papers. Your death followed me through three workshops like a debt collectors come to collect. I forged my journeyman's papers in my own hand because yours was too famous, and I did it *well*, Elias, I did it beautifully, and I have been afraid every single day since that someone will notice how well." Her throat closed. She made herself finish. "You got to be dead. I got to be a liar. That was the trade you made, and you didn't ask me."

Below, in the gravel, Elias Vane closed his eyes. When he opened them, the fear was still there—but behind it, terribly, was relief. She recognized that too. It was the face of a man who has been carrying something alone for two years and has just heard footsteps behind him and cannot decide whether to be saved or caught.

"I didn't ask you," he said quietly, "because asking you would have killed you. And because—" His jaw worked. The burr dropped away entirely, and it was his real voice now, the voice from the workshop, the voice that had taught her that a gear train was an argument and a well-made escapement was a proof. "Because I am a coward, Freya, and I have had two years to become a better one. Come down. Not the main stair—the yard door by the instrument shed. Please. There are things in this observatory that are not what they seem, and I think you have already begun to find them."

"The clock," she said. "Eleven seconds long on the sidereal day. Cut, not worn."

Something moved through his face—pride and grief in the same spasm, an expression she had seen him wear over her finest and worst work alike.

"Yes," he said. "That, and a great deal more. Come down, and I will tell you what I carried out of the fire."

She should have refused. Every instinct of survival she had built in six years of being nobody said *refuse, close the shutter, be invisible, live.* Instead Freya Coldharbour blew out her lamps, picked up her mother's thimble from the workbench, and put it in her pocket where it always went when the world became untrustworthy.

"I'm coming down," she called softly into the dark. "And you're going to tell me whose body they buried in your coat."

The gravel crunched once, twice—and then, from the direction of the compost yard, a lantern flared. Voices. Renwick's night watchman, making his rounds early, calling out to ask why the eastern shutter of the clock tower stood open at half past one in the morning.

In the shadows below, Elias Vane swore in fluent Latin.
