## 4. What Elias Carried Out of the Fire

There is a particular mercy in being mistaken for a simpleton, and I had learned to live inside it the way a crab lives inside an emptied shell. The groundskeepers at Thornfield left me alone once they'd decided I could neither read nor reckon. The steward paid me four shillings a week to be invisible, and I had become so fluent in invisibility that some mornings I woke and genuinely did not know my own name until I'd said it wrong aloud—*Ned*, they called me, Ned of the wheelbarrow—and heard how comfortably the lie sat in my mouth.

Two years of saying nothing. Two years of watching Sir Aldous Renwick descend each evening from his tower with his charts folded under his arm like love letters, and holding my tongue while stars were moved across the heavens with a pen.

Then Freya Coldharbour came up the carriage road with her box of tools, and everything I had buried rose out of the ground and walked toward me wearing her face.

I knew her before I saw her work. That was the cruelty of it—I had trained those hands, and hands cannot lie about their training. I watched her from the ha-ha wall on her first evening, pretending to rake gravel while she knelt before the master clock's opened case, and I saw her fingers pause over the fourth wheel exactly where they should have paused, because the fourth wheel was where the lie began. She tilted her head the way she used to when a mechanism argued with her. She did not curse it. She listened to it.

*She knows,* I thought, and the thought was terror and relief in equal measure, two wires twisted into one cable, load-bearing both ways.

For three nights I let her work alone. For three nights I told myself that if she did not find me, then I had not summoned her, and if I had not summoned her, then I was still a dead man, and dead men do not have to explain themselves. I raked gravel. I mended the ha-ha. I watched Venus rise where Renwick's printed tables insisted it could not.

On the fourth night she set a trap for me, and I walked into it like a moth that has read too many books about flames.

She stood at the transit instrument in the meridian shed, calling out observations to no one, her voice pitched just loud enough to carry across the yard—a declination figure, wrong by eleven minutes of arc. Any laborer would have passed by. Any literate man would have passed by too, because what does a stonemason care for the declination of Altair? But eleven minutes. She had chosen the error deliberately, generously, the way a teacher leaves a door open for a student who is late. Eleven minutes was the exact discrepancy between Renwick's published tables and the true position—the discrepancy only someone who had studied *both* skies would catch.

And I opened my mouth and corrected her. In my own voice. My own voice, which I had not heard aloud in two years except in muttered fragments and prayers, and it came out whole and warm and entirely recognizable, and Freya turned from the eyepiece with a face like the closing of a ledger.

"Hello, Elias."

I have stood in burning buildings. I have held a drowning man's coat. Neither compared to standing in the meridian shed with my name spoken back to me, my true name, after two years of answering to Ned.

* * *

We did not speak properly until past midnight, in the north tool-cellar where the wine barrels sweated and a single lamp made our shadows enormous against the vaulting. She sat on an overturned crate with her arms crossed, and I knelt on the flags like a penitent, which was fitting, because I was one.

"Dead," she said. "They fished your coat out of the Thames with your papers in the pocket. Master Hargrove closed the workshop's books on you. I stood at your graveside, Elias. There was a stone."

"There was a stone," I agreed. "It was cheaper than a coffin would have been, given that there was no body in it."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on the word and reassembled itself into steel. "Don't make it clever. Don't you dare make it clever."

So I didn't. I told it plainly, the way you'd describe a fault in a gear train, tooth by tooth, because plainness was all I had left to offer her.

It began, as most corruptions do, small enough to fit through the eye of a needle. Renwick had come to Thornfield five years ago with royal ambitions and mediocre eyesight—he could not resolve a double star to save his soul, and everyone knew it, and everyone was polite about it. What he lacked in sight he possessed tenfold in appetite. When the Board of Longitude began hinting that a reliable set of southern star tables might earn their compiler a monopoly on navigation printing—charts sold to every ship, every merchant house, every navy yard in England, a tithe on the ocean itself—Renwick saw his fortune hanging in the sky like ripe fruit just out of reach.

But his observations would not ripen. His instruments were fine; his eye was not. Winter after winter his figures came in scattered, imprecise, embarrassing next to the quiet excellence of the older tables compiled by Dr. Nathaniel Ferren, who had kept Thornfield's records for twenty years before Renwick arrived.

"Nathaniel Ferren," Freya said slowly. "His name is still on the—"

"On the dedication plaque, yes. Under glass. Very respectful." I heard my own voice go flat. "Renwick courted him first. Flattered him, sought his methods, sat at his elbow during every observation. And then he began to correct Ferren's figures. Quietly. A tenth of an arc-second here, smoothed there, a suspicious outlier discarded as atmospheric. He called it *tidying*. He showed me the ledgers himself, early on, laughing—laughing, Freya—as though it were a joke we were both in on. *The sky is untidy, Vane. Science is the art of presenting it better than God left it.*"

The lamp flame leaned in the draught from under the cellar door. Somewhere above us, the sabotaged clock ticked its crooked heartbeat.

"I objected," I said. "I want that recorded, even if only the mice hear it. I objected in writing. I wrote to Ferren himself, laying out what Renwick was doing, and Ferren—" My hands had found each other in my lap and were gripping hard enough to ache. "Ferren agreed to confront him. He said he'd been keeping duplicates of the true observations for years, waiting for proof of what he suspected. We arranged to meet at the White Hart stairs on the fifteenth of March. He never came. They pulled him out of the river at Limehouse three days later, and the coroner recorded misadventure, and Renwick wore black gloves at the funeral and wept beautifully."

Freya had gone very still. In the lamplight her face was all planes and shadow, and I saw my own reflection in her silence: the coward who had done the arithmetic.

"And you ran," she said. Not a question.

"I ran." I made myself say it without ornament. "Because Nathaniel Ferren had objected louder than I had, and Nathaniel Ferren was floating down the Thames, and I did the sums every apprentice learns to do when the master isn't looking. One objector plus one river equals silence. I understood that if I stayed, my objection would be subtracted the same way, and the truth would go into the ground with me, unrecorded. So I made sure the truth survived its author. I copied what I could carry—Ferren's duplicate tables, my own correspondence, the pages of Renwick's tidied ledgers I'd seen with dates and figures—and I put them somewhere no one would burn them, and then I drowned myself in the Thames in a borrowed coat."

"The papers in the pocket."

"A decoy. Let them fish up a keeper's body of paperwork and declare it a man. Grief closes books quickly when grief is convenient to other people. Hargrove believed it. You believed it." I looked up at her. "You were safest believing it. That was the one part I did correctly. Every person who ever learned what I carried became a person in danger, so I cut every thread, including yours. Especially yours."

"Especially mine," she repeated, and something moved through her voice that was not forgiveness but was at least the ground where forgiveness might someday take root—or might not. I had no right to assume. I had abandoned her to a corpse's reputation, to a closed workshop, to forged journeyman's papers and a life measured in portable things. I had done it to keep her alive, and keeping someone alive is not the same as staying.

"How did you survive?" she asked. "Here. Under his roof."

"Because Renwick needed a saboteur, and I volunteered without appearing to." I gave her the bitter mechanics of it. "The old keeper's post fell vacant after my death—my death—and Renwick appointed some well-paid nonentity who understands nothing, while the actual work of altering the clock went to hired hands brought in secretly. But hired hands talk. I made myself useful instead. Illiterate Ned, strong back, empty head. Who suspects a simpleton of reading starlight? I have spent two years making small adjustments to Renwick's sabotage—loosening what his men tightened, filing a tooth here, offsetting a wheel there—slowing the corruption the way rust slows a blade. Not stopping it. Slowing it. And hating myself efficiently every single day, because slowing a lie is still serving it."

"Why the summons?" Freya asked. "Why now? Why risk everything to pull me here?"

I could have lied to her. God knows I had rehearsed nobler answers on the walk down—*I needed someone I could trust; I wanted to protect you from what's coming.* But she had crossed a country to stand in a cold cellar with me, and she deserved the naked mechanism, all teeth exposed.

"Desperation," I said. "Nothing finer than that. Renwick's commission from the Crown arrives within the month—the Royal Commission comes to inspect the clock and certify his tables, and once they do, the monopoly is signed and the false sky becomes the official one, forever, stamped on brass and vellum in every chart room from Bristol to Bombay. Against that I had two years of stolen papers hidden where no one credible can retrieve them—because who believes a dead man? Who believes an illiterate laborer? I am a ghost, Freya, and ghosts cannot testify. Ghosts cannot hold a micrometer steady. Ghosts cannot finish anything." My voice failed on the last word and I let it. "Everything I know is trapped inside a man who does not exist. I needed hands that the world still believes in. Yours are the only hands I ever trained to read a train of wheels the way scholars read Latin. I summoned you because I could not do this alone, and I have hated myself for the selfishness of it since the day I wrote that letter in code and sealed it with a dead man's wax."

The lamp guttered. Neither of us moved to trim it.

"You should have written to me two years ago," she said quietly. "You should have trusted me with the truth while I still had a workshop to hide it in."

"Yes," I said. "That is the shape of my sin, and I will carry it whatever you decide tonight. But Freya—there is less time now than even I thought. This afternoon a courier came to the west gate. I carried the man's bag to the house myself, and I read the seal upside down while I did it, because two years of invisibility teaches you to read everything upside down." I met her eyes. "It was a warrant. Royal arms on it. Orders for the seizure and destruction of all 'irregular' records at Thornfield—and a schedule of associated premises to be searched. There was a London address on that list."

Her face went white in the lamplight. "The workshop."

"The workshop," I said. "Hargrove's shop. Every apprentice Elias Vane ever trained is about to be erased from the earth along with the sky."
