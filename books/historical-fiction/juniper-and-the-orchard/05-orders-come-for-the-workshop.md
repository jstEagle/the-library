## 5. Orders Come for the Workshop

The warrant arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside a leather dispatch case sealed with the royal arms, and Freya knew before she touched it that the wax had been broken and resealed by someone careless with heat.

She was elbow-deep in the clock's going train when the footman brought it up to the instrument room, setting it down on the ledge beside her with the reverence servants reserved for things that might bite. She wiped her hands on her apron—old habit, though grease had long since stopped offending her—and broke the second seal.

The language was formal. The intent was not.

*By order of the Crown, all records, registers, charts, tables, and observational papers held at Thornfield Observatory determined irregular by the Commissioners of Longitude are to be seized and destroyed by fire, that no corrupt figure survive to mislead His Majesty's navigators...*

She read it twice. The first time as a horologist reads anything, hunting for errors. The second time as a woman reads her own name.

Because it was there. Not hers—but the workshop's. *Coldharbour Lane, the establishment of the late Master Horologist Elias Vane, deceased,* listed among the sites of irregular instruction, its ledgers and apprentice rolls and drawings to be gathered and burned alongside the observatory's falsified sky. Every notebook she had filled under his eye. Every exercise in gear ratios inked in her careful hand at fourteen, fifteen, sixteen years old. The bench where he'd first set a file in her fingers and said, *you will make small things, Freya, and small things outlast empires.*

They were going to burn the evidence that she had ever been taught.

Her mother's thimble sat in her pocket as it always did, worn thin at the crown from thirty years of another woman's needle. Freya pressed her thumb against it now, feeling the cold brass, and understood with terrible clarity what a warrant like this actually meant. It was not housekeeping. It was amputation. Renwick was not merely correcting his charts—he was cutting away every hand that could say otherwise.

*Irregular.* Such a gentle word for arson.

Footsteps on the stair. Not the laborers' heavy tread. She knew the particular cadence of Sir Aldous Renwick by now—the measured, unhurried pace of a man who had never once needed to hurry, because the world arranged itself around him at his leisure. He entered without knocking, which was his right, and stood a moment in the doorway letting the morning light arrange itself flatteringly across his silver hair.

"Mistress Coldharbour." He inclined his head. "You've read it, I see."

"I have." She kept her voice level, her eyes on the paper. "It lists my master's workshop."

"It lists many places." He crossed to the window, hands clasped behind his back, surveying the grounds below where the laborers moved about their morning work. Among them, somewhere, a broad-shouldered man who read starlight and answered to a name not his own. "The Crown is thorough when its navigation is threatened. Corrupt tables sink ships, Mistress Coldharbour. Drowned men. Widows. One cannot be too careful."

"Of course, sir."

"You're wondering whether your name appears."

The question hung in the air between them like a pendulum at the top of its swing, momentarily still, all its force gathered. Freya felt the terror she'd been carrying since the courier arrived finally take shape—not a fog anymore but a thing with edges, cold and precise.

"No," she said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Not yet."

He turned from the window and came toward her workbench, and she watched his gaze travel over the disassembled clock with the expression of a man reading a letter written in a language he'd been assured was dead. His eyes stopped at the escapement. At the new pivot she'd cut. At the ratio of wheels that should have been something else entirely.

"Remarkable restoration," he said softly. "I confess when I engaged you, I expected competence. I did not expect—" He paused, choosing the word with care. "*Fidelity*. Fidelity to an original design most people would have dismissed as eccentric. A second train, hidden in the frame. Whoever taught you to see such things must have been extraordinary."

The workshop smelled suddenly of smoke that wasn't there yet.

"I don't know what you mean, sir."

"Don't you?" He smiled, and it was almost kind, which made it worse. "Elias Vane had a way with left-handed files. A peculiarity of his method. Every apprentice he trained filed the same way—I've inspected enough of their work to know it when I find it. And these pivots, Mistress Coldharbour, are filed left-handed. Cut by someone trained at that bench on Coldharbour Lane. Someone trained by a man who has been dead"—he consulted nothing, simply recited it from memory, savoring it—"two years this past March."

Freya's hands, resting on the bench, did not move. She had learned invisibility the way other girls learned music: daily practice, endless drill, until it lived in the fingers. Keep your eyes down. Keep your answers short. Let them look through you. But Renwick was looking *at* her now, fully, deliberately, the way one looks at a specimen one has finally identified, and every practiced hour of her life seemed to fall away at once.

"Your groundskeeper," she said, because silence was surrender and surrender was death by inches. "He corrected me on a declination last week. Perhaps you should inspect his filing."

Something flickered across Renwick's face—amusement, genuine and brief. "Ah. You know each other. How touching." He drew off his gloves, finger by finger. "Let me speak plainly, since plainly seems to be the day's currency. Two years ago I lost a colleague—a fine astronomer named Harrow—who objected loudly and publicly to certain corrections being made to our records. He drowned. A tragic accident on the Thames. Shortly after, Master Vane also objected, rather more quietly, and then died of a fever so sudden no physician ever saw him." He said it lightly, a man discussing weather. "Grief makes men careless. Carelessness makes them dead. You follow."

"I follow."

"Good. Then hear my offer." He laid his gloves on her workbench, atop her drawings, a small territorial act she would remember later with more fury than the warrant itself. "The Commission requires a keeper for this clock. A *royal* appointment, with rooms, a pension, and a name entered in the rolls—which, I note, yours currently is not. Your papers are forged; I've known since the week you arrived; I do not care. Under my patronage they become unnecessary. You will maintain this magnificent instrument. You will keep its face telling exactly the time I require. And when the bonfires are lit next week, you will stand at my shoulder and watch, and you will say nothing, ever, about second trains or left-handed files or groundskeepers who read the sky."

He paused at the door.

"Or you may decline. In which case your name will appear on a revised warrant by Friday, and there is a very efficient clerk at the College of Heralds who can make a person difficult to have existed at all. Ask Elias how that works. Oh—" the smile again, terrible in its warmth, "—forgive me. You can't."

He left. The door closed. The clock ticked on above her, steady as a heartbeat, and Freya stood in the empty room with the warrant in one hand and her mother's thimble in the other and let herself, for exactly sixty seconds by the regulator on the wall, be afraid.

She counted it out. Sixty seconds of shaking hands and shallow breath and the image of her notebooks curling black on a pyre, of Elias dragged from the shrubbery with his false name shouted after him, of herself erased so thoroughly that even the pivots she'd cut would testify against everyone she'd ever loved.

At fifty-nine, she noticed something.

At sixty, she was already crossing the room.

Because Renwick had made an error, and he did not know it, and it was the most beautiful error she had ever seen. He had looked at the hidden train in her restoration and believed it was old—believed it belonged to the original maker's eccentric design, some dormant curiosity she had faithfully revived. He thought he was buying silence about a relic.

He did not know it was new. He did not know it was *finished*, or nearly. He did not know that the clock on the wall above his handsome silver head had been keeping two times since Tuesday last—one for him, and one for the truth.

She went down the back stair to the walled garden where the groundskeeper kept the tools, and found Elias on his knees turning soil with a spade, and the words were out of her before she reached him:

"They're burning the workshop. Everything. The ledgers, the drawings, the apprentice rolls—all of it, next week, by fire." He rose slowly, dirt falling from his hands, and she saw the fear land in him and watched something harder arrive behind it. "And Renwick knows whose hands are in his clock. He offered me a pension and a lie. I refused." Her voice did not shake now; she marveled at it distantly, as if it belonged to someone braver. "There is no invisible left, Elias. There's only what we finish before the matches come."

Elias leaned on the spade and looked at her—really looked, the way masters look at apprentices when the apprentice has, without ceremony, become something else.

"Then tell me," he said quietly, "what a second train can prove."

Freya took the warrant from her apron and held it out to him, and above them, through the observatory's high windows, the great clock ticked on toward midnight, keeping its secret time.

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She sat at the workbench with the original maker's drawings spread beneath a single lamp, and she traced the strange ratios the long-dead horologist had buried in his wheels—ratios too deliberate to be error, too elegant to be accident—and began, line by line, to understand what he had built into the brass: not a clock that told time, but a clock that told *truths*, waiting two hundred years for someone to ask it the right question.

Outside, far to the south, she imagined she could smell London burning already.

It was only the lamp oil. But it would not always be.
