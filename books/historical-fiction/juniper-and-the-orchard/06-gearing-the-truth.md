## 6. Gearing the Truth

Freya had been awake for two days when she finally saw it.

She had not seen it in the clock. That was the humiliation of it. She had seen it in a spilled cup of small beer.

She had set her mug down on the maker's original drawings—the ones Elias kept rolled in oilcloth beneath a floorboard under his cot, as if they were love letters from a dead man—and when she lifted it again, the ring of moisture had darkened one corner of the plan into translucence. And through the wet paper, backlit by her candle, she could see both sides of the sheet at once: the front elevation of the great clock on Thornfield's south wall, and behind it, printed faint and reversed, the wheel train in profile. The escapement. The ratios.

The two drawings did not match.

Not because either was wrong. Because together, they were something else entirely.

"Elias." Her voice came out cracked. She hadn't spoken in hours; her throat had forgotten how. "Elias, come here."

He came up from the clock pit with grease to his elbows and suspicion all over his face, and she turned the drawing toward him, held her damp thumb flat against the paper so the candle shone through, and watched two years of hiding fall off him in a single breath.

"That's Hargreave's hand," he said quietly. "Josiah Hargreave. The man who built this clock before either of us was born."

"I know whose hand it is. I've copied enough of it." She tapped the profile view. "Look at the ratio between the escape wheel and the fourth pinion. Sixty-three to one. No clockmaker alive uses sixty-three to one—it's an ugly number, it fights you at every turn, any apprentice would round it to sixty and lose three seconds a day and call it good enough."

"So Hargreave was a perfectionist."

"Hargreave was encoding declination." She heard herself say it and felt the floor tilt under her certainty, that vertigo of a thing becoming true as it is spoken. "Sixty-three degrees. The northern limit of the ecliptic as measured from this latitude—*correctly* measured, Elias, not the way Renwick's charts have it. And here—" her finger moved down the train, wheel to wheel, counting teeth she had counted a hundred times without ever asking why, "—forty-seven, fifty-one, twenty-three. Every ratio in this escapement is a number that has no business in horology and every business in astronomy. He didn't build a clock. He built a table. In brass. Anyone who knows how to read a train of wheels can read the true sky right out of its gearing."

Silence. Somewhere below them the pendulum swung, patient as ever, ticking out numbers neither of them had understood until now.

Elias sat down slowly on the cot, the way old men sit, though he was not old. He looked at the drawing for a long time.

"He knew," Elias said at last. "Hargreave knew what Renwick would become—or what someone like Renwick always becomes—and he couldn't stop it with words, because words burn. So he put the truth somewhere no magistrate would think to look. You can burn paper. You'd have to melt the whole clock to kill this."

"And Renwick almost did," Freya said. "That's what the sabotage was. He wasn't breaking your clock, he was *editing* it. Swapping wheels. Changing ratios until the brass agreed with his false charts. He knew there was something in it—he just didn't know how much."

"He didn't know how to read it." Elias laughed, a short, disbelieving sound. "Thirty years I've walked past this mechanism and called myself its keeper, and I never once asked why the fourth wheel had such an odd tooth count. I kept the time. I oiled the truth and never read it."

"You're reading it now."

"Yes." He looked up at her, and there was something in his face she had never seen there in the workshop in London, not once: not pride exactly, and not fear. Recognition. The look of a man meeting an equal. "We are."

*

The work began that night, and Freya learned what it meant to build a sentence out of metal.

It was Elias who laid out the method, because it was Elias who had spent two years pretending to be illiterate while memorizing everything he'd ever known, and a man who hides knowledge learns to store it carefully. He chalked the original ratios onto the flagstones of the clock pit in a cipher of his own devising—stars drawn where numbers should be, a crescent for six, a full disc for nine—so that even if a search party found the floor, they would find only a madman's garden.

"The tables have to be complete," he said, crouched over the chalk, his voice low even though they were alone. "Declination and right ascension for every navigable star, through the full year. If we leave gaps, Renwick's men fill them with lies, and we've built a monument to fraud instead of an indictment of it."

"That's forty-one stars," Freya said. "Times three hundred and sixty-five nights. Do you know how many wheels that is?"

"No." He smiled, and for a moment he looked twenty years younger and standing in his London workshop scolding her for filing against the grain. "But I know a girl who measures her life in portable things, and a train of wheels is nothing but a very long argument made small. Start with Polaris. Everything else hangs off Polaris."

So she started with Polaris.

She cut the new escape wheel herself, because her hands were steadier than his now—two years of hauling gravel do things to a man's fine motor control that no amount of remembering undoes—and because cutting brass was the one prayer she had ever believed in. She worked by lamplight with the drawing propped before her and Hargreave's ghost leaning over her shoulder, that long-dead maker whose ugly ratios she now kissed with her file. Every tooth was a digit. Every ratio, a coordinate. When the wheel was done she held it to the light and turned it, and it carried the position of the pole star in its body the way her mother's thimble carried her mother's thumb.

"You understand what this means," Elias said, watching her from across the bench. It was near dawn. Neither of them had mentioned sleep. "A clock like this doesn't just survive us. It teaches. A hundred years from now, some apprentice with grease to her elbows opens this train, wonders at the ratios, works them out—and the sky tells her the truth again. Hargreave built a letter that posts itself forever."

"Then we're only adding a postscript."

"We're adding witnesses." His voice roughened. "Hargreave wrote alone and died alone and his letter waited eighty years for someone literate. We won't make that mistake. The Commission comes, the clock speaks, and *we* speak with it. Two voices saying the same thing." He paused. "Three, if I can make myself stand up and say mine."

Freya set down her file. She looked at him across the brass and the lamplight, at the mentor who had let her mourn him, and decided—not for the first time, but perhaps for the last—that grief was a debt she would collect later, with interest.

"Then say it well," she said. "Because I am not building this twice."

*

They fell into a rhythm over the following days that felt, dangerously, like happiness.

By day, Freya was the hired repairer performing honest-looking maintenance for Sir Aldous Renwick's benefit, letting him watch her reseat bearings and adjust the pendulum, letting him believe the clock was returning to *his* version of correctness. She had learned invisibility so young it sat on her like her own skin, and she wore it now deliberately: eyes down, hands busy, answers short. Yes, sir. Nearly done, sir. The going train wanted cleaning, sir.

By night, the second clock assembled itself inside the first.

Elias worked the calculations, checking each encoded figure against the observations he had smuggled out of the fire two years ago—a single notebook, water-stained, hidden in a dry-stone wall by the orchard like scripture. Freya translated number into gear. They invented a language of taps for working inside the case within earshot of the house: one knock for correct, two for recalculate, three for someone coming. On the third night she taught him to file again, guiding his ruined-and-relearning hands over a pinion, and neither of them remarked on it, and both of them remembered it.

On the fifth night, standing ankle-deep in chalk stars with the rebuilt escapement turning slow and sure above them—Polaris and Vega and Altair riding their wheels like passengers—Elias said, "When this is done, they'll come for it. You know that. Truth in brass is still truth, and truth is the one cargo this kingdom confiscates on sight."

"Then we finish it," Freya said, wiping her hands, "and let them come. Brass doesn't scare."

"It doesn't scare," Elias agreed. "But brass can be melted, and men who falsify the sky are not shy about furnaces." He looked at her, and the lamplight found every one of his two years on his face. "Which is why, tomorrow, I'm going into Renwick's study to retrieve the rest of Hargreave's papers. There's a correspondence—his letters to the Royal Society, laying out these ratios openly, dated, witnessed. Drawings signed by three men who are conveniently dead but whose signatures live in the Society's own archives. With those in hand, the clock isn't a curiosity. It's a confession countersigned by history itself."

"The study is searched daily."

"The study is searched daily," Elias said, "by men who think I'm a groundskeeper who can't read."

He smiled when he said it. Freya did not smile back. She stood in the pit beneath the great turning wheels, beneath the true sky ticking in brass above her head, and felt the first cold thread of the future go through the warm cloth of their hope.

"Tomorrow, then," she said.

It was the last quiet night they were given. Before dawn, hoofbeats came up the observatory road—too many horses for a courier, too fast for routine—and a light was already burning in the director's study, as though Renwick had been waiting for the sound.

Freya pressed her palm flat against the clock case, feeling the new escapement beat beneath the wood, steady as a pulse.

*Hold on,* she told it. *You're nearly finished.*

Then she snuffed the lamp and went up the stairs to see what the morning intended to take from them.
