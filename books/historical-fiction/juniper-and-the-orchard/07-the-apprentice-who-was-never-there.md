## 7. The Apprentice Who Was Never There

They came at dusk, four of them, wearing the loose authority of men who had been told they need not explain themselves. Elias Vane heard the cart before he saw it—iron-rimmed wheels on wet gravel, a sound like a blade being drawn slowly—and he set down his rake and became, in the space of three breaths, nobody at all.

He had gotten good at being nobody. It was a craft like any other, requiring discipline and daily practice: the slight stoop, the hands held wrong, the face arranged into that bovine patience which invited no questions. For two years he had worn the laborer Josiah Cree as a tailor wears a coat, and for two years the coat had held. But tonight, watching Renwick's men climb down from the cart with lanterns and a folded paper—one of Renwick's own assistants among them, a thin creature named Pell who had once sat at Elias's lectures with ink-stained fingers—tonight the coat felt thin as paper.

*The drawings.* The thought arrived whole and cold. The maker's original drawings, rolled in oilcloth inside the false bottom of the old records chest in the north archive, and if Pell knew even half of what to look for—

He bent back to his rake. Waited. The men crossed the courtyard, their boots loud, and were admitted by a frightened under-clerk, and the great door of the observatory swallowed them.

Elias counted to two hundred, as he had taught Freya to count when she was sixteen and all elbows and impatience, her file biting too deep into every brass wheel. *Patience is the first tool,* he had told her. *Everything else is decoration.*

She had learned it better than he had ever taught her, he realized now. She had learned it so well she had made herself invisible, forged papers and a borrowed name, a journeyman ghost moving through London's workshops—and he had done that to her. He had left her a corpse's reputation to inherit, a master's death like a stone hung round her craft, and she had carried it without complaint because complaint was visibility and visibility was death. He thought of her hands on the clock's gears, sure and quick and angry, and felt the old shame turn over in him like something alive.

There was no more time for shame. He dropped the rake against the wall where a laborer would leave it, careless, unremarkable, and went in through the coal passage.

* * *

The north archive smelled of tallow and dust and the particular dry sweetness of old paper, and it was lit badly, which saved him. Elias moved along the shelving in the dark the way he had once moved along the aisles of his own lecture hall—with total ownership, every obstacle known to his body. The records chest stood where it had stood for thirty years, under the window whose latch he had broken himself, quietly, six months ago, the way a man might prepare an escape he prayed never to use.

He knelt. His fingers found the seam of the false bottom. And then, from the corridor beyond the door, came light—lantern-glow spilling yellow across the floorboards, growing, and voices.

"—Renwick wants the irregular papers inventoried tonight. Everything predating his directorship goes on the burn list."

Pell's voice. Nearer.

Elias did not think. Thinking was a luxury of men with names. His hands went into the chest, found the oilcloth roll, slid it into the breast of his shirt where it lay against his ribs like a second heartbeat—and then the door handle turned, and there was nowhere in the room left to be.

Except the shadow behind the door itself.

He pressed into it as the door swung wide. The lantern light swept the shelves, the chest, the floor. Pell stood in the doorway with another man behind him, both of them holding papers, and Elias could see the nape of Pell's neck, the pulse in it, close enough to touch.

"The chest is locked," said the other man.

"It's not." Pell stepped forward. "Look—the hasp's been forced. Recently, by the shine on it."

Elias's heart performed one long, deliberate contraction. *Patience,* he told himself, and had never less felt it.

"Someone's been in these records," Pell said, crouching at the chest, lifting out bundles of observation logs. "Someone's been reading them."

And then, from the corridor, footsteps—quick, hard-heeled, unmistakably deliberate—and Freya's voice, pitched exactly between irritation and deference, the voice of a tradeswoman kept waiting:

"If someone is going to disturb my maintenance logs, I'd like it done in my presence, thank you."

She came through the door carrying her tool roll and a leather folio, and she did not glance at the shadow behind the door—not a flicker, not a hair's breadth of acknowledgment, and Elias understood that she had known he was here, had perhaps followed Pell here on purpose, had walked into this room armed with nothing but nerve and paper.

"What logs?" Pell said, still kneeling.

"The clock's logs. Thirty years of them." Freya set the folio on top of the records chest, opened it, and fanned out pages—yellowed, foxed at the edges, written in a small upright hand that Elias recognized with a jolt that went through him like current. His own hand. His keeper's log, in entries dated across years he had spent as a dead man, each one precise, each one dull, each one perfectly, boringly authentic in its details of winding weights and cleaned pivots and replaced escapement pins.

Only he had never written them.

"You'll find the chest's contents match these," Freya was saying. "The keeper logged every intervention on the mechanism. If someone forced the hasp, it would have been me—I've had the chest open for a week cross-referencing gear replacements against the sabotage I was hired to repair. You're welcome to compare hands."

Pell compared hands. Elias watched him do it, watched the thin man hold a page from the folio against a page from the chest, his brow furrowing, his lips moving—and Elias, who had taught half the Royal Society's clerks their penmanship standards, examined his own forged handwriting with a craftsman's horror and admiration. She had not merely copied his script. She had *aged* it. The iron-gall ink sat correctly in the paper. There was a water stain on the March entry. There was a page where the writing deteriorated slightly toward evening, as a tired man's does.

"These are consistent," Pell admitted at last, sounding disappointed about it.

"Of course they're consistent," Freya said. "I don't repair clocks by guessing. Now—do you want those inventories or not? Because I have an escapement to finish and your director wants it working before the Commission arrives."

They took her inventory. They left. And when the last boot had faded down the corridor, Elias came out from behind the door, and the roll of drawings was still pressed against his ribs, and Freya turned and looked at him with such controlled fury that he felt seventeen years old again, caught at some dangerous shortcut.

* * *

They argued in the clock room, below the great face, their voices kept low out of habit though the nearest living soul was forty feet of stone away. The clock ticked around them like a patient heart, and above them Renwick's painted heavens wheeled in their circles, lying serenely about the sky.

"You should give yourself up," Freya said. Not for the first time tonight, and not gently. "You should walk into that Commission chamber and say *I am Elias Vane and these are my observations and Aldous Renwick falsified them over my signature.* That ends it. A dead man's testimony is rumor. A living one is evidence."

"And what happens to the evidence while they decide whether to believe me?" Elias kept his voice level. He had negotiated with admirals; he could keep his voice level. "I am a fugitive who faked his death and hid as a laborer for two years, Freya. The first thing Renwick's counsel will ask is why a man of science let a colleague drown rather than come forward. They will spend a week establishing whether I'm a witness or a madman, and then the burn warrant will arrive and everything I am will be ash in the courtyard."

"So we run."

"With what? With the drawings?" He pulled the oilcloth roll from his shirt and set it on the workbench between them, and unrolled it, and there was Hargreave's original design, the dead maker's fine ruled lines, the second train of wheels drawn in the margin almost as an afterthought—a man's private joke with posterity. "Paper burns, Freya. We have both seen how fast."

"Then what do you propose?" Her voice cracked at last, and beneath the crack was something older and rawer. "Because I have spent two years being no one because of you. Do you understand that? I buried my name in the same grave you buried yourself. I have walked past men who trained me and dared not greet them. I ate supper alone in lodgings under a false name while everyone I respected mourned you—and I mourned you, God help me, I mourned you *properly*, I lit a candle, I—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "And now you stand there telling me patience, telling me the plan must hold, as if I haven't already paid the full price of your caution once."

The clock ticked. Somewhere above, wind moved through the cupola.

"I know what it cost you," Elias said quietly.

"Do you? Do you actually know it, or do you *pity* it?" Her hands were shaking; she folded them, forcing them still, a gesture he recognized because it was his own. "You want to stay hidden until the clock speaks for itself. But the clock can be destroyed—you said so yourself. Brass melts. And if it's destroyed before the Commission understands it, then all that remains is your word against Renwick's monopoly, and your word comes wrapped in the fact that you're a liar. You lied about being dead. You know how they'll use it."

"Yes," Elias said. "I know precisely how they'll use it. I've rehearsed it every night for two years."

"Then surrender," she whispered. "Please."

And there it was—the thing beneath all their arguments, laid bare at last between the workbench and the great ticking face. She wanted him visible because invisible people could be erased, and she had spent two years learning exactly how erasure worked. He wanted to stay hidden because hidden things survived, and he had built his entire remaining life on that single principle. Love and resentment braided together so tightly neither could be cut free without cutting the other.

"Elias." Her voice changed. Quieter. Almost the girl she had been. "If they take the clock, and you're still Josiah Cree raking gravel somewhere—there will be no one left who remembers the truth in his own bones. Gearing can be copied. Hands can be forged. But a witness—"

"A witness can be drowned," he said. "Like Merrick."

"Not before the Commission. Not in front of royal commissioners with the whole city watching." She leaned across the workbench, and took his wrist—his actual wrist, flesh and pulse, the first person in two years to touch him knowing who he was. "You brought me here with your handwriting. Your words in my hands started this. Finish it with your words in theirs."

Elias looked down at her hand on his wrist, and thought of Merrick's boat, empty, turning in the grey estuary water. Thought of the candle she had lit for him. Thought of the forged logs in his own hand, aged beautifully by the apprentice he had abandoned, who had spent two years keeping him alive on paper while he was too much a coward to be alive in person.

Above them, the clock struck ten. The true hour. Renwick's dial, a quarter-hour ahead, said nothing at all.

Before either of them could speak again, boots sounded in the corridor—many boots, moving fast—and the door burst open, and Pell stood there white-faced with a paper in his fist.

"The Commission," he said. "They've arrived early. They're coming up the drive *now*, and Sir Aldous is readying the clock for demonstration—and he wants the mechanic present. Both of you. Immediately."

Freya's grip on Elias's wrist tightened once, hard, and released.

The demonstration was hours early. The hidden train was not yet fully wound.

And the clock, their testimony in brass, was about to speak before either of them had agreed on what it should say.
