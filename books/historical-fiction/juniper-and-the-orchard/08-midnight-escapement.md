## 8. Midnight Escapement

They came at half past eleven instead of dawn.

Freya knew it before the knock reached the great doors—knew it in the way the house changed its breathing, servants scattering like startled birds, and in the single sharp look Renwick gave her across the entrance hall as he pulled on his best coat. Not dawn. Tonight. The burning was set for morning, the warrant pinned to the observatory door like a death notice, and Sir Aldous Renwick had decided the Commission should see his restored clock working *before* they watched everything else go to ash.

*He wants them to admire it first,* she thought, wiping her hands on her apron out of habit. *Then burn the rest without a whisper.*

Her heart did not race. That surprised her. Two years of learned invisibility had taught her body to go very still when danger walked into a room, and so she went still now, folding herself into the background of the instrument room with an oil can and a rag, the portrait of a journeyman finishing her labors.

The Commissioners arrived in a gust of cold air and candlelight: three men in wigs of state, their secretary trailing behind with a portable writing desk and the harried expression of a man who would rather be asleep. Renwick received them like a host at his own wedding—warm handshakes, wine already poured, his voice pitched exactly between humility and pride.

"Gentlemen. You honor me. I know the hour is unconventional, but there is a reason." He smiled, and Freya, watching from beside the clock's case, saw how practiced it was—the smile of a man who had rehearsed this scene the way she rehearsed the filing of a gear tooth. "A clock may be inspected by lamplight. But what it *tells* you is best read against the sky itself. And tonight the sky cooperates."

The master clock filled the east wall of the instrument room as it had for sixty years: a case of dark oak gone almost black, a face of painted brass, and behind that face—now—a second train no drawing had ever shown, built by a dead maker who had hidden his testimony in ratios of wheel and pinion, and finished these last three weeks by Freya's own hands while Elias kept watch in the yard below.

She had set the hidden train moving at dusk. It had been turning for five hours.

Renwick led the Commissioners to the face. "You will recall, my lords, the irregularities in the old tables. Errors of observation, accumulated over decades. I have corrected them. Observe—" he gestured to the subsidiary dial, the one marked with the slow crawl of stellar positions, "—the clock now agrees entirely with my revised charts. The precession, the declinations, all brought to order."

Freya kept her eyes on the oil can in her hands. Her thumb moved over the worn brass cap of it—her mother's thimble rode in her apron pocket, always, a small weight against her thigh—and she counted seconds the way other people counted breaths.

The eldest Commissioner, a dry stick of a man named Lord Ashcombe, leaned close to the dial. "And your corrected figures have been lodged with the Admiralty?"

"Every table, my lord. Under seal. The monopoly on navigation rests upon them; I would hardly leave such a thing to memory."

*No,* Freya thought. *Only to fire.*

"Perhaps," said the second Commissioner, a younger man with spectacles and the particular hunger of someone who enjoyed finding flaws, "we might see the mechanism itself? His Majesty's Commission has been assured the restoration is complete. It seems only prudent to confirm it."

Renwick's pause lasted half a heartbeat too long. "Of course," he said, and unlocked the case.

* * *

The back of the clock opened like a chest, and the Commissioners leaned in over the glittering architecture within—wheels within wheels, the pendulum swinging its patient arc, brass catching candleflame until the whole interior seemed to hold a small captive sunset.

Freya had spent three weeks inside that architecture. She knew every tooth she had cut and every tooth she had left deliberately rough. She knew which wheels were hers and which were the maker's, dead forty years, and she knew the hidden train nested behind the going train like a secret whispered behind a spoken sentence—its escapement ratios encoding the true celestial tables in numbers of teeth and beats per hour, legible to any craftsman who knew how to read a train of wheels the way scholars read Latin.

And she knew, with the certainty of her own heartbeat, that the two trains could not both be honest. The false figures Renwick had fed into the visible dials and the true figures locked in the gearing were arithmetic enemies. She had not been able to reconcile them. So she had chosen.

She had let them speak.

"The escapement is of unusual construction," the spectacled Commissioner observed, squinting. "Two trains driving the same hour hand?"

"A redundancy, sir, for accuracy," Renwick said smoothly. "Cross-checking. The finest modern practice."

"Is it." The man straightened, produced a small notebook. "Then the hour strike—"

"Ah." Renwick raised a hand, and his voice took on the warm cadence of a showman arriving at his finale. "Gentlemen, allow me. The clock is about to demonstrate precisely why the Crown may trust my charts above all others." He turned to the face, where the hands crawled toward midnight. "At the stroke of twelve, the subsidiary dial displays the computed position of the pole star's transit for this date—per my corrected tables, soon to be law. You will find it agrees to the second."

Freya set down the oil can.

Elias was somewhere below, in the cellar where they'd moved the last of the maker's drawings, and she felt his absence the way one feels a missing tooth—the tongue returning again and again to the gap. If this went wrong, there would be no one to catch her. She had known that when she wound the hidden train at dusk, and she had done it anyway, because the work was the weapon and a weapon left unwound was only decoration.

The minute hand touched the twelve.

Renwick began, "You will observe the pole star's altitude given as fifty-eight degrees and—"

The clock struck.

It did not strike twelve.

Eleven strokes rolled through the instrument room, deep and deliberate, each one landing in the silence like a stone dropped down a well. Eleven. And with the final stroke, the subsidiary dial—driven now by the hidden train Renwick had never asked enough questions about—climbed through its gears and displayed, in crisp engraved numerals, the pole star's transit altitude for the date:

Fifty-four degrees, twenty-two minutes.

Six degrees of difference. In navigation, six degrees was the distance between a safe harbor and a graveyard of ships.

"What—" Renwick said.

The spectacled Commissioner was already consulting his notebook, then a printed ephemeris from his secretary's desk, then looking up at the dial with an expression Freya would remember the rest of her life: the pure, predatory delight of a correct man catching a wrong one. "Sir Aldous. Your corrected table places the transit at fifty-eight degrees. The clock you present as restored places it at fifty-four and a third. Which are we to believe?" He paused, savoring. "Or shall we step outside and simply *look*?"

The room did not erupt. It did something worse—it went very quiet, the way water goes quiet before it freezes. Lord Ashcombe drew off his spectacles-adjacent lorgnette and studied the dial himself, lips moving over the numerals. The secretary's pen scratched. Renwick stood before his own demonstration, his face performing surprise while something behind the performance worked furiously, and Freya watched him understand—in real time, tooth by tooth, the way she might diagnose a broken train—exactly what had happened inside his clock.

Not a malfunction. A testimony. Gearing that contradicted him in front of the King's own Commissioners, in numbers no jury could argue with, because brass does not commit fraud and brass does not forget.

Triumph rose in her like sap in spring. *We did it. Elias—we did it, you impossible ghost, it's done—*

Renwick turned from the clock.

He did not look at the Commissioners. He looked at her.

Across the room, over the heads of three great men of state, Sir Aldous Renwick found the journeywoman with the oil can and held her gaze, and she watched triumph curdle in her own chest into something cold as the frost forming outside. Because he was not confused. He was not even, anymore, afraid. He was *calculating*, and the calculation had clearly just resolved, and the answer had arrived at her name.

"You," he said quietly, and then, louder, smooth as poured cream: "Gentlemen, forgive this—this embarrassment. Some sabotage has evidently been introduced into the secondary train. My workmen cannot all be trusted; the times being what they are." He smiled at the Commissioners, and it was still, impossibly, charming. "But you have seen enough to understand the gravity. A clock that contradicts its maker's tables cannot be suffered to stand. Whatever errors it harbors, whatever hands corrupted it—" his eyes stayed on Freya, "—the remedy is the same."

"My lord," the spectacled Commissioner began, "surely an examination—"

"Examination after destruction, sir," Renwick said, "when the tables themselves are under royal seal and the clock is merely a vessel for treasonous arithmetic. I invoke the terms of the warrant. This mechanism is irregular. It is to be treated as the records are to be treated." He crossed to the bell pull and rang it, hard, twice. "Hammers. Now. Tonight."

"You would destroy evidence," Ashcombe said slowly, "before our eyes."

"I would destroy a *lie* before your eyes, my lord, so that no one may ever mistake it for truth again." Renwick's voice was gentle, reasonable, terrible. "Bring light. Bring axes. We shall stay and watch it done together—shall we not? A Commission ought to witness."

Boots sounded in the hall. Freya's hands were steady on the oil can and her mind was anything but, running the arithmetic of survival: the hidden train, the proof, the escapement assembly that contained every ratio, every encoded table—complete in itself, small enough for a satchel—

*Small enough to carry.*

Soldiers filled the doorway. Renwick raised his hand toward the open back of the clock, toward three weeks of her work and forty years of a dead man's foresight, and said, pleasantly:

"Begin with the escapement."
