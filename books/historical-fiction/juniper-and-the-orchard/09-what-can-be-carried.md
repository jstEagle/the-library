## 9. What Can Be Carried

### Freya

The soldiers came at dawn with hammers, and the first blow against the great clock sounded to Freya like a bell tolling for a person.

She stood in the transit room with the satchel already packed—she had packed it without admitting to herself that she was packing it—and listened to the iron pry-bars working at the case. Twelve men. Renwick had not troubled to bring fewer. The oak splintered; brass wheels clattered to the flagstones and were kicked into crates marked for the Thames. Two centuries of the maker's patience, undone in an hour by men who could not tell an escapement from a door-latch.

*Let them,* she told herself, and her hand found the satchel strap. *They are destroying the wrong thing.*

Because the clock's face was only a face. The testimony lived in the assembly she had finished at three in the morning, the hidden train of wheels with its ratios cut true—the true declinations of four hundred stars, the true tables, the fraud made legible in brass to anyone who knew how to read a running gear. It sat now in the bottom of the satchel wrapped in oilcloth, weighing perhaps nine pounds, and it was worth more than the observatory entire.

Renwick's voice carried from the instrument room, pleasant as a man discussing drainage. "The dial first. Mind the mechanism beneath it—I want the trains intact until I've seen them myself."

He wanted to see what had contradicted him. He wanted to hold the proof in his own hands before it went into the river.

Freya moved along the gallery behind the instrument racks, keeping the great telescope's tube between herself and the doorway. She had done this all her life—made herself narrow, made herself furniture—and she hated it now with a fresh hatred, because invisibility had been a choice once, a craft, and this morning it was merely fear wearing her shape.

Elias was somewhere below. They had argued until the sky greyed, and neither had won, which meant both had.

A soldier came up the stair with a crowbar over his shoulder and stopped short when he saw her. "You there. Girl. You're the mender?"

"I'm the clockmaker's journeyman." Her forged papers said so. Her whole life said so.

"Director wants you below. He's questions about the works."

She lifted the satchel. "My tools."

He shrugged and let her pass. That was the thing about being no one: even soldiers' eyes slid off you like rain off waxed canvas. She descended into the smell of split oak and lamp oil, past two men levering the lunar dial off its arbor, and found Renwick standing before the opened movement, his gloved hands folded behind his back like a gentleman at a viewing of paintings.

"There she is," he said warmly. "The hands inside my clock."

The room went quiet around her. Soldiers paused mid-labor.

"You'll want to be careful with the fourth wheel," Freya said. "It's got a hairline at the root. Drop it and you'll never find the fault."

Renwick smiled. Behind the smile his eyes were doing arithmetic. "Mrs. Coldharbour—or should I say Miss? Your papers are very fine. Almost as fine as the maintenance logs that appeared last week in a dead man's hand." He stepped closer. "I offered you a position once. I withdraw it. Aldous Renwick does not extend charity twice to the same nest of vipers. Sergeant—this woman and the laborer who calls himself Marsh are to be held for the Commission. Gently. I want them presentable."

Two soldiers took her arms—not roughly, but finally, the way a vice closes—and the satchel swung heavy at her hip and she thought, with terrible clarity: *nine pounds, and I cannot reach it, and Elias is not here, and everything we built will go into the Thames in crates.*

That was when the doors of the transit room opened, and Elias Vane walked in wearing a groundskeeper's coat, carrying himself like a man arriving punctually for his own trial.

### Elias

He had rehearsed nothing, because rehearsal was lying and he had done enough of that for two lifetimes.

He had expected to feel terror. What he felt instead, crossing that floor with every musket in the room turning toward him, was *tallness*—the strange, unearned lightness of a man setting down a weight he had carried since the night he watched Nathaniel Prew's body pulled from the mill pond and said nothing, did nothing, wrote nothing, was nothing.

"Sir Aldous Renwick," he said, and his voice did not shake, though it was rougher than the voice Renwick remembered. "You have in your custody the finest clockmaker in England, and you are holding her arms as if she were the criminal."

Renwick turned slowly. Elias watched recognition arrive in stages—the stance first, then the hands, then the face itself—and watched the director's composure hold by sheer force of will, like a cracked wheel holding true through one revolution.

"That is impossible," Renwick said. "Elias Vane is dead. I signed the certificate."

"You signed a good many things." Elias kept walking, one step, another. Every step bought a second. Behind him, faintly, he heard Freya's voice low and urgent, heard a scuffle, heard her say something about tools spilling—and he did not turn, because turning would spend the seconds she was buying with his name. "You signed observations you never made. You signed charts with stars moved a full degree east so that no longitude but yours would resolve. You signed Nathaniel Prew's effects over to his widow with your condolences, and you have slept every night since."

"Take him," Renwick said, but softly, to the sergeant, as if volume might make the thing real too soon. "Hold him."

Hands seized Elias's shoulders. He let them. He looked up at the ruined movement of the master clock, its wheels half-stripped, its beautiful lie exposed down to the arbors—and then across the room to where two soldiers had released Freya to help with him, and where Freya was crouched over her scattered tools, gathering them one by one into the satchel at her hip, unhurried, invisible, brilliant.

*Take the train,* he willed her. *Leave me the rest.*

"You'll hang for this," Renwick said, coming close, lowering his voice so only Elias could hear it. "Desecration of a royal warrant. False identity. Whatever name you give the Commission, I will bury you under a worse one. I have buried better men."

"You buried them in paper," Elias said. "Paper burns. Brass doesn't."

Something flickered across Renwick's face then—doubt, quick as a shadow of a bird—and Elias understood that the director had seen the satchel after all, or guessed at it, and did not know yet whether it mattered. That doubt was worth more than freedom. That doubt would keep Renwick careless before the Commission, defensive, a man arguing with arithmetic he hadn't checked.

The sergeant bound Elias's wrists with cord, not kindly. "Name?" the sergeant demanded, for the record.

Elias straightened. Two years of *Marsh*, of mumbling and stooping and letting men laugh at his imagined illiteracy, fell away like a shed coat.

"Elias Vane," he said. "Keeper of the Thornfield clock. Formerly first assistant at the Royal Observatory, Greenwich. Witness to Sir Aldous Renwick's falsification of the celestial tables in the year 1746, and witness to the murder-by-convenience of Nathaniel Prew, who objected to it." He raised his voice then, deliberately, so that every soldier, every laborer, every man hauling crates toward the river could carry the words out of this room and repeat them in taverns until they grew legs. "Write it down. I am alive, and I remember, and I have put what I remember into gearing that cannot lie."

"Get him out," Renwick said, and for the first time his voice was not charming at all.

### Freya

They marched Elias past her, and there was no time, and so she did the only thing left: she reached out as he went by and gripped his bound hands, cord and all, for the space of one breath.

His fingers pressed hers back. His eyes said what his mouth had spent on the record already: *finish it.* And something else, something younger than the rest of his face: *forgive me.* And something else entirely, quiet as a closing door: *proud.*

Then the doors swallowed him, and the morning light outside showed her the column of soldiers, the cart, the director standing in the courtyard consulting his watch as if the world were still punctual.

Freya stood alone among the wreckage of the master clock. The soldiers were finishing—two men carried the great dial out flat as a tabletop; another swept broken pinions into a barrow. No one watched the journeywoman. No one ever had.

She crouched, gathered the last of her tools, and stood with the satchel on her shoulder, nine pounds heavier than the woman who had walked into this room. Through the window she watched the cart take the road toward London, toward the Commission, toward whatever cells waited for honest men. She watched until it cleared the gate.

Then she adjusted the strap, felt through the oilcloth the familiar geometry of the escapement assembly—her work and his, truth in miniature, complete—and stepped over the splintered remains of the greatest clock in England, out the doors, and onto the road, walking toward London at a pace just short of running.

Three days. The Commission sat in three days, and Renwick believed the proof was at the bottom of the Thames, and somewhere in Newgate or a magistrate's cellar Elias Vane was waiting to see whether his apprentice's hands could do what they had been trained for.

Freya Coldharbour, who had measured her whole life in what she could carry, tightened her grip on the strap and counted the miles.
