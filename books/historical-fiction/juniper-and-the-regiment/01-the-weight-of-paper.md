## 1. The Weight of Paper

Ines Sandoval measured her life in things that could be carried.

A leather satchel with a cracked strap, wide enough for ledgers and narrow enough not to advertise itself. A spare tire wrapped in canvas beneath the floorboards of the Hupmobile, because the roads north ate rubber and no garage between here and the border would sell to a woman alone. A pencil stub sharpened with a kitchen knife. A hairpin bent into a letter-opener's shape, which was a tool nobody would ever ask her to explain.

And secrets. Those weighed nothing at all, which is why she carried so many.

By half past seven she had the engine running outside the Marchetti house on Calle de los Tilos, because Señora Marchetti believed punctuality was a form of respect and Ines believed it was a form of armor. If you were always where you said you'd be, doing exactly what you said you'd do, no one looked closely at you. No one asked what else you might be carrying or knowing or wanting. Ten years of driving had taught her that the world barely registered a woman behind a wheel until she gave it a reason to, and Ines intended to die without ever giving it one.

The morning smelled of eucalyptus and coal smoke. She let the engine idle and watched the front door in the rearview mirror, counting the seconds as she did every morning—not impatiently, but the way a sailor counts waves. Adela Marchetti emerged at eight exactly, hat pinned at a defiant angle, a stack of correspondence clutched to her chest like a breastplate.

"Good morning, Ines."

"Señora." She opened the door from inside—the handle on the passenger side still stuck in cold weather—and took the letters when they were handed back to her, because that too was routine: the mail rode up front with the driver.

It had started as practicality. Adela received more letters than any one person should—petitions, invitations, threats dressed as courtesy, money wrapped in tissue paper from women who could spare almost nothing—and there wasn't time to read them all between meetings. So at the first red light, Ines would sort. Urgent to the left. Money to the middle. The rest by district, because Adela liked to know which neighborhoods were waking up.

Somewhere in the second year, sorting became reading. Reading became memorizing. Memorizing became something she had never named aloud, even to herself, though she suspected it had a name and that the name was not flattering.

"The committee in Rosario wants to move the rally," Adela said, settling her skirts. "They're frightened."

"Rosario is always frightened."

"Rosario has reason to be." Adela looked out the window at the fruit sellers arranging their oranges into pyramids, at the boys racing hoops down the cobblestones, at the ordinary morning going on around them as if the century weren't turning underneath it. "There's a new undersecretary at the Interior Ministry. He believes we can be managed."

"Everyone can be managed," Ines said. "It depends on the price."

Adela laughed—a short, surprised sound—and Ines felt the small warm flare of it, and extinguished it immediately, the way you'd pinch out a match. Warmth was not portable. Warmth stayed behind when you drove away.

The light changed. She drove.

* * *

The first meeting was at the Casa del Pueblo, a hall that smelled of dust and decades of other people's causes. Ines waited outside with the engine off and the hood up, because a woman under the hood of her own automobile attracted less attention than a woman loitering beside one. She tightened something that didn't need tightening. She listened.

Inside, faint through the hall's high windows, Adela's voice rose and fell like weather. Ines knew the speech by now—all four versions of it. There was the gentle version for nervous newcomers, the fiery version for the newspapers, the legalistic version for officials, and the fourth version, the one she used only when the room felt safe, where she spoke about her mother and her grandmother and the long unbroken chain of women who had wanted things they were never allowed to name. Ines had heard the fourth version eleven times. She had never been inside for it. Drivers waited outside. That was the arrangement, and arrangements were how Ines kept herself intact.

A man in a gray suit crossed the street, paused, and pretended to read the hall's notice board. He had done the same thing Tuesday. He had good shoes and bad posture and the particular stillness of someone listening through his eyes. Ines wiped her hands on a rag slowly, thoroughly, a woman with nothing in her head but grease.

He moved on. She committed him to memory anyway: gray suit, part on the left, cigarette case with an engraved lid. Her memory was the largest thing she owned. It held street names and license plates, the faces of men who photographed meetings, the exact wording of forty threatening letters, and the fact that Tomás Marchetti had drunk three brandies at Sunday dinner and then lied about the number. It held everything, and it weighed nothing, and some nights it was so heavy she couldn't sleep.

At ten minutes past eleven, the meeting broke. Women spilled onto the steps in clusters of two and three, adjusting hats, exchanging the quick fierce embraces of people who see each other rarely and risk much. One of them pressed an envelope into Adela's hand. Ines watched the exchange from thirty feet away and catalogued it automatically: plain envelope, no stamp, hand-delivered, the woman's glance over both shoulders afterward.

"Back to the office," Adela said, "and then the meeting with the deputy at two. We'll eat in the car."

"We always eat in the car."

"One day I'll take you somewhere proper, Ines, with tablecloths."

"I'd only spill on it."

Adela smiled at that, genuinely, and Ines felt again the treacherous pull of being liked by her employer, and turned the key harder than necessary.

* * *

At the corner of Alem and the boulevard, stopped at a light, Ines sorted the morning's mail against the steering wheel. Two petitions, urgent-left. An envelope of money from a widow in Tigre, middle, with a note that made Adela look away out the window for a moment. An invitation to a garden party hosted by a senator's wife who wanted, Adela said, to be seen associating with the movement now that associating was becoming fashionable.

Then the last envelope. Plain paper, cheap stock, the kind sold in any stationer's. No return address. The handwriting was careful in the way handwriting is careful when the writer does not write often, or is writing while angry, or both.

Ines had learned to read upside down before she'd learned to drive. She read this one in six seconds, at the red light, while her face did absolutely nothing.

It was not the worst letter Adela had received. It was not even close. But it used the word *daughter*, and it used the word *regret*, and its grammar had the flat, unhurried confidence of an institution rather than a crank. Cranks signed their names. This one offered only the printed crest of nothing at all.

"Ines?"

"Petition from La Boca," she said smoothly, sliding the letter to the bottom of the pile and handing the rest back. "Forty signatures, but half are illegible."

The light changed. She drove. The letter sat against her thigh like a coal.

She would decide later what Adela needed to know. That was how Ines had come to think of it—not *whether* to tell, but *what* the señora needed, as though she were a doctor administering doses. It had seemed, at the beginning, like a kindness. Filter the noise. Spare the woman who already carried everything. Somewhere along the way the kindness had become a habit, and the habit had become a power, and the power had become hers alone, exercised daily at red lights across the city, invisible and complete.

She hated it a little. She kept doing it anyway. It was, after all, the only room in the world where she was the one holding the keys.

* * *

Lunch was cold chicken and bread on Adela's side of the car, an identical portion passed forward to Ines's side, which was another thing that had no name—employers did not feed their drivers the same food, and yet Adela Marchetti had done it since the first week, without comment, as if it were obvious. Ines ate it the way she ate all kindness: quickly, gratefully, and looking for the catch.

Between bites, Adela read the garden party invitation twice and did not laugh.

"My brother wants me to stop," she said suddenly, to the windshield.

"Tomás says many things."

"He says the government is assembling files on us. Real files, in real ministries, with our photographs." She turned the invitation over in her hands. "He would know. He stamps things all day at the customs house; he knows how paper becomes power." A pause. "Do you think about that, Ines? Being a file in a drawer somewhere?"

"No, señora."

"Liar," Adela said, fondly, and went back to reading, and Ines gripped the wheel and thought: *you have no idea.*

Because yes. Yes, she thought about it constantly. She thought about the drawer her whole life would fit into—one folder, maybe two: employment references, a bank book with modest sums, the certificate from the mechanics' course she'd taken under her father's name because they wouldn't admit a girl. A file so thin a clerk could read it during a lunch break. Some days the thought terrified her. Other days—it was the shameful other days—she understood that the thinness was the point, that invisibility was the safest country she would ever live in, and she paid its taxes gladly: no husband, no close friends, no signature on any petition, her name absent from every list Adela's movement kept. The other organizers joked that Ines Sandoval was the most careful woman in the province. They meant it as praise. They didn't know they were describing a wall.

The deputy's meeting ran long. Dusk came down purple over the city, and the lamps came on one by one along the avenue like a slow idea spreading, and Ines drove Adela home through streets she knew better than her own heartbeat. At the door of the house on Calle de los Tilos, Adela gathered her things, then stopped.

"There's a dinner Thursday," she said. "Family only, technically, but Tomás wants to talk strategy and he trusts you, oddly. Will you stay? You could sit at the table, for once, instead of the kitchen."

For once. As if Adela knew Ines had never once sat at anyone's table. As if she'd noticed.

"I'll stay," Ines heard herself say.

"Good." Adela climbed out, then leaned back in, and for a moment her sharp clever face was unreadable in the lamplight. "The mail today. Anything I should know about?"

The letter lay in the satchel at Ines's hip, four inches of cheap paper weighing nothing at all.

"Nothing urgent, señora."

"Good night, Ines."

She watched the door close. Then she drove to the garage on the corner, put the Hupmobile away, walked the four blocks to her boardinghouse in the dark, and sat on the narrow bed with the letter in her lap, reading it a third time under the weak gas jet.

*Your days are numbered,* it said. *We regret only that your daughter's name will be spoken alongside yours when the accounts are settled. Institutions do not make fine distinctions.*

Adela had no daughter. Ines had checked, long ago, in the way she checked everything. Which meant either the writer was ignorant—or the writer knew something about the Marchetti family that Ines did not.

Outside, a tram bell rang, far off. Ines folded the letter along its original creases, slid it beneath the false bottom of her satchel next to the hairpin and the pencil stub, and lay down with her boots on, the way she always did, ready to move.

She told herself she was protecting Adela. She was getting very good at telling herself things.

Thursday was three days away.
