## 3. The Marchetti Household

The Marchetti house stood on Calle de los Tilos the way certain women did at parties—beautiful, guarded, and pretending not to notice who was watching. It was a narrow townhouse with an iron balcony and a facade the color of unripe lemons, and I had been driving up to it for eleven months without ever being invited through the front door. Servants went around the side. I was not quite a servant. That was the trouble with my position: it had no door of its own.

Adela emerged at seven o'clock precisely, in a dove-gray dress that meant family dinner rather than public speech. Family dinners required armor of a different weave.

"You'll take supper in the kitchen," she said, settling her gloves finger by finger. "But afterward I want you nearby. Tomás is back from the northern posts, and he'll have questions about the schedule. Let him think you're simple."

"Let him think I'm simple," I repeated. It was a familiar instruction and I performed it well, because I was not simple at all, and because Adela knew it, and because neither of us said so out loud. Our whole arrangement lived in sentences like that—half instruction, half conspiracy, entirely unexamined.

She went up the front steps and the brass knocker sounded once behind her. I drove the car around to the mews, where the cook's boy took the keys, and then I went in through the kitchen, as I always did, carrying nothing but myself and whatever I had read that day.

* * *

The Marchetti kitchen was warm and ruled by Señora Paz, a widow built like a church bell who fed me before she asked me anything, which is more courtesy than most houses offered. There was chicken in green sauce, bread still cracking from the oven, and two other servants eating with the efficient silence of people on the clock.

They talked around me. The gardener's cousin had been stopped on the northern road, he said—soldiers, asking names, turning over crates of fruit with their bayonets like they expected to find revolution under the oranges. Señora Paz clucked. The boy said his uncle called it "the nervous season." Nobody said what the soldiers were nervous about, because nobody in that kitchen needed to. We all knew whose name made the government flinch, and we all knew whose kitchen we were sitting in.

I ate and listened and kept my face arranged into mildness. This was my oldest skill, older than driving, older than reading other people's mail: the face that gives nothing back. A mirror dusted with fog.

From upstairs came the sound of the household assembling—the grandfather clock striking, chairs drawn across the parquet, and beneath it all a voice I hadn't heard in eight months. Tomás Marchetti had a voice like a customs stamp: decisive, slightly bored, designed to end conversations rather than begin them. Even through the floorboards it changed the temperature of the house. Señora Paz's shoulders climbed toward her ears. The boy stopped talking mid-sentence.

"The brother," she said to me, as if I were new. "He comes twice a year and reorganizes everything. Last time he dismissed the gardener for keeping accounts in a notebook." She crossed herself with a ladle still in hand, which I took to be unintentional blasphemy but sincere anyway.

"He's a customs officer," the boy supplied. "Northern posts. He inspects things."

"He inspects things," Señora Paz agreed darkly, and put more chicken on my plate, as though feeding me were also a form of fortification against him.

* * *

Afterward I was sent up with the coffee—not by Adela, but by Señora Paz, who pressed the silver pot into my hands and said, "You've got a face for it. She hasn't tonight." Meaning: Adela was rattled, and a rattled Adela drank too much coffee, and it was better if the person pouring it understood why without being told. Half-servant, half-confidante. Neither fully. I carried the tray up the back stairs, past the portraits of Marchettis dead and dignified, and came out through the butler's pantry into the dining room's edge like smoke finding a draft.

The table held four: Adela; Tomás at its head, though the house wasn't his to head; his wife Lucía, thin and bright-eyed, who spoke only when spoken to and looked at no one while doing it; and old Aunt Rosaura, who was nearly deaf and therefore the only honest person present, since she said exactly what she thought and couldn't hear anyone object.

"—and I'm telling you, Adela, the ministry has lists now." Tomás said it the way other men discussed weather. He had his brother's forehead and none of his sister's fire; where Adela burned visibly, Tomás was the kind of cold that organized itself into ledgers. His uniform collar sat open—he was home, technically—but the uniform was there anyway, draped over the chair like a third guest. "Names, affiliations, meeting places. You are on those lists. This is not speculation. This is Tuesday."

"How flattering." Adela's voice was light. Her hands, breaking bread, were not.

"It is not *flattering*, it is *exposure*. There is a difference between being a nuisance and being a file." He accepted coffee without looking up when I poured it. I was furniture; I had long practice being furniture. "A nuisance gets ignored until it exhausts itself. A file gets acted upon. Do you understand the distinction? Someone has decided your nuisance has structure. Structure implies leadership. Leadership implies—" he waved a fork, delicately, "—targets."

"And you came all this way to tell me to stop?"

"I came all this way because Maman asked, God rest her, and because Lucía worries, and because I would prefer my sister not be arrested in a manner that requires me to explain her to my superiors." He finally looked at her. There was something underneath the bureaucratic frost—I caught it for half a second, the way you catch a fish's flank flashing under gray water. Fear, maybe. Or its cousin, love, wearing fear's coat. "I am not asking you to stop. I'm asking you to be careful about who carries what, and who knows whom, and what passes through this house."

"Nothing dangerous passes through this house," Adela said, and I stood behind her shoulder holding the coffee pot, having read every letter that passed through that house for eleven months, and said nothing.

Lucía laughed—a small glass sound, quickly swallowed. Aunt Rosaura, mishearing everything, announced loudly that the chicken was excellent, and for a moment the table was grateful to her.

I refilled cups. I withdrew to the pantry doorway, where I could see without being seen, which was becoming less a habit than a residence. From there I watched Tomás watch his sister when she wasn't looking, and watched Adela do the same, two people conducting surveillance on each other across a dinner table, and understood that this was what family meant in this house: love conducted entirely through observation, affection expressed as vigilance. No one touched. No one said anything true. The food was excellent.

Tomás asked about the schedule. Adela gave him the public version—lectures, the printing cooperative, the schoolhouse meetings, all legal, all tedious. She left off the northern route entirely, the one she'd begun murmuring about three weeks ago, the one that required a driver willing to leave before dawn. I knew about the northern route because I memorized what mattered. Tomás did not know about it because Adela had decided he wouldn't, and watching her lie to him with her whole serene face, I felt something turn over in my chest—not alarm exactly. Recognition. She lied the way I did. Fluently, protectively, alone.

Perhaps that was why she trusted me. Not despite what I was, but because of it.

* * *

Dinner broke up at ten. Lucía retired with a headache that predated the soup. Aunt Rosaura was conveyed upstairs by the boy, still praising the chicken. And Tomás stayed at the table with the decanter, and Adela went to him—or rather, they arrived at each other the way tired countries arrive at borders—and I heard the register drop, the public voices set down like coats.

"Papá's letters," Tomás said quietly. "Have you kept them? All of them?"

"In the cedar chest. Why?"

"Because a man from the ministry came through my post last month asking about Papá's correspondences. Old ones. Twenty years old." A pause long enough to pour whiskey into. "Papa has been dead nine years, Adela. Whatever he signed, whoever he knew—it has not died with him. Someone is going through the past with a lamp. Be careful it isn't found in your chest."

"Our father signed nothing but shipping manifests."

"Everyone signs something eventually." Then, softer, almost human: "Burn them if you must. Or give them to me. I know how these things get weighed."

"They're mine," Adela said, in the voice she used on ministers and bishops, and the conversation closed like a drawer.

I should have withdrawn. The coffee was finished; my function had ended; the pantry draft was waiting to swallow me. But I stood in that doorway one minute longer, holding a silver pot gone cold, and I watched brother and sister sit in the wreckage of their father's name, and I wanted—with an ache so specific it felt physical—to be at that table. Not serving it. Sitting at it. To have a dead father whose letters concerned someone, a chair that was assumed, a place where my silence would be family silence rather than professional discretion.

Eleven months I had read their mail, memorized their routes, kept their secrets better than they kept their own. Eleven months, and I stood in a doorway holding cold coffee. Longing sharpens best at close range. At a distance you can call it ambition. Up close, across a dining room, with the decanter breathing between brother and sister, you have to call it what it is.

I went down the back stairs. I put the pot in Señora Paz's sink. And I told myself, as I walked out to the mews to check a tire that didn't need checking, that invisibility was a kind of safety, that doors were for people with names on files, that the cold air and the car and the road were mine in a way no dining room could ever be.

I was good at telling myself things. I'd had practice.

It was while I stood in the mews running my thumb along the spare tire's rim that the boy came running out of the kitchen door with an envelope in his fist—the late post, delivered by a courier who hadn't waited. For the señora, he said, marked urgent, and the courier had looked up at the lit windows a long time before leaving, the way men look when they're memorizing a house.

I took it from him. Of course I took it. I always took it.

The envelope was heavy cream stock, no return address, and the seal had been pressed so hard it had cracked. Under the mews lamp I turned it over once, twice, feeling its weight—paper, but paper with intention in it—and somewhere north of the city, on roads I hadn't driven yet, soldiers were turning over crates of oranges with their bayonets, asking names.

I slipped the envelope into my satchel, unopened. For now.

Some things you read immediately. Some things you carry a while first, letting them warm against your body, learning their weight—because once you know what's inside, you can never again be the person who didn't.

I carried it inside, and I did not sleep.
