## 4. Orders Come Down

The rumor arrived the way most dangerous things did that year: folded inside an ordinary envelope, passed hand to hand at a meeting Ines wasn't supposed to attend, spoken about only in kitchens and cars and other rooms where men in uniforms never stood.

*They're drawing up lists.*

Ines heard it first from a baker's wife in Santa Clara who had cousins in the provincial capital, then again from Mateo, who delivered it with his usual shrug, as though the imminent machinery of the state were a change in weather he'd read about in the almanac. *Lists of names*, he said. *Organizers first. Then the ones who drive them around.*

He'd smiled when he said it. Mateo smiled when he said everything, which was why it had taken Ines years to learn which smiles meant something.

She didn't tell Adela right away. That was becoming a habit she didn't examine too closely—deciding what her employer needed to know, curating the world before it reached her. The threatening note still lived at the bottom of Ines's satchel, wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth, and some mornings she felt it there like a stone in her shoe: small, constant, hers.

By Thursday the rumors had grown teeth. A printer in the north quarter who produced the movement's pamphlets found his shop padlocked with a customs seal on the door—customs, oddly, not police—and no one could tell him what ordinance he'd violated or when he might petition to have it lifted. A teacher was dismissed for allowing girls in her classroom to debate the vote. And on the road to Villaseca, where Adela was scheduled to speak Saturday, soldiers had stopped traffic at the rail crossing for the third day running, asking travelers for papers no traveler had ever been required to carry.

"The roads are sprouting fences," Mateo said, leaning into the car window while Ines waited outside the print cooperative. He smelled of ink and cigarettes, as always, and his eyes went past her to the street corners as he talked, cataloguing. "You've seen them?"

"I've seen soldiers," Ines said. "That's not new."

"It's new where they're standing." He tapped the roof of the Ford twice, a little drumbeat of warning. "River bridge yesterday. They turned back two carts of flour because the driver couldn't say who'd milled it. Flour, Ines. They're practicing."

"Practicing what?"

"How to make waiting feel like a favor they're doing you." He straightened up, glanced down the street again. "Tell Adela to keep her name off the passenger manifest for anything going north. If she travels, let her travel like nobody."

"Adela Marchetti travels like nobody?" Ines almost laughed. "She's six feet of voice in a green coat. She couldn't travel like nobody if she crawled."

Mateo smiled one of his unreadable smiles. "Then somebody should teach her to crawl," he said, and walked off into the crowd, unhurried, a man with nowhere particular to be and all the time in the world to get there.

Ines sat with her hands on the wheel and watched him go and felt, for no reason she could name, the cold slide of water down her spine.

* * *

The Marchetti house on Friday evening was lit like a ship making signals. Word had come that the Villaseca committee wanted Adela badly—their membership had doubled since spring, and a speech from her would cement it—but the same word came wrapped in caution: the garrison commander there was young, ambitious, and eager to be noticed by the capital. A speech by Adela Marchetti would be exactly the kind of notice he wanted.

"They'll photograph me at the podium and arrest me in the doorway," Adela said, pacing the length of the parlor. Ines stood by the sideboard with a tray of coffee she'd poured herself, because the household staff had thinned again and nobody had told her not to. Half-servant, half-confidante; she occupied both roles and neither.

"Then don't stand in the doorway," Tomás said from the armchair. He had his boots off and a newspaper across his knee, but he wasn't reading it; Ines had noticed hours ago that he'd been holding the same page since dinner. "Cancel."

"I don't cancel."

"You cancel when the alternative is worse than canceling." He turned the page without looking at it. "There are men in this province whose whole careers depend on finding a reason. You've spent ten years handing out reasons wrapped in ribbons."

"What a flattering account of my life's work."

"Ade." His voice dropped, and something older moved under it—the boy who had presumably once shared a nursery with her. "The lists are real. I've seen the paperwork move through my own office. Not the names yet, but the forms that carry names. I'm telling you as family."

The word *family* settled over the room like dust. Ines kept her eyes on the coffee service. She had learned, in the Marchetti house, to become very good at listening while appearing to arrange silverware.

"And I'm telling you," Adela said, "that the moment we go quiet is the moment they learn it works. One canceled speech becomes two. Two becomes ten. Then we're whispering in kitchens until we die, and the vote goes to our granddaughters' granddaughters, who will have been taught to be grateful for the waiting." She stopped pacing and faced her brother fully. "I'll speak in Villaseca. But I won't take the main road."

Tomás looked at her for a long moment. Then, without turning his head, he said: "Ines. Is the coffee poisoned, or do you intend to pour it?"

"Coming, señor."

She poured. Tomás took his cup without looking at her either, which was its own kind of message. But as she retreated to the sideboard, he spoke again, mild as milk, and the words landed on her like a hand on the back of her neck.

"She trusts you with that car the way other people trust surgeons with knives. Don't prove her romantic."

"No, señor."

"There's a back way to Villaseca," Adela said, already moving on, already three steps into the future the rest of them were still standing in. "The old mill road along the river. Tomás, you know it—the one your survey crew complained about in the spring?"

"I know it." Something crossed Tomás's face, quickly gone—a flicker, a hesitation, gone before Ines could read it. "It's rough. It hasn't been maintained since the mill closed. And it comes out past the rail yards, so you'd want to join the main road only for the last two kilometers."

"Two kilometers on a public road is nothing," Adela said. "Two hundred would be a problem."

"It's also," Tomás said, setting his cup down with a small precise click, "the route nobody watches. Which cuts both ways, Ade. If something happens to you on that road—if the car fails, if you meet anyone you didn't choose to meet—no one will see it happen either."

The clock on the mantel counted four seconds into the silence. Ines counted them without meaning to. It was another of her habits: measuring time in portable things, in counts and breaths and the number of steps between doors.

"Then Ines will make sure the car doesn't fail," Adela said, "and we will hope the road stays empty." She picked up the schedule from the mantel, unfolded it, held it out to Ines. "Saturday morning. Early—before light, if you think the Ford can find its way in the dark."

"The Ford can find its way anywhere I point it," Ines said, and heard how easily the confidence came out of her, how practiced it sounded even to her own ears.

But that night, alone in her narrow room above the garage with the schedule open on her knees, she traced the route with one finger and understood what Adela had actually asked her.

Not a road. A confession of fear. Adela Marchetti, who stood on stages in front of hundreds and dared governments to blink first, had asked her driver to take her a way she'd never driven, in the dark, because she was afraid—and she had chosen to say it in the language of logistics rather than admit it aloud. *Drive me somewhere you can't protect me,* she had said, *and pretend it's about efficiency.*

Ines folded the schedule and put it in her satchel, next to the oilcloth packet, next to the spare tire irons and the jack handle and every other small hard thing she carried so that larger soft things wouldn't break.

* * *

She drove the mill road alone that night.

She hadn't planned to. She'd told herself she was checking the ford at the river bend, where the spring surveys had flagged erosion; she told herself the headlights needed testing against unlit ground; she told herself a great many reasonable things while she rolled the Ford out of the garage at eleven o'clock and pointed it north with no passenger and no permission.

The truth was simpler. She could not ask the car to keep Adela alive on a road she herself had never seen. Whatever else Ines Sandoval was—thief of letters, keeper of secrets, a woman who had made invisibility into a trade—she was competent, and competence was the one form of love she trusted herself to perform.

The road was terrible. It began confidently enough behind the tannery, hard-packed clay between poplars, but within two kilometers it narrowed into ruts baked hard as iron, and the poplars gave way to scrub, and the scrub gave way to the river, sliding dark and quick below the left-hand bank. Her headlights carved a tunnel out of the black and the night closed behind her like water. No houses. No lights except, far ahead, a single orange point that resolved slowly into the rail yards' signal tower, patient as a lighthouse.

Twice she stopped and got out to test the ground ahead with the tire iron. Once she startled a herd of goats that materialized out of the darkness like a committee, regarded her with flat yellow disapproval, and dissolved again. She stood in the middle of the road with her heart hammering and laughed—one short bark of sound that the river swallowed immediately—and got back in the car.

At the old mill, ruins slumped against the sky. At the river bend, the ford was passable if taken at an angle, the water running shin-deep over gravel, and she sat with the engine idling in the shallows longer than she needed to, watching the moon come up over the water and thinking about lists.

Names on paper. She had spent her whole adult life believing that names on paper were the safest thing in the world—that a woman who appeared on no list, in no ledger, in no photograph, moved through danger like light through glass. Now the government was building its machine out of precisely that: paper, lists, seals. The weapon had changed shape and she had not.

Somewhere behind her, back toward the city, there were men whose job was to decide whether *Sandoval, Ines — driver* belonged on a page. She thought of the sealed note in her satchel, and of Rufina Ochoa's name, which she'd heard mentioned twice now in the movement's inner circles—a journalist, they said, who wasn't afraid of garrisons—and pushed the thought away before it could become a plan. Plans were for later. Tonight was for ruts and fords and getting home before the household woke.

The last stretch joined the main road two kilometers south of Villaseca, exactly as Tomás had said. And there, lit by a lantern on a tripod, she saw it: the checkpoint. Four soldiers, a sawhorse barrier, a canvas-topped truck pulled onto the shoulder with its rear doors open. A farmer's cart stood at the barrier, and one soldier was going through sacks of onions with the thoroughness of a man being paid by the onion.

Ines slowed, stopped in the shadow of the last poplar, and watched.

They weren't stopping everyone. They waved through a bicyclist. They waved through a couple in a motorcar—an older man driving, a woman beside him—after barely a glance. They stopped carts. They stopped men traveling alone on foot. And when a young woman came walking up the verge with a market basket, they stopped her too, and made her set the basket down, and one of them crouched to go through it while the others watched, and the woman stood with her hands clasped tight in front of her apron, smiling the fixed empty smile of someone performing harmlessness with her whole body.

Ines knew that smile. She had invented it.

She eased the Ford backward off the road, killed her lights, and turned around at the mouth of a farm track with the care of a woman defusing something. The return run through the dark was faster, meaner, the ruts trying to shake her teeth loose, the river flashing white at the bend, and all the while her mind worked at the problem the way her hands worked a gearshift—automatically, competently, without consulting her fear.

The main road was watched. The mill road was clean, for now, but it ended two kilometers from the same soldiers. There was no route to Villaseca that did not, eventually, pass a lantern and a sawhorse and a young man with the power to make a woman unpack her whole life onto the roadside.

Tomorrow morning she would drive Adela Marchetti—the loudest six feet of voice in the province, green coat and all—into that arithmetic.

Back at the house, the garage clock showed half past two. Ines parked, checked the oil by matchlight, wiped her hands on a rag, and stood a moment in the cold doorway looking at the sleeping shape of the car.

Then she went inside, took out her satchel, and laid everything on the workbench in a neat row, the way she inventoried her life when it started to feel unsteady: jack, handles, rag, schedule, oilcloth packet.

She weighed the packet in her palm. The threatening note. The first secret she had ever decided Adela shouldn't have.

If the lists were real, then secrets had a shelf life now. Everything hidden would eventually be found, or sold, or used. The question was not whether her satchel would be opened—it was who would be standing there when it was, and whether what spilled out would protect Adela or bury her.

Ines put everything back except the oilcloth packet. That she sat with, turning it over, the candle guttering.

Outside, far to the north, a train whistle crossed the valley, and beneath it, fainter, another sound she might have imagined: the slow bell of the garrison, marking an hour she couldn't name.

She slid the packet into her coat pocket instead of the satchel. She didn't let herself ask why. Some decisions you made with your hands before your mind agreed to sign the order.

Tomorrow, before light, she would knock on the kitchen door and Adela would come out with her notes and her green coat and her terrible brave certainty, and Ines would say, "The mill road, señora. I drove it tonight. It holds."

And Adela would look at her—really look, the way she occasionally did, as if the driver were a window she'd never thought to clean—and Ines would have to decide, in that moment, whether a woman carrying one secret could afford to pick up a second.
