## 6. Three Checkpoints

I learned the road before I learned the car.

That was Tomás Marchetti's doing, though he never knew it. In my first week driving for Adela, he stood in the courtyard with a cigarette and a map folded to the size of a hymnal, and he said, "A driver who knows only one road is a passenger with a steering wheel. Learn three ways out of every place, señorita, or don't bother learning any."

He'd meant it as an insult dressed as advice—I was a woman holding a crank, and it amused him—but I took it the way I took everything in that house: silently, completely, and without giving him the satisfaction of knowing I'd heard. That night I copied the map onto butcher paper by lamplight, and for two years since, I have kept the roads of this province in my head the way other women keep recipes. Not written down. Never written down. Written things can be found, and found things can be confiscated, and confiscated things can hang you.

So on the morning after I read what the seal concealed, while Adela sat at her desk upstairs composing a speech about municipal franchises with a steady hand and no idea her family's name was circulating through government offices like a counterfeit coin, I stood in the garage with a chamois cloth and polished the windshield of the Delaunay-Belleville, and I mapped my route to Rufina Ochoa.

Three checkpoints. I counted them the way you count the gaps between your ribs—by touch, in the dark, hoping all your fingers come back.

* * *

The river bridge first. Everyone's route north crossed the Puente Viejo eventually; there was no way around it unless you wanted to add six hours and two mountain passes, and a woman alone in the passes was more conspicuous than a woman at a bridge. The garrison had posted men at the northern end since the unrest began in the spring—young soldiers mostly, bored, their rifles slung with the careless cruelty of boys carrying something they hadn't grown into yet. They waved through most traffic. They searched carts with tarpaulins. They made farmers unload melons into the dust while they laughed about something among themselves.

They did not search ladies in motorcars. That was the arithmetic of the thing: my sex was a uniform, my class a passport, my silence a signature on papers no one had ever asked me to sign. A soldier would look at the car, see the brass fittings and the leather and the woman behind the wheel wearing gloves, and his eyes would do what trained eyes do—slide off, find nothing interesting, move to the next cart of melons.

Invisibility, I had always told myself, was armor.

Polishing the windshield, cloth moving in slow circles, I let myself examine that sentence the way you examine a coin you suspect is shaved. Armor protects you. But armor also weighs, and after enough years of wearing it, you forget where your body ends and the metal begins. Two years of reading other people's letters. Two years of being the woman who was never quite introduced—"our driver," Adela would say, and the word *our* would do its work, folding me into the household furniture. I could pass through any door in this province carrying anything, because no one had ever once looked at me long enough to wonder what I carried.

The letter against my body said otherwise now. The letter made me the most dangerous woman in the province, and the only reason that danger would survive the bridge was that nobody would think to look for it inside someone so unremarkable.

Fine. Then let me be unremarkable. Let me be furniture with a secret.

I turned the cloth over and started on the brass headlamps.

* * *

Second crossing: the rail line at Kilometro Cincuenta, where the coast road met the tracks and the army had built a wooden gatehouse that hadn't existed in April. I'd driven past it twice, both times at a distance, both times noting the layout the way I noted everything—a rut here, a blind curve there, the width of the shoulder, whether the telegraph wires ran alongside (they did, which meant reports traveled faster than I did).

The rail crossing worried me more than the bridge, and I'll tell you why plainly, because I told myself plainly, standing there with chamois and polish: bridges are old. Bridges have routines. Soldiers at a bridge have been waving people across since before their fathers were born, and routine makes men lazy. But a new checkpoint is a checkpoint with something to prove. New orders circulate. New sergeants want to be noticed by their colonels. And the men at the rail crossing were not checking for weapons—they were checking names. They had a list, or the beginnings of one, and lists are different from rifles. You can outrun a rifle. You cannot outrun your own name written in someone's ledger.

Which raised the question I turned over and over while the polish dried to a haze on the lamp housings: what name would I give them?

Sandoval was true, and truth at a checkpoint is a gamble—you win nothing for honesty except the chance to lose everything if your name has already found its way onto paper. I thought of the forged document, the Marchetti name stitched into anarchist sedition like a deliberate stain, and I understood with a cold settling feeling that somewhere in a government office there was very likely another list, and that the household I served might be on it, and that anyone connected to that household—the driver included—was a thread that could be pulled.

Marchetti, then? Claiming the family's protection at a rail crossing would get me through—but it would tie me to them visibly, in front of witnesses, in ink if the sergeant kept a log, and the whole point of what I was doing was to keep the Marchettis clean of this errand until the exposé could protect them better than their own name could. No. Their name was not mine to spend.

There were other names. There was Mateo's cousin who managed freight at the depot—no, that drew Mateo in, and Mateo had enough risk. There was—

I stopped polishing. My hands had gone still on the lamp housing, and I made them start again, because a woman who stands motionless in her employer's driveway looking stricken is a woman somebody remembers.

There was Tomás.

Tomás Marchetti, customs officer, brother of the suffragist, a man whose official signature opened gates precisely because everyone assumed he'd want them closed. If I invoked him at the rail crossing—if I said, with the flat boredom of a servant delivering her mistress's car for service, that Señor Marchetti had sent me north on family business—no sergeant alive would delay a customs officer's errand to satisfy his own curiosity. Customs officers signed the pay documents that fed garrison kitchens. Everyone knows who signs.

It was a good lie. It came to me whole, with details attached, the way good lies do when you've spent years memorizing other people's correspondence and can reproduce a man's phrasing in your sleep. I knew how Tomás spoke about his errands. I knew the particular irritated authority of a man who expects the world to step aside. I could perform that world-stepping-aside from behind a steering wheel, in gloves, with a bored half-smile, and the sergeant would wave me through and feel flattered afterward that he'd been sensible about it.

And I hated it. Not the lying—I'd made my peace with the mechanics of that years ago, reading letters, pocketing threats, deciding what Adela needed to know. What I hated was the taste of it, which was the taste of spending someone else's trust. Every lie I told in Tomás's voice would draw a little credit from an account he didn't know he held. And if the story ever surfaced—and stories surface, that was the entire point of the letter in my coat, that secrets surface and drown people—then Tomás would learn that his sister's driver had borrowed his name to run seditious papers through an army checkpoint, and there was no version of that conversation that ended with anyone in the Marchetti household forgiving me.

I did it anyway. I rehearsed it anyway, right there, saying it under my breath to the windshield glass: *Family business, Sergeant. Señor Marchetti sends his regards.* Practicing the boredom. Practicing the small, dismissive flick of the gloved hand toward the road ahead. Fear is useless at a checkpoint—soldiers can smell it the way horses smell dogs—so I took my fear and I cut it into pieces and I sewed each piece into a sentence, and I practiced the sentences until the fear was gone and only the sentences remained.

That is what method is. Method is fear with nowhere left to tremble.

* * *

Third: the garrison gate itself, on the return leg, because the newspaper office lay beyond the town and any route home passed the Cuartel Central. This was the worst of the three and I saved it for last in my thinking, the way you save the deepest wound for last when counting your ribs.

The garrison gate was not a checkpoint; it was an institution. Stone archways, a permanent sergeant-at-arms, a duty roster that changed daily so no one could predict which officer you'd face. They searched vehicles there—actually searched, mirrors on poles beneath the chassis, a corporal who looked in tool compartments with genuine professional interest. The bridge soldiers waved. The gate corporals looked.

Could I carry the letter through that? I asked myself honestly, because dishonesty with yourself gets you killed faster than any sergeant. Could I sit in a motorcar while a man with a bayonet went through my toolbox, my spare tire well, the space under the seats, and keep my hands loose on the wheel and my breathing at sixty to the minute?

No. Not with the letter in the car.

But the letter would not be in the car. By then the letter would have done its work—it would be in Rufina Ochoa's hands, or copied in her back room, and the original would be wherever she told me to put it. The garrison gate was a problem for the Ines who came *home*, and that Ines would be empty-handed, boring, invisible again. I almost laughed at myself, wiping down the fender: I had built my whole life into a return trip through a checkpoint, and now my only comfort was the promise that going home I would once again be carrying nothing.

Carrying nothing. Is that what I called it?

* * *

By noon the Delaunay gleamed like a bride, and I had my route fixed in my head with the mileages counted and the lies fitted to each post like teeth in a lock. Bridge: be a lady, be dull, be furniture. Rail crossing: be Tomás's errand, spend the trust, hate myself on schedule—hate yourself *after*, Ines, there's no time before. Garrison gate: be empty. Be nothing coming home. Nothing has never once been stopped.

Adela came down at one o'clock with her speech folded in her reticule and her hat pinned at the angle that meant she expected to be photographed. She paused at the garage door and looked at the car, then at me, and something moved across her face—something that on another woman I'd have called uneasiness.

"You've been polishing for hours," she said.

"The roads are dusty, señora."

"Mm." She settled into the seat and arranged her skirt. "Everyone says the roads will be worse before they're better. Tomás says there are checkpoints now, north of the river. Have you seen them?"

I had. From a distance. Twice. I said, "I've heard the same," and cranked the engine, and the noise of it covered whatever my face was doing, which was exactly why I loved engines and trusted no faces, including my own.

We drove south toward the meeting hall, away from the bridge, away from the rails, away from Rufina Ochoa—for today. Tomorrow I would ask for the day Adela wouldn't question: the dentist, the farce I'd already begun constructing, a plausible tooth in a plausible jaw. Tonight I would take the letter from its hiding place and sew the plan tighter around it. And at some point between now and the bridge, I would need to decide the thing I had been circling all morning like a hawk over a henhouse:

whether I went alone, or whether I finally let one living soul know what I was carrying.

The answer arrived before I wanted it, the way answers do. It arrived in Adela's voice from beside me, light and ordinary and devastating: "Ines, dear, when we stop at the pharmacy, would you mind posting these?" She handed back two letters she'd written at lunch. "You know how I distrust leaving anything in the house overnight."

Two letters. Sealed. Unread.

I took them, and my hands were steady, and I thought: *tomorrow, alone, then.*
