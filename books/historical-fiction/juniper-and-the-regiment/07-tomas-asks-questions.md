## 7. Tomás Asks Questions

I was polishing the headlamps when Tomás came out to the garage, and I knew before he spoke that the conversation would cost me something. Men like Tomás Marchetti did not visit garages. They had staff for garages. They had me for garages, which was different—staff belonged to a house, and I belonged to a car, and a car could be summoned.

He came around the Bonnet's wing with his hands in his pockets, and he stood for a moment watching me work the chrome with a chamois, the way a man watches an animal he is deciding whether to buy.

"Good afternoon, Sandoval."

"Señor Marchetti."

"Tomás," he said, as he always did, though neither of us believed in it.

The afternoon was doing what afternoons did at the Marchetti house: pouring gold down the cypress hedge, making the gravel look richer than it was. Somewhere inside, Adela was dictating correspondence to her secretary, and I could hear the rise and fall of her voice through the study window—not the words, just the music of it, which I knew better than most hymns.

"How's the engine?" Tomás asked.

"Purring."

"And the routes? Still sound?"

"Every one of them."

He nodded slowly, and I went back to the headlamp, because my hands needed employment more than my mouth needed silence. He watched them move. That was the first thing I understood about this conversation: he had come to watch my hands.

"You know," he said, "when Adela first hired you, Father worried. A woman driver. Young, unmarried, no family anyone could name." He said it lightly, an anecdote, a coin flipped onto the table. "Father worries about everything twice."

"Señor Marchetti senior worries so I don't have to."

Tomás laughed—a real laugh, brief and warm, and I hated him a little for making me want to laugh back. That was his gift, and I had watched him use it at dinner tables for two years: he made you feel like a confederate before you'd agreed to anything. Customs men were made of that. You smiled, and while you smiled your ledger was being read.

"She's lucky," he said. "To have someone steady. Someone who notices things." He tilted his head toward the house. "Speaking of noticing. Has the mail been regular lately?"

The chamois kept moving. I am proud of that, even now—the chamois never stopped.

"Regular, señor?"

"Tomás."

"Regular, Tomás. Letters, circulars, the usual flood. The movement gets more paper than a stationer's shop."

"Mm." He picked up a spare lug nut from the fender, turned it over in his fingers as if inspecting it, set it back exactly where it had been. "Only, we've had some questions from above. Nothing dramatic. The ministry has taken an interest in certain organizations, and certain organizations' correspondence. Routine. But they've asked whether mail coming into this house has been—" he weighed the word like the lug nut— "*irregular.*"

"Irregular" was a beautiful word. It meant nothing and accused everything. I let it sit between us for the length of one slow breath.

"What would irregular look like?" I asked. "Torn envelopes? Late delivery?"

"That's what I asked." He smiled, showing me the question had pleased him, which frightened me more than a frown would have. "They couldn't say. Which suggests they know more than they're saying. Or less." He looked at me directly then, and his eyes were not laughing at all. "You handle the mail sometimes, don't you? On the drives. Adela says you sort it at red lights like a card sharp."

"She teases."

"She brags." He paused. "She does that about you, you know. *Ines reads faster than my secretary. Ines remembers everything.* It's practically a speech she gives."

I put down the chamois. There are moments when stillness tells a better lie than motion, because guilty people keep their hands busy and innocent ones forget they have hands at all. I had learned this the way I had learned everything: by watching, by not being watched.

"If someone opened her letters," I said, "she'd want to know. She'd want to know before the ministry did."

Something crossed his face—there and gone, quick as a bird's shadow. Not guilt. Concern wearing guilt's coat. Or guilt wearing concern's. With Tomás I had never been able to separate the two, and standing there in the gold light I understood, with a cold settling feeling, that I might be looking at the man who had made the ministry curious in the first place—or at a man terrified that his sister had.

"Yes," he said quietly. "She would want to know." And then, lighter, the customs officer sliding back over the brother like a lid over a well: "So. If anything struck you as off. A seal sitting wrong. An envelope heavier than its contents. You'd tell someone."

"You," I said.

"You," he agreed. "Or Adela. Whoever seemed safest." He smiled again, and it was the smile that told me everything—that *whoever seemed safest* was not kindness but method, that he was offering me two doors and would remember which one I walked through.

* * *

Afterward I sat in the driver's seat with my hands on the wheel and did not drive anywhere, and thought about how easily the lie had come. That was the part that frightened me. Not Tomás—Tomás was a known quantity, a man with a badge-shaped hole where his conscience should have been flexible, or perhaps too flexible; I honestly could not decide which frightened me more. What frightened me was the fluency. Two years ago, sorting Adela's mail at red lights, I had felt every envelope like a small sin. Now I could lie to her brother's face in the sunshine and my pulse didn't even change gears.

When had that happened? When had reading other people's words become writing my own?

Because I lied to him. Let the record show it plainly: he asked if anything was irregular, and I said no, and I knew—with the particular certainty of a woman who has steamed open history itself—that everything was irregular, that the irregularity had a name and a date and a government stamp on it, and that it lived folded in oilcloth inside the false bottom of my satchel not four feet from where he stood.

He suspected a leak from inside. He'd said it without saying it: *they know more than they're saying, or less.* But the ministry did not ask questions out of ignorance. The ministry asked questions out of inventory. Somewhere a clerk had compared a list against a list and found a gap shaped like the Marchetti name, and now the family's own son was walking out to garages with his hands in his pockets, turning lug nuts over, offering doors.

Which meant the letter was right. Which meant the plot to discredit the movement—to discredit *this house*—was already moving through channels, and channels end at checkpoints, and checkpoints end at garrisons, and garrisons end in cells where women who drove cars and read fast were not treated as invisible.

I should give it to him, a voice said. Give the letter to Tomás. He's family. He's customs. He can bury it in an official channel and no one will ever connect the girl at the wheel.

But I had read enough letters to know what official channels did to inconvenient truths: they processed them. They filed them. They lost them on purpose, in triplicate. If the Marchetti name appeared in a forged document linking the family to an anarchist plot, and that document passed through the hands of a customs officer whose ministry had *taken an interest*, then the letter would die in a drawer and the story would die with it, and someday Adela Marchetti would be arrested for a conspiracy invented in her own brother's filing cabinet.

No. Rufina Ochoa. The newspaper. Print was the only channel that couldn't be sealed.

* * *

Dinner that evening was an ordeal performed in candlelight. I served at the sideboard—I always poured wine when the regular girl had her night off—and so I stood within earshot of the whole performance, invisible in plain sight, which is the only kind of invisibility worth having.

Tomás was charming. He asked after the movement's plans with the idle air of a brother humoring a sister, and Adela, glowing from the day's organizing, gave him a version scrubbed clean of names and dates. Watching him listen to her was like watching a man count another man's cards while pretending to admire his sleeves.

"The northern roads," he said, spearing a potato. "You're still holding meetings up there? The garrison's put crossings on the river bridge. Whole business. Very tedious for travelers."

"Then travelers will be delayed," Adela said, "and will have time to think about why."

He laughed. Everyone laughed. I poured wine into his glass and saw his eyes flick up to mine—one instant, no longer—and I understood that the garage conversation had not ended. It had merely adjourned to a venue with witnesses.

Later, in the hall, catching my cloak, he stopped beside me under the mounted antlers that had hung there since before either of us was born.

"They say the garrison's orders mention women traveling alone," he said softly. "New instructions. Coming down any day now." He straightened my collar with two fingers, a brotherly gesture performed with a customs officer's hands. "Drive carefully, Ines. Roads are full of irregularities these days."

And he left me standing in the hall with the antlers and the candle smoke, knowing three things at once: that he had warned me, that he had tested me, and that he did not know which of those he was doing himself.

Upstairs, in my narrow room, I took the satchel down from behind the loose brick and held it a while without opening it. The letter inside was heavier than paper had any right to be. Tomorrow I would drive Adela to the city, and after I delivered her to her meeting I would drive north alone, past the river bridge, past whatever new orders were coming down, to the back room of a newspaper where a journalist waited for the truth.

I sewed that night. Coat lining, oilcloth, small stitches my mother taught me before she taught me nothing else. And as the needle went in and out I thought about Tomás's fingers straightening my collar, and Mateo's easy laugh across folding tables, and Adela's voice through the study window saying my name like a boast—*Ines remembers everything*—

and I hated, with a quiet and total hatred, how good I had become at exactly this: keeping things, carrying things, telling no one.

Because tomorrow I would lie to my way through a checkpoint, and the day after that I would need a document from Tomás Marchetti's own locked desk—and the man had just spent an afternoon teaching me, politely, in the sunshine, precisely how much he suspected.

The needle slipped. I sucked the bead of blood from my thumb and kept sewing.
