## 10. Rufina Ochoa's Back Room

The *Clarín* occupied the ground floor of a building that had once been a bank, and you could still see it in the bones of the place—the marble counter where tellers had leaned now stacked with galley proofs, the vault door at the back standing open to reveal not money but four years of archives bound in string. The front room smelled of ink and cold coffee. A boy of maybe fifteen fed paper into a press that hammered like a fist on a door that would never open.

I stood in the doorway longer than I meant to. My hands were still shaking from the bridge—not badly, not so anyone on the street would notice, but enough that I pressed them flat against my skirt and counted the presses' rhythm until it slowed. One. Two. Three. The soldier's face kept surfacing, young and bored and utterly incurious, and I kept waiting for the relief of having passed to arrive.

It didn't arrive. What arrived instead was a clearer and colder understanding of what I had just learned about myself: that I could carry a forged document accusing my employer of treason through a military checkpoint with a steady pulse, and that the steadiness frightened me more than the soldiers had.

"Are you buying or selling?" said a voice behind the counter.

The woman who owned the voice was small in the way that a closed fist is small—compact, dense, all of it arranged around something central. She wore an apron spotted with ink over a good dress, spectacles pushed up into gray-streaked hair, and she looked at me the way no one had looked at me in years: directly, appraisingly, as if I were someone whose opinion might matter.

"I'm looking for Señora Ochoa," I said. "Rufina Ochoa."

"You found her. Buying or selling?"

"Selling," I said, and then, because the word felt too small for what was in my satchel: "Telling."

Something moved in her face—nothing warm, nothing I could name, but a kind of attention sharpening, like a lens being screwed into place. She came out from behind the counter, wiping her hands on the apron, and looked past me through the front window at the street, both directions, unhurried, thorough. Then she turned.

"Not here," she said. "Back room. Walk like you're bored."

* * *

The back room had been the bank's records office. A table scarred with cigarette burns, three chairs, a stove that gave off more iron smell than heat, and shelves of type trays that rattled faintly whenever the presses shuddered in the next room. There was one window, high and barred, letting down a square of gray afternoon light. Rufina Ochoa shut the door, sat, and did not offer me a chair.

"Adela Marchetti sent you."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway, because lying to her seemed suddenly both easy and obscene. "She doesn't know I've come."

That interested her. She took off her spectacles, put them back on, and studied me across the table. "Then who sent you? Think carefully before you answer, because I have a rule. I print nothing that arrives by mystery. Mystery is how they get to us. They invent a courier, they invent a document, we print it, and by Friday the paper is ash and I am in Punta Carreteras breaking rocks." She tapped the table once. "So. Who sent you?"

"No one sent me. Adela Marchetti doesn't know what I'm carrying. Her brother may know it exists—I don't think he knows I have it. And the man who wrote the forgery thinks it's still in a mailbag in the capital." I heard my own voice go quiet and level, the voice I used at red lights when I memorized addresses. "I read other people's letters, Señora. It's been my habit for years. This time the letter told me things nobody was supposed to know, including me."

Rufina Ochoa leaned back. Outside the door, the press hammered on, and the type trays rattled their small bone-rattle, and neither of us moved.

"A driver," she said. "For Marchetti. How long?"

"Two years."

"And in two years she never noticed you read her mail?"

"She notices what I let her notice," I said. "That's the arrangement. It's been a useful arrangement. Until this week."

"Show me."

I opened the satchel. My fingers knew exactly which fold of oilcloth held the letter; I'd sewn the packet into the lining of the bag itself that morning before dawn, working by matchlight in the boardinghouse privy, and unpicking the stitches now felt like opening my own chest. I laid the letter on the table between us and stood back from it the way you stand back from something that has bitten you.

Rufina did not touch it at first. She bent close instead, reading through the spectacles, and I watched her eyes travel down the page twice, three times, slowing. Then she pulled a drawer from the table, took out a pair of long steel tongs of the kind printers use for hot metal, and lifted the letter by its corner onto the blotter, handling it like evidence, because that is what it was.

"Government stock," she murmured. "This paper. You can't buy it at a stationer's—it comes from the ministry supply house, watermarked. See?" She tilted it toward the window light and there it was, faint as breath: an eagle, wings half-raised, swimming inside the fibers. "Which means whoever wrote this didn't write it at a kitchen table. They wrote it at a desk with a lock on it." Her finger hovered above the signature block without touching it. "And this seal. Look at the impression. Too clean. A real wax seal carries the hesitation of the hand—you get bubbles, drag marks, a thumb smudge somewhere. This one was pressed with a jig. Machine-made. Perfect." She looked up at me. "Perfect is a confession. Nobody honest seals anything perfectly."

"So it's forged."

"It's forged beautifully," she said. "Someone spent money on this. Someone with access to ministry stock, a die-cutter, and a reason to want the Marchetti name nailed to the word *anarchist*." She set the tongs down and finally, deliberately, touched the page with her bare finger. "Do you understand what this document is for? It isn't evidence. Evidence convicts people after the fact. This is theater. They hold it in reserve until the moment they need it—arrests, say, a crackdown, the movement's leaders in a cell—and then it surfaces, and the newspapers that aren't mine print it as gospel, and the public shrugs and says well, if the seal is genuine. It's a knife made in advance and wrapped in paper. Where did it come from?"

"The mail. Addressed to a man named Herrera, care of a tobacconist on Calle Zaragoza. Except Herrera, as far as I can tell, doesn't exist, and the tobacconist is a drop." I hesitated, then went on, because there was no sense coming this far to ration the truth. "There was a second sheet mentioned in it. A signature page. Names collected at meetings—some of them false names, some of them servants and shopgirls who thought they were signing a petition for a school. The forgery needs those signatures underneath it to look authentic. That's why it hasn't surfaced yet. They're waiting for the page."

Rufina Ochoa removed her spectacles entirely this time and rubbed the bridge of her nose, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and then some.

"They'll already have it," she said. "Or a copy of it."

"No. It's still at the Marchetti house."

"How do you know?"

"Because Tomás Marchetti keeps it locked in his desk," I said. "And I know this because I drive him twice a week to his club and I hear everything a man says when he thinks his driver is furniture. He took custody of the signature pages himself, months ago, after the threats started. He told Adela it was to protect them." I heard the flatness come into my own voice. "He told someone else it was insurance."

* * *

We drank tea that tasted of iron pipes, and while the stove ticked and the presses thudded their patient fists beyond the door, Rufina Ochoa laid the whole machine out on the scorched table for me, piece by piece, the way she probably laid out a front page.

The plot had a shape. There was a faction in the ministry—she named no names, and I understood that the omission was discipline, not ignorance—that had decided the suffrage movement had grown one season too bold. Petitions were one thing; the rally planned for spring, with its expected ten thousand, was another. But arresting women outright was bad copy even for papers that printed nothing else, so the plot required that the women arrest themselves. Produce anarchists among them. Produce documents. Produce the Marchetti family—a respected name, a customs officer brother with government standing—as the poisoned root, and the movement would be strangled politely, by its own scandal, with the public thanking the ministry for the shears.

"The letter you stole is the blade," Rufina said. "The signature page is the handle. Without the handle, a man can hold the blade all day and cut no one but himself. Which is why," and here she looked at me with a directness that made the room feel smaller, "you are going to bring me the original."

"Not a copy?"

"Copies can be dismissed. Photographs can be called fabrications—we've watched it happen to better papers than mine. The original, with the ministry watermark on the stock and the jig-pressed seal, examined by two independent experts under oath—" she spread her ink-stained hands, "—that is a different animal entirely. That is an animal that bites back. I publish that, and the story isn't *scandal among the suffragists*. The story is *ministry forged evidence*, and every arrest they make afterward poisons their own case. The plot dies in daylight. But it has to be the original."

"The original sits in a locked desk in a house where I sleep in a room off the kitchen," I said, "belonging to a family whose name is written on that paper, guarded by a man who is either the plot's victim or its instrument, and I don't know which. And you're asking me to walk back in and take it."

"Yes," said Rufina Ochoa, without any softening whatsoever. Then she took off her spectacles again, and I saw that the bluntness had been a kind of respect, the way you speak plainly to someone you've decided to treat as a colleague rather than a messenger. "You walked into my back room uninvited, past a street full of soldiers, carrying the most dangerous piece of paper in this province, sewn into a satchel you stitched yourself. Who else would I ask? Who else *could* I ask?" She leaned forward. "Señora Sandoval. Ines. May I call you Ines? Every organizer I know is followed, watched, catalogued. Adela can't go near her brother's desk without a dozen eyes noting it. Mateo Reyes is known to the garrison by sight—he thinks he isn't, which is worse. But you—" and she gestured at me, at all of me, the gray dress and the quiet hands, "—you're invisible. Two years of invisibility, and now I'm telling you it's worth something. I'm sorry for what that costs you. I'd be a fool to pretend it isn't true."

*I'm invisible,* I thought, and for the first time the word didn't comfort me. It sat in my stomach like a stone swallowed whole. On the bridge that morning, the soldier had looked straight through me, and I had been grateful, and the gratitude had a taste I was only now identifying: it was the taste of not existing. Of being a function. A car with a woman attached.

"When do you need it?" I heard myself say.

"There's a window. The ministry won't move before the budget session closes—they can't spare the attention. Call it eleven days. I go to press with the exposé the morning after, whether or not I have the original—but understand what that means. Without the original, I print the forgery itself and accuse them, and they call it libel, and maybe I survive it and maybe I don't, and the Marchettis hang in the wind either way." She met my eyes. "With the original, we end them. Eleven days, Ines."

* * *

She wouldn't let me leave through the front. There was a yard behind the building where the delivery carts came, and a gate into the alley, and she walked me to it herself, carrying a bundle of old newspapers as if I were a customer and this were the most ordinary errand in the world.

"One more thing," she said at the gate, quietly, not looking at me, looking at the alley. "Who else knows what you're carrying? Truly knows—not suspects, knows."

"Adela doesn't. Tomás doesn't." I paused. "Mateo Reyes knows I've been uneasy. He doesn't know why."

"Mateo Reyes," Rufina repeated, and her voice did something small and careful, the way a floor does something small and careful before it gives way. "He's offered you help, hasn't he. He will. He's generous that way." She turned to me then, and behind the spectacles her eyes were tired and very old and very certain. "When he offers—and he will offer, probably before the week is out—you come and tell me first. Not because I have proof of anything. Because a woman in my trade learns to count who survives our bad years, and I have been counting Mateo Reyes for a long time." She pressed the bundle of newspapers into my arms. "Now go. You've been here too long already, and the light's changing."

I went, out the gate and down the alley with the newspapers against my hip like laundry, past a wall where a poster for a traveling circus was slowly peeling, and it wasn't until I reached the corner where the car was parked that I let myself feel what I had been refusing to feel since the tea.

Hope. Sharp, dangerous, physical hope—the exposé, the plot dying in daylight, Adela saved, the family saved, the whole rotten machinery dragged into the light by a printer's tongs and a driver nobody saw. I stood with my hand on the door of the car and let it move through me, and it was almost sweet, and then, right behind it, arriving the way thunder arrives behind lightning, came the rest of it.

Eleven days. The original. Tomás's locked desk, in Tomás's study, in the house where Adela trusted me completely and Tomás watched everyone, and where I would have to become, for one night, a thief in the only home I had left.

And beneath that, quieter than the rest, Rufina's last words ticking like the stove: *when he offers, you come and tell me first.*

Mateo. Who had taught me the routes. Who had sat with me through the winter of the first arrests and shared bread at midnight. Who had promised, laughing, that when we won, there'd be a place for everyone—even drivers.

I got into the car. My hands were steady again. I noticed that, and hated noticing it, and started the engine anyway.
