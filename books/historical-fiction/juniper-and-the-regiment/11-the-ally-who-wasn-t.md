## 11. The Ally Who Wasn't

Mateo Reyes whistled when he saw the state of my tires.

"That left rear is a confession waiting to be made," he said, crouching beside the Hupmobile with his hands jammed in his pockets, the way he always stood when he wanted to look casual and never managed it. "You could fit your whole hand through that wear."

"Then it's good I don't keep my hand in there."

He laughed. He had known me six years and he laughed at everything I said, which I had once found flattering and now understood was simply Mateo's way of moving through rooms—agreeable, warm, frictionless. A man who had never once made an enemy because he had never once taken a side that cost him anything.

Or so I thought then.

It was Tuesday evening and the printing works on Calle Herrera had emptied except for the night typesetter, who clattered somewhere above us like rain that wouldn't commit to falling. The organizing committee had adjourned twenty minutes earlier, chairs scraped into stacks, and Mateo had lingered the way he sometimes did, walking me out, talking about nothing—the price of coal, his mother's hip, the new tram line—while I checked the car by habit: water, oil, the spare strapped behind the seat, the jack with its bent handle that only worked if you swore at it first.

I did not know yet what I was carrying. That came later, or rather had already come, three days before, on a road north of the city with steam curling off the radiator and a letter opening in my hands like a wound. But knowledge sits strangely in the body. It waits for a place to be set down. And when Mateo straightened up from the tire and looked at me—really looked, which he rarely bothered to do—I felt the weight shift in my coat as though the document itself were listening.

"You're tired," he said. "Driving her every day now, all these extra routes."

"Adela needs driving. That's the work."

"The work." He smiled. "You make it sound simple, Ines. You're the only person I know who makes revolution sound like a train schedule."

"Trains arrive," I said. "That's more than most revolutions can claim."

He laughed again, but this time he let it die quickly, and then we were standing in the blue half-dark of the yard with just the one lamp over the door making a yellow circle that we stood at the edge of, neither of us quite inside it. Above us the typesetter's clatter stopped. Started again. Stopped.

"Ines." His voice dropped into a register I hadn't heard from him before. Lower. Careful. The voice of a man checking whether a door is locked before saying something out loud. "How bad is it? Truly. The rumors say checkpoints on every road north. Say the garrison's been given names."

"Rumors say a lot of things."

"I'm not asking as rumor." He stepped closer. "I'm asking as someone who's been in this since the strike of '07 with you. Someone who's carried your satchel through more police lines than either of us can count. Someone you trust."

And there it was—the word, laid out between us like a coin on a table. *Trust.* Six years of it. Mateo holding umbrellas over my head while I fixed a chain in the rain. Mateo learning the routes I drove so that if I ever failed to arrive he'd know where to start looking. Mateo teaching me which gendarmes took bribes and which took photographs. When I counted the people in the world whose opinion of me mattered, the list was short: Adela, my sister far away and silent, and Mateo. Perhaps in that order. Perhaps not.

"You've heard something," I said.

"I've heard that something is moving. Paper, not people. Something that has to get to Rufina Ochoa's desk before the government moves, or the movement doesn't survive the winter." He held up his hands. "I don't want details. Details are dangerous. I'm offering what I always offer—hands, routes, silence. Except this time the stakes are higher, so the offer is bigger."

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere down the street a cart rattled past, its driver singing tunelessly, a man with no secrets in the world worth protecting.

"What kind of bigger?" I asked, and hated myself even as I asked it, because a part of me—a part I hadn't consulted—had already begun to unclench. Three days I had been alone with that letter. Three days of rehearsing lies for checkpoints, of sewing pockets into my coat lining by lamplight with my hands steadier than they had any right to be, of lying awake in my narrow room above the garage cataloguing everyone in the world who might be watching me and finding the list too long and myself too singular. One woman. One car. Three crossings. If there was another pair of hands—

No. That was exactly how I should have known better. Wanting help is how you learn whom you cannot afford.

"I know the rail men," Mateo said. "Second checkpoint, the crossing at Kilometer Nine—they wave my cart through without counting crates. I could carry anything you needed through that gate inside a shipment of printer's paper, and nobody searches paper going *to* a press. Nobody. It's the one cargo in this country that's heavier than it looks and still boring." He grinned. "You drive the river bridge, you handle whatever they do at the garrison, and let me take the middle off your shoulders. Divide the risk. That's not cowardice, Ines, that's arithmetic."

Arithmetic. He knew me well enough to know I trusted numbers more than promises.

I turned away from him and pretended to check the tie-down on the spare, which did not need checking, because I did not want my face to be readable. This is what no one tells you about being invisible: after enough years of it, you stop knowing whether anyone sees you at all, and so you start performing yourself even in front of mirrors. I made my face neutral. I made my voice flat.

"Why now?"

"What?"

"You've been in this since '07, you said. Six years of carrying satchels and never once asking to carry the dangerous thing. Why put your hand up tonight?"

For a fraction of a second—no longer than the gap between lightning and thunder—Mateo's face did something I couldn't read. Not guilt. Nothing so legible. More like a man adjusting a collar that had ridden crooked. Then the warmth flooded back in, and he spread his hands, wounded and open.

"Because I heard you were doing it alone and it kept me up half the night. Because if they take you at some crossing, who drives Adela? Who does any of us call?" He shook his head. "You think everyone calculates the way you do. Some of us just hate watching our friends walk into gunfire by themselves."

*Friends.* The word went into me warm as brandy, and I noticed—I noticed even then—that it went in too easily, the way flattery goes into people who have been starved of it. That is the whole trick, isn't it. You don't deceive a woman with lies. You deceive her with the thing she's been missing.

"I'd have to ask Adela," I said.

"You'd have to tell Adela," he corrected gently. "There's a difference, and you know it. She should know her letters are traveling with company." He glanced up at the dark windows of the works. "Bring it tomorrow. Midday, when the typesetter breaks for food and the yard's empty. Whatever needs to ride in the paper shipment—we'll pack it together, seal the crate with the press stamp, and by Thursday it'll be sitting on Rufina's desk wearing an innocent face." He patted the fender of the Hupmobile twice, like a man blessing a horse. "Think about the arithmetic, Ines. It's good arithmetic."

He walked me to the mouth of the alley and peeled away toward the tram stop, raising one hand without turning around, and I stood a long time in the cold listening to his footsteps fade, trying to locate the wrongness I could feel somewhere under my ribs like a splinter too shallow to grip.

Here is what I told myself that night, driving home through streets where soldiers were already building their little wooden barriers for morning: that I was tired; that dividing risk was arithmetic; that six years was a fact and facts don't lie; that Mateo had never given me a reason.

Here is what I would not let myself remember until later, much later, when remembering could do nothing but hurt cleanly: that in six years, Mateo had never once been searched. Not at a picket line, not at a checkpoint, not once in six years of smuggling pamphlets past men whose whole profession was searching. I had marveled at it, early on, called it luck. He'd winked and said he had an honest face.

An honest face is a thing you can be issued.

* * *

I did not sleep. At two in the morning I sat on the edge of my cot with the letter out of my coat lining and read it again by the light of a candle stub I was saving for a power cut, as though repetition might change the words, as though the fourth reading might reveal some error of mine instead of an error of the world. The Marchetti name, printed in a forged hand among signatures that tied the family to a plot none of them had touched—engineered, the letter said, at a level high enough that exposure was the only weapon that could reach it. Rufina Ochoa could fire that weapon. But she needed the original signature page, and the original sat in Tomás Marchetti's locked desk, forty minutes' drive away, guarded by nothing but a lock and a family's fragile peace.

And now, apparently, guarded also by Mateo's helpful hands.

I dressed in the dark. I sat at the little table with my pencil and drew the route the way I always did when fear needed converting into method: river bridge, rail crossing at Kilometer Nine, garrison gate. Three circles. Then I drew Mateo's offer over the middle circle, and I sat looking at the way his kindness covered exactly one third of my danger, and asked myself the question I should have asked in the yard.

What did Mateo know, and when?

Because here was the arithmetic he hadn't counted on me doing. I had told no one about the letter. No one. It had existed for three days inside my own coat, and in that time I had spoken to perhaps thirty people, and precisely one of them had walked up to me tonight and offered to carry *paper*. He hadn't said a letter. He hadn't needed to. He'd said *something is moving, paper not people*, with the confidence of a man who already knew the shape of the parcel.

Either he had guessed—and guessed perfectly—or he had been told.

And if he had been told, there was only one direction the telling could come from, and it wore a uniform, and it built wooden barriers on my roads at night.

I blew out the candle and sat in the dark and let myself feel, for exactly one minute by the factory whistle, the full weight of what I wanted to be untrue. That Mateo was Mateo. That the umbrella in the rain was real. That somebody in this century had liked me without billing me for it.

Then I got up, folded the letter back into its seam, and threaded the needle.

I would not give him the original. That much I settled before dawn, and settling it steadied my hands. If Mateo was clean, I had insulted a friend in the privacy of my own heart, and no harm done. If he was not—then tomorrow at midday I would hand him a decoy, a few lines of harmless committee correspondence in a sealed envelope, and watch what he did with it. Watch whether it traveled toward Rufina Ochoa or toward the garrison. Watch the middle circle of my map fill up with evidence.

Trust, I wrote to myself on the back of the route card, in the small neat hand I used for memorizing other people's mail: *trust is a document. Verify the seal.*

At midday the yard was empty the way he'd promised, the typesetter gone to his lunch, the lamp over the door burning pale against a white sky. Mateo came across the gravel with his collar crooked again—he never could keep a collar straight, I'd found it endearing for six years—and he was smiling, and he rubbed his hands together against the cold, and he said, "Well? Did the arithmetic come out right?"

I gave him the envelope. I watched his fingers close around it, light and practiced, the fingers of a man who had handled a great many envelopes that were never his.

"It's not the whole of it," I said. "It's the piece that proves the forgery. The rest follows separately, for safety. You understand."

"Of course I understand." He tucked it inside his coat, against his heart, the exact place I kept mine, and for one absurd moment I nearly warned him—nearly reached out and said *Mateo, whoever you are, whoever you've become, be careful.*

"Thursday," he said. "By Thursday it's on her desk."

He walked toward the gates, and the sun caught him, and he raised one hand without turning around, the same as the night before, the same friendly wave going away from me down the long empty street—

and it was only after he had turned the corner that I remembered the envelope I'd given him was blank. Every page of it. Blank paper in a sealed envelope, because I had run out of time at four in the morning to write even a decent lie.

Which meant that whatever Mateo Reyes delivered on Thursday—to Rufina Ochoa, or to anyone else—he would have to write it himself.
