## 12. Second Crossing: The Rail Crossing

I saw the new sign before I saw the soldiers.

RAILWAY CROSSING — INSPECTION POINT — ALL VEHICLES SUBJECT TO SEARCH. It had been nailed to a telegraph pole at the bend where the road dropped toward the rail yard, and someone had nailed it crooked, so that the words tilted like a ship taking on water. Beneath it, in smaller type, an addendum that had not been there three days ago, because three days ago there had been no addendum and no reason for one:

*By order of the Provincial Command. Unaccompanied women will be referred to the station master for verification of travel purpose.*

Unaccompanied women.

I read it twice, the way I read everything now, and then I pulled the car over onto the gravel shoulder and sat with my hands on the wheel and let myself feel, for exactly ten seconds, what I was feeling. Fear first, cold and specific. Then arithmetic. Ten seconds was all I allowed. I had learned that from Adela, who could stand on a platform in front of two hundred hostile men and let her hands shake inside her pockets for precisely as long as it took to name the thing, and then take them out and speak.

The engine ticked. The sun sat low over the freight yard, turning the rails into long orange wires running out of the world. A queue of four vehicles ahead of me: a milk wagon, a farmer's truck stacked with cane knives, a doctor's motorcar I recognized distantly from the city, and a dust-caked touring car with a man asleep against its window.

I went through the inventory the way a priest goes through a rosary. Satchel on the seat beside me, stripped of everything but invoices, road maps, a tin of mints, Adela's laundry list in her own hand—which I had decided counted as legitimate cargo, since it was. The letter itself was not in the satchel. The letter lived against my body now, folded into eight panels and slipped into the lining of my coat through a gap I had opened that morning with a seam ripper borrowed from the Marchetti housekeeper and returned before she missed it. Four stitches held it in. A soldier's pat-down would find nothing unless he thought to tear a woman's coat open along the hem, and they did not think to do that. That was the whole architecture of my safety: built on the assumption that no one looks closely at what no one sees.

Except now they were looking. *Unaccompanied women will be referred.* Someone in the Provincial Command had done his own arithmetic. He had noticed that the movement moved on wheels, and that the wheels were very often driven by women who traveled alone and asked no questions and were, therefore, invisible—and he had understood, as I was only now understanding from the other side of it, that invisibility is not emptiness. It is capacity. A woman nobody sees can carry anything.

The line moved. I let out the clutch.

* * *

The checkpoint straddled the level crossing itself, which meant there was no going around it; the rails ran through cut rock on either side for a mile in each direction, and everyone knew what happened to cars that tried the farm tracks near the cut. Two soldiers worked the barrier, and a third stood apart by the signal box with a ledger, and it was the third one who mattered, because ledgers are how governments remember, and I needed this government to forget me by morning.

The soldier at the barrier raised his hand and I stopped the car where he pointed, close enough to be cooperative, far enough that he would have to walk around it. Small things. You learn small things.

"Papers."

I gave him the registration and my license. Both genuine. This was a principle I had settled on early: lie with your mouth as much as you must, but never put a lie on paper, because paper travels and mouths stay home. The registration said Sandoval, Ines R., occupation: driver. The license said the same. Neither said for whom, which was about to become a problem, or an opening, depending on how I played it.

"Purpose of travel?"

"Returning to the city from the Marchetti estate." True. "Errands for the household." Also true, if you counted a newspaper office as an errand, which I had decided I did.

He glanced at the addendum sign behind him with the air of a man passing a chore up the chain. "You'll have to see the sergeant. Women traveling alone, new orders." He said it without apology and without interest, the way you'd report rain. That frightened me more than cruelty would have. Cruelty you can work with. Indifference means they've made you into a category, and categories don't get sympathy, they get processed.

The sergeant by the signal box had my license in his hand before I reached him. He was maybe fifty, with a gray mustache trimmed with terrible care and a ledger open on a plank across two crates. Behind him the afternoon train whistled somewhere south, long and mournful, and I felt the sound go through me like a hand through water.

"Ines Sandoval." He read it off the card as if confirming it against some internal list. "Driver."

"Yes."

"For whom?"

Here it was, then. The fork in the road. I could say a merchant family, a widow, anyone small—and spend the rest of my life hoping no one ever cross-checked it. Or I could say the name I had been avoiding saying, the name that belonged to a man who had stood in the Marchetti hallway three days ago asking whether Adela's mail had been irregular while I lied to his face with such fluency that I had surprised myself.

"Tomás Marchetti," I said. "Customs. I drive for the family."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened in my chest when I said it, because honesty is the one debt I keep paying down these days and never seem to clear.

It felt like spending money.

That was the image that came to me, standing on the gravel with the train whistle dying away: I had walked into the Marchetti house two years ago with an account balance of zero—no family presentable enough to claim, no name that opened doors, nothing but steady hands and a memory like a filing cabinet—and the family had deposited trust into me, small sums at first, then larger ones. Adela leaving letters unsealed in the motorcar. Doña Elvira telling me which relatives not to invite to Christmas. And Tomás—Tomás pouring me coffee in the kitchen that time and asking my opinion on tire brands as though my opinion were a real thing that existed.

And every lie I told now drew against that account. Using Tomás's name was not a victimless transaction. If this sergeant wrote it in his ledger, and the ledger was ever read beside another ledger—the kind Mateo fed, the kind with informants' initials in the margins—then what the record would show was: *Sandoval, driver, claims connection to Marchetti customs officer at rail inspection, date.* A thread. And threads get pulled, and the sweater comes apart, and the people wearing the sweater are not the ones who unraveled it.

But the alternative was detention at a rail crossing at dusk, and a search thorough enough to find four stitches.

So I spent the money.

"My brother-in-law handles their security matters," the sergeant said, and something in my scalp tightened, because *brother-in-law* meant the garrison kept a file with names in it, and Tomás's name was in it, which meant Tomás was known to them in a way that could cut both directions. "Marchetti. Customs at the river port."

"That's right."

"He'll vouch for you?"

"If you telephone him," I said, "you'll wake him at the port house, and he'll ask why his sister's driver is being delayed on a household errand, and I'll have to explain to Señora Marchetti that I couldn't manage a simple trip without involving the family." I let a little weariness into it—not defiance, never defiance, just the bone-deep fatigue of a working woman at the end of a long day. "Or you can write my plate number in your book and we'll both be home before dark. Whichever serves you."

The sergeant looked at me. I looked at the middle distance over his shoulder, at the signal box, at nothing, the way servants have looked at nothing for a thousand years. It is a discipline, that look. I had practiced it so long I sometimes worried it had eaten the rest of my face.

He wrote my plate number in his book.

"Go on, then." And then, as I turned: "Tell Marchetti the river gate rotation changed. He'll want to know."

"I'll tell him," I said, and got into the car, and discovered that my hands were perfectly steady on the wheel and that everything behind my sternum was shaking like a struck bell.

* * *

The barrier lifted. The tires crossed the rails—one bump, two, that old double heartbeat of every rail crossing in the country—and then the road opened northward, empty and blue in the last light, and I drove half a mile before I trusted myself to breathe properly.

I had gotten through. That was the fact. I had spent a piece of the family's trust like currency and purchased passage, and the purchase was complete, and the seller would never know what had been bought with his good name. Except that I would know. That was the tax on every transaction now: I kept the receipts. The threatening note I'd pocketed instead of delivering. The sealed letter steamed open over a kettle while the engine idled. The lies to Tomás, fluent and hated. And now this—his name in a government ledger, attached to my errand, a thread waiting for someone to pull it.

Each lie cost more than the last. That was the compounding nobody warned you about. The first lie was cheap because you told it to protect someone. The second cost a little more because the first one had to be maintained. By the tenth you weren't protecting anyone anymore; you were only servicing the debt, paying interest on interest, and the principal—the truth—sat untouched in some vault, gathering value and dread at the same rate.

And still. Still. I thought of Rufina Ochoa's back room, the smell of ink and cold coffee, her thumb moving across the forged signature, her voice flat with professional certainty: *This seal is wrong here, and here, and any magistrate worth his salary can see it—but only if the public sees it first.* Only if it ran. Only if the original signature page left the Marchetti house and met this letter in print, forgery and template side by side, undeniable.

Which meant going back. Which meant the house, and Tomás's locked desk, and a second document heavier than the first, and eventually a third crossing at the garrison gate where the searches were not performed by bored sergeants with ledgers.

Ahead of me the road ran down out of the hills toward the valley, and in my mirror the rail crossing shrank to a smudge of lamplight behind the barrier. I had a full tank, four stitches holding, and a name in a government book that wasn't mine to spend.

I was halfway down the grade when I saw the figure standing at the junction where the mountain road meets the valley track—a man on foot, collar up, waving both arms at me like someone who had been waiting a long time.

I knew the shape of him before I could see his face. Everyone has a few people whose silhouettes they know better than their faces, the way you know a key by touch in the dark. Mateo.

He was supposed to be in the city.
