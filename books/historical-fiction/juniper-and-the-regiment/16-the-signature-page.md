## 16. The Signature Page

**Ines**

The plan was made in the garage, which had become our parliament.

Adela stood under the bare bulb with her arms crossed, still wearing her dinner dress from an hour before, and I sat on the running board of the Hupmobile turning a lug wrench over in my hands like a woman who thought better while holding iron.

"Thursday," she said. "The Alvarados come at eight. Tomás never leaves a dinner early—he says the brandy is the only honest part of his job."

"He came home early once," I said. "In March. The night the furnace broke."

"The furnace is not going to break for you twice." She almost smiled. Then it went out of her face and there was just the arithmetic underneath. "Twenty minutes. That's how long the lock should take if Rufina's man taught you properly."

"Twelve, if the pins are old. His are old. I watched him oil it last month when he thought no one was looking."

"You watch everything."

"It's the only skill they gave me room to practice."

She crouched so we were level, and for a moment she wasn't my employer or the most dangerous woman in the province; she was just tired, and close, and real. "If he catches you, you're a thief in his house. He can do anything he wants to you and no one will ask questions. You understand that."

"I've understood it since I was fifteen," I said. "It's why I'm good at this."

Something moved across her face—not pity, exactly. Pity would have been easier for both of us.

* * *

**Adela**

I have lied to ministers, to bishops, to three hundred women in a rented hall with my chin up and my voice like a bell. None of it cost what Thursday cost.

Because Thursday I had to laugh at Tomás's jokes.

He held court at the head of the table telling the Alvarados about a smuggling ring at the customs house—smugglers as fools, himself as the inevitable hand of law—and I laughed, and heard my own laughter come out warm, and understood that I had finally become everything I'd accused the men of being: a performer, a fraud, a person whose public face and private ledger bore no relation. Ines refilled the wine glasses behind me. I did not look at her. Looking at her would have been like flinching before the shot.

Dona Alvarado leaned toward me, eyes bright with gossip. "And your brother, Adela—he works so hard for order. Don't you find the suffragists disorderly?"

"I find order without justice to be the most disorderly thing there is," I said, and the table laughed, and I laughed too, and somewhere behind my shoulder a woman in a grey uniform slipped out through the service door.

God keep her, I thought, and passed the potatoes.

* * *

**Ines**

Tomás's study smelled of ink and cigar and the particular dust of paper that has been handled by one man for twenty years. I left the door open two inches—the hallway was empty but sound carries differently through a closed door, and I wanted to hear feet before I saw them.

His desk was oak, older than the house's pretensions, brass locks gone soft with neglect. Rufina's man had given me three picks and a tension wrench rolled in oilcloth, and ten minutes of instruction in a back room smelling of printer's solvent, and one sentence I kept returning to now: *The lock tells you what it wants if your hands are quiet.*

My hands were quiet. They had been trained quiet—years of sorting mail at red lights, of folding letters into my sleeve, of moving through other people's houses like weather. All of it, every invisible day, had been rehearsal for this.

The first pick found the pins. The tension wrench turned a fraction, caught.

From the dining room came a swell of laughter—Adela's, riding over the men's voices like a flag over a fort she was pretending not to defend.

Second pin. Third. The lock wanted to open; it was only waiting for permission.

Fourth—

A chair scraped in the dining room. Not a small scrape. A man pushing back from a table.

I went still, wrench between my fingers, breath flat against the top of my lungs. Footsteps. Male. Moving not toward the hall but away—to the water closet, by the sound, door latch, done.

Exhale, slow, through the nose. My father used to say a frightened woman smells like a lit fuse. He meant it as insult. I had made it a discipline.

Back to the lock. Fifth pin set, the plug turned, and the drawer slid open with a whisper that felt enormous and was, probably, nothing.

Ledgers. Receipts. A revolver I chose not to see. And beneath a folio stamped with the customs seal, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string that had been untied and retied more than once—the retie was sloppy, a customs man's knot done in haste—pages in a fine copperplate hand.

I didn't read them. Reading takes time and time was the enemy. I took the signature page only—the one with the column of names, the forged flourish of *Marchetti* near the bottom in ink too fresh for its pretended date—and folded it along Rufina's crease into my coat lining, where its brothers already lived.

Then the front door of the house opened.

Not the water closet. The *front door*.

* * *

**Adela**

The trouble with lying well is that people believe you, and then they stay.

Dona Alvarado had launched into the story of her cousin's nervous illness, which required, apparently, all of its details, twice. I nodded. I murmured. I calculated: Ines had been gone eleven minutes. Twelve would be competent. Fifteen, worrying. Beyond that—

The hall clock counted for me, each chime suppressed in my chest like a heartbeat I couldn't govern.

"—and the doctors say it's the nerves entirely, the modern world is very hard on the nerves—"

"It is," I agreed. "May I fetch you some coffee? Our cook makes it with cinnamon."

Anything. Anything to move the room toward its ending.

And then the front door opened and Tomás walked in—early, damp from the walk from wherever he had actually been all evening, because I realized with a cold drop of clarity that I did not know, that I had never known, that the man laughing at my table twice a week was a stranger wearing my brother's face—and he was saying, loudly, cheerfully, "Don Alvarado! They told me at the club you'd be here—" and coming down the hall toward his study to fetch cigars, past the study door that stood open two inches onto lamplight.

"Ines," I said, standing, too fast, "will show you the garden. The moonflowers open at nine precisely. It's the most reliable thing in this household."

* * *

**Ines**

I heard him coming down the hall and I want to say I thought clearly, but the truth is the body decided. There was no second exit except the window, and the window faced the drive, and the drive faced the dining room windows. So I did the thing invisibility had trained me for: I became furniture.

I closed the drawer with my hip, dropped to my knees beside the desk, and when Tomás filled the doorway I was on the floor with a shoebrush in my hand and his boot in my other hand, polishing, eyes down, the picture of a servant caught mid-task.

The signature page lay flat against my ribs, warm as a sleeping animal.

"What are you doing in here?" His voice had the mildness of a man who has not yet assembled his suspicion into a shape.

"The master's boots for the morning, señor. Your housekeeper said the black pair scuffed at the Alvarados' last week." A lie built from a true detail—that's the strongest kind; I'd learned that from reading other people's mail. "I didn't want to disturb the dinner."

He looked at me for a long moment. In the hall beyond, Adela's voice rang out, steering Don Alvarado toward the garden, loud with charm.

"My sister let you in?"

"Yes, señor."

"She's been odd lately." He crossed to the sideboard, poured himself a brandy from the decanter, drank standing, watching me in the glass. Watching me work the brush over leather I hadn't dirtied. "You've been with her a long time, Ines."

"Four years, señor."

"Do you read her letters?"

The brush stopped for half a beat. Half a beat too long. I made it into smoothing the polish with my thumb.

"Only the addresses, señor. To sort them. She likes them ordered by importance."

"And which are important?"

"Ones with government seals," I said, and looked up at him with the flat honest stupidity of a woman who has understood nothing in her life, and he laughed—one short bark—and waved me out.

"Go. Tell Cook I'm home, she can bring the coffee."

I gathered the brush and the boot and rose, keeping my shoulders bent, keeping the coat buttoned high though the study was warm. At the threshold he spoke again, quietly, and the quietness was worse than the questions.

"Ines. If anything irregular ever came through this house—you'd tell me. Wouldn't you. Before anyone else."

I turned. I gave him the truth shaped like a lie, or the lie shaped like truth; after four years I no longer knew the difference, only the weight of it.

"You'd be the first, señor."

* * *

**Adela**

I met Ines in the kitchen corridor, and one look told me everything: her color was wrong, high on the cheekbones, and her hand, when it brushed mine passing the folded linens we were both pretending to carry, was ice.

The coffee tray gave us ninety seconds of privacy by the stove.

"He came back early," she breathed.

"I saw. How?"

"Thirty seconds." Her jaw worked. "He talked to me while I knelt on his floor with a boot brush. He asked about the mail, Adela. He asked if I'd tell him if anything irregular came through." She set down the coffeepot with a precision that frightened me more than shaking would have. "He's suspicious. Not of us specifically—of the air. He came home early because something spooked him at whatever he was really doing tonight, and suspicious men search rooms."

"Then the page leaves tonight."

"With soldiers on every road north?"

"With soldiers on every road north," I confirmed, and heard how mad it sounded, and did not care.

We stood shoulder to shoulder at the great black range, two women watching a fire neither of them needed to tend, and the house noise went on around us—Tomás's voice raised in false bonhomie, the Alvarados' carriage bell, the ordinary music of an ordinary evening that was no longer either of those things.

"Ines." I kept my eyes on the coals. "When he had you on that floor. Were you afraid?"

"Afraid?" She considered it honestly, the way she considers everything, like counting coins. "Yes. But less afraid than I've been my whole life hiding from smaller things. Isn't that backwards?"

"No," I said. "That's the whole movement, in one sentence. We just put it in a petition instead of a coat lining."

She almost laughed. Then, low, so low it barely cleared the fire's crackle:

"There's something else. When I opened the drawer—I saw the revolver, and under the folio, there were telegrams. New ones. Addressed to him, marked from the garrison." Her eyes came up to mine. "One word repeated on the topmost. *Mañana.*"

Tomorrow.

I looked down the corridor toward the dining room, where my brother was finishing another man's brandy and wearing another man's smile, and I understood that we had until sunrise, and that the woman beside me—thief, liar, driver of my car, keeper of four years of my stolen secrets—was the only person alive I trusted with the dawn.

"Get the motor running," I said.

* * *

**Ines**

I went out through the kitchen garden with the linens under my arm and the page against my heart, and behind me the house glowed yellow and content, every window full of people who believed they knew one another.

The Hupmobile started on the third crank, the way it always does, patient and ready, the one thing in my life that has never asked me to be smaller than I am.

Adela came down the drive five minutes later in her coat, hatless, carrying a carpetbag, looking younger and more terrified and more alive than I had ever seen her.

"North?" she asked.

"North," I said, and put us on the dark road toward the river bridge, and the garrison gate, and whatever was waiting at the third checkpoint—where, though neither of us could know it yet, Mateo already stood talking to the soldiers, describing a woman and a motorcar, describing *me*.
