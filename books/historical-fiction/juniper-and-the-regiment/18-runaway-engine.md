## 18. Runaway Engine

The garrison gate was still rattling in my mirrors when I made the decision that would either save me or hang me: I stopped hiding.

For eleven years I had driven like a woman apologizing for the road. Ten under the speed the engine could give. Shoulders folded in. Eyes down at intersections, because eyes-down women don't get remembered. The whole architecture of my life had been built on the principle that if they couldn't see you, they couldn't name you—and if they couldn't name you, they couldn't take anything from you but everything.

Mateo had taught me what that cost. Adela at the gate had shown me the alternative: stand in the open and let them look.

So when the second army truck swung out of the garrison yard a half mile behind me—headlights even in daylight, which is what they do when they want you to know—I did not check my coat, did not rehearse a lie, did not reach for the smallness I'd worn like a second corset since girlhood.

I pressed the accelerator to the floor.

* * *

The Marchetti car was a 1910 Delaunay, black as a closed door, and Adela's brother Tomás had always treated it with the condescension men reserve for machines operated by women. He would be sorry now. I knew this car the way I knew my own hands. I knew that the carburetor ran rich in cold air and that she wanted her spark advanced a hair past where any mechanic set it. I knew the third gear sang a particular note at forty kilometers an hour, a thin silver whine like a wire pulled tight, and I had heard that note so many lonely mornings on empty roads that hearing it now felt like being spoken to by the only honest friend I had.

The mountain road unspooled ahead of me, climbing through pine toward the pass, switchbacking along the river gorge below. The river—the same river I'd crossed that morning at the bridge checkpoint, invisible, untouched—ran silver-green between the rocks, going wherever it pleased without asking anyone's permission.

The documents were against my heart. The signature page, sewn into my coat lining next to the letter with its broken government seal, warm now from my body heat, as if the paper had a pulse. As if it were alive and I was its mother or its hostage or both.

In the mirror: two trucks now. The second carried six soldiers, I estimated, counting heads over the cab. Behind them, dust, and behind the dust, nothing visible, which frightened me more than what I could see.

My hands were steady. That surprised me. I had spent so many years expecting fear to arrive and stay that I had built rooms for it, furnished them, kept the lamps lit. But fear, it turned out, had only been subletting. What moved into the space behind my sternum as the engine climbed into third gear and sang its tight-wire note was something I didn't have a name for yet. It took me another switchback to recognize it, because I hadn't felt it since I was a girl driving my father's cart before the world taught me to fold:

It was joy. Terrible, inappropriate, entirely mine.

* * *

They say a chase is chaos. They're wrong. A chase is arithmetic done at speed.

I did the sums while the pines blurred green at the edges of my vision. The trucks were heavier than the Delaunay and would fall behind on the climb—but their engines were newer and they would gain on the descents, and there were three descents between here and the pass. The road surface: gravel for the first eight kilometers, then graded clay that turned to soap in shade. Time of day: late afternoon; the sun would be in their eyes on the western-facing curves, mine on the eastern ones, and the road alternated its face every few kilometers like a woman changing her mind.

The first descent came at Kilometer Six—a long, falling left-hander with the gorge yawning off my shoulder, no guardrail, only the memory of one. The trucks' brakes began to sing behind me before I'd finished the curve, a high animal sound, and I understood something then that I filed away with the rest of the arithmetic: they feared the drop more than they feared their colonel. Men who fear the drop brake early. I did not fear the drop. I had been carrying heavier weight than gravity for weeks.

I let the car run. The needle passed sixty. Seventy. The gravel hissed under the tires like rain, like applause, like static before a broadcast. In the mirror the trucks shrank to toys.

"Where have you been all my life," I said aloud to the engine, and heard myself laugh—a short, ugly, delighted sound, a stranger's sound, and knew it wasn't a stranger's at all. It was the sound of Ines Sandoval, age thirty-four, doing exactly what she was built for, in full view of God and the Fourth Regiment.

* * *

At the Kilometer Nine turnoff I faced the first real choice. Right went down to the valley road—longer, flatter, the route a fleeing woman would take, the route that led eventually to the coast and to Rufina Ochoa's press by way of four more hours and four more chances to be caught. Left went over the pass—the short way, the hard way, a road I had driven exactly twice, once in fog, both times alone, both times telling no one.

Stealth said right. Stealth had gotten me across one bridge and one rail crossing today, had sewn documents into linings and kept my voice level through three searches. Stealth had been a good servant.

But stealth had also been Mateo's language. *Be small, be quiet, be useful, and we'll keep you.* He had offered me belonging priced in silence, and I had very nearly paid.

I took the pass road at fifty, tires chirping on the loose clay, and the truck behind me overshot the turn by twenty meters before wrestling back onto the climb, its horn bellowing once, furious, absurd.

The pass road narrowed. Pine crowns closed overhead, and the world went green-dark and rushing. Here the sun came through in blades, light and shadow strobing across the windshield at speed, and I remember thinking—later I would think of it often—that it looked like film flickering in a projector, like I was inside a moving picture about a woman who finally moves.

The signature page shifted against my ribs as I leaned into a curve. Tomás's desk. Adela's candle. The lock pick made from a hatpin. All of it behind me now except the paper itself, which rode forward with me, which had become, somewhere on this road, not evidence but testimony—not something stolen but something spoken.

A bullet cracked off the rock face ahead of us. Not near. A warning, or bad aim, or both. The second shot came closer, kissed stone somewhere behind my rear wheel, and I felt my whole body go cold and then hot and then strangely calm, the calm of the ledger, the calm of red lights and sorted mail.

They would not shoot the car's engine if they wanted the driver alive. And they needed the driver alive, because they needed to know how much had reached the press and into whose hands. Every meter I gained was bought with their hesitation, and their hesitation was purchased with my certainty: *I matter now. I am worth taking alive.*

Eleven years of making myself worthless, and here was the reward of the opposite strategy.

I laughed again. The engine answered.

* * *

The hairpin at Kilometer Fourteen nearly ended everything.

Graded clay, deep shade, the surface greased with meltwater seeping from a spring above the cut. I went in too fast—I knew it was too fast while I was doing it, some part of me already writing the postmortem—and the rear end stepped out with the casual treachery of a friend turning informer. The world rotated. The gorge appeared where the windshield had been. The trees spun.

Counter-steer. Throttle. My hands did it before my mind caught up, ten thousand hours of roads speaking through them, and the Delaunay gathered herself like a horse brought back from the brink, tail sliding one last inch, and bit, and held.

We came out of it pointed uphill, engine screaming in second gear, and behind me—one truck only. The first had stopped at the hairpin entirely; I saw it parked crooked, a soldier standing beside it with his hands on his knees, being sick over the edge.

The remaining truck was better driven. It stayed with me through the next climb, close enough now that I could see the driver's face: young, jaw set, competent. We were two drivers on a mountain road, and for one strange moment I felt a professional kinship with the man trying to capture me, the way you might respect a rival fencer. Then he flashed his lights and gestured for me to pull over—an almost courteous gesture, palm down, patting the air—and the kinship curdled into clarity.

He thought I was a courier running contraband. He didn't know he was chasing the story that would end his regiment's little industry of forged letters and disappeared organizers. He didn't know the woman he was waving to a stop had spent her whole life learning roads just like this one, alone, unseen, until the unseen miles added up to this.

I flashed my lamps back at him. Once. Then I downshifted and stood on the throttle and left him.

The summit came at Kilometer Nineteen—a bare brown crown of a hill with a stone cross leaning at its top, and beyond it the far slope falling away toward the valley, toward town, toward rooftops and telegraph wires and Rufina Ochoa's typesetters waiting for copy. I crested it wide open, all four wheels kissing air for half a heartbeat, the whole valley laid out below in gold evening light like a page someone had forgotten to censor.

And I understood, at the top of that mountain with the engine roaring and the trucks falling behind and the documents drumming against my heart like a second pulse, what the whole book of my life had been about.

I had thought invisibility was armor. It was only a smaller cage. I had thought being seen was the danger. It was the exit. Every secret I'd ever kept had been a wall in a house that was never mine, and I had just driven through the wall at seventy kilometers an hour with the state's own soldiers in my mirror, and the air coming through the window tasted like the first day of a calendar nobody had printed yet.

Ines Sandoval. Say it at full throttle. Let them hear.

* * *

The descent should have been easy and nearly wasn't. The last truck hung on my mirror all the way down the north face, gaining on every straight, its driver good enough and stubborn enough to make me earn the valley. Twice he came within a hundred meters, close enough to read the regimental number stenciled on his hood—*R-4*, the same outfit whose seal had been forged onto that letter, which struck me as a kind of poetry the censors would hate. Twice I lost him again in the curves, using roads I'd memorized the way other people memorize prayers.

At the valley junction, where the mountain road spills onto the flat and forks toward town, I saw the thing I had been dreading without knowing I dreaded it: a motorcycle, parked at the junction shoulder, its rider standing beside it reading a paper.

Orders travel faster than cars. Someone had telegraphed ahead.

He looked up. I was two hundred meters out and closing at eighty, and I watched him do the arithmetic I had done all afternoon—single woman, government plates missing, speed inconsistent with innocence—and I watched him step toward his machine.

There was no room to go around him. There was only room to be *seen*, one more time, unmistakably, by one more man who would remember me forever.

I leaned on the horn—a deep, brass, imperious bark, the voice of a car that had carried a famous woman for eleven years and learned some of her authority—and I did not slow down.

He jumped. Actually jumped, sideways, papers flying, and I was past him in a slather of noise and wind with his shouts thinning behind me like a dropped thread, and the town rose up ahead, chimneys and church spires and the print shop's smoke, and the last truck gave up at the town limit, braking hard where the jurisdiction ended and the witnesses began.

I drove the final streets slowly. On purpose. At the lawful speed, hands at ten and two, a woman driving sedately through her own town in a car full of revolution.

Rufina Ochoa was standing in the print shop doorway when I pulled up, sleeves rolled, ink to the elbows, and I got out on legs made of rope and lightning and held up the coat with its sewn lining like a midwife presenting a child.

"It's all here," I said, and my voice didn't shake, and that was its own revelation. "Every name. Every seal. And I know the road now—all three crossings, all the guards, the times they change shifts." I heard myself plan aloud, fast, fluent, a woman speaking in her own tongue after years of translation. "Adela can get the rest out through the valley road if she leaves before dawn. I'll drive her myself."

Rufina looked at me for a long moment, ink-stained and appraising, the way she must look at copy.

"They'll come for you tonight," she said.

"I know." I smiled, and felt the smile fit my face as if it had been tailored there. "Set your presses. I'll be back before dark—I'm collecting Mrs. Marchetti."

I turned toward the car, and that was when I saw the boy from the newspaper's back steps sprinting up the street toward us, waving a telegram, shouting my name—

Not my name. Adela's.
