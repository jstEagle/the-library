## 1. The Weight of Water

The bell at Meridian compound rang before dawn, as it always did, and Petra Villalobos rose out of sleep the way she rose out of water: smoothly, without splashing, without letting anyone see her surface.

She had learned that in her ninth year, from the sea itself. A diver who broke the water gasping gave away her position to every current and every ear above it. A diver who came up quiet could hear what the world said about her before she was seen. The lesson had cost her mother a switch across the shoulders when she'd surfaced shrieking with cold once too often, but the lesson held. Eleven years later, Petra woke in silence, dressed in silence, and was standing at the drying racks before the eastern sky had finished deciding whether it would be copper or ash.

It chose ash. Ash meant wind coming off the gulf by noon, which meant the nets would need to come in heavy and wet, which meant her arms would be singing by midday. She noted this the way she noted everything — without comment, filed somewhere behind her ribs where she kept the weather and the moods of the overseers and the number of days left on her indenture (three thousand one hundred and forty, though she did the arithmetic only on Sundays) — and she began unpinning the first nets from their lines.

Salt had stiffened the mesh overnight. It crackled like old sugar under her fingers, and the smell of it rose up around her, brine and rope-rot and something faintly green where weed had caught in the twine. She worked by touch more than sight. Her hands knew this work the way other people's hands knew prayer: the rhythm of unpinning, folding, stacking; the way to shake a net so the crabs fell out through the door she made with her thumb and forefinger instead of pinching her wrists raw. She freed three crabs this morning and let them scuttle into the shadow of the rack rather than into the bucket marked for the kitchen. This was not kindness, exactly, or not only. It was the small currency of the world beneath the world — the crabs belonged to no ledger, so neither did her mercy toward them.

"Villalobos." Old Sefita's voice, gravel over water, from the end of the drying yard. "You'll take the north racks today. Girl from the south quarter didn't come."

Petra nodded. Speaking before the sun was fully up was a waste of breath; Sefita understood this better than anyone. She had been a diver herself once, before her lungs filled with the sea a little at a time over thirty years, and now she stood at the compound gate each morning counting heads like a woman counting what remained of her own life. Her eyes were the color of driftwood. They passed over Petra without lingering, which was the highest compliment the compound had to offer.

To be overlooked here was to be safe. Petra had built her whole existence inside that principle, stone by patient stone, the way an oyster builds — layer over layer over irritant, until the thing that hurt became the thing that shielded.

* * *

By seven the light had thickened and the yard was full of sound: the slap of wet canvas, the creak of the winch, the overseer Tamarez pacing the walkway with his boots ringing on boards still slick from the night's damp. Petra hauled nets at the north racks with two of the younger girls, Nena and Ilsa, sisters who had been sold into the company together three years ago and who still slept curled back-to-back like bookends, as if they could hold each other against everything the ledger held them by.

"The new levy," Nena whispered, low enough that even Petra, kneeling an arm's length away, nearly lost it under the wind. "They say the boats are next. That they're taking the dive-boats for the fleet levy and calling it requisition."

"I heard my uncle's boat is already listed," Ilsa said. Her hands never stopped moving; that was the rule of whispering at Meridian, that your hands must never stop moving. "Listed in a book somewhere. Nobody's seen the book. But it exists, they say. A list."

*A list.* Petra felt the words settle somewhere below her sternum, cold as a swallowed stone. Her mother had co-owned a boat once — half-share in the *Salomé*, with old Bastian Reyes, before annexation, before the fever took her mother and the company absorbed her debts along with her body. Half a share in a boat should have died with the debt or passed down. Instead it sat, presumably, wherever such things sat, waiting for someone with a pen to decide its weight.

She said nothing. She folded the net, shook out a trapped moon-jelly, set it gently aside to dissolve back into the tide pool it had blown from.

"You're quiet," Nena observed, which was rather like remarking that the sea was wet.

"She's always quiet," Ilsa said, and there was no malice in it. Among the divers' children, Petra's silence was simply a fact of the landscape, like the compound wall or the smell at low tide. They had stopped expecting anything else years ago. Some of them had decided it meant she was content. *Quiet Villalobos*, they called her, almost fondly, the way you'd name a rock formation. *Nothing bothers Quiet Villalobos.*

She let them believe it. It was easier than the truth, which was that she had spent her childhood learning exactly how much could be taken from a person who made noise, and had resolved, with the cold thoroughness of a child grown old early, never to find out how much could be taken from one who didn't.

At the midday break she ate rice and dried fish in the shade of the sail loft, sitting where she always sat, third piling from the seaward corner, where the breeze came through the slats and carried off the worst of the heat. Maren, the cook's ancient helper, shuffled past and — without appearing to look at her — set a second portion of fish beside Petra's knee.

"You've gone thin," Maren muttered, to no one, to the air, and moved on before gratitude could attach itself to anything.

This, too, was part of the economy Petra lived in. Maren gave her extra food because two winters ago Petra had mended the hole in Maren's sleeping mat and never mentioned it. Petra gave Sefita half her tea ration on the first cold morning of each year because Sefita had once, a decade past, looked away when Petra had come in late from visiting — from *somewhere* — and said nothing. Nothing given, nothing recorded. The compound kept its ledgers of hours and debts and names, but underneath ran this older accounting, unwritten, unenforceable, and therefore the only one Petra trusted. Kindness traded in whispers could not be taxed.

She ate slowly, watching the gulf. From the loft's gap-toothed porch the water lay flat as hammered tin all the way to the horizon, and on the horizon, faint as a pencil mark, the shapes of the Atlas fleet's transports rode at anchor — six ships, maybe seven at this distance, come to carry away whatever the colony had that the crown wanted. The divers' children had grown up reading ships the way farmers read clouds. Everyone on the drying yard had glanced at those shapes at least twice this morning and then carefully not mentioned them.

Three thousand one hundred and forty days. Petra turned the number over and put it away.

* * *

Afternoon went to the sails. This was her favorite labor, though she would have bitten through her own tongue before admitting to a favorite anything. In the sail loft the light came down in dusty gold bars through the high windows, and the work demanded a different attention than the nets — closer, quieter, solitary. You spread the canvas on the long table and walked it slowly, running your palms over the weave until you found the weak place, and then you sat cross-legged with the palm of your hand flat against the cloth and you sewed, and the needle's small sounds were the only conversation required of you.

She was an hour into patching a chafed seam on a jib when the door at the far end opened and Tamarez came in with a stranger.

Petra's hands did not stop. This was discipline older than thought: whatever enters the room, the hands keep sewing.

But she saw them, from beneath lashes kept deliberately low. Tamarez she knew — broad, sweating, a man who wore authority the way some men wear a coat too heavy for the season, constantly shrugging at it. The stranger was another order of creature entirely. Thin, precise, dressed in a grey traveling suit that had been good before the voyage and had not surrendered its dignity regardless, carrying a leather case and a roll of paper under one arm. He moved through the loft the way a clerk moves through a market he cannot afford: observing, cataloguing, touching nothing. Even at forty paces Petra could see the ink stains on his right hand's fingers, the particular shadows that never wash out, that mark a man who has given his life to pens.

A company man? No. His case bore no Meridian stamp. And Tamarez — she watched him sideways — Tamarez was being polite to him in the strained, overcareful way he was polite only to men whose authority he couldn't measure. Which meant the stranger's authority was imperial.

"...the census," the stranger was saying, and his voice carried oddly well in the loft, dry and level, a voice used to being written down rather than listened to. "Every soul resident at the compound. Names, birthplaces, terms of service. The schedule must be complete before I move on to the harbor district."

"And they're all here," Tamarez said. "Divers, dockhands, servants. You'll want the young ones first, they're easier to sort—"

"They are not livestock to be sorted, Mr. Tamarez. They are subjects of the crown, and they will be recorded as such." A pause, the sound of the case being set down. "I'll begin whenever convenient. There is no hurry that justifies doing this badly."

Something shifted in Petra's chest — a small, involuntary warmth toward a man she had never seen before in her life, a warmth she immediately distrusted. *No hurry that justifies doing this badly.* She bent lower over her seam. Men who spoke like that were dangerous in ways she couldn't yet map, precisely because they believed in the work. A lazy official could be dodged. A careful one saw things.

Tamarez's boots crossed the loft toward the stairs down to the yard. Then his voice floated back up, casual as a dropped glove:

"Start with the drying yard lot, sir, if you like. They're all accounted for there. Well—" and here Petra heard the shrug in his voice, the coat shifting on his shoulders, "—all except the quiet one. But she's always somewhere about. Villalobos. You won't get trouble out of her. Been here since she was a sprat, no family worth the ink, happy as anything hauling nets. Content, they say."

*Content.* The word landed on Petra like a hand between the shoulder blades, pressing her down over her needle. Happy as anything. No family worth the ink. She stared at the jib's pale canvas until it swam, and thought, with a clarity that surprised her after eleven years of practice: *he is going to write that down. Someone is going to write me down, and he will write me wrong.*

It should not have mattered. Being misdescribed was the whole architecture of her safety; she had built it that way. And yet the stranger's footsteps were crossing the loft now, unhurried, and she could feel the roll of paper under his arm like weather changing, and for the first time in years she understood that the sea of her invisibility had a far shore, and a man with questions was standing on it, and the tide was already pulling her toward him.

She finished her stitch. Tied it off. Bit the thread.

Then she rose, smoothed her apron, and turned to face the census taker of the imperial crown, wearing the face of Quiet Villalobos, content as anything — while behind her ribs, three thousand one hundred and forty days ticked softly, one by one, like water filling a hull.
