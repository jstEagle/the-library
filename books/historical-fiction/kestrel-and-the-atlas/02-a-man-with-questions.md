## 2. A Man with Questions

The steamer brought him to the pearl coast on a morning the color of unpolished silver, and Elias Kestrel stepped onto the pier with one trunk, one valise of instruments, and a commission under imperial seal that weighed more than both together. He had crossed eleven days of open water to arrive at this particular stretch of the gulf, where the sea lay shallow as spilled milk over sandbars, and where the air carried a smell he would never quite grow used to: brine and tar and the sweet rot of oyster shells heaped in white mountains behind every compound.

He was forty-one years old and tired in a way that sleep did not touch.

The Meridian Trading Company compound announced itself before he saw it — a sprawl of warehouses and drying racks and long low dormitories the color of bleached bone, all of it ringed by a palisade whose timber had gone gray with salt. The company flag snapped above the gatehouse, its blue meridian line stitched in thread that had once been gold. Below it, smaller, the imperial pennant he himself had shipped on the same steamer, folded in oilcloth beside his ink pots. He had insisted on that arrangement: empire above company. It was the kind of distinction that mattered to men who kept records for a living, and to no one else on this coast at all.

A clerk met him at the gate — a narrow young man named Pellworthy whose collar was too tight and whose handshake was damp — and led him through the compound's morning business as though giving a tour of a cathedral. Divers' children hauling nets heavier than themselves. Women mending sails under the eaves, needles flashing. The great sorting tables where shell was graded by lamplight even now, in daylight, because the lamplight showed the flaws. And everywhere, everywhere, people who moved quickly and looked at nothing and made themselves small in doorways as he passed.

Kestrel counted them. He could not help it. Twenty-six years of taking censuses in territories that did not want to be counted had given him a habit of arithmetic that ran beneath his thoughts like a tide.

"How many indentures at this station?" he asked.

Pellworthy consulted a slate he carried like scripture. "Two hundred and forty in the rolls, sir. Two hundred thirty-one present and accounted. Nine out on the water with the morning boats."

"And how many are children of indentures? Born into it?"

That gave the clerk a moment's pause — not offense, but the blankness of a man who had never needed to separate one question from another. "Most of them, sir. It's the old families, mostly. Been with Meridian since the annexation."

*Since the annexation.* Kestlet wrote the phrase in his mind and set a mark beside it. In his trade, phrases like that were seams in a garment, and seams told you where the cloth had been joined — or torn.

They gave him a room in the counting-house, second floor, with a window over the yard and a table that wobbled until he folded a census blank into quarters and set it under the short leg. He unpacked with the economy of long habit: ink, blotters, the brass scale for weighing seals and paper, the register itself — a ledger bound in black calf, its spine stamped with the imperial cipher, its first page bearing an order in Secretary's Hand that instructed him, in language of perfect bureaucratic gentleness, to record *the persons, holdings, origins, and dispositions* of all souls resident upon the Meridian concession.

*Dispositions.* He had read that word four times on the crossing and each time it had meant something slightly different. That, too, was the trade. Words that shifted weight when you lifted them.

He began that afternoon.

* * *

The counting-room smelled of lamp oil and dried ink, and by the third day it smelled also of the people who came through it: coconut oil dressed in hair, the mineral tang of the sea that no amount of washing surrendered, the smoke of the cook-fires, and underneath it all, faintly, the sweetness of the shells — as if everyone on this coast carried the oyster beds home in their lungs.

Kestrel sat behind the table with the register open before him and asked his questions in the trade tongue, which he spoke with an accent the divers found faintly amusing and which he had long ago stopped apologizing for. One at a time they came. Name. Year and place of birth. Mother's name. Father's name, if known. Trade. Years of service remaining. And then the final column, the one the Secretary's Hand had added in a different ink from the rest, the one he asked last so that it would land last:

*Loyalty. Do you acknowledge the crown's lawful authority over these waters, and do you bear it goodwill?*

They all said yes. Of course they said yes. A fish said yes to the net. He recorded their answers in his careful, upright hand, and he watched their faces while he did it, because faces were also a kind of record, and often the only honest one.

There was the sail-mender Oyinkan, sixty and straight-backed, who answered *yes* in a voice like a door closing and looked at the wall behind his head the whole time. There was Bautista the rack-keeper, who said yes twice, once for the crown and once, unprompted, for Director Halloway personally, which told Kestrel something about the ecology of fear in this place. There were twins born in the year of the fever, who stood shoulder to shoulder and answered in unison like a single person wearing two bodies, and he wrote down both names and thought: *the register wants to make you singular, and you have refused.*

He worked from seven in the morning until the light failed, and after the light failed by lamp until Pellworthy came to blow the lamp out and remind him the yard bell rang curfew. His wrist ached. His eyes burned. On the fourth evening he caught himself writing the word *loyalty* in the margin of his private notebook and staring at it until the letters lost their meaning, the way a coin goes strange when you look at it too long.

Weary, yes. But weariness was not the problem. The problem was that he was good at this, and being good at it had begun, somewhere in the last decade, to feel like a moral event rather than a professional one.

* * *

On the fifth day the morning boats came in early, and among those summoned to the counting-room was the girl from the yard.

He had noticed her before he knew her name — noticed, more precisely, her *unnoticeability*, which is a thing a census taker learns to see the way a jeweler sees paste. On his first evening walking to the gatehouse he had passed perhaps forty indentures in the yard and remembered three of them: the boy with the burned arm, the woman who sang to the nets, the old man who spat when the imperial pennant went up. The rest had been weather. But this one — slender, early twenties, skin the deep brown of wet walnut, hair braided flat against her skull in the diver's fashion — she had done something subtler than hiding. She had arranged herself. Standing at the edge of a work party just so; turning her head at exactly the angle that put her face in shadow of the drying racks; moving through doorways in the wake of larger people. She was not invisible. She was *practiced*, and practice left a grain in a person the way handling leaves a patina on silver.

Her name, when she gave it, was Petra Villalobos.

"Year of birth?"

"Eighteen seventy-nine. Dry season."

"Place?"

"The diving grounds off Serpent Cay. My mother was working the beds." A pause, precise as a cut. "She didn't stop for me long."

He noted it. Most women of the indenture went back to the water within days; the rolls were full of births logged between tides. But the phrasing stayed with him — *she didn't stop for me long* — grief flattened into fact, delivered without inflection, which is the hardest way to deliver grief.

"Mother's name?"

"Lucía Villalobos. Deceased. Nineteen and two."

He knew that already, from the death rolls he'd cross-checked against the compound's own ledgers the night before. Lucía Villalobos, diver, age thirty-four, drowned. What the death roll did not say — what no roll he had yet seen said — was anything else about her.

"Father?"

"Not recorded."

"Your mother's village?" he asked. "Where was she born? Place of origin matters in the—"

He stopped.

He stopped because he heard the question as it left him, heard it land in the room, and understood one heartbeat later that it was the wrong question — not improper, precisely; the Secretary's Hand required origins — but wrong *here*, wrong for her, wrong the way a hand laid suddenly on a wound is wrong. Because the girl had gone still. Not frozen, exactly. She had done the opposite of freezing: everything about her continued to function, hands resting on the table's edge, breath moving, expression attentive and mild. But some essential current beneath it all had shut off like a lamp behind a shuttered window, and in the half-second of that shutter closing he saw, briefly, the thing it had hidden — a calculation, fast and total, the look of someone measuring the distance to every exit in a room.

And he understood, in the same instant, that she was measuring him.

"My mother," Petra Villalobos said, "was born on the coast. Like everyone here."

It was a perfectly reasonable answer. It was almost certainly a false one. He looked down at the form, at the empty line, and he had a choice that took less than a second and that he would examine, later, at length, in the dark: he could press. *The coast is four hundred miles, madam. Which hundred?* He could press because the instructions pressed him, because origins were the whole architecture of this census, because somewhere in a capital office a man with clean cuffs wanted grids drawn around every family that predated the annexation, and a mother's village was exactly the kind of thread that, pulled, unraveled a whole history of boats and co-owned beds and claims older than the company's charter.

He wrote: *Born on the coast.*

His pen scratched. The harbor breathed outside the window. When he looked up again the shutter was closed and the mild face was back, attentive, forgettable, offering itself up to be forgotten.

"Loyalty," he said, making his voice flat, procedural, an apology of a kind. "Do you acknowledge the crown's lawful authority over these waters, and do you bear it goodwill?"

"Yes." No hesitation at all. That was the remarkable thing — the loyalty question, the one that stuck in every other throat on this coast, slid off her like water off wax. Because it was easy to answer if you had decided, long ago, that answers cost nothing and meant nothing, and he recognized the decision because he had watched it settle over entire islands.

"Do you bear it goodwill?" he repeated anyway. It was not required. He was not sure why he asked it twice, except that asking twice was sometimes the only way to see whether a face would crack along the seam.

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. Recognition, maybe — the smallest possible acknowledgment between two professionals that a ritual was being performed and both parties knew the steps.

"Yes," she said again, softer. "I bear it goodwill."

He wrote it. He dated the entry. He dismissed her with the formula he gave everyone — *you may go, thank you for your cooperation* — and she inclined her head with a courtesy so exact it was nearly insolent, and left.

At the door she paused. Half a heartbeat. He would replay that half-heartbeat many times in the weeks to come, hunting it for meaning the way a man shakes a locked box to guess at its contents.

"You ask about mothers," she said, not turning around. "Nobody asks about mothers."

Then she was gone, down the stairs, into the noise of the yard, and within ten seconds of watching her he had lost her — truly lost her, dissolved into forty identical figures hauling and mending and making themselves small — and he stood at the window with his pen still uncapped, ink drying on its nib, and admitted to himself that he had been unsettled.

Not by defiance. He knew defiance; it was cheap and he priced it weekly. By the craft of it. By the realization that he had spent five days cataloguing this compound's souls and had recorded, in his meticulous hand, a fiction sitting directly in front of him wearing the uniform of fact — and that she had known, the whole time, exactly which questions were dangerous, which meant she knew things about this coast, about its ledgers and its losses, that no mere diver's child should need to know.

He sat back down. He opened his private notebook — the one that belonged to no office, the one he would burn if ever ordered to surrender it — and beneath the day's date he wrote, in the cramped cipher he allowed himself:

*Villalobos, P. Lie #1: birthplace of mother. Competent liar. Why does a diver's daughter need to lie well?*

He looked at the sentence for a long time. Then, because he was honest with his records even when he could not be honest with anyone else, he added:

*Lie #2 (mine): I did not press.*

Outside, the tide was turning. He could hear it — that long slow exhale the flats made when the water drew off them, a sound like the coast clearing its throat before it spoke. Tomorrow the boats would go out again, and he would sit behind his wobbling table and ask his questions, and two hundred souls would pass through the register and come out the other side as columns and loyal yeses.

But that night, before curfew, Pellworthy brought him the compound's internal rolls to cross-reference, as Halloway's office had instructed, and Kestrel turned the pages by lamplight until he found what half of him had been looking for since the morning he arrived: the entry for Lucía Villalobos. Diver. And beside her name, in the column marked *holdings and shares*, an annotation in faded violet ink — a co-ownership mark, a boat share, half interest in a vessel called the *Paloma*, entered jointly with a name that made Kestrel set down his lamp-trimmed pen very carefully indeed.

The second name on the line was Amara Sefu — daughter of the house of Sefu. The deposed barons. The enemies of the crown.

The violet ink had been struck through by a later clerk's heavy black stroke, but ink remembers what it was, and so did Kestrel.

He closed the book and sat in the dark of the counting-room, listening to the flats breathe and the curfew bell toll across the water, and thought: *a drowned woman's boat, a baron's daughter, and a girl who lies like a lady — and my orders are to find out which of them the empire should be afraid of.*

Somewhere below, in the women's loft, a shutter closed quietly. He did not hear it. But he felt it, the way one feels a door shut in a far room — as a change in the air.

In the morning, he would begin asking about boats.
