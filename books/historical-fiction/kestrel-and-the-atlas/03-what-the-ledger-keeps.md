## 3. What the Ledger Keeps

The rice went into the basket first, because rice was the reason. Everything else — the salt fish wrapped in banana leaf, the twist of dried chilies, the stub of tallow candle salvaged from the compound kitchens — could be explained away as charity, as habit, as nothing. But rice was currency on the waterfront, and Petra carried it low against her hip where the basket's weave hid its weight, the way she had learned to carry everything that mattered.

She left the Meridian compound by the north gate, past the cooper's shed where Old Ferro was bending staves and did not look up, past the lime kilns breathing their white smoke into the morning. Nobody stopped her. That was the whole architecture of her life: nobody stopped her, nobody noticed her, nobody marked the fourth-year indenture with the sun-cracked hands and the basket of nothing going out along the seawall road with a stride too purposeful for an errand girl. Invisibility was not something that happened to Petra Villalobos. It was something she made, daily, the way other women made bread — kneaded and shaped and set to rise in the dark.

The tide was out far enough to show the bones of the harbor. Pilings stood exposed in the mud like a congregation of drowned men, and the smell of it — sulfur and brine and old wood rot — was the smell she had known longer than any other smell, older than memory itself. She had been diving these flats before she was tall enough to name what she was doing. Her mother had held her under the green water the way you'd hold a child's hand crossing a street, and the oysters had opened for them both like little mouths saying yes.

Her mother had been dead eleven years. The boats were still hers. Or they had been. Or they were supposed to have been.

Petra walked faster.

* * *

The house at the end of Anchor Row had been beautiful once, in the way that certain people are beautiful once — before grief or weather or empire got hold of them. It stood three stories of salt-bleached coral stone, its balconies sagging like tired shoulders, its shutters hanging at angles that suggested either decay or a shrug. A coat of arms had been chiseled off the lintel above the door; you could still see the ghost of it, a pale rectangle in the stone where the baron's pearl-and-crown had been hammered away by men who thought removing a symbol removed a family.

Petra knocked twice, paused, then once more. The code mattered now, even if neither of them said so aloud.

Amara answered with her hair down, which meant no one had visited, which meant it was safe, which meant — for another hour, anyway — the world had left them alone.

"You're late," Amara said.

"The tide was out. I had to walk around the mudflats."

"You always walk around the mudflats."

"Because I always come at low tide." Petra stepped inside, into the cool dimness that smelled of dust and camphor and, faintly, of the sea that never quite let this house go. "And because the mudflats are where nobody is. You should try nobody sometime. It's very restful."

Amara laughed — a short, real laugh, quickly closed like a hand over a mouth — and took the basket from her. This was their liturgy: the complaint, the joke, the handing over of food. They had been performing it for six years, since the year after the annexation, when the deposed baron had begun his long public ruin and his daughter had stopped receiving visitors. Everyone else had drifted away, the way people drift from a sinking ship, politely, gradually, without ever admitting they were abandoning anything. Petra had stayed. She could not have said why, in those early years. She would not have wanted to say why anyway. Reasons were things that could be used against you.

They climbed to the kitchen at the back of the house, the only room Amara kept fully alive — copper pots polished, herbs drying from the beams, a window cracked to let the gulf breathe through. Amara put water on to boil and measured out rice with the careful economy of someone who knew exactly how many meals remained in any sack.

"There's gossip," Petra offered, settling onto the stool by the cold stove. "Ferro's apprentice ran off with the paymaster's wife. The one with the teeth."

"All of Ferro's apprentices run off with someone's wife eventually. It's how we know they're ready for journeyman work."

"This one took the strongbox."

"Ah. Then he'll make foreman."

Petra smiled and let it sit between them, this small warm thing they built out of nothing every week. Outside, a gull cried and cried and got no answer. Amara's back was turned, her dark hands moving over the pot, and Petra watched her the way she always watched her — with an attention she permitted herself nowhere else. Amara Sefrou, last daughter of a house that had owned half the pearl fleet before the crown decided owning things was a privilege rather than a right. She had been raised on floors Petra couldn't have walked on without permission, and now she lived on rice smuggled in a servant's basket, and somehow she sat straighter than anyone Petra had ever met. There was a lesson in that. Petra had never decided whether it was an inspiring one or a terrible one.

Amara stirred the pot. Then, without turning: "A census man came to the row yesterday."

The warmth in the room shifted, the way a room shifts when a door opens somewhere in the house. "I know," Petra said carefully. "He's at the compound now. Kestrel. He counts us."

"He counted us. He came to Anchor Row with his book and his imperial seal and he asked my father's old boatman how many vessels lay off our property. Not how many people. Vessels." She turned around then, and her face was doing something Petra had rarely seen it do: it was afraid, and it was furious about being afraid. "He asked whether the Sefrou fleet was registered under the old charter or the new one. Whether any of it had passed into other hands since annexation. Other hands, Petra. Whose hands did he imagine?"

The boiling water made its small insistent noise. Petra found that her own hands had gone still in her lap, and she made herself unstill them, made herself shrug, because shrugging was what an unremarkable woman did with alarming news.

"Census men count everything. It doesn't mean—"

"It means," Amara said, "that I've seen what these lists are for. Do you remember the Rashidi? Two years ago, up the coast?"

"I remember."

Their property was seized in a single week. Declared enemy assets, because a cousin twice removed had spoken against the governor at a market. The family was given notice printed in a language half of them couldn't read, and by the time they understood, the ships were flying company colors. Amara crossed the room and knelt by the loose floorboard beneath the herb shelf, the one Petra knew better than to look at directly, and lifted out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth.

She set it on the table between them like a patient setting down a burden.

Petra knew what it was before the wrapping came open. She had known for six years that it existed; she had helped carry it, once, on a night when the patrols were doubled and Amara's father was already sick. But she had never been allowed to look at it. It had been Amara's mother's, and Amara's mother's instructions had been specific, and Amara honored the dead with the same iron thoroughness with which she honored everything.

Now Amara unwrapped it.

The ledger was smaller than Petra expected. Bound in scuffed blue leather, its corners worn round, swollen slightly with damp years. Amara opened it with hands that were almost gentle and turned it toward the window light.

"My mother's registry. Every vessel of the fleet, recorded under the old charter, with her signature and my father's and the harbormaster's — the true harbormaster, the one before the company appointed theirs. Dates of purchase. Co-ownership agreements. Everything, inked and witnessed before there was a crown on this coast to argue with it." Her finger came down on a line of browned script. "Look."

Petra looked. Reading was still, for her, a labor — letters that had to be caught one at a time like slippery fish, assembled, interrogated. But she could read this. She read it twice.

*Villalobos, Maren — part owner, two shares of four. Perla and Santa Rosa. Recorded in perpetuity, heirs and assigns.*

Her mother's name. In her mother's own hand, probably — the signature had the confident downward slash she remembered from the few papers her mother had ever signed. Two shares of four. Two boats. *In perpetuity.*

"She bought in the year you were born," Amara said quietly. "Diving shares. Fourteen years of diving, traded for two shares. My mother always said Maren Villalobos drove the hardest bargain of anyone on the coast, and she was a baron's wife who'd bargained with emirs."

Petra said nothing. There was a sound in her ears like the sea heard from underwater, that vast pressure-hum, and behind it her own pulse. Her mother had owned two boats. Had owned them on paper, legally, forever — and had died owing the Meridian Company an impossible debt for medicine and burial, and the debt had eaten Petra instead, twelve years of indenture, and the boats had gone... where? She had assumed they were taken against the debt. She had assumed it was fair, because assuming the company's arithmetic was fair was easier than the alternative.

"They're on the seizure list," Amara said. "Your mother's boats. The census goes in, the lists come out, and everything flagged — everything touched by an enemy name, everything co-owned, everything ambiguous — gets taken before the Atlas fleet sails. That's the deadline, don't you see? The fleet departs, and whatever's aboard it or claimed by it becomes a matter for admiralty courts across the gulf, and no diver's widow's daughter can follow the paper that far." Amara's voice stayed level, but her knuckles were white on the table's edge. "Halloway isn't counting people, Petra. He's counting what people have, and deciding it was never really theirs."

*In perpetuity.* The words sat in Petra's chest like a swallowed stone.

"They're mine," she said. It came out barely louder than breath. "By law. By the old law, the charter law — they're mine. Half of them are mine."

"And the company holds your indenture, which means until it's served, the company holds you, and anything yours it may administer as it pleases. That's the new law." Amara reached across and closed the ledger, softly, as if shutting a sleeping child's eyes. "I'm not telling you to break laws. I'm telling you what's coming, because you asked me once — do you remember? Years ago, you asked me what I'd saved from the fire, and I told you: proof. Paper is how they took everything from us. And paper—" her hand rested flat on the blue leather "—is the only thing that can take it back."

The kettle shrieked. Neither of them moved for a moment. Then Amara rose to pull it from the fire, and the small domestic motion broke something loose in the room, and Petra breathed again.

* * *

They ate rice and salt fish at the table with the ledger between them, closed but present, like a third guest. Petra talked about the census man — carefully, giving away nothing of Halloway, nothing of the compound's currents, because some knowledge was dangerous to share even here, especially here. But Amara watched her too closely, the way she always did, and near the end she said:

"You've changed since spring. You carry yourself differently. Like someone handed you something heavy and told you not to drop it."

"It's the rice," Petra said. "Very heavy, rice."

Amara didn't smile. "If it ever gets heavy enough — whatever it is — you bring it here. Whatever it costs you. Promise me the opposite of what everyone promises, for once. Most people promise to share the good. Promise me you'll bring me the bad."

Petra looked at her — at the ruined house behind her, the dead parents, the stolen fleet, the straight spine — and felt the fierce, frightening tenderness that had lived in her chest for six years now, unnamed, unacted upon, fed weekly on rice and gossip.

"If it comes to that," she said, "you'll be the second to know."

"And the first?"

"The first will be whoever I have to lie to on the way."

It was meant as a joke. Amara didn't take it as one. She nodded slowly, once, as if a contract had been executed and witnessed, and Petra understood with a small cold clarity that in this house, among the ruins of a family destroyed by documents, everything was a contract.

* * *

She walked back along the seawall with the empty basket bumping her hip and the tide beginning to turn, the mudflats drowning again inch by inch, patient as bureaucracy. The compound chimed the afternoon bell ahead of her. Behind her, Anchor Row shrank; ahead, the Meridian compound squatted over the harbor with its warehouses and its ledgers, holding everything, accounting for everything, dropping its ink into every record on the coast.

*Two shares of four. In perpetuity.*

All those years she had believed her mother's boats were lost fairly — swallowed by debt, by misfortune, by the ordinary cruelty of the world. But there was a page in a blue ledger that said otherwise, witnessed and dated, and somewhere in Director Halloway's offices there was a list, and on that list her mother's name was not a legacy but a flag, and when the Atlas fleet sailed—

Petra stopped walking. Stood at the seam between the seawall road and the compound gate, one foot in each world, and felt fear turn over inside her like a keel righting itself — felt it become something with edges, something with direction.

Fear was a current. She had grown up in currents. You did not fight a current head-on; you let it carry you somewhere it did not know it was taking you.

She picked up the basket and walked through the gate, and by the time she reached the drying racks she had already begun, without naming it, to think like a forger's apprentice thinks: *Where does the paper live? Who watches it? What does a true seal look like, pressed close, in good light?*

She did not yet know that the census man Kestrel would be the one to teach her.

She knew only that the list was coming, and that this time, she intended to be holding the pen when it did.
