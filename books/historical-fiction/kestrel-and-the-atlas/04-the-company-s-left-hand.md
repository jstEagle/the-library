## 4. The Company's Left Hand

The Meridian Trading Company did not believe in beauty, but it believed in weight. Elias Kestrel felt this most keenly on the morning he carried the finished census up from the counting rooms to the Director's floor — three weeks of names and births and loyalties bound in oiled canvas, heavy as a paving stone, heavier for what it had cost him in patience and small betrayals of attention.

The stairwell smelled of tallow and salt-rot, the building's two permanent perfumes. On the third landing a clerk was beating a carpet over the rail, sending dust down through shafts of light like something being punished. Kestrel climbed past him without meeting his eye. He had learned, in eleven years of imperial service, that the safest way to move through any institution was as if you were expected somewhere better and slightly late.

Director Halloway received him in a room that contradicted everything Kestral knew about the colony's poverty of comfort. Here were rugs from the far side of the gulf, deep enough to swallow footfalls. Here were windows of true glass, not oiled cloth, and through them the harbor lay arranged like a ledger entry: the masts, the drying nets, the low hulks of the pearl fleet at their moorings, and beyond them the grey line of the Atlas squadron, anchored in the roads, its guns run out for no reason anyone official would name.

"Kestrel." Halloway did not rise. He was a broad man gone soft in the way of men who had never once needed to hurry, with a face like a coin worn smooth by too many hands. "Sit. You've been thorough. I could tell from the sound of your boots."

Kestrel set the census on the desk. His boots, he reflected, made the same sound going everywhere.

"I've completed the enumeration," he said. "One thousand four hundred and six souls resident at the compound and adjacent quarters. Names, ages, places of birth, conditions of indenture. The loyalty declarations are appended in the final section."

"Loyalty declarations." Halloway said the words the way another man might say *vintage*. He drew the canvas off the ledger and turned pages with a forefinger that left faint damp ghosts behind it. Outside, a gull screamed at something and was answered. "You asked each one directly? Face to face?"

"It's in the instructions."

"Yes. I wrote the instructions." Halloway smiled without warmth or intent, the way furniture smiles. "I'm asking whether you actually looked at them while you did it."

Kestrel considered the question honestly, because lying to a man like Halloway seemed less dangerous than being caught rehearsing a lie. "Some of them I looked at. Most of them I heard. There's a difference."

"There is." Halloway closed the ledger and rested one hand flat upon it, a man taking possession. "That difference is why you're here and not some junior clerk who can spell but not listen. Tell me, Kestrel — how long have you been on this coast?"

"Six years. Two in the customs house, four with the survey office."

"And before that?"

"The capital. Debt service." Kestrel kept his voice level, though the old arithmetic stirred in him like sediment: nine hundred forty pounds owed to the Company, inherited from a father who had died owing it, amortized across years of his life at an interest rate he had never been permitted to see written down. "You know my file."

"I've read your file. I read everyone's file; it's a habit of the trade." Halloway leaned back. Sunlight moved across his desk, catching the crystal stopper of a decanter, the brass of a small cannon cast as a paperweight — the empire's two arguments rendered in miniature. "Six years observing this coast. You'd have opinions, then. About the divers. About how they feel toward the Company."

"They dive," Kestrel said carefully. "They bring up shell. They're paid in scrip they spend at Company stores. Feelings don't enter into the transaction."

"That," said Halloway, "is the answer of a man who wants to keep his job. It's also wrong. Sit still, I haven't dismissed you."

He rose instead, crossing to the window, and stood looking down at the harbor with his hands clasped behind him, a silhouette against all that water.

"You've seen the fleet in the roads," he said. "Three ships of the line and their tenders. Officially, a show of force ahead of the punitive expedition — a reminder to certain parties across the gulf that the crown remembers insults. Unofficially—" he turned his head, and his profile was unreadable against the brightness, "—unofficially, the fleet sails in eleven days, and while it is here, this coast will be *orderly*. Do you understand me? Whatever else happens across the water, nothing happens here. No fires in the net stores. No boats gone missing in the night. No diver refuses a descent and has his refusal turn contagious."

"And the census?" Kestrel asked, though he already knew, in the way one knows a thing just before it is said aloud, the way one knows a wave has lifted the boat before the boat itself feels it.

"The census is the instrument." Halloway came back to the desk and opened the ledger again, but now he turned to the last section, the loyalty declarations, and began marking names with a pencil stub, quick little strokes like a man ticking cargo. "Your instructions asked each subject whether they held grievance against the annexation. Whether they mourned the old baronies. Whether they would shelter persons declared enemies of the crown. You wrote down their answers, Kestrel — every hesitation, every evasion, every silence that went on a breath too long. I know because I told you to record hesitations, and you did, because you are a meticulous man." The pencil paused. Ticked. Paused again. "What you may not have understood is that a census is a net. And a net does not care which fish it catches. It cares only that the mesh holds."

* * *

Halloway worked through the ledger for the better part of an hour, and Kestrel sat in the leather chair that swallowed him and watched the empire do its quiet arithmetic. There was no drama in it. That was the thing he would remember afterward, in the nights when memory ambushed him: there was no cruelty in the room at all, only procedure. A man with a pencil and a warm afternoon, sorting neighbors into columns.

Occasionally Halloway asked questions. "This woman, Sefra, boat-share holder, fourth row — she wept during her declaration?" "She asked to be excused until evening. She came back." "Mm. Note that. Grief that organizes itself is worse than grief that doesn't." Or: "Here's a man who praised the Company too much. Over-praise is fear wearing its good coat. Flag him." Kestrel found himself answering, found himself *useful*, and hated the usefulness the way a man hates his own hands when they have done a thing before his mind consented to it.

At length Halloway set the pencil down and rubbed his eyes with thumb and finger, and for a moment he looked merely tired, merely middle-aged, a man who had spent too many years indoors in a country built for open water.

"Forty-one names," he said. "Out of fourteen hundred. That's a reasonable harvest. Sympathizers, debt-desperate, the grieving, the ambitious. Every one of them a door." He looked up. "And now, Kestrel, the part where I make you an offer, and you pretend to consider it."

* * *

The offer was laid out on the desk between them like merchandise.

Debt forgiveness. Not reduction — forgiveness. Nine hundred forty pounds, struck through in a single administrative act, the whole long chain of his father's borrowing dissolved. In exchange: continued enumeration, expanded. Monthly reports instead of annual. Interviews conducted not once but repeatedly, so that changes in mood, in allegiance, in the small weather of the divers' quarters could be tracked like tides. He would be given a clerk's assistant, access to the manifest office, a salary adjustment. He would be, though Halloway never used the word, an officer of the Company's intelligence apparatus — its left hand, the one that did the work the right hand signed.

"You'll want to think about it," Halloway said. "Everyone wants to think about it, and thinking changes nothing, but courtesy costs me little." He poured two glasses of the decanter's contents and pushed one across. The spirit was amber and smelled of heat. "Consider it this way. You are already compromised, Kestrel. Your hand wrote those hesitations into the record. When Sefra's boat is seized — and it will be seized, she flagged herself the moment she wept in front of a crown official — your handwriting will be on the document that sent the assessors. You are in the machine whether you pull the lever or not. The only question the offer answers is whether you're paid."

Kestrel turned the glass on the desk's polished surface, watching the light bend through it. Through the window, far below, a line of divers was coming up the compound path from the shore, wet suits steaming in the afternoon, moving with the rolling gait of people whose bodies belonged more to water than land. Among them, he recognized — and was surprised he recognized — the slight figure of the woman from the eastern quarter, the one whose mother's village he had asked after against all sense. Villalobos. She carried a basket on her hip and walked, as she always walked, as though invisibility were a craft she had mastered and practiced daily.

He wondered, not for the first time, what her hesitation had meant — the half-second pause before she gave her mother's village, the pause he had recorded faithfully in his neat clerk's hand, item thirty-seven, column four.

"Suppose I refuse," he said.

Halloway shrugged, expansive, unbothered. "Then you go back to the survey office, and someone else does the work, and your debt continues compounding at eight percent, and in roughly nine years you will be free of it, assuming your health holds and the coast doesn't eat you first. I lose nothing. You lose nine years. The only person who benefits from your refusal is the next man I hire, and frankly he won't be half as careful." He drank, and set the glass down with a click like a full stop. "I don't threaten people, Kestrel. Threats create martyrs. I offer arithmetic."

Arithmetic. Kestrel looked at the ledger on the desk, forty-one doors marked in soft grey pencil, and thought of the faces behind them. The old man in the sail loft who had answered every question with a question. The girl who had recited her loyalty declaration in a schoolroom voice, word-perfect, terrified. The woman named Amara — no, not in the census, but present throughout it like a watermark: the deposed baron's daughter, branded enemy of the crown, whose name appeared in seven separate declarations as the thing respondents had been asked about and had conspicuously declined to discuss. Seven silences, entered faithfully into the record by his own pen. Seven doors.

He thought of his father, who had signed notes of debt in exactly this kind of room, in exactly this light, telling himself each signature was the last.

"When do I start," Kestrel said, and was not sure afterward whether it had been a question or a surrender.

* * *

He left the Director's office with his new commission in his breast pocket — a single folded sheet, stamped, heavier than the whole census had been — and descended through the building as the day tipped toward evening. The carpet-beating clerk was gone from the stairs. The lamps were being lit floor by floor, so that as Kestrel descended he passed from light into shadow and back into light, landing by landing, like a man surfacing and diving.

In the courtyard the compound bell rang the change of watch. Cook-smoke drifted from the kitchens, carrying cumin and char. Near the gate, a knot of servants had gathered around one of the older women, who was laughing at something, and the laughter spilled out across the stones, ordinary and warm, entirely unaware of the paper in Kestrel's pocket that would decide, over the coming months, which of these voices stayed and which were subtracted.

Complicity, he thought, does not arrive dressed in black. It arrives dressed as opportunity, wearing your own debts for a coat, and it holds the door for you politely, and it thanks you.

He stopped in the middle of the courtyard, in the smell of smoke and tide-flat, and took out the commission to look at it once more. The seal was red and perfect. His name was spelled correctly. Below it, in Halloway's round merchant's hand, the scope of his duties was summarized in three lines, and the third line ended with the phrase: *with particular attention to associations touching the household formerly styled Barony of the Eastern Shore.*

The household formerly styled. Amara. The name surfaced in him with a strange specificity — not the enemy of state from the proclamations, but the woman he had glimpsed twice at a distance on the seawall, tall and straight-backed, walking her crumbling waterfront property as though the tide might yet recognize her rank.

Eleven days until the fleet sailed. Forty-one doors. And somewhere in the eastern quarter, a woman who paused half a second too long before naming her dead mother's village.

Kestrel folded the commission away, and went to find his new assistant, and begin the work he had already begun.
