## 5. Names on a List

The monsoon season came late that year, and when it finally broke over the gulf it did not fall so much as lean, a grey pressure slanting off the water for three days without pause. The compound stones drank it and darkened. The drying racks sagged under wet nets. Everything smelled of salt gone sour, of oyster shells left too long in the sun before the rain found them, of the particular iron tang that rose from the harbor mud at low tide when the sky pressed down hard enough.

Petra had learned as a child that rain was a kind of mercy for the invisible. Rain gave everyone an excuse to keep their heads down. On clear days a supervisor might notice a girl standing too long at the net-house door, or mark the way her eyes moved across a room; but in rain, all eyes narrowed against the weather, and a body could pass unremarked from one end of the Meridian compound to the other carrying nothing more suspicious than a basket of shell.

She was carrying such a basket on the fourth morning of the rain, when the order came down that the upper records office wanted the season's shell-weight tallies carried over from the weighing shed. It was not her job. It was nobody's job in particular, which meant it would fall to whoever was nearest and least likely to complain, and Petra had spent nineteen years arranging herself to be precisely that person. She took the ledger rolls wrapped in oilcloth, tucked them under her arm where they would stay driest, and went out into the leaning grey.

The path between the weighing shed and the records office ran along the back of the Director's wing, past a covered colonnade where clerks crossed between buildings without wetting their boots. Petra knew the rhythm of that colonnade. Clerks moved in pulses, timed to the bells of the company clock — a rush at the quarter hour, emptiness after. If you walked in the empty quarter, you met no one, and no one saw a diver's daughter with ink-stained fingers passing close to windows she had no business looking into.

She did not intend to look. That was the thing she would return to later, turning it over like a stone in her pocket: she had not intended anything. She was simply moving through the rain with her head angled down against the wind, and one of the tall casements along the colonnade stood open a hand's width despite the weather — propped, perhaps, because the records office ran hot with lamps and bodies — and through that gap came the smell of lamp oil and the sound of two men talking, and her name.

Not *Petra*. Nobody in that building said *Petra*. Her name, spoken aloud, was a small flat coin she kept for herself and Amara. What came through the window was the other name, the owned one: *Villalobos girl, indenture number eleven-forty*.

*Eleven-forty.* She stopped. Not deliberately — her legs simply ended their motion the way a tide ends, without announcement, and then she was standing in the rain beneath the gap in the casement with the oilcloth bundle pressed to her chest, listening.

"—preliminary, mind you," said a voice she recognized after a moment as Mr. Corvane, Halloway's under-clerk, a thin man with a cough who signed documents with a flourish entirely disproportionate to his importance. "The director wants thirty names by the fleet's sailing, and I've got twenty-two solid. The rest are guesses."

"And this one?" A second voice, lower, unfamiliar — or familiar in a way she couldn't place until later, when it would matter. "You've flagged her twice."

"Villalobos. Diver stock, mother dead, no family on the rolls. She's been running messages for the office since she was twelve — reliable, never asks questions." A paper rustled. "And there's the other matter. The baron's daughter. The Azhar woman."

Petra's heart did not leap or pound. It did something worse: it went very still and very quiet, the way a diver goes still and quiet on the seafloor when a shadow passes overhead.

"She has access," Corvane said. "Years of access. Delivers rice to that crumbling mausoleum on the waterfront like it's charity day. The director thinks — and I concur — that a friendship cultivated over years is worth more than any informer we could buy. The natives trust their own. We only have to make sure our own knows which side of her bread is buttered."

"And does she?"

"She will. That's what the census is for. Kestrel's lists tell us who can be leaned on and how. Her mother's village —" the paper rustled again — "there's a debt recorded against it, pre-annexation, never discharged. Property held in common. When we call it due, our friend eleven-forty will find that gratitude flows most freely downhill."

There was laughter then, brief and dry as scale, and Petra made herself move. Not quickly — quickness drew the eye — but at the pace of a girl with a delivery and no interest in rain, past the casement, around the corner of the records office, up the steps, into the lamplit warmth where a junior clerk took the shell tallies from her without looking up and said, "Wait, there's a receipt," in the tone of a man addressing furniture.

She waited. She watched him scratch the receipt out with a wet nib. Her hands did not shake. Nineteen years of practice held her hands steady and her face smooth and her breathing even, and inside, somewhere below the ribs, something was coming apart with the slow, silent thoroughness of a hull seam opening under pressure.

They had written her down. Not as a suspect — that she could have borne, that she would even have understood, because suspicion was a tax everyone of her blood paid simply for existing on Company water. They had written her down as an instrument. A tool already sharpened, already tested, waiting in the shed to be picked up and used. *Reliable, never asks questions.* All those years of being unremarkable, of trading small kindnesses and keeping her eyes down and her name small — she had thought of it as survival, as craft, as hers. And all the while they had been reading it as exactly what it looked like from the outside: obedience. Trainability. A girl who could be pointed at her best friend and trusted to report back.

The clerk pushed the receipt across the counter. "Sign."

She signed. Eleven-forty. Her hand was steady.

* * *

The rain kept its lean through the afternoon, and Petra worked, because working was what her body did while the rest of her thought. She mended a trawl net whose knots she could tie blind, her fingers moving through the old grammar of hemp and twine while her mind turned the morning over and over.

Here was the arithmetic she could not escape. The census — that weary, meticulous man with his imperial seal and his questions — was not merely counting them. It was sorting them. Every answer given at the compound gate, every name and birth-village and loyalty affirmed under oath, had been fed into the mill at the Director's wing and ground out as lists: who could be leaned on, and how. Her mother's village. A debt recorded against land held in common. She hadn't known that debt existed; she doubted her mother had, by the end. But it sat in some ledger drawer like a loaded spring, and Halloway's hand rested on it, waiting for the moment release would be most useful.

They would call it due. They would come to her with the paper and the seal and the reasonable voice, and they would say: your mother's village, girl, your inheritance, your name — all of it can be freed, if you will only tell us what Amara Azhar says when the lamps are low. Who visits her. What she keeps. Whether she still holds her mother's pearl ledger.

And they were right that she would be believed. That was the cruelty of it — the efficiency. Years of deliveries, years of sitting in Amara's kitchen shelling beans while the rain came off the gulf, years of gossip traded like currency: none of it had been secret, none of it hidden, and so none of it could be denied. If Petra reported, every word would be credited. If she refused, she had nothing to offer in refusal's place but silence, and silence, in Halloway's arithmetic, was just a number awaiting correction.

She thought about refusing anyway. She examined it honestly, the way she examined a doubtful knot. Refuse, and lose the village, the boat rights, whatever fragment of her mother's world the debt had mortgaged. Refuse, and be marked unreliable — and unreliable girls on the pearl coast did not simply lose standing; they lost assignments, lost rations, found themselves transferred to the deep-water crews where the mortality tables spoke for themselves. Refuse, and gain nothing for Amara except the certainty that the company would find another pair of eyes, someone hungrier, someone already bought.

Refusing was pride. Pride was a luxury item, sold in shops she'd never entered.

And yet.

She tied off the last knot and sat back on her heels in the net-house doorway, watching the rain stitch itself into the harbor. *Yet* was Amara's laugh, low and rough, when Petra told the story about the supervisor who fell in the shell pit. *Yet* was the way Amara said her actual name — *Petra*, two syllables given back to her whole, unshortened, unnumbered — as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world, which made it the most extraordinary. *Yet* was a promise made years ago in peacetime, in sunlight, that felt now like it had been made yesterday in gunpowder: *I'll keep it safe. Whatever happens to the house, I'll keep the ledger safe.*

She had promised. She had promised without knowing that promises appreciate in value, that a vow worth nothing in calm water could weigh a thousand tons when the storm came in.

* * *

Evening brought the curfew bell early, rain muffling everything, and Petra walked the long way to the waterfront with her basket — genuinely full tonight, rice and dried fish bought with the small credits she hoarded, a luxury she could justify to herself and to anyone who asked. The old baron's house stood at the end of the quay like a tooth going soft in the gum, its shutters warped, its sea-wall cracked, the family crest above the door half-eaten by salt air. Soldiers had not come for it yet. But Petra had noticed, these two weeks, that men in plain clothes walked the street at intervals, unhurried, remembering.

Amara let her in through the kitchen as always. Inside smelled of woodsmoke and camphor and the particular dust of houses that are slowly being surrendered to the weather room by room. Amara herself was at the table with a candle stub and her father's atlas, tracing coastlines with one finger — she did this sometimes, mapping voyages she would never take, and Petra loved her a little more each time she saw it and said nothing.

"You're soaked," Amara said. "Sit. There's heat in the stove."

Petra sat. She unpacked the basket with hands that knew the motions. And she listened to Amara talk — about the fishmonger's new grandson, about the leak in the east roof, about the rumor that the Atlas fleet would sail within the month — and all the while the morning's words lay in her chest like swallowed stone. *A friendship cultivated over years is worth more than any informer we could buy.*

Tell her, said something in her. Tell her now.

But tell her what, exactly? That the census was a snare? Amara had guessed as much already; she was not a fool, and deposed barons' daughters learned early to read the intentions of empires. That Petra's own name sat on a list of instruments? That confession bought nothing unless Petra also knew the shape of what was coming, and she didn't — not yet, not precisely. Premature fear made people run, and running was what the soldiers on the street corner were hoping for. Running justified sealing a house. Running turned suspicion into evidence.

So instead she said, "The fleet sails soon, you think?" and Amara said, "Soon enough," and they talked about boats, and Petra laughed once at the right place, and hated herself for how easily the performance came — for how well those years of being unremarkable had trained her for exactly this, for wearing a smooth face over an open wound. The company hadn't built the tool. They'd only discovered it, fully formed, and reached for the handle.

When she rose to leave, Amara walked her to the kitchen door and caught her wrist briefly, the way she did, and said, "You're carrying something heavier than rice, little fish. One day you'll set it down and tell me."

"I will," Petra said, and meant it as a promise, and heard in her own voice the exact register of a woman reporting nothing at all.

* * *

The rain had thinned to a drizzle by the time she crossed back through the compound gate, and the lamps along the colonnade had come on, each one a blurred gold coin in the wet air. She was nearly to the net-house when she saw the figure waiting under the eaves of the records office — a tall man, hatless despite the weather, holding a leather satchel close against his coat. The census taker. Kestrel. He had been a fixture at the compound for weeks now, patient and precise, and she had catalogued him long ago as weather rather than threat: a condition to be endured.

But he was waiting. Under her eave. Watching her come.

"Miss Villalobos." His voice matched the rest of him: careful, tired, precise. "Forgive the hour. Director Halloway has approved an arrangement — certain indentures with legible hands are to assist me in collecting the remaining household forms. Yours was among the names proposed." He paused, and in the lamplight she saw something flicker behind his weariness, some hesitation she would think about for a long time afterward. "You would be compensated. In credits toward your indenture."

Toward my indenture. Toward eleven-forty. The company's oldest trick, dressed in its newest coat: pay a woman in the currency of her own cage.

"Forms," she said. Her voice was level. "I can read well enough."

"So I'm told." He adjusted the satchel. "We begin tomorrow, after the bell. There are seals on these documents you'll need to learn to recognize. Which are official, which are copies, which carry legal weight." He said it flatly, instructionally, a man reciting a syllabus — and yet the words *seals* and *legal weight* seemed to hang in the drizzle between them a moment longer than they needed to.

"Yes, sir," Petra said.

He nodded, turned, then stopped halfway, rain gathering on his shoulders, and looked back at her with an expression she couldn't name — something between apology and warning, delivered by a man too careful to give either.

"Good night, Miss Villalobos."

She watched him go down the colonnade, a dark shape against the gold-lit stones, and stood alone in the wet courtyard doing the terrible new arithmetic of her life. Tomorrow she would sit beside him and learn the seals. Tomorrow she would handle the forms that sorted her people into lists — the leanable, the buyable, the expendable. The company believed it was picking up its tool.

Her hands, steady all day, finally trembled — and she closed them, and held the trembling inside her fists, and began, in the dark, to think.
