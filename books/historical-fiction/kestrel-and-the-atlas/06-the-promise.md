## 6. The Promise

The rice had gone soft in its cloth wrapping, warmed by Petra's body through the whole walk from the compound kitchens to the waterfront, and she carried it against her ribs like something stolen — which, she supposed, it was. A double handful of grain, a twist of salt, half a dried mackerel that the cook would never miss because the cook was Old Sefi and Old Sefi counted everything twice but always rounded down when it came to Petra. Nine years of small kindnesses compounded like interest. You could build a life out of such arithmetic if you were careful.

The baron's house rose at the end of the shingle road where the town gave up pretending to be prosperous. It had been handsome once — everyone said so, though Petra had only ever seen it dying — with its shuttered galleries and salt-scabbed stucco the color of an old man's teeth. The annexation had taken the family's boats, their divers, their standing; what it could not take was the house, because the house sat on land the crown had never bothered to claim, being worthless except for the view of the harbor, and the harbor these days belonged entirely to the Meridian Trading Company. So Doña Isadora's daughters lived on in a house that was less a home than a held breath, watching the water their grandfather had ruled.

Petra knew the loose plank in the fence line the way she knew the scar on her own palm. She slipped through into the garden, where the frangipani had gone feral and sweet over everything, and found Amara where she always found her: on the back steps with a book, or pretending to read one, her eyes on the water.

"You're late," Amara said, not looking up.

"The tide was wrong for sneaking."

"The tide is always wrong for sneaking. You invent excuses the way other people invent saints." But she shifted on the step to make room, and Petra sat, and the rice changed hands, and the fish, and for a while neither of them spoke, because they were seventeen and fifteen respectively and had long ago used up the small talk of the shore. Below them the harbor lay flat as poured metal, and out past the mole the Atlas fleet rode at anchor — three company ships then, not yet the punitive armada they would become — their hulls dark against the last light.

"I hate them," Amara said suddenly, chin on her knees. "You can say it," Petra said. "Nobody hears you down here." "I hate them," she repeated, louder, testing the word in the open air like a diver testing a new weight belt. "I hate their flag on our customs house. I hate that my mother signs papers with her left hand so the ink won't smudge and still they call her disorganized, dishonest, *deposed*, like she's a piece of furniture that's been moved." She spat toward the water, a small precise gesture learned from the fishermen. "I hate that you work for them."

"I don't work for them," Petra said quietly. "I belong to them."

The distinction mattered. They both knew it mattered. Amara's hand found hers in the dark, quick and warm, and squeezed once, hard, an apology without language.

* * *

But that was last spring, and memory is a tide chart: it looks orderly laid out, and then it comes for you all at once.

Now, three years later, Petra stood in the same garden at the same hour and the frangipani smelled the same and none of it was the same at all. The census man had come and gone from the compound eleven days ago, and his questions had followed her like gulls follow a gutting boat. *Your mother's village.* Nobody asked about mothers' villages. Nobody needed to know where a dead indentured woman had been born unless they meant to count the people who remembered her, and counting was the first thing you did before you took things away.

She had come tonight without rice. She had come with nothing but herself and a question she couldn't stop turning over, the way you turn a shell to see if anything still lives inside it.

Amara was waiting on the steps. Older now — twenty, sharp-featured, her mother's jaw and her father's stubbornness — but sitting in exactly the same posture, chin on knees, eyes on the harbor. Except the harbor had changed too. The Atlas fleet was real now, not rumor: nine hulls at anchor, gun ports open, riding the anchorage like a sentence waiting to be read aloud.

"You've heard," Petra said.

"Everyone's heard. The fleet sails within the month, and before it sails, there will be seizures. Enemy property." Amara smiled without any joy in it. "That's me, apparently. That's my mother's house. That's—" she stopped.

"That's what?"

Amara looked at her then, really looked, and Petra saw that she'd been carrying this alone for days and had decided, on the steps, in the dark, to set it down between them. "The boats," she said. "The old fleet. My mother's pearl fleet, six hulls, seized at annexation under a provisional writ. Provisional means temporary. Temporary means nobody's bothered to make it permanent because nobody with a ledger ever looked twice at a few oyster boats." Her voice cracked, steadied. "Until now. Now someone is making a list of everything that was ever ours, and everything on the list becomes the company's, permanently, legally, with the fleet's departure as witness."

Petra sat down on the step beside her. The wood was warm from the day, or she imagined it was; her hands felt cold.

* * *

Four years earlier. Peacetime, though no one called it that then either; they called it *before*. The rains had come late that year and the harbor stank of low tide and possibility, and Doña Isadora had begun, very quietly, to die.

Petra was thirteen and already good at being unremarkable. She ran errands along the waterfront for the compound, which meant she also ran errands along the waterfront for anyone else who pressed a coin into her hand, and among those someones was the deposed baron's younger daughter, who paid in books and conversation and the dangerous luxury of being spoken to as though she had a mind worth addressing.

That afternoon, Amara met her behind the fish market, breathless, which was unlike her. Amara was never breathless. Amara walked through the world as though it ought to be honored to receive her.

"She's giving me the ledger," Amara said without preamble.

"What ledger?"

"The pearl ledger. Grandmother's, then Mother's. Every dive, every bed, every yield going back sixty years. Weights and pearls and beds and shares — who owns what, who worked which ground, what was paid, what was owed. It predates the annexation by thirty years." Amara's hands were doing something strange, folding and unfolding a scrap of paper. "It proves the fleet was never the crown's to give. It proves the boats belong to us — to the families who worked them under contract with my grandmother, half of whom are your neighbors, your aunts, the women who taught you to hold your breath."

"My mother," Petra said, before she could stop herself.

Amara went still. "Your mother co-owned the *Cormorant's Wake*. Yes."

A boat Petra had never seen, named in a document she had never read, owned jointly by a woman who had died owing the Meridian Trading Company four years of labor that passed, upon her death, to her daughter. That was how the arithmetic worked here. Debt flowed downhill like water.

"She wants me to keep it," Amara said. "Safely. Where the company will never find it. And she wants— she says I'm to have someone I trust. Someone outside the house, in case soldiers ever seal it. Someone who can move freely." She looked up, and her eyes were fierce and frightened and fifteen years old. "She says I should ask you."

The tide sucked at the pilings. Somewhere across the harbor a bell buoy clanged, patient and idiotic, keeping its own time.

"Why me?" Petra asked. "You have friends. Real ones. Girls whose mothers didn't drown in debt."

"Because you're careful," Amara said simply. "Because you know every loose plank and unlocked door between here and the compound, and you've never once been caught doing anything. Because when you give your word I think you mean it the way other people mean vows." She swallowed. "And because I trust you, Petra, and I don't have many people left I can say that about."

What Petra remembered most clearly afterward was not the promise itself but the moment just before it, when she understood exactly what was being asked and could still have refused. There is a door in every great mistake — a hinge-point, a breath — where the person you might have been stands on the far side looking at you. She stood on that threshold for the length of one wave.

"All right," she said. "Where does it go?"

* * *

They had buried the ledger that same week, in a waterproofed oilcloth inside a hollowed section of bamboo, beneath the floor of the drying shed behind Amara's house, under a patch of concrete Amara's cousin patched back so seamlessly even the cousin's own dog forgot what was underneath. Petra memorized the location the way divers memorize reef passages: not as a map but as a body of knowledge, turns and distances and landmarks written into muscle. Seven paces from the north wall. Beneath the crack shaped like a river delta. Two handspans deep.

For four years it lay there, and once a season Petra checked it — dry, safe, forgotten by everyone but two girls who never spoke of it directly, referring instead to *the well* or *grandmother's sewing*, code phrases that had begun as caution and become, over time, a kind of affection, a private country with its own language that only they inhabited.

Tonight, sitting on the steps with the fleet at anchor and Amara's voice still explaining what the census would mean, Petra understood with sudden terrible clarity that the private country had borders now, and those borders were drawn in whatever ink Halloway's clerks used, and the language they had spoken for years had just become the language of treason.

"They'll take the boats," Amara was saying. "Permanently. Unless someone can prove prior ownership before the fleet departs, at which point the imperial inspectors aboard are obligated to recognize legitimate claims — it's in their charter, it's the one decent clause in the entire colonial code — but proof takes time we don't have and documents we can't produce and—" She stopped. Turned. Looked at Petra with an expression that was almost apologetic. "I'm sorry. I shouldn't be telling you any of this. If they knew you knew—"

"If who knew?"

"You know who."

*The company,* Petra thought. *Meridian. Halloway. The men in the counting house who own my next eight years and possibly my lungs.*

"The ledger's safe," she said.

Amara stared at her. "You remember where."

"I remember everything about it. Seven paces, delta crack, two handspans. I could find it blind."

"Don't," Amara said sharply. "Don't go near it. Not now, with the census fresh and strangers writing down names. Promise me. If they watch you and find you digging up my mother's floor—"

"It's your mother's *shed*."

"Petra." Amara reached out and took her by both shoulders, and her grip was stronger than her slight frame suggested, and her eyes were bright with something between fury and terror. "Promise me you'll stay away from it. Please. Whatever happens with this list, this census, this fleet — you stay invisible. You stay *safe*. That was always part of it. Keeping it meant keeping yourself."

And there it was, the old promise, resurfacing like something drowned coming up gasping: *keep it safe*, four words that had seemed simple at thirteen and now contained multitudes, contained ledgers and boats and a fleet of warships and a census taker with careful eyes and a director named Halloway whose arithmetic Petra was only beginning to learn.

*Keep it safe.* Even — she understood now, with the cold settling into her stomach — if keeping it meant lying to the people who owned you. Even if it meant, eventually, choosing.

"I promise," Petra said.

* * *

Walking back along the shingle road in the dark, Petra counted her assets the way Old Sefi counted stock: what she had, what she owed, what could be lost. She had a strong back, a good memory, and a face no one remembered. She had four years of seasonal visits to a hollowed bamboo cache, undetected. She had a promise made in peacetime to a girl who was now, technically, an enemy of the crown.

Against that: eight years of indenture. A bed in a loft she could be turned out of. Food, shelter, the small kindnesses she'd spent nearly a decade banking. Everything, in short, that survival required — all of it conditional, all of it held at the pleasure of the Meridian Trading Company, all of it forfeit the moment she chose a dead woman's ledger over her living masters.

She stopped at the top of the compound road. Behind her, the harbor: nine ships at anchor, lanterns strung along their decks like carnivorous flowers, beautiful the way traps are beautiful. Ahead, the compound walls, the loft, the bell that would ring curfew within the quarter hour. Between the two, a narrow strip of dark road where a person could stand, briefly, belonging to neither place.

She had always believed invisibility was safety. That was the whole doctrine of her life: be small, be useful, be forgettable, and the machinery of the world will pass you by. But the census taker had asked about her mother's village, and somewhere in the company's offices her name sat on a list she hadn't seen yet, and the machinery, it turned out, did not pass anyone by. It only decided, for its own reasons, whom to swallow slowly and whom to swallow quickly.

If she was going to be recorded regardless — catalogued, assigned, put to use — then let the record show something true. Let it show a woman who kept her promises.

The curfew bell began to ring. Petra Villalobos pulled her shawl tighter, walked through the compound gates, and answered her name at evening roll in a clear steady voice, and no one marked anything unusual in her face at all.

But three days later, a message reached her folded inside a bolt of mending canvas: *Halloway wants divers' names. You're on the list. Not the suspect list — the other one.*

She read it twice, burned it in the cookfire, and stood a long time watching the flames, understanding at last what her promise was going to cost.
