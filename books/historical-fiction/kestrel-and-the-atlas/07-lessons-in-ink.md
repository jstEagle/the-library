## 7. Lessons in Ink

The census office smelled of lamp oil and old glue, and by the third morning Elias Kestrel had stopped noticing either.

He had requisitioned a room at the back of the Meridian compound's counting house — a room with one salt-fogged window, two tables, and a population of spiders who paid no taxes and therefore fell outside his mandate. Stacked along the walls were the forms: thick folios of the census proper, the household registers, the loyalty declarations that had begun arriving from the outlying villages wrapped in oilcloth and reeking of fish smoke. Each page bore the imperial seal in the upper corner, pressed in violet ink that faded to the color of bruised plums where the humid air got at it. Each page required a reader. That was the problem. That was always the problem on the colonial coast: too much paper, too few literate hands, and none of them his.

Director Halloway had solved it for him with a single sentence delivered over breakfast coffee thin enough to read through. *Take one of the divers' children. The Villalobos girl. She's quick, and she's quiet, and nobody watches her.*

Nobody watches her. Kestrel had turned the phrase over more than once since. It was meant as praise. It sounded like an instruction.

So at seven each morning, when the tide was low and the compound's labor rolls were being called, Petra Villalobos came to him. She arrived without ceremony — no knock, no greeting, just the door opening and a slight young woman with rope-scarred palms and eyes the flat gray-green of storm water stepping through it as though she had been in the room all along and he had only now thought to see her. She hung her outer shirt on the nail by the door, rolled her sleeves to the elbow, and waited to be told what was wanted.

He was going to teach her what was wanted. There lay the difficulty he had not anticipated.

* * *

"You know your letters," he said on the first morning. It was not quite a question. He had watched her, during the household interviews, tracking his pen across the register with an attention that could not have been idle.

"Enough." She stood with her hands folded, a diver's stillness in her, the kind that comes from learning to spend breath like coin. "The sisters at the mission school kept me a year before the company called me back. I can read the psalms. I can write my name."

"The psalms won't help you here." He drew a form from the stack and laid it flat between them, turning it to face her. "This is Schedule C. Occupations and vessel holdings. You'll be helping me complete them in the fishing camps north of the point — recording names, ages, what boats a household owns or shares or lost. The men will talk to you more readily than to me. To me they say what they think the crown wants heard. To you they'll say what's true, because you're one of them and they've forgotten you're also the company's."

Something crossed her face at that — quickly, like a fish's shadow under a boat. "And if what's true is dangerous?"

"Then you write it down anyway." He said it flatly, because it was true, and because saying it plainly was the only honesty his position allowed him. "What gets done afterward is not your doing. Write only what is asked. Nothing more."

"And nothing less?" Her gaze came up and held his, and for a moment he had the uncomfortable sensation of being counted himself — weighed on some private scale whose markings he could not read. "Nothing less is harder, Mr. Kestrel. Nothing less is most of the work."

He looked at her then, really looked, the way he looked at a document whose second page had been sewn in crooked. Nineteen years old, perhaps twenty. Indentured since childhood, which meant she had been property longer than she had been a person in any legal sense, and she stood in front of him correcting his understanding of her own situation as calmly as a clerk amending an entry.

"You'll do," he said.

She almost smiled. Almost. On her face it appeared mostly as a change in the weather around her eyes.

* * *

The lessons settled into a rhythm over the following days. In the mornings, forms; after the midday meal, the harder work of vocabulary. The census demanded its own language — *head of household, chattel interest, severalty, abatement* — words that had been built in marble offices a hemisphere away and arrived on the coast smelling of nothing, meaning everything. He wrote lists of them for her in his small, exact hand, and she copied them onto scraps of sailcloth with a carpenter's pencil she produced from somewhere about her person as if by right.

Her handwriting startled him. He had expected the round, obedient script of the mission schools. What she produced instead was narrow and slanted, economical, every stroke doing double duty — the writing of someone who had very little ink in her life and had learned not to waste a drop of it. When he remarked on it, she shrugged.

"When you write something," she said, "you should mean it. Extra flourishes are lies about how much you cared."

"That is either very wise or very bitter."

"Both usually."

He found, to his discomfort, that the hours passed quickly in her company. This alarmed him in a way he could not initially name, and so he named it precisely, because precision was the only prayer he knew. It alarmed him because she was, by Halloway's own design, a tool — and tools were meant to be used and put away. But she did not behave like a tool. She behaved like a colleague who happened to be unpaid, unfree, and considerably better at hiding her opinions than he was. She caught his errors. Twice she corrected his pronunciation of divers' family names before he'd ever spoken them aloud, quietly, without looking up from her copying, and both times she was right.

On the fifth morning she paused over the vocabulary list, pencil hovering.

"This word." She turned the list toward him. *Sequestration.* "It means taking things."

"It means taking things lawfully. Under authority."

"Is there a difference?"

"There is a very great difference," Kestrel said, "to the people who write the laws."

She held his gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed, then nodded slowly, as though he had confirmed something rather than dodged. She wrote the word on her cloth scrap. Beneath it, unasked, she wrote the word *theft*, small, in parentheses, and showed him the finished list with a perfectly neutral face.

He should have objected. He said nothing. It was becoming, he noted with unease, a habit.

* * *

It was on the eighth day that he caught her with the seals.

The afternoon had gone heavy and windless, the kind of heat that made the ink dry too fast in the well and the flies stupid against the window glass. Kestrel had been summoned briefly to the counting house to answer a question about the northern village tallies, and he was gone perhaps ten minutes. Ten minutes. Long enough for tea to cool, long enough for a spider to rebuild half a web. Not, in any reasonable accounting, long enough for anything else.

When he returned, Petra sat exactly where he had left her, copying boat names from his master list into Schedule C, her posture unchanged, her pencil moving. Everything was in order. Everything was precisely, immaculately in order — and that was itself wrong, because the light through the salt-fogged window had shifted while he was gone, and the stack of completed schedules beside her had been squared to its edges with a care beyond even hers. Things get disturbed when people leave a room. Hers had been restored.

He crossed to his own table and took up the wax impression plate he used for authenticating the schedules — a brass die, the imperial seal, the one instrument in the room that mattered more than all the paper combined. It sat exactly where he had left it. Its inked surface bore a faint ring of disturbance in the dried residue, a circle within a circle, as though something circular had rested against it recently. A jar lid, perhaps. The bottom of a teacup.

Or a sheet of paper, pressed flat and careful, lifted again with the same patience.

Kestrel set down the die. He did not look at Petra. He poured the cold tea into the potted fern that Halloway's clerk had left as an offering and that had died immediately, and he considered what he knew.

She was intelligent. She was desperate — everyone indentured on this coast was desperate, desperation being the company's principal product. And somewhere in her life there existed a reason, a person or a debt or a grave, for which she would risk pressing paper against a seal she had no right to touch. He found that he could imagine the shape of it easily. That was the part that unsettled him: not the suspicion, but how little imagination it required. How naturally he could supply the motive from the inventory of his own heart.

"Villalobos." His voice came out even. Teaching-voice, he realized. He had not intended that.

"Mr. Kestrel."

"Come here a moment." He picked up the brass die and held it to the window light, turning it slowly, ostensibly inspecting the engraving. "Do you know why the seal looks the way it does? Most people think it's decoration."

She came and stood beside him, at a respectful distance, and looked where he pointed. "No."

"The anchor and the crown, entwined." He rotated the die a quarter turn. "Anchor above, crown below. Deliberate. It means the law holds where the navy holds, and nowhere else. Out here, past the harbor chain—" he tapped the fine border of waves engraved around the rim— "the seal isn't a promise. It's a threat wearing a promise's clothes. Anyone who understands that understands the empire better than most of its magistrates."

She studied it. Her face did the thing it did — went smooth, went still, went unreadable as a closed ledger. But her eyes stayed on the die one heartbeat past curiosity's natural span, and in that heartbeat he saw what she was doing: not admiring it. Memorizing it. Counting the anchor's flukes, the turns of the border, the way the violet ink pooled in the engraving's valleys.

A moment too long. A moment precisely calibrated to be deniable, and failing at it only because he had been watching for exactly such a moment, because he had spent eleven years as the empire's pen and had learned that the most dangerous people in any colony were the ones who loved documents — who understood that paper, not powder, decided who owned what and who belonged where and who would be free when the ships sailed.

She knew what the seal meant. She wanted to make one.

The knowledge sat in Kestrel's chest like a swallowed stone. He could report it. A single sentence in his evening summary — *the assistant shows irregular interest in instruments of authentication* — and she would vanish into the company's machinery, quietly, the way she did everything. Or he could say nothing, and carry the knowing, and see where it led. Two careful people in a hot room full of paper, each deciding how much the other one knew.

He set the die down on the table with a soft click.

"The ink," he said, picking up the bottle as though the whole exercise had been about ink all along, "is imported. Violet forty-two, from a chandler in the capital. The company buys it by the crate. If you ever spill any—" he set it beside her elbow, deliberately close— "use the sand, not your sleeve. It stains."

"I'll remember," Petra said.

Her voice was level. Her hands, when she took up her pencil again, were steady. But she pulled the ink bottle a half-inch further from her papers than efficiency required, and he saw that she understood he had seen, and that she understood he had said nothing, and that neither of them would ever speak of it — because speaking would require admitting, and admitting would require consequences, and they had both, evidently, concluded in the same silent instant that consequences served neither of them yet.

They worked until the light failed. She copied boat names; he annotated village tallies; the flies gave up; the spiders resumed their tenancy. It was, he reflected, the most honest conversation he had conducted in eleven years of imperial service, and not one word of it had been spoken aloud.

At the door, hanging her shirt back on, pausing with the fabric half over her shoulders, she said: "The psalms were good practice. But thank you for these." A small gesture at the stacked forms, the seals, the whole apparatus of the state. "A person should know the shape of the cage. It helps with the bars."

Then she was gone, footsteps fading down the corridor toward the divers' quarters, and Kestrel sat alone with the lamp and the violet ink and the brass die catching the flame.

He told himself he had said nothing because reporting her would compromise his own position — a census taker who couldn't control his assistant, a man already deep in Director Halloway's confidence, already carrying secrets home in his breast pocket like contraband. Prudence. Self-preservation. The arithmetic of survival. All true, as far as it went.

But when he opened his ledger that night to record the day's progress, his pen hovered over the page far longer than the lamplight warranted, because beneath the arithmetic ran another current, colder and faster: the memory of her eyes on the seal, patient and hungry, memorizing the shape of power. Somewhere on this coast, Kestrel thought, capping the ink, there is a piece of paper waiting for that woman, and when she finds it, no seal on earth will hold her — unless someone teaches her first how the seals are broken.

He wrote: *Progress satisfactory. Assistant competent.* Then, after a moment, in the margin, in letters so small he could pretend later they were not there: *and watching.*

Across the compound, in the dark of the divers' quarters, a scrap of sailcloth lay hidden in a seam, covered edge to edge in narrow slanted script — boat names, yes, and vocabulary words, yes, but along the bottom margin, drawn four times over in pencil until the lines were sure: the anchor and the crown, entwined, with every turn of the bordering waves counted and correct.

And below it, in parentheses, one small word: *soon.*
