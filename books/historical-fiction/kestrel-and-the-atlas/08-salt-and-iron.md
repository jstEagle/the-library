## 8. Salt and Iron

The tide went out before dawn, and with it went the smell of the compound — the tallow and ink and boiled linen of the Meridian buildings — leaving behind the smell of everything real: mud and salt and rotting weed, the great wet lung of the flats breathing itself empty. Petra crossed the shallows with her hem kilted to her knees and the levy notice folded flat against her ribs, where it rode like a coal.

She had taken it from the scribes' room an hour ago, while the clerk who was supposed to be guarding it dozed over his tea. That had not been difficult. What had been difficult was standing there long enough to copy nothing at all, because copying nothing was the discipline now. She read the notice twice through — *by order of the Director, all vessels without registered colonial title to be surrendered at the company wharf by the first of the month* — and she put it back exactly as it had lain, squared to the desk's edge, and she walked out with her hands empty and her heart hammering like a diver's on ascent.

The boats were already being gathered when she reached the diving beach. Six of them, hauled above the tide line in the ugly way of seized things, their hulls chalked with numbers that meant nothing and would come to mean everything: inventory marks, lot numbers, the arithmetic of theft done neatly in white. The divers stood apart from them in a loose crescent, the way men and women stand around a grave before anyone has begun to dig.

Petra counted them without seeming to count. Old Sef, who had dived forty seasons and whose ears no longer worked above water but heard everything below it. The Ferreira sisters, Marta and Ines, who dove paired breaths and could stay down longer than any two bodies had a right to. Young Tomas, who was seventeen and angry the way only the young can afford to be, with his whole face. And others, eleven in all, drawn in by the chalked hulls the way gulls are drawn by offal.

"That's my father's boat," Tomas said, to no one, to everyone. "That's got my father's name carved in the stern."

"It's got a number on it now," said Sef, mildly, which in Sef's mouth was a warning.

"Let them number me, then."

"Boy." Sef turned his ruined ears toward the water as if the sea might advise him. "When they take a boat they take a thing. When you give them your temper, they keep that too."

Petra let the conversation wash past her the way she had learned to let everything wash past her, standing at the crescent's edge with her basket of mending — sailcloth patches, twine, a palmful of wax — the props of a woman who belonged here and wanted nothing. She had been doing this work for Halloway for three weeks now, and she had learned its true shape: not spying, which at least would have been dramatic, but listening. Standing in rooms and on beaches with her eyes down and her ears open, gathering the small combustible facts of people's lives and carrying them to the compound like a child carrying embers in a apron, praying the whole way that she would not burn anyone.

*Report on sentiment among the divers*, Halloway's instruction had read, in his tidy accountant's hand. *Names optional. Attitudes essential.* Names optional. She understood the mercy in that phrasing and did not trust it. A man like Halloway did not leave doors open; he left bait.

"They say the Atlas fleet sails within the fortnight," Marta Ferreira was saying now, low, to her sister. "And every hull without paper goes with it, or gets burned on the ways. They'll not leave seaworthy boats for us to think with."

"Paper." Ines spat into the sand. "My grandmother fished these waters before there was a colony. Before there was a flag. What paper does the sea want?"

"The sea wants nothing," said a voice behind them all, dry as barnacles. "It's the men on the wharf who want everything."

He came down the shingle with his boots off and carried over one shoulder, trousers rolled to the knee, an old man named Joaquim whom Petra had known all her life without ever once having spoken to him alone. He looked at the chalked hulls for a long time. Then he looked at the divers. Then, last and longest, he looked at Petra — a half-second too long, the look of a man filing something away — and she felt it land between her shoulder blades like a thrown stone she could not turn to find.

"There are boats," Joaquim said, "that were never entered in any company ledger. Boats bought before annexation, paid in pearl weight, witnessed proper. There are papers in this town older than the company's claim, if anyone still keeps them." He said it lightly, the way a man talks about weather or fish prices. But every diver in the crescent had gone very still, and Petra's blood had gone cold, because she knew precisely which papers he meant, and where they were hidden, and whose hands had held them last.

"You talk like a man with papers," Sef observed.

"I talk like an old man," Joaquim said. "Old men remember things. It's all we're good for." He sat down on an upturned skiff and began working his feet back into his boots, and the meeting — if it had been a meeting; it had been nothing, it had been a morning on a beach — dissolved the way foam dissolves.

* * *

The trouble with double living, Petra thought that afternoon, hauling her basket up through the fish market, is that it is not one life made heavier but two lives made thinner, until you can see daylight through yourself.

Three weeks she had been walking this knife-edge, and she had learned its geography intimately. On the compound side: the ledgers she kept, the forms she helped Kestrel collect, the careful nothing she reported. She had become, to her own astonishment, a master of the useless fact. *Diver Marta Ferreira complains of the levy price. Diver Tomas, son of Rui, speaks hotly; the elders restrain him. Sentiment generally: resentful but passive. No organization observed.* All of it true. None of it dangerous. She fed Halloway a diet of truths so thin they starved on the page.

On the beach side: the other labor, which no one had assigned her and which exhausted her worse. Because resentment does not stay passive on its own; resentment needs tending in both directions, and Petra spent herself keeping it banked — catching Tomas's elbow before he swung at a soldier, laughing at the right moment, spreading the small patient gospel of *wait, wait, the fleet sails soon, anything done now is done alone* — while all the time the thing she was actually waiting for grew slowly in the loft above her sleeping room, in the form of a forged deed and a wounded woman's careful handwriting.

She passed the stalls where the morning's catch lay silvering in the sun, and old Nena Ochoa pressed a smoked fish into her basket without a word, because Nena had been pressing fish into Villalobos baskets since before the debt was a debt, and kindness between the indentured ran on rails laid long ago and needed no station stops. Petra took it the way she took all such kindnesses now — with a gratitude that had teeth in it, because every gift reminded her that she was lying to the givers. Not about what she did. About what she was. They thought her still invisible, still unremarkable, still the quiet Villalobos girl who mended nets and asked nothing. Only Amara knew different, and Amara was shut in a loft room with a stitched shoulder, reading her mother's pearl ledger by candle stub, and could tell no one either.

At the far end of the market, near the customs house, she saw the census taker.

Kestrel stood at the top of the customs steps with his ledger under his arm, watching the harbor the way he watched everything — as though the world were a document he was required to transcribe accurately and feared getting wrong. Even at this distance she could read his weariness in the set of his shoulders. He had not looked at her since the lesson in ink, since the moment she had lingered too long over the official seal and he had seen her lingering and said nothing, and that silence had become a third country between them, neither compound nor beach, where she suspected they both now lived more than anywhere else.

He raised one hand — not quite a wave, an acknowledgment, the smallest possible coin — and she returned it the same way, and they went on being strangers, expertly.

*Names optional*, she thought. *Attitudes essential*. And what attitude would Halloway read, she wondered, if someone reported that the census taker and the informant had begun to greet each other like conspirators? She put the thought away carefully, the way she put everything away now, in the deep hold where she kept the things that could hang her.

* * *

Evening brought the summons, and the summons brought the fear.

Not Halloway this time. Worse: a junior clerk named Pell, pink and apologetic, appearing at the net-mending shed with the information that the Director wished the informant's report delivered tonight rather than Thursday, as he wished to review sentiment before the fleet's provisioning orders were finalized. Tonight. Now. Petra wiped her hands on her apron, thanked Pell with the correct dull courtesy, and walked to the compound offices with her mind moving faster than her feet.

What did she have? Nothing. Three weeks of nothing, and now the nothing was due, and Halloway was a man who could weigh emptiness in his hand and know it for what it was.

But Joaquim. Joaquim and his speech about papers older than the company's claim. There was a fact with meat on it. She could report that — true, alarming, and aimed, if she aimed it right, at a dead end. Let Halloway chase the ghost of pre-annexation documents through forty years of drowned archives. Let him look everywhere except the one waterfront house where the papers actually lived, or the one loft where they lived now.

The trouble, she realized as she climbed the compound stairs, is that even a well-aimed truth is a weapon handed to a man who will use it however he likes. She was feeding the fire to keep it from spreading, and some nights, scrubbing the compound floors after delivering her reports, she could not tell anymore whether she was the cook or the fuel.

Halloway received her in his office, which smelled of camphor and cold tea, beneath the great framed chart of the gulf with its red lines showing the fleet's intended course — a route drawn not around dangers but through them, through the fishing grounds, across the oyster beds, the empire's pen strokes laid over living water like a hand pressing down.

"Informant," he said, without looking up. He never used her name. She had decided early that this was deliberate — that names created obligations, and Halloway dealt only in instruments. "Your report."

She gave it. Diver resentments, rising with the levy notices. The Ferreira sisters' talk of the fleet. Tomas's temper, restrained by elders. And then, folding her hands in her lap like a woman reporting laundry:

"One matter more. An old diver, Joaquim, spoke on the beach today of vessels never entered in company ledgers. Boats purchased before annexation, he said, paid in pearl weight. He spoke of papers — deeds predating the company's claim — and said such papers exist in this town, if anyone still keeps them."

For the first time Halloway looked up. His eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who has spent his life counting other people's losses and found the work satisfying. "Named?"

"He named no one, sir. He spoke in generalities. Old men talk."

"Old men," Halloway said, "remember." He drew a fresh sheet toward himself, and she watched his pen move — quick, economical, the pen of a man who had reduced a coastline to columns — and she understood with a lurch that she had just sent him hunting, and that hunts do not confine themselves to the game you intend. He would pull records. He would question clerks. Somewhere in forty years of drowned paper was a trail, and trails ran through the baron's house, and the baron's house had been sealed, and sealed houses got searched—

*Amara*, she thought, with a clarity like cold water. *Move the ledger.*

"Efficient," Halloway was saying, in the tone of a compliment. "This is useful. You've a talent for noticing, girl. Notice that I reward usefulness." He slid something across the desk: not money, which would have insulted them both, but a chit — a reduction of debt, entered against her account, three months of her life signed away and then forgiven in the same stroke. Complicity arriving dressed again in its fine clothes. "Continue."

She took the chit. She thanked him. She walked out through the corridors smelling of ink and lamp oil, past the clerks bent over their desks like a field of grey flowers, and down the stairs, and out into the night, where the wind had come around and carried the smell of the open gulf all the way up the streets — brine and distance and somewhere, faint under everything, tar heating on the ways where the Atlas fleet lay growing fat on confiscated tonnage.

Three months of her life, forgiven. As if her life were a debt that could be adjusted. As if she had not just understood, standing in that office, exactly how much of it was already owed elsewhere.

She walked fast, head down, invisible, past the customs house dark now, past the fish market shuttered and stinking sweetly, up the outside stair to her loft with the practiced ease of a woman who had climbed it ten thousand times and been seen doing it none. Inside, she lit no lamp. In the dark she listened — the sea, the compound bell marking the half hour, Amara's breathing, shallow and even, from behind the curtain of the alcove where she slept sitting up, always, still, the way the wounded sleep.

Petra crossed to the floorboard beneath the loom where the ledger and the unfinished deed lay wrapped in oilcloth, and knelt, and put her hand flat on the boards, and felt — not heard, felt, through her palm, through the grain of the wood — that the wrapping was wrong. Too light. She pried the board up with her nails.

The oilcloth lay open. The ledger was there. The forged deed was there.

And on top of the deed, folded once, in a hand she did not know — small, precise, clerk-trained — was a single sheet of paper, blank on its face.

She turned it over. On the reverse, in pencil, four words:

*I know what you're doing.*

From the alcove, in the dark, Amara's voice, very quiet: "Petra. Someone's been here."
