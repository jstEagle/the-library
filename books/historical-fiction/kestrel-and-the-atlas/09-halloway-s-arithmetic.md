## 9. Halloway's Arithmetic

The Director's office smelled of cedar and camphor, the way a coffin maker's shop might smell if the coffin maker were proud of his work.

Elias Kestrel stood at the threshold with his census ledger wrapped in oilcloth against the morning damp, waiting to be noticed. He had learned long ago that powerful men did not like to be hurried, and that the appearance of patience was often mistaken for the possession of it. So he waited while Halloway finished whatever he was writing, the nib of his pen moving in small, contented scratches, sunlight from the harbor-facing window laying a bright bar across the desk — across, Kestrel observed, a spread of papers quite unlike anything a director of trade had any honest reason to be reading.

"You found the weather on the north shore," Halloway said without looking up. It was not a question. He had a habit of opening conversations in the middle, as though Kestrel's last three days were chapters he had already skimmed.

"Damp," Kestrel said. "The divers' quarters are damp. Half the children cough through the winter."

"Useful." Halloway signed something with a flourish and set down his pen. He was a compact man, silvering at the temples, with the pleasant square face of a butcher or a banker — professions, Kestrel thought, that differed mainly in what they counted. "Sit down, Kestrel. Let us look at your work."

Kestrel sat. The chair was lower than Halloway's, which he understood to be deliberate, and accepted it the way he accepted most indignities: as a cost of doing business, entered in a column somewhere in the back pages of himself.

He unwrapped the oilcloth and passed the ledger across. It was the third of five — he had two more districts to complete before the census could be bound and sealed — but Halloway turned its pages with the proprietary ease of a man who considered everything on his desk already his.

"Mm," Halloway said. "Mm." A pause at one entry. "You've recorded birth villages for the diving families."

"The form requires it."

"The form requires it," Halloway agreed pleasantly, "and yet most clerks would have written *coastal* and moved on. You've got actual villages. Al-Madfan. Qarath. That drowned place — what is it — Sahr el-Bahar." He pronounced the names carefully, wrongly, each vowel flattened under some internal weight of disinterest. "You have an ear, Kestrel. That's why I asked for you."

"You asked for me because the Colonial Office sent me."

"I requested that they send you specifically. There is a difference, and it matters more than you think." Halloway closed the ledger and laid both hands flat upon it, a gesture Kestrel would come to know well in the following weeks — the gesture of a man blessing something he intended to spend. "Your predecessor compiled lists of names and occupations. Inventory, nothing more. You've compiled *connections*. Who owes whom. Who shares a hearth. Who has a boat with another family's name on the deed." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which made it worse. "Do you know what a connection is worth, Elias? May I call you Elias?"

"You may," said Kestrel, who had not given permission so much as declined to fight about it.

"A name is worth a levy. A connection is worth a confiscation." Halloway rose and went to the tall cabinet behind him — iron-banded, locked, the kind of furniture that existed in every colonial office in the empire, always slightly too large for the room, as though bureaucracy itself required ballast. From a drawer lined with green felt he lifted a folio, buff-colored, stamped with the Meridian crest and the imperial double-anchor. He carried it back to the desk like a man bringing out photographs of his children.

"Now," Halloway said, settling again into his chair. "Let me show you something beautiful."

* * *

The folio contained the inventory of the Hadid family's holdings, seized eleven days earlier under provisions of the Enemy Property Act, Schedule Four.

Kestrel read it because he was expected to read it, and because some part of him — the part that had spent twenty years making himself useful to institutions and calling it a career — believed, still believed, wanted desperately to believe, that there would be something in the paperwork that made it right.

There was paperwork. There was always paperwork. That was the first thing conscience learned about empire: it never asked you to do anything without first showing you a form.

*One dhow, *Bahri*, 34 feet, sound keel, appraised at 210 pesos.* One dhow, half-share, held jointly with the Nasser family of Qarath village. Nets, eleven. Copper diving weights, four sets. Pearl tools, assorted. One house, stone, two rooms, waterfront access. Household goods as listed: three beds, one stove, one chest containing linens, one chest containing — and here the clerk's hand grew smaller, more careful — *papers, family records, one book of accounts.*

"One book of accounts," Kestrel read aloud.

"Ledgers," Halloway said warmly. "Everyone keeps them. Fishermen, pearl merchants, shopkeepers. Little books of who owes what to whom." He tapped the line with one blunt finger. "Under Schedule Four, any record evidencing ownership claims predating annexation is *contraband*. Did you know that? Most people don't. They think we take boats. We take the *proof* of boats. Much more efficient. A boat floats away; a claim, once burned, stays burned."

"And this justified the seizure how?" Kestrel kept his voice level, professional, the voice of a man discussing rainfall.

Halloway flipped back several pages in the folio, to a report Kestrel recognized by its format, because he had invented the format himself three years ago, when he was a junior census clerk in the southern provinces and had thought, naively, that better forms would mean better government. *Sympathies and Associations, District Survey.* Columns: Name. Village. Kin abroad? Debt-holder? Sentiment (estimated).

"This," Halloway said. "Your format, incidentally. I told them you'd be perfect for this."

The names ran down the page in his own careful hand — no, not his hand; the format was his, the entries were another clerk's, gathered from informants whose names appeared nowhere. But three lines were marked in blue pencil, and those three lines traced a web: Hadid, brother-in-law to Nasser; Nasser, debt-holder to two other diving families; a cousin in the northern ports described as *agitation-prone*. At the bottom, in Halloway's own square script, a summary: *Household assessed as disaffected. Assets vulnerable to enemy influence. Recommend seizure prior to fleet departure.*

"You see the arithmetic," Halloway said, leaning back, gesturing at the whole spread of it — report, recommendation, warrant, inventory — like a teacher unveiling a solved equation. "One informant's estimate of sentiment, properly documented, converts directly into two hundred and ten pesos of company asset, plus a house we can quarter garrison troops in, plus the suppression of a claim that might otherwise have surfaced in ten years and cost us triple in litigation. And the beauty of it, Elias — the real elegance—" he tapped the informant column, blank, "—is that none of it can be traced. Not to the source. Not even to us, really. A man cannot sue a whisper."

Kestrel looked at the inventory a moment longer. Three beds. He did not know why three beds was the detail that caught — not the dhow, not the tools, not the *papers, family records* that would feed Halloway's stove — but it was. Somewhere in the city this morning there were children sleeping on the floor of someone else's house, and their father had been assessed as *disaffected* by a stranger who had perhaps never learned to pronounce his name.

"What happens to the family?" he asked.

"Relocated." Halloway said the word the way other men said *recycled*. "Labor allocation. The Gulf settlements need net-menders." He closed the folio and regarded Kestrel with frank, friendly appraisal. "I can see you're troubled. Good. Troubled men are careful men, and careful men make accurate reports. I don't want zealots, Kestrel. Zealots embellish. I want exactly what happened, written down plainly. Can you do that?"

It was, Kestrel would think later, the strangest feature of his recruitment into the company's intelligence apparatus: that they never lied to him. They simply showed him the machine with its casing off, all the gears visible and turning, and trusted that a man with debts and no alternatives would look at the gears and see wages instead of teeth.

"Yes," he said.

"Splendid." Halloway slid the census ledger back across the desk. "Finish the north shore districts within the fortnight. Flag any household with pre-annexation property interests — boats especially, deeds, partnerships, anything co-owned across families. The fleet sails in six weeks and the Directorship wants the schedule of seizures settled before departure. Oh —" he added, as Kestrel reached the door, "— there's a diver girl in your district. Villalobos. Petra Villalobos. Company indenture since she was six, unremarkable record, which itself is remarkable, if you follow me. She's been flagged for development. When you reach her household, take particular care with the interview. We'd like to know everything about her associations. Everyone she speaks to. How long she lingers anywhere."

"Why her?"

"Because someone above me says so," Halloway said cheerfully, "and because when I looked into it, I found she spends her rest days carrying rice to a ruined house on the waterfront. Do you know whose house?"

Kestrel stood very still. Outside the window, the harbor glittered, innocent as glass, masts nodding in the morning chop.

"I don't believe I do," he said.

"The deposed baron's daughter lives there. Amara. Branded enemy of the crown, restricted residence, watched lightly because watching closely costs money." Halloway smiled his butcher's smile. "And an indentured diver girl visits her every week with rice and gossip. Now *that*, Elias, is what I mean about connections being worth more than names. Write it up carefully."

* * *

He walked back along the seawall the long way, which was to say the way that took him past nothing he needed and let him think.

Six weeks. The phrase had lodged in him like a splinter. In six weeks the Atlas fleet would weigh anchor — twelve ships of the line and their tenders, bound on the punitive circuit that the newspapers called *consolidation* and the sailors, more honestly, called *collection*. Halloway's seizures were timed to it: houses emptied, boats requisitioned, families relocated before anyone could organize, appeal, or run. The census was not a census. It was a targeting map, drawn street by street, hearth by hearth, in Elias Kestrel's own meticulous hand.

He stopped at the rail and watched the water. Below, on the shingle, divers' children were picking through the tide line — shell, weed, the occasional dead gull — with the serious industry of children who knew the sea owed them nothing. One of them coughed. North shore lungs. He had written it down. *Half the children cough through the winter.* Even his observations had become raw material.

Here was the thing he had told himself for twenty years, the load-bearing lie of his whole careful life: *I only record. I don't decide.* The pen is not the sword. The clerk stands outside the event. He had built a personality out of that distinction the way a poor man builds a house out of driftwood — it had sheltered him, and it had never once been true. Because a form that asks the right question is a decision. A column labeled *Sentiment (estimated)* is a decision. He had designed those columns in a rented room in the southern provinces, aged thirty-one, full of reformist conviction, and now his own invention had come home to him wearing Halloway's smile, and it was counting beds.

Three beds.

He thought of the Hadids' book of accounts, logged into evidence and destined for a stove. Every family on this coast kept such a book; it was the local custom, older than the annexation, older than the company — debts and shares and salvage rights recorded in whatever hand could write, sometimes in letters, sometimes in knots and notches, one generation teaching the next. A civilization of small ledgers. And Halloway's genius — no, Halloway's *method*, genius deserved better — was to understand that you did not need to burn a people if you burned their memory of what they owned.

Kestrel discovered that his hands were cold, though the morning was warm.

He had debts. That was the arithmetic that had governed him since his father's bankruptcy: nine hundred and forty pesos, transferred twice, compounding quietly, the company patient as tide. Forgiveness of that debt had been implied in Halloway's offer yesterday, dangled without being named, the way the best bait is never touched. Nine hundred and forty pesos against three beds. Against a girl who carried rice through the fish market to a branded woman because — why? Kindness? Friendship? Something the forms had no column for?

He walked on. Past the chandlery. Past the customs shed where seized goods were ticketed. Past the company archive, a low granite building whose windows he had passed a thousand times without seeing, the way one stops seeing walls.

At his lodging he took out his working case — pens, blotter, the little traveling inkpot, a sheaf of blank forms — and looked at it for a long time.

What he did then, he did methodically, because methodical was the only way he knew how to do anything, and because a thing done methodically could be pretended, for a while, to be ordinary. He took two sheets of the thinnest foreign paper he owned, rice-paper bought years ago in the south for copying fair, and folded them inside a blank census form. He reviewed in memory the layout of Halloway's folio: the sequence of report, recommendation, warrant, inventory. He wrote out, in compressed notation of his own devising — abbreviations, numbers, arrows — everything he remembered of the Hadid file, and everything he remembered of Halloway's instructions regarding Petra Villalobos, whose name he wrote and then, after a moment, circled, and then, after another moment, crossed out, as though the crossing-out could undo the circling.

He was not yet copying documents. He told himself that. He was only recording a conversation, and a man may record anything; recording is not deciding; the pen is not the sword.

But he knew, walking to the window with the thin sheets trembling faintly in the harbor breeze, that he had crossed a line whose exact location he would never again be able to deny. Informants reported outward. This paper was aimed inward, at the machine itself, and it was the first document of his life that belonged to no office, no directorate, no crown — only to the man who had written it and to whatever that man was becoming.

He hid the rice-paper inside the false bottom of his inkpot, where the camphor smell of the Director's office could not follow it, and sat down at the table to finish the north shore census.

The next name on his list was Villalobos, P., diver, indenture no. 4471, b. coast.

He dipped his pen. He found his hand, for the first time in twenty years of professional writing, was not entirely steady — and he understood, with the clarity of a man reading fine print by lightning, that whatever he asked her and whatever she answered, one of them was already lying, and he was going to have to decide which side of the lie he stood on before the ink was dry.

He wrote: *Villalobos, P. Interview scheduled.*

And below it, in the margin, small enough that only he would ever know it was there, he added a single word in shorthand of his own invention:

*Careful.*
