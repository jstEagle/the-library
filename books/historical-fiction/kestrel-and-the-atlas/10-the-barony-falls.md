## 10. The Barony Falls

The soldiers came at first light, and Petra learned of it the way she learned of most disasters on the pearl coast: through gulls and gossip.

She was hauling brine barrels up from the curing sheds when the birds went up off the waterfront — a white shrieking column of them, wheeling over the old baron's quarter like ash sucked backward into a fire. She set down her yoke and stood very still among the barrels, and Old Sefa, who mended nets beside the seawall and missed nothing this side of the horizon, did not look up from her work when she spoke.

"Redcoats at the Almeida house," she said, fingers never pausing on her twine. "Six boats of them. They've sealed the whole quay."

Petra's hands stayed steady on the yoke. She had trained them to do that, years ago — trained her face the same way, trained her breathing, trained even the rhythm of her blinks. Unremarkable. Invisible. A girl who lifted barrels and looked at nothing.

"Whose house?" she asked, and made her voice bored.

"The one that used to matter." Sefa bit a thread. "They're nailing papers to the door."

Petra shouldered the yoke and walked on toward the salt store, counting her steps the way she counted everything when fear came — one for each plank in the walkway, one for each breath, each number a small stone laid against the flood. Papers on the door. Seals on the quay. The census had been delivered to Halloway nine days ago; she knew because Kestrel had told her, in his careful ledger-keeper's voice, that it was done, that his part was finished, that he hoped — he had actually said *hoped*, the word sitting strangely in his mouth — that it would be used gently.

It was not being used gently.

* * *

The morning passed in pieces. Petra hauled what she was told to haul, mended what she was told to mend, and let the compound's ordinary noise wash over her while beneath it her mind ran the waterfront streets like water finding cracks. The Almeida house stood at the end of Fishgut Lane with its back legs in the tide; Amara had lived there half-ruin and half-queen since Petra was twelve, since the year the annexation came and the flags changed and the baron's ledgers were declared worthless by men who could not read the language they were written in. Nine years Petra had climbed that leaning stair with rice hidden under her shawl, with dried fish, with charcoal sticks and cheap paper, with gossip gathered like shellfish at low tide. Nine years of Amara laughing at the ceiling beams, translating her mother's old pearl-marks by lamplight, teaching Petra the names of stars her own mother had never gotten to teach her.

If they had taken Amara — if she was already on a ship, already in irons —

No. She put the thought down like something hot. The gulls had risen at first light, but Sefa had said *sealed*, not emptied. Soldiers sealing a house meant they expected someone inside. It meant a hunt.

At midday the order came down from the compound offices that all indentured laborers were confined to quarters until further notice — an order that made no sense for divers and sailmakers and made perfect sense if the company wanted its property where it could be found. Petra obeyed it the way she obeyed everything: visibly, promptly, and then not at all. There was a gap in the compound's south wall where the storm of '93 had taken out a section of palisade and the company had patched it with a fence of old dunnage that any child could shift sideways. She had gone through it a hundred times over nine years. She went through it now, in the long gold hour before evening, when every soldier on the coast would be watching doors and no one watched the mud behind the fish market.

* * *

The fish market at closing time was a country of leavings: scales on the cobbles flashing like spilled coins, the reek of blood and brine and lime, tables overturned, awnings rolled. And soldiers — two pairs of them at the head of Fishgut Lane, one pair at the quay stairs, bayonets catching the low sun. Petra pulled her headscarf forward and became a woman carrying a bucket, which required only a bucket and the truth of her posture. She had been invisible so long it fit her like a second skin; tonight, walking through the market that smelled of everything her friend loved, invisibility felt like drowning in slow motion.

She made one slow circuit of the market's edge. Nothing. Then, near the gutting tables where the offal went to the crab pots, she saw the crabs behaving strangely — swarming where they shouldn't swarm, drawn to something behind the last table, something the fishwives had left untouched though it was nearly closing bell.

A pile of sacking. And under the sacking, a boot.

Petra did not run. Running was a thing remarkable girls did. She walked, set down her bucket, knelt as if to retie a lacing, and pulled the sacking back far enough to see hair — black, tangled, stiffened at the temple with blood gone dark as pitch.

"Amara."

Nothing. Then the sacking shifted, and one eye found her through the gap — Amara's eye, fever-bright and ferocious even now, even half-conscious in the stink of the gutting tables, and Petra felt joy go through her like a harpoon line, sudden and terrible, joy with a barb in it.

"Don't," Amara whispered. "Don't kneel so long. Buy your fish and go."

"I'm buying my fish," Petra said, and got an arm under her friend's shoulders, and stood up with the whole weight of her — Amara had always been all edges and elbows, but terror had whittled her down until Petra could lift her, could turn her, could arrange her against her own hip like a woman helping a drunk sister home, which was exactly the story she composed aloud in case anyone was watching. "My sister, she's had a fall, too much palm wine at the feast—" No feast today. "—too much sun." Heads turned anyway; faces in the market were turning, and Petra's heart slammed against the story, waiting for it to crack.

It was Maren who saved it. Old Maren, who gutted mackerel at the end table and had dove with Petra's mother once upon a time, back when the boats had names instead of numbers. Maren looked at Amara's bloody temple, looked at the soldiers at the lane's head, and then picked up her knife and went back to work, saying loudly to nobody, "These girls can't hold their drink. Take her down the alley past the ice house, there's a stool there."

And the whole market bent itself around them like water around a keel. The woman selling conch moved her cart to block the sightline from the lane. Two boys began an argument over a scale-weight directly in front of the nearest redcoat, loud and stupid and perfectly timed. Nobody looked at the two women stumbling past the ice house. Petra understood, with a feeling close to vertigo, that they were all looking away on purpose — that the divers' coast had been keeping secrets from the crown since before she was born, silently, the way the sea keeps its own counsel, and that she had been part of it without ever knowing she belonged to anything.

She got Amara through the dunnage gap in the wall with the last of the light, and heard the fence slats settle behind them like a held breath released.

* * *

Her loft above the net store was eight feet by ten and held a cot, a crate, a lamp, and forty-one books' worth of contraband reading hidden inside a false bottom she'd built herself. Now it held two lives instead of one.

She got Amara onto the cot and lit the lamp with the wick turned low, and saw the damage entire. The head wound was worst-looking but shallowest — a split at the temple, glued shut with dried blood. But her left side was wrong. When Petra peeled back the ruined blouse she found the bruise blooming across two ribs, purple-black as deep water, and Amara hissed through her teeth and grabbed Petra's wrist hard enough to leave marks.

"Not broken," she gritted. "Cracked maybe. I've dove with worse."

"You've *drowned* with worse, you absolute fool—"

"Shh." Amara's hand found Petra's face instead, and for a moment her ferocity guttered out and Petra saw underneath it the girl she'd met at fourteen on a falling-down stair, all laughter and borrowed titles. That girl was terrified. "They came at dawn. I went out the window into the tide pool — you remember, the one behind the kitchen garden — and I lay in the water under the dock boards while they searched the whole house. Three hours, Petra. I counted their boots." A breath, hitching on the cracked ribs. "Then the market. I almost didn't make the market."

"Who hit you?"

"A corporal with opinions about enemy daughters." She said it lightly, which was how Amara said everything that mattered most. Then her eyes went around the little loft — the lamp, the books' hiding place, the narrow cot — and softened in a way Petra had never seen them soften, not in nine years of ruin and jokes. "You shouldn't have come for me. You understand that. If they find you—"

"They won't."

"—they'll hang us both, and I'll die having ruined the only good thing on this coast." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she covered it with a cough, and Petra pretended not to hear, and both of them were lying, and both of them knew it.

"There's water in the jug. Drink. Small sips." Petra worked as she talked, because working was how she prayed: she cleaned the temple wound with salt water and a clean rag, bound the ribs with a strip of sailcloth torn from the bolt she kept for patching, propped Amara's feet on the crate, fed her three swallows of the coconut spirit she kept for lamp fuel and pain in equal measure. Under her hands her friend was shaking, and under her own hands Petra was shaking too, and neither of them mentioned it.

Only when Amara's eyes were closing did Petra say, quietly, "The ledger. Tell me you didn't go back for it."

Amara opened one eye. From somewhere — from inside the bloody blouse, sewn into the lining where a modesty panel ought to be — she drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the blue cord Petra had given her years ago off a fishing net, back when such things were only cord.

"Of course I went back for it," Amara whispered. "Mother's marks. Every boat, every bed, every dive-ground. Proof older than their flag." She pressed it into Petra's hands and her grip, for all her broken ribs, was iron. "It's yours now. Yours and mine. Which means it belongs to no one they can touch."

Outside, across the compound grounds, the curfew bell began to ring.

* * *

Eight strokes. The sound rolled over the coast like a slow iron wave, and in its wake the lamps of the compound went out one by one, and boots began somewhere below — the night watch making its rounds along the net store, unhurried, thorough. Petra pinched the lamp cold and lay down on the floor beside the cot, close enough that her shoulder touched Amara's hanging hand.

In the dark, the loft filled up with small sounds: the watchman's boots crossing the yard below, pausing, moving on; the tide knocking softly at the pilings; Amara's breath, ragged at first, then deeper, dragging her down toward sleep whether she wanted to go or not. And underneath all of it, steady as a keel, Petra's own heartbeat, saying what it had been saying since the moment she pulled back the sacking in the fish market: *she is here, she is alive, she chose me.*

Terror and joy had always seemed to Petra like opposite shores. Tonight they were the same water. She lay in it and let it hold her.

Sleep was nearly on her when Amara spoke from the edge of a dream, so quietly it might have been the tide:

"They'll search the houses next. House to house. It's what my father would have done." A pause, breath catching. "Petra. Whoever comes to that door — don't let it be someone who knows your face."

And Petra, staring into the dark with the ledger warm against her ribs where she'd tucked it into her shirt, thought of exactly one person whose face knew hers — a tired man with ink-stained fingers and careful eyes, who taught her letters and noticed seals and said *I hope it will be used gently* — and heard, faint and terrible, footsteps on the outside stair.

Not the watchman's rhythm. Slower. Hesitant.

Someone knocked, softly, twice, on the door of her loft.
