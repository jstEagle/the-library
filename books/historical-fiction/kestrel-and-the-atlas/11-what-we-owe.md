## 11. What We Owe

The compound bell had long since stopped ringing curfew when Elias Kestrel climbed the outer stair to the scribes' loft with a lantern in one hand and a sheaf of unfiled census forms in the other. The night was thick with the gulf's particular humidity, the kind that made ink dry slow and paper go soft as skin, and his shirt clung to his back in a way he had stopped noticing years ago. He noticed it now. He would remember, later, that he had noticed — that some animal part of him had already smelled the change in the air before his mind knew what it was smelling.

The routine check was his own invention, or nearly so. Halloway required quarterly inventories of the records rooms; Kestrel had expanded them into weekly walks through the clerks' quarters, ostensibly to verify that no colonial documents left the premises. In truth he had begun making these rounds for reasons he declined to examine — because the loft above the boathouse was where Petra Villalobos slept, and because the ledger of his attention had lately developed a column he could not reconcile.

He told himself it was diligence. He was, by now, an accomplished liar in at least one direction.

The stair creaked its familiar complaints. At the top, the loft door stood closed, and beneath it — he saw this before he understood it — a thread of light. A candle guttering low, orange at the seam of the door, thin as a hair. The loft was supposed to be empty. Petra worked the evening nets on Thursdays; he knew her schedule the way he knew the imperial tariff codes, without effort and without forgiveness.

His hand went to the latch. Some other hand, some older and more sensible hand, might have turned him around then, might have descended the stairs and recorded in his log only that all was in order. But Elias Kestrel had spent eleven years being sensible on behalf of the Meridian Trading Company, and something in him had begun, quietly and irreversibly, to starve.

He opened the door.

* * *

Petra heard the latch and was moving before thought arrived.

She had Amara's boots in one hand and Amara's wrist in the other, and there was nowhere to put either. The loft was a single room; its hiding places were a curtain, a sea chest, and a floor of old planks that announced every footstep like a town crier. Amara was already on her feet by the far wall, one hand pressed to the bandage at her shoulder, her face in the candlelight gone the color of pearl — that same terrible stillness Petra had seen in her once before, the summer the soldiers came to the baron's house the first time and took the silver but not the deeds.

The latch turned.

There was no time. There was only the chest, and even the chest was wrong — too small, too obvious, the first place any searching man would look. Petra shoved Amara down behind the stacked sailcloth instead, the bolted canvas they used for mending the skiffs, a wall of folded cloth taller than a man and smelling of salt and tar, and she pulled the loose top layer down over Amara's head with both hands, and then she did the only thing left: she sat down at her little table, took up the letter she had been copying, and dipped her pen.

Kestrel came in with his lantern raised.

For a moment neither of them spoke. The candle on the table fluttered in the draft from the door. Petra wrote her name at the top of a practice sheet, the letters coming out steady, which surprised her, because her heart was going like a diver's pulse at thirty fathoms.

"You're late returning," Kestrel said.

"Net repair." She did not look up. "The Thursday crew lost a trammel off the north stakes. I stayed to help re-hang it."

"Thursday crew finished at the docks an hour ago. I checked the tally board."

Of course he had. That was the thing about Kestrel that everyone misread and no one survived misunderstanding: he was not a bureaucrat playing at thoroughness. He was thoroughness wearing a man's tired face. Petra set down the pen and looked up at him, and made herself do the thing she had decided, in the half-second between the latch and the lantern, that she must do.

"Then you know I'm lying," she said quietly. "And you're standing there deciding what to do about it."

His face did not move. But behind his eyes something shifted, tectonic and slow, the way the harbor bottom shifts under a mooring chain.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"Yes, you do." She stood. Her legs were steady. Later she would not be able to account for this; terror and certainty, it seemed, could occupy the same body at the same depth. "You came up those stairs knowing the loft should be empty and seeing light under my door, and you opened it anyway. You've been opening doors you shouldn't for weeks, Mr. Kestrel. I've watched you do it. So whatever you find tonight, you were already looking for it."

"That is a very dangerous sentence," he said, "to have put in my mouth."

"It was already in your mouth. I only read it back to you."

Behind the sailcloth, Amara did not breathe. Petra could feel her not breathing, feel the silence of it pressing against the canvas like a hand.

Kestrel lowered the lantern. Its light swung across the room — the table, the practice sheets covered in her careful, improving hand, the row of pens cut from reed and trimmed at angles no honest student would ever need. He looked at the pens a moment longer than was comfortable. Then his light passed over the sailcloth, and stopped.

The tar smell. That was what had reached him on the stairs, he realized. Not the loft's usual salt-and-damp, but tar, fresh tar, the smell of recently disturbed boat canvas. And beneath the bottom fold of cloth, just visible in the lantern's swing, the toe of a boot — a good boot, city-made, stitched in a pattern no diving family on this coast could afford.

He thought of the census form for the Villalobos household, line forty-one, *mother's village of origin*, and how Petra had answered *gone* in a voice like a stone dropped down a well. He thought of Halloway's finger tracing names. He thought of the confiscated inventory of the al-Rashid family, itemized in his own fair copy: *one cooking pot, three chairs, a child's shoes, two pairs.*

He thought: *If I lift that cloth, I am still the company's man. If I do not lift it, I have already stopped being one.*

The strange thing, the thing he would carry to the tribunal and beyond, was how quiet the moment was. No thunder of conscience. No cinematic trembling. Just a tired man in a hot room, holding a lantern, choosing.

He raised the lantern again and turned away from the sailcloth.

"Your letter," he said. "Show me your pen angles. Your descenders drift left when you're hurried. If Director Halloway ever asks you to copy company correspondence — and he will, you're on the list now whether either of us likes it or not — drifting letters get noticed."

Petra stared at him.

"Mr. Kestrel—"

"The hour is late," he said, in a voice pitched exactly for the room, for the canvas wall, for whoever might or might not be breathing behind it, "and I have a quarter's inventory to certify. Lock your door when I'm gone, Villalobos. The compound is not what it was."

He crossed to the door. His hand was on the latch when the knock came.

* * *

Three stairs below, climbing: boots and a voice. "Mr. Kestrel? That your light up there? It's Odell — I've the requisition forms for the fleet manifests, sir, Halloway wants them countersigned tonight—"

Odell. Junior clerk. Nineteen years old, ambitious as a rising tide, and due to reach the top of those stairs in perhaps ten seconds.

Kestrel did not think. Thinking was a luxury of people whose bodies were not already moving. He shut the loft door behind himself and stepped onto the landing, pulling it to with a click that sounded, in the ringing silence, like a rifle shot.

"Odell." His own voice came out level, mildly irritated, the voice of a man interrupted at tedious work. Eleven years of practice. He blessed and cursed every one of them. "You'll wake the whole boathouse."

"Sorry, sir — the forms, sir—"

"Are wet." It arrived from nowhere, fully formed, smooth as forged seal. "Ink's barely dry. You can't stack wet foolscap, boy, you'll ghost the whole manifest and we'll re-copy everything by lamplight before the fleet sails. Give it twenty minutes and bring them up. Where are they now?"

"In my desk, sir."

"Then they'll keep in your desk. Twenty minutes." He came down two steps, so that his body filled the stairwell, so that Odell's lantern light fell on his face and nothing beyond it. "And Odell. The scribes' loft is certified empty after net-repair hours. If you're knocking on doors up here after curfew, I'll want to know who authorized it. Halloway's very particular about unauthorized traffic near the records. You understand."

Odell understood. Odell's face performed the entire sequence — confusion, alarm, recalculation, the swift arithmetic of a nineteen-year-old realizing that the tall quiet man on the stairs could make his career or end it before breakfast. "Yes, sir. Sorry, sir. Twenty minutes, sir. I'll wait in the office."

"Good lad."

Kestrel stood on the landing and listened to the boots descend, cross the yard, fade toward the clerks' office. He listened another full minute after they were gone, to the wind in the rigging of the compound's flagpole, to the water slapping the pilings below, to his own heartbeat, which was behaving disgracefully.

Then he opened the loft door one last time.

Petra was standing in the middle of the room. Behind her, the sailcloth had been drawn back, and a woman stood in the gap of it — tall, dark-eyed, one shoulder bound and stained, holding herself with the particular ruined elegance of a house that has burned but refuses to fall down. Kestrel knew her instantly from the census marginalia he had written with his own meticulous hand: *Amara, daughter of the baron Sef, household flagged for review.*

So. He had written the flag himself, weeks ago, a single notation in a marginal column, the kind of thing a census taker adds almost idly — and the machine had taken it and ground it fine, as machines do. He had known it intellectually. Here was the intellectual fact wearing boots.

"You didn't lift the cloth," Petra said. It was not quite gratitude. It was an accounting — the first entry in a ledger between them, entered without sentiment.

"No," Kestrel said.

"Why?"

Because I am done, he thought. Because somewhere between the inventory of a dead family's shoes and the light under your door, the sum of what I owe them stopped covering what I owe myself. Because my mother died owing a company store eleven pounds and was buried in a company shroud, and I swore at her graveside that debts were only numbers, and I have spent my life proving it, and I am tired.

"There's a junior clerk named Odell," he said instead, "who will be countersigning the fleet manifests starting tomorrow. Which means within a fortnight, every document in that office will pass under his nose, including anything that shouldn't exist. Whatever you're doing in this loft—" his eyes went, briefly and deliberately, to the reed pens trimmed at forger's angles, to the practice sheets where a colonial administrator's signature was taking shape in Petra's careful hand, "—you should finish it quickly, and finish it better than you are. Your capital letters are too honest. Honest letters hang people."

Amara spoke for the first time, her voice low and roughened. "You're the census man."

"I was the census man."

"And now?"

Kestrel stood in the doorway with the night behind him and the lantern guttering, and found that he did not have a title for what he now was. Informant who informed for the other side. Pen that wrote backwards. Debtor, at last, to something other than the company.

"Now," he said, "I'm a man with twenty minutes before a boy comes back with wet forms. Tell me what you need copied, and how many inspectors the flagship carries, and I will tell you where the manifest seals are kept — because if you mean to put anything aboard that fleet, Villalobos, you'll need a seal impression, and the wax alone won't survive Captain Reyes. He checks provenance like a moneylender."

Petra looked at him for a long moment. Then, wordlessly, she pulled the second stool from beneath the table and set it down.

He sat. He took up a reed pen. And in the small hours of that Thursday, in a loft above a boathouse owned by an empire, three people bent over one table and committed, quietly and without ceremony, the treason that would cost each of them nearly everything.

It did not feel like treason. It felt like arithmetic finally balancing — the point of no return crossed not with a leap but with a step, taken in ordinary lamplight, by ordinary tired hands.

Outside, the tide turned. Somewhere across the black water, the Atlas fleet rode at anchor, lamps lit, waiting to be robbed honestly or saved criminally.

They had four days.
