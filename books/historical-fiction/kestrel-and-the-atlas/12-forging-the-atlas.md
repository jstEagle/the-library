## 12. Forging the Atlas

The loft above the drying sheds held three kinds of dark: the blue dark before the compound bell rang the night watch, the amber dark of the single shuttered lamp, and the dark under the eaves where Amara slept with the pearl ledger strapped to her body like a second heartbeat.

Petra had learned to work in all three.

She rose before the gulls did, as she had every morning since the soldiers sealed the baron's house — rose quietly, banked the coals, and laid out her materials on the overturned crate she had come to think of as a desk. The lamp threw its small circle of light across the things she had gathered over weeks, each one acquired so gradually and so innocently that no single purchase could raise an eyebrow: a bottle of iron-gall ink bought in halves from two different shopkeepers. A pen knife sharpened past any usefulness for mending nets. Blotting sand. And paper — paper was the hard part, paper was everything — four sheets of the heavy rag stock used for colonial instruments of title, lifted one at a time from Kestrel's own field case, each absence logged in her memory the way a diver logs breath: *this much taken, this long down, this certain of surfacing unseen.*

She had not asked him for them. That was the arrangement they had made on the mudflats without ever saying it aloud: he would leave gaps in his attention, and she would walk through them, and neither of them would carry the whole weight alone.

Tonight the gap would close around the most dangerous hour yet. Tonight she would write.

* * *

Amara woke the way she always did now — instantly, completely, one hand going to the ledger before her eyes were open. Months ago she had been a girl who slept like the dead, who could be shaken by both shoulders and only murmur about pearls. The soldiers had cured her of that.

"Is it time?" she whispered.

"It's time."

Amara slid down from the cot and came to kneel beside the crate, careful not to let her shadow cross the paper. She had appointed herself Petra's second pair of eyes, though there was little enough for her to do but hold her breath. She did that beautifully.

"Tell me again what it has to say," Amara said. It was not impatience. Petra understood it was ritual — Amara feeding the words into the work the way a diver feeds air into her lungs before dropping.

"The deed of transfer, executed in the year of annexation minus nine." Petra's voice was low and flat, the voice she used for counting. "Between Doña Inés Amaral de Qadiri, widow, of sound mind and lawful estate, and the Meridian Trading Company, acting through its factor one Gerhardt Halloway, then junior clerk." She let herself smile at that. "He witnessed his own theft, Amara. His name is already on the original. All we are doing is writing the document that should have existed — the sale *back*."

"And the seal?"

"The seal is the war."

They both looked at the seal. It sat on the corner of the crate under a scrap of oiled cloth: a lump of beeswax and lampblack mixed to the exact ochre of colonial sealing wax, and beside it the impression itself — pressed into a sliver of the same wax days ago from the brass matrix on Kestrel's traveling desk, when he had left the desk open and the room and gone down to the shore to stand in the water with his boots on. He had said nothing when he returned and found the wax cooled and the matrix wiped. He had only looked at her for a long moment, and then he had said, "The registry office countersigns with a hand-stroke, two descenders, like a gull's footprint. Halloway's own hand crosses the seven."

Two careful people, recognizing each other's carefulness. She thought of it now as ballast. It kept her from floating up out of herself with fear.

* * *

She began with the date.

This was the first lesson Kestrel had given her, back when he was teaching her to read census forms in the shed among the drying sails, his finger moving down columns of names while she pretended the letters were strange to her. *Forms are read by suspicion,* he had said. *Suspicion always starts at the top left. A false date is written like a confession — too careful. Write your dates tired.* So she wrote the date tired. Her wrist remembered eleven years of hauling nets; she let the ache into the numerals, let the ink pool slightly in the final stroke, and the date came out looking like something a weary clerk had set down between tides, unremarkable as salt.

Then the parties' names, in the spidery formal hand of the old factor's office — a hand she had studied for six nights running in the company archive, where she hauled ledgers for the tally clerks and was invisible among the shelves. Old Factor Brandt had died two years before the annexation, which meant his handwriting belonged to no living supervisor's memory. That was her margin of safety, thin as paper and just as strong if you trusted it.

The words themselves she had memorized whole: *in consideration of divers sums lawfully owed and discharged, the said Doña Inés does grant, bargain, sell, alien, remise, release, and forever confirm...* The legal language had a music to it, a liturgy of ownership, and she had come to love it the way she loved the shape of knots — not for what it said but for how exactly it held.

Her pen scratched. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the tide turned against the pilings with a sound like slow breathing.

* * *

Halfway down the first sheet her hand stopped of its own accord, hovering, and she made herself look at what she was doing.

Forgery. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. In the company's arithmetic she knew exactly what it weighed: they hanged people for less. They had hanged a cooper in Port Anselm two years ago for falsifying a manifest, and the whole coast had been required to attend.

But here was the thing she had discovered in the last ten nights of practice sheets burned in the coal scuttle, ten nights of copying flourishes until her forearm trembled — here was the thing no one had ever told her, because no one had ever imagined she'd learn it:

Writing was power. Literal, mechanical, weight-for-weight power. Whoever held the pen held the truth, and she had spent her whole life on the wrong side of other people's pens. Her mother had died owning half a pearl boat and unable to write her own name; the company had simply written her share out of existence, the way you write anything — ink on paper, and then the paper is the world. Petra had raged at that once, quietly, the way the sea rages under glass. Now the rage had found a use. Now her hand moved across the page and *made* the world say something else.

It felt nothing like sin. It felt like swimming.

"You stopped," Amara whispered.

"I'm remembering why I'm not afraid."

"Liar," Amara said gently. "You're terrified. Keep going anyway."

Petra laughed — one soft syllable, immediately smothered — and bent back to the page.

* * *

* * *

The signature of Doña Inés took her three attempts, all burned.

The problem was that the woman had signed her true deed — the original, which Amara kept wrapped in oilcloth inside the ledger — with a strong, slanting hand full of merchant confidence, and Petra's hand wanted to imitate the *shape*, and shape was not the thing. *You don't copy a hand,* Kestrel had told her, in the one conversation they had allowed themselves on the subject, spoken sideways over a census form while his clerk sorted papers three feet away. *You become it. Find the rhythm. Where does she lift the pen? Where does she rush? A signature is a habit, not a picture.*

So Petra had studied the old signature the way she studied the water before a dive — not looking *at* it but *into* it, finding the currents. The Doña had lifted her pen after every third letter, a merchant's habit, someone used to signing many documents in one sitting. She rushed the terminal flourish of her name the way people rush the end of anything done often. Once Petra found the rhythm, her own hand disappeared inside it, the way her face disappeared in a crowd, and the name came off her pen looking thirty years old and entirely, boringly authentic.

The wax was worse. Wax remembers heat. She had to melt her counterfeit mixture to the temperature that produced the right brittle fracture at the edges — too hot and it flowed glossy and new, too cool and it went matte and crumbly. She ruined two seals learning the difference, holding the candle-flame at the height a tired clerk would hold it, pouring in a slightly-off circle because perfect circles were for careful people and careful people got examined. Then the matrix, pressed firm, rocked slightly — Kestrel had shown her, wordlessly, with his own thumb, how the old registrars rocked the stamp — and peeled away.

The gull's-footprint countersign went beside it in faded walnut-brown ink, two descenders, casual as a bird landing.

She sat back. The lamp had burned down to its last finger of oil. Across the crate lay a deed of sale, twenty-three years old, returning the pearl fleet of Qadiri — nine boats, nine families' worth of livelihood — to its rightful owner.

It was beautiful. That frightened her more than anything else. She hadn't expected forgery to be beautiful.

* * *

"There's one thing missing," Amara said.

Petra followed her gaze and felt the cold move up her spine. "The registration stamp. The fleet inspection won't honor a deed that isn't entered in the colonial registry — and the registry book is locked in Halloway's office."

"No," Amara said slowly. "Listen. The Atlas fleet inspects at departure. Inspectors don't check the registry book — they can't, it stays ashore. They check the *ship's manifest* against the papers presented. If the deed is aboard the flagship, entered in the manifest as a verified instrument..." She trailed off, and Petra saw the shape of it assemble behind her eyes, the way you see a reef assemble under choppy water.

"The manifest gets copied from whatever the purser holds," Petra said. "And the purser's clerks are conscripts like me."

They looked at each other. The plan grew legs while neither of them moved.

"They'll be hiring extra scribes for the fleet's departure paperwork," Petra said. "Tally hands. Anyone with a steady hand and a company collar. They always take indentures, because we're cheap and we can't run."

"Can you get yourself onto that list?"

Petra thought of Halloway's preliminary list, where her name already sat in the column marked INFORMANT — *eyes among the divers*, the director had written in his tidy accountant's hand, never dreaming what those eyes would choose to see. Being useful to Halloway was a door. Every tool believes the hand belongs to it.

"I can get onto any list in this colony," she said, and heard, with a small cold shock, that it was true.

* * *

She hid the deed that morning the way she hid everything: folded into the lining of her mending basket, beneath the sailcloth needles and the coils of waxed twine, in plain sight of a hundred passing servants none of whom had ever once looked at her. On her way down from the loft she paused at the slatted window and let herself have five seconds of looking at the harbor.

The Atlas fleet rode at anchor in the dawn gray — the great punitive convoy, gun-brigs and transports and press-ganged colliers, and lashed among them like hostages the nine pearl boats of the Qadiri barony, seized under Halloway's confiscation orders, their sails furled tight, their hulls still wearing the names their mothers had given them. At first light they would begin loading. Within the week they would sail for the capital, where the boats would be auctioned as lawful spoils and the divers' families would lose the last thing left to lose.

Unless a piece of paper said otherwise.

It seemed impossible, still — that the whole weight of the empire's seizure could be balanced on four sheets of rag paper and a lump of doctored wax. But then, Petra thought, closing the shutter, the empire itself was only paper. Deeds and censuses and lists of names. She had simply learned, at last, to write.

Below her, the compound bell rang the morning watch, and she went down to her ordinary day with ink still ghosted under her fingernails, scrubbed pink but never quite gone.

At the tally shed, Kestrel stood at his table with his head bent over the fleet manifests, meticulous and weary as ever. He did not look up when she passed. He did not need to.

"Villalobos," said the muster clerk behind him, scanning a fresh sheet. "You've got a steady hand, don't they say? Fleet departure scribes report to the flagship Thursday. You're on it."

Petra kept her face smooth and her voice bored. "Yes, sir," she said, and picked up her nets.

Across the shed, very faintly, Kestrel's pen stopped moving for exactly one heartbeat — and then went on.
