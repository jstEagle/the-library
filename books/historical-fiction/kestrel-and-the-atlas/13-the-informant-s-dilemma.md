## 13. The Informant's Dilemma

The monsoon had come early that year, and it sat over the pearl coast like a brooding animal — low, gray, unwilling to move. Rain came in long silver curtains that turned the harbor to hammered tin and made the Meridian compound's roofs drum all night like distant applause for something no one wanted to celebrate.

Kestrel stood in the antechamber outside Director Halloway's office and watched the rain through a window too narrow to escape through. He had counted the panes of glass once, idly, months ago — eleven, each one warped so the world beyond bent sideways. He thought about that now. How a thing could be transparent and still distort everything it showed you.

"Send him in," said Halloway's voice through the door, though Kestrel had heard nothing knock.

* * *

Halloway's office smelled of camphor and lamp oil and, beneath both, the faint brine of a man who had lived on this coast twenty years without ever once going into the water. The Director was not a large person. He occupied his chair the way a ledger occupies a desk: precisely, completely, leaving no room for anything else. He did not look up when Kestrel entered. He was reading.

Kestrel waited. He had learned early that waiting was a language here, and that whoever broke the silence first paid a toll.

It took four minutes. Then Halloway laid down the paper he held — Kestrel recognized the cream stock of his own reports — and folded his hands upon it as if upon a Bible.

"Elias." He almost never used the first name. When he did, it was either a kindness or a knife, and one learned to check one's pockets afterward. "Sit."

Kestrel sat.

"I've been reading your informant summaries," Halloway said. "The Villalobos woman's material. Very thorough. Very consistent." He let the word hang there like a fishhook. "In fact, I would say she is the most *consistent* source we have ever cultivated among the divers. Do you know what strikes me about consistency?"

Kestrel said nothing.

"It's rare," Halloway answered himself. "Human beings are inconsistent creatures, Elias. They forget. They exaggerate. They grow frightened and contradict themselves. A truly consistent report is like a truly round pearl — beautiful, valuable, and statistically improbable." He turned the report around on the desk so its face looked up at Kestrel, though the words were upside down between them. "I've read forty-one of these now. Forty-one reports, and in none of them has your informant ever named a name above the rank of boat-owner. In none of them has she ever confirmed a rumor we already hold from another source — not once, though confirmation is half an informant's function. And in three of them, Elias, her observations directly *contradict* our other sources. Always in the direction of less guilt. Always in the direction of fewer arrests."

The rain filled the pause. Kestrel kept his breathing even and his face at the particular blankness he had practiced for years, the face of a man listening to weather.

"She may simply be cautious," he said. "Divers are careful people. Their lives depend on knowing what to say underwater."

"Divers are careful people," Halloway agreed pleasantly. "Informants are useful ones. There is a difference, and I pay salaries based on the difference." He rose then, and went to the tall window behind his desk, where the harbor lay gray and churning below. "Here is my difficulty. The Atlas fleet departs in nineteen days. Before it does, the punitive levies must be assessed against the diving families — those who sheltered enemy sympathizers during the annexation troubles, those whose boats were bought with baron money, those who have been *insufficiently grateful*. My lists are nearly complete. Your informant's reports are meant to be the final verification. Instead they are—" he searched for the word with visible relish "—*soft*."

Kestrel felt the floor tilt beneath him, very slightly, the way a deck tilts. He had known this day would come. He had known since the third report, when he began shaving Petra's findings the way a barber shaves a jaw — close enough to pass inspection, smooth enough not to cut. He had told himself the gaps were small. He had told himself Halloway read forty reports a week and could not possibly weigh each grain.

He had been wrong. Halloway weighed grains.

"So," Halloway continued, still watching the water, "we will do what any prudent merchant does with suspect goods. We will inspect them ourselves." He turned. His eyes were pale, unhurried, terrible. "You will go out to the diving camps personally. You will walk the beaches where your informant walks, speak to the families she speaks to, and verify her findings directly. If she is accurate, she will be commended and compensated. If she is not—"

He left it there. He always left things there; it was his gift, making others finish his sentences and thus make his threats their own.

"When do you want me to begin?" Kestrel asked. His voice came out level. Somewhere beneath his ribs, something small and cold began to sink.

"Tomorrow morning. The tide favors an early start." Halloway returned to his desk and drew out a fresh sheet of company paper, dipping his pen as though the interview were concluded and pleasant. "Oh — one more thing, Elias." He wrote as he spoke, not looking up. "Take the Villalobos woman with you. Let her show you her own ground. An informant walks differently on her own territory than she does under observation. Watch how she walks."

* * *

He did not sleep that night. Or rather, he slept the way one drowns — in intervals, surfacing to the drum of rain and the same thought turning over and over in the dark like a stone in a breaker: *she will know. She will look at my face and she will know.*

Because that was the true cruelty of Halloway's order, and Kestrel understood it was cruelty by design. To verify Petra's reports honestly was to hunt the woman he was protecting. To falsify his verification was to forge documents under imperial seal — a crime with a rope attached. And whatever he did, he would have to do it beside her, walking the same wet sand, close enough to touch, while every word between them became evidence.

Near dawn the rain slackened, and he dressed by candlelight in his oldest coat — the one that said *clerk*, not *inquisitor* — and went down through the compound gates into the washed gray light.

Petra was waiting at the tide gate.

She stood with her back to him, looking out at the harbor, her hair braided tight for work, a woven basket on her arm as though this were any errand. She had dressed plainly even by her standards. But she turned before he spoke — he never managed to surprise her; he suspected she counted footsteps the way he counted windowpanes — and her face was calm and unreadable and gave him nothing.

"Director Halloway sends his regards," Kestrel said.

"He sent his regards last time too." Her voice was flat as slate. "Right before he doubled the levy on the Salt Row families."

They walked. The beach road west of the compound was all mud and shell and stranded jellyfish, translucent as dropped lanterns, dissolving slowly back into the sea. The storm had torn nets from their moorings; they hung in the mangroves like the cast-off skins of enormous animals. Petra moved along the shore with the unconscious authority of someone whose body knew this ground better than her own name, stepping where the footing was firm, pausing where the water ran deep across the path, and Kestrel watched her walk and tried to see her as Halloway would want her seen — as a specimen, a mechanism, a line in a ledger — and failed utterly.

"The reports," she said, after a mile of silence. "You've come to check them."

It was not a question, and he flinched as if it had been a slap.

"They've noticed the gaps," he said quietly. "I couldn't stop them noticing. I could only delay them noticing."

"And now you're here to measure me against my own lies." She said it without heat, which was worse. She stopped and faced him, and the wind coming off the water pushed her braid sideways. "Tell me the truth, Elias, because I'll know if you don't. If I fail your inspection, what happens?"

"You'll be reassigned. Interrogated. Possibly—" He stopped.

"Possibly drowned in a holding cell and listed as a runaway," she finished for him. "Yes. That's the standard arithmetic. And you?" Her eyes were dark and steady and terribly kind, which was the most frightening thing about them. "What happens to the man who vouched for a faulty source?"

"That depends entirely on how well I lie," Kestrel said.

Something passed across her face then — not a smile, but the place where a smile would live in a person who could no longer afford one. "Then we should both be excellent at it," she said, and turned, and led him up toward the camps.

* * *

The diving village at Windward Point clung to the coast like a limpet: stilt houses bleached bone-gray, racks of drying fish, children everywhere, naked to the waist and quick as gulls. Word of their arrival traveled faster than they did — Kestrel could feel it moving ahead of them through the lanes, a ripple of silence, doors easing shut, conversations folding themselves away like fans.

This, he realized, was what Halloway had sent him to witness. Not the reports. The fear.

Petra performed her role flawlessly, which was to say she performed nothing at all. She greeted women by name, asked after children by name, examined a cracked hull and pronounced judgment on the pitch mixture with the offhand competence of long habit. She introduced him as "the census man" — a title that had curdled since his first arrival, he noted, from curiosity to suspicion — and the villagers gave him the courtesy one gives a snake in the road: wide berth, watchful eyes.

And all day, in the spaces between greetings, she fed him the verification Halloway demanded. Here was the family who had indeed harbored a fugitive clerk in the annexation summer — true, verifiable, already on the list, nothing new surrendered. Here was the boat-owner whose vessel had genuinely been purchased with baron funds — also true, also already flagged. She gave him only what the company already possessed, wrapped in fresh observation, and Kestrel wrote it down in his field book with a hand that did not shake because he would not permit it to shake, and understood that she was saving them both, grain by grain, the way she did everything: invisibly, competently, without thanks or complaint.

Late afternoon found them at the northern end of the beach, past the last house, where the mangroves closed in and the tide had gone out far enough to expose the mudflats — acres of glistening brown emptiness threaded with crab holes, smelling of sulfur and salt and life. Petra stopped at the tree line and stood looking out at it, and did not speak, and Kestrel came to stand beside her because standing beside her had become, without his permission, the place where he was most himself.

"My mother used to dig oysters out here," she said at last. "Before the company took the beds. She said the flats fed us better than the pearls ever did. Pearls fed the barons, and the barons fed the empire, and nobody fed us except the mud." She crouched, and pulled a shell from the muck, and turned it in her fingers — an ordinary cockle, ridged and worn. "You're wondering if I'm lying to you."

"I'm wondering if you're lying to *them*," Kestrel said. "To me, I hope, you'd tell me the truth even if it killed us."

"Would I?" She looked up at him, still crouched, the shell in her open palm. "Elias. You arrived on this coast eight months ago and in eight months you have asked more questions than any man I've ever met, and I have answered almost none of them honestly, and you have written down almost everything I've said. Why haven't you turned me in yet? Because you know I'm hiding something. You've known since Lessons in Ink, when I stared at the seals too long. You've known since the reports started coming in soft. So why am I still walking free?"

The question sat between them on the flat gray light like a third person.

"Because I flagged your household," Kestrel said.

The words came out of him before he had decided to release them, the way blood comes from a wound — and having begun, he could not stop.

"In my second week. When I was still doing exactly what I was told. Amara's household — the old baron's house — I marked it as irregular. Unregistered occupants, undeclared assets. It was a notation, one line among hundreds, and I didn't think—" He stopped. The rain had started again, fine as sifted flour, beading on the mangrove leaves. "Three weeks later soldiers came for their records. I told myself I hadn't known what the notation would do. Which was true. And which changed nothing."

Petra rose slowly. The cockle shell stayed in her hand. She studied his face with that terrible steadiness, and he let her, because he owed her the sight of it — a guilty man's face, unguarded, at last.

"You put her name in their book," Petra said quietly.

"Yes."

"And you've spent eight months trying to take it out again."

"Yes."

The tide sighed against the flats, a long withdrawal sound, the sea pulling back from the land like a breath held. Petra looked at the shell in her palm, and then she closed her hand around it, and when she raised her eyes again there was something in them Kestrel had never seen before — not forgiveness, not yet, but the ground where forgiveness might someday be planted.

"Then we are even," she said.

"We are not—"

"We are *even*, Elias." Her voice was low and fierce. "Because I have done a thing too. A thing you don't know about. A thing that would hang me twice over if it were found, and that I am not finished with, and that I need—" She stopped herself. He watched her rebuild the wall behind her eyes, brick by swift brick, and almost mourned it. "Not today. Today you write your report, and I give you exactly what keeps us both alive. But soon. On the flats, at low tide, when no one can come near without leaving footprints. I'll tell you what I've done. And then you'll decide which side of the ledger you stand on. Truly decide. No more soft reports. No more halfway."

Kestrel looked out at the mudflats, miles of them, gleaming and empty and treacherous, the kind of ground that looks solid until you step onto it and begin to sink.

"When?" he asked.

"Market day," Petra said. "Two days hence. Everyone will be at the fish market — including eyes that shouldn't see what I have to show you. Wear your oldest coat." She turned back toward the village, toward the lamps beginning to bloom in the dusk, and paused. "Bring your census ink too. You'll understand why when you see it."

She walked on ahead of him through the rising rain, and Kestrel stood alone at the tree line a moment longer, holding the weight of what was coming — confession, choice, the whole fragile architecture of his survival balanced on whatever she had hidden in that crumbling waterfront house — before he followed her home.
