## 14. Market Day

The market woke before the sun did, the way it always did on the first Saturday of the month, when the company opened its granary doors and the whole waterfront came to trade what the gulf had given it. Petra had been coming since she was small enough to ride her mother's shoulders, and the rhythm of it lived in her body like a second pulse: the fishwives' first shouts before dawn, the smell of charcoal and frying dough drifting down from the upper stalls, the wet slap of mackerel against scales that hadn't been cleaned since her grandmother's day.

She walked through it now with a basket on her hip and Amara three paces behind her, and every step was a held breath.

They had dressed Amara as a diver's widow — that had been Petra's idea, and it had seemed sound in the grey light of the loft, soundness being whatever you could convince yourself of before breakfast. A widow's indigo wrap, frayed at the hem where a careful woman would have let it fray. Hands stained with squid ink so they'd look work-worn. The shawl drawn low over the forehead, because widows hid their faces and no one questioned grief. Amara had studied herself in the polished bottom of a copper pan and said, quietly, "I look like someone's mother," and Petra had said, "You look like nobody," and both of them had understood that this was the highest compliment the coast could pay.

But the market was not the fish market. The market was where the whole coast came to see and be seen, and Amara — who had grown up riding through these lanes in her father's lacquered chair while vendors bowed and divers' children pressed against walls to let her pass — Amara walked through them now like any other woman, and Petra watched her do it, and marveled, and feared.

"Keep your eyes down," Petra murmured without turning her head.

"I am keeping my eyes down."

"You're looking at the pearl stalls."

A pause. "I'm looking at the prices," Amara said. "They've doubled."

"They've doubled twice since the annexation. Keep walking."

The basket on Petra's hip held dried rice, a twist of salt, two candles hoarded from the compound stores — the ordinary cargo of an ordinary errand. Under the rice cloth, wrapped in oilskin, lay the ledger. It went everywhere with them now; it could not stay in the loft, where Kestrel's inspections might find it, and it could not be buried, because buried things got found, and it could not be burned for reasons neither of them ever said aloud. So it traveled, this small brick of paper that outweighed everything else on the coast, tucked beneath a layer of rice like a knife under a pillow.

They bought what they needed. They moved with the crowd toward the far end of the square, where the net-menders set up their stalls and the air smelled of tar and hemp instead of blood and brine. Petra felt herself loosening, degree by degree, the way a knot loosens when you worry it with your teeth. Three more stalls. The tarpaulin awning. The alley behind the rope-seller's that cut through to the fish market and home.

It was at the third stall — the one run by old Sef's daughters, who sold fishhooks by the dozen and knew Petra's face because Petra's mother had bought hooks here for fifteen years — that the day broke.

* * *

The woman was at the stall when they reached it, haggling over hooks, and Petra knew her before she saw her face, because you learn to know divers the way you know weather: by the set of the shoulders, the roll of the walk, the way the hands hang calloused-side out. It was Maren. Maren Oduya, who had dived beside Petra's mother in the years before the boats were taken, who had pulled Petra out of the water half-drowned when Petra was nine and greedy for a deep bed of shell. Maren, whose husband's lugger had been confiscated in the spring seizures, who had stood in Halloway's courtyard and been handed a receipt for her own livelihood.

Maren turned to ask the stall-keeper something, and her eyes crossed Petra's face — and passed it, because invisibility held, invisibility always held — and then went past Petra entirely, to the woman in the widow's indigo standing just behind.

Petra saw it happen. She would see it for the rest of her life: the exact moment when recognition moved across Maren Oduya's face like a cloud crossing water. The eyes narrowing. The head tilting, a fraction. The lips beginning to shape a name — *Amara* — a name that had been shouted in the squares and whispered in the barracks and printed on the proclamation boards for eight months, a name worth a reward, a name worth more than any diver earned in ten seasons of bottom-work —

Amara's hand found the edge of the stall. Her knuckles went white against the wood. Beneath the squid-ink stains, Petra could see the tendons stand out like cables.

And Maren closed her mouth.

She did not look away — that would have been its own confession. She simply finished the motion her mouth had begun as something else entirely, turning it into a grunt of dissatisfaction at the price of hooks, and she picked up a hook from the display, tested it against her thumbnail, and said, in the flat carrying voice of a woman complaining about commerce, "Three coppers for a hook that won't hold a snapper? My grandmother sold better steel than this and charged less." And then, without any change in her tone at all, without looking at either of them, without anything in the world changing on the surface of the morning: "Widow, you want to watch your purse. Square's thick with cutpurses today. Thick as fleas." A beat. "Back way by the rope-seller's is cleaner."

Then she paid for her hooks, counted the coppers out loud and slowly, insulted the price again on principle, and walked away toward the fish stalls with the unhurried roll of a woman with nowhere urgent to be and nothing at all on her mind.

Petra stood very still.

Her heart was going like a diver's heart at depth, that great slow drum that fills your ears and tells you: *not yet, not yet, be calm or die.* Beside her, Amara breathed once, deeply, the way Petra had taught her to breathe when the loft's walls felt like they were closing. The stall-keeper's daughter held up a packet of hooks. Petra heard her own voice say, "Two packets, please, the fine ones," and count out the coins, and take the change, and turn away, and walk.

They did not speak until they were in the alley behind the rope-seller's, in the blue shadow between the walls, where the only witnesses were coils of hemp and a sleeping cat.

"She knew," Amara said. Her voice was thin. "Petra. She *knew.*"

"Yes."

"Why didn't she—"

"I don't know."

"You know why." Amara leaned against the wall and put the back of her hand against her mouth. "Because she hates them more than she wants the reward. Because—" and here her voice cracked, and something young came up through the crack, something from before the proclamations, from the years when a baron's daughter had played in the shallows while divers' children dove for coins thrown off the pier — "because she *knows me,* Petra. She used to carry me on her back in the water. I'd forgotten. I'd forgotten and she didn't."

* * *

They did not go straight home. Going straight home was what frightened people did, and frightened people drew eyes. Instead they walked the long loop along the seawall, bought fried dough from a cart and ate it leaning on the rail like women with nothing in the world to fear, and watched the gulf work itself into its afternoon chop. Only when the market noise had faded behind them did Petra let herself think about what had actually happened out there — and having thought about it, she could not stop.

Maren Oduya had known. Had weighed, in the space of a single breath, everything that knowing was worth — the reward money that would have fed her family through the winter, the favor it would buy with Halloway's men, the safety of being a loyal informer in a colony gone mad with loyalty — and had thrown all of it away to mutter a warning about cutpurses and point them to a clean back way.

And it had not cost her nothing. Petra had seen the hesitation. That hairline pause, no wider than a fishhook, in which a whole life turned. Maren had children. Maren had a seized boat and a receipt for it folded somewhere in a tin box, and a debt to the company that grew every season like a barnacle. If it ever came out that she had seen Amara Serrano in the market and said nothing — it would not be a tribunal for Maren. It would be a dark room and questions asked with fists.

Why, then?

Petra turned the question over all the way along the seawall, and the answer kept resolving into the same shape, the shape she had been circling all her life without naming it. Because the divers had always had a country the maps didn't show. Because when your breath is held for a living — when every working hour is spent below the surface, where no one can help you and no one can hear you and the only thing that keeps you alive is what the others taught you — you learn that some debts are not written in ledgers. Maren had carried Amara on her back in the shallows twenty years ago. Maren had pulled Petra half-drowned from the deep beds. These were entries in a ledger older than the company, older than the crown, older than annexation, and that ledger did not accept payment in reward money.

*We cover for our own,* the silence said, all along the waterfront, in every compound and lean-to and net-shed. Not loudly. Never loudly. But Petra had spent her whole life learning to read silences, and now she understood that the silence she had taken for her own private language was in fact spoken fluently by hundreds — thousands — of people who had never met, who needed never to meet, who needed only to recognize each other the way Maren had recognized Amara: instantly, completely, and without a single word that could be testified to later.

She had thought she was alone in her double life. She had built her carefulness like a wall and lived inside it and called the wall *survival.* And all the time there had been a whole city of people building the same walls, wall against wall, until they were not walls at all but streets.

"Breathe," Amara said, and Petra realized she had stopped walking, stopped mid-stride on the seawall path with the wind pulling at her collar, and that she was crying — not sobbing, nothing so loud, just tears running freely down her face into the salt wind, the way rain falls upward off the waves in a blow.

"I'm all right," Petra said, which was true, which was more true than anything she had said in years.

"You're weeping into the sea like a widow."

"I am a widow," Petra said, gesturing at Amara's indigo wrap, and Amara laughed — the first real laugh since the soldiers sealed her house, a laugh with a sob buried in the middle of it like a pearl in a bad oyster — and they walked on, two widows in the wind, and Petra carried the new thing inside her chest carefully, the way you carry a full bowl of water across a moving deck.

Belonging. Not bought, not bartered, not traded in small kindnesses against future favors. Offered. Freely. By a woman with nothing left to give, in the space of a breath, in front of a stall of fishhooks.

* * *

Kestrel was waiting in the loft when they returned, and he read the whole of it off their faces before Petra got the door barred.

"What happened."

"A near thing," Petra said. She set the basket down, unwrapped the oilskin, checked the ledger with fingers that shook only slightly, and told him. All of it. The stall, the recognition, the pause, the complaint about hooks, the warning delivered in the grammar of grumbling. When she finished, Kestrel sat very still on the crate he used for a chair, his long census-taker's hands hanging between his knees, and she watched him do the same arithmetic she had done on the seawall, and arrive at the same remainder.

"If she talks," he said.

"She won't."

"You can't know that."

"No," Petra agreed. "But I know what I saw. She chose. In front of God and the fishhooks, she chose us." She paused. "Chose *her.* But I was standing right there wearing the same face."

Kestrel rubbed his eyes. He looked, as he often did lately, like a man who had slept badly in a room where someone was playing the same note over and over. "Do you understand what this means?" he said at last. "Not the danger. The other thing." He looked up at her, and there was something in his face she had never seen there before — not hope exactly; hope was too grand, too clean, for a man like him. Something more like a man discovering the tide has come in further than any chart predicted. "If Maren Oduya will lie for you — a woman she hasn't spoken to in years — then the lists are worthless. Halloway's lists. The informants he thinks he owns. Every name he's collected." He laughed, short and dry, almost a cough. "He's paying people to report on a community that has already decided, unanimously and silently, to report nothing. He's buying water from the sea."

"He'll find out eventually."

"Eventually." Kestrel nodded slowly. "Everything is eventually. But eventually runs on the tide, and the fleet sails soon, and if the deed is aboard before he understands what's happened—" He stopped himself, the way he always stopped himself, the habit of a careful man refusing even to think in completed sentences that could be overheard by an empty room.

But Petra had seen enough of his carefulness now to finish his sentences herself, and besides, she had news of her own, news she had been turning over since the seawall, deciding whether to spend it.

"There's something else," she said. "Something I want to do. Before the inspection." She took a breath, and the bowl of water inside her chest did not spill; it had grown heavier and steadier both. "Maren has a receipt in a tin box for a boat the company stole from her. And she is not the only one. If I'm forging one deed, Kestrel — if I've learned to make paper tell the truth it should have told all along — then I can make more than one."

Kestrel stared at her. Outside, far off across the water, a bell buoy clanked in the rising wind, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of hammers — the Atlas fleet, fitting out at the company docks, getting ready to carry away everything the coast had left.

"The fleet sails in nine days," Petra said. "I'll need paper. Good paper, aged. And wax." She was already clearing the table, already reaching for the lamp. "How good are you at writing a shipping manifest you've never seen?"
