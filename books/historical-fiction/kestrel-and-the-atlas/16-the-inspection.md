## 16. The Inspection

The flagship *Meridian Star* rode at anchor like a fat white gull, her hull furred with salt to the waterline, and Petra climbed her ladder with the forged deed folded flat against her ribs, bound in oilcloth beneath her sash where it lay warm as a second heartbeat.

She had counted the steps of the plan so many times that they had worn smooth as river stones. Kestrel would present himself at the gangway with his imperial seal and his census ledger and his talent for being exactly as tedious as a situation required. He would ask Captain Voss about tonnage, about crew rosters, about the proper classification of pearl-shell bycatch — questions with answers long enough to bury a moment in. And in that buried moment, Petra, carrying manifests as any company scribe might, would slip a single sheet into the flagship's document chest, third drawer, beneath the bills of lading where no inspector bothered to look twice because no one looked twice at paper that belonged.

Paper that belonged. That was the whole alchemy of it. A forgery did not need to be perfect; it needed only to be *expected*. She had learned that from Kestrel without either of them saying it aloud, in the long evenings over census forms when her eyes lingered on the seals and he pretended not to see. The empire ran on the assumption that its own documents were true. It never checked the ones it trusted.

She reached the deck with her legs burning and stood a moment in the smell of tar and brine, letting the sea's rocking settle into her knees. Divers' children learned early that the water moved whether you resisted it or not. Only the timing mattered.

The deck was chaos dressed as order: casks of fresh water, crates of shell, forty divers from the seized fleet standing in a listless line while a bosun shouted names they answered to slowly, deliberately, in the manner of people who had decided that obedience was a currency and they were done spending. Petra kept her face arranged in the mild boredom of clerical work and walked toward the great cabin as though she had walked it a hundred times, because the trick of belonging anywhere was to move like grief or habit carried you, not curiosity.

A marine at the cabin door put out a hand. "Papers."

Petra produced her scribe's chit — genuine, borrowed from Marisol in the counting house, who owed her three favors and had asked no questions about the fourth. The marine glanced at it the way men glance at anything printed. He waved her through.

Inside, the cabin smelled of beeswax and old smoke. Two imperial inspectors sat at the long table with the company's manifest ledgers open before them, and between them, like a bone dropped between two dogs, sat the ownership file for the entire Atlas fleet. Petra's mouth went dry. The deed she carried was meant for that file. But there were two inspectors here now, not one, and the younger of them was doing something she had not planned for: he was actually reading.

* * *

She should retreat. Every instinct that had kept her alive since childhood said *retreat, come back, find another tide.* But the fleet sailed on the morning tide tomorrow, and after sailing it would be searched — Halloway had already demanded that much — and if the deed was not aboard before departure, Amara's claim would die in some distant port's customs house, unproven, unheard. There would be no other chance. There was only this room, this hour, these hands that had been mending and copying and hiding things since she was seven years old.

She crossed to the document chest against the aft bulkhead as if summoned by duty, knelt, and began sorting papers with the brisk, apologetic efficiency of someone whose job it was to fix other people's disorder. Her fingers found the bills of lading. Third position from the top, beneath them the space where a deed of title would naturally live among its cousins. Her heartbeat was very loud. She thought of it instead as the sea, as swell against a hull, something external, something she could ride.

"Who are you?"

The older inspector. Grey-whiskered, spectacles low on his nose, watching her over their rims with the flat patience of a man who had spent thirty years catching smugglers by asking simple questions.

"Villalobos, sir. Company scribe." She held up the chit again. "Sent to reconcile the manifest against your count. The third drawer's misfiled — has been since the annexation audit, I expect it drove your predecessor mad."

Something in the smallness of the complaint, its petty administrative truth, eased him. Bureaucracy recognized its own ailments. He grunted and returned to his ledger.

Petra turned back to the chest. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were perfectly steady. Some cold-boned part of her had understood, ever since the night she hid Amara bleeding on her loft floor while the curfew bell tolled, that terror and steadiness were not opposites; they simply took turns.

She lifted the bills of lading. Slid the oilcloth packet free. The forged deed inside was her best work — Kestrel's letter-forms taught back to him, the colonial registry's watermark replicated with rice-paste and lamplight and three ruined attempts, the seal pressed from a cast she had made in candle-wax and plaster from a genuine impression borrowed and returned within the hour. It named Doña Ines Amaral, mother of Amara, sole owner of eleven vessels of the pearl fleet, the claim predating the annexation by four years. It was, in its way, more true than anything else in the chest. It was simply true in the wrong handwriting.

Footsteps behind her. Kestrel's voice, arriving like weather.

"Captain Voss — forgive me, one further matter of classification." He came through the door with his ledger open, his pen already uncapped, radiating the weary authority of a man who would die on the hill of proper terminology if permitted. "Your cargo manifest lists pearl-shell as 'marine product, miscellaneous.' Under the new schedule that invites a duty dispute at every port of call. I've drafted a clarification—"

"Inspector," the captain said, from somewhere near the doorway, in the voice of a man being handed a chore, "we sail at dawn."

"Which is precisely why we should resolve it tonight."

Petra did not look up. She placed the deed in the third drawer, beneath the bills of lading, squared its edges with two fingers, and closed the drawer on it softly, the way one closes a coffin, or a door on a sleeping child.

* * *

The trouble was the young inspector. He had risen from the table and was looking at the chest, and then at her, with the particular unhappiness of a man who suspects the world is cheating him but cannot yet prove where.

"You said the drawers were misfiled," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Then perhaps I should review the filing myself."

The cabin went very quiet. Somewhere above, the bosun was still shouting names; the hull knocked gently against its mooring; the lamp flame leaned and recovered. Petra heard all of it with the strange clarity of a diver below the surface, where sound arrives late and from everywhere at once.

"By all means," Kestrel said, before she could speak, and Petra felt the sentence land in the room like a coin spun in the air, heads or tails, everything on it. "Though I warn you, the Meridian's filing conventions would try the patience of a saint. Villalobos, stay and assist the inspector. He'll need the key to the annex chest, and the cipher key for the freight codes, and someone who knows which year's audits were double-entered." He was already turning pages, already frowning at the manifest, already drawing Captain Voss deeper into the swamp of classification. "Captain — 'miscellaneous' — I circled it. You see the difficulty."

The young inspector hesitated at the chest. His hand rested on the drawer pull.

"The ownership file," he said. "That stays under my eye until we're done."

"Of course," Petra said. "It doesn't leave the table."

He opened the third drawer. He looked at the bills of lading. He lifted them, riffled them with a clerk's quick thumb, and set them down again — and the deed lay beneath them, one inch of oilcloth showing at the edge, plain as a wound beneath a bandage, and he did not see it. He saw paper. He saw expected paper. He closed the drawer and turned away, satisfied, and Petra breathed out through her nose so slowly that no one in the room could have marked it as breath at all.

"Double-entered audits," the young inspector muttered, sitting back down. "God help us."

"Amen," said Kestrel, and did not look at her, and in not looking was louder than any glance could be.

* * *

Afterward there was the leaving, which was its own country of danger, because leaving too quickly confessed. Petra stayed forty minutes by the cabin clock, reconciling a manifest that needed no reconciliation, while Kestrel buried the captain and both inspectors under an avalanche of tonnage classifications and the young inspector's initial suspicion curdled into simple boredom. She corrected figures. She initialed margins. Her hand did not shake until the deck air hit her face, and then it shook so hard she had to grip the rail and watch the water for a while, the grey-green water with its patient swells, and let the shaking pass through her like weather through a rigging.

She had done it. The deed was aboard the flagship, sewn into the empire's own paperwork, indistinguishable from the truth it told. Tomorrow the fleet would sail with Amara's inheritance riding in its belly, and no inspection anywhere would find anything but what everyone already believed.

She was still holding the rail when the boat that had brought the inspectors' relief clerk came alongside, and a voice floated up from it that turned her blood to seawater.

It was Odell, Halloway's senior clerk. The rival clerk. The one who counted everything and forgot nothing, who had once told her, smiling, that a compound ran on paper the way a body ran on blood, and that he made it his business to know where every drop went.

Odell came up the ladder carrying a leather case, and he stopped on the deck, and he looked at her — not at the fleet, not at the inspectors' launch, but at her, standing wet-eyed at the rail with a scribe's chit at her waist — and his smile arrived one full second before his words.

"Scribe Villalobos," he said pleasantly. "Busy day. Everyone's aboard but the kitchen cat." He shifted his case to his other hand. "Funny thing. I was reconciling the counting-house chits this morning, and Marisol's chit is out — signed to you, third favor running, or is it the fourth? — and I couldn't for my life remember what a diving-crew scribe would want aboard the flagship a day before departure."

The gulls cried over the masts. The tide was turning; Petra could feel it in the rope under her hand, the whole anchored weight of the fleet beginning, imperceptibly, to swing.

"I'm sure you'll work it out," Odell said, and went below.
