## 17. Betrayal's Price

The trouble, when it came, did not announce itself. It came the way rot comes into a hull — quietly, from below, in a place no one thought to watch.

Kestrel noticed it first as an absence of noise. The counting room at the Meridian compound had its own weather: the scratch of pens, the cough of the adding machine that never worked properly, the low complaint of clerks who had been at their desks since before dawn. On the morning after the Atlas fleet's final inspection papers were filed, that weather changed. A hush moved through the room like something living, and Kestrel felt it pass over his shoulders before he understood what carried it.

He looked up from his column of figures and saw Ovid Brant.

Brant was junior to Kestrel by two years and senior to him in every appetite. He had come to the colony on his cousin's money and stayed on Halloway's favor, and he had the particular shine of a man who believed the world was a market that simply hadn't gotten around to selling him everything he wanted. He was standing at the door of Halloway's inner office with a piece of paper held flat between both hands, the way a man carries something that is either worthless or worth more than he has ever earned, and his eyes found Kestrel across the room with an expression Kestrel would spend the rest of his life trying not to remember.

Not triumph. Worse than triumph. Tenderness, almost — the soft look of a man who has found a wounded bird and knows exactly what it will fetch.

Halloway's door closed behind them both.

Kestrel kept writing. His hand did not shake, which surprised him; it was as if his body had already made some arrangement with catastrophe that his mind hadn't been invited to. He finished the line of figures. He blotted it. He turned the page and began the next, and all the while he listened to his own heartbeat and counted the minutes like a diver counting the length of a breath.

*It might be nothing,* said the part of him that still negotiated. *Brant sells rumors by the sheet. It could be anything.*

But he knew. He had known since the night Petra first showed him the draft deed and he had watched her hand move through the flourishes of a dead woman's signature — known it the way you know a boat has taken water, not because you see the sea coming in but because you feel the deck sitting lower than it should. There had been drafts. Of course there had been drafts; forgery was rehearsal, rehearsal was paper, and paper outlived intention. He had burned what they gave him to burn. But there had been other sheets, practice sheets, discarded sheets, and somewhere in the great indifferent drift of the compound's waste — the bins, the kindling boxes, the rag-bags that the servants carried out to sell — one of those sheets had survived its own destruction and gone looking for a buyer.

Brant had been going through the rag-bins for months. Everyone knew it and no one had minded, because who minds a man digging through rubbish?

Halloway's door opened again at half past ten.

"Kestrel," said the director's clerk, a thin man named Sorrel whose voice always sounded apologetic about itself. "Director will see you."

* * *

Halloway's office smelled of ink and camphor and, faintly, of the salt-preserved figs he ate through every working morning. He sat behind his desk with his hands folded, a posture Kestrel had learned to read over four years: folded hands meant Halloway was enjoying himself. When he was angry, his hands were everywhere.

On the desk between them lay a single sheet of paper, laid square to the desk's edge with the director's usual terrible neatness. Kestrel did not need to look at it to know what it was. He looked anyway.

A deed. Half-finished. The name of Amara's mother written twice — once well, once better — and beneath it the ghost of an official seal practiced in pencil until the pen came, the compass-work of the colonial registry's rosette rendered again and again in a careful student's hand. He knew that hand. He had taught it, in another life, three weeks ago: *hold the pen like a bird, not like a knife.* He had stood behind Petra's shoulder and corrected her letterforms and felt, absurdly, like a man teaching someone to swim while the flood rose.

"Well," said Halloway.

It was all he said for a long moment. He let the word sit there like a coin on a counter, and Kestrel understood that the silence was the transaction. Halloway was watching what he bought with it — watching Kestrel's face the way Kestrel himself had once watched the faces of indentures at the census table, waiting for the question that cracked them open.

"The rag-man brings me interesting things sometimes," Halloway said at last, pleasantly. "Brant brings me interesting things more often. Do you know what I admire about Brant, Kestrel? Nothing is beneath him. A man willing to go through garbage will never starve." He turned the sheet around with one finger so that it faced Kestrel fully, a courtesy, an offering. "Now. This is a forgery of a pre-annexation title. It is very nearly good. It is, in fact, too good — which tells me it was made by someone who understands our forms intimately, someone I have recently spent time and money training." He smiled without warmth or any pretense of it. "And it concerns, of all things on this earth, the pearl fleet."

Kestrel heard himself say, "May I examine it?" and was distantly impressed by the steadiness of his own voice.

"You may look. You may not touch."

He looked. The paper was compound stock, the cheap rag-paper used for internal correspondence — which meant it had been made here, wasted here, rescued from a bin here. The ink was the iron-gall they issued to scribes' assistants. The date was left blank. Thank God, thank God, the date was blank; a blank instrument proved intent but not execution, preparation but not crime, and intent alone had never yet hanged anyone on this coast. His mind ran down the ledger of what the sheet could prove and could not, and it was only afterward, walking back across the compound in the white heat of noon, that he understood how naturally he had done it — how quickly a man learns to think like a lawyer for the people he is betraying everything else to protect.

"The fleet departs in six days," Halloway said. "Its manifest was certified four days ago on the strength of a title search conducted by my office. If a forged title exists, then either it was made and failed, or it was made and succeeded — and if it succeeded, then my certification is compromised, my inspectors have been made fools of, and the Company's claim to the fleet is worth exactly the paper it isn't printed on." He leaned back. "I have sent for the girl."

The words went into Kestrel like a fish-hook going in: clean, deep, barbed where no one could reach.

"Which girl, sir?"

Halloway looked at him with something almost like affection. It was the worst thing in the room.

"Sorrel," he called toward the outer office, "bring in the census abstracts, would you? The Villalobos file." And then, to Kestrel, gently, as if confiding a small domestic matter: "Your informant. The one you verified personally. You wrote me such careful reports about her findings, Kestrel. Such thorough verification. I wonder — did she fool you, or did you arrange to be fooled? Because I will tell you now, it makes no difference to what happens next. But it makes a great deal of difference to me, and I am a curious man, and curiosity is the only luxury this coast allows."

Kestrel said nothing. There was nothing to say that was not a door, and every door led to her.

Sorrel brought the file. Halloway opened it with two fingers, delicately, as though it might be soiled, and read aloud the particulars of Petra Villalobos's indenture — her age, her term, her mother's name entered in the old registers as a diver deceased, her conduct noted in three successive years with the same single word: *quiet.*

"Quiet," Halloway repeated, closing the file. "I have bought quiet for thirty years. It is the most expensive commodity in the world and everyone sells it cheap." He rang the little bell on his desk, and when the guard appeared he said only, "Now, I think," in the tone of a man ordering tea.

* * *

They brought her through the yard at midday, when the whole compound was at its business, and Kestrel understood that this too was deliberate. Halloway did not arrest people in private; Halloway taught lessons, and lessons required an audience. Two guards walked her across the crushed-shell yard from the net-mending shed, where she had been at work among the other women, and she came with her chin level and her hands loose at her sides, and if she was afraid she carried it the way divers carried the cold — somewhere far down, where the body keeps things the heart doesn't need to know about.

She saw him. That was the unbearable part. She saw him standing on the counting-room steps with his ledger under his arm like any other clerk, and her eyes went to him once, briefly, without appeal and without accusation, and then she looked ahead at the doors of the custody house as though they were simply the next task in a long day's work. As if to say: *don't.* As if to say: *not here, not yet, don't spend yourself where it buys nothing.*

He had spent four years learning to be invisible, and in that moment he hated the skill like a disease. He stood on the steps. He held his ledger. He did nothing, and the doing-nothing was the loudest act of his life.

The yard went back to its noises. Nets were hauled, carts were loaded, someone laughed at something near the sail loft. The fleet rode at anchor beyond the seawall, forty hulls in the roadstead with the imperial pennant at their masts, and the tide was turning, and everything continued as though the world had not just dropped out from under the only people in it that Elias Kestrel cared about.

He walked back into the counting room and sat down at his desk. He opened his ledger to his column of figures. He wrote one number. Then he took out his penknife, closed the ledger, and began, methodically, to think.

*Six days.* Six days before the fleet sailed and the seizure became irreversible, six days before Petra stood wherever Halloway chose to stand her, six days in which the original registry — the true pre-annexation record, the thing that would break Halloway's claim entirely — sat in the colonial archive under seal, retrievable only by a census officer of record, which is to say by him, at the cost of his post, his immunity, and quite possibly his liberty.

There had been a version of him, four years younger, who would have calculated this differently. That man would have weighed the value of one indentured diver against a career, would have told himself that survival was also a form of resistance, that a man inside the machine could do more good than a man ground under it. Kestrel examined that argument now, turning it over like a coin he suspected was clipped, and found he couldn't even remember how it had ever purchased anything.

Brant passed his desk on the way out to lunch and paused, savoring.

"They say the girl's mother dove till her lungs bled," Brant said conversationally. "Is that true, do you know? I've always wondered what it costs. To hold your breath that long for someone else's profit."

Kestrel looked up at him. He did not answer, and the silence had a quality that made Brant's smile flicker and die, and the man moved on. But Kestrel marked it, and marked the hour, and marked the fact that Brant would drink at the Salt House tonight and tell the story of the arrested girl to anyone buying. Everything was currency now. Everything was arithmetic. Very well. He had learned arithmetic from the best practitioner on the coast.

* * *

At dusk he went down to the mudflats, though he knew the risk and knew it precisely — knew that a census officer walking the tideline at curfew was a man composing his own evidence. He went anyway, because there was a message to be got to the divers' quarter through channels Halloway didn't own, and because there are hours when staying away costs more than going.

The flats at low tide were a country unto themselves, silver and stinking and alive, the whole seabed turned briefly inside out for inspection. Out past the channel markers, the Atlas fleet lay at anchor with riding-lamps lit, each one a low orange eye in the gathering dark. Forty ships. Six days. Somewhere aboard the flagship, in a manifest folder bound in oiled canvas, a forged deed either waited or did not — and if Halloway ordered the fleet searched before departure, the document would surface within a day, and with it proof enough to hang Petra twice over.

That was the floor dropping out, he thought. Not the arrest — the arrest he had been braced for, had perhaps been braced for since the day he'd first asked her one question too many about her mother's village. The floor dropped when you understood that the fall wasn't finished. That there were further floors. That the search order could come tomorrow morning with the tide, signed and sealed, and everything they had built — the planted deed, the divers' silence, Amara's escape hidden inside a legal fiction — would come apart in the hands of men going through cargo holds with lanterns.

Behind him, on the seawall, a bell began to ring the second curfew. He counted the strokes. Nine.

In nine days' worth of nights he had lied to his own clerk, falsified verification reports, and buried a fugitive in his conscience alongside his debts. He had done all of it quietly, in margins, in the spaces where empire does not bother to look. But Halloway was looking now, and Halloway's suspicion had only one direction left to grow, and when it reached him — not if, when — the choice he had been deferring since the day he delivered his first census would arrive all at once, in public, in daylight, with no margin left to hide in.

There was one document in the colonial archive that could unmake everything Halloway had built. It was signed, sealed, and older than the annexation, and retrieving it would be the most public act of his life.

Elias Kestrel stood on the flats until the lamps of the fleet blurred and swam, and then he turned back toward the compound, toward the archive key in the registrar's office and the morning that was already bearing down on him, knowing that whatever he did next, he would do it where every eye on the coast could watch him fall.
