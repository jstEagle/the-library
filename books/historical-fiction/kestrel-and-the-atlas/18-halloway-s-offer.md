## 18. Halloway's Offer

The room they kept her in had once been a counting office. Petra knew it by its bones — the long scarred table bolted to the floor, the iron ring in the wall where a strongbox chain had hung, the high salt-fogged window set too narrow for even a determined child to climb through. Someone had stripped the shelves bare and taken the ledgers away, but the smell of them stayed: paper, dust, the ghost of tallow ink. A room built for arithmetic. Now she was the sum being worked.

They had given her a stool, which she had not expected, and water in a tin cup, which she had not touched. The guards who brought her in had been rougher than necessary on the dock and gentler than necessary at the door, and Petra had spent the whole walk between reading that contradiction the way she read tide charts — looking for where the depth changed. Rough hands meant an audience. Gentle hands meant someone had given instructions. Someone wanted her whole.

She sat and waited the way her mother had taught her to wait underwater: body loose, breath rationed, mind moving fast beneath the stillness. Her wrists ached inside the irons they'd finally removed at the threshold of this building, but she could still feel them, the way divers felt the water long after coming ashore — a memory pressed into skin.

Through the narrow window came the sounds of the harbor waking toward departure. The Atlas fleet had been ordered searched before it sailed, and the search was going badly for everyone. She could hear the hammers of the inspectors' crews, the shrill arguments of captains whose cargoes were being pulled apart plank by plank, and beneath it all the deep, restless groan of ships at anchor straining against their chains like dogs that smelled rain coming. Somewhere out there, Amara was hiding — or gone already, or caught, and there was no way to know which, and that not-knowing was the cruelest cargo Petra had ever carried.

*They have me,* she told herself. *So they do not yet have her.*

It was thin comfort. It would have to do.

Footsteps in the corridor, unhurried, polished heels on old boards. Petra straightened her spine and put her hands flat on her knees, palms down, the posture of a woman with nothing hidden in her sleeves. It was a lie, but it was a practiced one.

Director Halloway entered as if he were entering his own parlor, carrying a leather folder under one arm and two cups of coffee in a small wire stand he set on the table with the delicacy of a man laying a trap for flies.

"Miss Villalobos." He did not sit so much as settle, arranging himself across the scarred table from her with the ease of forty years of being obeyed. "I hope they haven't treated you roughly. That would be unfortunate. Unnecessary."

"The irons were tight," Petra said.

"I'll speak to whoever chose them." He said it lightly, the way a man might comment on weather, and poured the coffee. He pushed one cup across the table. The steam curled between them. She did not move. He smiled at that — a real smile, she thought, or something wearing one well — and drank from his own cup instead.

"You're wondering why you're not already in the cellar below this floor," Halloway said. "Most people wonder. I find it saves time to answer questions people are too frightened to ask." He opened the folder but did not look into it yet; he watched her instead, the way Kestrel watched forms, reading her face for entries. "Forgery of company instruments is a hanging matter. Obstruction of a fleet inspection compounds it. There are men above my head who would have you flogged first simply on principle." He turned a page. "You will notice that none of that has happened."

"I notice," Petra said.

"Because I prevented it. Because I believe you are more useful whole than broken, and because—" he leaned back, and the lamplight found the gray at his temples, the tired lines around eyes that had spent decades doing sums no one else wanted to count— "because I am not a cruel man, whatever you've been told. Cruelty is expensive. I prefer economy."

Outside, a ship's bell struck the half hour. Somewhere below, boots ran past in the corridor — the search continuing, doors opening, lives being inventoried.

"You know what I want," Halloway said.

Petra kept her voice level. "You haven't said it."

"Haven't I?" He slid a single sheet from the folder and laid it before her, facing her, and she understood then that he had known all along exactly how literate she was — that Kestrel's lessons, the careful hours over census forms, had never been entirely secret, or never entirely mattered. The page was blank except for a heading in a clerk's fair hand: *Statement of Petra Villalobos.* Below it, ruled lines waited like furrows waiting for seed.

"Every name," Halloway said. "Everyone who helped hide Amara Sefrou. Everyone who covered for her in the market. Everyone who carried messages, moved goods, kept silent when silence was a service. Every diver on every boat in this harbor who has ever passed her a crust of bread or looked the other way." His voice stayed gentle throughout, which somehow made each word land harder. "You will write them in your own hand, and you will sign the statement, and I will see it done properly — witnessed, filed, made safe."

"And what does it buy?"

"Ah." He set down his cup. "Now we arrive at the honest part of the conversation."

He drew from the folder a second document, unfolded it with ceremony, and turned it so she could read. She read slowly, making herself go word by word, refusing to let her eyes snatch greedily at the shapes.

*A full and unconditional discharge from indenture.* Not reduced. Not commuted. Discharged — the word stamped in the company's red wax, the seal pressed so hard it had bitten clean through the paper. Beside it, in the same clerk's hand: *Further, the Director undertakes that no charge shall be preferred against Amara Sefrou, and that she shall be permitted to depart the colony unmolested.*

Petra made herself breathe. Made herself keep her face still, because Halloway was watching her face the way she had once watched the surface of the sea from below — measuring how far away it was, how much air was left.

Her freedom. On a page. With a seal on it. Twenty-two years of hauling nets and mending sails and being nobody, and here was nobody's opposite: a woman belonging to herself, signed and witnessed, for the price of a list of names.

And Amara safe. Amara free to go. The thing she had forged documents in the dark to buy, offered up complete, guaranteed, *unmolested*, in a clerk's tidy hand.

It was perfect. It was exactly what she wanted. That was how she knew to start counting.

* * *

"Say it again," Petra said. "Slowly. All of it."

Halloway's eyebrows rose a fraction — approval, almost. He liked thoroughness; she had counted on that. "You provide the names. Complete, verifiable names. You sign the statement. In exchange: your discharge, effective immediately, and Miss Sefrou's safe passage from this colony. No charges. No pursuit. My word on it."

"Your word."

"My word," he repeated, without offense, without flinching. "Which has held longer than most men's contracts. Ask anyone in this compound."

*I have,* Petra thought. *That's rather the problem.* She let the silence stretch instead, filling it with nothing, watching him watch her, and in the quiet she took inventory of the offer the way she'd take inventory of a boat before a crossing — checking every seam for the flaw that would sink her.

There. And there. And there.

A discharge written in the company's hand was worth the paper it bit through only as long as the company honored it. But the second promise — *no charges against Amara, permitted to depart unmolested* — was not a document at all. It was a sentence spoken aloud in a stripped counting room. It lived nowhere but in Halloway's mouth, and the moment her statement was signed, his mouth would no longer need to hold anything.

And the names. She turned that part over slowly, feeling its weight, feeling how light it seemed at first — a few words, a few lines — and then heavier, and heavier, the way a pearl feels heavier than stone once you know what it cost.

Suri, who had stood in the fish market with a stranger's bundle in her arms and lied to a soldier's face without blinking, covering for a woman she barely knew, because that was what divers did for each other. Old Mateo, who kept his boat low in the water and his opinions lower. The widow Chao, who fed half the waterfront's secrets along with her dumplings. The boys who slept on the nets and saw everything and told each other everything and would tell a stranger nothing. Half a harbor. A whole quiet nation of small kindnesses, every one of them now a crime with her signature attached to it.

If she wrote those names, the company would come for them one by one, politely, economically, the way Halloway did everything. Boats seized. Levies doubled. Men questioned in rooms like this one until their neighbors learned what questions cost. The network that had sheltered Amara — that had sheltered *her* — would be unpicked thread by thread until there was nothing left of it but informants and fear, and the harbor would belong to the company the way the ledger said it already did.

And even then — even paid in full — what guarantee? *Verifiable names,* Halloway had said. Which meant the company would check them. And when the list proved true and useful, why should they release the woman holding it? A discharged witness was a loose thread. A dead informant was a closed file. She thought of the cellar below this floor, which Halloway had mentioned so casually precisely so she would think of it, and understood that the offer's true price was printed nowhere at all.

But the temptation — she would not lie to herself about that; lying to herself was a luxury of women with easier consciences. The temptation sat in her chest like warm bread, like her mother's boat riding easy on a kind sea. Freedom, after twenty-two years. Amara, safe. Both of them, bought with ink. It would be so easy to believe the kind version of this man. It would be so sweet to sign.

She measured it the way she measured a dive: How deep. How long. What waits below. What waits above if I don't come up.

Then she lifted her eyes to Halloway's and began, quietly, to refuse.

* * *

"I can't write these names," Petra said.

"You can," Halloway said mildly. "You've had schooling enough. I've seen the samples."

"That's not what I mean."

"Then say what you mean. Economy, Miss Villalobos. I'm a busy man, and the fleet sails on tomorrow's tide, with or without either of us."

She put her hands flat on the table again — not the posture of surrender now, but the posture of a diver planting her feet on bottom before pushing off. "If I give you these names, you'll use them. Not just for Amara — for everything. For the boats. For the levies. You'll turn half the harbor into witnesses against the other half, and the ones I name will hang their own neighbors without meaning to, because everyone talks eventually in rooms like this one." She heard her own voice steady and was glad of it; underneath it, her heart was going like a hammer on a hull. "And after, when the list has done its work, you'll still have me. A woman who sold a whole community to buy two lives. Do you know what happens to people like that in this colony? Nothing good. Nothing you'd have to arrange. Your problem solves itself."

Something shifted behind Halloway's eyes — not anger. Respect, maybe, or the accountant's pleasure at a figure that finally balanced. He did not deny any of it. That, more than anything, told her she had read the ledger right.

"You're assuming the worst of me," he said.

"I'm assuming the arithmetic of you," Petra answered. "You told me yourself. Cruelty is expensive. So is mercy, unless it buys something." She nodded at the discharge, still gleaming on the table between them like something stranded by the tide. "That paper buys a hundred arrests. I've done sums too, Director. Long ones. In the dark."

For a long moment the only sound was the harbor: hammers, bells, the groan of chained ships. Halloway gathered the documents together with unhurried fingers, squaring their edges, and when he spoke again his voice had cooled by several degrees — the parlor closed, the office open.

"You understand what happens now. The search continues. Sooner or later my men find what they find, and when they do, this conversation is void. No discharge. No protection for Miss Sefrou. A cell, a trial, and a rope, and every kindness I extended today remembered by no one." He rose, buttoning his coat with the composure of a man closing a shop for the night. "I am leaving this offer on the table until morning. Not out of sentiment. Out of certainty. You're a survivor, Villalobos — I've read your file since you were nine years old. Survivors do the sums. And when you finish yours, you'll find mine come out ahead."

He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and looked back at her with something that was almost, terribly, paternal.

"One correction to your figures," he said. "You called it buying two lives. It's more than that, isn't it? Read the second page of your heart as carefully as you read my papers. There's a man with a census satchel somewhere in this building whose debts I also hold. His fate is in your figures as well."

The latch clicked. The footsteps receded. Petra sat alone in the old counting room with the untouched coffee going cold and the harbor bells marking time toward the tide.

*He knows about Kestrel.* Or he suspected, and wanted her to carry the suspicion like ballast — either way, the weight was hers now, added to all the rest. She looked at the empty table where the offer had lain and found, strangely, that she felt lighter without it. Some things grew heavier the longer you carried the possibility of putting them down.

Morning, Halloway had said. The fleet sailed on tomorrow's tide.

Which meant that whatever was going to happen — the trial or the rope or the miracle she no longer dared plan for — it would happen before dawn, and it would have to be built out of nothing but what she had left: a head full of other people's letters, hands that could copy any hand, and a community of divers who owed her nothing and had given her everything.

Somewhere above her, in the corridor, she heard familiar uneven footsteps stop outside her door. A key turned. The hinges sighed.

Kestrel stood in the doorway with his satchel under his arm and a guard's clipboard in his hand, and his eyes, when they met hers, were not the eyes of a man come to take her statement.
