## 19. The Departure

*Kestrel*

Dawn came to the harbor the color of old tin, and Elias Kestrel stood at his window watching the Atlas fleet wake itself into motion. There were nine ships now — he had counted them every morning for a week, as if repetition might make their meaning change. Nine hulls black with tar, nine forests of rigging, nine holds that would carry away, if Halloway had his arithmetic right, the entire pearl wealth of the coast: boats seized under fabricated lists, shares confiscated from families who had dived these waters before the annexation, before the flag, before men like Kestrel arrived with ledgers and questions and imperial seals.

He had not slept. The colonial registry lay beneath the false bottom of his document case, wrapped in oilcloth against salt air, and it seemed to him that he could feel its weight through the floorboards, through the whole building, pulling at him like the tide.

Three hundred pages of original entries in the hand of the first colonial surveyor. Dates of purchase. Bills of co-ownership recorded in triplicate and filed, because clerks were what the empire made of ambitious men, filed and stamped and forgotten — including page two hundred and eleven, where in faded iron-gall ink a woman named Serafina Villalobos and a woman named Ines Marchetti were recorded as joint owners of a pearling lugger called *The Quiet Hour*, hull number fourteen, along with six sister vessels later absorbed into the baron's fleet and then, after the annexation, into the company's.

Halloway believed the registry destroyed. Halloway had ordered it destroyed, four years ago, in the great purge of inconvenient paper. But orders traveled down through men like Kestrel, and some men carried them to the archive room and then carried something else back out instead — a bundle of rotted manifests, a lie told once to no one, a truth hidden under floorboards for four years like a coin saved against famine.

Kestrel dressed slowly. He chose his good coat, the one with the census office's braid still on the sleeve, because if a man was going to be hanged he might as well be hanged in his uniform. He looked at himself in the spotted glass and thought, absurdly, of his mother asking whether he'd remembered his gloves.

*You are paid to erase truths,* he had written in his own private ledger, months ago, the night after Halloway showed him the confiscated family's inventory laid out like a harvest. *What are you when you stop?*

There was only one way to find out.

He took the long road to the docks, past the fish market already loud with gulls, past the chandlers' row where tar smoked in open pots, past the compound wall where — he did not let himself look up — a certain loft window stood dark. Petra was either already in position or she was not. Either the forged deed sat in her sleeve or it did not. He had made his peace, or something adjacent to peace, with the fact that her part of this plan belonged wholly to her, as his belonged to him. Two careful people. One morning.

The docks were a solid mass of people by seven o'clock. The company had turned the departure into a ceremony — Halloway's idea, naturally; punishment performed as festival offended fewer people. Bunting hung between the cranes in imperial colors. A band played somewhere near the customs house, brass fighting a losing battle against the wind. Company clerks in their gray coats moved through the crowd with manifests, checking names against names, and behind the rope barriers stood the divers and their families, come to watch their own boats sail away from them forever.

Kestrel saw faces he knew from his census rounds. The widow Oyelaran, whose husband had died in a diving accident the company had never paid compensation for. Old Tomás and his grandsons. A dozen others who had answered his questions honestly, believing — why should they not? — that an empire which asked for your name had some obligation attached to having it.

His hands were steady. That surprised him. He had expected tremors, sweat, the body's ordinary treacheries. Instead there was only a terrible clarity, the kind he imagined divers must feel at thirty fathoms, when the light goes thin and the surface is a rumor overhead and all that remains is the next movement, and the next.

Director Halloway stood on the customs house steps in a white linen suit, shaking hands with the inspection commissioners, three imperial officials flown in from the capital with seals on chains around their necks. The flagship, the *Atlas Rex*, rode low at its mooring, its manifest already sealed aboard. At half past eight, the commissioners would board. At nine, the fleet would sail.

At twenty minutes to eight, Elias Kestrel walked through the crowd toward the steps, carrying a document case that weighed nothing and everything.

A sergeant of the company guard recognized him and nodded, one clerk to another. "Morning, Mr. Kestrel. Business with the commission?"

"Business with the truth," Kestrel said, and heard his own voice as though it belonged to someone taller.

He mounted the steps. Halloway saw him coming and his face went through a small, swift rearrangement — surprise, then courtesy, then, at the last instant, something older and colder, the look of a man who has spent his life reading other men's ledgers and recognizes, too late, an entry in the wrong column.

"Kestrel." Halloway's voice was warm, pitched for the commissioners' ears. "Come to see the fleet off? Patriotic of you."

"Director." Kestrel stopped three steps below the platform. Below him the crowd murmured, sensing weather. "Honored commissioners. Before you certify the manifest of the Atlas fleet, I ask you to examine a document."

He opened the case. He drew out the oilcloth, unwrapped it, and lifted the colonial registry into the hard white light of the morning, and he heard the sound the crowd made — not words yet, just a shift in breathing, a thousand lungs answering each other.

"The official record of this colony's first survey," he said, and his voice carried better than he expected, the wind cooperating at last. "Recorded twelve years before annexation. It lists the true ownership of every vessel this fleet is carrying. Ownership that predates the crown's claim by more than a decade. Ownership that Director Halloway's confiscation lists have declared void on the basis of documents his own office fabricated."

"Commissioners—" Halloway began.

"I was ordered to destroy this registry four years ago," Kestrel said over him. "I did not. Every boat in that fleet — the *Quiet Hour*, the *Sable*, the *Mercy of Tides* — is listed here, with her owners' names. Names of living people. Names you can read for yourselves."

He held it up. His arms did not shake. Page two hundred and eleven faced the wind and the sun and the crowd below, and somewhere in that crowd, he knew, stood a woman whose mother's name was written there in iron-gall ink, watching.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The band, unaware, brayed on about glory.

Then Commissioner Vasseur, the eldest, the one with the reputation for incorruptibility that everyone assumed was performance, came down two steps and took the registry from Kestrel's hands.

*Petra*

Petra Villalobos had been invisible for nineteen years, and so she stood in plain sight at the foot of the customs house stairs, in a company scribe's gray coat that Amara had altered overnight to hang correctly, and watched Elias Kestrel commit treason forty feet above her head.

Her heart was not beating. She was fairly sure of it. The whole dock could have heard the silence inside her chest.

Beside her, squeezed among the fishwives near the rope barrier, Amara stood veiled in a market scarf, her hands folded over her stomach as though in prayer, which Petra suspected was the oldest trick in the coast's repertoire — a woman hiding anything hides it under stillness. They had not spoken since dawn. There was nothing left to say that had not been said on the mudflats, in the loft, in the small hours when forgery became prayer and prayer became ink. *Finish it together or hang together.* Those had been Kestrel's terms and hers, and now the finishing was happening above them in the voices of men.

"He's holding it up," Amara breathed. "Petra. He's actually—"

"Hush. Watch."

Commissioner Vasseur turned pages. The second commissioner crowded at his shoulder. The third — the young one, Ferro, whom Halloway had wined and dined for a week — hovered near Halloway, and Petra watched the director's hands: white-knuckled at his sides, then moving, smoothing his coat, arranging his face into sorrowful patience, a man preparing to explain an unfortunate clerk's derangement.

Then Vasseur looked up from page two hundred and eleven, and his voice cracked across the docks like a whip over water.

"Sergeant. No vessel in this fleet sails until ownership is verified against this register. No vessel. Halt the boarding."

The sound that came off the crowd was unlike anything Petra had ever heard. Not a cheer — not yet, not with the guards everywhere — but a roar's first cousin, a great indrawn gasp that exhaled as murmur, as weeping, as the widow Oyelaran's voice crying out in dialect, *"That's my boat, that's our boat,"* and old Tomás's grandsons lifting him onto their shoulders so he could see the steps.

The sergeant hesitated. He looked at Halloway. Halloway's face had gone the color of the harbor in January.

"That document is stolen property," Halloway said, recovering, his voice dropping into the register he used on defaulting debtors. "This man is a disgraced clerk. Commissioners, I can produce sworn testimony within the hour that this registry was ordered destroyed by legitimate authority—"

"Then produce it," Vasseur said mildly. "After the verification. Sergeant — the gangways."

And that was when Petra saw the future arrive early, the way disasters do: Sergeant Brack, Halloway's creature, the man who had arrested her outside the counting room, pushed through the guard cordon and started up the steps toward Kestrel, and behind him four more guards, and Halloway saying, quietly, only for those close enough, "Arrest him. Theft of company documents, sedition, whatever fits. Get that paper back."

"No—" Amara's fingers dug into Petra's arm.

"It's all right," Petra lied, because it was not all right, because she had known since the mudflats exactly what this cost would be, and knowing did not make the watching survivable, it only made it honest.

Brack reached Kestrel at the top of the steps. She saw Kestrel turn to face him — saw him straighten his coat first, that absurd, meticulous gesture, the census taker to the end — and she saw him say something too quiet for the crowd, though she read his lips well enough, having learned them across ledgers and lamplight: *Careful with the register.*

Then they took his arms, both arms, and the document case fell from his shoulder and clattered on the stone, and the crowd surged against the rope barriers like a tide remembering the moon, and Petra was running — she didn't remember deciding to run — shoving through fishwives and sailors toward the steps, toward the man who had flagged Amara's household and confessed it on the mudflats with shame naked on his face, the man who had taught her to read the seals and said nothing, said *nothing*, when he caught her studying them.

Two guards held him between them. His collar was torn. He was looking out over the crowd, over the heads, the way a man looks for someone when he knows he won't get another chance, and his eyes found hers, gray finding gray in all that morning noise, and he did not struggle. He smiled. It was a small smile, crooked, exhausted, entirely his.

*It's done,* the smile said. *Your turn.*

Then they dragged him around the side of the customs house, and the crowd roared for true now, and Commissioner Vasseur was shouting for order and for the register to be secured in the commission's custody, and none of it, none of it mattered to Petra at all except the cold weight in her sleeve where Amara's forged deed waited, and the flagship waiting beyond it, and the hours remaining.

She stopped running. She made her hands be still. Nineteen years of practice: breathe, lower your eyes, become the gray coat, become furniture, become weather.

Then she turned to Amara, whose veil had slipped, whose face was wet and blazing, and said the only thing left to say:

"They'll search him. They'll search everything he touched. We have until noon, maybe less. Come on."

* * *

*Kestrel*

The room they put him in had once been an office; he could tell by the rectangle of unfaded paint on the wall where a map had hung. They had taken his coat, his boots, his penknife, and, with a delicacy he almost admired, the thread from his sleeve braid, as if rank could be confiscated separately from the man. Through the high window he could hear the docks: the band had stopped playing, and in its place rose a sound like the sea deciding something.

They would charge him by nightfall. Theft, forgery by extension — they would try to tie Petra to him, he knew, and he had rehearsed the architecture of his silence the way he once rehearsed census questions. Whatever they did to him, there was nothing in his rooms, nothing in his ledgers, nothing in his mouth that would lead them to her.

He sat on the desk, because there was no chair, and listened to the gulls, and found that he was — there was no other word for it — happy. Frightened, ruined, quite possibly bound for the tribunal and then the penal colony at Saltmere, and underneath all of it, steady as ballast: happy. Four years of erasing truths, and this morning he had held one up to the light in front of the whole harbor, and the light had not refused it. The inspectors had halted the seizure. Somewhere out there the *Quiet Hour* still rode at anchor, her owners' names legible in the sun.

A key turned in the lock. He expected the magistrate's man.

It was Halloway.

The director closed the door behind him and stood for a moment with his back against it, and Kestrel saw, with clinical interest, that the white suit had a wrinkle in it. Halloway never wrinkled. The man was a machine for appearing pressed.

"Do you know what you've done?" Halloway asked. Not shouting. Worse than shouting: conversational, almost gentle, a teacher with a disappointing pupil.

"I ended a theft in progress."

"You've cost me a fleet, Kestrel. Or you've cost the company a fleet, which is the same thing, which is the point of you, and always has been." Halloway came closer, hands clasped behind his back, examining him like an inventory discrepancy. "But fleets can be re-taken. Registries can be challenged. Commissions can be — advised." He smiled without warmth. "Here is what you will not cost me: your friends. The girl, for instance. The diver girl. Clever with documents, I'm told, though I'm told many things. Give me something useful about her and the Barón de la Costa's daughter, and your tribunal finds you foolish rather than criminal. Debt relief restored. A post in the capital, even. Men have rebuilt from less."

Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. Then he laughed — softly, helplessly, the first honest laugh of his adult life.

"There it is," he said. "Complicity arriving dressed as opportunity. You offered me the same thing in your office, do you know that? Word for word. I said yes the first time. I've been trying to figure out for months how to say no properly." He folded his hands. "No."

Halloway studied him the way he'd once studied the confiscated family's inventory, and Kestrel understood suddenly that he was seeing, in Kestrel, a sum that refused to balance, and that this offended him more than the treason did.

"So be it," Halloway said, moving to the door. "Tribunal tomorrow morning. I hope your conscience keeps better accounts than you do, Mr. Kestrel."

The lock clicked. The footsteps receded. Kestrel sat alone in the bare room with the sea-sound swelling beyond the wall, and he bowed his head, not in defeat but in the manner of a man counting what remains.

His career: gone. His immunity: gone. His debts: reinstated, compounding, ruinous. His name —

His name was his. It was the one thing they had not thought to take, and it was the one thing he had finally given himself permission to use.

Through the window, faint under the cries of the gulls, he heard the harbor bell begin to ring the boarding call — and then, seconds later, ringing wrong, ringing urgent, ringing alarm.

Something had happened on the water.

Something, he thought, rising slowly to his feet with his heart hammering like a freed thing, involving the *Atlas Rex*.
