## 20. Amara's Wake

The limbo lasted eleven days.

Eleven days in which the Atlas fleet sat at anchor like a held breath, its pennants limp in the windless mornings, its decks crowded with men who had nothing to do but wait and therefore did nothing but talk. The seizure order hung over the pearl fleet like weather. The inquiry Kestrel had bought with his career hung over it too, and between the two documents — one demanding the boats be taken, one questioning whether they could be — the whole harbor stalled into a silence that everyone understood to be temporary and no one understood how to live inside.

Petra lived inside it the way she had learned to live inside everything: with her hands moving and her face still. She mended nets that didn't need mending. She scrubbed the compound's flagstones until they showed her reflection back to her, warped and gray. She carried messages she was no longer asked to carry, since Halloway's apparatus had been quietly dismantled around her, its clerks reassigned, its lists burned or filed into oblivion — she never learned which — and in the evenings she climbed to her loft and lay awake listening to the garrison patrols count their way down the waterfront, boots on planking, a rhythm like a heart that was not her own.

Amara stayed hidden through all of it.

Eleven days in the loft above the cooper's shed, where the heat gathered at noon and rolled off the tin roof in visible waves, and the smell of hot oak staves seeped up through the floorboards until Amara said she dreamed in the language of barrels. Eleven days of cold food eaten in the dark, of speaking only in whispers after the third bell, of measuring her world by the square of window above the water cistern, through which she watched the gulls and, once, a line of frigate birds riding a thermal so high they seemed painted onto the sky.

"I used to watch them from my mother's veranda," she said on the ninth night, her voice sanded thin by confinement. "I thought they were free. Then I learned they steal from other birds midair. They make other birds do the hunting, and they take the catch." A pause. "I have decided I admire them."

Petra, sitting with her back against the wall and the pearl ledger open on her knees — she had taken to reading it by feel now, in the dark, tracing her mother's co-ownership marks with her thumbnail like a woman counting rosary beads — laughed for the first time in what felt like a season. It came out rusty. Amara laughed too, and then neither of them spoke for a while, because laughing that close to grief has a short shelf life.

Because that was the arithmetic of those eleven days, the sum both of them were always working: the inquiry might save the boats. It would not save Amara. Halloway had fallen, but Halloway's paperwork survived him, and somewhere in the colonial registry — copied, indexed, cross-filed in triplicate as such documents always are — the name *Amara Sef* still sat beside the words *enemy of the crown, whereabouts unknown*. Petitions took months. Tribunals took longer. And every day Amara remained in the colony was a day the wrong clerk, the ambitious clerk, the one who had sold Halloway the forged deed's discarded draft and been paid in nothing but trouble for his pains, might look sideways at a girl who mended nets and see the answer to a question worth money.

The neutral trader came into harbor on the twelfth morning.

Petra saw her first from the loft window: a low, salt-white hulk called the *Cormorant's Mercy*, flying the colors of a gulf port so small and so scrupulously unaligned that ships out of her harbors were welcome everywhere and suspected everywhere, which amounted to the same thing. She was taking on cargo at the outer quay — dried fish, indigo, the usual indifferent freight of survival — and her manifest, posted by custom at the harbor office, listed a destination that made Petra's chest tighten and release all at once: across the gulf. The far shore. Free water.

She went down the ladder faster than was safe and crossed the compound yard with her stride deliberately slowed, because a running woman draws eyes, and she had spent her whole life teaching herself not to draw eyes. But under the stillness something was beating its wings.

*Three days,* she thought. *Maybe less.*

* *

The arrangements took two days and cost Petra nearly everything she had learned to hoard.

Money first: the small iron box beneath her floorboard, nine years of coins earned shucking oysters after hours and selling her neat hand to illiterate divers who needed letters written home. She counted it out on Amara's knee in the dark, and Amara pushed half of it back, and there was the only argument of their last days together — brief, fierce, conducted entirely in whispers, ending when Petra closed Amara's fingers over the coins and said, "You'll need it to eat until you find work," and Amara said, "And you'll need it to—" and stopped, because neither of them knew how to finish that sentence, and the silence finished it instead.

Passage itself was harder. The *Cormorant's Mercy*'s captain was a broad, leathery woman named Oyelaran who took bribes the way other captains took on ballast — routinely, without sentiment, and strictly according to capacity. She wanted three times the going rate for an undocumented passenger and looked Petra up and down when Petra flinched.

"Undocumented means invisible," Oyelaran said. "Invisible costs. You want cheap, send her documented. Send her to the harbor office. Let them stamp her pretty little name in a book." She smiled without warmth. "We sail on the morning tide day after next, whatever the fleet does. Neutral means I don't wait for empires."

Petra paid. What remained in the iron box would not have bought a boat's worth of pitch.

The deed worried her most, and she would not let herself admit it, which is how she knew it worried her most. She checked it a final time on the second night, holding it close to the shuttered lamp, examining her own forgery the way a diver examines a shell for the crack that will let the poison in. Kestrel had taught her the forms; Halloway's fall had taught her the stakes; and the work itself, done in stolen hours with a steady hand and borrowed seals, was the finest thing she had ever made. The paper was right. The ink had aged correctly under her smoke-stained cloth. The registry number matched the original colonial rolls that Kestrel had copied before the tribunal seized them, and the chain of ownership ran backward through the annexation like a river running home: the pearl fleet, six boats and two tenders, rightful property of Suleika Sef, deceased, thence to her daughter and sole heir.

It would hold. It had to hold. If the inquiry ruled for the fleet, the deed would surface like a drowned bell and the boats would pass legally, irrevocably, to Amara — not to the company, not to the crown, not to any clerk with a stamp and an appetite. Her mother's co-owned share, the boat Petra had been born aboard and indentured off of, was folded into the transfer by a clause she had drafted four times before dawn before she got the language to sit down properly. Amara would own Petra's mother's boat. It was the closest thing to a will Petra would ever write.

"It's good work," Amara said behind her, quietly. "You know it's good work. Stop staring at it like it owes you an apology."

Petra rolled the deed, sleeved it in oilcloth, and tied it with cord to the ledger. Two objects. Everything either of their families had ever owned or been owed, bound together with a knot she tied twice.

* *

They went down to the outer quay on the morning tide, before full light, when the harbor belonged to fishermen and gulls and the kind of fog that comes off warm water into cool air and makes the whole world soft and uncertain.

Petra had planned the walk a dozen times in her head: the back lane past the cooper's shed, the fish market before the stalls opened, the seawall where the stone was slick with spray. She had planned it because planning was how she survived, because a route rehearsed is a route that cannot ambush you. But no plan accounted for the weight of the last fifty yards, when the *Cormorant's Mercy* rose up out of the fog ahead of them, patched and ugly and beautiful, her crew singing some tuneless working song as they loaded the last crates, and Amara's hand found Petra's arm and gripped it, and Petra understood that all her rehearsals had prepared her for everything except this.

There was no time. That was the mercy of it. There was no long moment on the dock, no speeches, no weeping farewell with the sun rising symbolically behind them. There was a bosun calling the tide, and Oyelaran at the gangway checking a slate, and perhaps ninety seconds in which two women who had kept each other alive for years could say what needed saying.

Amara slung the bundle — ledger, deed, one change of clothes, the iron box's surviving half — across her back and looked at Petra with the fog moving between them.

"The boat," she said. "Your mother's. When the inquiry settles it, I'm going to send for you. I'll pay any price. I'll hire the *Cormorant's Mercy* herself if I have to, and Oyelaran will charge me triple, and it will be worth it."

"Don't send for me," Petra said.

Amara's face did something complicated and settled into something simple. "No," she agreed. "You'd hate it. Being sent for." She almost smiled. "Come instead. Or don't. But own your coming."

A year ago — a month ago — Petra would not have known what to do with that. Belonging had always come to her as conscription: the company's list, Halloway's arithmetic, names entered in ledgers by men who owned the ledgers. But Amara was offering her the other thing, the rare thing, the thing the divers' network had offered her at the fish market when old Sefina had stepped between them and the soldiers and lied with her whole weathered face: belonging freely given, which can be freely refused.

"I'll come," Petra said. "When my time is served. When my hands are my own. Look for me across the gulf and don't look early."

"Tide's turning," the bosun shouted.

Amara caught Petra's face in both hands — quick, rough, her palms smelling of oakum and the cooper's shed — and pressed her forehead to Petra's forehead, the old greeting of their childhood, the one they had invented on a beach in peacetime because they were girls and thought they were inventing everything. Three seconds. Four. Petra counted them the way she counted strokes swimming, automatically, uselessly, wanting more.

Then Amara stepped back, turned, walked up the gangway without looking around — because looking around is how people get remembered, and they had both survived too long to forget it — and was swallowed into the fog and the ship and the far side of the water.

The *Cormorant's Mercy* cast off within the hour. Petra stood on the seawall and watched her go, low and white and shrinking, until the fog took her the way the sea takes everything: completely, and without malice. She did not cry. She stood with her hands loose at her sides and her head high, and she let the grief move through her like a swell under a hull, lifting her, passing, gone but leaving the water changed.

Then she turned and walked back along the seawall toward the compound, toward the bells and the ledgers and the final year of her indenture, and she found that her feet knew the way without instruction, and that the harbor, waking up around her — fishermen hauling their skiffs down the sand, the smoke of the first bread ovens, a child running the tideline with a kite made of torn sailcloth — was hers. Not the company's harbor. Not the crown's. Hers, because she had chosen it, because she had buried her mother in its mudflats and taught its children their letters and nearly hanged for its boats, and you belong to the place you have paid for.

At the mouth of the compound lane she stopped. A runner was coming up from the docks at an official pace, government blue sash bright against the fog-damp morning, and behind him, walking slower, his coat travel-worn and his face thinner than the last time she had seen him, came Elias Kestrel.

He carried a leather portfolio under one arm, and he was looking at the harbor the way a man looks at a country he has just been exiled from, and when he saw her standing in the lane he stopped too.

"Petra Villalobos." He said her name carefully, as if it were a form he had finally learned to fill out correctly.

"Mister Kestrel." She studied him — the portfolio, the sash, the absence of a company pin at his collar. "You've lost your post."

"And gained a tribunal." He glanced back at the empty road, then at her, and something in his tired face suggested he already knew about the fog, the ship, the ninety seconds on the quay. He had a census taker's gift for knowing things. "I've also gained a witness summons. For you. It seems the tribunal wishes to hear from the woman whose name appears on Halloway's informant list — and whose handwriting, according to the examining clerk, appears nowhere else in his files. Which the tribunal finds very interesting indeed."

Behind him the fog was burning off the water, and the pearl fleet rode at anchor, six boats and two tenders, waiting to learn whose they were.

"When?" Petra said.

"Tomorrow morning," said Kestrel, "at nine."
