## 21. Testimony

The tribunal convened in the customs house on a morning so bright it felt like an accusation.

Kestrel had passed that building a hundred times in his year on the pearl coast, had noted its columns and its brass fittings the way he noted everything — as data, as inventory — without ever imagining he would enter it as anything but a functionary. Now his name was spoken by a clerk at the door, written into a record, assigned a seat: not the witness bench, which stood low and exposed before the dais, but the accused's chair, which was only a wooden kitchen chair carried up from somewhere below stairs. Someone's chair. A cook's perhaps, or a dockhand's. It smelled faintly of salt and pipe smoke, and Kestrel found this oddly steadying. He sat where ordinary men sat.

Three officers presided. Commissioner Vane of the colonial judiciary, dry as August grass; Captain Orell of the Atlas fleet's marine detachment, who wore his impatience like epaulettes; and between them, in civilian grey, an auditor sent from the capital, whose name Kestrel had already forgotten because the man seemed designed to be forgotten — pale, precise, with ink-stained fingers and the patient stillness of someone who read ledgers for pleasure. Kestrel understood immediately which of the three mattered. Vane would conduct the theatre; Orell would glower on behalf of the fleet; but the auditor would decide what was true, and truth, Kestrel knew better than almost anyone alive, was a matter of which documents survived scrutiny.

He had dressed carefully. Not well — well was a flag, and flags drew fire — but carefully: the same grey coat he'd worn to deliver the census, brushed clean; boots mended rather than replaced; no watch chain. He wanted to look like exactly what he was, a man of modest means who had done an immodest thing. Anything more polished would smell of preparation, and preparation implied conspiracy, and conspiracy implied others. He would not let them pull names out of him like nails. There were no other names left to give anyway. Halloway had seen to that, scattering his subordinates across three colonies before the summons came, and Petra —

He did not let himself think of Petra yet. He would need all of himself for the hours ahead.

The gallery held perhaps forty people: clerks, company men, a few merchants, and — he saw her only when the murmur of seating died — Amara, seated alone near the back in a plain travelling dress, her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a ferry. She had chosen to come. That frightened him more than the tribunal did. Her presence was a message to everyone watching that the deposed baron's daughter considered this proceeding worth attending, and messages like that could be read as provocation. But when their eyes met she gave him nothing — no nod, no plea, only the level gaze of someone who had already lost nearly everything and had learned to stand inside the loss. He turned away first.

* * *

Vane opened with the charges, and Kestrel listened to his own life read aloud in the flat cadence of bureaucratic indictment: falsification of census records; unauthorized duplication of company documents; disclosure of privileged material to parties hostile to the crown; and, appended almost as afterthought, *conduct unbecoming an officer of the Meridian Trading Company*.

"How do you answer?" Vane asked.

"I don't deny any of it," Kestrel said. "I wish to explain what I denied."

A rustle through the gallery. Orell's mouth tightened. Vane raised one eyebrow a fraction — permission grudgingly granted.

"The census," Kestrel began, "was presented to me as an administrative instrument. Counting souls for tax rolls. I believed that, or told myself I believed it, for eleven weeks." He kept his voice even, the voice he used for reading figures aloud. "It was Director Halloway who showed me its purpose. The questions about kinship and loyalty were filters. Answers were scored. A diver whose grandfather fished the old waters scored one way; a diver whose cousin married into a branded family scored another. Those scores became lists. I have brought the lists."

He lifted the satchel at his feet and set it on the table before him, unhurried, though his pulse hammered in his wrists. "Copies. The originals are with a solicitor in the capital, along with affidavits, in the event anything happens to me between this room and my lodging. I say that not as a threat but as arithmetic. I have simply made sure the numbers balance."

The auditor's pen had stopped moving. Kestrel took note of that the way he once took note of a hesitation in an interview subject's answers — the small tell that meant the hook had caught.

"Director Halloway," Vane said slowly, "is a man of thirty years' distinguished service—"

"Is present today?"

"He is not."

"Then allow me to describe his service." And Kestrel laid it out, document by document, the way he had assembled it in the long sleepless weeks since the docks: the preliminary list dated six days before the census concluded, bearing Halloway's seal, containing names the census had not yet collected; the confiscation inventories cross-referenced against informant reports that traced back, in every case he could prove, to reports Halloway had authored himself under borrowed initials; the arithmetic of seized boats sold below value to buyers whose names recurred with the regularity of tide. "Thirty-one boats," he said. "Forty-one families dispersed or indentured further. Two hundred and six souls reclassified from free subjects to debtors. I counted them myself. Counting is what I am for."

He heard how it sounded — the bitterness in it, the confession folded inside the accusation. *I was good at this. I was made for this.* He did not soften it. Let the tribunal hear what the company built men like him to be.

* * *

They broke at midday, and he was taken not to a cell but to a side room with a window, which he understood as a signal: the outcome was not yet fixed, and signals were being sent in both directions. Bread, cheese, water. He ate half and watched gulls wheel over the harbour, where the Atlas fleet rode at anchor, its seizure order suspended, its fate now knotted to his testimony. Through the glass the ships looked peaceful, white sails furled, hulls dark against the shallows. He thought of Petra aboard the flagship with the forged manifest, her hands steady, and of how narrowly the whole structure of their deception had held until the discarded draft had undone it — one piece of paper, carelessly burned too late. All his life he had believed in the sanctity of records, and here he was, undone by a record. There was a symmetry in that. He almost smiled.

The door opened at the quarter hour. Not a guard. The auditor.

The man helped himself to the other chair without asking and sat looking out the window for a moment, as if they were colleagues sharing a view. Then: "You understand what you're asking the company to admit."

"I'm not asking the company anything. I'm telling you what the documents show."

"The documents show a director exceeding his mandate. They do not show a mandate that permitted what he did." The auditor's voice was mild, conversational, terrible. "There is a difference, Mr. Kestrel, and men have hanged over smaller differences than that."

"And yet the boats are still seized."

"For now."

"That word is doing a great deal of work."

The auditor almost smiled — a movement of muscle without warmth. "I've spent four days with your copies. Cross-checking against capital registries. You are either the most meticulous forger on this coast or the most honest man in the company, and I confess I cannot find the seam. So I will ask you plainly, off the record, and you will answer plainly, and neither of us will ever speak of this conversation." He leaned forward. "Why? Debt relief was on the table. Halloway offered it to you. You threw away a fortune and a career. Men do not do that for strangers. Who is she to you?"

Kestrel held the man's gaze. He thought of the mudflats at low tide, of confessions exchanged like knives handed over handle-first. He thought of a woman teaching herself seals by lamplight, tongue caught between her teeth in concentration, turning forgery into a kind of literacy, literacy into a kind of freedom.

"A colleague," he said. "And a friend. And a better person than anyone in this building. Which you may verify independently — that is, after all, your trade."

The auditor studied him for a long moment, then rose, straightened his coat, and paused at the door. "The lists will be struck. The boats will go to inquiry — some of them. Not all; some sales will be upheld on grounds of good-faith purchase, because upholding some is the price of voiding any. Your post is finished. Your debt stands; relief granted under Halloway's authority is revoked with the rest of his acts." He said it without apology, an accountant closing columns. "For what it is worth, Mr. Kestrel, partial justice is the only kind I have ever seen dispensed. In thirty years. You may consider whether a whole loaf was ever truly on offer."

He left. Kestrel sat with the bread going stale beside him and thought: *a crust, then. But a crust, weighed honestly, is more than nothing.* He had spent his whole life among people who mistook full ledgers for full lives. He was learning subtraction.

* * *

The afternoon session lasted four hours. Orell tried twice to turn the testimony toward the fleet — toward the question of whether the seizure itself had been lawful, as if legality were a weather that happened to men rather than something written by men in rooms — and both times Vane redirected him with the weary skill of a man who had read the auditor's preliminary findings and understood which way the wind now blew. The gallery watched Kestrel as he answered, and he felt their attention change over those hours, cool to warm by imperceptible degrees, the way a crowd's attention always changes when a man declines to save himself.

Near the end, Vane asked the question Kestrel had been waiting for since morning.

"You flagged the household of Amara Serrano-Veyra in your initial census summary. This tribunal has seen the annotation in your hand. Everything that followed — the surveillance, the seizure order, the arrest of Miss Villalobos — traces to that notation. Do you accept responsibility?"

The room went very quiet. Amara, in the back, had gone still as carved wood.

"Yes," Kestrel said. "I wrote it. I had observed a friendship between two women on this coast and recorded it as a matter of civic interest, which is what I was paid to believe it was. I did not know what it would feed. But I know that ignorance of consequences is not innocence of them. I flagged her, and the machine I served did what machines of that kind do." He looked directly at the dais, at all three of them. "I have testified here in the hope of breaking that machine's grip on this coast. I will not insult the tribunal, or the people in this room, by pretending I was not one of its gears."

He heard Amara exhale — a small sound, quickly mastered, but he heard it. Later, outside, in the colonnade where the light had gone amber and the spectators dispersed in murmuring clusters, she stopped beside him. Neither spoke for a moment. Below them, the harbour glittered, indifferent and beautiful.

"My mother kept that ledger for twenty years," Amara said at last, eyes on the water. "She used to say paper remembers what people are made to forget. I thought it was sentiment." A pause. "She would have liked your arithmetic, Mr. Kestrel. Both kinds."

"She'll write when she's settled," Kestrel said carefully. "Across the gulf, letters take time."

Amara's mouth curved — the ghost of a smile, gone as soon as it arrived. "Tell her the boat should sail by spring tides. She'll understand." And she was gone down the steps before he could answer, which he suspected was the point; some debts are discharged best in silence.

* * *

The verdict came the following noon, and it was exactly what the auditor had promised, delivered in Vane's parched voice while Orell stared at the middle distance: the Halloway lists struck from record; the director relieved of office pending criminal referral to the capital — a referral Kestrel knew would dissolve somewhere in the capital's corridors, but a referral nonetheless, a mark that would follow Halloway like a scar; an imperial inquiry constituted to review all seizures executed under the intelligence apparatus, with powers to void titles and restore property. Several boats, the auditor's arithmetic had already suggested, would come home. Not all. Never all.

As for Elias Kestrel: dismissed from the company's service with prejudice. Struck from the rolls. His debt relief revoked; the original debt reinstated in full, plus accrued interest, a figure that would take him the rest of his natural life to discharge at honest wages. No gaol — the auditor had evidently decided that prosecuting the man who broke the case open would look worse than letting him walk away poor — but no future within any institution that mattered, on this coast or any other. Vane pronounced it with the tone of a man reading a menu. Kestrel stood to receive it and found, to his genuine surprise, that he felt lighter with each clause. Post, gone. Pension, gone. Relief, gone. What remained standing in the wreckage of the sentence was strangely intact: his name. Not his reputation — reputations belonged to institutions, and he had none left — but his name, clean, unbought, unowned. For the first time since he had signed his first contract at nineteen, Elias Kestrel belonged to no ledger anywhere.

He walked out through the customs house doors into the hard afternoon light, and the crowd in the square parted around him without parting for him — he was nobody now, officially — and he stood on the top step and breathed the harbour air, tar and brine and fish, and thought: *this is what freedom smells like. It smells exactly like the place that imprisoned us.*

He had lodgings above a chandler's shop, rented with the last of his honest savings, and a table, and a lamp, and a stack of cheap paper. The inquiry would need sworn statements, dozens of them; the divers would need help composing claims in language the commissioners could not twist; there was work, unfashionable and unpaid and entirely his own, waiting for his careful hands. He climbed the stairs two at a time for the first time in months.

On the table, propped against the lamp where the chandler's boy had left it, was an envelope.

Foreign postmark. Gulf crossing. The handwriting slanted rightward, quick and confident — letters learned late and mastered thoroughly, each one a small victory he recognized, because he had watched her win them. He stood holding it for a long moment without opening it, letting himself feel the whole weight of what it meant: that she had landed, that she had found a pen, that she had thought of him across all that water.

Then he broke the seal, unfolded the single sheet, and read the first line.

*"Kestrel — the deed held. The inspectors never looked twice..."*
