## 22. Busy Hands

The monsoon season came and went without ceremony, the way most mercies do. By the time the rains lifted, the Meridian compound had learned a new sound: children's voices reciting the alphabet in the shade of the drying sheds, where the salt-stiffened nets once hung in silent ranks.

Petra stood at the slate board with a stub of chalk worn down to her knuckle and wrote the letter *A* for the eleventh morning running. Behind her, twenty-three children sat on overturned cargo crates, their spines straight with effort, their feet swinging above the ground. Most were divers' children — Sefi's boy, the twins from the family that had lost their boat to Halloway's second confiscation list and gotten it back through the inquiry, the small solemn girl named Idris who had been born the year of the annexation and did not remember any other flag.

"Again," Petra said, and they said it again: *ah*, round-mouthed, uncertain, hopeful.

She had learned her own letters at an age she could not fix precisely, kneeling beside Amara in the baron's crumbling waterfront house while Amara's mother read aloud from shipping manifests and old poetry in equal measure, as though the two were cousins of the same language. She had learned arithmetic from Kestrel's census forms, learned the weight of official ink, learned what a seal could do when pressed into wax by the right hand. Every skill she owned had arrived dressed first as someone else's need.

Now the chalk moved under her fingers and the children copied her, and the sound of twenty-three slates being scratched at once was like rain on a tin roof, like the gulf itself speaking in a smaller, kinder voice.

* * *

Her name had been cleared in the spring. Cleared was the tribunal's word; Petra thought of it differently. A name is not a garment you get back after it has been confiscated and searched. It is something you must reweave, thread by thread, in front of everyone who watched you wear it wrong.

The inquiry had taken testimony through the worst heat of the year. Divers came up from the water and gave their names — some hesitantly, some with their chins set at the angle of people daring the empire to write them down again — and one by one the seized boats came home. There was no ceremony for it either. The boats simply reappeared at their moorings one dawn, as if they had only drifted, and the women who owned them walked down to touch their hulls and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of speech.

Her mother's boat came back on a Tuesday.

It was called the *Salomé*, a pearl boat of the old style, shallow-drafted and sweet-lined, and it had spent eleven years in company hands, repainted twice, renamed once — briefly the *Meridian Grace*, until the clerk responsible had died or transferred, and the paint had peeled back to show her true name underneath like a scar remembering itself. Petra found the foreman waiting at the mooring with the key to the padlock the company had never bothered to use, and when she stepped aboard, the deck gave under her feet the particular way a deck gives when it knows you.

She had not wept at the tribunal. She had not wept when Kestrel was dragged from the docks, nor when the fleet's seizure was halted mid-manifest, nor even at the low-water confession on the mudflats that had cost them both whatever safety they had left. But she sat down in the *Salomé*'s bilge with the brine smell rising around her like a memory of her mother's hands, and she put her forehead against the rib of the hull, and she wept the way water comes through stone: slowly, then all at once.

Sefi found her there an hour later and did not ask. She only handed Petra a rag for the deck and said, "If you're going to cry on a boat, you may as well be working," and that was the whole of the condolence, and it was enough.

* * *

The final year of an indenture moves strangely — each day long as a crossing, the whole of it short as a held breath. Petra rose before the bell, worked the ledgers in the tally house until mid-morning because Director Halloway was gone and someone had to keep the numbers honest and the clerks who remained trusted her more than they trusted each other, and then she taught until the light failed.

Halloway himself had gone east under escort in the autumn, his accounts impounded, his famous arithmetic read aloud at last in a room where it could not flatter him. Petra had not attended. She had heard about it the way she heard about weather, from people coming in off the water, and she had felt — not triumph, not exactly. More like the moment after setting down a load so heavy you had forgotten your own weight. She kept expecting to feel lighter. What she felt instead was the ordinary gravity of days, and she learned, slowly, to love it.

Kestrel came back to the coast in the winter.

He had lost everything the tribunal could take: his post, his debt relief, the imperial seal he had carried against his chest like a second heart. His name survived — that was the bargain he had made, knowingly, in the dock — but a name is thin currency in a colony, and for a season he had lived in the port town doing copying work for merchants too small to care about his history. Then the inquiry's findings opened a schoolhouse question — whose children would learn to read the new registries, if not the divers' own? — and the council of boat-owners, such as it was, had voted to petition for a teacher, and Sefi had said, with the bluntness of a woman who gutted fish for a living, "We have one already. Pay her properly."

So Petra was paid properly, which still astonished her in quiet moments, and Kestrel arrived on the mail packet with two trunks and a face that had decided to stop apologizing for itself.

He taught the older children on alternating days — navigation arithmetic, chart letters, the grammar of manifests — and he was patient in a way Petra had not known him to be during the census years, as though he had finally forgiven himself enough to extend the grace outward. Sometimes in the late afternoon they corrected slates side by side at the same table, shoulders almost touching, and neither of them spoke of the mudflats or the docks or the draft of a forged deed that had nearly hanged them both. It was not avoidance. It was more like two divers surfacing at the same time, checking each other's faces, and descending again into the same shared water.

"You hold the chalk too tight," he told her once, watching her demonstrate the letter *R*. "You always did."

"I hold everything too tight," Petra said. "It's why anything stayed in my hands."

He smiled at the slate instead of at her, which was his way, and wrote the letter *R* again beneath hers, looser, better.

* * *

In February the *Salomé* went out for pearls for the first time in eleven years, crewed by Sefi's family and flying no colors at all, which was the only flag anyone trusted now. Petra stood on the seawall and watched her shrink toward the horizon and understood that she was watching her mother's life resume after a pause longer than her own memory — that the water had kept the appointment all these years, patient as a ledger, and had finally been paid.

She dove herself on weekends. Not for pearl — she had no concession and wanted none — but for the pure work of it, the pressure closing over her ears, the world narrowing to breath and light and the small bright business of her hands. Down there, everyone belonged to the water equally. Down there, no census had ever been taken.

Amara was somewhere across the gulf, in the free ports, rebuilding a trade from a ledger written in her mother's hand. Her letters came rarely, carried by neutral traders, sometimes months between, always brief, never sentimental — the farewell on the trader's deck had taught them both the mercy of brevity — but each one ended the same way: *The fleet is well. The deed holds. Your name is spoken here with respect.* As if respect across an open sea could be folded like cloth and sent. As if it could.

* * *

The letter came on an evening in late spring, with the light going bronze over the harbor and the children already gone home to their suppers.

Petra was washing slates at the rain barrel when the packet boat's runner came up the path — a boy of twelve with wet shoes and a canvas sack — and handed her an envelope heavier than paper had any right to be. The handwriting on it made her stop breathing for a moment: the tall, confident loops she had first seen on shipping manifests in a crumbling waterfront house, the hand that had taught her that language and law were the same country, and that borders could be drawn by anyone bold enough to hold the pen.

She did not open it at the barrel. She carried it up to her loft above the drying shed — her loft, hers by the simple fact that no one else claimed it — and lit the lamp, and sat on the edge of her cot with the envelope on her knees while the harbor bells rang the half hour and the gulls settled into their evening arguments on the roof.

Inside were two things.

The first was a page in Amara's hand, dated six weeks past. It told her, in the unsentimental cadence they had agreed upon, that the pearl fleet had cleared inspection in the free port under the forged deed — that imperial lawyers had examined it, argued over it, and let it stand, because the paper was good and the truth behind the paper was better. That her mother's claim was recognized. That there was work on the far shore for divers who could read, and teachers for divers' children, and a berth on the *Salomé*'s sister ship for anyone the council would release. That the gulf was wide but crossable. That the choice, this time, would be entirely hers — no conscription, no list, no hand on her shoulder telling her where she belonged.

*You were never theirs,* the letter finished. *Come and see what you are.*

The second thing was smaller: a rectangle of stiff paper, and Petra turned it over twice before she understood what she was holding. It was a certificate of completion — the kind trading companies issued to clerks who finished their apprenticeships, the kind Kestrel had once shown her, half-mocking, in the early lessons, saying *this is how the empire says you exist*. Except this one was not the empire's. The seal at its foot was new-drawn, a diving shell inside a circle of rope, and the names along the bottom were the council of boat-owners in their own several hands, and the words in the middle, in Sefi's blunt blocky script dictated to Kestrel's careful pen, read:

*Petra Villalobos has served her indenture entire and free. She belongs to herself. Let no ledger say otherwise.*

Below that, in the space where the company director would have signed, someone had written simply: *The undersigned.*

And beneath that, every name. Sefi. The twins' mother. Old Rassool who had not spoken above a whisper since the second confiscation. Idris's father, who could not write and had made his mark anyway, a firm X pressed hard enough to tear the paper. Twenty names, thirty. Names she had mended nets beside, traded kindnesses with, betrayed nothing to protect. Names that had appeared on Halloway's lists once, marked as suspects, and now appeared on a document that meant the opposite of a list: a witness, signed by everyone.

Petra sat with it in her lap while the lamp burned low and the tide turned outside with its long sighing exhale, and she understood that she was reading the only census that had ever been taken honestly on this coast — not who belonged to whom, but who belonged to each other.

She thought about the far shore. She thought about the berth on the sister ship, and Amara's voice across the water, and the wide gray gull-colored distance that had swallowed her dearest friend and might, if she let it, carry her too. She thought about the children on their crates in the shade of the drying sheds, sounding out their letters like divers sounding for shell, and how someone had once sounded for her, patiently, in the dark.

She was not finished here. That was the answer, and it came without anguish, which surprised her — arrived plain as a tide table. Not yet. There was a final year to finish, and children who needed their letters, and a boat that needed sailing, and a coast that was learning, slowly, painfully, to spell its own name. The gulf would keep. Amara knew how to wait; she had learned it in a crumbling house from a mother who believed in ledgers older than empires. And Petra had learned something in return, these long months of busy hands: that freedom was not a place across the water. It was this — a lamp, a loft, a paper full of names, a morning with work in it that was hers by choice.

But she took out a fresh sheet of the good paper, the heavy stock Kestrel used for charts, and she dipped her pen, and at the top of the page, in tall confident loops borrowed from a girl who had once taught her the alphabet by lamplight, she began:

*Dear Amara —*

*The fleet is well. The deed holds. Your name is spoken here with love.*

Outside, the tide came in over the flats, filling everything it could reach, and in the loft above the drying sheds the lamp burned steady while Petra wrote — not fast, not slow, but exactly at the speed of a woman who finally knew her own hand, and trusted where it would take her.
