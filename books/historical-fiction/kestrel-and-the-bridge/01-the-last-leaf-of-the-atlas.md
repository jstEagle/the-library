## 1. The Last Leaf of the Atlas

My master used to say that a mapmaker is the only honest man in a kingdom of liars, because the river does not take bribes. He said this while taking bribes, occasionally, from surveyors who wanted their parcels drawn slightly larger than God had made them, but Aldous Vane was never a man to let his principles inconvenience his supper. I loved him for it. I love him still, which is a strange tense to use for a dead man, but grief has never shown much respect for grammar.

On the ninth of January, 1747, with the cold pressing against the workshop windows like a creditor, I inked the final plate of the atlas of the Vell River.

I had imagined that moment perhaps four hundred times over the eighteen months of its making, and in none of those imaginings did my hand shake. In my imaginings I laid down the final stroke — the little hatching along the western escarpment at Kestrel Bend, if you must know, the last leaf as it were — and Master Vane had looked over my shoulder and said something dry and perfect, something I would carry in my pocket for the rest of my life like a coin too valuable to spend.

What actually happened was this: my hand shook, the nib spat a blot no larger than a flea directly onto the escarpment, and I said a word our housekeeper Marta would have charged me a penny for. Then I scraped the blot out with a razor, re-inked the line, and sat back in a chair that creaked like an old man climbing stairs, alone in a workshop that smelled of linseed and tallow and the ghost of a man who had been murdered in it eleven weeks before.

*Murdered.* The magistrate's men said *found drowned*, and there is a difference between those words the way there is a difference between a map and the country. Master Vane had fished the Vell for thirty years. He knew every stone in it by its Christian name. Men who know a river that well do not drown in the shallows by the town landing in October, when the Vell is moving slow as syrup and no deeper than a cart axle. But nobody in Vellmark says such things above a whisper, and mostly not even below one.

So: proud. That was the first thing I felt, looking at the finished plate. One hundred and fourteen maps, drawn to a scale fine enough to show a mill wheel turning, covering two hundred miles of the Vell from its source at the Threlkeld fells down to where it widens into estuary mist. Fords marked. Depths sounded. Every crossing, every sandbar, every place where the ice went thin because the current ran quick beneath. It was, I do not doubt, the finest atlas of any river in the northern provinces, and I had made it out of the wreckage of a dead man's notes because it was the only eulogy I knew how to write.

The second thing I felt came later, and I will get to it.

* * *

You should understand something about me, Orsen Dunmore, apprentice mapmaker, nineteen years old, owner of one good coat and several opinions: I believed, in that January, that history happened elsewhere. This is the great luxury of small towns and smaller trades — the conviction that the world's business is conducted in capitals, among men whose coats cost more than your father's barn, and that nothing that happens in those marble rooms could reach down the river valley and touch a boy bent over a copper plate. Wars happened elsewhere. Rebellions happened elsewhere. Murder, certainly, happened elsewhere, except for the single local exception we had all agreed not to examine too closely.

A map encourages this belief. A map flattens the world into civility. On my plates, the Vell flowed in elegant blue serpentine; villages sat in their positions like well-behaved children; roads connected things in sensible lines. Nothing on a map freezes to death or cheats a widow or gets hit over the head and rolled into a slow grey river. A map is the world as God might have made it if He had been in less of a hurry.

Master Vane had known better, I think. There were times he would stand at the big window with his hands clasped behind his back — a posture I learned later meant he was deciding something — and say, "Orsen, what does the map leave out?" And being seventeen and clever, I would answer things like "the weather," or "the fish," and he would laugh in a way that had no floor in it.

"It leaves out *want*," he said once. "Every inch of this river, some man wants, and another man wants to keep him from having it. You draw the water. Remember somebody else is drawing the wanting."

I remembered it the way you remember proverbs, which is to say not at all until the moment you need it.

* * *

The workshop stood behind our house on Tanner's Lane, close enough that Marta could shout through the frost-furred glass when dinner was ready, far enough that my father complained every winter about the coal. My father, Tam Dunmore, is a cooper by trade and a Methodist by temperament, and he had opposed my apprenticeship on the grounds that drawing pictures was not honest work. He had come around eventually, in his fashion, which meant he now complained about the coal rather than the trade. My mother never came around at all; she simply decided her younger son was going to be remarkable and adjusted her conversations with the neighbours accordingly, so that Mrs. Hetch at the bakehouse had heard the phrase "the finest atlas in the northern provinces" no fewer than sixty times before the atlas existed.

That evening I carried the final plate inside to show them, because pride is a fire that wants feeding. My mother held it at arm's length and pronounced it beautiful, which it was. My father squinted at it and asked whether the river really looked like that, and when I said yes, he grunted the grunt that in Dunmore means *approved, pending further review*. Then they went to bed and I took a candle back out to the workshop, because I am that particular kind of fool who cannot let a finished thing lie. I wanted to assemble the whole atlas, folio by folio, and look at it entire — the way you might walk the length of a wall you have built, laying your palm on every stone.

The folios lived in the long cedar chest under the north bench, wrapped in oilcloth against damp. There were nine of them, each holding twelve leaves, plus the working folio — Master Vane's own, older, softer at the corners, carrying the field sketches and soundings from thirty years of tramping the riverbanks in all weathers. His handwriting filled its margins: depths corrected in pencil, a ford here struck out and moved there, notes in his cramped leftward slant. *Re-measure at low water, June.* *Old ferry stake rotted — mark new.* Reading his marginalia was like hearing his voice, and I confess I read them slowly, greedily, the way you drink in a room where someone you miss once stood.

I counted the leaves of the working folio twice, because the first time I assumed I had miscounted.

One hundred and seven. It should have been one hundred and eight.

* * *

Now, a word about counting. An apprentice mapmaker counts the way a miser counts coin; it is bred into the bone. I knew that folio the way a shepherd knows his flock — not just the number but the shape of it, the thickness, the particular drag one leaf had against another where the paper had swelled from a wet August survey in the year I turned fifteen. One leaf missing did not feel like arithmetic. It felt like a tooth gone from my own jaw.

I went through it leaf by leaf, matching each against the fair copies in the other folios. The sequence held. Every sketch accounted for. Except —

Except that between the leaf showing the mills at Wetherholt and the leaf showing the meadows below Cold Harrow, there was a gap where the sequence should have continued downstream. The order ran true everywhere else, mile following mile in patient progression, and here the river simply stopped and resumed, as if a stretch of country had been lifted clean out of the world. Between those two leaves the Vell ran past — I calculated it — some three miles of channel. Three miles including, though I did not yet know why I noticed it, the great horseshoe bend where the Kestrel family had grazed sheep since before anyone kept records, and where the river ran shallow over gravel for half a year and deep enough to float a loaded boat for the other half.

Kestrel Bend.

I sat with the candle guttering and told myself six reasonable things. Papers are moved. Leaves are lent. A folio can be thumbed wrong. Master Vane might have sent a leaf to the engraver, though there was no engraver, because the plates were mine, because he was dead. The candle fluttered. Somewhere out on the Vell the ice boomed — a long deep note, the sound the river makes at night when the cold tightens on it, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Here is what I could not make reasonable, and it is a small thing, so small I nearly leave it out: the cut was clean. If a leaf tears loose, it tears ragged; if it is removed carelessly, fibres trail at the gutter like threads from a wound. This cut was done with a blade and a steady hand. Someone had opened that folio, chosen a leaf, and severed it out with the same patience Master Vane taught me for trimming plate edges — *let the knife do the thinking, Orsen.*

And the last time I had seen that folio whole — I tried to remember, and my memory gave me an evening in early October, Master Vane at the workbench with the folio open, copying something onto a slip of paper with an expression I had never seen on him. I had asked him what he was about and he had closed the folio, which he never did while I was in the room. He said, "Nothing that concerns you yet." Eleven days later they pulled him out of the shallows by the town landing.

I want to be honest with you, since a narrator who lies is worse than useless: that night I did not connect these things. I was cold and tired and grieving, and I folded the folio shut, and I told myself that a missing leaf was a missing leaf, and that in the morning I would search the flat files and the drawer of unmounted proofs and find it mislaid like everything else in a busy shop. I blew out the candle. I walked home across the frozen yard with my collar up, and the stars over Vellmark were hard and brilliant and perfectly indifferent, and I remember thinking — my last comfortable thought, as it turned out, for a long while — that the atlas was finished, and that whatever else winter intended, my part in it was done.

History, I believed, happened elsewhere.

Three days later a courier in a mud-splashed coat came up Tanner's Lane asking for Orsen Dunmore, and he carried a letter under seal, and the seal was broken already, which I did not notice until later either, and the handwriting inside it stopped my heart in my chest because I had seen it a hundred times on folded notes passed across a churchyard wall, in summers when the world was green and simple:

It was Maren Kestrel's.
