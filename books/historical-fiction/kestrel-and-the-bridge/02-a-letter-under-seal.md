## 2. A Letter Under Seal

The courier arrived at half past nine on a Tuesday morning, which I mention only because I had always believed that ruin preferred dramatic hours — midnight, say, or the gray edge of dawn when the candles gutter and a man's courage is at its thinnest. Ruin, it turns out, keeps merchant's hours. It knocked politely. It waited while I wiped my inky fingers on my apron. It even apologized for the cold it brought in with it.

"I've a letter for Dunmore," the courier said. "Dunmore the mapmaker."

"There are three Dunmores in Vellmark," I said, "but only one who smells of iron gall ink. That's me."

He was a government man; I could tell by the boots and by the way he looked at our workshop the way a customs officer looks at a hold — cataloguing, weighing, pricing everything in it for confiscation. Our workshop was not impressive. Master Aldous Vane had kept it for thirty years, and when he died last autumn it passed to me along with his instruments, his debts, his unfinished atlas, and a cough I seemed to have inherited sympathetically from sharing his rooms. There were maps pinned to every wall like moths, the great drawing table scarred with forty years of compass points, and a smell of paper, wax, and linseed that I have never found anywhere else and would know blindfolded in a foreign country.

The courier produced a letter sealed with black wax. Not the Crown's seal, which surprised me then. The wax carried no device at all — just a thumbprint pressed into it while warm, deliberate as a signature.

"Reply is not required," he said. "Acknowledgment is." He had me sign a docket, tipped an imaginary hat, and was gone into the snow, leaving boot prints in our entryway that I stared at far longer than a grown man should stare at boot prints.

I set the letter on the drawing table and did not open it for some minutes. I want to be honest about that, because later — when everyone asked, and everyone asked — I wanted to claim I'd torn into it without fear. I didn't. I made tea first. I sharpened a pen I did not need. I performed all the small ceremonies of a man pretending his morning is still ordinary, because the weight of that letter through its paper was wrong. Letters weigh what they weigh. This one weighed more, the way a verdict weighs more than the parchment it's written on.

Then I broke the seal.

*To Orsen Dunmore, mapmaker of Vellmark, and to Sela and Bram Dunmore, his mother and father.*

*You are named.*

*These are the words you will hear again soon enough, so let them come from me first: that the family Dunmore has conspired with persons unknown in the running of untaxed goods across the Vell River; that your master Aldous Vane died in service of that conspiracy rather than expose it; and that the magistrate Corvus Hale will require your answers before the month is out.*

*I am sorry. Flee if you love them.*

*There is no signature, but you know my hand.*

And there it was — the cruelty of it, the unbearable intimacy of it. I knew the hand. I would have known it drowned, burned, buried under ten years of snow. A slanted, impatient script, letters leaning forward like a runner at a mark, the crossbars of the *t*'s struck hard enough to tear the paper if the writer felt strongly, and Maren Kestrel felt strongly about most things. She had written me four letters in her life, all before she turned sixteen: one apologizing for throwing my best burnisher in the river (she had thrown my best burnisher in the river), one describing a heron's nest in detail so precise I could have mapped it, one that said only *the ice is singing tonight, come hear it*, and one I still kept folded in the back of my waistcoat, whose contents are my own affair.

This was the fifth letter. It was not about herons.

*Flee if you love them.* Written in the hand of a girl I had once — well. Written in Maren Kestrel's hand.

I sat down on Master Vane's stool, the one with the worn seat shaped to a dead man's bones, and I did what any rational person does upon receiving a letter accusing his family of crimes: I decided it was a mistake. A joke, even. Someone imitating her hand. Some clever devil in the taverns cutting quills the way she cut hers. Because the alternative — that Maren Kestrel, whom I had known since we were both small enough to hide under the same fishing cloak during summer storms — that *she* had named my family to the magistrate's attention — that was not a door I intended to open before breakfast.

My mother found me there an hour later, the letter untouched on the table and the tea gone cold beside it.

"You look," Sela Dunmore said, "like your master did the day they told him the survey commission was cancelled." My mother has a talent for comparison that stops just short of comfort. She read the letter twice, standing, her face doing nothing at all, which in Sela is the loudest expression available. Then she set it down flat and smoothed it with two fingers, as if she could press the words back into ink.

"Bram should see this," she said.

"Father will laugh."

"Yes," she said. "That is what worries me."

*

My father laughed. Bram Dunmore ran the ferry at the town landing — or what there was of it in winter, when the ferry became a sled and the river became a road and the whole idea of crossing became, temporarily, everyone's business. He read the letter twice as well, and then he laughed the way he laughed at drunk passengers who insisted the ice would hold their oxen: a short bark, entirely without humor, designed to frighten the thing being laughed at.

"Conspiracy," he said. "Me. I can't conspire with my own knees, Orsen. They won't agree on anything."

"Someone believes it enough to send it."

"Someone sent it unsigned. Cowards write unsigned letters, and magistrates read them, and that's the whole of the trade between those two kinds of people." He handed it back. But I watched his hands afterward, all that day and the next — how they checked knots that were already sound, how they went to the window when hooves passed on the road. My father is not a subtle man, except with fear, where he is among the subtlest I have ever known.

We could agree on nothing else. My mother counseled composure — go about our business, answer honestly if asked, give the world no handle to grip us by. My father counseled the same thing louder. And I counseled nothing, because I spent the better part of three days doing something I am not proud of and cannot regret: I interrogated a dead woman's handwriting.

I laid the letter beside Maren's old notes — she used to leave messages pinned to our door when Master Vane needed river conditions taken, back when she ran errands for anyone who'd pay a girl a copper. I compared the *k*s. The peculiar backward lean of her *r*s when she wrote quickly. The way her *e*s opened wider near the end of a line, as though even her alphabet grew tired of holding things in.

It was hers. Every stroke of it. Either Maren Kestrel had written this letter, or someone had studied her hand with the patience of a forger — and I knew something about forgery, or thought I did, in the abstract way a mapmaker knows about forged maps, which is to say I believed such things happened elsewhere, to other people, in cities where history was said to occur.

Because here is what I believed in those days, sincerely and stupidly: that history happened somewhere else. London had history. The wars had history. The Vell River had water in it, fish, ice, and the occasional smuggler of the romantic fireside sort, but history — real history, the kind that breaks men — belonged to bigger places. Vellmark was the margin of the page. I was a man who drew margins. I genuinely believed the center of things could not reach me, in the way a man standing in a dry field believes rain is a phenomenon of mountains.

The letter was rain. It fell on a Tuesday, at half past nine.

*

On the third evening I walked down to the landing, ostensibly to help my father bank the sled tracks against drift, actually to stand where I could see Kestrel land across the ice — the low white shoulder of the far bank, dark with pine, where the Kestrels had scratched their living for three generations out of whatever the river gave and the tax collectors missed. Smoke stood up from a chimney there, thin and blue in the cold. Somebody was home. Somebody had written my family's name next to the word *conspiracy*, and then written *flee*, and I needed very much to ask her why, and very much did not want to hear any answer that existed.

"Orsen." My father, behind me, without ceremony. He put a hand on my shoulder the way he steadies cargo. "Whatever this is, it isn't yours to solve by standing on ice staring at it."

"That's precisely how I solve things, Father. Standing and staring. It's why Master Vane kept me."

His mouth twitched despite everything. Then it didn't. "Hale's men came by today."

The cold found a new place to live, somewhere under my ribs. "Came by?"

"Asking after the ferry schedule. Asking who crosses, when, carrying what. Friendly-like. The big one — Rennick, the one with the laugh." Everyone in Vellmark knew Rennick's laugh the way sailors know a storm front. "They were friendly," my father repeated, and looked at the river as though it owed him money. "Your mother says pack a bag. Just quietly. Not to run — she'd skin us both — but a man should know where his coat is, these days."

"A bag," I said. "For a letter."

"For a letter," he agreed, "that names us in a plot we never heard of, signed by nobody, delivered by the Crown's own boots." He squeezed my shoulder once, hard, and went back up the landing. That was the whole of his advice. My father's wisdom has always been of the load-bearing kind: not decorative, but you notice it when it's gone.

I did not pack a bag. Instead I went back to the workshop and lit every lamp I owned and sat down in front of Master Vane's folio — the great leather-backed gathering of his working plates for the atlas of the Vell, five years of his life, the thing he'd been carrying the day he died on the river road with his skull broken and his pockets full and everyone in town satisfied that his horse had spooked, because Master Vane was old and horses are unreliable and grief prefers simple arithmetic.

I had finished the final plate that week. That was the terrible neatness of it: the atlas was complete, or nearly, and I had been feeling — God forgive me — *proud*. Proud, with a dead man's stool under me and his name going smaller than mine on the title page.

I turned through the folio slowly, plate by plate, the way you visit a grave. The lower Vell. The middle reaches. The narrows. And then I stopped, because the folio was wrong.

Not the drawings — the drawings were perfect, would be perfect when the sun burned out. The *order* was wrong. Master Vane kept his plates in survey sequence, always, upstream to down, a discipline he'd beaten into me before he ever taught me shading. Between the plates of Miller's Reach and the bend below the old millrace there was a gap in the stitching — a stub of cut paper, sheared close and clean with a blade, where a leaf had once lived and no longer did.

One plate. Cut out. By a blade that knew paper, which meant by Vane himself, or by someone with a cartographer's hands.

I sat looking at that pale stub for a long time. Outside, the wind moved snow across the river ice with a sound like sand poured on a drum. Somewhere across the water a light burned in a window on Kestrel land. Somewhere in town a courier was reporting that acknowledgment had been given, that the Dunmores had received their letter, that they had signed for it like honest men. Somewhere Magistrate Corvus Hale — whom I had seen perhaps a dozen times, a soft-spoken collector of other people's difficulties — was learning that his summons had been, in its way, accepted.

A month. I had a month before I owed answers. I had a cut folio I couldn't explain. I had a letter in a hand I loved, telling me to flee from a crime I hadn't committed, warning me kindly, the way you warn someone you're aiming at.

I am a careful man. I was raised by careful people to do careful work, and the first law of careful work is that you do not touch a document until you understand why it is lying the way it is. So I closed the folio gently, and I put the letter inside my waistcoat where the older one lived, and I blew out the lamps one by one until only the moon on the snow remained, and I thought, with the remarkable calm of a man who has not yet understood his own situation:

*Tomorrow I will go and find out what page is missing.*

I wish I could say I sensed, then, how much that missing page was going to cost. But I stood there in the dark believing, still, that history happened elsewhere — and the ice on the Vell sang that night, long and low under the wind, the way it does when spring is still weeks away and the river is only gathering its strength.
