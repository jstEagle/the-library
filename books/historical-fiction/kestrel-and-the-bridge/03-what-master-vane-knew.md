## 3. What Master Vane Knew

Grief, I have since learned, is a kind of work. It has hours and tasks and a terrible insistence on being done properly. In the days after the letter came, I discovered that the only way to survive an hour was to give it labor — split wood Mother didn't need split, mend pens that didn't need mending, sweep a floor already clean enough to eat off. My father watched me do all this with the weary patience of a man who knows his son is bailing a sinking boat with a thimble and has decided not to say so.

But on the fourth morning after Maren's letter arrived — and I will confess now what I would not confess then, that I had read her handwriting perhaps forty times, holding it to the window light like a jeweler assessing a stone — on that fourth morning I woke with a thought so obvious I was ashamed it had taken four days.

Master Vane had known.

He had known *something*. A man does not hide a folio page from his own atlas unless the page is dangerous. And a man who is careful about pages is careful about other things. Papers. Notes. Correspondence. The small paper archaeology of a life spent measuring the world.

If there were answers in this house — and the letter from Magistrate Hale insisted my family had answers to give — then they would not be in my father's ledgers or my mother's strongbox. They would be in the workshop, under Aldous Vane's dead hand, waiting for someone to read them.

So I went looking. What I found rearranged everything I believed about my master, my town, my trade, and — though I did not know it yet — myself.

* * *

The workshop sat at the back of the property like an apology. It had been built before the house, by some earlier tenant, and when Master Vane bought the plot he'd kept it rather than pull it down, saying that a building which had survived forty Vellmark winters deserved better than demolition. It was a low room, half cellar really, dug into the slope so that its north-facing windows sat at ground level and caught nothing but grey reflected light for most of the day. Perfect for cartography, he used to say. The sun lies about color; the north sky doesn't.

I unlocked the door with the key I still wore on a cord around my neck. He had given it to me two years ago, along with the title of apprentice, and had said only: *You'll want this when I'm not here to be annoying.* I had assumed he meant his tools. His tools were worth more than the house.

Inside, everything was as he left it on the last morning of his life. This was not sentiment on anyone's part; it was simply that no one else knew what anything was for. My mother had come in once, a week after the funeral, stood in the doorway, and said, "I can't clean a room full of ghosts," and left. So the room kept him. His smock hung on its peg with the collar turned up where he always turned it against the draught. His tea cup sat by the lamp stand with a fossil of tea at the bottom. His good magnifier lay in the drawer where it lived, wrapped in chamois, because he never trusted me not to scratch it, and he had been right not to.

I lit the lamp and stood a moment just breathing the smell of the place — ink and linseed oil and damp stone and, underneath it all, the particular mustiness of old paper, which is the smell of time itself if time had been pressed flat and dried. I had spent six years breathing that air. It smelled like being a boy.

Then I began.

I started where any sensible searcher starts: his desk, his letter box, the locked drawers of the plan chest. All empty, or near enough. Either he had kept nothing, which was impossible — Aldous Vane kept receipts for receipts — or someone had gotten here before me. That thought put a cold finger on the back of my neck. Hale's men had come after the murder, of course, official-like, poking through the front rooms and asking my father questions in the tone of men who have already written their conclusions. But they had not struck me as the sort to be thorough in a workshop full of instruments whose names they didn't know. To them it would all look like junk. Calipers are junk if you don't know what they measure.

Still, the desk gave me nothing but dust and one dried apple core, which told me either the searchers were fools or my master was cleverer than they were, and I knew which way I'd have bet.

I turned to the shelves instead, and this is where the morning changed character. You cannot spend six years grinding ink and burnishing vellum for a man without absorbing his habits the way your clothes absorb smoke. And one of Aldous Vane's habits — a small madness, we had all teased him about it — was that he never trusted a wall. Plaster, he said, was a liar's best friend. Any wall can hold a secret; that is what walls are *for*, in his view, and he had grown up in a border town where the difference between a smuggler's cellar and a magistrate's was often just a question of masonry.

So I began tapping the walls. I felt like a fool. The lamp threw my shadow huge against the shelving, and every hollow knock sounded to me like an accusation. Panel by panel, shelf by shelf, working from the corner outward, the way he'd taught me to survey a field — establish your baseline, then walk the perimeter before you trust the middle.

The third panel past the drying rack sounded wrong.

Not hollow, exactly. Hollow is common in old buildings. Wrong — a thinner, tinnier note, the sound of a space with something in it. Or possibly I imagined that part, and the truth is simpler: I wanted so badly to find something that my ear obliged me. Grief does that. It makes you hear voices in weather. But I set down the lamp, worked my thumbnail into the seam of the panel, and pulled, and the board came away on a leather hinge so well made I'd have missed it entirely if I hadn't been looking.

Behind it, in a cavity between the studs, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the plain twine he used for survey parcels, was a bundle of papers.

* * *

I did not open them immediately. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger heaven keeps of my conduct: I sat down on his stool, with the bundle on my knees, and for a long moment I simply could not. Because as long as those papers stayed wrapped, Aldous Vane was still only a man who had died in an accident — a fall on the ice, a bad night, bad luck. Terrible, but a thing that happens to people. The world where my master was murdered was a different world, and unwrapping this bundle was the door into it, and my hands shook, and I am not ashamed of that.

Then I remembered the cut page in the folio. The clean knife-line where a map of the Vell River should have been. And I remembered that accidents do not remove pages from atlases.

I untied the twine.

There were three things inside. The first was a memorandum book, cheap paper, soft-cornered, the kind he carried on survey. The second was a letter, folded but unsealed, addressed to no one — or rather, begun to someone and abandoned, the salutation scraped out. The third was a map. Not a finished plate but a working draft, pencil over ink, corrections crowding the margins, and it took me only a heartbeat to recognize the stretch of river it depicted, because I had spent the last month inking its twin onto the final plate of the atlas.

Kestrel Bend.

I read the memorandum first, because it was dated earliest, and because reading his handwriting again was like hearing his voice, and I was weak enough to want that even if the voice was going to tell me something terrible.

It was survey notes, at first. Soundings, depths, seasonal marks. Ordinary work. Then, midway through, the entries changed. They stopped being measurements and started being observations, and the observations had a direction. *Ford below the bend usable at low water Sept–Oct, cart width, approaches via the alder stand — NOT marked on county map.* Another: *Second crossing above the millrace, gravel bar shifts yearly, locals use it, excise does not.* Another, later, in a tighter hand, the pen pressed hard: *Asked directly why these crossings appear nowhere in the Crown surveys. Told it is not my concern. It is precisely my concern. An atlas that omits half the river is not an atlas; it is an advertisement for whoever holds the omissions.*

I sat back on the stool. Outside, the wind moved snow across the window at ground level, a white breath against the grey.

He had found the fords. Of course he had — you cannot survey a river honestly without finding where it can be crossed, and the Vell was crossed in more places than any official map admitted. That was the whole dirty arithmetic of the border: the Crown maps showed bridges, because bridges are taxed and gated and watched. The fords went unmapped. And an unmapped crossing belongs to whoever uses it, and what gets carried across unmapped crossings in a famine winter is grain, and salt, and letters, and everything else the law had decided the poor could do without.

My master, with his obsession that an atlas should tell the truth, had mapped the truth. Every ford. Every approach. Every season and depth.

And then the entries darkened further.

*Visitor today, well-dressed, claims to represent interests in Halden city. Offers generous sum for "revision" of the atlas before printing. Revision meaning removal. I declined. He smiled as though I had haggled well.*

*A second offer. Doubled. Declined with less courtesy.*

*I begin to understand what I have made. It is not a map of the river. It is a list of everyone's sins and doors. In the right hands it hangs smugglers. In other hands it protects them. There are no innocent hands large enough to hold it.*

And then the last entry, undated, the writing hurried, the pen torn through the paper in two places:

*I have refused a third time. The gentleman was no longer smiling. If anything happens to me, the plate is safe. Let whoever finds this understand: I did not fall. I was removed, like a page.*

I put the book down because my hands were not steady, and I looked at the draft map of Kestrel Bend for a long time without seeing it, and what I felt — I record this honestly — was not grief anymore. Grief is heavy and formless, water in the lungs. This was different. This had edges. This had a *direction*.

Anger. Clean, cold, articulate anger, the first useful emotion I had been offered in weeks.

They had killed him. Smiling gentlemen in well-brushed coats, or men hired by them, had ended the finest mind I ever knew on an ice patch he'd walked safely ten thousand times, because he wouldn't lie about where a river was shallow. And then — the elegance of it, the sheer bureaucratic filth of it — they had let his death stand as accident while they hunted for the atlas, and when they couldn't find it, they had invented a conspiracy to hang on my family until the atlas surfaced.

The letter was next. The unfinished one, the scraped salutation. It was short. He had written it the same night as the last memorandum entry, I'm sure of it — the ink matched, the hand equally hurried.

*I write to you because you are the only person I trust to be both honest and brave, and I know those two qualities rarely share a house. If you are reading this, I did not survive the week. The atlas must not reach the magistrate complete, nor must it be destroyed — destroyed it becomes rumor, and rumors can be forged. It must exist, whole and true, in hands that will use it justly. I have cut the Kestrel Bend plate from the folio and hidden it—*

And there it stopped. Mid-sentence. The rest of the leaf was blank.

He had hidden the plate. Somewhere. The missing page from the folio — the true chart of Kestrel Bend, the ford the gang used, the ford the smugglers' innocence and Hale's forgery both depended on — existed, separate, concealed, and Aldous Vane had died before writing down where.

Or died before writing down where *legibly*, a sour voice in me added, because I knew my master. Six years of watching him work had taught me that Aldous Vane never left one lock without hiding another key behind it, for pleasure, on principle, or simply to keep his own wits sharpened against boredom. The answer would be hidden. Probably behind another puzzle. Possibly behind several. He had once encoded his grocery list as a joke and then forgotten the cipher and sulked for two days.

I laughed. Actually laughed, alone in the cold workshop, sitting among a dead man's secrets — a short, cracked sound that turned into something else halfway through and took me a minute to master. That is grief too. Nobody warns you about the laughing.

But underneath the laughing, the anger had settled into something with more spine. Purpose, I suppose you'd call it, though at the time it felt less like a noble resolve than like a load-bearing wall discovering it is one. I had spent my life believing history happened elsewhere — in capitals, in wars, to important people — and that my job was merely to describe the world accurately for people who acted in it. Now the description *was* the action. The atlas was not a book. It was a weapon, and an indictment, and my master's testimony delivered posthumously, and I was the only living soul who knew how to read all of it.

Inheritance and indictment. Both at once. He had left me his trade and his enemies in the same parcel.

* * *

I worked until the lamp burned low and daylight came up grey through the ground-level glass, and by then I had done three things.

First, I copied the memorandum entire into cipher — our old apprentice cipher, the one he'd taught me with the grocery list — so that if the originals vanished, the words would survive somewhere. Second, I returned every paper to its cavity exactly as found, re-seated the panel, and swept my footprints from the dust of the floor, feeling absurd and conspiratorial and, I am sorry to report, rather enjoying it. Third, I sat down at the workbench with the fair copy of the atlas and studied the gap where the Kestrel Bend plate should have been, and forced myself to think like Aldous Vane instead of like a grieving boy.

Where would he hide a single plate?

Not in the workshop — too obvious, and he'd hidden the notes here already; he wouldn't stack his treasures in one drawer. Not at his lodgings — searched long ago. He would hide it somewhere connected to the plate itself. Somewhere the map pointed to. That was how his mind worked: the key hidden in the territory. *A map should lead you somewhere,* he used to say. *Otherwise it's wallpaper.*

I spread the neighboring plates side by side — the Bend's upstream and downstream neighbors — and looked at what the missing page was framed by. Upstream plate ended at the old quarry steps. Downstream began at the millrace. Between them, the alder stand, the shingle bar, the drop to the ford.

And then I remembered — with a jolt so physical I gripped the bench — that the atlas had a dedication page. Unfinished. One line in his hand, added late, that I had taken for sentiment:

*For M., who taught me that rivers remember.*

Maren. Who swam the Bend every summer of her childhood. Whose family had fished that water for three generations. Who knew every stone in that river by the feel of it underfoot.

He had met her. He had talked to her. And I suddenly understood that the missing plate might not be hidden in a place at all. It might be hidden with a person — entrusted, like these notes, to whoever would use it justly.

I blew out the lamp and climbed the stairs to the kitchen, where my mother was setting bread on the table, and my father sat with the magistrates' letter open in front of him for the hundredth time, wearing the expression of a man reading his own sentence in a language he almost speaks.

"Father," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, "how soon can you get word to anyone who knows the Kestrels?"

He looked up. And across the table our eyes met, and I watched him understand — in the way of parents, instantly and completely — that his son had opened a door that could not be closed, and that the house was now standing in a draft coming straight off the river.

"Why," he said carefully, "do you ask?"

"Because," I said, "I believe Maren Kestrel is holding the one piece of proof that can save us. And I believe she may not know she's holding it."

What I did not say — what I was only beginning to admit to myself as the wind rose outside, rattling the shutter like a polite knock — was that the letter in my coat pocket had named a deadline of one month, and that the ice on the Vell River had already begun, very quietly, in the deep places where nobody watches, to break.
