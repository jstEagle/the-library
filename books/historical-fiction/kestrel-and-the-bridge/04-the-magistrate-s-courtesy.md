## 4. The Magistrate's Courtesy

There is an art to ruining a family, and I have practiced it long enough to know that the brushwork matters more than the paint. Any fool can drag a man from his bed and put him in irons. The result is a martyr, and martyrs breed questions. But a man who *confesses* — a man who weeps at his own table while his wife holds his hand and his son watches from the doorway with the particular gray horror of the newly disabused — that man breeds nothing at all. He is finished quietly, like a candle pinched between fingers, and the town goes back to its bread and its grievances, and I go back to mine.

So on the fourth morning of February, in the year of our Lord 1747, I did not send soldiers to the Dunmore house. I sent my carriage.

It is remarkable what a carriage does. A squad of redcoats says *you are suspected*; a carriage with lacquered doors says *you are already convicted, but we are being civilized about it*. Tam Dunmore stood in his doorway holding a chisel he had no idea what to do with, saw the crest on the panel, and aged five years before my driver had finished setting the step. Good. Fear is a currency and I prefer to arrive with my purse full.

"Magistrate Hale," I said, removing my hat as though the cold were the only imposition. "I hope I find your household well."

"You find us — " He looked at the chisel, set it down on the sill with enormous care, as if it might go off. "You find us at our work, sir."

"Work is a blessing," I agreed. "May I come in? The wind has teeth this morning."

He stepped aside. They always do.

* * *

The Dunmore house smelled of glue and lamp oil and boiled turnips, which is to say it smelled of honest poverty, the kind that pays its taxes late and its debts never but harms no one. I have always found honest poverty slightly offensive, the way one finds a badly painted room offensive — not because it injures, but because it insists on being looked at.

The wife, Sela, met me in the common room with her hands folded and her chin at an angle I recognized. I have seen that chin on women whose husbands are about to be ruined and who have decided, in advance, that they will not be ruined with them. It is a fine quality in a woman and an inconvenient one in a witness.

"Magistrate," she said. "We've tea."

"I'm sure it's excellent." I sat in the chair they offered me by the fire, unhurried, letting the room learn my weight. "Mrs. Dunmore. Master Dunmore. I will come to the point, because I respect you too much to do otherwise." This was a lie so smooth it hardly qualified as one; lies should fit the mouth like a well-worn glove, and this one did. "A matter has arisen concerning certain goods that entered this province last autumn without paying His Majesty's duty."

Tam's face did the thing faces do: the flicker of incomprehension, then the arithmetic, then the fear arriving in full, like weather. "Goods, sir? We're mapmakers. My brother's son is an apprentice to — was an apprentice to Aldous Vane, God rest him. We don't deal in goods."

"No," I said pleasantly. "You deal in maps. That is rather the heart of it."

I let that sit. Silence is a tool most men misuse; they wield it like an axe when it wants handling like a knife, slipped in slowly and turned. I counted four heartbeats of the clock on their mantel — a cheap thing, painted flowers, half the numbers worn off — before Sela said, evenly:

"What heart of it, Magistrate?"

"The atlas," I said. "Vane's atlas of the Vell. A beautiful work, I'm told. Rivers, crossings, depths. The kind of document that, in the wrong hands, tells a man where the King's revenue officers cannot easily go. Where the river is shallow enough to walk a cargo across at night, for instance. Where the watch can't ride. Where a barge might lie up unseen." I smiled at Tam. "You'll know all this better than I. It's your trade."

"We never — Aldous would never — "

"Aldous Vane is dead," I said, with the mild regret of a man remarking on bad roads. "Drowned, they say, or fell through the ice, or was set upon by persons unknown. The coroner's jury was admirably brief. Dead men sign no depositions, Master Dunmore, which is why I am obliged to speak with the living. And the living, in this instance, are you, your wife, and a young man named Orsen who completed that atlas after his master's death. Completed it beautifully, I understand. In time for spring."

I watched the boy's name land on them like a stone on thin ice. There it is, I thought. Every family has its keystone. Remove it gently, and the arch comes down all the same.

"Orsen has done nothing," Tam said, and his voice cracked on the boy's name, and I loved him a little for it. Guilty men protect themselves. Innocent men protect their children, and innocence so displayed is a gift to a prosecutor.

"I'm delighted to hear it," I said. "Then he'll have nothing to worry about."

* * *

Sela poured the tea. I drank it. It was, as predicted, excellent, and I told her so, and she received the compliment the way one receives a coin from a pickpocket's hand — watching the other hand the whole time.

"Now," I said, setting down the cup, "to demonstrate the seriousness with which the Crown regards this matter — and to spare us all a tedious winter of subpoenas — I've brought something for your inspection."

My clerk, Wexford, a man with the face of a disappointed ferret and the discretion of a grave, brought the folio in from the carriage and laid it on their table. I opened it with two fingers, tenderly, the way one handles a relic.

"An atlas," I said. "Of the Vell River. In the hand of Aldous Vane — or very nearly in it. Remarkable, isn't it, how faithfully a copyist can reproduce another man's line? The plate margins, the little flourishes on the north arrows. Almost an act of love." I turned pages while the family watched. "Here is Vellmark. Here is the mill reach. And here—" I stopped, and tapped the page, and looked up at Orsen's mother with an expression of gentle sorrow— "here is Kestrel Bend, marked with a ford that does not appear in any survey the Crown ever commissioned. Depth of three feet at low water, says the note. Gravel bottom. Suitable for loaded drays. How useful such a ford would be. To smugglers, say. To the Kestrel gang, say. And how very unfortunate that the letter now lodged in my office — sealed, witnessed, and sworn — names the Dunmore family as parties to the traffic that ford serves."

"That is a lie," Sela said. Not loudly. That was the trouble with her; she didn't say things loudly, she said them *flatly*, the way a level says flat, so that you felt the floor shift under your own feet.

"Almost certainly," I agreed, and watched all three of them — Tam, Sela, and the daughter, who had been hovering in the stairwell pretending to spin and doing it badly — flinch as one body. "And yet here it sits. Sworn. Sealed. Bearing names. The Crown does not require the truth of me, Mrs. Dunmore; it requires results. A smuggling route closed. Principals named. An example made. I confess I would prefer the example to be made of someone who deserves it, and I am prepared — genuinely prepared, this is not rhetoric — to believe that person is not you."

I closed the folio and rested my hand upon it, palm flat, the way a priest rests a hand on a Bible.

"But belief is not evidence, and evidence is not yet assembled, and assembling it is such *slow* work. Witnesses must be found. Ledgers subpoenaed. The atlas compared, stroke by stroke, against whatever original exists in your late master's workshop — assuming the original still exists, assuming no page has gone missing, assuming young Orsen's additions are what he claims them to be. Months, perhaps. And meanwhile the winter grinds on, and grain grows dear, and the assizes sit in April, and juries in April, Mrs. Dunmore, are hungry things. They want someone fed to them. I cannot always choose the dish. I can only choose the seasoning."

Tam had gone the color of tallow. "What is it you want from us?"

Such a good question. Such a *relief* when they ask it. I leaned back and gave him the whole of it, plainly, because plainness at the right moment is more terrifying than any threat.

"A confession. Narrowly drawn. That you received smuggled goods knowing their nature — once, in the famine winter, out of necessity, under pressure of hunger, repentant since. Names withheld. Circumstances mitigated. The Crown shows mercy to the contrite; the fine will ruin you, but ruin is survivable, Master Dunmore. Hanging is not. And the boy — Orsen — need never appear in it at all. His apprenticeship, his atlas, his good name: preserved. All of it, in exchange for a paragraph in your own hand."

"And if we don't," Sela said. Not a question, quite. A door held open to see what I'd do with it.

"If you don't," I said, rising, retrieving my hat, settling my coat with the unhurried care of a man who has all the time in the world because all the time in the world is precisely what he has arranged to have, "then the machinery runs. The letter stands. The atlas is examined. Witnesses come forward — witnesses always come forward, Mrs. Dunmore; it is astonishing how many people remember things once there is a fee attached to remembering — and in April the assizes are given their dish. Perhaps it is you. Perhaps your husband. Perhaps the boy, who at seventeen — is he seventeen? — would make, I am told by those who consider such matters, a very affecting spectacle at the gallows. Youth condemned unjustly stirs people. It stirs them to *pardon*, sometimes, years later, when it no longer helps anyone."

I paused at the door and gave them my warmest look, the one my mother used to call my church face.

"You have until the ice breaks. Consider it my courtesy. Most magistrates wouldn't grant the season."

* * *

In the carriage, rolling back through the dirty snow toward the courthouse, Wexford cleared his throat. He has one question per annum and spends it wisely.

"They'll run," he said. "The boy, at least. They'll warn him and he'll run."

"Of course they'll warn him," I said. "That is the intention."

I watched Vellmark slide past the window — the crooked roofs, the smoke standing straight up in the cold, the river lying white and patient between its banks like a road waiting for travelers. Somewhere across that white plain, in some camp I could not yet find, rode the actual smugglers, the actual witnesses, the girl whose handwriting graced the letter in my office with a slanting, defiant hand I had enjoyed reading twice. And somewhere in a workshop smelling of glue and grief sat the true atlas, with its true fords, its true depths — the one document in this province that could hang me as neatly as I intended to hang others.

The forged folio was good. I had paid for good. But a forgery is a mirror, and mirrors show you only what stands in front of them; somewhere behind me, in that workshop, stood the original, and the boy who had inked it.

Let him run, I thought, and almost smiled. Running men leave tracks. And I hold the only map worth having.

"Drive on," I said.
