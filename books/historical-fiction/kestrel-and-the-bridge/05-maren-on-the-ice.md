## 5. Maren on the Ice

The letter had been sitting on my desk for three days, and by the third day I had developed a theory about it: that if I refused to look at it directly, it would lose its power, the way a star does when you look at it sideways. This theory was wrong. The letter knew it was wrong. The letter sat there with its black seal and its neat, terrible handwriting and waited for me to be honest with myself, which is the one thing letters and magistrates and I have never managed.

So on the fourth morning I did what I always do when my mind will not hold still. I went walking.

Vellmark in February is a town that has given up on conversation. The river freezes hard enough by midwinter that the Crown road runs straight across it — the ferryman winters in his cousin's attic and drinks at the Dunmore expense of nobody, because he owns nothing worth taxing — and the ice stretches from bank to bank like a sheet of grey paper someone forgot to write on. I had been drawing that paper since I was nine years old. I knew every crack in it. There was the long seam below Miller's Stair where the current ran deep and the ice grew blue and thick as church glass. There was the bad stretch near the reed beds where the water moved underneath all winter and the surface wore a skin of white lies. Every apprentice mapmaker learns the river twice: once with his eyes, and once with his boots, and only the second lesson sticks.

I walked out past the ferry stairs and onto the ice with my collar up and my breath hanging around me like a debt, and I told myself I was only clearing my head. I was not only clearing my head. Somewhere in the back of my skull, where the honest thoughts live, I had already done the arithmetic: Maren Kestrel had left Vellmark four years ago, and the letter was in Maren Kestrel's hand, and the Kestrel gang rode the high country on the far side of the Vell, and none of those three facts could be true together unless something had happened to the girl I remembered, something I could not imagine and therefore refused to.

I was halfway across the river, standing over the blue seam at Miller's Stair, when I heard the horse.

You do not expect a horse on the river. Ice will carry a man easily, two men, a sledge loaded with salt pork. A galloping horse concentrates everything it has onto four small iron shoes, and the ice knows it, and the sound it makes is not hoofbeats so much as argument — a rapid, cracking dispute between weight and weather. I turned, and coming down the west bank with the morning sun behind her, riding that argument like she'd invented it, was a rider in a grey wool coat cut for the saddle, a scarf across the lower half of her face, and a carbine slung along her thigh with the casualness of a woman who had stopped thinking of it as a weapon years ago.

Every sensible nerve I owned said *run*. But you cannot run on river ice; you can only fall on it with dignity or without, and I had left dignity at the ferry stairs. So I stood still, which I have since learned is my answer to most things, and watched the rider come out onto the ice and slow, and walk the last fifty yards toward me at a pace that let me see her eyes above the scarf.

Grey eyes. The color of the river in November. Four years, and I would have known them in the dark.

"Hello, Orsen," said Maren Kestrel.

"Maren," I said. And then, because my mouth has never once waited for my brain, "You've got a horse."

"Remarkable observation. Four years apprenticed to a mapmaker and he can still find north." She swung down and stood beside the horse, one hand resting on its neck, and pulled the scarf down. She was thinner than I remembered, and harder, the way driftwood is harder than the tree it came from. There was a scar through her left eyebrow, new since the summer we were seventeen, and a pistol at her belt that was not decorative. "You shouldn't be out here."

"I shouldn't be out here," I repeated. "That's an interesting thing to say to a man on his own river."

"It's not your river. It's the King's river. That's rather the whole problem." She glanced upstream, then downstream, with the quick professional sweep of someone counting sightlines, and I understood that she had not come to this place by accident any more than I had. "I need to know if you read the letter."

"You know I read the letter. You wrote the letter."

"I know what I wrote. I don't know what you read." Something moved behind her eyes — not guilt exactly, but its cousin, the one that looks like guilt from a distance. "What did you think, Orsen? When you saw the seal."

"What did I think." I heard my own voice go flat and cold, and was vaguely impressed; I hadn't known I could do that. "I thought my family would hang. I thought about Corvus Hale's face when he says *within the month*, the way he says it, like a man reading a recipe he's made before. I thought about my mother's hands. And then I thought — the handwriting. Because I'd know your handwriting anywhere, Maren. You used to write your *k*s like little flags. You still do." I took the letter from inside my coat, and I confess my hand shook, but I held it out. "So after the terror came the arithmetic. And the arithmetic doesn't work. The girl I knew didn't sell a family to a magistrate. So either I'm wrong about the girl, or there's something in this I can't see yet. I've spent four days trying to decide which."

For a long moment she didn't take the letter. The wind came down the river valley and pushed her horse sideways a step, and she quieted it without looking at it, the way you'd quiet a child.

"There's a third thing," she said. "That it's exactly what it looks like and also a lie. Those aren't opposites, Orsen. Out here they're usually the same document." She took it, finally, turned it over once, and handed it back. "I wrote it. I'll not insult you by denying it. I wrote it because I was told to write it, by people who hold my brother's life the way Hale holds yours, and because the words were true enough to sign."

"True enough to—"

"The names are real. The conspiracy isn't." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice, though there was no living soul within a mile of us but the crows. "Listen to me now, because I'll only say this once, and then I'm going to ride away and pretend this meeting never froze over. The letter was never meant to hang you. It was meant to *move* you. Do you understand the difference? A letter that hangs a family brings the whole town out with lanterns and dogs. A letter that frightens a clever boy makes him run — quietly, alone, at night, across the river. There's a ford three miles upstream that isn't on any map the Crown owns. We were watching it every night for nine days waiting for you to come through it."

The river cracked somewhere under our feet, a long groan like a door in an empty house, and neither of us moved.

"You wanted me to run," I said slowly, "to the outlaws."

"I wanted you to run *somewhere*, anywhere, before Hale finished whatever he's building against you. Running to us was Da's idea—" she stopped herself on the word, and I watched her decide whether to keep going, and she kept going, because whatever else Maren Kestrel had become in four years, she had not become a liar in the small ways. "It was Tam's idea. He remembers you kindly. I argued against it. I said Orsen Dunmore doesn't run, he stands in doorways and corrects people's grammar until they give up. I appear to have lost the argument in both directions, because instead of running you walked out onto the middle of a frozen river at dawn like a man auditioning to be a monument."

"And instead of staying away you came to meet me. Also auditioning, apparently."

"Yes, well." For one moment — one — the hardness slipped, and under it was the girl from the boathouse summers, the one who could name every bird on the Vell and laughed at my compass because it always pointed at her. "Old habits. I promised someone once I'd stand where he could find me."

My chest did something complicated and unhelpful, and I put it away, because the carbine was still slung on her thigh and the letter was still in my coat and neither of those facts cared about boathouses.

"If the letter was meant to save me," I said, "then explain the rest of it. Explain why the gang's name is on a smuggling indictment at all. Explain why the indictment reads like it was written by someone who's *read* an atlas of the river crossings. Because it does, Maren. Whoever drew up that charge knows the Vell the way I know the Vell — every ford, every seam, every place the ice lies. And there are perhaps three people alive who know it that well. One of them is dead. One of them is standing in front of me with a horse. And the third is Magistrate Corvus Hale, who has recently acquired, by means I'd very much like to hear explained, what appears to be a copy of Master Vane's atlas."

I had not meant to say the last part. It had been sitting in me for three days too, and it came out the way confessions do — all at once, and aimed at the wrong person.

Maren went very still. Horses notice these things before riders do; hers shifted, and she pressed it quiet again.

"A copy," she said. "Of Vane's atlas."

"So you've heard of it."

"Orsen." Her voice had changed entirely. The wryness was gone, the summer was gone; what was left was flat and fast and frightened, and it frightened me worse than the pistol had. "Where is the atlas now?"

"In my workshop. Under the floor where Master Vane kept the good paper. All except one plate." I hesitated, and then gave her the thing that had been keeping me awake even more than the letter. "One folio page is cut out. Cleanly, with a knife, before he died. I found it the night I inked the last leaf. I told myself he'd removed it for recopying. I've been telling myself that for eleven days."

"How many plates in the atlas?"

"Thirty-one. Thirty now."

"And do they include," she said, each word placed like a foot on bad ice, "the fords?"

"That's rather the point of an atlas of a river, yes."

"Not the ferry landings. Not the fords a cart drives through in August with a tax receipt in his pocket. I mean the *other* ones. The ones that run in February. The ones that fed this valley through the famine winter while the granaries stayed locked and the grain went downriver to buyers with Hale's stamp on the manifests." She was close enough now that I could see her breath shaking, which her voice was not. "Your master mapped the river honestly. Every crossing, including ours. Do you understand what that document is, Orsen? To Hale it's not a book. It's a noose with thirty pages in it. Every honest ford on Vane's map proves our routes were open, known, and *ordinary* — that half this valley used them, that the crossings predate any smuggling law by a hundred years. And every ford that's *wrong* on Hale's copy proves the copy is forged. Your atlas is the one thing in this county that can hang Hale instead of us. Where is the missing page?"

"I don't know."

"Guess."

"Maren—"

"*Guess.*"

And because I had, of course, been guessing for eleven days — because guessing is what mapmakers do when the ground refuses to match the paper — I heard myself say it out loud for the first time. "Kestrel Bend. If a man wanted to make your gang look like conspirators instead of smugglers, he'd need the one crossing that can't be explained away as common use. The deep-water ford at Kestrel Bend only runs in hard frost and only your people ever crossed it. If Vane's true plate shows the Bend fordable, it proves your winter routes. If it's missing from the original and *present* in Hale's copy, drawn wrong—" I stopped. The wind filled in the sentence for both of us.

"—drawn wrong," Maren finished quietly, "then whoever holds Hale's copy can prove the Kestrels crossed where no one could cross. Which is how you turn smugglers into traitors. Smuggling gets you fined. Treason gets you trees." She looked downstream, toward the Bend itself, a dark fold in the river two miles off, and I watched her do sums of her own, and I did not like the total. "Your master wouldn't sell his maps. Everyone knew that; it's why we trusted them. So someone took the page they needed and killed him for the rest."

"They hanged a poacher for that," I said. "In November. In nine days, on Hale's warrant. A poacher who couldn't have found Aldous Vane's house with a guide and a dog."

"Yes," Maren said. "We know. We've known since November, Orsen. Why do you think we're declared enemies of the Crown and not common thieves? You don't declare war over stolen brandy." She turned back to me, and now the tenderness and the suspicion were both fully present in her face at once, and I understood that they were not colliding at all, that they had simply learned to live in the same room. "Here is what happens next. You go home. You put that atlas where no magistrate's man will ever find it, and then you look at what Vane left you, because Aldous Vane was the most careful man in this county and careful men leave instructions. Whatever's in that workshop — the notes, the proofs, the cut page — it's the only testimony that can't be bought or hanged. And Orsen — *do not* come looking for me. Not across the ford, not through the high country, nowhere. If Hale's people see you so much as glance west, the letter stops being a warning and becomes evidence, and I can't—"

She stopped. Four years of hard country, and what defeated her was the end of a sentence.

"You can't protect me," I said. "I remember the arrangement. It was supposed to run the other way."

"The arrangement," she said, swinging up into the saddle, pulling the scarf back across her face, "was a promise made in peacetime by two idiots on a half-built bridge. Nobody's been at peace since. Go home, Orsen. Count your pages. And when you find what Vane hid — and you will find it, because you're exactly the kind of stubborn he liked — ask yourself one question before you show it to anyone. Ask yourself who else has read it, and what they'd pay to make it disappear."

She turned the horse upstream. Then, at the last, she looked back down at me, and for a heartbeat the grey eyes were just grey eyes, and she said, almost gently:

"The ice at Miller's Stair is safe to the twenty-eighth. After that, trust nothing flat and white. Not the river, and not anything written on it."

Then she was gone, west along the bank at that same cracking trot, and I stood alone in the middle of the frozen Vell with the sun climbing and the letter in my coat, doing the only thing I knew how to do with a world that had stopped matching its map.

I started counting.

Thirty-one plates. Thirty in the folio. One page cut clean, with a knife, by a careful man who knew he was going to die and had time to hide everything but the thing they came for — or thought they'd hidden it so well that even I would have to earn it.

I walked home across the safe ice faster than I have ever walked anywhere, let myself into the workshop, barred the door, and lifted the loose floorboard where Aldous Vane kept the good paper.

The atlas was gone.

Not hidden. Gone. In its place, weighted with a drafting stone, was a single sheet in my dead master's hand, folded twice, addressed in letters I had traced a thousand times as a boy learning my pen strokes.

It said: *If you are reading this, Orsen, then you have met the river's other daughter, and it is time you knew what I knew.*
