## 6. The Promise in Peacetime

I did not sleep that night. I sat at my desk with Maren's letter lying under the lamp like something venomous that had wandered indoors, and I told myself the reasonable things. People's hands change. Grief changes a hand. Three years of frostbite and hard riding could turn any script into something jagged. The girl who wrote me letters in the summer of '44 — round o's, tails that curled like fern fronds — was not necessarily the hand that had written *Dunmore* twice, underlined once.

And then again, maybe she was.

I am a mapmaker's apprentice, or I was until my master was murdered in his own workshop, and one thing cartography teaches you is that a coastline does not lie about itself. It only lies to people who want it to be something else. I wanted Maren Kestrel to be something else. That was the whole trouble.

So instead of sleeping I went looking for the summer.

* * *

The bridge across the Vell at Vellmark was never finished. You should understand that about it, because it matters to everything that came after. The Crown voted funds for it in the spring of '43 — timber, stone for two piers, a toll house that got as far as its foundation before the money evaporated into somebody's pocket in the capital. What remained was four piers standing in the river like pulled teeth and a skeleton of beams reaching from the north bank halfway out, then stopping, as if the carpenters had simply walked off the end of the world.

We called it the bridge anyway. Children do not require completion.

I met Maren Kestrel on it in July of '44, though "met" is generous; we had known each other all our lives the way children in a small town know each other, which is to say entirely and without knowing anything at all. She was fourteen and I was fifteen, and I was on the bridge because it was the only place in Vellmark where nobody asked what I was doing. Master Vane had me grinding pigment that week — Prussian blue, which stains everything, hands, cuffs, thoughts — and my mother had banished me from the house on grounds of blueness.

Maren was sitting on the third beam out, feet dangling over the water, eating cherries from a stolen-looking cloth bundle and spitting the stones at the piers with an accuracy that suggested practice.

"You're going to fall," I said, because that is what one says.

"I'm going to eat cherries," she said. "You're going to watch. One of us has a better afternoon planned."

That was Maren. She talked like someone who had already won every argument she would ever be in and was merely being kind enough to let you arrive late.

I climbed out onto the beam because the alternative was standing on the bank looking like a boy who had been scolded by a fourteen-year-old, which I had been. The wood was hot under my stained hands. Below us the Vell ran green and slow, fat with summer, so unlike the grey blade it becomes in January that you would swear they were different rivers wearing each other's names.

"What's all over your hands?" she asked.

"Ink. Blue ink."

"Let me see."

She took my wrist without asking, the way she took everything without asking, and turned my hand over and examined it with the frown of a magistrate reviewing a deed. "It looks like you strangled a kingfisher."

"Thank you. I made it myself."

"From what?"

"Ground cobalt and — actually it's a trade secret."

"It's a trade secret," she repeated, delighted. "Orsen Dunmore, keeper of secrets." And she laughed, and I remember thinking — this is the part I have gone over so many times it has worn smooth, like a coin handled too long — I remember thinking that her laugh sounded exactly like the river in summer, which is to say like something that intended to keep going forever.

* * *

Here is what I know now that I did not know then: that summer was the famine winter's gentle older brother, and we spent it ignorant. The harvest of '43 had been poor but not yet ruinous, and the grain was still in the granaries, and Magistrate Hale had not yet begun selling relief at prices calculated to make widows weep arithmetically. We were sixteen and fifteen respectively, and our troubles were proportionate: my apprenticeship, her father's temper, the question of whether either of us would ever be permitted to leave Vellmark, and the larger unspoken question of whether leaving it together was a thought either of us was allowed to finish.

We finished it anyway, in the way of the young, which is to say constantly, wordlessly, and without ever once saying it aloud.

All through that summer we met on the bridge. It became ours the way places become ours — not through ownership but through accumulation. She taught me to skip stones off the pier shadows; I taught her the names of clouds, which she mocked, then secretly used, then pretended she'd always known. I brought her maps I was copying — she liked the old ones best, the ones with sea monsters drawn where the surveyors had given up — and she brought me gossip with the serial numbers filed off, tales of the Kestrel cousins downriver who moved goods past the toll stations without troubling the King's paperwork.

"They're not criminals," she said once, flatly, in the tone she used when daring me to disagree. "They're practical."

"Practicality is not a legal defense."

"It should be. Half the laws are just impracticality written down by rich men."

I laughed at that, because I was fifteen and clever and didn't yet understand that she was quoting her father, and that her father meant it, and that within three years those words would be worth hanging for on both sides of them.

* * *

The promise happened in August. I can date it precisely because of the cherries — the last of the season, she claimed, though Maren's claims about fruit were always suspect — and because of the heat, which sat on the river that day like a lid.

We were far out on the unfinished span, farther than usual, sitting where the beams simply stopped. Below us the water made its patient argument against the stone piers. Somewhere upstream a dog barked at nothing. It was the sort of afternoon in which nothing happens, which is the sort of afternoon in which everything gets decided.

"My father says we're leaving," Maren said.

"Leaving where?"

"Vellmark. After harvest. Downriver, to my uncle's people." She said it lightly, tossing a cherry stone into the water, watching it vanish. "He says there's no future here for anyone whose name isn't Hale."

I felt it go through me like cold water finding a leak — that specific dread, the one where the world reorganizes itself around a sentence someone else said casually. "Your uncle's people" were the Kestrels of the lower Vell, the practical ones, the ones whose barges ran dark past the toll lights.

"When?" I asked.

"After harvest." Another stone. Another vanishing. "Maybe. He says maybe."

"And what do you say?"

She looked at me then, and for once she didn't have the argument already won. Her face did something complicated — the look of a person holding two futures up to the light and finding both of them cracked.

"I say I don't want to be a maybe," she said. "Orsen. If I go — if it happens — what are you?"

The question made no sense and perfect sense. I was fifteen; I understood it completely and answered it badly, the way one does, groping for something large enough.

"I'm here," I said. "I'll be here."

"That's not an answer. Here is a place, not a promise."

"Then — " and I stood up on the beam, which was stupid, the beam was narrow, and I put my blue-stained hand over my heart like a fool swearing fealty in a ballad, and I said the words that I have since measured every other decision of my life against:

"Whatever comes, I will stand where you can find me."

She stared at me. A kingfisher went past below us, low over the water, a bolt of the same blue that was ground into my skin. For a moment she looked almost angry, as if I'd cheated her somehow, given her something she hadn't prepared a counter for.

"That's a terrible promise," she said finally. "It doesn't say what you'll do."

"It says where I'll be."

"And if where you'll be is somewhere I can't come?"

"Then I'll move," I said. "That's the whole point of standing. You can relocate it."

And she laughed — that river-in-summer laugh — and grabbed my blue hand and held it, ink to ink, because her fingers were stained too now, and she said, "Witnessed, then. The river saw it. The river keeps accounts, my father says. Everything that goes in, it remembers."

I thought she was being poetic. I was fifteen; I thought most things she said were poetic rather than load-bearing.

* * *

She left after harvest. Of course she did. Maybes resolve downward; that is their nature. There was no farewell scene worth the name — her father loaded a cart at dawn and she waved from the back of it with one hand, casual as anything, as if she were going to market, and I stood in the road like a fence post and raised my own hand, and the cart went south along the river road and around the bend, and that was peacetime ending. Not with soldiers. With a wave.

We wrote. Letters, that first year — hers full of downriver news dressed up as jokes, mine full of atlas gossip dressed up as indifference. Then the famine came, the true one, the winter of '45–'46, when Hale's grain prices did their arithmetic on the poor and men began moving goods on the Vell who had never imagined themselves criminals, and her letters thinned, and changed, and then stopped. The last one I kept. It said, in the round-o handwriting I knew, *Things are different here than the maps say.* I thought at the time it was a joke about my trade. I no longer think so.

Then Master Vane died in October with his skull broken and the workshop tidied by someone careful, and I finished the atlas alone with my grief and my Prussian blue, believing history happened elsewhere. And then a courier brought a sealed letter accusing my family of conspiracy, in a jagged hand that curled at the tails like fern fronds grown wild, and signed, or rather unsigned, in a way I could not mistake: *K.*

* * *

Dawn found me still at the desk, the lamp burned to a memory of oil, and I had arrived — as one does after a night of interrogating the past — nowhere new and everywhere different.

Because here is what the night's work had produced, laid out on the desk beside her letter like evidence on a magistrate's bench:

If the hand was hers, then Maren Kestrel had sent my family's name to the Crown's magistrate. Which meant either she had betrayed me — possible; three years is long, and famine is longer — or she had done it for a reason I couldn't see, some reason that required the Dunmores frightened, or moving, or looking somewhere other than where the truth was standing.

And if the hand was not hers — if someone had forged it — then someone knew about us. Someone knew enough to reach into the summer of '44 and pull out the one person whose name would stop me from thinking clearly.

Either way, the letter was not an accusation. It was a hook. And I was the fish, and I had been thrashing beautifully all night.

I looked at my hands. Still faintly blue at the cuticle, after all these years — the stain never quite leaves a mapmaker, Master Vane used to say, it goes into the blood. I remembered her turning my wrist over, examining it. *It looks like you strangled a kingfisher.*

*Whatever comes, I will stand where you can find me.*

Well. Whatever had come. And the river, my father's river, the Vell in January, frozen to a foot of black glass — the river that kept accounts — ran right past the town walls, and I had heard, three nights ago now, a horse on the ice where no honest horse had business being.

I put on my coat. I took the atlas folio from its hiding place and hesitated over it — take it, leave it, take it — and left it, because whatever happened next, the atlas stayed hidden. Some promises are made of paper. Mine had been made of standing.

I went out into the cold to find the river, and to find out what Maren Kestrel had become — and whether she had sent that letter, or whether someone had learned to counterfeit a girl who once promised me the river never forgets.
