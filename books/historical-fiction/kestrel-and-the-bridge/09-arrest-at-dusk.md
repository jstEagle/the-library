## 9. Arrest at Dusk

There is a particular quality to the light in Vellmark in late February — the sun goes down at half past four and takes all the color with it, leaving the town the shade of old dishwater. I had spent twenty-three years learning to read that light, because a woman who bakes for a living learns early that everything worth knowing announces itself in shades of grey: dough gone over, lard turning, a sky deciding whether to snow.

The light on the evening they came for my husband was the color of a lie.

I want to say I knew at once. Wives in stories always know at once; they drop a plate or feel a chill or see an omen in the flight of birds. But the truth is less flattering and more useful: I noticed the light, thought *the oven stone wants raking*, and went on rolling pastry. It was only when Tam Brase's boy came running past the window with his coat half-on that I understood something was moving through my town faster than cold weather gossip, which until that day had been the fastest thing in Vellmark.

"Sela," he said through the glass, not stopping. "Soldiers. They're going house to house. They're at the Weirs'."

I set down the rolling pin. I wiped my hands on my apron. And then, because I am my mother's daughter and she survived two famishes and one husband, I did not run to the door. I took the bread out of the oven first. You do not waste good bread, not even for catastrophe. Catastrophe can wait four minutes while the loaves come out.

My husband Tammas — yes, Dunmore men are named with no imagination whatsoever, we have had Tams and Tammases back to before the parish books begin, and my son Orsen was our single act of rebellion in three generations — was in his chair by the fire with a ledger open on his knee, though he had been looking at the same line of it for the better part of an hour. He is a cooper by trade and a worrier by vocation, and ever since the letter came he had been worrying in silence, which is the worst kind, the kind you can hear anyway.

"Tams," I said. "They're coming."

He looked up. I watched him understand me, and I watched the fear arrive, and I will tell you what I told nobody since: in that moment I did not think about the letter, or the magistrate, or the gallows they were building in their minds. I thought, *he has flour on his collar from this morning when he kissed me before dawn, and if they take him looking like that, people will say the Dunmores couldn't even keep themselves clean.* Pride is an ugly thing in a woman of my station, but it keeps better than salt pork, and I have lived off it whole winters.

"Orsen?" he asked. His first word. Not *what have I done*, not *God help us*. Our son.

"Gone to the workshop since noon." Which meant either safely across town or directly in the soldiers' path, depending on how stupid the evening intended to be.

* * *

They arrived the way soldiers do everything — too many of them, too loudly, and slightly late. Six of them and a sergeant whose name I never learned but whose boots I would have known anywhere after, because he stood on my clean floor in them as though the floor owed him money.

I opened the door before they knocked. This is a small tactic and I recommend it to every woman who expects trouble: let them find you standing upright in your own doorway, arms loose, face arranged into something polite and empty, like a shop counter. It unsettles them. Men with authority are used to doors opening inward upon crouched and weeping things.

"Madam." The sergeant had a paper in his glove, and he held it the way a man holds a thing he cannot read well and hopes you cannot read at all. "Warrant. Tammas Dunmore, on suspicion of conspiracy to defraud His Majesty's revenue, in company with persons known to be in rebellion against the Crown."

"In company with," I repeated. "That's a great many words to say very little."

"Madam—"

"I heard you the first time, Sergeant. My husband is by the fire. He has trouble with his left knee in cold weather, so walk slowly behind him and don't startle him on the stairs, there's a step that talks."

I stepped aside. I did not offer them drink. There is hospitality and there is surrender, and I know the difference better than most, having served both at table.

Tammas rose when they came in. That is the thing I keep returning to, the thing I turned over in the dark for weeks after: he rose. He did not cower, did not protest, did not do any of the small scrambling things a frightened man does. He closed his ledger, laid it square on the table, and stood up straight as a plank, because whatever else my husband is, he learned young that the way you meet a thing is often the only part of it you get to choose.

"Where is my son?" he said.

"That's Crown business," said the sergeant.

"He's seventeen."

"Then he'll want to be found soon, won't he." He said it mildly, almost kindly, which is how cruelty prefers to dress when it's out visiting. Two soldiers went through my pantry. One of them opened my crock of rendered goose fat and looked inside it as though sedition might be floating on top. Another shook out the linens. A third went upstairs, and I heard my mother-in-law's old trunk being thrown open above us, and the sound of her wedding linens hitting the boards, and I confess I gripped the doorframe hard enough to leave marks in the wood, because those linens were the last of her hands in the world, and no paper warrant covers disrespecting the dead.

But here is what I did not do. I did not burn anything. I want that recorded somewhere, in some margin, because afterward, when people spoke of that night in lowered voices as though the Dunmores must surely have had something to hide, I wanted to stand on the market cross and shout it: *we burned nothing*. We hid nothing. When the sergeant asked — softly, reasonably, the way you'd ask after a sick aunt — whether there were "papers in the house relating to cartography or river traffic," I fetched them myself. Every bill of sale, every receipt for staves and hoops, every schoolbook page my sons had ever scribbled a river onto. I put the whole bundle in his hands.

"There," I said. "Now you've seen everything a cooper's family owns that has ink on it. If you find treason among my grocery accounts, you're a better reader than the parson."

He almost smiled. That was the worst of it — he *almost smiled*, because he knew, and I knew, and we both knew the other knew, that none of it mattered. Papers are not evidence to men like him. Papers are decoration. The verdict was written before the wax was melted on his warrant; the question was only which Dunmore name would go under it.

* * *

They took Tammas out the front door at dusk, manacled, past the neighbors who had come out to stand in their doorways the way people do — present, sympathetic, useless. Mrs. Weir was crying, which I appreciated and did not need. Old Cotter from the smithy touched his cap to Tammas, and Tammas nodded back, and between those two gestures passed more honest communication than anything the sergeant said all evening.

What I did next, the town discussed for months, so I may as well tell it plainly. I put on my good shawl. I went to the kitchen and wrapped four of the hot loaves in a cloth — the ones I'd pulled from the oven, still warm through the linen. And I followed my husband down my own street to the guardhouse, walking three paces behind the soldiers, and when they shoved him through the door I pushed past the sergeant, who was too surprised by a calm woman to stop me, and I put the bread into Tammas's bound hands.

"You'll want eating," I said. "Jail bread is sawdust with ambitions."

"Sela." His voice cracked on it. Twenty years and I had heard that voice crack twice before — once at Orsen's birth, once at Aldous Vane's funeral. "The boys. Keep the boys—"

"I have kept them since they were the length of my forearm. I'll keep them now." I straightened his collar, right there in the doorway of the guardhouse with the soldiers shuffling and the whole street watching, and I got the flour off it with my thumb, and I said, quietly enough that only he could hear: "You stand up straight in there, Tam Dunmore. Whatever they say, whatever they ask, however they make you sit. Straight. Let them write down in their book that they arrested a man, and not a guilty one."

He laughed. Actually laughed — short, wet, but real. "You're a terrible comfort, woman."

"I'm the only comfort you've got and the best one, and don't forget either fact." Then I kissed him on the mouth in front of God, the Crown, and six embarrassed soldiers, and walked home without hurrying, because hurrying would have been running, and Dunmores do not run. Not in their own streets.

* * *

The house after was the worst part, and no one warns you about the worst part. They warn you about the arrest. Nobody tells you about the quiet after, the kettle you fill without thinking and then cannot remember why, the chair across the fire with nobody in it and the shape of him still worn into the cushion.

Orsen did not come home that night. I sat up until the candles drowned, doing the arithmetic of my fear. Either my son had heard the soldiers coming and kept wisely away — in which case he was sleeping rough in a winter that kills stray dogs — or he had gone to ground somewhere with the atlas, in which case he was carrying the exact thing they wanted, wrapped in oilcloth, like a man carrying a lit lantern through a powder store. Neither possibility improved on the other.

And beneath the fear, colder and quieter, sat the thing I could not say aloud even to myself, so I will say it here where no one hears: I was angry at Maren Kestrel. I had known that girl since she was small enough to carry on one arm, since she and Orsen ran the riverbank together getting their boots ruined, since the summer she stood on the half-built bridge and extracted promises from my son with the seriousness of a magistrate administering oaths. I had loved her without ceremony, the way you love the children of your town. And whatever the truth of the letter under her seal — and I believed even then, with the stubbornness of a woman who has raised boys, that my family was innocent of every word in it — she had lit the fuse that burned down to my doorstep. She had used my son's heart as the wick.

I was angry at her, and I was frightened for her, and both things were true, and I mention it only because everyone since has wanted the story simple: the wronged mother, the outlaw girl, the wicked magistrate. Nothing about that winter was simple. Hale himself was not simple — that was precisely the horror of him. He did not rage or bluster. He sent, the following morning, a basket.

A basket. To the wife of the arrested man. Good white sugar, a lemon — a *lemon*, in Vellmark in February, a fruit most of my neighbors had never seen outside a picture — and a card in a beautiful hand: *In sympathy for your troubles, madam. Justice, rightly understood, is a mercy. — C. Hale.*

I stood in my kitchen holding that card and I understood, finally and completely, what kind of man we were dealing with. A fool burns your house. A bully breaks your windows. But a man who sends lemons to a woman whose husband he is strangling — that man is telling you he enjoys the work, and wants you to know it, and knows you cannot prove enjoyment is a crime. I fed the lemon to the fire. Not the sugar; I'm wasteful, not stupid. The children needed sweetening that week.

* * *

On the third morning, before the town was fully awake, someone scratched at my back shutter. Not knocked — scratched, quick and low, the signal Orsen had used since he was nine to summon bread without waking his father.

I opened the shutter onto the blue dark and my son's face, thinner than I liked, rimed with frost, eyes hollowed and burning in a way that made him look suddenly like his grandfather, whom I had also loved and also worried over.

"Mum," he whispered. "Is Da—"

"Guardhouse. Unharmed. Fed, because I make sure of it." I reached through the gap and gripped his coat before he could move. "Now you listen to me, Orsen Dunmore, and you listen once. I can see what you've decided to do. You have your father's face and my stubbornness and your master's maps, and you've decided you're going to fix this alone, like a character in one of your books."

"Mum—"

"Hush. I'm not done. I don't know what you're carrying out there and I don't want to know — nothing you tell me can be beaten out of you later, so tell me nothing. But hear this." I leaned closer, close enough to see the frost melting on his lashes. "Whatever you do, whoever you go to, however far past the law you have to walk — you remember that you are not the first person in this family to choose the harder right over the easier safe. Your father chose it Tuesday evening in our front room, when he stood up instead of begging. Do it as well as he did. Come back as clean as he did. And Orsen—" my voice betrayed me then, just barely, just at the edge, "—come back. Whatever else. Come back."

He put his hand over mine on his coat. Seventeen years old, and he held my hand the way a man holds a thing he is afraid of breaking.

"They say the ice will break within the fortnight," he said. "Everything happens before then. Everything comes to a head."

"Then let it come," I said, "and let it find us standing."

He slipped away along the wall and was gone into the blue morning, and I closed the shutter, and banked the fire, and began the ordinary work of the extraordinary day — because that is courage's dirty secret, friends: it looks exactly like kneading dough. It looks like feeding the remaining children, and walking to the guardhouse with clean bread, and answering the jailer's smirk with good afternoon.

It was two days later, at the market, that the Weir girl caught my sleeve and breathlessly told me the news already running the streets ahead of the soldiers: that Orsen Dunmore had vanished entirely, that watchmen had been posted on every road out of Vellmark, and that Magistrate Hale was saying — saying openly, in the courthouse yard, with relish — that a son's flight was confession enough for the whole family.

I paid for my flour. I thanked the miller. I walked home unhurried through a town that was watching me the way people watch a lit fuse.

And I thought: *run, boy. Run clever, run fast, use whatever your master drew on those pages — and God forgive me for saying it, use the outlaws too.* Because the law in Vellmark had a lemon-scented smile and a cell with my husband's name on it, and if justice lived anywhere anymore, it had clearly moved out beyond the river, to wherever the desperate and the honest were forced to keep each other's company.

The ice would break within the fortnight. Everything would happen before then.

I put the flour away and started the bread.
