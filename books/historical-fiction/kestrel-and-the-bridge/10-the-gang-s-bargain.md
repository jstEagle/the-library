## 10. The Gang's Bargain

My uncle Brannic kept a saying that he deployed the way other men deployed fists: *trust is a knife you lend handle-first, and you'd best remember which end you gave away.* He'd learned it in the press gangs of Harrow Quay, and he repeated it now, standing over the fire with Orsen Dunmore's name in his mouth like a piece of gristle he couldn't swallow.

"He's Vane's apprentice," I said. "That makes him worth more than any three men we could buy."

"Vane's apprentice is also the brother of a girl whose name appears in Hale's ledger, next to ours." Brannic spat into the fire. The flames flinched. "Names in ledgers are how we got declared enemies of the Crown in the first place, girl. You want to hang a rope around our whole company because you knew him when he still had soft hands?"

The camp had gathered in the hollow below the old charcoal burners' platform, thirty-one of us arranged in the loose half-circle we used for council, close enough to the fire to feel it and far enough back that no one's shadow could fall across the argument without everyone seeing whose it was. That was camp law, unwritten but absolute: nobody argued from darkness. It had started as a practical measure against informers and had become something closer to a philosophy. In the Kestrel gang, you stood where your words could find your face.

I stood, therefore, fully lit, feeling every eye on me, and wished — not for the first time that winter — that I had kept my mouth shut about the letter.

It had seemed simple when I'd written it. Simple as a lever. Corvus Hale wanted the Dunmores broken, and I wanted Hale looking at the wrong family while we moved the last of the grain before thaw. A forged accusation, delivered under seal, naming Orsen's family in a conspiracy they'd never touched — it would scatter his attention, put watchmen on the town lanes instead of the river fords, and cost me nothing but ink and one sleepless night composing my hand into something Orsen might almost recognize and then deliberately marring. I had even enjoyed it, in the way you enjoy anything done well for a bad reason.

What I had not accounted for was Orsen himself. I had expected him to run. Running men ask no questions. Instead, three nights ago on the ice, I had found him standing in the middle of the frozen Vell River at moonset like an idiot, holding my letter out to me the way you'd return a defective tool, asking me — politely, in that maddening careful voice of his — whether I had lost my mind or merely misplaced my morals.

And I had made it worse by telling him things. About the atlas. About what his master had really been mapping. About the ford at Kestrel Bend that Hale needed erased from every honest chart in the province. I had watched his face do what Orsen's face does — that slow rearrangement, grief and arithmetic happening at once — and I had known, with the particular dread of someone who has just handed a knife to the wrong person handle-first, that he would not let this go.

Now here we were, the whole company, deciding what to do about it.

"Kill him," said Wick Thorne from the back, because Wick Thorne believed every problem was a nail and had never met a hammer he didn't like. "He knows the Bend. If he tells Hale—"

"If he tells Hale what?" I said. "That there's a ford there? Hale already knows there's a ford there. That's rather the entire problem. He knows where it is; he just can't prove it was ever charted honestly, which means he can't prove his own copy is a forgery. Orsen is the only living soul who can prove it, because he's the only one who's seen every leaf of Vane's folio."

"And that," said Brannic quietly, "is exactly why some at this fire are thinking the way Wick thinks."

I looked around the circle. Thirty-one faces, and maybe eight of them wearing the particular blankness people wear when they've already agreed on something and are waiting to see if you'll make them say it aloud.

"You're talking about murdering a mapmaker," I said.

"We're talking about survival," Brannic said. "Which occasionally wears the same coat."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being declared an enemy of the Crown: it is mostly bookkeeping.

You imagine fires and midnight rides and pistols, and yes, there are those, but the truth of the famine winter was that we spent most of it as clerks. Ledgers of houses on Hollow Row that would take a sack of meal and say nothing. Lists of which constables ate bribes and which were too stupid to be bought, the second category being larger and, oddly, more useful. Tallies of children in Vellmark parish who'd gone gray-eyed from hunger, because gray-eyed children were a debt we owed somebody, though I could never have told you who and have stopped asking myself.

We smuggled grain through a hole in the world. That was all. Hale taxed the harvest until the granaries belonged to him and his friends, sold exemptions to merchants who could pay and starvation warrants to those who couldn't, and when the winter came down hard and people began eating seed corn and shoe leather, a few of us decided the Crown's laws on the movement of flour were, that particular season, obscene. We ran meal and salt fish and dried apples down the Vell through fords no crown surveyor had ever bothered to chart properly, because the river's honest crossings had been mapped by Aldous Vane, and Aldous Vane was the finest cartographer between the mountains and the sea.

Until he wasn't anymore, because he was dead in the shallows above the mill weir with his skull staved in, and his atlas was the most dangerous document in the province.

I had known Vane my whole life, or near enough. He'd taught me letters along with Orsen, both of us bent over the same table in the workshop that smelled of ink and tallow, and he had this habit of tapping the table twice when he caught you in an error, tap-tap, patient as a clock, so that even his disappointment arrived on schedule. When they pulled him from the water I did not cry at the burial, because Magistrate Hale was watching the mourners the way a man watches cards, and I had already learned that winter what crying in front of Hale cost you.

But I had promised Orsen something once, in summer, on the half-built bridge, and promises made in peacetime have a way of coming due in war. *Whatever comes,* he'd said, sixteen years old and solemn as a psalm, *I will stand where you can find me.* And I had said the fool thing back, and meant it, and here we both were, finding each other across a frozen river in the worst winter of our lives, on opposite sides of a line one of us had drawn.

Me, in case that wasn't clear. I drew the line. The letter was mine.

So when Brannic said *some at this fire*, I understood precisely whom the fire was being built to roast, and I understood something else besides: that this council was also a trial of me, though nobody had announced it. Because I had written the letter. Because I had gone to the ice alone, against camp law, against sense. Because I had come back and instead of reporting a contact I had reported a *feeling*, which in this company was currency worth less than counterfeit coin.

"Maren," Brannic said, gentler now, which was worse, "nobody doubts your heart. But your heart signed this boy's name to a hanging offense four weeks ago, and now you're asking us to gamble the company on it. Which is it? Is he yours to spend or yours to save?"

The silence after that had weight, like snow on a roof.

"The letter was mine," I said. "I'll wear that. Court-martial me after, if we're still alive to bother. But hear me on this one thing, because I will only say it once." I stepped to the fire's edge, into the heat, where my own shadow went huge and unsteady behind me. "Orsen Dunmore held the true atlas in his hands and had every reason — every reason — to trade it to Hale for his family's necks. His father is in custody as of yesterday. His mother's house gets searched by week's end. All the boy has ever wanted, since he was nine years old and corrected Master Vane's longitude tables, is for the world to be *accurate*. And accuracy, right now, cuts our throats. He could end us with a testimony and an exhibit."

"Then why in God's name," said Wick, "are we not killing him?"

"Because he hasn't done it. Because he stood on the ice and asked me if the gang fed the Hollow Row families during the famine — asked *me*, like I was the one on trial — and when I told him about the gray-eyed children, he went quiet in that way of his, and then he said—" and here I had to stop, because saying it aloud in front of thirty-one armed outlaws made it real in a way I hadn't prepared for. "He said, *then the atlas has to tell the truth about everything. Including the parts that hurt you.*"

Tap-tap, said Aldous Vane's ghost, somewhere behind my ribs.

"That's not a boy who sells anyone," I finished. "That's a boy who will burn down his own house to make the map honest. And I am asking this company — I am asking *you*, Uncle — to stake my life against his word. If he betrays us, you may hang us both from the same branch and I'll apologize to every one of you on the way up."

* * *

Council broke apart the way councils do, into knots of two and three, voices low, knives — literal ones — being cleaned by the fire. I sat on the woodpile behind the charcoal platform and let the cold work on me and tried not to replay my own speech for flaws, and failed, and replayed it anyway.

Brannic found me there. He lowered himself onto the next log with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions, and for a while neither of us said anything, which was its own kind of conversation. He'd raised me from eleven years old, after my father was transported and my mother went wherever mothers go when there's nothing left to eat and too much pride to beg. He taught me to shoot, to read weather, to count a crowd, and to never, ever be the most certain person in the room.

"You were the most certain person in the room tonight," he said.

"Yes."

"That's usually how you get people killed."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, chewing on something — tobacco, or the situation. "The vote goes at dawn. You know that. And you know Wick has six who'll follow him anywhere stupid."

"I know."

"But here's what you don't know, girl, because you were too busy making speeches." He turned and looked at me, and in the dark his face was all ridges, a topography of its own. "Hale sent riders up the north road today. Three of them, civilian cloaks, asking after a young man answering Orsen's description. Asking whether he'd been seen toward the river."

My stomach dropped through the woodpile and kept going. "Watchmen. He's already—"

"Hale's not hunting the boy," Brannic said. "Hale's herding him. Think. The magistrate needs a confession or a corpse to close his case against the family, and the apprentice is the only Dunmore left walking free. Either the boy runs and proves himself guilty, or the boy comes to us and becomes the thread Hale pulls to unravel the whole company. There's no direction that lad can walk that doesn't lead where Hale wants. Except one."

"Except he comes to us," I said slowly, "and we get him to the assizes alive, with the atlas plate, before the ice breaks."

"Which requires moving him past three riders on the north road, two constables in Vellmark, and whatever watchmen Hale has salted along the banks." Brannic stood, knees cracking like green firewood. "The vote will go your way, Maren. Not because your speech was pretty. Because dead men make poor witnesses and Hale knows it, and half this company figured out before you finished talking that keeping the boy breathing is the only move that hurts the magistrate. But understand what you've bought." He looked down at me a long moment. "You've made that boy's life the price of your standing. Every man at that fire now holds you responsible for every breath Orsen Dunmore draws until spring. Trust is a knife lent handle-first. You just handed yours across a fire in front of thirty witnesses."

He shuffled off toward his tent. I stayed on the woodpile, doing sums I didn't like: three riders, two constables, unknown watchmen, one mapmaker who had never in his life run from anything except a social engagement, and a river that would begin coming apart in days.

Somewhere downriver, I knew — the way you know where a scar is without looking — Orsen was deciding, right now, in his careful ink-and-honesty way, whether to come to me. Whether I was worth trusting after the letter. Whether I'd betrayed him once and might again.

He'd come. That was the terrible part. He would come because of a promise made on a bridge in peacetime, because his moral compass pointed at fixed points and I had, God help me, become one.

The question was whether I could get to the ford before Hale's riders did — and whether I could look him in the eye when I arrived, knowing what I'd written, what I'd risked, and what I had just wagered on his behalf without asking his leave.

I got up, checked the load in my pistol out of habit, and went to wake the horse.

Dawn was perhaps three hours off. The ice, when I passed over it toward the river path, groaned once, long and low, like something enormous turning over in its sleep — and beginning, at last, to wake.
