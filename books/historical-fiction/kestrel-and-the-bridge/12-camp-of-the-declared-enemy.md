## 12. Camp of the Declared Enemy

I had imagined outlaw camps my whole life, in the way that boys imagine things — all firelight and swagger, men laughing with knives between their teeth. What I had not imagined was laundry.

Yet there it was: a line strung between two birches, shirts and stockings cracking in the wind off the river, and a woman named Petra Voss beating a shirt against a rock as though it owed her money. She was perhaps fifty, broad as a barn door, and she looked at me the way a farmer looks at a fox that has wandered into the hen yard — not alarmed, merely calculating how much trouble I was worth.

"That's the mapmaker," she said to no one in particular.

"That's the mapmaker," echoed a boy of ten or so, perched on a stump whittling nothing into a stick. He said it with the reverence other boys reserved for highwaymen. I did not correct him. It seemed ungracious, arriving at a man's camp at dawn with frost in your boots, to begin by disputing his legends.

Maren had gone ahead to speak with her father, which left me standing in the middle of the Kestrel gang's encampment like a surveyor's post — planted, exposed, waiting for someone to build something around me or pull me up by the roots. The camp sat in a hollow above the river, screened by pines, arranged with more sense than romance: pickets posted on the ridge, horses hobbled in a cut where they couldn't be seen from the water, cook-fire smoke drawn through a green bough so it came out thin and gray. These were not romantics. These were professionals.

"Sit," said Petra Voss, pointing at a log near the fire without looking at me. "You're making the boy nervous."

"I wasn't nervous," said the boy.

"You were composing ballads about him. Sit, both of you."

I sat. Someone put a bowl in my hands — barley and salt pork, overcooked and under-seasoned, and the finest meal I have ever eaten, because I had been running through frozen woods since moonset and because fear is the best sauce ever invented. The boy edged closer on his stump.

"Did you really draw the whole river? Every stone?"

"Every stone we could find," I said.

"What about the sunken mill at Tarn's Reach?"

I blinked. "There's a sunken mill at Tarn's Reach?"

"There is," he said solemnly. "My uncle's boat hit it."

"Well," I said, "then my atlas has an error in it."

The idea seemed to delight him beyond measure — the famous map, wrong, corrected by a boy — and I confess it delighted me too, in a small tired way. There is a peculiar freedom in admitting error to someone who has no power over you. I would think about that later, when I understood what else my beautiful atlas had gotten wrong, not in its lines but in its silences.

* * *

Maren found me an hour later, sitting on a fallen pine at the lip of the hollow, looking down at the river through the trees. Below, the ice lay white and featureless from bank to bank, innocent as an unwritten page. Somewhere under it, at Kestrel Bend, was the ford my master had died rather than falsify.

"My father wants to see you," she said.

"Should I be frightened?"

"He's in a reasonable mood. That's different from safe."

Dane Kestrel was older than I expected, gray at the temples, with a face like a map itself — every line earned, nothing decorative. He sat outside the largest tent on a three-legged stool, mending a harness strap, and he kept mending it while he talked to me, which I understood to be a technique. A man who won't look at you can't be read, and can't be interrupted properly.

"So," he said. "Orsen Dunmore. Apprentice to Aldous Vane."

"Journeyman now," I said, before I could stop myself. Grief speaks at strange moments.

His hands paused on the strap. "Journeyman," he repeated, and there was something in it that wasn't mockery. "Aldous Vane taught you well?"

"He taught me everything."

"Then tell me what he told you about the famine winter." He looked up then. "Two winters back. Tell me what the atlas says about that."

"The atlas doesn't say anything about any winter. It says where the fords are. Where the channels shift. Where a heavy cart can cross and where it drowns."

"Yes," said Dane Kestrel softly. "It does say that. And do you know who needed to know it?"

I didn't answer. He set down the harness.

"In the famine winter, the granaries in Vellmark were full. Full, Mr. Dunmore. The Crown stores. Grain taxed out of these valleys two years running while people ate elm bark and boiled hides. Magistrate Hale held the keys, and Hale sold release permits — grain for those who could pay his price, and his price was never money alone. And the roads were snowed under, and the river was the only road left, and the river was ice you couldn't trust except where a man knows the bottom."

He let that sit.

"My people ran grain across at night. At Kestrel Bend, mostly, where the bar shifts but never shifts far — your master charted it to the inch. We bought from farmers up-valley who'd hidden a portion of their yield, and we carried it down to villages that the magistrate had written off. Four hundred people ate that winter who otherwise wouldn't have. I can name some of them if you like. I can name the ones who didn't make it, too, and where they're buried."

"And the Crown calls it smuggling," I said.

"The Crown calls anything smuggling that it isn't paid for," he said. "Here is what puzzles me. Vane knew. He charted our crossings knowing what they carried — he told me so himself, the autumn before he died, standing on this very bank. He drew the truth anyway. So why does his apprentice look at me like I'm the first lawless thing he's ever seen?"

I wanted to say: because the law is the law. I wanted to say it because my father had said it over supper my whole childhood, the way other men say grace. But I thought of Master Vane's hidden notes, the careful hand recording which plates he had been pressed to alter and refused; I thought of the cut page in the folio; and I heard myself say instead:

"Because I believed maps don't take sides."

Dane Kestrel almost smiled. "No map ever made took a side, boy. Every map takes every side at once. It shows the ferryman's route and the revenue cutter's route in the same ink. What takes sides is what happens next." He picked the harness back up. "Hale knows about the atlas now. Which means the Crown will know. Which means every crossing my people bled for becomes a garrison post by summer. You understand what you carried across the ford last night?"

"An inheritance," I said.

"A weapon," he said. "Yours to aim. Think on where you point it."

* * *

They gave me a tent corner and, oddly, work. Word travels fast among outlaws that a mapmaker is in camp, and by midday I had a queue: the boy with the stick wanting Tarn's Reach surveyed properly; a scarred horse-thief named Willet wanting to know if a certain marsh track was passable in thaw (it was not, and telling him so may have saved his neck); old Petra Voss, of all people, wanting to know the exact depth of the channel below the bend, because her husband had drowned there years back and no one had ever been able to tell her whether he'd gone under in six feet of water or four.

I measured nothing for her. But I got out Master Vane's field book — his real one, the one with the soundings — and found the entry, and read it aloud: *Below the Bend, channel depth 5 ft 3 in at low water, autumn 1744.* Five feet three inches. She thanked me very gravely, as though I had done her husband a service, and perhaps I had. It is strange work, cartography. You spend your days fixing the world into lines, and only years later learn what the lines were holding.

That evening the camp ate together, outlaws and their families both — and that was the thing that undid me, more than any speech. I had pictured a gang. What I found was a village. There were children. There was a girl learning letters on a slate, using charcoal off the fire. There was an old man with a cough everyone took turns sitting up with. The men Dane called his riders were farmers' sons and dismissed soldiers and one defrocked curate who blessed the food before anyone asked him to, and everyone let him, the way you humor a grandfather.

And I sat among them with their bread in my hand and did the arithmetic I had spent my life refusing to do.

The law said these people were criminals. The law, administered by Corvus Hale, had also taxed grain out of starving valleys and sold it back at the price of daughters. The same law that named Maren Kestrel an enemy had accepted Tobias Rench's testimony as gospel and my father into irons on the strength of it. If the law was true, then everything in this hollow was false — and yet the bread was real, and the slate-letters were real, and five feet three inches of channel was real.

I have always believed history happened elsewhere — in capitals, in courts, to important men. Sitting on a log in an outlaw camp, watching a child sound out the word *river*, I understood at last that history is whatever survives the winter. Everything else is commentary.

Maren sat down beside me with her bowl.

"Now you see," she said quietly.

"I see a crime," I said. "I'm still looking for where it is."

She laughed — short, surprised, real — and for a moment we might have been back on the half-built bridge in summer, two people with no dead masters between us. Then her face went serious, and she nodded toward the ridge, where one of the pickets was coming down at a run.

"Orsen," she said. "There's a rider coming up the valley with a magistrates' badge on his coat. My father says he's asking at every farm after a runaway apprentice." She looked at me, and the firelight made her eyes hard and bright at once. "He's carrying papers. And he's announcing that by order of Corvus Hale, if you're not surrendered by the spring ice-break — your father hangs in your place."
