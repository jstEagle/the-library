## 13. Hale's Trap Springs

There is a particular pleasure in bad news delivered by someone who does not yet know it is bad news. My clerk, Pell, came into my study on the morning of the fourth of February with his hat in his hands and a report in his voice that he had rehearsed on the stairs, and I let him get through nearly all of it before I stopped him.

"The Dunmore boy," he said. "Gone, sir. His bed slept in, his mother says — she swears it — but the watchman at the tannery row saw a figure go out the yard gate before first light, and when they went back with a warrant, the workshop was—"

"Empty."

"Empty, sir. And the strongbox open. And—" Pell hesitated, because the next part was the part that mattered, and he knew it, and he wanted to be the man who said it properly. "And the atlas gone. The original one."

I set down my pen. I remember being aware of the small click it made against the inkwell, and thinking: *there it is.* Not surprise, exactly. Surprise is for amateurs. What I felt was nearer to satisfaction — the satisfaction of a trap that has sprung on schedule, even if the wrong animal is in it.

Because here is what Pell did not understand, bless him, standing there with his hat going soft in his hands: I had never needed Orsen Dunmore caught. I had needed him *gone*. A fugitive is a confession you do not have to extract. A boy who runs in the night writes his own indictment in footprints, and the assizes read footprints fluently.

"His mother," I said. "She swore the bed was slept in."

"She did, sir. Stood very straight about it. The father too."

"And the father is already in the cell beneath us."

"He is, sir."

"Then the family holds together like wet paper." I smiled at Pell, who smiled back with the cautious smile of a man who has learned that my smiles are invoices. "Thank you. Leave the reports. Send for Sergeant Wick and tell him I want riders on every road south of the river before noon. And Pell — find out which roads those are. Consult a map." I let that sit a moment. "Not the Dunmore map."

He left. I sat a while with my hands folded and allowed myself, briefly, the luxury of annoyance.

It had been an elegant arrangement, once. Aldous Vane had understood, in the end, the value of accommodation; he had simply come to that understanding too slowly and then died of it, which was regrettable but not, strictly speaking, my fault. Accidents happen on icy riverbanks. Men fall. The Vell takes people every winter; it is practically a civic service. And after Vane fell — as men do — I inherited the only problem worth having: an apprentice with clever hands and an honest atlas, and no way of knowing how much honesty the boy had absorbed along with his inks and dividers.

So I had done what any careful administrator does. I had commissioned a copy. A faithful copy, faithful in every particular except the ones that mattered — fords shifted, shallows deepened, the river redrawn to suit my purposes. With the true atlas in my possession and the false one in evidence, the Kestrel gang's routes would die on paper, the smuggling would collapse into my hands rather than away from them, and the Dunmores — decent, obscure, conveniently literate people — would hang for the conspiracy that kept the whole arrangement profitable. A magistrate needs a scapegoat the way a bridge needs piers. Nobody admires them. Everything rests on them.

The letter had been my idea too. Oh, not my hand — the hand was purchased, and well purchased, from a scribe in Grimsby Row who could reproduce anything from a sample, and the sample had been plentiful. Maren Kestrel wrote to the boy all through that last summer; I have seen the letters, or enough of them. A girl's looping script, promising things. All I required was the shape of her hand around the names *Dunmore* and *conspiracy*, and the boy's world would tilt. Frightened people run. Running people are guilty. Guilty people are hanged. It is a tidy little machine, and I had built every gear of it myself.

What I had not accounted for — and I record this now with the clarity of hindsight, which is the only honest kind — was that the boy might run *with* something. I had expected fear. I had not expected purpose.

* * *

Sergeant Wick arrived within the hour, smelling of the guardroom stove, and stood at parade rest before my desk while I gave him the shape of the thing.

"The boy has fled," I told him. "Which tells you what it tells any reasonable man. He knew the warrant was coming. He took the atlas. He has gone either to destroy it or to sell it, and given whose daughter Maren Kestrel is, we may guess which hearth he's warming himself at."

"You want him taken quiet, sir?"

"I want him taken," I said. "Quietly if the river obliges us, loudly if it doesn't. But understand the priority, Sergeant. It is not the boy." I turned the false atlas on my desk so its spine faced him, a small theatrical gesture, and watched his eyes follow it. "It is this. Or rather, it is the *other* this — the original — wherever he has hidden it. That book is Crown property by my declaration, and every ford in it is worth more than the boy, the family, and possibly the town."

"And the family?"

"Stay where they are. Guarded, not moved." I rose and went to the window. Below, in the exercise yard, the town moved through its morning like nothing was happening, which is the genius of towns. Smoke from forty chimneys, straight and white in the cold. Somewhere down there Sela Dunmore was sweeping a step or mending a shirt or performing whatever small dignities women perform when their world is being dismantled politely, room by room. I found the thought distasteful, which annoyed me. Sentiment is rust. I have filed it off before.

"The deadline," Wick said. He was a dull man but thorough, which in a sergeant is the correct exchange. "The ice. You want it tied to the ice."

"I want everything tied to the ice," I said. "Ice-break is the natural term of this country's accounting. Everything waits for the thaw — the ferries, the grain, the assizes circuit. So let the family wait too." I turned back to him. "Proclamation, posted tonight, copies to every parish within twenty miles: Orsen Dunmore stands accused of conspiracy, theft of Crown documents, and flight from justice. His apprehension is demanded. And — this is the elegant part, Sergeant, attend — the family will be held to answer *jointly* unless the fugitives present themselves by ice-break. The boy, the Kestrel woman, whoever else has harbored them. By the breaking of the ice, or the whole nest hangs together."

Wick frowned the frown of a man doing arithmetic with unfamiliar numbers. "Hang the lot? Even the mother? She's nobody, sir. Sweeps her own step."

"That is precisely why it works." I came back around the desk. "Nobody believes we hang nobodies, Wick. They believe it the way children believe the stove is hot — abstractly, until they touch it. A deadline that costs nothing is not a deadline. If the town believes the Dunmores swing regardless, the boy has no reason to surface. But if the boy knows that surrender saves them — that his body, in a courtroom, buys his mother's life — then every day the ice holds, he chooses. And choosing is exhausting. Men will do almost anything to stop choosing."

"You're hoping he gives himself up."

"I am indifferent to hope," I said, which was a lie so comfortable I barely noticed wearing it. "I am interested in pressure. Give me a man with a choice and a clock, and I will show you a man who makes my choice instead of his."

He saluted and went, and I sat down to draft the proclamation myself, because Pell's prose had the cadence of a funeral sermon and I wanted something with teeth. I am a fair hand with a threat. It is mostly architecture: begin with the crime, proceed to the consequence, end with the door left open — the door always left open, that is the trick, because a closed door makes martyrs and an open one makes informers.

I was three lines in when the other matter surfaced, the one I had been keeping below the waterline of my attention since Pell's report, and I made myself look at it squarely, because I did not reach my position by looking away from inconvenient things.

The boy had taken the atlas. The *original*.

A frightened boy runs with money, or with nothing. A frightened boy does not pause, with the watch coming up the tannery row, to open a strongbox and carry off a folio of maps. Unless the maps were the point. Unless somewhere between the sealed letter and the empty bed, Orsen Dunmore had stopped being a frightened boy and become something else — a man who understood what he was carrying, and what it proved, and against whom.

Vane's notes. Had the boy found Vane's notes? I had searched the workshop twice after the master's accident, once myself and once through Wick, who was thorough but illiterate in cartography, poor soul, and couldn't distinguish a surveyor's bearing from a grocer's bill. If there had been a cache — if Vane had documented our negotiations, or worse, documented the *refusal*, the refusal was the dangerous document, a refusal in writing is a sermon — then the boy now held paper that could unmake me.

But paper needs a courtroom. Paper needs a witness standing alive beside it. And the boy was now a fugitive, and a fugitive's papers are called evidence by no one but other fugitives. I could hang the context along with the text. I had hanged better arguments than his.

Still. Still. I drafted the proclamation with more care than the thing strictly required, and I caught myself reading each sentence twice, and I disliked the boy a little for making me careful. Careful is a tax. I had been paying it since Vane fell through my arrangements, and I was tired of the levy.

* * *

In the afternoon I went down to the cells. Not to see the father — I had seen him at the arrest, a big quiet man who had looked at the soldiers the way a man looks at weather, and I did not need another interview with that sort of patience; patience is the one quality I cannot purchase out of a prisoner. I went down for the ritual of it. Magistrates should be seen among the cells the way pastors should be seen among pews. It reminds everyone, including oneself, of the shape of authority.

The turnkey lit me down the stairs with a lantern, and the cold came up to meet us, stone-cold with a ghost of straw and iron in it. Dunmore's cell was the third. He was sitting on the bench with his hands loose between his knees, and he looked up when the light reached him, and I confess the look did me no good at all. There was no fear in it. There was no hatred either, which would at least have been conversational. There was simply assessment — the flat, measuring gaze of a man who builds things and wants to know what I weigh.

"Magistrate," he said. Not *sir*. Just the word, laid down like a plank.

"Master Dunmore." I stood where the light favored me; these are small advantages but they compound. "Your son has fled."

Nothing moved in his face. Nothing. I had expected — I had *counted on*, if I am honest, counted on it the way I count on most levers — a flinch. A father hears his son named a fugitive and something gives at the joints. But Dunmore only nodded slowly, the way a man nods at a forecast.

"Aye," he said. "I'd expect he would."

"You'd *expect*—"

"He's Vane's apprentice." He said it as though it explained everything, and the maddening thing was that it did. "Master taught him the country's honest down to the inch. Boy won't sit still under a lie. Never could. Used to redo the ledgers when the tally clerks rounded wrong." Almost, horribly, there was pride in it.

"Then he has condemned himself," I said, "and very nearly your household with him. You understand the proclamation. Ice-break. The family answers jointly."

"I heard the soldiers read summat at the arrest." He flexed his hands once, watching them, a craftsman's habit. "You'll do what you'll do, Magistrate. I've carried heavy timber before."

There was nothing to grip. Do you see? I stood there in the lantern light holding every instrument of terror the Crown permits a border magistrate, and the man handed me nothing, not a tremor, not a plea. It was like trying to hang a sentence on a loadstone wall. I made my closing remarks anyway — I always make closing remarks; silence reads as doubt — and climbed back up into the daylight feeling oddly winded, and angrier than the occasion warranted, and I examined the anger on the walk home and found at the bottom of it something I did not care to name.

It was *envy*. Briefly, and in small denomination, I envied him. He knew exactly what he was, and what he was worth, and what he'd lose, and none of it required a forged document to maintain. Whereas I — I have built so careful a structure of copies and countersignatures and purchased hands that some mornings I am not entirely certain which of my papers is the true one. Only that they must all match. Matching is survival.

* * *

The proclamation went up at dusk. I watched from the market square as Pell nailed it to the post beside the grain notices, and the crowd gathered the way crowds do, drawn by the smell of other people's catastrophe. I heard the pieces travel through them: *the mapmaker's son — the atlas — by ice-break — the whole family.* A woman near the front crossed herself. A carter spat. Two boys read it aloud to each other with the relish of the young for other people's hanging weather, and an old man said, just loud enough, "Shame, that. Decent folk, the Dunmores."

Decent folk. Yes. That is what makes the example instructive.

I stood in the cold with my collar up and felt the machine engage, gear by gear, and it was sweet — I will not pretend otherwise; vanity recorded honestly is still a kind of honesty. The roads would be watched. The river crossings — the real ones, the ones I controlled — would be watched harder. Every eye in Vellmark would be hunting a boy with a book, and every tongue would bring me what it found, and the deadline would sit over the town like the ice itself: silent, thickening, patient.

And then, at the edge of the gathered crowd, I saw the flaw in my beautiful machine, and I have not stopped seeing it since.

A horse. Gray, rough-coated, standing saddled in the shadow of the wool warehouse with a rider mounted motionless in the saddle — small, hooded, waiting, in the manner of someone reading a notice they already know by heart. The crowd's noise washed around horse and rider and neither moved, and something in the set of the shoulders, in the stillness that is not calm but coiled economy, put a name in my mouth before I permitted it.

Maren Kestrel. In my town, in my market square, under my own proclamation, close enough that I could have counted the stitches in her hood.

She was not fleeing. She was not hiding. She was *reading* — taking the measure of the deadline I had posted, and then her head turned, unhurried, and across thirty feet of twilight and townsfolk she looked directly at me. Held it. Long enough. Then she turned the gray with her knees and walked it out of the square at a pace no constable could lawfully challenge, and vanished down the wool-market lane.

The crowd murmured on about the Dunmores, not knowing what had passed among them. I stood with my heart going hard and slow, and I understood two things at once, the way one understands a blow and its pain on separate delays.

The first: she had come to see the trap for herself, which meant the boy was with them, which meant the atlas was with them, which meant my deadline had been received and *answered* — answered with a look, insolent as a slapped glove.

The second was worse. She had looked at me the way old Dunmore had spoken to me. Without fear. As a thing to be measured.

I had built my case on the assumption that frightened people run. I had forgotten to ask what happens when they stop being frightened, and start being *determined*, and have a river full of secret ways and three months of dark nights ahead of them.

Ice-break, I had promised them. Very well.

I turned for the courthouse steps, and already, faint under the town's noises, from the direction of the Vell, I thought I could hear it beginning — the first far-off groan of the river, talking in its sleep.
