## 14. Rench's Conscience

There is a particular smell to a magistrate's back room in winter, and I have become an expert in it, the way a gravedigger becomes an expert in soil. Tallow smoke, wet wool, ink gone sour in its pot, and beneath it all — and this is the part they never put in the ballads — the faint sweetness of paper. Paper is where men like me live now. I am made of testimony. Somewhere in Vellmark there is a file with my name on it, and inside that file there are sentences I spoke about the Dunmore family, and every one of those sentences is a lie, and every one of them has been notarized.

I want to be clear about something, because cowards are rarely permitted clarity: I knew what I was doing when I did it.

I was paid eleven shillings a week to sit in the taproom of the Salt Ewe and listen. That is the whole of my trade. Listening. A man who talks too much after two ales will tell you where he keeps his ledger; a woman proud of her son will tell you when he comes home; and I carry it all to Magistrate Corvus Hale in a bucket, the way a knacker's boy carries offal. Eleven shillings. It was enough. It has always been enough. That is the entire biography of Tobias Rench: things were enough.

And then one gray Tuesday in February, Hale sent for me, and offered me thirty shillings a week and a coat, and asked me to do nothing more than what I had already done — except this time to do it under oath, at the assizes, with my hand on a book that supposedly binds a man's soul to his words.

I said yes before he finished asking. That is the detail I cannot forgive. Not that I said yes, but the speed of it. My mouth was ahead of my conscience by a full sentence, which for me constitutes a record.

* * *

Hale's office above the courtroom smells different from the back room — beeswax and bay rum, the scent of a man who has decided the world will accommodate him. He sat behind his desk with his fingers steepled, and he looked at me the way a carpenter looks at timber: measuring, approving, already deciding where the nails go.

"Rench," he said. "Sit. Do you know what a justice of the peace is worth?"

I sat. The chair had been placed closer to the desk than chairs usually sit, so that you felt you were leaning forward even when you weren't. Everything in that room was arranged like that. I once watched him seat a grain merchant so that the light fell full on the man's face while Hale sat in shadow, and by the end of the interview the merchant had confessed to short weights just to escape being seen so clearly.

"I couldn't say, sir."

"Neither could I," Hale said pleasantly, "and I am one. Worth is a question of context. In peacetime, a magistrate's word is worth whatever his reputation carries. In a time of smuggling and sedition, his word is worth whatever he can prove." He opened a drawer and set something on the desk between us. It was a folio, leather-bound, and I knew it at once because I had helped carry it out of Vane's workshop after the old man died — or rather, I had stood in the doorway holding my hat while better men than me carried it. An atlas. The dead cartographer's atlas of the river.

"This," said Hale, "is going to hang three people. Perhaps four, if the son runs his mouth. And you, Rench, are the hinge of it. Your testimony places Dunmore at the ferry steps on the night of the twelfth of December."

"Yes, sir."

"Can you place him there *confidently*? Can you look twelve jurymen in their honest stupid faces and swear to it?"

The question hung there. There are moments when a man is handed his soul back for inspection, given one chance to say *this isn't mine*, and I want the record to show that I understood the moment completely. I understood it, and I heard myself say:

"He was there, sir. I saw him."

"Good lad." Hale smiled. He has a good smile; that is the unfairness of the world, that the worst men get the best smiles. "Thirty shillings a week. A blue coat with the assize colors. And at the spring quarter-day, if all goes as I expect it to go, the post of court crier. Steady wages, a hat with fur on it, and your name spoken aloud in a room full of people. You'd like that, Rench. Don't lie to me — you'd like it very much."

He was right. God help me, he was right. I have wanted, my whole life, to be someone whose name is spoken rather than murmured. I wanted it so badly that my hand was already reaching for the oath he slid across the desk.

I signed. Of course I signed. Let no one reading this ever believe that courage is a matter of character and cowardice a matter of weakness, as if they were opposite muscles. They are the same muscle, and most of us use it in only one direction our whole lives, the way a door swings only inward.

"There is one more thing," Hale said, as I rose. "The Kestrel girl. If she comes near the town before ice-break, you'll send word to me within the hour. She trusts you still, I think — or trusted you once. Girls keep old maps of people. Find out if she means to come back for the Dunmore boy."

And there it was. Maren Kestrel. I had sold smaller things before — nights, names, the contents of other men's pockets as reported over ale. But she had once pulled me out of the flooded millrace when we were children, both of us screaming, and I had never repaid her, and I had told myself that this counted as a kind of credit. That day in Hale's office I learned what my credit was worth. Thirty shillings a week, payable quarterly, plus a hat.

* * *

I did not sleep that night, which surprised me, because I had slept perfectly well after worse. After I put the ferry-steps story into Hale's ledger, I slept like a fed dog. After I described — invented, wholly, from nothing — the sound of Tam Dunmore's voice saying *after ice-break, then*, words the man has never spoken to me or anyone, I went home and ate half a goose pie.

But the blue coat broke something. It lay across my chair where Hale's clerk had left it, and in the candlelight it was the most beautiful thing I owned, and I sat looking at it with the peculiar horror of a man watching himself drown in shallow water. Thirty shillings. A fur-trimmed hat. My name spoken aloud — *the Crown calls Tobias Rench* — in a room where Sela Dunmore would be sitting with her hands folded, listening to me describe a night I had spent drunk and alone.

My mother used to say I had a talent for making myself small. She meant it kindly. Small men survive; she buried two large ones.

Here is what I did instead of sleeping, and I will describe it exactly, because exactness is all the penance I can afford. I put on my old coat — brown, patched, honest as a turnip — and I walked through the frozen lanes of Vellmark at three in the morning to the magistrate's office, and I let myself in with the key I should not have had, which Hale gave me himself two years ago along with instructions to deliver certain papers quietly and read none of them.

I read all of them.

It took until dawn. There were letters in a locked drawer — I won't pretend picking the lock was difficult; the lock was older than Hale's ambitions — and the letters were addressed in a small cramped hand to one *Aldous Vane, cartographer, deceased,* and later, after Vane stopped answering, to one *Fettermile, engraver, Gull Row.* The letters discussed plates. Fords. Depths of water at particular bends of the Vell River, and the placement of stones that showed only at low water, and — this was the sentence I copied four times because my hand kept shaking and blotting it — *the true crossing at Kestrel Bend must not appear in any version that leaves this office.*

You see what that means. Everyone in Vellmark believes Aldous Vane died in a fall, a sad accident, an old man on ice. But here was Hale writing to an engraver, months before Vane died, arranging for a copy of the atlas — a copy with alterations — and complaining that Vane himself *"will not amend his plate to suit the requirements of order."* Requirements of order. That is what Hale called it. He needed the true ford erased and a false one drawn in its place, because a false ford drowns smugglers and a true one feeds them, and Corvus Hale had been selling the true ford's location to the tax farmers for years while letting the Kestrel gang take the blame for every keg that moved on the river.

Vane wouldn't falsify his own work. So Vane died. And the page that proved everything — the original plate of Kestrel Bend — had been cut from the folio, and Hale had been hunting for it ever since, and somewhere out on the frozen river a boy named Orsen Dunmore was probably hunting for it too.

I sat in Hale's dark office among his warm-smelling papers and I understood, with the terrible simplicity of three in the morning, that I was the only living man who could connect the letters to the folio to the grave. Fettermile would never talk — engravers are paid to be silent. Hale would never confess. The gang was outlawed and their word was worth less than mine, which is to say nothing. It came down to a paid informer in a patched brown coat, kneeling on a cold floor with a stolen key in his fist, copying letters by candle stub.

So I copied them. Every letter, word for word, in my best hand — and my hand is good; years of forging other men's observations taught me a fair round script. I copied the dates, the seals, the little marginal calculations where Hale worked out his percentages on confiscated grain. I copied the line about Vane twice, pressing hard, so that even if the ink faded the impression would remain in the paper beneath. I folded the pages flat against my chest, under my shirt, where they lay against my skin like a hot iron, and I locked the drawer, and I let myself out into the blue hour before dawn, and I walked home past the church where the Dunmores will sit in the dock, and I threw up in the snowdrift outside it.

I mention the vomiting for honesty's sake. Courage in stories is clean. Mine smelled of bile and bay rum.

* * *

The next problem was where to put the copies, and this nearly undid me, because I discovered that morning that a coward's first brave act is also his loneliest. There was no one to tell. Hale owns the constables. Half the town owes him money and the other half fears him. If I handed the papers to a neighbor, I would be in the river by Friday and the papers would be kindling by Saturday.

I thought about taking them to the Dunmores themselves, and dismissed it — their house would be watched now, surely, with the trial coming. I thought about a priest, and remembered that Vellmark's priest owed Hale nine pounds. I thought, absurdly, of putting them in the church collection plate and letting God sort it out, and I laughed alone in my rented room, and the laugh came out wrong, and I decided I was going mad at a very reasonable pace.

Then, on the third day, the town solved my problem for me, the way towns do, badly.

Word came down from the north road with the mail sled: Orsen Dunmore had fled. Slipped Hale's watchmen like a fish through a net — and the watchmen, I learned later, were telling anyone who'd buy them ale that the boy had vanished at the river, at the ford below the old stepping-stones, a stretch of water no honest map marks. He'd crossed where the atlas says to cross. He'd gone over to the gang.

By evening the notice was nailed to the courthouse door. ACCOMPLICE, it said, in letters tall enough to read from a moving sleigh. TEN POUNDS. And beneath it, in Hale's own hand, a deadline dressed up as mercy: *Let the fugitives surrender themselves before the breaking of the ice, that the assizes may proceed with leniency toward the family.*

Leniency toward the family. I have read enough of Hale's correspondence now to translate. It meant: bring me the boy and the girl, or I hang all of them and call it grief.

That night I took off the blue coat — I hadn't worn it once; it hung on my chair like a debt — and I folded the copied letters inside it, and I put the bundle in my market pannier under the dried herring, because nobody searches a herring basket, the smell defends itself. And I walked out the north gate at dusk, past the tollhouse, past the last farm, onto the river road, walking toward the one place in the province where a man carrying proof against Corvus Hale might find someone with both the motive and the nerve to use it.

The camp of the Kestrel gang.

Me. Tobias Rench, informer, walking voluntarily into the camp of outlaws, carrying papers that would hang me beside them if I were caught, to hand them to a girl I informed on, to save a family I lied about. If you had told me a month ago that this was my future, I would have laughed, and then checked whether you were one of Hale's men testing my loyalty.

The river groaned underfoot as I walked — a long, deep sound, like a giant shifting in his sleep, the ice speaking its intentions. Everyone in Vellmark knows that sound. It means the winter is ending whether we're ready or not. It means the water underneath is awake and running, and the crossings we have relied on all these cold months are counting down their last days.

I have perhaps a fortnight. Maybe less. The ice will break, the assizes will convene, Hale will mount his scaffold of lies, and somewhere between here and Kestrel Bend, a coward carrying herring has to find a hardened outlaw girl in the dark and convince her that the biggest liar in Vellmark has finally, at ruinous expense, told the truth.

Behind me, the town lights came on one by one, small and yellow and obedient. Ahead, the river cracked again — louder this time, closer — and I walked toward the sound.
