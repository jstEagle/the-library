## 15. The Forger's Confession

I had killed men before the winter of '47 was out — not with my own hands, not always, but I'd sent them down roads I knew ended badly, and I had learned to sleep. What I could not learn to sleep through was hope. Hope kept me up like a toothache.

Orsen Dunmore came to our camp at dusk looking like a man carrying a lit coal in his shirt, and he said, "The forger's name is Ambrose Pell," and I felt that toothache start.

"You're certain?"

"Rench's papers say Hale's clerk paid a draftsman in Hollowmere three times last autumn. Three payments, each larger than a draftsman earns in a year. And there's only one man within fifty miles who can rule a border line that clean." Orsen spread Rench's stolen correspondence on the tarp by our fire, and there it was: *A. Pell, engraver, Hollowmere* — written in a hand so tidy it looked embarrassed to be involved in anything at all.

Our gang's leader then was a man called Duncrake, and Duncrake did not like plans that involved towns. Towns had walls, and walls had ears, and ears had a way of arriving at Hale's magistrate's house attached to warrants. But even Duncrake understood what sat on that tarp. A confession from the man who forged the atlas copy would undo everything Hale had built. It would prove the atlas Hale waved about like scripture was a lie drawn for money. It would prove my family had never smuggled anything worse than flour through a famine.

"He'll never talk," Duncrake said, poking the fire with a knife. "Men who sell their pens don't die for them."

"Men who sell their pens don't get paid enough to die *quietly* either," I said. "Hale silenced Vane because Vane wouldn't lie. He'll silence Pell because Pell already has. The only question is whether we get to him first."

Duncrake looked at me a long moment. He had a face like a root — gnarled, patient, hard to read — and when he finally nodded, it was the nod of a man agreeing to weather he doesn't trust. "Take Kestrel," he said. "She talks to townfolk without wanting to stab them. Take Dunmore too, since this is his crusade. If you're not back in four days, I assume you're dead and plan accordingly."

"Charming," said Orsen.

"That's the general idea," said Duncrake.

* * *

We went into Hollowmere separately, meeting at the churchyard wall where the dead of three generations stood around like disapproving chaperones. Hollowmere in late February was mud pretending to be a town — one street, a wool market that had seen better decades, and above a cooper's shop, a window with a lamp burning past any honest hour.

"A draftsman working late," Orsen murmured beside me. "Either he's industrious or he's afraid."

"With men like Pell it's usually both."

We climbed the outside stair as quietly as two people can climb when one of them is a mapmaker who narrates every creaking board with an apologetic look. I knocked once. The lamp inside guttered, went dark, stayed dark.

"Mister Pell," I said to the door. "We know about the atlas."

Silence. Then, muffled: "I don't know what you mean."

"You copied Master Vane's atlas for Magistrate Hale," Orsen said. "You changed the fords. You moved the true crossing at Kestrel Bend and drew a false one two miles south. We have the original plate. We have Hale's payment records. What we don't have is you, saying it aloud, where the law can hear you."

Another silence, longer. Then bolts — two, three — and the door opened on a small stooped man holding a candle like it might defend him. Ambrose Pell had ink stains halfway to his elbows and the eyes of a man who had spent twenty years squinting at things smaller than truth.

"I knew this day would come," he said, simply, and let us in.

* * *

His workshop was a single cold room that smelled of varnish and fear. Maps hung everywhere — good maps, honest maps, the kind of work a man spends a life learning and then, apparently, sells in one bad autumn. Orsen drifted toward them the way I've seen him drift toward any flat surface with lines on it, and I put a hand on his arm to bring him back to business.

"Why did you do it?" he asked Pell. Not cruel. That's the thing about Orsen — he asks questions like a priest, like the answer matters to him personally, and it undoes people faster than a knife would.

Pell sat down heavily at his bench. "Because my daughter was sick, and Hale paid, and I told myself a map is only paper." He looked up. "It isn't, though. Is it. I've known that thirty years and told myself it was, for eleven weeks."

"What exactly did Hale ask for?" I said.

"To copy the atlas. Exactly. Every river, every crossing — except Kestrel Bend. There was a true ford there, marked in Vane's hand, deep and reliable. Hale wanted it erased and replaced with a false crossing downstream, over the shallows near Miller's Rill." Pell's hands made the shape of it in the air, unconscious, a draftsman explaining his crime in geometry. "He said it was to catch smugglers. Draw them into waters they thought were safe." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "I believed him for a fortnight. Then I understood. A false ford doesn't catch smugglers. It kills anyone who trusts the map. And if it ever came to trial, the false map wouldn't hang smugglers. It would hang whoever Hale needed it to hang. He showed me the testimony he intended to use. Names. Your names." He nodded at Orsen, and at me. "I drew the noose myself. In iron gall ink. It's permanent, you know. Iron gall. Outlasts the paper."

"Then un-draw it," I said. "Testify. At the assizes. Say all of this again with Hale in the room."

"You don't understand what—" Pell stopped. He was listening to something below us, or beyond us, or inside us; I couldn't tell. His face had gone the color of the candle. "He watches me. He has a man watch me. There's a fellow drinks at the Woolpack who never finishes his pint, and he's been there every night since November—"

"Pell," I said. "In three weeks the ice breaks and the assizes sit. If you testify, your daughter gets a new life somewhere far from here, paid for by people who owe you more than they know. If you don't testify, you and she are both dead anyway, because Hale does not leave loose pens lying about. Ask yourself how Aldous Vane died."

That landed. I watched it land. A man who has spent his life being precise knows precisely when his arithmetic has run out.

"I have the draft commission," he said slowly. "Hale's letter. I keep everything — it's the disease of my trade. Signed instructions. His seal." He got up, steadier now, crossed to a cabinet with three locks, and began working them. "You'll want the date, and the second payment record, and the note where he specifies Miller's Rill. I kept the note. God help me, I kept the note because some part of me always meant to use it, or meant to be found holding it, which is the same thing for a coward—"

The lamp in the street below went out.

I want to be fair to myself in this account: I heard the door of the cooper's shop ease open, and I moved. But Pell had his back turned and three locks' worth of confidence in his own importance, and men in his state do not stop talking.

"—he wrote it himself, you see, no clerk, that's how careful he—"

The window broke inward.

Afterward, whole scenes of it stay with me like splinters. The crossbow bolt taking Pell mid-word, high in the back, so that his sentence died in his mouth and he folded over the cabinet like laundry. Orsen shouting and pulling me down behind the bench as a second bolt buried itself in the plaster where my head had been. The shooter moving away across the rooftops with the unhurried competence of a man doing a chore — not fleeing, *leaving*, which is different and much worse.

And the cabinet open. Papers fanned across the floor like dealt cards. Hale's seal staring up at me from half a dozen letters.

Victory snatched away — that's what I'd call it later, in the years when I could afford to call it anything. That night it was just noise and blood and Orsen kneeling beside a dying man who was trying very hard to say something.

"The note," Pell whispered. "Miller's Rill. Under the false bottom. Third drawer. He wrote it *himself*—"

Blood, and then less blood, and then none. Draftsmen are precise to the end. He died between one word and the next, leaving the last one forever unsaid, and I have wondered since whether it was his daughter's name.

* * *

We searched the room while the town slept its stupid innocent sleep, and we found what he'd promised: a drawer with a false bottom, and under it, folded into the size of a playing card, a note in Magistrate Corvus Hale's own unmistakable hand.

*For the amendment described — the relocation of the crossing to Miller's Rill — twenty guineas upon delivery, and silence thereafter. C.H.*

Below that, the date. Below that, his seal, pressed fat and red as a wound.

Orsen held it under the candle and read it three times, and I watched my promise-made-in-peacetime mapmaker turn into something harder while I watched.

"This is it," he said. "This, with Rench's testimony, with the original plate — this ends him."

"Yes," I said. I was looking at Pell, at the ink-stained hands, at the small neat room full of honest maps that would now hang unsold forever. "And it cost a man his throat to write it. Do you notice how everything that convicts Hale arrives pre-murdered? Rench steals papers at the risk of hanging. Pell dies the hour before he can speak. We keep winning, Orsen, and the dead pile up like receipts."

Orsen folded the note with the care he gave to plates of the atlas. "Then we make sure this receipt gets paid," he said, "and not to us."

From the street below came the sound I had been dreading all evening and pretending not to hear: boots. Several pairs. Moving with purpose toward the cooper's stair.

"Hale's watcher wasn't alone," I said. "Out the back. Now."

We went down through the cooper's yard, over a fence slick with February, Orsen clutching the note inside his coat like a heartbeat, and behind us the upstairs window of Ambrose Pell's workshop bloomed orange as someone set it alight — burning the room, burning the honest maps, erasing the crime scene the way Hale erased crossings.

We ran along the frozen ditch toward the dark fields and the river road, and it was only when we stopped, both of us heaving, that Orsen said what we were both thinking.

"The assizes sit when the ice breaks. Hale will say Pell was murdered by the Kestrel gang — by *you* — to silence a witness against smugglers. He'll build a whole case on a corpse he made." He looked at me, and there was something breaking and something hardening in his face at the same time. "Maren. We have days. Maybe less. He won't wait for the law this time — he'll move against the camp, against my family, against everyone, and claim it was justice after the fact."

"Then we don't wait either," I said.

Downriver, faint under the stars, came a sound I had known all my life and never feared until that winter: the river, groaning in its sleep. The ice was beginning to talk.

Everything was converging on the water, and we had three days to get ahead of it.
