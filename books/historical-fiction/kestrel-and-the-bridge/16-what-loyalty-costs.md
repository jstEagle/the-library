## 16. What Loyalty Costs

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that no master ever teaches an apprentice mapmaker, and it is this: how to weigh a father's neck against a hundred strangers' secrets, and how to do the sum at three in the morning by the light of a tallow stub that has burned down to a puddle of itself.

I had been attempting it for most of the night. I was not succeeding. Mathematics, I have found, holds up well under most conditions, but it has never once survived contact with love.

The atlas sat before me on the workbench — *my* atlas now, though I still could not say the word without hearing Vane cough disapprovingly from somewhere behind my left shoulder. Twelve plates of the Vell River, drawn in his hand and finished in mine, each one honest to the width of a hair. And folded inside the back board, wrapped in oilcloth like something that might bite, the thirteenth plate. The cut leaf from the master's folio. The true ford at Kestrel Bend.

One piece of paper. It could hang Magistrate Corvus Hale as neatly as any rope, prove his forged atlas a forgery, scatter his case against my family like chaff before wind.

It could also, if it ever reached the wrong desk in the capital, deliver every crossing, every shallow, every secret passage of the Kestrel gang into the Crown's hands — and Maren with them.

*Choose,* said the candle. *Choose.*

I told the candle what it could do with its advice.

* * *

You should understand something about me, since we have come this far together: I have spent my whole life believing that truth was a place you could stand. Solid ground. Surveyed, charted, marked with a bench-mark cut into the stone. My master taught me that a false map kills people — shepherds led onto scree, ferrymen trusting a channel that silted over — and so the cartographer's first vow, unwritten but iron, is *draw what is, not what is wished.*

Vane died for that vow. Refused to bend a river two inches to the left for men who wanted the bends kept dark, and had his skull opened for his stubbornness. Every hour I had spent hunched over his plates since, every aching wrist and ruined eye, had been a kind of prayer to the same altar. *Truth is ground. Stand on it.*

Then came the winter when standing on truth meant feeding Maren Kestrel to the gallows, and I discovered that ground, surveyed enough, will always show you a fault line somewhere.

Because here was the thing I had learned at the camp, wrapped in a borrowed blanket smelling of woodsmoke and horse, eating stew that the law called stolen and hunger called salvation: those fords were not abstractions. They were the difference between grain reaching a famine village and grain rotting in a magistrate's warehouse while its owner waited for a better price. They were children who did not starve, in the winter the taxes starved them anyway. If the Crown took those crossings, it would not merely catch outlaws. It would close a door that half the border country was living behind.

And here was the other thing: my father was in Hale's cells, and the ice would break in a fortnight, and when it broke, Hale's deadline broke with it. *Produce the fugitives by ice-break or the whole family hangs.* He had said it with the pleasant weariness of a man discussing weather, because to Corvus Hale everything not himself was weather.

Truth versus loyalty. It sounds clean when you say it that way, doesn't it? Two noble words, one difficult choice. Nobody tells you the truth has teeth on one side and loyalty bleeds on the other, and whichever one you grip, you are going to need bandages.

* * *

Maren found me at dawn, because Maren always finds me at dawn, or I find her, or the dawn simply arranges itself around whichever of us is more lost. She came over the ridge above the camp with frost in her hair and her rifle slung with the casual geometry of someone who had stopped noticing it was there, which frightened me more than if she'd carried it pointed.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"What gave me away? The trembling, or the fact that I've been talking to a candle?"

"The candle started it, didn't it." She sat down beside me on the cold bench, shoulder just touching mine, the way she had sat beside me a thousand times at the drafting table when we were young and the worst thing in the world was a smudged coastline. "Tell me."

So I laid it out, because lying to Maren Kestrel has never once worked and I am too old a dog for new tricks. The plate. The choice. Publish and the Crown gets the fords; withhold and my father swings; find some third road and — I gestured at the blank space where a third road ought to be.

She listened all the way through without moving. That was the worst part. Old Maren would have interrupted four times, argued twice, laughed once. This Maren let silence sit on my words like snow.

"If you give them the river," she said finally, "they won't just take the fords. They'll take the villages that used them. Every farmer who carried a sack across at night. Hale's ledger already names half of them — he's been keeping score for years, waiting for someone worth trading them against." She looked at her hands. "My brother crossed at Miller's Shallows eleven times last famine winter. He's fourteen. They hang boys of fourteen, Orsen. I've seen the lists."

"And if I don't," I said, "they hang my father, who has never in his life broken anything worse than a sabbath, and probably not even that. My mother next, likely. Then me." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "You see my difficulty. Both roads are paved with rope."

"Yes," she said. "I know. I've been walking them since I was fifteen."

We sat with that a while. Outside, the camp was waking — Rench's borrowed papers spread under Brann's fist, the murmur of the gang deciding whether the mapmaker in their midst was a tool or a liability, a debate from which Maren had so far shielded me at the cost of whatever credit she'd banked with them. Somewhere a horse complained about existence. Ordinary morning sounds, for people the law had declared extraordinary.

"There might be a third road," Maren said at last, slowly, as if testing ice ahead of her boots. "The papers Rench copied. Hale's letters to the forger. They prove the commission — they don't need the plate to prove it."

"They prove Hale commissioned a forgery," I agreed. "They don't prove what the forgery hid. At trial, Hale's advocate waves them off — a disgruntled clerk, a bribed informer. Words against a magistrate." I shook my head. "No. Judges believe paper they can hold. The plate is the paper they can hold. It matches my master's plates line for line, watermark for watermark, and the moment a court lays the forged copy beside the true one, Hale isn't a corrupt official anymore. He's a murderer holding the murder weapon."

"Then you've answered yourself."

"I've answered myself three dozen times tonight and un-answered myself three dozen and one." I put my head in my hands. "Don't you see the cruelty of it? The only proof strong enough to save my family is proof that destroys yours. Whoever drew this trap understood maps, Maren. They understood that a map of the truth is also a map of everything you'd rather keep hidden."

She was quiet for a long moment. When I looked up, she was studying the oilcloth packet as though she could read the plate through it.

"My father used to say," she said, "that the river doesn't choose sides. It just goes where the land tells it." A pause. "He also used to say never trust a man who quotes rivers when he's avoiding the point, so I'll make the point. You asked me once to let you stand where I could find you. Well. Here I am, finding you. Whatever you choose, I'll not pretend I'll thank you for choosing against my people. But I'll know why. That's worth something. It may be all there is."

* * *

I wish I could tell you I then rose, inspired, and saw the clever third road glittering ahead. I did not. I spent the middle of that day doing what sensible cowards do with impossible choices: I deferred to an authority higher than myself, namely my dead master, and went looking through Vane's notes for a man who no longer had to live with the consequences of his decisions.

His field journal was with the rest of our salvaged effects, and I sat with it in the lee of the supply tent while the wind worried the canvas, reading entries I must have read ten times before, hunting for a sentence that had never been written.

But near the end — the last week of his life, the ink hurried, the hand less certain than I had ever seen it — I found something I had read and not *understood*, the way you can walk past a door your whole life and one day notice it opens.

*The Crown's man offered triple the survey fee for the eastern sheets alone. Asked that the fords be generalized. 'Approximated.' Told him a map that approximates a ford drowns men. He smiled as if I'd quoted scripture he intended to ignore. There are two buyers in this country for the same lie, and neither cares whom it drowns. If anything happens to me, Dunmore — the boy reads well, but warn him: a map made to serve a master serves everyone but the traveler.*

A map made to serve a master serves everyone but the traveler.

I sat with that until my hands stopped shaking, or until I stopped noticing they were shaking, which amounts to the same thing in practice.

Because that was it, wasn't it? Both of my roads — publish or withhold — were roads drawn to serve a master. Publish, and the atlas served the Crown. Withhold, and it served the gang, or served my fear, which amounts to the same thing in different livery. What Vane had died protecting was not a set of coordinates. It was the principle that the map belongs to the traveler — to the shepherd on the scree and the ferryman in the channel and yes, to the starving farmer at Miller's Shallows, and to the honest magistrate too, if the country ever grew one.

The truth of the river wasn't mine to spend. Not on my father's life. Not even on Maren's.

Which left me precisely nowhere, except that *nowhere*, for the first time in three days, felt like solid ground.

* * *

I found Maren at the river's edge that evening, watching the ice. You could hear it changing — a deep, irregular groaning underfoot of the world, like some enormous animal turning over in its sleep. Three days early, the thaw was coming. Chunks of shore-fast ice had already calved away and were riding the black current downstream, grinding and spinning.

"It's breaking," she said, without turning. "Brann wants to run the ford tomorrow night. Last crossing before the water takes it till summer. After that the gang scatters to the high country." Now she turned. "And you?"

"I'm going to the assizes," I said. "With the plate, and Rench's papers, and my testimony. All of it. Everything true, laid out in open court, including the ford."

Her face didn't change, but something behind it did — a shutter drawing, slow.

"Including the ford," she repeated.

"Every line of it. In front of judges and clerks and Hale himself." I pulled my coat tighter against the river wind. "Here's the thing I finally understand, Maren, standing where you could find me. If I publish the atlas quietly, the Crown takes the fords and uses them against your people, and my father maybe lives. If I burn the plate, Hale wins and my father dies and your people get hunted anyway, because Hale doesn't need the truth to persecute you — he only needs the lie, which he already owns. But if I *say it* — if I stand in a courtroom and swear to every crossing on that river, openly, with my name attached, with Hale's crimes attached to the same sheet—" I stopped, because I hadn't quite finished building this thought aloud before, and saying it made me hear its holes. "Then it isn't intelligence delivered to an army. It's testimony. Public, sworn, argued over, challenged. The fords become history instead of ammunition. Maybe. It's the only version where the map serves the traveler and nobody else."

"That's a beautiful speech," Maren said. "Do you actually believe a courtroom will hear it that way?"

"No," I admitted. "Not entirely. I believe it might. I believe it's the only road where I'm not spending other people's blood to buy my own peace. And I believe—" and here my voice betrayed me, wry narration failing at the exact moment it mattered, "—that if it goes wrong, if the Crown does take your crossings and hunt your people, then I will have done that, and you will have every right to never find me again. I'm not asking you to forgive it in advance. I'm asking you to know that I see the cost, and I'm choosing it with my eyes open, because the alternative choices require closing them."

The ice groaned again, long and low, and somewhere out in the dark a crack ran like a whip-crack across a mile of frozen river.

Maren looked at me for a long time. Frost had formed on her lashes. Behind her, the water showed black between the white, widening by the hour.

"Brann will call it betrayal," she said at last. "Running to the assizes with the river in your pocket, whatever pretty word you dress it in. Some of them will want to stop you." Her jaw set. "I'll want to let them, some part of me, and I won't. Because you stood where I could find you, and what I found was a man telling me an expensive truth to my face, which is rarer in this country than dry powder." She stepped closer. "So here's mine, given the same way. Run the ford tonight, before the ice goes. I'll take you as far as the south bank myself. Because come sunrise, that crossing won't exist — and Orsen?" The ghost of the old smile, the peacetime smile, surfaced and sank. "Neither will the easy version of anything. From here on, every road runs through water."

Out on the river, the ice sang — one long descending note, like the whole winter being drawn off a whetstone — and beneath our feet, faint but unmistakable, the first shudder of the breaking began.
