## 17. Ice Breaking

The river spoke first.

I'd been listening to it my whole life, so I knew its voices the way other folk knew their mothers'. The whisper of open water in October. The groan when the cold first clamped down and the Vell turned to stone. But this — this was new, or old, or both. A deep cracking from under the world, like some enormous thing turning over in its sleep. Three nights past the full moon, and the ice was talking.

I sat up in the lean-to with my knife already in my hand, which tells you something about how I'd spent the last two years. Beside me, Doss Kestrel — my father's brother, a man built like a root cellar — was awake too, staring at the ceiling of woven branches as though he could see through it to the sky.

"You hear it," he said. Not a question.

"Hard to miss."

"That's early." He said it the way a priest says *amen*, flat and final. "Ice-break's not due for a fortnight. Warm wind out of the south last night, and now she's letting go ahead of schedule." He rolled onto his feet with the effort of a man half his age twice his size. "We run tonight, Mare. Tonight or never."

*Never* was a word that had been following our family around like a stray dog for years now, and I was tired of feeding it.

* * *

The camp woke the way camps do — badly, in stages, with complaining. Forty-odd souls scattered along the high bank above Kestrel Bend: smugglers, sure, if you liked the Crown's word for it, though most of what we'd carried across that river in the famine winters was flour and salt pork and medicine for farms that Hale's tax men had stripped to the floorboards. Outlaws, the proclamation said. *Enemies of the Crown.* I'd stopped arguing with proclamations around the time one of them put my name on it.

By the fire pit, the council gathered without anyone calling it. That was how we did things. My uncle Doss. Brannic, who ran the north shore contacts and trusted no one further than he could throw a horse, which was, in fairness, quite far. Old Wenna, who kept our books in her head and our secrets everywhere else. And me, holding a place I hadn't asked for and couldn't put down.

"We run the ford one last time," Doss said. "Full cargo. The Halvorsens are out of meal, the Millers have sick children needing the quinine, and after the thaw there'll be no crossing till the ferry's rebuilt — if it's ever rebuilt. This is the last load before the river shuts us out."

"And the magistrate?" Brannic asked. His eyes found me, the way everyone's eyes found me lately, since the Dunmore boy had come stumbling into camp with his ink-stained fingers and his honest face. "Hale knows the season's ending. If he means to catch us at the ford, this is when he sets his net."

"Hale has a problem," I said. "His problem is currently sleeping in the willow thicket by the horse lines and dreaming about an atlas."

That got the thin smile that always went around at Orsen's expense, though it wasn't unkind. Hard to sneer at a man who'd walked away from a warm bed and a clean name to cross a frozen river in the dark because a girl he'd known in peacetime told him the truth was worth more than safety. Some of them thought he was a fool. All of them remembered being young enough to do it themselves.

"The papers," Wenna said. She said it softly, but the word landed heavy. Rench's papers — copies of Hale's own correspondence with the forger who'd built the false atlas, copied out by a paid informer with shaking hands and a conscience that had arrived late but arrived. "If the boy reaches the assizes with those, Hale falls."

"If he reaches the assizes," Brannic said. "Assizes sit at Harrowgate in nine days. It's four days' hard ride, and every road between here and there has Hale's men on it like fleas on a dog. And meanwhile—" he jerked his chin toward the river, where the cracking had gone from a murmur to a low argument, "—that's coming apart under our feet."

"So we do both," I said.

They all looked at me.

"We split. Doss runs the ford tonight with the cargo and half the band, like we've done twenty times, because those families downstream don't eat good intentions. And I take Orsen south — the long way, the shepherd tracks, away from the roads — and get him to Harrowgate before the sessions close." I looked around the circle. "The papers die if we all hang back waiting for safe. Safe died with the famine winter. We just haven't held the funeral."

Silence, except the river. Then a crack so loud and so deep that several heads turned toward the water, and somewhere down the bank a horse screamed and kicked its picket stake loose.

"Ice is breaking faster than your plan," Brannic said.

"The ice doesn't care about any of our plans," I said. "It hasn't from the start."

Doss put his hand on my shoulder, heavy as a roof beam. "Your father would've said the same thing," he told me, "and then he'd have said you're not going alone, and then he'd have lost the argument, same as I'm going to." He sighed. "Take Tolly. He's light in the saddle and useless in a fight, which is the correct configuration for a messenger."

Old Wenna caught my sleeve as the council broke. Her eyes were milky at the edges but missed nothing.

"You love him," she said. Not a question either. That night was full of statements dressed as questions.

"He's the key to clearing my name and forty others," I said. "I'm fond of keys."

"Mm." She patted my hand with fingers like birch roots. "I've watched you lie to better liars than yourself, girl. Don't waste a good lie on me. Just — bring him back or leave him whole, whichever the road allows. Hearts keep. Men don't, always."

* * *

I found Orsen at the map table — of course he'd found the map table; he'd had the look of a man finding religion ever since he'd laid out Master Vane's atlas on it three nights ago and shown us what the missing plate proved. He was hunched over Rench's papers with a candle stub, reading them for the fourth time that I knew of, as though repetition might change what they said. It didn't. They said Hale had commissioned a forged atlas. They named the man who'd drawn it. They were going to hang a magistrate or get everyone in this camp strung up trying.

"You heard the ice," I said.

He didn't look up. "Is that what it is? I thought it was the world ending."

"It's the world beginning. Spring. Come on — you're leaving."

That got his head up. There was a smudge of ink on his jaw, which shouldn't have made my chest hurt, and did anyway. "Leaving. You mean—"

"Harrogate. Assizes. Nine days, and you're the witness, and these—" I tapped the papers, "—are the hammer. I'm taking you myself. Shepherd tracks the whole way. Tolly rides with us."

He stood slowly, gathering the papers with the care of a man handling a bird's eggs. "Maren. The ford. Your people need every hand for the crossing, if the ice is—"

"My people need a magistrate in chains more than they need one extra pair of hands hauling flour sacks through slush. Besides," and here I made my voice light, because it was easier than making it true, "you'd sink like an anvil out there. Mapmakers are ballast, not boatmen."

He almost smiled. Almost. Instead he looked at me with that terrible directness of his, the one that had undone me in a summer workshop full of ink and sunlight, when the worst thing either of us feared was a badly drawn coastline.

"You promised you'd explain everything once we were riding," he said. "I'm riding. Explain."

So I did — the plan, the timing, the risk, all of it — while he packed his satchel and I checked girths and cinches in the dark of the horse lines, and the river talked and talked behind us like an old woman who knows exactly how the story ends and won't be hurried.

* * *

We went out before dawn, three riders in a white world. Tolly led, whistling under his breath, navigating the sheep paths and drover cuts by instincts older than maps. Behind him Orsen, riding stiff and careful, a town boy on a horse but improving. Behind him me, watching our backtrail and thinking about how a life can turn on the weight of paper.

The cracking followed us for miles. Even inland, even with the river hidden behind hills, we could feel it — a tremor in the ground, a pressure in the ears, the sound of a great lock turning over. Once, near the abandoned shepherd's cot at Wolf's Rest, the sound rose to a boom like cannon fire, and Orsen's horse shied and he handled it well, which surprised us both.

"How long has it been since you crossed in summer?" he asked as we walked the horses up a scree slope. He said it casually, but I knew a mapmaker's question when I heard one. He was charting me. He'd been charting me since the ice, the day we met again — the armed girl where the promised girl used to be.

"Four years," I said. "Last time I swam there, I was fifteen. There was a sandbar you could stand on mid-river. Da called it the parlor." I tightened my reins. "The flood took the sandbar the next spring. Took my father the winter after. Different rivers, same appetite."

Orsen was quiet a while. Then: "When this is over. After the assizes. When your name's clear—" He stopped himself, started again. "There's a bridge half-built at the bend. From before. I remember when they broke ground on it. Do you think it'll ever be finished?"

I thought about lying. I was, by trade and talent, a very good liar.

"Ask me after Harrowgate," I said, and spurred up the slope before my face could report anything to his survey.

* * *

We made better time than I'd hoped and worse than I needed, which is the arithmetic of every desperate journey. By the second dusk we could see, far off across the flats, the torch-glitter of a checkpoint on the coach road — Hale's men, doing his bidding, hunting a fugitive cartographer and the girl who'd signed one dangerous letter. We cut wide around them, losing two hours in reeds, and camped in a hollow with no fire and my cloak spread over the three of us like a bad tent over worse odds.

Tolly slept. Orsen didn't. In the dark, his voice came low.

"Maren. If it goes wrong tomorrow. If they take me at the road and the papers are seized—" He shifted, and I felt the satchel against my back, where he'd moved it without asking, trusting me with it more than with himself. "You burn them. You ride east and you don't stop. Promise me."

"They won't take you."

"Promise me anyway. It's what promises are for."

And there it was, the old wound, opened gently: *whatever comes, I will stand where you can find me.* He'd said it on the unfinished bridge when we were children playing at forever, and he'd kept it in ways that should have killed him, and now he wanted another promise stacked on top like weight on a frozen river.

"I promise," I said, because he was right about what promises were for, and wrong about nearly everything else.

Behind us, faint as a heartbeat, the river boomed again. Twice. Three times. Breaking its old shapes. Getting ready to be something new and impossible to cross.

* * *

The third morning, the world changed its mind.

We came down out of the hills at first light to make our final push — one day's hard riding to Harrowgate, sessions opening in two — and below us, where the Vell should have been a single white seam stitching the valley, there was instead a long black wound. Open water. Leads and leads of it, steaming faintly in the cold, plates of ice grinding against each other with a sound like millstones. The break had come south fast in the night, warmer than anyone guessed, eating the river from the mouth upward.

Tolly pulled up short. "Sweet saints," he breathed. "That's a day early. That's a week early."

But it was Orsen I watched. He'd gone rigid in the saddle, and his face had the particular gray of a man doing sums he doesn't want to finish.

"Maren." His voice was very quiet. "The ford. At Kestrel Bend. Your uncle's crossing — the cargo, the families waiting on the south shore—" He turned to me. "The break comes up from the delta. The Bend will go before noon. Maybe sooner. The ice won't hold a laden sledge, it won't even hold—"

He didn't finish, because he didn't have to. I was already doing the arithmetic myself: Doss running the ford with half the band and forty stone of flour per sledge, expecting river-ice hardened by a fortnight more frost, finding instead a rotting shell over black water. My uncle. Tolly's cousins. Wenna's grandchildren hauling rope on the far bank.

Everything we'd ridden three days for sat in my hands — Rench's papers, the fall of Corvus Hale, my name scrubbed clean, justice arriving on schedule. And everything I loved was standing on a lid that was already opening, a hundred miles from anywhere I could help.

"Harrogate's south," Tolly said uselessly. "The Bend's north."

"I know my directions, Tolly."

Orsen leaned forward over his horse's neck, and when he spoke, he spoke like a man laying out a map, which was the only way he knew how to be brave.

"There's a crossing," he said. "Not the ford. The gorge narrows at Miller's Rock — the ice bridges between the boulders might hold a rider if the line is exact. Vane mapped it. I've got it in my head, every bearing. It could be ridden. By someone light. Someone who knows the water."

The wind came up the valley carrying the grind and boom of a river unmaking itself, and Orsen looked at me with those honest eyes that had never once learned to lie.

"Ride for Harrowgate," he said. "Deliver the papers. Save us all. I'll go north."

"No," I said.

"Somebody has to warn them, and Tolly can't navigate the gorge, and you're the only witness Hale truly fears—"

"I said *no*, Orsen."

But even as I said it, I was swinging down from the saddle, pulling the satchel with the papers from where he'd tucked it against me, pressing it into his arms, and we both understood, in the way that people understand each other best in the worst moments, exactly what we'd just decided and who was deciding it.

"The papers go to Harrowgate," I said, mounting hard, turning my horse north. "I'll deliver them after. Assizes sit nine days. I can make the warning, make the gorge, and still reach the city before the sessions close — if the ice lets me, and nothing else goes wrong, and God has forgotten to be cruel for once."

"That's four *ifs* and a prayer," Orsen shouted after me.

"Then pray!" I called back, and drove my heels in, and rode north along a river that was coming apart beneath the whole world — toward the Bend, toward the breaking water, toward whatever was left to save.
