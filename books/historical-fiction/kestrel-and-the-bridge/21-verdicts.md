## 21. Verdicts

The assizes sat in Vellmark's moot hall for eleven days, and for eleven days the town discovered that justice smells like tallow candles and wet wool. I know this because I sat through every hour of it, in the second row behind my mother, close enough to hear the clerk's nib scratch and scratch and scratch, transcribing the ruin of Magistrate Corvus Hale one careful stroke at a time.

My father was acquitted on the fourth day.

I want to tell you it was a moment of triumph, that he came down from the dock with his head high and my mother weeping into her good shawl. What actually happened was smaller and stranger than that. The foreman of the jury — a flax merchant named Odway who had sold grain to the Kestrel gang through two famine winters and knew exactly which side of his bread bore the butter — stood up, cleared his throat, said "not guilty" as though confessing to a crime himself, and then everyone filed out to the yard for ale while my father stood blinking in the dock like a man who had walked into a room and forgotten why.

He found us by the door. He put his arms around my mother first, then me, and he smelled of the gaol — straw and iron and something sour underneath — and he said, into the top of my head, "They let me keep my spectacles. I'd been worrying about the spectacles."

That was my father. Nine weeks in Hale's cells, accused of conspiring with outlaws against the Crown, and his great accounting of suffering was that they had let him keep his spectacles.

I laughed until I cried, which is not a figure of speech. I laughed and then I could not stop crying, and my mother held both of us in the doorway of the moot hall while the whole town pretended not to look, which is the traditional kindness of small towns: they will watch your ruin with perfect attention and grant you privacy only for joy.

* * *

The rest of it took longer, because the rest of it was complicated in ways an acquittal never has to be.

Hale's case had collapsed in the manner of a bad bridge — first one plank, then the whole span going down at once. Rench testified on the sixth day. I had expected him to be pathetic; he was worse than that. He was *thorough*. He produced the ledger of payments he'd received from Hale, itemized in his own hand because he kept records of everything, including his own shame, apparently out of some bookkeeper's compulsion that no amount of guilt could overcome. He described how Hale had coached him, corrected his lies the way a writing master corrects a boy's letters, until the false testimony was neat enough to pass before any jury in the county.

"And why," asked the King's counsel, a dry stick named Ferris who had been sent up from the provincial court when it became clear the local bench was compromised, "did you agree to do this?"

Rench looked at his hands for a long time. The hall waited. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and gave up.

"Because it was easy," he said at last. "Because Mr. Dunmore would never pay me and the magistrate would. Because nobody told me not to." He looked up. "That's not a defence. I know it's not a defence. But you asked why, and that's the true answer, and I'm tired of answers that aren't."

Ferris, who had cross-examined smugglers and murderers and once, famously, a bishop, nodded slowly, as if Rench had passed some private examination. Later I learned that those words — *nobody told me not to* — were repeated in taverns all along the river for months afterward, usually followed by laughter, but sometimes not.

Then came the forged atlas. Ferris laid the copy beside my original, open to the same page, and even from where I sat I could see what every eye in the hall could see: the same confident lines, the same hand-drawn hatching, and beneath the ford at Miller's Reach, a depth notation that did not exist anywhere in nature or in fact. Three feet, the forgery promised. My atlas said nine, because I had measured it myself in August water, three times, because Aldous Vane had taught me to measure everything three times.

A man can hang on the difference between three feet and nine. That is the whole argument for honest cartography, and I made it aloud, under oath, standing in the hall where I had sat as a child watching my master receive a commendation from the same bench that now wanted my family dead.

It should have ended there. Justice in stories ends where it should. In Vellmark it went on for five more days.

* * *

The pardons were Ferris's idea, though he framed them as necessity rather than mercy. The Kestrel gang could not simply be declared innocent, because innocence required establishing what they had done in the first place, and what they had done was smuggle goods across the King's river without paying the King's duties for the better part of a decade. That they had fed half the famine-starved valley doing it; that they had done it over Hale's head while Hale taxed grain and sold passage at the ferry and grew fat on other people's hunger — none of that was a legal defence. It was only the truth, and the law and the truth have always been distant cousins who meet awkwardly at funerals.

So there was a bargain. The gang would come in — all of them, Tam Kestrel at their head — and accept formal conviction on the smuggling counts, and in exchange the Crown would pardon, remit sentences, and set them free. Their names would carry the stain. Their bodies would carry nothing.

"They'll take it," Maren had told me, two nights before, in the yard behind the moot hall where she waited under a borrowed cloak because she could not enter the building where her own fate was being decided. "Tam's tired. He wants to die old. A pardon with a stain attached is more than he ever hoped for." She had turned her face up to the sky, which was doing its March trick of snowing and not snowing at once. "Most of them will take it."

"Most?"

She hadn't answered. I should have known then.

* * *

The pardons were read out on the eleventh day, name after name after name, the clerk's voice going hoarse somewhere around the ninth, and each man and woman rose when called, stood while the terms were recited, bowed or didn't according to temperament, and sat back down free. Tam Kestrel took his pardon standing very straight, and when the clerk finished he said only, "Thank you for Your Honours' attention," in a tone that managed to make gratitude sound like a declaration of war. There was laughter in the public benches. Even Ferris's mouth twitched.

And then: "Maren Kestrel."

She stood.

I had seen her across ice and firelight and the wreckage of Hale's trap. I thought I had seen her in every state the world could offer. But I had never seen her the way she looked standing in the moot hall on that morning, in a clean dress borrowed from my mother, her hair braided tight, her hands folded in front of her like a girl at her own wedding or her own funeral, which — as it turned out — was not far wrong.

Ferris read the terms. Conviction entered on the smuggling counts. Pardon granted. Sentence remitted. Conditions of release: acknowledgment of the Crown's authority, a bond of good behavior posted against future offense, and — here the clerk stumbled, because the clause was unusual — formal submission to the jurisdiction of the magistracy of Vellmark, meaning that Maren Kestrel, free woman, would live the rest of her life under the eye of whatever office replaced Corvus Hale's, answerable whenever it pleased, movable whenever it chose.

"Do you accept these terms?" Ferris asked.

"Yes," Maren said.

There was a breath in the hall, a collective exhale, the sound of a story ending well.

"No," Maren said.

The breath went back in.

Ferris looked up from his papers for the first time in eleven days. "You are addressing the court, Mistress Kestrel. Be precise."

"I am being precise." Her voice carried. It had always carried; even as a child she had never needed to raise it, only to decide to speak. "I beg the court's pardon for begging its pardon, but I won't take this one." She turned, slowly, so she was facing the whole hall — the jurors, the clerks, my mother's white face, mine. "You are offering me my life on condition that I spend it asking permission. You convicted me of feeding people. Not in those words — the law uses better words than that, the law always uses better words — but that's the crime. And now you'll un-crime it if I kneel. If I promise to be watched. If I agree that the men who bought this mess—" her eyes crossed Hale's empty chair, which had sat vacant throughout the proceedings like a wound in the room "—were the exception and not the rule."

"Mistress Kestrel—"

"A pardon that says I was guilty is a lie with my name signed to it. A pardon with conditions is a leash with my name on the collar. I've worn both already, in effect. I decline to wear them in law."

"You understand," Ferris said, and for the first time he sounded less like a dry stick and more like a tired man, "that without the pardon, the conviction stands. The sentence stands. The sentence is death."

"I understand arithmetic," Maren said. "I learned it during the famine winter, same as everyone on that list. The difference is what I'm willing to count."

The hall was absolutely silent. I could hear the candle flames. I could hear my own blood.

What I did next I did without deciding to do it, which is either cowardice or courage depending on which of my acquaintances tells the story, and I have stopped correcting either version. I stood up.

"My lord," I said. "Ferris. Sir. May I address the court?"

"You may sit down," Ferris said.

"The atlas," I said, and I heard my voice crack and hated it and pressed on. "The original. The evidence. This court has it because I gave it. I want the record to show what the record shows: that the routes in that book were used to feed this valley while its magistrate sold its grain. Every name on that list of pardons bought food with those routes. If the court holds Maren Kestrel guilty of smuggling, then it must hold the atlas guilty too, and the atlas is the only honest thing anyone in this county produced these three years, and I will not stand in this hall and watch the court pardon everyone except the person who—" and here my prepared argument, such as it was, ran out entirely, and what came out instead was simply: "She was twelve years old when I promised her. Everything since then has been me trying to keep one sentence. Don't make me break it in front of witnesses."

Somewhere behind me my mother made a small sound, halfway between a laugh and a sob.

Ferris regarded me for a long moment with the expression of a man who has spent thirty years listening to lawyers say elaborate things and has just been handed something plain. Then he looked at Maren. Then he looked at his papers, and shuffled them, and squared them, and did all the little things a man does when he is buying time to decide whether he can bend the law just far enough to call it something else.

"The court notes," he said finally, "the witness's statement. The court further notes that the pardon offered to Maren Kestrel was drafted under conditions of extraordinary irregularity, originating with a magistrate now himself under indictment for murder, forgery, and conspiracy, and that the conditions attached to said pardon therefore derive their entire authority from a corrupted source." He set down his papers. "The court finds the pardon void ab initio — void from its beginning — and the conviction obtained through tainted process likewise void. The charge is dismissed. The prisoner is discharged without conditions, without bond, without submission." He paused. "The court observes, without observing anything, that this outcome is greatly preferable to the alternative."

For a heartbeat nobody moved. Then the hall broke — not cheering, exactly; Vellmark doesn't cheer in the moot hall, it *exhales*, loudly, all at once, a sound like a river letting go of its ice — and Maren turned around and looked at me, and her face was doing several things at once, and I couldn't read any of them.

* * *

We met afterward in the churchyard, which sounds more romantic than it was; the churchyard was simply the only place in Vellmark where a crowd wouldn't follow. Snow lay thin on the graves. The kestrels were back — they come up the river valley in March, riding the wind off the breaking water, and two of them were turning slow circles over the tower.

"That was the stupidest thing anyone has ever done in a courtroom," Maren said. "And I once watched Tam argue jurisprudence with a customs officer while drunk."

"It worked."

"It worked because Ferris wanted a reason. You gave him a reason. That's different from working." She pulled her cloak tighter. She was leaving; I knew it the way you know weather is coming, from a change in the air before any cloud appears. "The others are dispersing. Tam's taking the northern ones toward the lakes. Some are staying — the valley's theirs now, in a way it never was, with their names stained and their bellies lawful." She smiled, briefly, without much joy in it. "I'm going west. Over the divide. There's country out there nobody's mapped."

"Everything's mapped," I said. "Badly, most of it."

"Then badly-mapped suits me fine. Badly-mapped is free." She looked at the kestrels, and then at me, and for a moment I saw her at fifteen on the half-built bridge, all the years between folded up and put away like a winter coat in spring. "You kept your sentence, Orsen. All of it. Every word. You stood where I could find you, and when I got there you were still standing."

"It felt less dignified at the time. Mostly it felt like talking too much."

"That's how keeping promises feels. Nobody warns you." She reached out and took my hand, briefly, hard, the grip of someone memorizing. "Don't come west. Don't write. Let this stay finished. Some things are only complete if you don't add to them."

"And if I map the western passes someday? Professionally? For money?"

"Then," she said, releasing my hand, already turning away, "measure everything three times."

She walked out of the churchyard between the graves, and the kestrel above the tower cried once, high and thin, and she did not look back, because she knew I would be looking, and one of us remembering was enough for both.

* * *

Justice, I have decided, is like a ford. From a distance it looks like a single clean line across the water, a place where crossing is simple. Up close it is stones and holes and current, and you get across mostly wet, and you lose things in the water that you do not discover the loss of until miles downstream. We got across. My family's name came through whole. Twenty-odd men and women traded a stain for their lives and counted it a bargain. Maren counted differently, and paid more, and walked away owning herself entire, which she would tell you — has told me, in the one letter that reached me two years later, unsigned, postmarked nowhere — was the only currency worth carrying.

But that letter belongs to spring, and to another chapter. On the day the verdicts came down, I walked home past the unfinished bridge, and stood on the near bank, and looked at the pilings sticking up out of the grey water like the ribs of something that had died mid-stride, and understood — for the first time, really understood — what the trial had cost that no verdict could return.

The river was beginning to rise. Any day now, the ice would go out.
