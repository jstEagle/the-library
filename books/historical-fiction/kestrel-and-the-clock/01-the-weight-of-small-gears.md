## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The pocket watch had belonged to a sea captain, and it had stopped at the moment his heart did, or so the widow claimed when she set it on the counter between them, as though laying down a relic rather than a repair.

"He wound it every morning at six," Mrs. Corvus said. "Forty years. And now it won't run for me at all."

Hazel Almeida turned the watch over in her palm. It was heavier than it looked, the way all honest things were, its case worn to a soft gold at the edges where a thumb had worried it across four decades of weather and distance. Through the crystal she could see the second hand lying still against the dial like a small dead insect. She thought it was likely the mainspring, or perhaps only dirt—forty years of salt air working itself into the pivots, grinding the whole delicate machinery toward its rest. But she said nothing yet. Saying nothing was part of the trade. A watch told you its troubles in its own time, if you were quiet enough to listen.

"I'll do what I can," she said.

"You'll do it yourself? Not—" Mrs. Corvus hesitated, and Hazel watched the hesitation assemble itself into its familiar shape. "Not Mr. Marchetti's work?"

"Mr. Marchetti has retired from the bench." Hazel kept her voice even. "I do the repairs now."

The widow's eyes moved over her the way they always did—the hands first, because those were undeniably capable, then up to her face, where youth and sex undid whatever confidence the hands had earned. Twenty-two years old, and three years into an apprenticeship that should have taken five but had been compressed by circumstance, namely that Elias Marchetti's eyes were failing faster than either of them would say aloud.

"Well," Mrs. Corvus said. "If anyone can, I suppose it's whoever he trained."

She left a deposit and left the shop, and the bell above the door rang her out into the morning, and then the workshop was Hazel's alone, which was how she liked it best, though she would not have admitted that to anyone.

* * *

The shop of Marchetti & Almeida—it had been so named two years ago, a generosity on Elias's part that still made something complicated move in Hazel's chest whenever she read the painted letters—sat on Tanners' Row, three doors up from the harbor stairs, in a town called Serevan that appeared on almost no maps worth consulting. It sat at the edge of an empire the way a barnacle sits at the edge of a hull: attached, incidental, carried along without ever being asked. To the west the hills folded themselves into vineyards and then into mountains and then into other countries entirely; to the east the harbor opened onto a gray sea that went on until it became someone else's problem.

That was how Hazel had come to think of the world beyond the harbor: as someone else's problem. History, she understood from newspapers and from the talk of older men at the chandlery, happened in capitals. It happened to ministers and generals, to people whose names were printed in heavy type. It happened elsewhere, to other people, and then—only occasionally, and diluted, like dye dropped far upstream—did it drift down as far as Serevan, arriving as a new tariff, or a naval officer on leave, or a fashion in hat brims that made the fishwives laugh. The town absorbed these things the way sand absorbs rain. Nothing changed that the tide didn't change first.

She believed this the way one believes in a floor beneath one's feet: completely, wordlessly, without any awareness of believing anything at all.

She cleared a space at the bench, laid down the leather cloth, and opened the captain's watch.

Under the loupe, the movement revealed itself in layers, the way a coastline reveals itself to a ship coming in. First the plates, gilt and grained; then the wheels, brass-bright in their jewels; then, deeper, the true country of the machine—the springs and levers, the escapement ticking its small stubborn argument with time. The smell rose up to meet her: oil and old metal and something faintly organic, forty years of the captain himself worked into the crevices. Watches carried their owners inside them. She had learned this early and had never found a way to say it that didn't sound like nonsense.

The trouble, when she found it, was beautifully simple. The barrel arbor had worn its seat in the plate, letting the mainspring slip a tooth's width out of true, so that the power train bound at intervals and died. The watch hadn't failed all at once. It had been failing for months, maybe years—a little less each day, the intervals of stopping growing shorter—and no one noticed until it stopped entirely.

*Everything fails gradually,* Elias used to say, tapping his temple with a finger yellowed at the nail from brass polish. *And then it fails suddenly. The gradual part is the warning. Nobody ever thanks you for pointing at it.*

She worked. This was the hour of the day when she was most herself, when her hands knew things her mind had never bothered to learn, when the world narrowed to the circle of light under the loupe and everything outside it—the harbor sounds, the gulls arguing over the gut pile behind the fish market, the foghorn moaning its slow note through the morning mist—receded into irrelevance. Her fingers were long and quick and stained faintly silver along the sides, a staining no amount of pumice soap would lift. She was, by every measure that mattered to her, good at this. Good with her hands.

And there, if she was honest—and alone at the bench, she tried to be—was the boundary of her life, the wall her ambition ran against every single day. *A girl good with her hands.* That was what she was, in the arithmetic of Serevan. Not a girl who would inherit the shop, because girls did not inherit shops, they married into them or out of them. Not a girl who would be written about. The tradesmen on the row called her *the apprentice* in a tone that made the word mean something smaller than it should. Even Mrs. Corvus, leaving, had needed reassurance the way one reassures a child holding a hammer near a window.

But here was the thing she could not say to anyone, the thing she fed quietly each night at the bench after Elias had gone home to his rooms above the chandlery: she wanted to matter. Not to be famous—she could not imagine fame, could not picture her own name in heavy type without embarrassment—but to be *necessary*. To be the person without whom something essential simply would not go. When she imagined her future, which she did sparingly, guiltily, the way one imagines stealing, she saw herself old and indispensable, keeper of some mechanism everyone else relied upon without understanding. The fantasy had no face in it, no audience, no applause. Only function. Only the deep satisfaction of being the hidden reason a thing worked.

She filed the worn seat true, fitted a bushing of hard brass, cut it flush, polished the bearing until her reflection swam in it, distorted and tiny. Outside, the church bells rang ten—St. Aubin's cracked old tenor, then the harbor chapel's thinner answer, half a beat behind. Two clocks disagreeing, as they did every hour of every day. In a town with two times, nobody was ever exactly late.

Elias had a theory about that. He said official time was a kind of fiction everyone agreed to, and the closer a town got to one clock, one voice, one truth, the more dangerous the place became. She'd laughed at him then. He had not laughed with her.

* * *

The letter came at noon, carried by a boy from the post office who took payment in a penny and a look at the magnifying lamp, which he examined with open disappointment before tramping back down the stairs.

Hazel knew the seal before she broke it. The Observatory. Director Voss's crest—a starburst over crossed meridians—stamped in wax the color of dried blood.

Serevan Observatory stood on the hill above the town, a white dome and its cluster of outbuildings, visible from the harbor on clear days and invisible in fog, which was most days. Hazel had been inside once, at fourteen, on a school visit she remembered mostly for the staircase and for the master clock.

Everyone in Serevan knew about the master clock, even if they had never seen it. It lived in its own room at the base of the observatory, a great regulator built in another country by men whose names were on a plaque no one read, and from it, by wire, the time went out to the telegraph office, the rail station, the customs house, the harbor master—all of it. One clock, one truth. When the master clock spoke, the trains obeyed and the tide tables agreed and the churchwardens came, hat in hand, twice a year, to have St. Aubin's corrected against it. It was the closest thing Serevan had to a king.

The letter was brief. Voss wrote the way he presumably wrote his observations—compressed, exact, no ornament:

*Miss Almeida — The regulator develops an irregularity in its rate. My previous man is unable to attend. I am given to understand you possess competence. Come Tuesday, at first light. Ask for me at the north door. Do not bring tools; I have tools. — A. Voss*

She read it three times.

*My previous man.* She knew who that meant—Old Ferrand, who had climbed the hill quarterly for twenty years and who had, according to the chandlery gossip, gone to his daughter's house inland in the spring to die slowly among grandchildren. So the position had been vacant, and Voss had let it lie vacant through summer, and now, in the first cold weeks of autumn, he had written to *her.*

Not to Elias. That was the part she kept circling back to as she stood at the counter holding the letter, hearing the harbor sounds wash up Tanners' Row. Voss knew of Elias; everyone did. Voss and Elias had history—she had gathered that much in fragments across six years, fragments offered and withdrawn, a friendship conducted in letters that Elias answered in his careful, failing-eyed script and never discussed. If the observatory wanted a clockmaker, the natural choice was Elias Marchetti, exile, master of the trade, the man whose name was already painted on her shopfront.

He had chosen her instead. Or Elias had refused and suggested her—she would ask, later, and Elias would deflect, later—or Voss had simply reached past the old man for reasons of his own. Whatever the mechanism, the result was the same: the observatory had summoned *the apprentice*, alone, to attend the most important clock on the coast.

Her hands, when she looked at them, were not trembling, and she was proud of that.

* * *

She spent the afternoon the way a soldier spends the night before inspection, though she would not have used the comparison. She closed the shop at the usual hour because closing early would invite comment. She ate the usual bread and cheese standing at the counter. At four o'clock she climbed the narrow stair to tell Elias.

He sat by his window with a letter in his lap—not reading it, she suspected, but holding it, using his fingers where his eyes could no longer serve. The light came in low and gold off the sea and caught the white in his beard, and he looked, as he often did now, like a photograph of himself taken some years ago and left in the sun.

"Well?" he said, without turning. He always heard her on the fourth step; the stair had a voice and he knew it.

She held out the letter. He read it closer than he needed to, angling it to catch the light, and when he finished he held it a moment longer, looking at the broken seal rather than the words.

"So," he said. "Anton finally wrote."

"You knew he might."

"I knew his clock. I've known that clock longer than I've known you." He gave the letter back, and his eyes, milky at the rims and sharp at the center, found her face and stayed there. "Ferrand used to fight it every visit and lose a little ground each time. It wants a full service. The escapement especially. It will take days."

"Then I'll stay the nights."

"Yes," Elias said mildly. "You will."

There was something in his tone she couldn't place—approval folded inside something older and sadder, a father watching a daughter pack for a journey he had made himself and not enjoyed. He rose, moving carefully by memory through his crowded room, and took from the shelf above his bed a book bound in green cloth, its spine sun-faded to the color of shallow water.

"Take this. My notes on the Voss regulator—the drawings I made the year we overhauled it together. Your hands are better than my eyes now; you'll make use of them." He pressed it on her before she could refuse, and then, holding her wrist a moment longer than the transaction required, added: "And carry a message to Anton, from me. Tell him—" He stopped. His mouth worked around several beginnings and discarded them. "Tell him the debt stands. He'll know what it means."

"Will he?"

"He will. That's the nature of debts between old men. They're never explained, only honored." He released her wrist and turned back to the window, to the darkening harbor and the water going pewter and lead. "Go Tuesday. Wear the coat with the pockets. And Hazel—" He paused again, and this pause had a different weight, deeper, deliberate, as though he were setting something down very carefully on a high shelf. "Whatever you find up there, remember who taught your hands their honesty. Whatever comes. Can you promise me that?"

It seemed an easy promise. It seemed, in fact, barely to require making—of course she would remember him; he was Elias; there was no version of her that did not contain him, the way there was no version of the watch that did not contain its maker.

"I promise," she said.

He nodded, satisfied or pretending to be, and turned back to the sea, and she left him there, a silhouette against the last light, and went down the stairs past the shop with its painted letters—*Marchetti & Almeida*—and out into the evening, where the gas lamps were coming on one by one along the row, each flame a small act of agreement between the town and the dark.

* * *

Tuesday arrived wrapped in fog.

She was on the harbor road before full light, the green notebook in the pocket of the coat with the pockets, and the town moved around her in pieces: the fish carts, the sweep's brush, the bakery exhaling warmth into the wet air. The fog lay on the water so thickly that the anchored boats existed only as sound—the slap of halyards, the clink of a buoy bell—and Serevan seemed less like a town than like a rumor of one, something assembled each morning out of mist and habit and dissolved again by noon.

She climbed the hill road. Behind her the town sank into the white; ahead of her the fog thinned by degrees, and by the time she reached the observatory wall the sky had cleared to a pale rinsed blue, and the dome stood above her white and complete, and below, spread out suddenly and wholly, lay the entire town—the roofs, the harbor, the masts, the two churches, the smoke rising straight up in the windless cold—all of it small, all of it legible, all of it arranged beneath her like parts of a mechanism viewed through the loupe.

She had never seen Serevan whole before. From the streets it was endless; from the hill it fit inside one turn of the head.

At the north door she raised her hand to knock, and before her knuckles touched the wood, the door opened. A tall man with iron-gray hair and ink-stained fingers looked down at her with an expression she would spend years learning to read, and which, on that first morning, she took for simple appraisal.

"Miss Almeida," Director Voss said. "You're punctual. That matters here more than you can yet imagine." He stepped back to admit her, and behind him, from deep inside the building, from somewhere below the floor, she heard it—a sound she felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears. A slow, vast tick, patient as a heartbeat, filling the whole structure like a pulse fills a body.

"Come in," said Voss. "The clock is waiting, and lately—" He paused, and something crossed his face too quickly to name. "Lately it has not been waiting well."

She stepped inside. The door closed behind her with a sound like a decision being made, and the great tick went on beneath her feet, steady and irregular at once, a heartbeat missing beats—calling her down toward the ledger room, the clock room, and the morning, four days away, when the trucks would come rolling through the fog.
