## 2. A Promise Made in Peacetime

The letter from the observatory lay on Hazel's workbench like something dropped from a greater height, and she had turned it over three times before opening it, as though the weight of it might change between her fingers. It had not. *The master clock is failing*, Director Voss had written, in a hand that slanted downhill as though hurrying somewhere. *We require an apprentice of steady nerve and small hands. Your master recommends you. Come at once, and come before the fog lifts.*

That had been yesterday's news, delivered in ink. Today's news was older than that, and it lived in a narrow house on Rua das Amendoeiras, behind a shopfront whose window held four clocks and no customers, because Elias Marchetti had never in his life repaired a clock quickly enough to satisfy anyone but himself.

Hazel closed the workshop at midday, which her master would have forbidden had he been there to forbid it, and walked down through the town with the letter buttoned inside her coat like a second heart. The morning was the kind this coast did best: pale gold at the edges, salt-smelling, the light coming off the harbor in long flat sheets so that the fishing boats seemed to float on hammered tin. Gulls argued over something at the fish market. A woman was beating a rug from an upper window, and the dull percussion of it followed Hazel half the length of the street.

She had meant, for eleven years, to visit more often. She had meant it the way one means all soft things — sincerely, occasionally, and never.

The bell above Marchetti's door gave its small dry cough when she entered. Inside, the air was thick with the smell of oil and old wood and, beneath both, the faint mineral breath of brass — a smell Hazel associated so completely with safety that she felt her shoulders drop before she had even seen him.

He was at his bench by the back window, bent so low over the watch in his holders that his spectacles had slid to the end of his nose. He was thinner than she remembered. That was the first thing, and she hated herself for noticing it first instead of the way the lamplight still came off his bald crown exactly as it always had, or the fact that he wore the same grey cardigan with the burnt cuff, or that his hands — those hands — were as steady at seventy as hers were at twenty-two.

"You are breathing on my escapement," Elias said, without looking up.

"The door breathed on your escapement."

"Then the door is careless." He sat back, removed his spectacles, and looked at her, and his face did what his face always did: arranged itself slowly, like weather, from concentration into something warmer that he would never have permitted himself to name aloud. "Ah," he said. "The famous Hazel."

"I'm not famous."

"You are famous *to me*, which is the only fame worth the trouble." He gestured at the stool across the bench, the one that had always been hers, its legs shortened years ago with shims so that it fit the bench at working height. He had never shortened it for anyone else. "Sit. Tell me why a girl with work to do is visiting an old man on a Thursday."

"The observatory wrote to me."

Something moved behind his eyes then — quick, deep, gone. Like a fish under ice. "Yes," he said. "I heard the master clock has been unwell."

"How could you have heard that before me?"

"Because I am old, and old men hear everything two days early." He picked up his loupe, examined it against the window as though it were at fault, set it down again. "Voss wrote to me last month asking after a complication remontoire. He did not say for what. Now I understand." He folded his hands on the bench. His nails were trimmed with geometric precision; they had taught her, those nails, that a craftsperson's body was part of the instrument. "So. You are going up the hill."

"Tomorrow. First light."

"And you are frightened."

"I am not frightened."

"You are holding your left wrist with your right hand. You have done that since you were eleven, whenever you are frightened and wish not to be." He said it without triumph, merely as observation, the way he might note that a mainspring was set. Then he rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and went to the cupboard at the back of the shop.

* * *

The cupboard was where he kept the things he did not sell, and Hazel had seen inside it perhaps five times in her life. It was not locked — Elias Marchetti did not believe in locks; he believed, with a stubbornness that had cost him dearly, that the world should not require them — but it was understood, the way some rooms in some houses are understood, to be private.

He took down a bottle of madeira and two small glasses, and also a flat tin box, and carried all three to the bench. He poured the wine. Outside, the rug-beating had stopped, and the silence that replaced it seemed to make room for what was coming.

"To Voss," Elias said, raising his glass. "Who once kept me out of a newspaper, and does not know that I remember it."

They drank. The madeira tasted of figs and of somewhere far south of this coast.

"He was your friend," Hazel said. "In Lisbon. Before."

Elias turned the glass slowly on the bench, watching the light climb its curve. "Before," he agreed. "There was a country I lived in, once, that stopped wanting me. You know the shape of the story. Everyone knows the shape now; there are too many of these stories and they all hold water." He waved a hand, dismissing his own history the way he dismissed compliments. "Voss was a young astronomer then with more principle than sense, which is the best ratio a young man can have. When they wanted names from the institute — who was foreign, whose grandfather was what — Voss misplaced the ledger. Lost it. Very sorry. Terribly careless." The corner of his mouth lifted. "A whole ledger, lost. Like a glove."

"And then?"

"And then I came here, where the fog comes in and nobody asks questions until the sun burns it off, and by then they have forgotten they wanted to." He drank again. "It is a good town for forgetting. I have recommended it to several people, all of whom are now dead elsewhere, so perhaps I should stop recommending it."

"Elias—"

"But." He raised one finger, and the finger was the old finger, the teaching finger, the one that had hovered over her first attempt at a balance staff while she held her breath for a full minute. "But I did not ask you here to bury myself. I asked you here — well. You asked yourself here, which amounts to the same thing, since I knew you would come." He pushed the flat tin box across the bench. "Open it."

Inside, on a bed of cotton gone yellow with age, lay a pocket watch.

Not just any pocket watch — she knew it before she had properly seen it, the way one knows a voice through a wall. It was the watch he had been working on her first winter, the one he let her hold while he explained the cylinder escapement, turning it in her palms like an egg, saying *careful, careful, it is alive*. Silver case worn to warm bareness at the edges. The dial hairline-cracked at seven o'clock. On the caseback, engraved in a script so fine it was nearly a whisper: *E. Marchetti, Lisboa.*

"It doesn't run," Elias said. "It hasn't run in nine years. There is a fault in the fusee chain that I have known about for nine years and done nothing about, because—" he paused, and for the first time his voice lost its footing, "because I suppose I liked having something left unfinished. Old men like unfinished things. Finished things mean we are done."

"Why are you giving it to me?"

"Because tomorrow you go up the hill to the master clock, and the master clock is the most important machine in this town, and you will be frightened of it, and you should not be." He leaned forward. "That watch is a better mechanism than it has any right to be, and it failed anyway, and the world continued to turn. Machines fail, Hazel. What matters is whether someone loves them enough to open the case."

She looked down at the watch so she would not have to look at him. Her eyes had gone hot, which annoyed her.

"There is more," Elias said. "And this is the part where you will argue with me, so drink your wine first."

She drank her wine.

* * *

What he wanted was simple, and simplicity, she knew already, was how the heavy things disguised themselves.

He wrote the message while she watched — a single page, folded twice, sealed with wax from a stub candle he kept for the purpose though he had no correspondence worth sealing. He would not let her read it. "You will carry it, not audit it," he said. "Give it into Voss's hand. Not to a clerk, not under a door. His hand. Tell him it is from Marchetti, and tell him the word is '*lost, like a glove*,' and he will understand, and if he understands he will know what to do, and if he does not—" Elias stopped. He wet his lips. "If he does not, bring the letter back and we will think of something else. But he will understand."

"Understand what? What is it?"

"Hazel." He said her name the way he said *fusee*, or *detent* — with the particular care reserved for mechanisms under tension. "There are letters arriving in this town with crests on them. Have you noticed? The harbor master has new flags. Two men came last week asking at the shipping office about tonnage and schedules, and they wrote nothing down, which means they already had it written somewhere else. I am an old man who reads newspapers and watches windows. Perhaps I am wrong about what is coming. I hope every night that I am wrong." He laid his hand flat on the bench, over the empty tin box. "But if I am right, then a letter in my hand may be worth more than everything in my shop. And Voss should have it before whatever it is arrives, not after."

The fire in the little stove ticked. Somewhere down the street a cart passed, iron-rimmed wheels on cobbles, the sound rising and fading like a long breath.

"You think they'll come for the foreigners," Hazel said quietly.

"I think," Elias said, "that when a country begins keeping lists, it does not stop at the names it needs. Lists are like clocks, my dear. Once the train is set, the escapement does not consult your feelings." He smiled then, to take the edge off it, and the smile was worse than the words. "But none of this is certain. Drink your wine."

She did not want the wine. She wanted to put her fist through something, which was a feeling she was used to and had learned to file away among the other feelings a girl with quick hands learns to hide — along with the ambition, along with the certainty that she could true a wheel better than any man in the guild hall if any of them would let her try.

"Come with me," she said. "Tomorrow. Come up to the observatory with me. Voss is your friend. If anything is coming, you should be up there, not down here behind a glass door with no lock on your cupboard—"

"Hazel."

"—you're seventy years old and you live alone above a shop full of things people can steal, and you're telling me about trains being set—"

"*Hazel.*" And then, gentler, in the tone he'd used when she'd broken her first jewel and cried over it: "*Menina.* Sit. Breathe. You are winding too fast; you will snap the barrel."

She sat. She breathed.

"I will not go up that hill," Elias said, "and I will tell you why, and you may think it is pride, and you will be partly right. It is pride, and it is also this: I have run once already in my life. I left a country, a house, a language, forty-one years of my own name spoken correctly by people who loved me. I will not do it again on rumor. If the list comes, it comes, and I will meet it in my own shop with my own kettle on." He shrugged, an enormous shrug contained in small shoulders. "Besides. Someone must stay who remembers the trade. If they take me, and they take the shop, then the only Marchetti work left in the world will be in your hands. That is not a small thing, Hazel. That is the entire thing."

The light through the back window had changed; the morning was burning off toward noon. Down in the harbor a bell buoy clanged, slow and patient, counting the swell.

"Then promise me something," Hazel said.

"Promises are expensive."

"I can pay."

He waited, hands folded, and she found that the speech she had prepared dissolved on the way to her mouth and what came out instead was plainer and truer.

"Whatever happens — whatever lists, whatever letters, whatever they write about you in their ledgers — I will remember you. Your name. Exactly as it is. However many people strike it out or misspell it or pretend they never knew it, I will keep it. I'll keep it the way I'd keep a movement running. Daily. With oil. Whether anyone winds me or not."

For a long moment Elias Marchetti said nothing at all, and Hazel watched the weather cross his face again — surprise, then grief, then something so old and so tender it hurt to look at, the way it hurts to look at the sun through glass.

"You see," he said finally, and his voice had gone rough as a file, "this is why I shortened the stool."

He stood, took her hand in both of his — his hands still warm, still precise, still capable of the smallest work — and pressed it briefly, formally, the way colleagues greet one another across a finished repair.

"Then I accept your price," he said. "And I will tell you a secret about remembering, since you have bought it so dearly. A name kept only in the heart is a watch in a drawer. Beautiful. Dead. To keep a name truly you must put it somewhere it does work. Into a signature. Into a story. Into—" he smiled, sly suddenly, the old slyness, "—whatever strange places a girl with small hands may find. Promise me you'll be inventive."

"Inventive," Hazel repeated, not understanding, and would not understand for another year, standing in the dark of the clock case with a graver in her hand.

"I will carry your letter," she said. "Into Voss's own hand. And the word is *lost, like a glove.*"

"Lost, like a glove." He nodded once, satisfied, and began gathering the wine things, and the ordinary motions of it — the cork, the cloth, the glasses wiped and shelved — restored the room to itself, to Thursdays, to the smell of oil.

She stayed another hour. He showed her the fault in the fusee chain so she could fix the watch herself someday, and quizzed her on remontoires, and told her twice that her filing had improved and once that it had not, and when she left, the bell coughed behind her and he did not come to the door, because he never came to the door; he only raised one hand at the window among his four slow clocks, and she raised hers, and that was goodbye, though neither of them knew it, and both of them somehow did.

She walked home up through the town with the sealed letter inside her coat next to the observatory summons, two papers lying together like strangers forced to share a bench. Overhead the gulls wheeled against a sky going hard and clear. Out past the breakwater, where the fog had burned away entirely, she could see a ship that had not been there yesterday — grey, low in the water, flying no flag she recognized — standing in toward the harbor as though it had all the time in the world.

She stood a moment watching it, her hand resting on the letters over her heart.

Then the harbor bell rang the hour — the town's hour, the old hour, struck from the failing master clock on the hill — slightly late, as it had been all month, and Hazel Almeida turned and climbed toward first light and whatever was waiting for her there.
