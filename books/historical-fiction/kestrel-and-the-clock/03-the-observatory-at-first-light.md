## 3. The Observatory at First Light

The letter had said *before seven*, and so Hazel came up through the town while the lamps were still burning, walking against the tide of fishermen who smelled of salt and kerosene and were already talking about the weather as though it were a relative with a difficult temperament. The cobbles were slick with the night's damp. Gulls argued on the rooftops. And above everything, above the tiled roofs and the smoke rising straight up in the windless cold, stood the observatory on its hill — a white dome catching the first grey light like a pearl held under water.

She had seen it every day of her life, the way one sees one's own hands. It had simply been there, the way the harbor was there and the cliff road was there, part of the grammar of the town. But she had never climbed to it before. Apprentices did not climb to the observatory. Apprentices swept floors and filed pivots and were scolded for breathing near the polishing wheel. The observatory belonged to astronomers and directors and men whose letters arrived on thick paper with seals.

Now it belonged, apparently, to her morning.

The hill road wound up through pines that dripped with last night's fog, and with each turning the sea revealed itself again below — first a sliver, then a wide pewter sheet, then the whole luminous expanse of it, with the fishing boats strung across the bay like beads on an invisible thread. Hazel walked quickly, partly because she was late in her own estimation, and partly because if she slowed she would have to admit to herself how much she wanted this, and wanting things, she had learned, was a dangerous practice for a girl good with her hands and nothing more.

*Nothing more.* Her master had said it once, not unkindly, to a customer who had questioned the price of a repair. *She's nothing more than clever with small work, but you'll find that's enough.* She had carried the phrase for three years like a stone in her shoe. Nothing more. As though the hands were separate from her, as though they did their cleverness without her, and she were merely the person who happened to be attached to them.

At the top of the hill the observatory resolved itself into particulars: the dome, streaked where rain had run rust from the fixtures; the long low wing beneath it, built of pale stone; a brass door handle worn to brightness by a century of palms; and set into the wall beside the entrance, a clock face nearly two feet across, its enamel cracked like old porcelain, its hands stopped at eleven minutes past four.

Stopped. The master clock of the town, the clock by which the harbor bells were rung and the trains were scheduled and every church in the district set its Sunday services, standing dead at eleven minutes past four while the sky went pale behind it. It looked to Hazel like a face with the eyes open but nobody home.

She raised her hand to knock, and the door opened before her knuckles touched it.

"You're the Almeida girl." Not a question. A tall man filled the doorway, sixty or so, with a beard trimmed close and hair gone the color of ash, dressed in a woolen dressing gown over what were clearly yesterday's clothes. His eyes were red-rimmed and furious and frightened all at once, and he held a cup of coffee as though he had forgotten it existed. "You look twelve."

"I'm nineteen, sir."

"Nineteen." He said it the way a man says a figure from an invoice he intends to dispute. "Voss. Director Voss. You'll come in, and you'll tell me immediately whether my clock is dying, because I have spent nine nights listening to it die and I would very much like to know if I am mad."

* * *

Inside, the corridor smelled of dust warm on lamp bulbs, of machine oil, of paper. That last smell puzzled her until she noticed, as Voss led her down the passage, the doors along the left side — plain oak doors, brass numbers, and beneath them, visible in the gap between door and stone floor, the soft yellow glow of lamplight and the sound, faint but unmistakable, of pages turning. At this hour. Before dawn.

"The registry offices," Voss said, without slowing, following her gaze. "The civic ledger. Every soul in this town and where they came from, going back to the year the harbor was built. The clerks keep early hours now. Some new efficiency." There was something in his voice — a flatness, deliberately applied, like paint over woodworm — that Hazel noted without understanding. "Through here."

The clock room took her breath and held it.

It was a round chamber at the heart of the building, directly beneath the dome, and the master clock stood in its center like a monument: a case of black walnut three men high, glazed along its length so that the whole movement was visible, every wheel and lever and weight, lit from within by small concealed lamps so that the brass glowed gold in the dimness. The pendulum hung still. That stillness was the strangest thing — a machine of that size should breathe, should sway, should live, and this one stood rigid as a corpse in its glass coffin.

"It's a Grünewald," Hazel said, before she remembered that apprentices were not supposed to identify movements unasked. "Eight-day. But — no, that's not right. The barrel's too large. This is a Grünewald pattern but rebuilt. Extended." She stepped closer, her breath fogging the glass. "Someone re-engineered the escapement entirely. This isn't a catalog piece. This is *one*."

Behind her, Voss was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, the invoice-disputing sharpness had gone out of his voice.

"My predecessor rebuilt it. Forty years ago, after the fire in the tower. He worked on it for six years and never quite finished tinkering. It keeps time to two seconds a week when it is well. Two seconds, girl. There are observatories in capitals that cannot say the same." He set down his untouched coffee. "Nine nights ago it began to lose. Then it began to stutter — it hesitates, some seconds last forever — and then three nights ago it stopped altogether at twenty past four. I raised the weights myself. It ran four hours and stopped again. I have telegraphed the manufacturer and received in reply a letter suggesting I consult a qualified horologist, which I take to be the German word for *we do not know either*. The town office tells me the harbor bells rang wrong twice and the morning train sat at the platform for eleven minutes because no one would trust the schedule. Eleven minutes. Do you understand what that clock *is*, Miss Almeida?"

She did, suddenly and completely, in a way she had not understood from the outside. From the workshop below the hill, the observatory clock had been an abstraction, a source of the bells, a fact. Here, inside, she could feel it: every ship's departure, every train, every school bell, every wage hour, every court session, every appointment in every office of the town — all of it hanging from the pendulum in this room like fruit from a single branch. One machine, and the whole town arranged around its heartbeat.

"It's the town's time," she said quietly.

"It is the town's *authority*," Voss corrected. "Time is a thing nature does. This—" he gestured at the great dark case "—is a thing we agreed upon. And agreements require a keeper. Which brings me to the unpleasant portion of the morning. Your master wrote in his letter that you are his best pair of hands. I do not care about your age or your sex or your master's opinion of your prospects. I care that you touch nothing without telling me, that you clean your hands before you clean mine, and that if you break anything inside that case I will personally see that you never hold a screwdriver in this province again. Are these terms understood?"

"Perfectly, sir."

"Then begin. I will be at my desk pretending to be calm."

* * *

She washed at the basin in the corner, scrubbing to the elbows with the gray carbolic soap, and dried on the linen cloth he gave her — real linen, she noticed, hemmed and laundered, not rag — and then she unlocked the case door with the key Voss produced from a chain on his waistcoat, and put her hands inside the greatest machine she had ever seen.

The next hour passed somewhere beneath language. She worked by feel and by lamplight, tracing the train wheel by wheel: great wheel, center wheel, third, fourth, escape wheel, each pivot testing clean or dirty or worn between her fingertips. The movement was clean — whoever maintained it had been conscientious — but the oil had aged past its useful life, gone to varnish in the hard cold of the room, and in the escapement she found the trouble almost at once: a pallet stone with a hairline fracture, invisible to the eye until her loupe found it, its surface crumbled just enough to let the escape wheel occasionally skip instead of lock. A stutter. An endless second. And when the fractured edge finally caught wrong, the whole train locked and the heart of the town stood still.

A stone the size of a grain of rice, and eleven minutes lost at the railway station.

"I'll need a new pallet stone," she called up through the case, her voice strange inside the walnut throat of it. "Cut to fit. I can shape it here if there's stock — agate or ruby, synthetic will serve. Two days' work, perhaps three with adjustment."

There was no answer. She drew back out of the case and found Voss standing at his desk with a telegram form in his hand, and the color of his face had changed.

"Read this," he said, and his voice was careful in the way of a man walking on river ice. "Tell me what you make of it."

The telegram was short. Hazel read it twice.

*BY ORDER OF THE PROVINCIAL COMMAND. COMMENCING [date illegible to her eye, smudged] OBSERVATORY TIME TO BE DESIGNATED OFFICIAL TIME FOR ALL MILITARY AND CIVIL SCHEDULING. MASTER CLOCK TO BE MAINTAINED IN CONTINUOUS OPERATION. DAILY VERIFICATION LOGS REQUIRED. ACKNOWLEDGE.*

"Military scheduling," she repeated. "What military? There's no garrison here. There's a customs house and a bandstand."

"There was no military yesterday," Voss said. He took the telegram back and looked at it as though it might explain itself further. "There is likely to be military shortly. These orders precede the soldiers the way thunder follows light — you see the flash of paperwork first and hear the boots after." He folded the form with precise, unhappy fingers. "Something is being organized, Miss Almeida. Governments do not requisition clocks for picnics. Whatever is coming, it will run on schedules, and schedules will run on this machine, which means that your broken pallet stone has just become a matter of considerably more than civic pride."

Hazel looked at the clock. In its glass case, lit gold, it seemed suddenly less like a monument and more like a hostage — something valuable that other men had plans for.

"Then I'd better start today," she said.

"Yes," said Voss. "I rather think you had."

* * *

He showed her to a workbench in the anteroom — a proper bench, mahogany, with a vice and a pegboard of tools so well kept that Hazel felt a pang of professional longing — and left her to inventory the stock of stones while he went down to send his acknowledgment. She worked alone in the long light of morning, which came slantwise now through the eastern windows and lay in warm bars across the floor, and she was fitting her smallest file to the first test-cut of agate when she heard the footsteps below and the voices.

Two clerks, by the sound, coming up the stair from the registry offices with an armload of ledger volumes, talking in the low continuous murmur of men discussing salaries. They passed the anteroom door without pausing, and Hazel, filing, let her eyes follow them down the corridor to the room at the end — the door she had passed earlier, the lamplit room smelling of paper.

One of the clerks propped the door open against the mildew, and Hazel could see inside: shelves upon shelves of bound volumes, green and black, running back along the walls like a library; a counter; a stamping machine; and on the counter, open, the current ledger, its pages wide as newspaper sheets, ruled in columns. Name. Origin. Trade. Date of registry. Every resident of the town, entered and numbered. She thought of Elias Marchetti's name in such a book — *Marchetti, Elias, born Ferrara, watchmaker, registered [a spring date seventeen years ago]* — written in a clerk's indifferent hand, and found the thought strangely moving, that all of a human life could be reduced to a line in green cloth boards, and also strangely comforting, that the line existed at all, that somewhere in ink was the proof that he had come, had lived, had mended the town's watches and taught one apprentice girl everything her hands knew.

They know we're here, that book said. We exist. We count.

The clerk stamped a page. The sound carried up the corridor, dull and final, like a small door closing.

Hazel bent back to her agate. But the feeling of the morning had shifted, the way light shifts when a cloud crosses the sun though the sky remains blue — the work was the same, the bench was the same, the beautiful machine waited intact in the next room, and yet something in the air had acquired weight. A town's authority in one clock. A town's memory in one ledger. Both of them in one building on a hill, both of them tended by a handful of men with red-rimmed eyes and stamps and sealed telegrams, and everyone else — the fishermen, the bakers, the watchmakers born in Ferrara, the girls good with their hands and nothing more — living out their days according to what those men wrote and kept and wound.

She cut three test stones and rejected two. Through the window, far below, she watched the road that wound up from the town, and it was while watching it — idly, wiping file dust from her fingers — that she saw the trucks.

Four of them, maybe five, canvas-backed, coming up the coast road from the south in a line, kicking dust, too heavy and too regular to be farm traffic. They turned off toward the harbor. Behind them, at that distance barely visible, figures were gathering at the quay — and along the harbor front, someone was already climbing a ladder with a bucket and a brush, posting paper to the customs house wall.

The filing sound of the town changed. Even up here, even through stone and glass, Hazel could hear the difference — the gulls, the hammers, the fish-carts — falter, as if the whole place had paused to listen.

Down the corridor, the ledger room door closed. The lock turned.

"Miss Almeida," said Voss from the doorway behind her, and his voice had gone very quiet. "Perhaps you had better come and look at the harbor."
