## 4. Soldiers at the Harbor

The summons came with the tide.

Hazel had been at the observatory three days when the harbor woke to the sound of engines—low, guttural, nothing like the clean tick of machinery she loved. She was bent over the master clock's escapement, a jeweler's loupe screwed into her eye, her fingers steady on the pallet stones, when the sound reached up the hill and through the stone walls as though the building itself had swallowed a growl.

She straightened. The clock's pendulum swung behind her, indifferent, keeping the town's official time as it had for sixty years: *tock ... tock ... tock*, each beat a small iron certainty. Outside the tall south window, the sky was the pale grey of pewter, and against it, moving down the coast road in a line that seemed to have no end, came trucks.

She counted six before she stopped counting.

*Maneuvers,* she thought. That was the word she reached for first, the comfortable word, the one the newspapers used when soldiers appeared anywhere at all. The empire held exercises every spring; the boys in the town square played at drilling with wooden rifles; once, years ago, she had watched a dirigible pass over the harbor and everyone had cheered. History, she had always believed, was something that happened in capitals, in languages she did not speak, to people whose names appeared in print only after they were dead. It passed over small towns the way weather passed over—a wind felt at the edges, never the storm itself.

The trucks turned off the coast road and rolled into the lower town.

* * *

She went down through the observatory's stone stairwell two steps at a time, past the ledger room with its green-shaded lamps—dark now, this early—and out onto the broad front steps where Director Voss already stood, his hands clasped behind his back, his nightshirt still visible above his trousers where he had dressed in haste.

"You heard them too," he said.

"Everyone in the town heard them, sir."

He said nothing to that. Voss was a man who believed in observing before speaking, a habit the stars had taught him, and so they stood together on the steps like a pair of ill-matched statues—heavyset him, thin her—as the trucks descended into the streets below and began, without any ceremony whatsoever, to disgorge soldiers.

There were more of them than Hazel would have guessed the empire could spare for a fishing town of nine thousand souls. They came down from the truck beds with their boots hitting the cobbles in unison, a sound like a door being closed somewhere very large, and they spread along the waterfront with an efficiency that suggested they had rehearsed it—which, she understood later, they had, on charts, in rooms far away, using maps that marked the harbor but none of the people.

By full light the requisition notices were going up.

Hazel watched a lieutenant—young, pink-cheeked, methodical as a clerk—paste the first sheet to the customs house wall with a brush and a bucket of flour paste. He smoothed it flat with the side of his hand, twice, left to right, the way she might smooth brass foil into a die. She found herself noticing his hands. It was a habit of her trade, reading hands: the calluses of a file-worker, the ink stains of a clerk, the particular wear a lifetime of winding leaves on the right thumb. His hands told her nothing useful. That frightened her more than the rifles did.

The crowd gathered the way crowds do, drawn by the gravity of printed paper. Hazel could read the notice from the observatory steps only because Voss had thoughtfully brought field glasses, and he passed them to her without a word when she asked.

Through the lenses, the world jumped close. The paper trembled with her pulse.

*BY ORDER OF THE IMPERIAL MILITARY ADMINISTRATION.* The harbor was seized, effective immediately. All vessels subject to inspection. All warehouses subject to search. A curfew from dusk until dawn. And then, set apart in its own paragraph as though it were a kindness, a list:

*All persons bearing the following surnames will register with the military administration within forty-eight hours.*

Fourteen names followed. She read them twice, three times, mouthing them as if sounding them aloud might reveal some logic to their selection. Marchetti. Ostrer. Kowalczyk. Names she had seen on shopfronts all her life—the watchmaker's own among them, painted in gold leaf on the sign Elias had hung himself twenty years ago. Names of the men who mended nets, who kept the tobacco shop, who taught piano in the front room of houses with geraniums on the sill. Names, now, that were categories.

She lowered the field glasses. Her hands were shaking, which offended some professional part of her. Hands were meant to be steady. She had been trained in steadiness the way other girls were trained in cooking or modesty, and here her training abandoned her entirely.

"Sir," she said, "those are our neighbors."

Voss took the glasses back and looked for a long moment. When he lowered them his face was composed, but she noticed that his knuckles were white around the strap.

"Those," he said quietly, "are names on a list. It is important, Miss Almeida, to be precise about what things are." He turned toward the door. "Come inside. The clock will want winding by ten, and I suspect"—he glanced once more at the harbor, where a naval launch was now nosing toward the pier—"precision is about to become very fashionable."

* * *

The town changed in the way a clock changes when someone opens its case—not all at once, not violently, but with a series of small irreversible adjustments that leave the mechanism looking the same and running entirely differently.

Hazel saw it happen through the course of that single morning.

She saw Herr Brandt the baker—no relation, as yet, to any officer—come out of his shop in his flour-whitened apron and stand reading the notice, and then go back inside without baking anything, and by noon his shutters were closed and would not open again that day. She saw Frau Ostrer, who had sold flowers outside the church for longer than Hazel had been alive, gather up her baskets slowly, deliberately, the way one gathers belongings from a seat one does not intend to give up, and carry them away down a side street. She saw neighbors glance at one another and then carefully not glance, the whole town developing, between breakfast and lunch, a new habit of the eyes.

Soldiers moved through it all with clipboards. Two of them took up station at the harbor mouth. Another pair chalked numbers on warehouse doors. A corporal with a voice like gravel informed the harbormaster—Hazel could hear him from a hundred yards, there was no need for the exchange to be private—that the fishing fleet would not put out until further notice, that the boats were now imperial property on imperial water, and that any man who touched a tiller after dark would be shot. The harbormaster, a wide gentle man named Pesek who had once fished Hazel out of the harbor herself when she was eleven and foolish, stood very straight and said, "Yes, of course," in the voice of a man agreeing to the weather.

That was the thing, Hazel thought, watching from the workshop window while her hands went through the motions of cleaning the clock's second-hand wheel. That was the thing she could not stop seeing. Nobody shouted. Nobody wept in the streets. The town simply *complied*, quickly and politely, as though it had been waiting all these years for someone to arrive and tell it what it was permitted to do. She had grown up believing there was a spirit in this place—independent, salty, stubborn as its granite breakwater—and she watched that belief come apart thread by thread over the space of six hours, and what lay underneath it was not defiance but a terrible practicality. People had shops. People had children. People calculated, faster than conscience, the price of objection.

At midday an officer and two soldiers came up the hill to the observatory.

Hazel heard them before she saw them, boots on the outer stair, and she was moving toward the ledger room—it was pure reflex, some instinct telling her to stand between the soldiers and those green lamps—when Voss caught her arm at the corridor's turn.

"Not that room," he murmured. "Whatever happens, not that room. You are the clockmaker. Be the clockmaker."

The officer who entered the timing hall was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the kind of face that has been shaved by the same barber in the same manner for thirty years. His collar bore insignia Hazel did not recognize, which meant it was new, which meant it mattered to somebody. Behind him the two soldiers looked around the hall with frank curiosity—at the great meridian instrument under its dust shroud, at the star charts, at the master clock standing in its oak case against the north wall like a patriarch.

"Director Voss." The officer consulted a paper rather than waiting for confirmation. "I am Captain Weiss. The observatory is hereby placed under military administration. You will continue your duties. Your staff"—his eyes found Hazel, catalogued her in half a second, age, sex, trade, threat: none—"will continue theirs. The master clock is now the official timekeeper for the district. All schedules, all movements, all operations run on its time. Its accuracy is therefore a matter of military necessity."

"The clock is accurate to within two seconds a week," Voss said. There was something in his voice Hazel had never heard there before—a careful flatness, a man folding his convictions smaller and smaller so they might fit through a narrow opening.

"Then you have been doing useful work after all." Weiss allowed himself the ghost of a smile. He crossed to the master clock and opened the case with proprietary ease, studying the pendulum, the weights, the gleaming cage of the movement, and Hazel felt something twist in her chest that she recognized, with surprise, as jealousy. It was not his to open. It had never been anyone's to open but the clockmakers'.

"It will need servicing daily," she heard herself say.

Weiss turned. Up close his eyes were the color of shallow water and just as readable.

"Daily," she repeated, and was astonished to find her voice level. Three days ago she had been a girl good with her hands and nothing more. "A clock this size drifts. Temperature, humidity, the weight of the dust itself. If your schedules depend on it, Captain, then your schedules depend on me."

A silence. Somewhere below them, in the town, a truck gate slammed.

"Very well," Weiss said at last. "You will report to Officer Brandt at the harbor administration each morning at seven. He will escort you." He closed the case gently—more gently, Hazel noted, than he did anything else—and added, almost as an afterthought: "Almeida. That is a local name?"

"My family has been here four generations."

"Good." He put on his cap. "Rooted people cause fewer difficulties."

When they had gone, Voss sat down slowly at his desk and pressed both hands flat against the wood, as though holding the desk to the floor of the world.

"Miss Almeida," he said, without looking up. "In future, do not volunteer. Servants who volunteer are noticed. Noticing is dangerous in both directions."

"Yes, sir."

"And yet." He exhaled through his nose, something that was almost a laugh. "Well argued."

* * *

She should have gone home that evening. The curfew began at dusk; Voss offered her the cot in the assistant's room, and pride told her to walk down the hill through the gathering dark to her mother's house above the chandlery, and prudence—arriving late, as prudence does—told her to stay. She stayed.

From the gallery, past midnight, she looked out over the town.

It lay under a curfew moon, lamplit only at the corners where soldiers had lit braziers, and it looked to her suddenly like a clock with its case closed: the same streets, the same roofs, the same harbor water moving in its old silver rhythms, but running now to someone else's time. Out on the pier stood a sentry, rifle slanted, smoke from his cigarette rising straight in the windless cold. Beyond him the fishing boats knocked together at their moorings, idle for the first night in living memory, and their restlessness carried across the water to her as sound—the small blind knocking of things built for freedom, held fast.

She thought of Elias. It was inevitable; she had been avoiding the thought all day with the diligence of a woman avoiding a wound. *Marchetti.* Fourteen hours since the name had gone onto the list, and somewhere down there his shop stood shuttered and his name stood struck—or soon would be—from the civic ledger, reduced from a man to a category to an error to be corrected. Twenty years ago he had come to this town with nothing but his tools and a foreign accent, and the town had taken him in because in peacetime a town can afford generosity. She had learned everything at his bench: how to true a wheel, how to listen to an escapement the way one listens to breathing, how to hold patience in the hands. And she had promised him—lightly, laughing, both of them certain promises cost nothing in peacetime—that whatever happened, his name would not be erased.

She had thought, making it, that she was promising against forgetting. Against time, against indifference, against the soft erosion of years. She had not known she was promising against *this*.

Below her, at the harbor administration building, a light still burned. Behind its window a figure sat at a desk, working late over papers, and even at this distance she could see the rhythm of the hand: write, pause, strike through, write. Lists. The town was being rewritten, name by name, by lamplight, tonight, while everyone else slept behind doors that locked from the inside only.

Hazel stood in the cold a long while. Then she went down to the assistant's room, took her notebook and pencil from her coat, and laid them on the chair beside the cot where she would see them first thing in the morning.

At seven o'clock, she would present herself to Officer Brandt. At seven o'clock, they would escort her down the hill, past the customs house with its notices, past the shuttered bakery, into the building where the ledger lived.

And she would see, with her own eyes, exactly what was being done to the names.
