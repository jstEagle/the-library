## 6. What the Clock Remembers

The soldiers had been in the town four days when Hazel was finally left alone with the clock.

Not truly alone—the sentry at the observatory door counted her in and out, and Officer Brandt's shadow crossed the gallery twice an hour like a slow hand on a dial—but alone enough. The clerks below had finished their first purge of the ledger and gone back to their barracks. Director Voss had been summoned to the harbor office to sign forms he would not speak of afterward. Even the pigeons had been driven from the tower by the smell of gun oil and boot leather. And so Hazel climbed into the great case with her brushes and her benzine and her rag, and she began, for the first time since the army came, to work in something like silence.

The master clock did not care who owned the town. That was the first mercy of the morning. It ran as it had always run, its pendulum swinging in the long dark of the shaft below, its train of wheels turning with the patience of a thing that had outlived three directors, two wars fought elsewhere, and every government that had ever claimed to govern. When Hazel put her ear against the oak of the case, she heard it thinking: that deep, unhurried tick, older than any order posted on any door.

She started where she always started, at the top. The going train first—she drew off the dust of the week with a camel-hair brush, working between the wheel teeth the way you'd comb a child's hair, gently, following the grain. The escapement next, its pallets bright as wet stone. Then the striking mechanism, which had grown sluggish in the damp spring air and needed its pivots cleaned and oiled before the hour count began to drag.

It was while she was cleaning the strike-side great wheel that her lamp caught on something that should not have been there.

A mark. Low on the inside face of the wheel's rim, hidden in the shadow the crossing threw, so that only light entering at a certain angle—her angle, bent over the wheel with the lamp held low—could find it. Not a tool mark. Not a maker's stamp. Letters.

Hazel went very still.

The whole workshop discipline of her training rose up in her at once: *never alter what you do not understand; never assume a fault is a fault.* Elias had drilled that into her years ago, his dry old voice patient above the bench: *A clock tells you everything if you ask politely. Most apprentices break things because they cannot bear to wait for the answer.* She waited now. She turned the wheel by its teeth, slowly, slowly, and the letters came around into the light one after another, and they were not initials.

They were a name. A whole name, cut small and fine into the brass of the rim itself: **ANSELMO RIBEIRO — 1849 — LISBOA**.

And beneath it, in a different hand—a rounder hand, less sure, cut by someone who had learned engraving late or hurriedly or both: **MARIA DO CÉU RIBEIRO — 1851**.

Hazel sat back on her heels inside the case, her brush suspended, her heart doing something strange and quiet.

*1849.* She knew her history the way a clockmaker knows history, through the town's records of repairs and refits, the logbooks Voss kept in the ledger room below. In 1849 there had been trouble on the water—she'd read the marginalia without understanding it, the way you read weather reports for a country you'll never visit. Refugees had come ashore that year. Dozens of them. Families off ships that had been refused at other ports, people with names that sounded foreign in the mouths of the harbormasters, people the town had taken in and then, when the political winds turned, quietly struck from the rolls as though the sea had taken them back.

She had read about it in a single afternoon and forgotten it by supper, because history, she had believed all her life, happened elsewhere. To other people. To large cities and important men.

But here it was. Here it had been all along, hiding in plain sight inside the machine she serviced every month. Someone—an apprentice? a master? some keeper of this clock whose name had not survived even in the logbooks—had stood exactly where Hazel was standing, with a graver in hand and lamplight exactly so, and had cut two names into the rim of a great wheel where no clerk would ever look, because no clerk looks inside clocks. Clerks look in ledgers. Ledgers can be burned, amended, struck through in red ink. But a clock is maintained by hands, and hands are loyal to other hands across the centuries.

Her own were shaking. She set down the brush.

* * *

She should have reported it. She understood that even then, in the animal part of her mind that kept calculating Brandt's patrol schedule, the sentry's cigarette breaks, the exact minutes of solitude remaining. An anomaly in the movement was a matter for the director's log. She was an apprentice; the clock was now, in some bureaucratic sense, military property. The correct action was a note in the maintenance book, an entry in triplicate, and then she could go home and sleep.

Instead she took the lamp deeper into the case.

Because once you have found one name, you begin to see the way names hide. Her eye changed. That is the only way she could ever describe it afterward—that her eye changed, the way a person who has spent their life hearing one language suddenly hears the grammar underneath another. She stopped looking at the movement as a mechanism and started looking at it as a text.

They were everywhere.

On the inner face of the barrel, under the winding square, where the light of an ordinary inspection would never fall: a name in a spidery nineteenth-century hand. On the flat of a bridge, filed smooth and polished and then engraved over, so that you had to catch it at a raking angle to know the polish concealed writing rather than preventing it. Inside the case door itself, along the lower rail where the hinge shadow pooled permanently: three names together, a family, cut in descending size—father, mother, child—and beside the child's name, no date at all, which meant either that whoever cut it did not know the child's birth year, or that the child had been born too recently to have one, or—

Hazel made herself stop and breathe.

She worked for the rest of that day the way a diver works, surfacing at intervals to listen for boots on the stair, then going down again. By the time the light through the observatory slit windows turned from white to honey, she had catalogued eleven names. No—twelve. There was one more on the underside of the crutch, visible only from below, from the pendulum shaft, and she had to lie flat on the floorboards with her arm through the aperture and the lamp held out over the drop to read it, her heart hammering not from fear of the height but from the intimacy of it: someone had hung over this same drop, decades ago, in terror or grief or fierce deliberate hope, and cut a name where only a person willing to dangle over darkness would ever find it.

Twelve names. Dates ranging from 1849 to 1877. Three different hands that she could distinguish, maybe four—one of them hers in miniature, she realized with a start: the slanted, economical letterforms that Elias taught, that Elias must have learned from his own master, passed down like a trade secret. Which meant that somewhere in this chain of inheritance, one of those long-dead keepers had been trained in the same lineage as she was. The knowledge had come down to her through the same channel as the names.

She sat on the floorboards with her back against the case, the lamp guttering, and let the thought finish arriving.

*The clock has always been an archive.*

Not a metaphor. She was not a woman for metaphors; she liked things she could take apart. She meant it literally. For seventy years and more, this machine had been keeping time for the town upstairs while keeping record beneath it—two registers running in parallel, the public one in ink and the true one in brass. Every purge the ledgers had suffered, some keeper had answered. Every family erased from the civic record had been written again, smaller and more stubbornly, inside the one object in the town that everyone needed and no one suspected. The clock governed the trains and the tides and the church bells and the school day; it was touched with reverence and fear; it was *serviced*, regularly, by precisely the sort of quiet, unimportant, good-with-her-hands people whom history was supposed to overlook.

That was the design. That was the genius of it. You did not hide the archive in a vault, because vaults get opened. You hid it in the maintenance. You hid it in the ordinary. You trusted it to the girl with the brushes, generation after generation, on the faith that a person who loves a mechanism will protect everything living inside it.

Hazel thought of the ledger room below—the fresh red strikes through Marchetti, through the Okafor brothers, through half a street of names—and felt something in her chest turn over and settle into a new position, like a detent clicking into place.

For twenty-two years she had believed her hands were good for nothing much. Repair work. Patience. The small virtues of the bench. She had watched her brothers talk politics and her mother pray about history and she had polished pivots, and she had privately understood herself to be a spectator at the world's affairs, a person standing outside the glass of the important shop window.

Now she understood that the window was the wrong image entirely. History did not happen elsewhere. History happened *here*, in this case, in this brass—it had been happening here since before she was born, carried on by people whose names were not in any book she had ever read, people who had done the only thing their position allowed: they had written the truth where it could not easily be destroyed, and they had kept the mechanism running, and they had said nothing, and they had died, and the names had waited in the dark for a pair of eyes to find them.

Her eyes. Her hands. Her turn.

* * *

The sentry knocked on the doorframe at six—the hour she was expected to leave—and Hazel called out that the strike train wanted attention, that she'd be another hour. He shrugged; a clockmaker was useful, and useful people were permitted small liberties. She heard him go back to his post and his cigarette, and she turned back to the open case.

She copied every name into her notebook first—the same notebook where, three days ago with trembling fingers, she had copied Marchetti's struck page from the ledger. She wrote them in pencil, carefully, with the dates and whatever locations the engravings gave: Lisboa, Genova, one entry marked only *the islands*. Twelve entries. She checked each one twice against the brass, the way she'd check a parts list, because accuracy was the whole point—if the record was to survive, it had to be *right*, and rightness was the one form of courage she had always possessed.

Then she closed the notebook and sat for a long moment with her palm flat against the cold flank of the great wheel, feeling the train turn beneath the steel like a pulse under skin.

"Who were you?" she asked aloud, very quietly, in the empty tower. The clock answered with its tick. Of course it did. It had been answering all along; she simply hadn't known how to listen.

She thought of Elias then—not with dread, for once, but with a kind of fierce gratitude. He had taught her everything: how to true a wheel, how to listen for a worn pivot by sound alone, how to hold a graver so the cut follows intention instead of fear. He had taught her without ever telling her why such skills might matter beyond the bench. Had he known? Had his own master known? Was the whole lineage of their craft, somewhere far back, entangled with this secret work—clockmakers as keepers of the unspeakable, maintainers of machines that remembered what empires preferred to forget?

She would ask him, when she saw him again. If she saw him again. His name was in red ink downstairs now, and the trucks came to the harbor every morning, and the world outside the observatory was rearranging itself into categories with terrible speed. But the thought of Elias no longer felt only like grief. It felt like inheritance. Somewhere, in whatever room or cellar or camp the army had put him, the man who taught her her trade was still alive, and his name still existed in at least two places—in the struck-through ledger, yes, but also now in her notebook, in her memory, and soon—

Soon, she thought, looking at the great wheel turning in the lamplight, in the brass.

She did not decide it that night, exactly. Decision implies deliberation among alternatives, and she was aware, even then, that there had been no alternative from the moment the lamp caught that first hidden line of letters. What happened instead was simpler and colder: she opened her tool roll, chose the finest graver—the one Elias had given her for her eighteenth birthday, saying only, *you will need something precise eventually*—and tested its edge against her thumbnail the way he had taught her.

Below the twelfth name, on the flat inner face of the barrel where the shadows lived, there was clean space. Room enough for many names. Room enough, perhaps, for all of them.

Hazel fitted the graver to her hand, angled the lamp low, and began to practice on a scrap of sheet brass—cutting *Marchetti*, over and over, until the letters came out steady.

She did not hear, three floors down, the front door opening, or the low urgent voices—Voss's and another's, an old man's voice she knew better than her own mother's—rising through the stairwell like water finding its level.

But the clock heard. And it kept the time of it.
