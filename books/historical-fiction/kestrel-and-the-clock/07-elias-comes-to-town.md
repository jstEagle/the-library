## 7. Elias Comes to Town

The letter came to him folded inside a watch, which was the only way Elias Marchetti trusted messages to travel anymore. A cheap watch, a Lepine calibre with a cracked porcelain dial, the kind sold in dozens from harbor stalls to sailors who would lose it within a month. But the sender had known what he would do with it: open the case back, read the slip of paper coiled beneath the balance wheel like a spring, and then never quite trust a timepiece again without checking.

*They have struck your name,* the paper said, in Director Voss's cramped, upright hand. *Marchetti no longer appears in the ledger. Come if you are coming at all. The harbor road is watched after dark.*

Elias sat for a long while in his room above the chandler's shop, the watch open in his palm, its escapement ticking against his skin like a small insistent pulse. Through the window he could hear the gulls arguing over the fish market's leavings and, beneath them, a sound that had not been in the town a season ago: the grind of army trucks on cobblestones, low gears complaining, engines left idling like animals told to wait.

So it had come. He had spent forty years believing that history was a tide that broke against larger shores than this—against capitals, against borders, against men whose names were printed rather than written. He had chosen this town precisely because it seemed too small to matter, too far down the map's margins for anyone's ambition. And now some clerk in some office had drawn a pen through *Marchetti*, and the stroke of that pen weighed more than forty years of gears and springs and honest work.

He thought, as he always did when the fear came, of Hazel's hands. Small hands, quick, stained with rouge and oil, holding a hairspring to the light the way one holds something holy. He had taught those hands everything he knew. It had been his one unselfish act, perhaps: to pass the trade forward, out of himself, so that something of him would survive that was not shame.

He should stay away. Every instinct of survival, honed across two countries and one disgrace, said so. The girl was safer with him gone; the old friend Voss was safer; the town itself was safer. He knew the arithmetic of disappearance better than any man alive. He had performed it once before, leaving behind a workshop, a language, a grave.

But his name had been struck. Not *enemy*—he could have borne enemy; enemy was at least a category a man could argue with. Struck. Erased. As though the sixty-one years, the apprenticeships, the watches repaired for three generations of this town's families, the wedding gift he'd made for the harbormaster's daughter with a hidden moonphase dial—as though all of it had never happened. A line through ink, and Elias Marchetti became a rumor, a ghost haunting his own bench.

There was one person in the world who had promised otherwise. *I will remember you,* she had said, in peacetime, in the doorway of his shop, with the evening light coming gold through the glass behind her. She had said it lightly, the way the young say things they cannot yet imagine having to keep.

He packed one bag. In it went his tools—the small ones, the ones a man needs to prove who he is—and a photograph he should have burned years ago and never could, and nothing else. At the door of the chandler's shop he paid what he owed and told the chandler he was going to visit a cousin inland. The chandler looked at the soldiers by the harbor and then at Elias, and took the money without meeting his eyes, and Elias understood that everyone now spoke in these doubled sentences, everyone lived in the space between what was said and what was meant.

* * *

He walked the coast road in the grey beginning of morning, when the sea and the sky were the same color and the town ahead of him was only a suggestion—a church tower, the observatory's white dome catching the first light like a held breath. Twice he stepped off the road into the gorse when he heard engines. Once a patrol passed close enough that he could hear a young soldier coughing, an ordinary human cough, and found it stranger and more frightening than any boot on stone.

Fear, he had learned, was mostly a matter of detail. Grand terror was abstract; it was the particulars that undid you. The particular way a notice on the telegraph pole fluttered in the wind, printed lists of names in a font chosen by someone in a distant office. The particular smell of the harbor—tar and brine and, now, diesel and leather and something animal about the transports. The particular fact that the bakery on the corner, which had sold him bread every week for eleven years, had a soldier leaning in its doorway, laughing at something, and the baker's face above him white as flour.

Elias kept his head down and his collar up and let the town look through him. He had practice at being invisible. It was, he thought bitterly, the one skill exile taught thoroughly. A man learns to walk without arriving, to stand in queues without being seen, to become furniture in his own life.

The observatory stood above the town on its bald hill, and the road to it was mercifully empty. His knees complained on the climb; his heart complained louder, though not from the grade. At the top, the building waited with its dome and its flagpole—flying new colors now—and its small windows blinking in the morning sun. Below it, somewhere under his feet, was the ledger room. Below everything now, it seemed, was the ledger room. The whole town had rearranged itself around that book.

He knocked at the service door, the one Voss had indicated in no letter but which old friends understand anyway, and after a long interval the door opened and there was Voss—taller than Elias remembered, or perhaps Elias had shrunk—with grey in his beard now and a new wariness in his eyes that hurt more than the soldiers below.

"You got my letter," Voss said.

"I got your letter." Elias's voice cracked on the second word, and he hated himself for it. "Is she here? The girl?"

"Inside. Quickly." And then, as Elias passed him, quieter: "You shouldn't have come, old friend."

"No," Elias agreed. "But my name was struck, and I discovered I am not finished with it yet."

Voss almost smiled. Almost. Then he bolted the door behind them both.

* * *

The cellar smelled of lamp oil, cold stone, and apples—Voss stored a few crates down there each autumn, and the fruit had gone soft and sweet-sharp in the dark. There was a cot that had not been there before, and a washstand, and a chair. Preparations, Elias understood. Voss had built this small refuge the way Elias might have built a movement into an escapement: carefully, in advance, hoping never to need it.

He had been there two days—he learned later—when the door opened again, past dusk, and he heard footsteps on the stair that were not Voss's. Voss descended stairs like a man descending; these footsteps came down like someone arriving, quick and light, already talking before the last step.

"—the third wheel is worn, that's why the minute hand drifts, I told Brandt it needed a full day's attention and he actually wrote it in his book, he writes *everything* in his—"

She stopped. The lamp in her hand swung, throwing their shadows huge against the arched ceiling, and Elias stood up from the cot too fast, and for a moment neither of them moved, as though the light itself had frozen them.

Then Hazel set the lamp on the washstand with exaggerated care, the way she handled anything fragile, and crossed the cellar, and put her arms around him.

She had grown, somehow, since summer. Or he had diminished. Her hair smelled of machine oil and soap, and her hands against his back were steady—those hands—and he felt something in his chest unlock that had been locked so long he had mistaken it for his skeleton.

"Maestro," she said, and the word undid him entirely. He had not wept at his own exile. He wept now, quietly and briefly, into the shoulder of his apprentice, and she held him through it without embarrassment, as though tears were simply another thing a mechanism produced under strain and could be serviced.

"They struck you," she said when he had stepped back, wiping his face with a handkerchief she pressed on him. "I saw it. I copied the page. Everything—the dates, your origin, all of it. It's in my notebook, hidden, and it will stay there until—"

"Until what?"

"Until it goes somewhere better than a notebook." She said it simply, without drama, and that simplicity frightened him more than any proclamation. There was a new thing in her face, a hardness gathering behind the softness like frost forming in the workings of a lock. He recognized it. He had seen it in mirrors, decades ago.

"You should not have come," he told her, echoing Voss. "Hazel. Listen to me. If they find me here, they find you here. A clockmaker and her exiled teacher, in the cellar of the observatory where the deportations are timed—" He shook his head. "I have survived by being no one. You must learn the same trick."

"Trick." She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a bright angry edge. "You taught me that a clock is honest. That it doesn't care who winds it. That every part matters or the whole thing stops. And now you want me to pretend people are different—that some parts can be removed and thrown away and the mechanism runs fine?" She turned away, fussed with the lamp wick, buying her composure back trim by trim. "I promised you, maestro. In peacetime. Promises made in peacetime are the only ones that count. Anyone can promise in a storm."

He looked at her—at the oil stains on her fingers, at the burn scar on her wrist from the day she'd soldered too confidently at fourteen—and felt the fear and the tenderness braid themselves tighter together until he could no longer tell which was which. This was what he had made, in his one unselfish act. Not just a clockmaker. A keeper. And keepers, he knew from long study of history's mechanisms, were the ones who got broken.

"Sit," he said at last. "Tell me about the third wheel."

And she did—eagerly, gratefully, the old liturgy of the trade rising between them like a shelter. She described the wear pattern, and he asked questions, and for an hour the cellar was only a workshop, the town outside only a town, and the two of them were only what they had always been: teacher and student, bent together over an invisible movement, diagnosing the world's drift.

* * *

Later, when she had gone up to sleep, Elias lay on the cot listening to the house breathe. Above him, faint through the floorboards, came a sound he knew better than his own heartbeat: the master clock, transmitted through the observatory's bones, tick by tick governing the sleeping town. Somewhere in that great machine, official time was being manufactured, and schedules were being drawn from it, and trucks were being loaded by its authority.

He thought about names. About how a name is a kind of mainspring—all the tension of a life coiled inside a single word, released tooth by tooth into the days. Strike the name and you do not stop the life; you only make it unwound, unrecorded, running wild in the dark.

Footsteps again. Voss, this time, descending heavily with two cups and a bottle of something amber. He poured without asking, and they drank in silence, two old men in a cellar full of rotting apples, while above them the clock kept its indifferent count.

"She reminds me of you," Voss said finally. "Before."

"There is no before anymore."

"There is always a before. That's what makes it bearable." Voss turned his cup in his hands. "I can keep you here a week. Perhaps two. After that, provisioning becomes... visible. And there is an inspection coming, Elias. A senior man, from the capital. When he comes, this cellar must be exactly nothing."

Elias nodded. He had expected no more, and was grateful even for this. "A week," he said. "Long enough to teach her one more thing."

"What thing?"

Elias looked up toward the ceiling, toward the ticking. "How to leave a mark that doesn't show."

Voss went pale, and did not ask again, and climbed back upstairs with his candle, and Elias lay alone in the dark beneath the great clock, counting its beats like a prayer, waiting for morning, when Hazel would come down again—and when she did, he resolved, he would tell her what he had never told anyone in this country: the truth about why he had been exiled the first time, and what he had done, and whom he had lost.

If his name was going to be erased, it would at least be erased honestly.
