## 8. Officer Brandt's Schedule

The order came down from the observatory in the voice of a man who had never once been late for anything he considered important.

Hazel was cleaning the second-hand arbor when the door at the base of the tower boomed—three knocks, evenly spaced, like a metronome set to a tempo no musician would choose. Voss answered it. She heard the exchange only in fragments, the way one hears weather through a wall: *requisition, schedule, precision, by authority of.* Then footsteps on the spiral stair, boots rather than shoes, and Officer Brandt emerged into the clock room with his cap under his arm as though he were entering a church.

He was younger than she expected. That was the first obscenity of him—not his rank, not the grey uniform pressed to a knife's edge, but the youth of him, the soft unlined face of a man who might otherwise have spent his life measuring land or teaching arithmetic. He looked around the room the way a buyer looks around a shop: appraising, already calculating what each thing would be worth to him.

"Director Voss," he said, without looking at Voss. "And this is the technician."

"Hazel Almeida," Hazel said. "Apprentice clockmaker."

Brandt's eyes found her hands first. They always did, afterward—she would learn that about him, that he read people's hands like other men read faces. He glanced at the oil stains on her fingers, the fine scratches across her knuckles, and something in his expression warmed by a degree.

"You maintain this movement?"

"I do."

"Then you are the most useful person in this town." He said it pleasantly, almost kindly, and the kindness was worse than contempt would have been. "I am told this clock governs every official timepiece in the district. Trains, telegrams, harbor bells, municipal offices—all corrected against it."

"It is the master," Voss said carefully. "It has been the town's standard since before either of us was born."

"And now it will be the army's." Brandt walked the circumference of the great case, his boots ringing on the floorboards, and stopped before the dial with its painted stars and its single sweeping minute hand. "You cannot imagine, Miss Almeida, what a thing like this means to an operation. Where I trained, they kept time by wristwatch—forty officers, forty wristwatches, forty opinions about what o'clock it was. Chaos. But this—" he gestured up at the movement behind its glass, at the pendulum hanging in its long slow arc like a held breath, "—this is one opinion. This is discipline made physical."

Hazel said nothing. Her hands, resting on the cloth spread over the workbench, had gone very still, and she watched them as if they belonged to someone calmer.

"The schedule begins tomorrow," Brandt continued. "The transport office requires synchronization to the second. You will service the movement daily—at seven in the morning and again at six in the evening—and you will log every adjustment. I will initial the log myself." He produced a notebook, wrote something in it with a fountain pen that scratched like a small animal. "Accuracy is not merely a virtue here. It is the whole point. A train that leaves four minutes late carries four minutes of confusion, and confusion is where disorder lives." He smiled at her then, briefly, and she saw that he believed every word, that to him this was genuinely a matter of principle, of beauty even. "You should be proud. Most craftsmen spend their whole lives and their work is never needed for anything. Yours is needed."

*For what?* she wanted to ask, though she knew. The whole town knew by then; the notices at the harbor had seen to that, and the lists, and the trucks. She knew what the trains carried and she knew why the army wanted the seconds counted precisely, and Officer Brandt stood beneath the pendulum admiring it.

"I keep it running," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "That is all I've ever done."

"All any of us do," said Brandt, "and it is enough."

* * *

He came back the next morning at six fifty-five, five minutes early, and stood watching her work.

She had thought she understood humiliation before—she had been a girl with ink-stained cuffs in a workshop full of men who called her *the little one*, who handed her the fiddly jobs because a girl's fingers were cheap. She had thought humiliation was being underestimated. She had been wrong. Humiliation was this: kneeling at the crutch of the escapement with an eye loupe screwed into her socket, adjusting the pallet stones by fractions of a millimeter while a man whose entire trade was death stood behind her murmuring approval.

"Smoother," he said, on the fourth morning. "The tick is smoother than last week. You've done something."

"I re-polished the entrance stone," she said, not turning around. "There was a drag."

"A drag." He repeated the word as though tasting it. "You can feel a drag in the beat?"

"Anyone trained could. It's in the sound. A healthy clock has an even heartbeat—tick and tock exactly alike, exactly spaced. When something drags, one side goes heavy. Listen—" She let three beats pass. "Tick... tock. Tick... tock. Even. Like a sleeper breathing."

Behind her, silence. Then Brandt laughed—a short, genuine laugh, unguarded—and it frightened her more than anything he had done with his voice before. "A sleeper breathing," he said. "My God. And we hang these things on walls and never think of them." He wrote in his log: *Movement serviced. Escapement adjusted. Excellent condition.* His pen paused. "Miss Almeida. Do you know what your clock did yesterday?"

She closed the winding hatch. "It kept time."

"At eleven-forty precisely, a transport departed the east siding carrying four hundred and six persons registered for relocation. Departure verified against observatory standard time, error nil." He said it the way Voss read barometric readings aloud—as data, clean and weightless. "Four hundred and six human beings moved without one wasted minute. No confusion. No disorder. Because a girl from this town keeps an escapement polished." He capped his pen. "Somebody's mother taught them their prayers. Somebody's teacher taught them their trade. And somewhere tonight there is an officer writing a report that says everything proceeded according to schedule, and underneath that sentence, invisible, is your hand."

He meant it as praise. That was the unbearable part—that he had constructed, sincerely, a philosophy in which her craft and his schedules were partners, in which the polish on a pallet stone and the departure of a train full of neighbors were two ends of one honest chain of cause and effect. He believed he was paying her the highest compliment his world contained.

Hazel gathered her tools into their roll, one by one, letting the ritual steady her. Scissors. Oil bottle. Screwdrivers in descending size. Each socket in the canvas worn soft by Elias's hands before hers, by whoever's hands before his, back through the generations of people who had kept time because keeping time was a form of care.

"Thank you, Officer," she heard herself say. "Seven o'clock tomorrow."

* * *

After that, the days took on the shape of the schedule, which was, she understood dimly, exactly what schedules are for.

Six in the evening: service the clock under Brandt's eye or his corporal's. Log every adjustment. Initial, counter-initial. Seven in the morning: service again, alone but for the soldier at the door who had begun, unnervingly, to hum while he stood guard—an off-key folk tune, endless, that worked itself into her skull so thoroughly she caught herself matching the pendulum's beat to it and had to stop and retune her own listening.

Between those hours, the clock ran the town's erasure. She learned the rhythm of it whether she wanted to or not. Transport days had a texture: the telegraph chattering below in the ledger room, the harbor bell rung off-schedule, the particular quality of quiet in the streets when the trucks rolled through. And always, threaded through it, the master clock's tick, steady as a sleeper's breath, and her own knowledge lying over that sound like a hand over a mouth.

Voss changed too. He had always been a formal man—the director's stiffness was legend, the way he corrected visitors' pronunciation of stellar names—but now he moved through the observatory like a man walking on river ice, testing each step. He signed the requisition papers without reading them aloud anymore. He had stopped saying *this will pass*. Once, late, passing her on the stair, he paused and said, quietly, "The alternative to running it well is that they find someone else to run it badly, and then they find reasons about us." It sounded rehearsed, as if he had been composing the sentence for days, offering it to the dark, and she understood that it was not addressed to her so much as to his own reflection. She nodded. She did not tell him what she thought of the arithmetic, which was that it was probably correct and did not matter at all.

Because here was the thing she discovered in those weeks, the thing she could not have explained to Brandt even under interrogation: the clock did not know what it timed. Brass does not moralize. Steel does not refuse. The escapement released the gear train tooth by tooth with perfect indifference, and the same indifference governed the hands on the dial and the trains on the east siding. The machine was innocent the way weather is innocent. Which meant the guilt lived entirely elsewhere—in schedules, in signatures, in initials on a maintenance log—and it pooled wherever a human being chose to stand. Brandt stood in it gladly. Voss stood in it carefully, at the edge, keeping his boots dry. Hazel stood in it every morning at seven, and every evening at six, and she felt it rise past her ankles, her knees, and still she stood, because the alternative was a stranger's hands inside the movement, and she could not bear that. She could not bear it. That was the whole truth of her, in those weeks: not courage, not yet—just the possessiveness of a craftsman, which is love wearing work clothes.

But at night, after the corporal's humming footsteps faded down the stair, she sat at the workbench with her notebook open to the page she had copied from the ledger, and she read the struck names until she knew them better than her own. Marchetti, Elias—watchmaker, origin foreign, struck. And below him the others, forty-one lines of clerk's handwriting, each name crossed out with a single ruler-straight stroke, tidy as a pallet stone, final as a stopped heart.

She began, without deciding to, to study the crossings-out. They were all identical—same pressure, same angle, same bureaucratic serenity. Whoever wielded the ruler had done it seated, unhurried, perhaps with tea cooling at his elbow. That detail undid her more than the names did. Somewhere below her feet, in the ledger room, a man erased four hundred lives with the posture of someone balancing accounts, and upstairs the clock he synchronized his day by breathed its even tick-tock, and neither the man nor the clock thought anything of it at all.

One night she took her finest graver from the tool roll and held it under the lamp and did not use it. Her hand shook. Not with fear—with the specific trembling of a shape pressing to be made, the way a sneeze trembles before it arrives. She put the graver away. Too soon. The barrel was checked, the log was initialed, the corporal hummed. Too soon.

But she knew the shape of the thing now. It waited in her chest like a spring wound tight, storing every transport day, every initialled page, every kind word from Brandt's soft young mouth, and a spring, she knew better than anyone alive, exists for one reason only: to be released.

* * *

On the last evening of the month, Brandt arrived early and did not watch her work. Instead he stood at the window with his hands clasped behind him, looking out over the darkened harbor, and spoke without preamble, as if continuing a conversation she had not known they were having.

"Do you know what I admire about clocks, Miss Almeida? Everything in them is legible. Open the case and every part explains itself—the wheel does this because of that, the spring acts so because of thus. Cause follows cause, cleanly, all the way down. No mysteries." He turned. "People are not like that. People hide springs in themselves. Motives they won't admit. A man smiles at you and somewhere inside him a wheel turns that he has never once looked at." His eyes rested on her face with mild, patient interest—the interest of a man checking a pocket watch, not suspicious, merely confirming. "I prefer machines. You always know where you are with a well-made machine."

"Mostly," Hazel said, winding the barrel, "you know where you are with a well-made clock because it tells you."

He laughed his short laugh, tipped his cap, and went down the stair. His boots rang away into the dark, evenly spaced, like a metronome.

Hazel stood a long moment in the ticking room, her heart going hard and strange. Then she took up her lamp and her notebook and descended, past the ledger room where the clerks' lamps still burned, down toward the cellar where—for three nights now—she had been leaving bread wrapped in a cloth, and where, tonight for the first time, the cloth was gone, and from behind the far stacks of old charts came a voice she would have known anywhere in the world, low and careful and unmistakably alive:

"Hazel. Put down the lamp slowly, child. Let me look at your face."

Elias Marchetti stepped out of the dark, thinner, greyer, his hands—those hands—held out before him like a man approaching fire, and behind her, up the stair, the master clock ticked on, keeping everyone's time but its own.
