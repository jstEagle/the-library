## 9. Names in Brass

The observatory kept two kinds of night. There was the ordinary dark that came through the windows when the lamps were doused, salt-smelling and enormous, the same darkness that lay over the harbor and the hills and every sleeping house in town. And then there was the darkness inside the clock—a smaller, denser thing, oiled and brass-warm, a darkness with a heartbeat. Hazel had come to prefer it. Inside the case, with her sleeves rolled and her lamp turned low behind a tin shield, she could imagine the war was only a rumor told by people who had never held a fine tool in their hands.

Three nights after she had copied the ledger page into her notebook, she took the notebook down into the clock.

She had waited, at first, for courage to arrive like a delivery, wrapped and paid for. It did not come. What came instead was habit—the same obedience to craft that made her rise before dawn to wind the great barrels, the same steadiness that let her file a pallet face to within a hair's breadth without breathing on it. She discovered that courage was not a feeling but a discipline, and that her hands were better at discipline than her heart had ever been. So on the third night, when the sentry's boots had passed the east wall on his round and his footsteps faded toward the harbor road, she unlocked the case, slipped inside the cage of gearing with her notebook folded against her ribs, and began.

The barrel chamber was the clock's secret heart, a drum of polished steel where the great weights wound themselves down through the week. Around it, Voss's predecessors had built a housing of brass plates—thick, soft metal, chosen a century ago because it cut easily and did not rust in the sea air. No one looked at those plates. They were infrastructure. They were the dull interior of a magnificent exterior, and Officer Brandt, who could recite the train timetable to the minute, had never once shown interest in what he called *the plumbing*.

Hazel fitted her graver into its rosewood handle and chose a plate near the escapement, where her legitimate work gave her honest reason to linger. She laid the notebook open against the frame. The first name was *Marchetti, Elias — origin: Torino — watchmaker*, struck through in violet clerk's ink with a single impatient line, as if the name were an error in arithmetic rather than a man who had repaired the mayor's chronometer for thirty years.

Her hand shook so badly on the first stroke that she scored a crooked furrow an inch long and had to stop, pressing her forehead against the cold frame until her breath slowed. *This is not defiance,* she told herself. *This is maintenance.* The lie helped. She repositioned the graver, thought of Elias's hands guiding hers over a balance spring—you do not fight the metal, girl; you agree with it—and began again.

The letters came slowly. She was engraving, not writing: each character cut twice, once to outline, once to deepen, the swarf flicking away in bright curls she caught in her palm so nothing would fall into the works below. An hour passed. Two. Her neck ached; the lamp oil thinned; somewhere above her the pendulum swung with the patience of something that would outlive them all. When she finished the final letter and blew the dust from the plate, ELIAS MARCHETTI sat hidden in the shadow between two gear mounts, legible only to someone lying inside the clock with a lamp—which was to say, almost no one, ever, and yet someone, someday.

She read it back three times. Then she covered it with a whisper-thin film of plate oil and dust, aging it, and wept, briefly and silently, the way one does over finished work: not from sorrow exactly, but from the strange fullness of having made a thing that was true.

* * *

Elias found out on the fifth night, because Elias missed nothing.

He had been living in the cellar for nearly two weeks now, in the narrow room behind the coal stores where Voss kept his obsolete instruments—a sextant with a cracked mirror, a barometer that had given up on weather entirely. Elias had arranged these relics on a shelf as though they were still in service, and he spent his days repairing the cellar's other occupant: a marine chronometer Voss had confiscated from a condemned fishing vessel years ago and never found time to fix. It was, Hazel thought, very like him. A man whose own future had been struck from a book, content to make some dead ship's timepiece run again.

That evening he climbed the service stair to bring her bread wrapped in a cloth—he had appointed himself provisioner, though Voss forbade him above the ground floor after dark—and stopped dead in the doorway of the clock room. Her kit lay open on the floor. Beside it, the notebook, open to the copied page. She had meant to hide both before he came up. Weariness had made her careless.

"What is this?" His voice was very quiet. In the fortnight since he'd come down out of the hills, he had grown thinner; the lamplight found new hollows in his face. But his eyes went over the notebook the way they once went over a customer's broken watch—reading the whole failure at a glance.

"You know what it is," Hazel said. "You taught me to read movements. Read this."

He set down the bread. He crouched, and he opened the clock case with the familiarity of a man who had helped design half its repairs forty years ago, and he lay inside with the lamp, and he found the name. For a long moment he said nothing. When he backed out into the light his face was doing something complicated—grief and fury and something underneath both that might have been gratitude, though he seemed angry about the gratitude too.

"Do you know what they do to saboteurs?" he said. "It is not prison, Hazel. Not for a girl with a foreign father and no protector."

"It's not sabotage. It's—" She reached for the words and found they had been forming all week, waiting for someone to say them to. "The ledger is their book. They can strike whatever they like from it. This clock governs the trains, the harbor bells, the town's whole official hour. If your name lives anywhere, let it live in the thing that tells them what time it is. Let their own schedules depend on a machine that remembers you." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated that, and kept going anyway. "They erased you from paper. Fine. I am putting you into brass."

"That is not craft," Elias said harshly. "That is grief wearing craft's apron."

"Yes," Hazel said. "All good work is."

He stared at her. Outside, far off, the sentry coughed. And then Elias Marchetti, who had survived two countries and one betrayal and thirty years of being a foreigner, sat down on the floor of the clock room like an old man at the end of a long walk, and put his head briefly in his hands, and said, "Then we do it properly. Your serifs are wrong. You are cutting Roman capitals freehand; they will all match, and matching is a signature. If anyone ever finds one name, they find all of them, and they know who wrote them."

Hazel felt the floor tilt under her—not with fear but with the vertigo of being understood completely for the first time in her life.

"So we don't write in my hand," she said slowly.

"We write in everyone's hand," Elias said. "A different style for every name. The clerk's slant for some. A blackletter for others. Schoolroom loops, merchant's shorthand, a sailor's crude block letters. Forty names, forty writers. Let them find the archive and believe the archive made itself—that a hundred years of clockmakers each scratched a friend's name in passing, the way men mark tools." He was already reaching for her graver, testing the point against his thumbnail, and his eyes had gone bright with the old workshop fever. "Show me your plates."

* * *

They worked in shifts, on the nights when the moon was thin and the sentry's rounds predictable. Hazel took the hours after midnight; Elias came up from the cellar on the nights Voss attended his astronomy society dinner in the county seat—an irony Hazel declined to examine—and worked until the sky grayed. They left notes for each other tucked inside the case, disguised as maintenance memoranda: *third wheel endshake excessive, attended.* Beneath, in pencil so faint it barely existed, *the Torino names are done.*

Matching hands was harder than Hazel had imagined, and more instructive than anything her apprenticeship had yet taught her. To forge a clerk's slant she had to study the violet-inked ledger page until she understood not just the letterforms but the boredom behind them, the wrist that leaned right because the clerk braced his book awkwardly. To write like a sailor she had to imagine a man who learned his letters on a forecastle, gouging with whatever point came to hand. Each name required her to become, for twenty minutes, the person it belonged to—to think in Marta's father's blunt strokes, in the elegant descending flourishes of Signora Ferro who had once sung in the church choir. It was unbearable and it was necessary, and she could not have said where one ended and the other began.

Grief, she discovered, behaves like metal under a graver. It resists, and then it yields exactly along the line you hold it to, and what comes away is a bright curl of nothing, and what remains is the shape of what you refused to forget.

Some nights the work went badly. On the night she engraved the Ferro family—five names, the smallest the children's, and she had to invent children's handwriting, which meant imagining children learning letters who would now never need them—she cut two names too deep and had to start the plate over, and Elias found her at dawn sitting on the floor with her forehead on her knees.

"You cannot weep for each one individually," he said gently, lowering himself beside her. "There are forty. You will drown."

"Then what?"

"You grieve the way the clock does. One tick at a time, evenly, forever. That is the whole trick of a escapement, Hazel—it does not feel the weight. It releases it. A little at a time, in perfect measure." He picked up her graver and closed her fingers around it. "Feel everything. Cut steadily."

So she learned. By the third week she could finish a name in under an hour with the lamp shielded, listening past her own pulse for boots on gravel. She aged every inscription with oil and dust and the careful application of a burnisher worn smooth by dead men's hands. She catalogued the plates in her memory only—nothing written, nothing carried, nothing that could be found on her body if she were searched. The archive lived entirely inside the machine now, and she began to understand that this was the deepest hiding of all: not concealment, but ordinariness. The names were becoming part of the clock's body, indistinguishable from its age.

Elias grew more himself with every name restored to metal. He laughed once—a short, rusty sound—at his own imitation of a shipping agent's cramped capitals. He told stories as he cut: which names had ordered which clocks from him, whose grandfather had traded him a barrel of anchovies for a regulator. The struck page from the ledger became, in his mouth, a map of the whole town's history, and Hazel realized he was doing for himself what she was doing for him: refusing to be reduced to a line through a name.

One night near the end of the month, working side by side in the barrel chamber with their shoulders touching in the narrow dark, he paused over a half-finished letter and said, "My father cut his maker's mark inside every case. Always in the same place. He said the work should be able to testify, if it ever needed to." He blew away the swarf. "I thought it was sentiment. Now I think he knew something about countries. Countries lose their memories. Good work doesn't."

"How many are left?" Hazel asked.

Elias counted silently against the plate edges. "Eleven."

Below them, faint through the floor, came the rumble of the harbor bell counting the hour—one o'clock, then two—and beneath the bell another sound they had learned to hear past: trucks, on the coast road, moving north. The deportations ran on her clock. Somewhere in the dark the next list was already typed, already scheduled against times her own hands would certify accurate in the morning, when Officer Brandt came in with his polished boots and his approving nod and checked the master dial against his pocket watch.

Eleven names left. Hazel fitted the graver to the next plate and thought, with a calm that frightened her a little, that she could keep this up for years—night after night, tick after tick—if that was what it cost.

She did not know, then, how soon the cost would change currency. Because when she climbed out at dawn and stood at the observatory window watching the light come up over the harbor, she saw the fishing boats at anchor—and among them, at the quay, an army staff car she had never seen before, flying the pennant of the inspection corps.

Someone was coming to look inside things.
