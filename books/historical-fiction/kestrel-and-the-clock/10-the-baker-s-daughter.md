## 10. The Baker's Daughter

Marta Reinholt woke before the birds, as she had every morning of her nineteen years, and for four full seconds she did not remember. Those seconds were the sweetest thing left to her, that pale interval between sleep and knowing, when the ceiling above her cot was only a ceiling and the smell of yeast rising through the floorboards was only bread, only morning, only the ordinary miracle of another day. Then the notice on the kitchen table came back to her, the way it always did, unfolding itself in her memory with a sound like paper tearing: *Reinholt*. Her father's name, and therefore hers, printed in a government hand so clean it seemed proud of itself.

She dressed in the dark. She braided her hair without a mirror, because she had stopped looking at mirrors three days ago, when the notices went up and half the town stopped meeting each other's eyes. Downstairs, the ovens were already breathing heat, and her father stood at the peel with his sleeves rolled, flour to his elbows, working the way he always worked — as if the dough could not hear the news.

"You're late," he said, but his voice had no bite in it. It hadn't since the notice.

"The clock was wrong," Marta said, which was a lie. The observatory clock was never wrong. It was the one thing in this town that had never once lied to anyone, and now it counted down the days until the train came, and she hated it with a purity of hatred she had not known herself capable of. Every hour it struck, she flinched. Every quarter-hour, its small chime fell through the bakery like a stone dropped down a well.

Her father handed her the long paddle and they worked side by side in the amber dark, sliding loaves into the oven's mouth, and neither of them spoke the word *train*, and neither of them spoke the word *Tuesday*, and both words sat in the room anyway, plain as salt.

* * *

The town had changed its face overnight, the way a man changes after a funeral — same features, different light behind them. Marta made her delivery rounds at dawn because her father could no longer walk the streets without men spitting near his boots, and because someone had to keep the shop alive, and because walking kept the fear from pooling in her feet.

She noticed things now that she had never thought to notice. Which shutters opened when she knocked. Which housewives took the bread with bare hands instead of gloves. Frau Lindqvist, who had bought rye from them every Thursday for eleven years, who had held Marta as a baby and pronounced her fat and healthy — Frau Lindqvist now took her loaf by the paper wrapping only, two fingers, as though the bread itself might be contagious. "Times are hard," she said, not unkindly, which was worse than cruelty. Kindness that would not touch you was just arithmetic wearing a face.

At the harbor end of Kestrel Street, new paint. Stenciled letters on the shutter of the Marchetti workshop, the little place that smelled forever of oil and brass shavings, where an old man with ink-stained fingers had once repaired her mother's music box and refused payment, saying, *A child should have music, that is payment enough.* The stencil read: PROPERTY OF ABANDONED PERSONS. Marta stood in front of it a long moment, holding her basket, feeling the warmth of the loaves through the cloth going cold against her hip.

Abandoned persons. As if Elias Marchetti had walked out on them, and not the other way around.

It was there, outside the shuttered workshop, that she saw Hazel Almeida come out of the baker's own shadow — no. Out of the alley beside the cooper's yard, notebook under her arm, that ink stain on her cuff she always carried like a birthmark. They had been children together, distant as planets: Marta in the flour dust, Hazel in the metal filings, their orbits touching only at school, at church, at the fountain in the square where all the town's children drank from the same cold water without ever wondering about it.

"Marta," Hazel said. Not *hello*. Just her name, said low, the way you say something you've been carrying.

"I heard," Marta said. "That they called you up to the observatory. That you keep the clock now."

"I service it. For the army." Hazel's jaw tightened around the words like a wire around a bundle. "Marta, I—"

"I know what my name says now." Marta heard her own voice come out flat and strange, a voice borrowed from somewhere colder. "I can read. My mother taught me, God rest her, with a ruler across my knuckles. I can read very well."

They stood in the alley's shade, two girls with flour and iron filings respectively ground into their skin, and the town moved around them with its new careful footsteps, and Marta heard herself say the thing she had been not-saying for three days:

"Can you fix it?"

Hazel looked at her. And Marta watched the answer arrive in her friend's face before her friend's mouth had the courage to form it — watched it land like a bird striking glass.

"No," Hazel said. "No, Marta, I fix clocks. I fix—" Her hands came up, those clever hands, and made a shape in the air as if holding something small and intricate and useless. "I fix time. I can't fix what they've written."

"Then what are you doing up there?" It came out harsher than Marta meant, and she saw it wound, and she did not take it back. "Every day they march people to the station by your clock's good grace. Every train leaves on *your* time."

The color went out of Hazel's face entirely, leaving her gray as unpolished pewter. "You think I don't know that?"

"I think you go home at night," Marta said, "to a name that isn't on any list yet."

* * *

She regretted it by evening, the way you regret throwing a stone — not because the target didn't deserve it but because the throw revealed what your arm wanted, all your life, to do. She lay awake listening to the ovens tick as they cooled, that particular metal speech of contracting iron, and she thought: she is one girl with a screwdriver. What did I want from her? A revolution? A miracle?

What she had wanted, she admitted to the dark ceiling, was to be saved. That was all. The oldest prayer in the world, said in a new dialect.

On Monday she closed the shop early — there were fewer buyers now; the lists had emptied half the street of appetite — and she walked up Observatory Hill in her good shoes, the ones with the button hooks, because if she was going to beg she would do it standing straight.

The observatory door was open a hand's width, breathing out machine oil and cold stone. Inside, the stair spiraled up into the drum of the dome, and everywhere was the tick — not ticking exactly, Marta realized, but a deep continuous respiration, the master clock breathing through the whole building the way the ovens breathed heat through the bakery. She had lived her whole life under this sound without once hearing it. Now it seemed to be saying her name over and over in a language of gears: *Reinholt, Reinholt.*

"Hazel?"

"Up here. Mind the third stair, it wants mending."

She climbed. The clock room opened around her like the inside of some enormous patient animal, brass ribs and pendulum heart, and there was Hazel on her ladder with her sleeves pinned back, a jeweler's loupe screwed into her eye, looking for all the world like a surgeon operating on God.

Then Marta saw the soldier.

He sat by the window with a rifle across his knees, young enough that his ears stuck out, reading something with his lips moving. He glanced up, took her in — basket, shawl, the name stitched invisibly into her — and looked back down. A guard. Of course a guard. The clock told the trains when to leave; the trains were the army's business now; the army posted boys with rifles next to anything important.

"What is it?" Hazel asked, coming down the ladder, and her voice was careful, pitched under the soldier's hearing, and Marta understood suddenly that everything in this room would have to be said in that register, that lowered frequency, that everything important from now on would be whispered or nothing.

"Can we walk?" Marta said.

* * *

They walked the gallery path behind the observatory, where the hill fell away toward the harbor and the sea did its old indifferent work against the breakwater. Behind them the building held its breath. Ahead, gulls argued over something dead on the tide line.

"My father won't run," Marta said. "I've asked him. I've begged him. He says a man who runs stops being a man, and then they've won twice." She laughed, and the laugh came out broken, one dry syllable. "As if being a man will help him on Tuesday. As if dignity is edible."

"Where would you even go?" Hazel said quietly. "The roads are watched. The boats—"

"The fishing boats. Someone said the fishermen still run things out at night, for money."

"And where does a baker's daughter get that kind of money?" Hazel stopped walking. The wind came up off the water and pulled at both their shawls. "Marta. Listen to me. If I could carry you out of here in my toolbox I would. If I could engrave you onto something and screw you down safe somewhere they'd never look—"

"Onto what?"

Hazel's mouth closed. Opened. Closed again, and Marta watched her friend perform, badly, the act of swallowing a secret. Hazel Almeida had never been able to keep a secret; she had once ruined the surprise of Marta's sixteenth birthday entirely by accident, three weeks early, bursting with it.

"Nothing," Hazel said. "Maintenance work. There's — there are plates inside the movement that need replacing. Old brass. I've been cutting new ones."

"Why would you need new plates?"

"Wear," Hazel said, in the voice of someone reciting. "Wear and corrosion. It's normal."

And there it was, the lie, sitting between them on the path like a dropped glove. Marta picked it up whether she wanted to or not. She was a baker's daughter; she knew dough, knew how a lie rose and swelled and finally split along the scoring you'd cut into it. Hazel was hiding names. She didn't know how she knew — she only knew it the way you know bread is done, by smell, by the feel of the air — Hazel was putting the struck names somewhere inside that clock, somewhere the clerks would never think to look, keeping them the way you keep a flame cupped in two hands through wind.

"You're writing us down," Marta said slowly. "Somewhere in there. You're writing us down so that after — whoever comes after — finds us."

Hazel said nothing. The sea said nothing louder.

"That's what you'll do with mine? When they strike me Tuesday?" Marta's voice shook and she let it. "You'll carve *Marta Reinholt* into a piece of brass and hide it among the gears, and some day, fifty years from now, some person with a screwdriver will open the case and say, oh look, here was a girl, she made bread, they put her on a train — and that will be my monument? That will be what my father's dignity purchased?"

"Yes," Hazel said, and now she was crying, angry tears, wiping them away with the back of the hand that held the loupe so the lens flew off and swung on its cord, glinting. "Yes, that is what I can do, and I have lain awake every night this week wishing to God it were more, but it isn't, Marta, it is *brass*, it is only brass, it is all I have, and everyone keeps coming to me asking me to be more than a clockmaker and I *can't*—"

Her voice cracked apart on the last word and she turned away toward the sea, shoulders shaking, and Marta stood in the wind and felt her own fury dissolve into something much worse: pity. Pity for the strong one. Pity for the girl with the clever hands who was already doing the impossible and being hated, by people like Marta, for the size of the possible.

"I'm sorry," Marta said.

"Don't be. You're right." Hazel wiped her face with her wrist, briskly, the way her master must have taught her to wipe oil from a dial. "Being right doesn't save you either. That's the joke none of us are laughing at."

* * *

They sat on the gallery wall with the basket between them, and Marta took out the two rolls she'd brought — the good ones, the ones with the crust that sang when it cooled — and gave one to Hazel, and they ate in silence while the sun went copper over the water and the great clock behind them measured out their last shared hours in strokes too heavy to count.

"When I was twelve," Marta said, "you fixed my mother's watch. Do you remember? After she died. Father was going to sell it, it had stopped, and you took it in that little shop of Marchetti's and you had it running again in an afternoon, and you wouldn't take payment either. He said, *that girl has honest hands.*"

"He said that about you too. About your bread."

"Did he?" Something loosened in Marta's chest, unexpected as a bird flushed from cover. "Old Marchetti. Have you seen him? Since the notices?"

Hazel was quiet a moment too long. "No," she said. And then, softer, watching the harbor: "But I don't believe he's gone. Some things aren't finished yet."

There was no way to ask what that meant with the boy and his rifle twenty paces behind the wall, so Marta let it go, and finished her roll, and brushed the crumbs from her skirt carefully, every crumb, because waste had become a sin again the way it was in wartime stories.

"Hazel. If it goes badly Tuesday. If they put me on the train — will you do it properly? My name? Whole name, and the year, and—" she stopped, and made herself finish, "—and that I baked. Put that I baked. Not born, not died. Baked."

Hazel took her hand. Her palm was hard as saddle leather and warm as a fresh loaf, and she held on with the grip of someone who knows exactly how much force small things can bear.

"Baked," she promised. "Whole name and the year. I swear it on the movement itself."

They sat until the light failed, two girls on a wall above a drowning empire, and below them in the harbor the fishing boats knocked gently against their moorings like restless horses, and one of them — though Marta could not have known it, could not have guessed it — was already being bargained for.

When she rose to go down the hill to the last night of her old life, Hazel caught her sleeve.

"One thing," Hazel said, low and fast. "Tuesday. At the station. Whatever happens, whatever they shout — stay near me if you can. Stay where I can see you."

"Why?"

Hazel looked past her, down at the darkening town, at the stenciled shutters and the queued lamplight and the clock tower striking seven over all of it, merciless and beautiful.

"Because," she said, "the schedule changed today. Brandt moved the departure up. The train doesn't leave Tuesday anymore, Marta."

The clock struck the quarter hour beneath them, soft and enormous, counting down something Marta could no longer see the face of.

"It leaves tomorrow at dawn."
