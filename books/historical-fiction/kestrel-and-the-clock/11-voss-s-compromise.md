## 11. Voss's Compromise

Director Aldous Voss had kept the observatory for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one rule more valuable than any star chart: a man survives by knowing exactly how much he is willing to lose.

He stood at his study window in the hour before dawn, when the harbor fog came up the hill like something breathing, and he did the arithmetic he had been avoiding for six days. In the cellar beneath his own floor, forty feet below where he now poured his coffee with a hand that did not tremble only because he ordered it not to, an old man slept on a cot among the survey records. Elias Marchetti. A name struck from the civic ledger. A name which, if discovered in the cellar of the observatory, would be struck next from its owner.

And then there was the matter of Voss himself.

He drank the coffee. It had gone bitter. Everything had gone bitter lately; even the milk the dairies still delivered tasted faintly of fear, though he suspected that was his imagination assigning flavor to chemistry. He had always trusted numbers over impressions. Numbers did not flatter and did not lie. So he made himself count, the way he counted eclipse seconds.

One: the requisition order had exempted the observatory as a scientific installation under military administration, its personnel subject to review but not removal. *Subject to review.* Three words that meant nothing until they meant everything.

Two: Officer Brandt visited daily at 0900 precisely because the clock was precise, and precision men loved instruments they could trust. Trust could be spent like currency, but only once.

Three: there was an old man in his cellar whose existence, if reported, might purchase Voss's own good standing — and if concealed, might cost him everything he had spent a life assembling: the observatory, the archives, the reputation, the small immortality of having kept accurate time through two wars and one fire.

He set down the cup and heard, from below, the faint creak of the cot. The old man was awake. Old men woke early; it was one of the few mercies of age, that you got your suffering over with before breakfast.

Voss took the lamp from its hook and went down.

* * *

The cellar ran beneath the eastern wing, low-ceilinged and dry, built originally to house the meridian instruments' foundations — great blocks of stone sunk into bedrock so that the telescopes above would never shift. The survey cabinets lined one wall, full of triangulation notes going back seventy years, and behind them, in the gap Voss had measured himself and pronounced sufficient, was the cot, the washbasin, the stack of books Hazel carried down one at a time in her tool bag, and the old man sitting up straight against the pillows as if rising to greet a customer.

"You look tired, Director," Marchetti said. His accent had softened over the years but had never gone away entirely; it lived in the vowels like a grain living in wood. "That is bad. A man who keeps time should sleep by the clock."

"A man who hides in cellars should lecture less." Voss set the lamp on an upturned crate. The light swung and settled, throwing shadows across the stone. "How is the ankle?"

"Healing. Your apprentice binds it too tightly. She has her mother's hands, or someone's hands — hands that think force will fix what patience would."

"Her mother I never knew. Her father I knew slightly, and he was exactly as you describe."

Marchetti smiled, and for a moment the cellar smelled, or seemed to smell, of the old workshop on Ferrant Street: oil of cloves, brass filings, the particular sweetness of shellac warmed between palms. Thirty years ago Voss had been a young astronomer with a broken chronometer and no money, and Marchetti had fixed it for nothing and refused payment twice and finally accepted a dinner, and they had argued about longitude until the candles drowned. That was the whole of their friendship. It had been enough, in peacetime, to build a life's worth of loyalty on. Whether it was enough now, in this season of ledgers and lists, Voss did not know, and the not-knowing sat in him like a swallowed stone.

"I have to ask you something," Voss said. "I will ask it plainly, because you are too old to be spared and I am too tired to be delicate."

"Plainly, then."

"If they find you here, they will take me too. You understand that. The order says concealment of declared persons is itself a declaration. They will not distinguish between the hidden and the hider."

Marchetti looked at him. The lamplight found the lines around his eyes, deep as gear teeth. "I understand it completely."

"And yet you stay."

"And yet I stay, because your apprentice asked me to, and because the roads are watched, and because—" he paused, and Voss watched him choose honesty over comfort, "—because I am tired, Aldous, and a tired man's legs believe in walls more strongly than they should."

Voss nodded slowly. He had expected as much, and he had come down the stairs with the question already shaped, and the shape of the question had been this: *I can get you to the fishing boats. There is a man who owes me. Two nights from now, perhaps three. After that I cannot help you.* He had rehearsed it on the stairs. And now he sat down on the crate beside the lamp and found he could not say it, because saying it was easy and meaning it was another thing, and he discovered, examining himself with the same cold instrument he applied to pendulums, that he did not mean it.

Or rather: he meant part of it. He wanted the old man gone. He wanted the cellar empty and the risk dissolved and his ledger — his internal ledger, the one no clerk would ever strike names from — balanced. But wanting a thing and being able to purchase it were different matters, and the price of this purchase was a betrayal he could feel forming in his chest like weather.

"There is a boat," he said instead, and stopped, and started again. "There may be a boat. I will make inquiries. Not tonight. Tonight I need you to promise me you will not go near the upper floors, not after dark, not ever. Brandt's men walk the perimeter at irregular hours now. Irregular hours — do you appreciate the irony? They have taken my clock and they patrol it without regularity. It offends me professionally."

Marchetti laughed, low, careful of the sound. "It offends me too. She has it keeping better than it has kept in twenty years. Did you know she re-pins the pallets herself? I taught her the method but she has improved it. She always improved everything I gave her. That is what teaching is for — you hand a person a flame and they burn brighter than your candle ever did."

"Yes." Voss rose. His knees complained; he ignored them, as he ignored most things, on principle. "I will bring bread at seven. Do not open the door yourself. Knock twice if you need anything, once if it is urgent, and if soldiers are in the building—" he hesitated, "—knock nothing. Be stone."

"Be stone," Marchetti repeated softly. "I have practiced."

* * *

Upstairs, the morning assembled itself out of fog and obligation. Voss climbed to the transit room, where the master clock's cables ran up through the floors like tendons, and he stood a while with his hand flat against the stone wall, feeling the tick come through the rock — a pulse in the building's wrist. He had felt it every working day for thirty-one years and had long since stopped hearing it, the way one stops hearing one's own heart, but these days he listened deliberately, needing the reminder that some things still ran as designed.

At nine o'clock precisely, because the clock said so and Brandt believed the clock, the officer's staff car crunched up the gravel drive.

Brandt came in wiping his boots with unnecessary thoroughness, a habit Voss had decided was performance — the conqueror demonstrating respect for the conquered's cleanliness, which was a form of insult dressed as courtesy. He was younger than Voss had first thought, seen in daylight without his coat. Younger, and fond of the clock in a way that unsettled Voss more than contempt would have. Contempt he could predict.

"Director." Brandt consulted his pocket watch, an ugly service-issue thing. "Your instrument lost four-tenths of a second against mine overnight."

"Then your watch gained four-tenths," Voss said. "Ours does not lose."

Something flickered in the officer's face — amusement, possibly, or the memory of amusement. "Miss Almeida tells me the escapement needed adjustment. She works late."

"She works when the work requires. Clocks do not keep office hours."

"No." Brandt turned to the window, looking down the hill at the town, the gray roofs, the smoke standing up straight in the still air. "They keep ours." He said it lightly, almost idly, the way a man sets a coin spinning on a table to see who watches it. "There will be a second transport Thursday. The schedule will come. The clock must be exact, Director. My superiors are very interested in exactness."

"The clock," Voss said, "has never been anything but exact."

It was true, and it was the worst thing he had said in weeks, and he knew it as he said it, and he said it anyway, because the alternative was to say something else, and saying something else had consequences, and he was fifty-eight years old with weak lungs and a building full of irreplaceable instruments and a cellar with an old man in it, and so the sentence came out of him smooth and correct and complicit, and he watched it land and do its small work, and Brandt nodded, satisfied, and left the way he came, wiping his boots.

Voss stood alone in the transit room for a long moment. Below him, faint through the floor, the tick went on, indifferent and faithful, keeping perfect time for imperfect purposes.

*So that is what I am now,* he thought. *A man who winds the machine.*

* * *

He found Hazel in the clock room that afternoon, lying on the wooden platform with her head half inside the case, a jeweler's loupe screwed into one eye, her sleeves rolled past her elbows. She did not hear him at first. When she emerged, blinking, and saw him standing there, something crossed her face — quickly, but not quickly enough. He had raised two daughters; he knew the anatomy of guilt.

"Director."

"Miss Almeida."

She wiped her hands on her rag with elaborate calm. "The third wheel wanted truing. The barrel's running hot on the great side. I've adjusted the oil."

"Mm." He came closer, and lowered his voice to almost nothing, pitched beneath the tick. "How many nights have you worked in here after midnight?"

"Enough to fix it properly."

"How many nights, Hazel?"

She met his eyes. That was the thing about her — the steadiness of her, the strange gravity she carried in a slight frame. She had come to him at fourteen asking for work, and he had hired her mostly to annoy Marchetti, who had claimed she was too young, and she had proven them both wrong with the patient ferocity of the truly called.

"Every night since the ledger," she said.

The words hung in the clock room's warm air. Through the tall window the sea lay flat and pewter-colored, and somewhere down the hill a truck changed gears.

"Do you know," Voss said, and then stopped, because he had prepared several speeches and none of them fit anymore. He sat down on the platform's edge, which no director had done in living memory, and looked up at the great dial's inner face, the reverse of the town's official time, all counterweights and wire. "When I was a young man at Kiel, there was a purge. Before your birth. Professors, mostly. My thesis advisor was on a list — his opinions, his parentage, both, I never learned which mattered. A colleague came to me and asked me to sign a statement about the man's character. A true statement, as it happens. He was a decent man and a fine mathematician. All I had to do was sign my name under the truth."

"What did you do?"

"I signed it. And I sent it. And it made no difference whatsoever, except to me. It made a difference to me for the rest of my life, because it taught me that I was the kind of man who signed." He rubbed his thumb along the seam of his trousers. "You would think a man learns from such a lesson and becomes braver. Mostly we learn our exact weight. Nothing more."

Hazel said nothing. She sat down beside him, an arm's length away, and together they regarded the machinery.

"Here is my compromise," Voss said at last, quietly, formally, as if dictating minutes. "The man in my cellar stays. I will not report him. I will feed him, and I will forge whatever logs require forging, and I will hate every minute of it, and when this is over — if I am alive to be judged — I will account for it however I must. That is what I am willing to lose. Understand the boundary of it. What I am *not* willing to lose is this observatory and everyone under its roof, which includes you. So the engraving stops."

Her head turned. "You know about—"

"I have known since Tuesday. I can read tool marks the way you read a dial. And I am telling you, as the man responsible for this station and for your stubborn hide: it stops. No more plates. No more names. You have done what conscience required and more than prudence permits, and if Brandt finds fresh marks inside that barrel, he will not interrogate the clock. He will interrogate the clockmaker, and she is nineteen years old and I have watched her burn her fingers rather than admit a fault she couldn't fix."

"That was once."

"It was illustrative." He stood, knees cracking, and looked down at her with all the sternness he owned. "Promise me."

For a long moment she was silent, and he watched her weigh it — he could see the scales moving behind her eyes, the honest girl's difficulty with dishonesty, even the necessary kind. He understood then, with a lurch, that she might refuse him. That the apprentice he thought he knew had become, in the space of a season, someone whose promises cost her something real, and who paid her debts regardless.

"I promise," she said finally, "that I will not engrave any new names *this week.*"

"That is not—"

"It is what I have. Director—" she stood too, facing him, and her voice dropped to the register she used for hard truths, "—you signed a true statement once and it saved no one. I am signing a false one every day. We are both bargaining. The only difference is that you know what your silence buys, and I know what mine costs, and neither of us can afford to stop the other."

The tick filled the room, patient, enormous. Out on the gallery he heard the sentry shift his boots.

"Thursday," Voss said at last, heavily. "Brandt says there is a transport Thursday. The schedule comes tomorrow."

He saw the color leave her face and return, forced back by will, and he saw her hands close, once, at her sides — the gesture of a woman picking up a tool she hopes never to use again.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "the clock will need servicing. It always needs servicing. And I will be here, every hour, keeping it exact — since exactness is what everyone requires of me."

She turned back to the movement before he could answer, and he left her there, small and steady in the lamplight, and climbed the stairs to his study to write the maintenance log he would later have to forge, and it was only halfway up that he remembered the detail he had noticed and filed and not yet spoken aloud: the fresh scratches inside the barrel were not in Hazel's hand alone. Someone had helped her. Someone with an old man's tremor, steadied by an old craftsman's discipline, cutting letters into brass in the dark of a locked room.

Which meant the man in his cellar was climbing to the clock room at night, past the sentries, past the sentry schedules Voss himself was responsible for — and the second schedule was due tomorrow, and Brandt had begun, Voss had seen it in the set of his shoulders, to count more than seconds.
