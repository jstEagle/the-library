## 12. Inspection

The letter came on a Tuesday, and Tuesdays were winding days.

Hazel read it standing in the workshop doorway with the morning cold coming off the harbor in gray sheets. *By order of the Military Administration: Senior Inspector Halder will conduct a full audit of the observatory's timekeeping apparatus, including the master regulator, on Thursday at nine o'clock precisely.* The word *including* sat on the page like a splinter. She read it four times. It did not soften.

*Including.*

She thought of the barrel that night without meaning to—lying awake under the eaves of Voss's spare room, staring at the ceiling where moonlight laid the window frame in pale bars. Somewhere below her, two floors down and behind a door kept locked with Voss's own key, the master clock swung its pendulum through the dark, and inside its brass heart lay forty-one names. Forty-one people struck from the ledger like errors in arithmetic. Marchetti among them. She had cut each name herself into plates no thicker than a thumbnail, seated them between the gearing where only a hand that knew what it was looking for would ever find them—and now someone was coming whose whole profession was looking.

*Including the barrel.*

They would not say it so plainly, of course. Inspectors did not announce intentions. But she knew how audits went; Elias had told her once, years ago, in another country's context, his voice flat with old knowledge: *First they count everything. Then they open everything. Then they take what they want.* And what could be taken here? Names. Only names. Small things, weightless things—but Hazel had learned by now that the smallest things in a clock were the ones that stopped it dead.

She slept perhaps three hours, and in the morning her hands were steady anyway. Hands always knew before the rest of her did.

* * *

Wednesday was preparation, and preparation was its own kind of confession. Everything she touched told her what she'd done.

She cleaned the movement until it gleamed, which was normal. She checked the oil in every jewel, trued the escapement, adjusted the pendulum's rating nut by a quarter-turn to bring the rate back to zero—all the ordinary liturgy of maintenance. But then she stood before the open case with the lamplight pooling yellow around her and considered the barrel itself, that great brass cylinder as long as her forearm, and asked it silently: *Can I hide you better?*

She could not disassemble what she'd built. That was the trap of it. The names were engraved on plates fitted into recesses she had cut with gravers and patience over eleven nights, filed flush, polished to match the surrounding metal, arranged so that the tool marks fell along the same axes as the maker's original work. To move them now would be to disturb fresh metal beside old. Any inspector worth his calipers would see the difference the way Hazel could see a lie in a stranger's handwriting.

So instead she did what she could. She rubbed beeswax and whiting into the seams. She let dust accumulate deliberately—a week's worth of it, gray and honest-looking—over the plates' locations, the way dust gathers over anything untouched for years. Dust was testimony. Dust said *no one has been here*. She felt absurd, kneeling in the case like a penitent, manufacturing age, but she did it thoroughly, because thoroughness was the only prayer she owned.

Voss watched her from the doorway that evening, his keys silent in his hand.

"Halder is not Brandt," he said. "Brandt is a believer. Halder is a bureaucrat. Bureaucrats can be bored."

"Bored men open clocks out of curiosity," Hazel said. "Believers only open doors."

He had no answer to that. He looked tired in a way she had not seen before—the tiredness of a man who has begun keeping two ledgers in his head and knows one of them will hang him. "Nine o'clock," he said finally. "I'll receive him in my office first. That buys you twenty minutes. Whatever you need to do, do it in those twenty minutes."

"And after?"

"After," Voss said, "we are simply clockmakers being audited." He said it like a man trying the sentence on for size, checking whether it still fit.

* * *

Thursday came up cold and clear, the harbor bright enough to hurt. At five minutes to nine Hazel stood in the clock room with her tools laid out in their rolls, each instrument in its appointed pocket, because an orderly bench was the first thing an inspector read about a craftsman. Her notebook—the small one, the innocent one with gear ratios and oiling schedules filling most of its pages—sat squared to the bench edge.

At nine exactly the town bells rang, driven by the very clock they'd come to audit, and Hazel found a fierce, private comfort in that. Whatever else happened today, her rates were true. Let him measure. Let him measure all he liked. The time itself would defend her.

Halder arrived at four minutes past, which told her something already: a man who kept others to precision while drifting loose of it himself. He was sixty maybe, silver-haired, with the soft hands and rimless spectacles of a desk soldier, and he moved through Voss's office doorway the way water moves—politely, inevitably, filling every corner. Behind him came Brandt, uninvited and unannounced, and Hazel's stomach dropped a half-inch at the sight of him. Brandt stood near the door throughout the introductions and said almost nothing, and his saying nothing was louder than speech.

"Fraulein Almeida." Halder shook her hand. His grip was dry and brief. "You maintain the regulator?"

"I service it daily and wind it weekly, yes sir."

"Daily." He removed his spectacles, polished them on a handkerchief that appeared from nowhere, replaced them. "And in your servicing, you would naturally have occasion to open the various cases. The barrel housing as well."

There it was. Not even ten minutes in, and there it was, laid on the bench between them like a coin.

"The barrel requires attention twice yearly," she said, and heard her own voice hold level, which seemed a miracle. "It was serviced in spring. Before the requisition."

"Then we'll want to see the state of it." He smiled—the smile of a man who bore her no malice whatsoever, who would sign whatever paper needed signing with the same mild face. "Accuracy is a matter of national interest now, Fraulein. A clock that keeps the trains running is a clock worth understanding completely."

*Completely.* The word went through her like a probe through a wound.

They began at the top, and God help her, they began slowly. Halder was thorough in the way of men with unlimited paper: he examined the suspension, weighed the pendulum bob in his hand, asked the date of every repair and wanted it cross-checked against Voss's logs, which Voss produced with the calm of a man who had spent Wednesday night aging several pages with tea and a warm oven. Hazel answered questions. Her voice worked. Her hands, when he asked to see her technique, worked better—she demonstrated the oiling of the escape pivots with such economy of motion that Halder actually nodded, and something in her raged at the nodding. *I am good at this*, the rage said. *I am good at this and you are going to use my goodness to erase forty-one people, and I will stand here and let you admire my hands.*

Brandt, by the door, watched her the way a man watches weather.

* * *

It was past eleven when they came to the barrel.

Hazel had known the schedule of the audit the way one knows the schedule of a storm—there, on the horizon, advancing—and still, when Halder set down his magnifier beside the open case and said, mildly, "Now the barrel, I think," the floor of the world tilted.

"We'll need to let it run down first," she said. "The mainspring is under load. Opening the housing live is—" *dangerous*, she meant to say, but Halder was already shaking his head.

"There's a maintaining power," he said. "On a regulator of this class there's always a maintaining power." Of course there was. There was, because she herself had rebuilt it, because Elias had taught her its principle when she was sixteen, because the universe apparently intended her to be defeated by her own competence. "We'll stop the train carefully, ease the spring onto the maintenance ratchet, and open it properly. No shortcuts." He smiled again. "I was an engineer before I was anything else, Fraulein Almeida. You may assume I know the machinery."

So there was nothing for it. She stopped the clock—her clock, the town's clock, the thing whose heartbeat she had listened to every night for months—and the silence that filled the room was the loudest sound she had ever heard. Somewhere outside, gulls. Somewhere below, boots on the quay. The second hand lay still against its dial like a dead insect.

Halder released the barrel housing himself. His hands were careful; she granted him that. He lifted the great drum free of its bearings and set it on the padded cradle she used for exactly this work, and then he bent over it with his magnifier, turning it slowly under the lamp, reading the metal the way she read handwriting.

Hazel stood three feet away with a burnishing tool in her hand and understood, with terrible clarity, that she was about to watch either nothing or everything. The dust was right. The wax was right. The plates matched the original alloy—she had melted down a broken weight to cast them, the same brass, the same grain. But tool marks under magnification were signatures, and signatures could be read, and if Halder read hers—

He turned the barrel a quarter-rotation. Stopped. Turned it back.

"Interesting," he said.

The word landed in the room and sat there.

"Sir?" Hazel's voice came out half a tone too high, and she hated herself for it, hated the small betrayal of her throat.

"This reaming near the arbor hole." He angled the drum toward the light. "Later than the rest of the work. Post-dating the maker, certainly." He looked up at her over the rims of his spectacles, and his eyes were not unkind, merely exact. "Your repair?"

Her repair. He had handed her the exit himself and did not know it, and later—much later, in the long retellings of her own life—she would understand that this was the moment the story could have ended, if she had simply said yes. Yes, sir, a bearing repair in spring, routine. He would have nodded, logged it, moved on. The names would have slept on undisturbed beneath his approval, and the day would have closed over the moment like water.

Instead she saw Brandt, by the door, watching her answer. Watching with the particular patience of a man who had noticed extra tool marks weeks ago and had come today, quietly, without announcement, to watch what she did when the metal was opened. Brandt's eyes held no accusation. They held invitation. *Lie to us*, the eyes said. *Please. Show me what you are.*

And some cold, clear part of Hazel—some part she had not known she possessed until that instant, some inheritance from every exile who had ever hidden a name inside a machine—understood that a smooth truth was more dangerous than a loud lie. Because Brandt suspected, and a suspect satisfied is a suspect confirmed in his suspicion's shape. If she gave them nothing today, Brandt would come back. He would always come back. But if she gave them an emergency—if she gave them fire and panic and a fault so urgent it explained every mark on that barrel—

The thought assembled itself in less than a heartbeat, and her body was already moving before the last piece of it clicked home.

"That's not reaming," she said, and let alarm break across her voice like a dropped glass. "Sir, that's scoring. That's a dry pivot. If the maintaining ratchet slipped during the transfer—" and she reached for the drum as if to check the ratchet herself, reaching also, with her left hand, for the soldering iron she had set warming on the spirit lamp an hour ago and forgotten, visibly, carelessly, in plain sight of both men, because a girl good with her hands forgets things when inspectors crowd her bench—

Her bare palm closed on the iron's shaft just below the handle.

The pain arrived a full second after contact, a white, astonishing bloom, and what she felt beyond it—God forgive her, God forgive the person she had become—was relief. She cried out, short and genuine because no acting was required, and wrenched her hand back, and the soldering iron clattered against the bench and the smell of scorched skin rose sharp and ugly in the lamplight.

"Almeida!" Voss was across the room in three strides.

"My fault—clumsy—" She was fumbling for the cold-water jug, wrapping her palm in the wet cloth Voss thrust at her, gasping, and through the curtain of her own hair she watched Halder do exactly what she had gambled he would do: abandon the barrel entirely. He stepped back from the bench, spectacles flashing, hands raised slightly, a bureaucrat confronted with blood and chaos in a room he did not command.

"The ratchet," Hazel pressed on, voice shaking now with pain instead of fear, which was indistinguishable, which was the entire point. "If it slipped, the spring is bearing unevenly—that scoring means the arbor's been cutting dry for days—I should never have opened the train this morning, I should have caught it—" She was talking too much, the way injured people do, and she let herself talk, let the words pour out technical and frantic, dry pivot and load distribution and *the whole train could lock*, until the barrel on its cradle became, in the minds of both officers, not a mystery but a problem. A mechanical problem. Urgent, mundane, and entirely hers.

"Can it run?" Halder asked, and his mildness had acquired an edge, because a stopped master clock was a reportable event, and reports traveled upward to men less mild than he.

"With the housing closed, tonight, yes. But the drum has to come down to my bench. Properly. Days of work." She held up her bandaged palm, and the gesture was honest and calculated in equal measure, and she despised and needed both halves of it. "Unless you'd prefer to log it as a failure, sir. In writing. A regulator offline during—" she let the pause carry it— "current scheduling requirements."

Silence. Halder looked at the barrel. Looked at her hand. Looked at Brandt, and something passed between the two officers that Hazel could not read and did not try to.

"Days of work," Halder said at last. "Under guard, I'm afraid. Officer Brandt will arrange supervision." He gathered his papers with small precise movements. "A pity about your hand. Accuracy suffers when accuracy's keeper is injured." He paused at the door, and turned back, and for one suspended moment his gaze went past her shoulder to the drum sitting on its cradle, and Hazel felt her pulse knock once, hard, against her bandage.

"But you'll want to examine that scoring yourself again, Fraulein," he said. "Before you cut anything new. Metal remembers its history, in my experience. So do I."

Then he was gone, and Brandt went after him, and the door closed, and the clock room was quiet except for the gulls and the blood moving in Hazel's ears.

Voss took her elbow gently. "Let me look at that."

"It needs butter and a clean cloth," she said. "My grandmother's remedy." And then, because the adrenaline was draining out of her and leaving something raw underneath, she laughed—one breathless, terrible laugh—and pressed the wet cloth harder against her burned palm and looked at the barrel lying exposed on its cradle with its forty-one sleeping names.

"He almost read them," she whispered.

"Yes," Voss said.

"He handed me the way out. He asked if it was my repair. All I had to do was say yes."

"Yes," Voss said again, softly. "And instead you burned yourself." He was quiet a moment, and when she looked up at him his face held something she had never seen there before—not the weary compromise she had grown used to, but a kind of startled respect, as though he were meeting, years late, the person who had been apprenticed in his town all along. "That was very well lied, Fraulein Almeida."

"Days of work," she echoed. "Under guard." She flexed her bandaged fingers, wincing, already calculating. "He's given me the perfect excuse, Voss. Every night now, I'll be at this bench with a soldier watching me—and a legitimate reason to have the barrel apart in front of him."

"To do what?" Voss asked. But from her face, or from the clock, or from whatever long arithmetic he had been running since autumn, he answered himself before she could speak. "No," he said. "No, Hazel. How many more names could there possibly be?"

Outside, down on the quay, the trucks were idling. Through the tall window Hazel could see the new arrivals being counted—counted first, Elias had said, then opened, then taken—and she looked at her blistered palm and then at the still, waiting dial of the master clock, and heard herself say, in a voice steadier than any she had used all day:

"As many as arrive."
