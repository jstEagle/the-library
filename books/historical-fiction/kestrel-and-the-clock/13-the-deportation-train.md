## 13. The Deportation Train

The morning of the deportation, Hazel was woken at four by the clock itself.

She slept now in the narrow room off the pendulum chamber that had once belonged to the observatory's assistant—a cot, a washstand, a window too high to see out of—and she had learned, in the weeks since Brandt's schedule took hold, to read the master clock through the wall the way other people read weather. Its tick came to her in the dark as a pulse beneath her own ribs, and she woke when it faltered, or when it did not falter, or when it simply reminded her that it was there, that it was keeping time for purposes she could no longer pretend were innocent. That morning it was not the sound but the absence of sound that pulled her up: the pendulum's beat continuing, steady, indifferent, while everything else in the town held its breath.

She dressed without lighting the lamp. Her fingers knew the buttons and laces; they had been trained by five years of working blind inside watch cases, feeling for the shape of a fault. She thought, absurdly, of her mother telling her that a girl who could dress in the dark would never be late for anything worth being early to, and then she thought of nothing at all, because thinking was a luxury the day would not permit.

The gallery stairs were stone and cold through her boot soles. At the top, the observatory dome stood shut and dark against a sky just beginning to bruise grey over the harbor, and the long window on the eastern side—the one Voss had shown her on her first morning, when he'd said *from here you can see the whole town tell itself the time*—looked down across the tiled roofs, the market square with its stripped awnings, the rail spur that ran from the goods yard along the sea wall and out toward the junction where the line met the main track inland.

The train was already there.

It had come in during the night. Hazel had heard it—of course she had heard it, they had all heard it, the low complaint of an engine idling where no engine had any business idling—but hearing it in sleep was different from seeing it. In sleep it had been a dream-thing, deniable. In the flat early light it was forty wagons and three carriages standing on the spur like something washed up and left to die, and beside it, small as insects even from this distance, soldiers moved with clipboards.

She pressed her forehead to the cold glass and made herself look.

* * *

Brandt had given her the timetable himself, three days before, laid on the workbench with a fingernail tapping the printed column. *Seven-forty*, he had said. *Departure at seven-forty precisely. The General Staff wants schedules honored, Fräulein Almeida. Your clock has been a revelation. I confess I did not expect such precision from a provincial workshop.* He had smiled then, the thin administrative smile that was worse than a frown, and added: *See that it does not disappoint.*

So she had spent three days making certain it would not disappoint. That was the sentence she kept turning over while the sky lightened and the town below began, street by street, to empty itself toward the goods yard. Not the cruelty of it—that she had stopped expecting to escape weeks ago—but the craftsmanship. They had taken her repair, her careful work, the hours she had spent truing the escapement and adjusting the rate until the master clock agreed with the transit observations to within a half-second, and they had written it into their orders as though precision were a virtue that belonged to whoever held the instrument. Seven-forty. The clock would strike the hour and then count the forty minutes, and the train would move, and somewhere in a filing cabinet in a city she would never see, a clerk would note that the operation had proceeded according to schedule.

Her hands, she noticed, were perfectly steady. She found this obscene. She wanted to be shaking; shaking would have meant something. But her hands had been trained past trembling, trained to hold a graver against brass while the whole weight of her attention narrowed to a line a tenth of a millimetre wide, and so they hung at her sides in the cold gallery like tools set down overnight, ready for whatever the day required of them.

Below, the square had begun to fill. Even at this distance and in this light she could pick out the shapes of the procession—not a march, nothing so orderly; a drift, the slow reluctant confluence of families carrying too much, because the notices had said one suitcase per person and every family in the town had looked at one suitcase and understood that they were being asked to choose which parts of their lives would be permitted to exist afterward. A woman in a green shawl carried a birdcage with the cloth tied over it. An old man had his good coat on over his nightshirt because he had dressed in the dark too, and forgotten, or not bothered, or wanted to meet whatever was coming as a respectable man.

Hazel counted without wanting to. It was a clockmaker's sickness, counting. Two hundred and forty, perhaps two hundred and sixty, moving down the hill road in a straggle that the soldiers kept correcting with flat hands, herding them straighter, and behind them the town stood open and silent, doors ajar, curtains not quite closed, the windows of the bakery on the corner dark because Marta's father had been ordered to report with the others and no one had lit his ovens.

Marta. The name arrived in Hazel's chest like a gear dropping into the wrong wheel, jamming everything.

* * *

She had gone to the bakery on Tuesday. She should write that she had gone with a plan, but the truth was smaller and worse: she had gone with ten words of comfort rehearsed on the walk over and no idea what to do after them. Marta had been in the doorway in her flour apron, seventeen years old, chapped hands, the particular stubborn chin she had inherited from her mother along with the recipe for rye bread, and when she saw Hazel coming she had not cried, which somehow made it unbearable.

"They struck my name," Marta said, before Hazel reached the step. "Papa went to the clerk's office and the man wouldn't even find the page. He said there was no Baker-Reyes family registered. As if we hadn't paid rates here since my grandfather." She wiped her hands on the apron, the gesture of a woman twenty years older. "You've seen the ledger."

It was not a question. Word traveled strangely in an occupied town—it traveled through silences, through who was seen where at what hour—and Marta had evidently assembled enough of the truth about Hazel's daily presence in the observatory to stand in her own doorway and ask for confirmation of her own erasure.

"I've seen it," Hazel said.

"Can you—" Marta stopped. Started again, quieter. "Elias Marchetti says you can put names where they won't be found. He says the old clockmakers did it before anyone living remembers."

Hazel had felt the ground tilt under her, very slightly, the way the observatory floor seemed to move sometimes when the big pendulum swung through one of its long arcs. Elias. So Elias had been talking—not to the town, he was too careful for that, but to someone, in the cellar, in the margins, in the language of the trade that outsiders heard as shop-talk. And Marta had listened, and understood, and asked.

"Yes," Hazel said. "I can keep your name. I will keep your name. Marta Reyes, daughter of Tomás and—"

"Lidia," Marta said. "Lidia Ferro. Put her too. She died in February but they struck her after, like they were catching up."

"I'll put them both."

And Marta had nodded, and looked past Hazel down the empty street, and said the thing that Hazel had been carrying ever since like a stone swallowed and never digested: "That's not what I asked, though. Is it. You know that's not what I asked."

* * *

From the gallery window, the column of people reached the goods yard. The light had come up enough now to give everything its color back, which was the cruelest thing the morning could have done, because colors meant particulars: the green shawl, the red kerchief of a girl hauling a suitcase bigger than herself, the dull blue of the army greatcoats threading between them. Hazel could hear, faintly, the sounds rising off the yard—the shunting of couplings, a whistle, a dog, a child's voice lifted in some complaint that its mother was too far away and too tired to answer—and underneath all of it, through the floor, through the stone, through her boots and her bones, the master clock ticking toward seven-forty.

She went down to the pendulum chamber. She does not remember deciding to; her feet simply took the stairs, because if the clock was going to do this thing then she was going to be next to it, the way you sit with a dying animal, the way you stand witness. The great bob hung in its cage of brass rods, swinging its eleven-second arc, and above it the weight lines ran up into darkness, and around it the whole case breathed its familiar smell of oil and old wood and dust warmed by friction. She put her hand flat on the case. She had done this every morning since she arrived, a habit she had thought of as greeting. This morning it felt like complicity.

*You built this,* she told herself. *Not the army. Not Brandt. Voss's grandfather commissioned it and three generations of clockmakers kept it and you trued its escapement with your own hands, and none of them, not one of them, imagined it would be used for this.*

But instruments don't carry intentions. That was the first law of her trade. A movement is neither kind nor cruel; it does only what it is made to do, exactly, forever, until someone changes it. She had loved that about clocks once—their innocence, their perfect moral emptiness. Now she understood it was the most frightening thing about them.

The minute hand, seen through the aperture in the case door, stood at seven-twelve.

She went back up to the gallery. She could not say why; only that watching felt like a duty she had assigned herself, and that looking away would be the first small abdication, and abdications, she had learned, compounded like interest.

* * *

The boarding began at seven-twenty.

She watched it the way she had once watched Voss demonstrate the transit telescope: tracking a fixed point, noting deviations, refusing to let her gaze slide off. Families funneling toward the carriage doors. Soldiers checking papers against lists. A soldier lifting the old man in the nightshirt bodily onto the step, gently, horribly gently, the way you handle something that might break and that you have nevertheless decided to discard. The woman in the green shawl surrendering the birdcage to a sergeant who set it aside on the platform edge and wrote something on his clipboard, and Hazel understood with a physical pain in her throat that the cage would still be sitting there tonight, cloth still tied, and that the sergeant's clipboard did not have a column for it.

And then, near the third wagon, a figure in a coat too heavy for the season, dark hair coming loose from its pins, turning once—only once—to look back up the hill at the town, at the roofs, at the dark windows of the bakery on the corner, and Hazel's whole body knew it before her eyes confirmed it.

Marta.

Even at this distance, even through glass, the recognition was total. It wasn't the coat or the hair; it was the way she stood, chin up, stubborn, furious, seventeen, refusing to be hurried. Hazel's hand came up and pressed against the pane as if she could reach through the air and the distance and the whole machinery of the empire and close her fingers around that raised chin and turn it, physically turn it, away from the train.

*I can keep your name.* That was what she had promised. Standing in the bakery doorway with flour dust hanging in the light between them, she had believed—she had actually, shamefully believed—that the promise was a gift. That memory was a form of rescue. That engraving *Marta Reyes* and *Lidia Ferro* into hidden brass was the same act, in a slower medium, as pulling a girl back from a wagon door.

Now she watched Marta climb the step alone, unassisted, declining the soldier's hand with a jerk of her shoulder that Hazel could read at four hundred metres because she knew those shoulders, had watched them kneading dough through that bakery window a hundred mornings of her childhood, had eaten the Saturday loaves warm. The wagon door rolled. The sound reached the observatory a half-beat later, iron on iron, flat and final.

There are moments that a person survives by dividing. Hazel felt it happen in herself: the part of her that stood at the window, and the part of her somewhere slightly above and behind, taking notes, cold as a clerk. *Seven-thirty-one. Third wagon, rear section. She looked back once. The birdcage remains on the platform.* The second Hazel recorded everything with terrible fidelity, and the first Hazel understood that this recording was all she had, that it was the entire content of her promise now, that remembering was not the same as saving and never had been, and that she had offered Marta the smaller gift and let herself believe it was the larger one.

*Remembering is not the same as saving.*

The thought did not release her from anything. That was the horror of it. If it had excused her, it would at least have been bearable. Instead it inverted: because the names were not rescue, they became obligation. If she could not save them, then the record was not a consolation—it was a debt, owed to every name, collectable from her specifically, personally, forever. She had thought she was choosing memory as the best available mercy. She understood now that she had been conscripted, by Marta's question in the doorway, by Elias's struck page, by every clerk's pen in every ledger room in the empire, into a service with no discharge.

The minutes crawled. Seven-thirty-five. Seven-thirty-seven.

At seven-thirty-eight, whistles. At seven-thirty-nine, the engine released a column of steam that stood up white against the pale sky like something trying to rise out of its own weight. Hazel's eyes dropped to the gallery's small subsidiary dial, synced to the master, and she watched the hand cross the printed future that Brandt had tapped with his fingernail, and at seven-forty precisely—because her clock did not disappoint, because she had trued the escapement herself, because precision was a virtue that belonged to whoever held the instrument—the train began to move.

It moved slowly. That was the detail that would come back to her for years, in the dark, at winding time: how slowly it moved, as if even the engine were reluctant, wheels turning with a sound like a great file drawn across iron, gathering speed by fractions along the sea wall, past the customs house, past the last houses, and the people in the town who had stayed behind stood in their doorways and along the walls and did not wave and did not call out, because what was there to wave at, and the whole town watched itself commit its neighbors to the horizon.

Hazel did not look away. She had decided that in advance, without knowing it, and she held to it now as the train shrank, as the steam thinned and tore, as the sound flattened into the east and the sea wind came up the hill and pushed it apart. She watched until there was nothing on the spur except the empty wagons at the far end and, on the platform edge, small and absurd and exact, the birdcage with the cloth still tied over it.

Then her legs went, all at once, and she sat down hard on the gallery floor with her back against the cold stone of the pier, and the master clock ticked on through the floor and up through her spine, and she put her face in her hands and shook without crying, because even her grief had been trained past its natural expression, and what came out instead was not sound but a fine continuous tremor, like a balance wheel running fast.

She did not know how long she sat there. Long enough that the sun cleared the roofline and laid a bar of light across the floorboards, long enough that the tremor slowed and stopped and left behind it something harder than before. When she finally lowered her hands, they were steady again. Steadier than they had ever been.

She thought: *I owe them all. Every name. And I am behind.*

She had nine names engraved and finished, matched to Elias's samples of period handwriting, invisible among the gearing. She had eleven more copied in her notebook, waiting. And the notebook itself—she saw it suddenly with new eyes, saw the flimsy paper and the pencilled columns and the way it lived in her coat pocket, on her body, one search away from confiscation, one bad day at a checkpoint from ash. Names in paper were not safe. Names in paper had never been safe. The ledger proved that; clerks could strike paper out of existence with a stroke. Only the brass was real, only the brass hid in plain sight inside the belly of the machine the empire trusted to run its schedules, and every day she delayed was a day the remaining eleven names existed in no place more durable than graphite.

She got up. She went down the stairs, past the pendulum, into the workshop, and took out the plates.

* * *

She worked through the morning with the door bolted and the maintenance log open beside her, filled out in advance to justify the hours: *barrel inspection, wear assessment, lubrication of winding train.* The lies came easily now and she hated that most of all—hated the fluency of her own deception, hated that Officer Brandt had, by his suspicion, taught her a skill she had never wanted, which was the ability to construct a false world out of true-looking details and inhabit it without visible strain.

The fourth plate was Lidia Ferro's. Hazel cut the letters small and deep, matching the slanted commercial hand of the 1880s engravings, and as the graver whispered through the brass she spoke the name aloud under her breath, once, the way you speak at a graveside. *Lidia Ferro.* Struck in February, erased in April, restored in May by a girl with a burin in an observatory workroom. It was not rescue. It was not enough. It was, she told her hands, the work in front of you, and the work in front of you is the only honest prayer you know.

A floorboard shifted in the passage outside.

She was on her feet with the plate closed in her fist before the knock came—two knuckles, unhurried, the particular rhythm of a man who has never in his life needed to hurry through a door. Hazel slid the plate into the drawer of trueing weights, squared the log on the bench, and opened the door with her face arranged into the mild professional blankness that was the other skill the occupation had given her.

Officer Brandt stood in the corridor with a soldier behind him, and he was not smiling.

"The departure proceeded according to schedule," he said. It was not congratulations; it was calibration. He looked past her into the workroom, at the bench, at the log, and his eyes rested on the drawer of trueing weights a half-second longer than the object deserved.

"Yes, sir," Hazel said. "Seven-forty. To the second."

"To the second," Brandt repeated softly, as if tasting the phrase. Then: "I'll want the movement inspected again Thursday. There are marks inside the barrel I haven't seen before. See that you can account for every one of them."

He turned and went, boots measured on the stone, and Hazel closed the door and stood with her back against it, the drawer at her hip full of brass and names, and understood that the cat-and-mouse she had been playing without admitting it had just acquired rules, a deadline, and teeth.
