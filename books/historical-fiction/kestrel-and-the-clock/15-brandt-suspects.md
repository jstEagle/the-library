## 15. Brandt Suspects

He found the marks on a Tuesday.

Hazel knew it before he said anything—knew it in the way the morning light came through the observatory's east windows, hard and colorless, the kind of light that leaves nothing hidden. She was at the bench with a pallet of pivots spread before her, pretending to count them, when Officer Brandt's boots stopped at the foot of the stair that led up into the clock tower. She had learned his tread by then. Most men walked like weather, arriving everywhere at once. Brandt walked like intention.

He climbed slowly. That was worse. A man who climbed slowly was a man who had already decided something and was merely arranging the furniture of his arrival.

"Herrin Almeida." He emerged into the turret room, cap under his arm, his breath fogging faintly in the cold up there. The master clock stood behind her like a cathedral, its pendulum swinging with the patience of geology. "You have been busy."

"I service the movement every week," she said. "It's in my log."

"Your log is very thorough." He did not smile. He never smiled, but there were mornings when his face arranged itself into something adjacent to pleasantness, and this was not one of them. He crossed to the clock's case and laid his palm flat against the oak as though taking its pulse. "I have been studying horology. Did you know that? In the evenings. There is little to do here, and a man should understand the instruments he is responsible for."

*Responsible for.* As if the clock were a soldier under his command. As if she were.

"Admirable," she said, and heard how flat the word came out.

He opened the lower panel—the one she oiled, the one that swung wide on its hinges a dozen times a month—and crouched before the great wheel train with a lantern he had apparently brought for the purpose. Of course he had brought a lantern. She watched the light move across the brass and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like leaving her body: *he has been down here without me. He has come at hours when no one winds the stairs, and he has looked.*

"There," Brandt said. "Third wheel bridge. There are scratches there that do not appear in your maintenance photographs."

The photographs. She had forgotten the photographs—the record-keeping obsession of the military mind, every component documented quarterly, catalogued, compared. She kept her face still by an act of will that left her jaw aching. The marks he meant were hers, of course. Three weeks of night work with the graver, cutting names into plates tucked behind the barrel where only a dismantler would ever find them, and she had grown careless near the third wheel. A slipped tool rest. Two bright scratches across metal that should have held forty years of unbroken patina.

"Grain of grit," she said. "Brass dust works into the oil. If you drag a bridge across a pivot with carborundum in the grease, you'll mark it. I mentioned this to Director Voss in September; it's in the older logs."

Brandt looked at her. He had pale eyes, nearly colorless, the gray of dishwater, and they rested on her face with the unhurried attention of a man reading a document he intended to file. Up in the works above them, the escapement ticked out the seconds that governed trains and schedules and the departure of people who would not return, and Hazel stood in the sound of it and let herself be looked at.

"Grit," he repeated.

"In the oil."

"Mm." He closed the panel gently—too gently, the way one closes a door on a room one means to search later—and stood. "I would like you to keep your work confined to scheduled hours. Daylight hours. There have been irregularities in the winding records."

"There have been power fluctuations in the town mains. Irregular timekeeping damages a movement faster than anything; I've had to make corrections—"

"Daylight hours," Brandt said again, mildly, and that mildness was the whole threat entire. He settled his cap back on his head. At the top of the stair he paused, one hand on the rail, and said, without turning: "You love this machine, I think. That is good. A technician who loves her instrument takes care of it." Then he was gone, his footsteps descending in even measure, and Hazel stood alone in the ticking cold and discovered that her hands were shaking so badly she had to grip the bench edge to still them.

She had lied to him about the grit. It had come out of her smooth and quick and plausible, assembled from true details like a watch assembled from wheels, and it had *worked*, or seemed to, and some small traitorous part of her had noted with professional satisfaction how well it had worked.

She hated that part of herself. She suspected she would be needing it again.

* * *

The soldier arrived the next morning.

He was young—younger than her, surely, a face still soft at the jaw, ears slightly prominent, hands too big for his rifle—and he appeared at the workshop door at eight o'clock precisely with a paper that assigned him, in the bloodless grammar of requisition orders, to "assist the civilian technician in the maintenance of official timing apparatus."

His name was Kessler. He said it once, stiffly, and after that he said almost nothing at all. He sat in the corner of the workshop on a stool he had carried in himself, and he watched her work.

That was the punishment, Hazel understood. Not arrest, not accusation—those required evidence, and evidence was what Brandt lacked. What he had instead was suspicion, and suspicion he would feed by installing a pair of eyes between her and the clock. Every graver stroke she wanted to cut into hidden brass now had a witness. Every visit below the turret room now happened under the flat gray gaze of a boy who took his meals at the garrison and wrote, she presumed, a report each evening in careful schoolboy script.

She worked on a marine chronometer that morning, and a church clock's escapement in the afternoon, ordinary work, honest work, and all the while Kessler watched, and she performed her own hands for him the way an actress performs a role—deliberate, transparent, every motion saying *nothing here, nothing concealed*. It was exhausting. By dusk her shoulders ached as if she had carried sacks of grain up the tower stairs.

"You're left-handed," Kessler said suddenly, as she banked the lamp.

Hazel's heart performed a single enormous beat and resumed. "Ambidextrous," she said. "My master trained both hands. Fine work goes quicker when you can approach a movement from either side." She held up her right hand and turned it in the lamplight, easy, amused. "Why? Does it matter?"

"No, Herrin." He looked down at his boots. But he had noticed. He had been sitting there eight hours and he had noticed which hand she favored, and somewhere tonight that observation would march in a column of other observations across the page of his report, and Brandt would read it over his dishwater-gray spectacles—or rather over whatever he did in the evenings with his self-appointed study of horology—and add it to the growing weight of *irregularities*.

She walked home through streets blue with early dark, past the harbor where the fishing boats knocked against the pilings, past the notice board where the lists hung fluttering like strange migrating birds that had chosen to die in public. Her name was not yet on any of them. Elias's name was nowhere anymore except inside a clock and inside her mouth, where she could not say it.

At her door she stopped. Someone had chalked a small cross on the gatepost—not a symbol she recognized, or perhaps exactly the kind that meant nothing except *we have looked here*. She wet her thumb and erased it, went inside, and did not sleep.

* * *

The dead man's name was Aldous Wren.

He had kept the observatory clock for thirty-one years before Voss, and before Hazel's own apprenticeship, and he had died in the winter of 1908 with his tools laid out in order on the bench, so precise a man that his death had seemed less an ending than an absence—a room waiting for its owner to return. Hazel had inherited his files and his burnishers and a box of brass offcuts, and she had inherited, too, the habit of his hand, because Elias had trained under Wren in turn and taught her everything in Wren's idiom. She could reproduce that hand. She had spent years learning to cut letters the way Wren cut them, deep and slightly left-leaning, the serifs blunt as a carpenter's chisel.

On Wednesday night, unable to sleep, she sat at her kitchen table with a candle and the box of offcuts and understood, with the cold settling certainty of a solution presenting itself to a tired mechanic, what she had to do.

If Brandt believed the marks inside the clock were old—if they belonged to the dead—he would stop digging toward the living. Suspicion needs a direction; give it one. And who better than a predecessor? A man whose handwriting was already all over the mechanism, whose initials were stamped on three of the original bridges, whose ghost haunted every corner of the tower. *Wren hid names once,* the false story would say. *Old men grow strange loyalties. The dead carry their secrets quietly.*

But it could not be clumsy. Brandt studied horology in the evenings. Whatever she planted had to be consistent—consistent with period tools, period alloys, the wear patterns of genuine age. She chose an offcut of old brass, duller than the modern sheet, and began to practice.

This was the part that shamed her, later, when she let herself think of it: how good she was. Her hands, which trembled around Brandt, went steady the moment they closed on the graver. She cut *A. WREN — FECIT 1879* into soft metal with a warmth of feeling that was entirely manufactured, aging the fresh cuts with vinegar and lamp smoke until the grooves held shadow the way old grooves do. She made a second plate, and a third. She gave Wren dates. She gave him, absurdly, a small motto—*TEMPUS DONUM*—because dead sentimentalists always left mottos, and because she had found the phrase in one of Wren's actual notebooks and knew Brandt might find it there too.

She was forging a dead man's conscience, and she was doing it beautifully, and lying in bed afterward with the smell of vinegar on her fingers she thought: *this is what I am now. A person who can do this.* The thought sat on her chest like a cat, heavy and warm and unwelcome.

* * *

She planted the first plate on Thursday, in daylight, under Kessler's eyes—which was the whole point, or half of it. Discovery must look accidental. Concealment was confession.

She took the lower panel off to grease the winding squares, a task so routine that even Kessler's report could find nothing in it, and she drew the forged plate from her apron pocket wrapped in an oily rag along with the grease pot, so that it emerged into the light as if it had simply always lived among her tools—an orphaned component, a spare, a thing she'd carried for months without thinking.

"What's that?" Kessler asked. First full sentence in two days.

"Hm?" She looked at it as though noticing. Turned it over. Let her eyebrows rise. "Well, I never." She held it to the window light. "Wren's hand. This is old Wren's lettering—I'd know it anywhere, I trained under a man who trained under him." She shook her head slowly, letting wonder do its work. "He must have cut this during the refit of '79. Look at the date. He hid it behind—" she gestured vaguely upward, toward the clock, toward the machinery of official time, "—behind everything, apparently. Old man liked leaving his name in places."

Kessler leaned forward despite himself. He was young and bored and far from home and here, finally, was a mystery small enough to hold. "What does it say?"

"Fecit. Made it, more or less. Latin." She tapped the motto. "Tempus donum. Time is a gift." She laughed—a real laugh, to her horror, startled out of her by the sheer audacity of the thing she was doing. "Sentimental old goat."

She put the plate in her toolbox. Not hidden—set aside, visible, the gesture of a woman with nothing to conceal. And Kessler sat back on his stool, and she watched him decide, in real time, that the irregularities inside the master clock belonged to a dead sentimentalist, and she felt the lie settle into place in his young mind with a click as clean as a detent engaging.

That night she engraved one more name into the barrel's hidden archive—Anna Ferro, aged eleven, struck from the ledger in October—and wept while she did it, silently, the tears falling on the brass so that she had to wipe them away before they spotted the metal. Grief and deceit in the same pair of hands, in the same hour. She could no longer tell where one ended and the other began. Perhaps, she thought, they had never been separate things at all. Perhaps every keeper of forbidden memory since the world began had also been a liar, because truth in such times was contraband and contraband must be smuggled, and smuggling is a profession built entirely on a pleasant face and a well-made falsehood.

She finished the name. Closed the case. Wound the clock.

* * *

Brandt came on Saturday, and this time he brought the photographs.

He spread them on the bench in the workshop—Kessler standing discreetly by the door—and there was the third wheel bridge in glossy detail, before and after, the two bright scratches stark as scars. "Explain these again," he said. "Slowly."

She explained them slowly. Grit, carborundum, the drag of a bridge across a contaminated pivot. She picked up a loupe and examined his photographs with the critical eye of a craftsman apprasing another's work, and pointed out the micro-striations of the scratch, their direction, their shallowness at one end—all of it true, all of it verifiable, all of it beside the point, which was the best kind of explanation. Then, as if the thought had only just occurred to her, she frowned and said:

"Though if you want marks that genuinely puzzle me, Officer, I found something last week you may care to see." She fetched the forged plate from the toolbox and laid it among his photographs like a card set down in a game. "Wren's hand. Hidden plate. Dated 1879." She told the story lightly, with the rueful amusement of a tradeswoman amused by her predecessors' eccentricities, and watched Brandt pick up the plate and turn it under the lamp.

For a long moment he said nothing. His thumbnail moved across the letters. Outside, a gull screamed once over the harbor and fell silent, as if reprimanded.

"Wren," Brandt said. "The previous keeper."

"Thirty-one years. Died '08. His work is all through the movement—his stamps, his adjustments. You'll have seen his initials on the escape bridge."

"I have seen them." He set the plate down, aligned it square with the edge of the bench, a small fussy precision that told her he was thinking hard. "And this was found... where?"

"Aside in my spares—it came loose from somewhere in the case, I couldn't say exactly where. I'd assumed it was packing scrap until I saw the lettering." She shrugged. "Old clockmakers were a strange breed. Voss could tell you more; he knew him."

"Yes," Brandt said softly. "I will ask Director Voss."

And there it was—the hook beneath the bait, the barb she hadn't planned for. Voss. Who knew perfectly well that Wren had died a quiet conventional man. Who knew what Hazel had been doing at night. Who had covered for her, forged logs, looked the other way, and who would now be asked, over tea, in his own observatory, whether old Wren had been the sort to hide inscribed plates in the works—and Voss would have to decide, in the space of a breath, whether to lie, and whether he could lie as well as she could.

"He was very secretive," Hazel said, into the silence. "Voss used to say you never knew half of what the old man got up to."

Brandt gathered his photographs, squared them, tucked them into his coat. At the door he stopped, as he always stopped, and Hazel braced.

"You have good hands, Herrin Almeida," he said. "Steady. Precise." He settled his cap. "It is remarkable, the things steady hands can do."

Then he was gone, and Kessler with him, and Hazel stood in the empty workshop surrounded by the small bright wreckage of her lies—plates and vinegar rags and a loupe still warm from her eye—and admitted it plainly, at last, to the quiet room: she could lie well. She could lie beautifully. She had looked a hunting man in his dishwater eyes and moved the hunt onto a corpse, and she would do it again tomorrow, and the day after, for every name still waiting in her notebook.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark she could hear, faint through the walls, the master clock keeping the town's official time, second by second, faithful as a heart, carrying its cargo of stolen names up through the gears into the cold spinning dark above the harbor—and somewhere below in the cellar, Elias would be awake, listening to the same ticking through the floor joists, counting the hours she'd bought him.

Two days later, Voss sent word that Brandt had come asking about Wren, and that Voss had told him, with a straight face and thirty years of practiced institutional blandness, that the old man had indeed been peculiar about his privacy—and that Brandt had thanked him politely and left.

But he had left a photograph behind on Voss's desk, Voss said. A new one. Taken yesterday, of the inside of the barrel housing.

And in the corner of the frame, just visible, was a scratch that was not hers, not Wren's, and not old at all.
