## 17. Two Days of Wind

HAZEL

The wind came off the sea on the first morning with a sound like a hand drawn across taut wire, and Hazel thought: even the weather is counting down now.

She had counted the names twice in the dark before dawn, her finger moving down the notebook page in the grey light that came early to the observatory gallery. Forty-one struck from the ledger since the requisition began. Thirty-six already engraved and set into the barrel's hidden channels, each plate seated among the gearing where only a clockmaker's eye would know a plate from a shim. Five remained.

Five names, two days, one boat.

She had not slept well. Sleep came to her these days in shallow draughts, like water sipped from a cup she could not trust, and each time she woke she lay listening for boots on the observatory stairs. But this morning there was only the wind, and beneath it the deep, patient breathing of the master clock — the great pendulum swinging in its case like the heart of some animal too old and too dignified to be afraid.

She dressed quickly and went down through the ledger room without looking at the clerks' empty desks. The soldiers searched house by house now; she had heard them since four o'clock, door to door along the harbor streets, their voices carrying up through the wind in short barked syllables that meant nothing and everything. They were working toward the hill. Toward the observatory, eventually, because they worked toward everything, with the terrible patience of men who had been given permission to be thorough.

Elias was already in the clock room when she descended, kneeling before the open barrel with the lamp turned low and a jeweler's loupe screwed into his eye. He looked up when he heard her, and the loupe made his eye enormous, absurd, boyish — an old man's face wearing a young man's eye.

"You look like you slept," he said.

"I dreamed I was winding the clock and it wouldn't take the key." She knelt beside him, shoulder almost touching his shoulder, and spread her tools on the cloth. "The handle kept turning and turning and nothing moved."

"In my country we would say that means money coming."

"Your country says everything means money coming or a wedding."

"And your country," Elias said, "says dreams mean nothing at all, which is why your country builds machines instead of songs." He tapped the barrel gently with one fingernail. It rang, faintly, a note so small she felt it more than heard it. "Though I begin to think your machine is also a song. Listen—" He tilted his head. "Thirty-six plates, each one a little different in weight. The escapement doesn't care, the balance is true, but I can hear them. Here—" He took her hand, rough and warm, and pressed her fingertips to the barrel's rim while he turned it slowly. "There. And there. A flutter. Like a bird changing position in a nest."

She felt it. The faintest irregularity traveling up through the metal, through her wrist bones, into the place behind her sternum where all her fear and all her resolve seemed to live in the same cramped room.

"Voss says Brandt ordered the maintenance log brought to him daily," she said. "He'll want to know why we've opened the movement three times in two days."

"Voss knows what to write." Elias removed the loupe and rubbed his eye. His hands, she noticed, were steadier than they had been in the cellar weeks ago. Fear had done something strange to him — not hardened him, but settled him, the way sediment settles water. "He wrote last night that we replaced the third wheel and adjusted the pallet stones. It is even mostly true. We did adjust the pallet stones."

"We adjusted them because you said they chattered."

"They did chatter. Now they sing. So the log is honest, if the log has ears." He smiled, and for a moment the lamplight found the young man who had once stood in a workshop in another country, before exile, before confession, before any of it, cutting his first gear wheels with hands that had never trembled.

Hazel opened the notebook to the last page. Five names. She read them silently, the way she had read them every morning and would read them every night until they lived in brass: *Ferreira, Tomas. Ferreira, Adelaida. Okonkwo-Bell, Ruth. Lindqvist, Anna. Almeida, Hazel.*

Her own name at the bottom, in her own handwriting, waiting.

"Well," she said, and was surprised at how ordinary her voice sounded. "We'd better start."

* * *

ELIAS

They worked the way they had never worked together in the workshop years ago — not as master and apprentice, but as two hands belonging to one craft. That was the word Elias kept returning to as he cut the first plate, *craft*, because it was the only thing left standing between him and despair, and it was enough. It had always been enough. He understood now, at the end of a long life spent apologizing to himself in mirrors, that this was what he had been trying to teach her all those years ago and had not known it: that the work is a country. Exile takes everything else, but the work travels with you, folded small, like a letter you are never able to mail.

Tomas Ferreira first. A fisherman, sixty-one years old, taken on the second day of the requisition for arguing with a lieutenant about his boat. Elias had never met him. It didn't matter. You learned them anyway, through the names — Hazel copied out the whole ledger pages, occupations and origins and ages, and so Tomas Ferreira became a man to him, mending nets somewhere beyond the border of the world, unaware that his name now lived inside a clock on a hill.

The engraving itself demanded a stillness that felt, in those hours, like prayer. Hazel cut the letters; Elias had taught her a modified roman hand, open-bowled, slightly irregular, so no single style would betray a pattern. Then Elias seated each plate in its channel among the gearing, filing the brass until it sat flush, indistinguishable from the hundred legitimate shims and spacers a great clock accumulates over decades of repair. It was slow work. It was, God forgive him, beautiful work.

Outside, the searches came closer. They heard a truck on the coast road at midmorning, and then voices below the observatory wall, and then boots on stone — but passing, passing, angling down toward the fishermen's cottages. Hazel's shoulders stayed bent over her file the entire time. She did not look up. Elias watched her not look up and understood that this was its own kind of courage, the refusal to be interrupted.

"The boat," he said quietly, when the boots had faded. "The fisherman who'll take us. Have you met him?"

"No. Voss arranged it. He leaves from the north cove the night after tomorrow, on the ebb." She blew brass dust from the lettering. "He charges nothing. He says he owes somebody."

"Owes whom?"

"He wouldn't say. Voss just smiled."

Elias filed a fraction more from the plate's edge, testing the seat, thinking about debts that travel unseen through a town like current under ice. Somewhere in this town's past, someone had been kind to someone, and the kindness had waited thirty years to surface as a free passage across cold water. This was the other thing history did, he thought — the thing no clerk ever recorded. For every name struck from a ledger there was a debt remembered in a human chest, and the ledgers never found those.

"Hazel." He set the finished plate aside and waited until she looked at him. Her face in the lamplight was thin, smudged with brass, absolutely serious. "When the boat goes, you go."

"We agreed—"

"We agreed nothing. *I* agreed to leave, which was easy, because I have been leaving things my whole life and have become very good at it." He held up a hand before she could speak. "No. Hear me. If they find me here, they find an old exile with a false name in his pocket. Bad, but survivable, perhaps. If they find *you* here, with your own name half-cut into the barrel of their deportation clock—" He shook his head. "I will not have taught you a trade that kills you."

"You taught me a trade that keeps people," she said. "That's different."

And she went back to the file, and Elias, watching the small muscles of her forearm move with the patience of tide, understood that he had lost the argument before he began it, and loved her the more for it, and feared the more for it.

* * *

HAZEL

Voss came to them at dusk on the first day, carrying the maintenance log under his arm like a breviary and a bottle of the thin local brandy in his other hand, which meant, in the grammar of Voss, that the news was bad enough to require fortification and not yet bad enough to require flight.

"They searched the baker's street today," he said. He poured three measures into tin cups, though his hands were not quite steady and the brandy slopped. "House to house. Brandt himself walked the row." He sat down heavily on the clockmaker's stool, which complained beneath him. "Tomorrow they reach the market quarter. The day after, the hill."

"Then we have until the day after tomorrow," Hazel said.

"You have until tonight to decide sensibly, and until the day after tomorrow to ignore the sensible decision. Those are different quantities of time, Miss Almeida, and I have spent seventy years learning the difference." He drank, grimaced at the brandy, drank again. "I have written you into the log for tomorrow. Full overhaul. Escapement disassembly, lubrication of the train, replacement of the suspension spring. Eight hours of authorized work. No soldier will question a clock opened for overhaul; they question closed clocks, mysterious ones. An open clock is simply an obedient clock."

Elias laughed softly, a dry sound. "You forge beautifully, Director."

"It is not forging. It is scheduling." But Voss's eyes, above the cup, were tired and ashamed and clear all at once, and Hazel saw in them the whole architecture of the man: the compromises laid brick by brick, and now, at the top, this one final arch, thrown desperately across a gap, holding. "One thing more. Brandt has requested the clock verified Thursday morning by the garrison timekeeper. That is the day after the boat. If anything is discovered—" He did not finish. He looked at the barrel, at the hidden plates sleeping among the gears like seeds in winter soil. "It must be perfect. It must be better than perfect. It must be invisible even to a man whose job is suspicion."

"Then it's fortunate," Hazel said, "that invisibility is the one thing our trade does best. Every good clock disappears. Nobody sees a clock that runs true; they only notice the ones that fail."

Voss regarded her for a long moment, and something moved across his face — regret, or pride, or the recognition of a quality in her he had failed to find in himself until it was too late to be of use to anyone. "Yes," he said finally. "I believe you may be right. Heaven help us, I believe you are."

He left the rest of the bottle and went up the stairs, and they heard him stop on the landing to speak to the sentry in his blandest administrative voice, and the sentry's boots recede satisfied, and Hazel thought: *he lies for us now the way he once signed papers against others — fluently, and hating it, and doing it anyway.* Perhaps, she thought, that was all conscience ever was in bad years. Not heroism. Just hatred of one's own necessity, continued daily.

* * *

ELIAS

The second night the wind rose fully, screaming down the flue of the observatory tower, and the lamp guttered in the draft from the stairwell so that shadows swung across the clock room walls like the shadows of ships.

Ruth Okonkwo-Bell next. A schoolteacher. Forty-four. Born three seas away, arrived eleven years ago with a trunk of books and, according to the ledger's marginal note in some clerk's cramped hand, *"insufficient documentation of origin."* Elias cut her name slowly, carefully, giving each letter its full weight, because a teacher deserved a name cut well — teachers lived on in the hands of the taught, and he knew this better than most men alive.

Anna Lindqvist after her, and then Adelaide Ferreira, wife to Tomas, and with each plate seated Elias felt the clock change under his fingers — not mechanically, for the balance remained true, but in some register finer than mechanism. The clock was becoming crowded. It had always been crowded; he understood that now, having found the older names of the earlier purge hidden in the case's inner panels. Generations of the erased, layered in brass like strata in rock. He was adding his own small epoch to a geological record of cruelty, and there was a terrible comfort in the continuity. Towns forgot. Empires rewrote. But someone always wound the clock.

Hazel worked beside him, filing the seats for the final plates, and the lamplight moved on her hands, and Elias let himself watch her the way one is permitted to watch a fire — openly, gratefully, without explanation. He had buried a wife in another country, in another life. He had thought his heart finished, archived, its accounts closed. Then this girl had come to his workshop at fourteen with a broken pocket watch and an expression of such ferocious concentration that he had recognized, immediately, helplessly, his own younger self looking back at him from a different face. Teaching her had been his penance and his rescue in equal measure. Loving her had come later, quietly, the way affection comes to those who live with their grief long enough that it becomes furniture, then becomes family.

"What are you looking at?" she asked, not raising her head.

"A finished piece of work."

"I'm not finished."

"No one is," Elias said. "That is the whole secret. Clocks included."

Below them, faint through stone, the town bell rang two in the morning, governed by the very clock they were dismantling and rebuilding around its cargo of names, and Elias thought the sound had never been sweeter — the official time of an official lie, rung out over a town whose true record ticked away inaudibly six inches from the bell hammer.

One plate remained.

He looked at it lying on the cloth: blank brass, small as a wafer, catching the lamp. *Almeida, Hazel,* it would say. Her own name, in her own hand, cut by her own teacher, hidden in the heart of the machine that measured the departures of everyone she had failed to save and everyone the world had decided to forget.

She reached for the graver. He put his hand over hers — gently, the way he had corrected her grip twenty years ago, the first lesson, the beginning of everything.

"Let me cut yours," he said. "A teacher should sign his finest student's work. It's the only signature I have left."

The wind hurled itself against the tower windows. Hazel looked at the blank plate a long time, and then at him, and her eyes in the lamplight were wet and absolutely steady, and she nodded once and passed him the graver — and it was then, in the silence after the wind's gust died, that they both heard it.

Boots on the outer stair. Not one pair. Many.

And beneath the boots, ascending, unhurried, the sound of Officer Brandt clearing his throat.
