## 19. What Hazel Chose

The harbor at dawn was the color of pewter, and the fishing boat *Mercy of Sorrow* — a name too long and too hopeful for so small a hull — sat low in the water where it waited for Elias.

Hazel had walked him down through the sleeping streets the way you carry something breakable: slowly, close to the body, pausing at every corner to listen for boots. The curfew had lifted an hour ago but the town did not yet believe in mornings. Shutters stayed closed. Smoke rose from only two chimneys. Somewhere a gull complained about the state of the world, which was more honesty than most people could manage these days.

Elias walked with a canvas bag over his shoulder that held everything he had not been able to leave in the cellar: three files, a loupe on a ribbon, a photograph of a woman dead forty years, and nothing else. He had argued with her about the bag. He had wanted to leave even that, as if lightness might make him less of a burden, less of a fugitive, less of a man whose name had been struck from a ledger with a clerk's bored stroke of the pen.

"The tide turns at seven," Hazel said. "Voss confirmed it last night. The fisherman knows the patrol schedule better than the patrol does — he says they change shifts at half past six and again at eight, and there is a gap."

"There is always a gap," Elias murmured. "That is what I told your mother once about the escapement. There is always a gap where the tooth falls into the lock. The whole trick of living is knowing where."

She almost smiled. Her face had forgotten how, somewhere in the last weeks, and the attempt felt like trying to turn a rusted wheel.

The boat's master, a weathered man named Duarte who had never once met Hazel's eyes in his life and did not now, was coiling rope with the studied indifference of a man who saw no one, knew no one, and had been paid in coins he could not remember receiving. He looked at Elias, then at the eastern sky, and said nothing at all, which was the kindest sentence anyone had spoken all week.

* * *

They stood at the foot of the stone stairs that led down to the slip, out of the wind, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The harbor smelled of tar and salt and old fish, the smell Hazel had known all her life, the smell she had once thought was simply the smell of nowhere, of a town too small for history to bother with. She knew better now. History had been here all along; it had merely needed hands.

"You could come," Elias said quietly. It was not the first time. "There will be work. There are clocks everywhere, Hazel. Every town has its clocks. You would be a master within a year — you should have been one years ago, if the world were anything other than what it is." His voice caught on the last words and steadied itself, the way an old movement catches and settles. "Come with me. Let someone else keep the clock."

"No one else can," she said.

It came out flat and certain, and it surprised her, because she had expected to argue, to build the case piece by piece the way she had been building it in her mind for two days. But there was nothing to build. The case had assembled itself while she slept, while she engraved, while she wound the great machine above the town every morning at six so that Officer Brandt could consult its face before signing away human lives.

"One name left," she said. "Ferreira, the cooper's wife. The brass is cut, the plate is seated behind the third wheel, and I haven't touched the letters. If they open the barrel before I finish — if some clerk decides to inventory the movement —" She stopped. The gull screamed again, further off. "Then Ferreira is nobody. Then she was never born, and her daughters were never born, and the ledger wins."

Elias looked at her for a long moment. In the grey light his face was all lines and shadows, a watch face worn down by decades, and his eyes — those patient eyes that had watched her ruin her first mainspring and had only said, *again, from the beginning* — were wet.

"You know what they will do if they find you," he said. "You know what Brandt already suspects. You are not invisible anymore, child. That was the whole point of everything I taught you — to work unseen, to let the work speak — and now the work may hang you."

"Then the work will have spoken," Hazel said.

He made a sound that was half laugh and half grief, and he took her hands. They were rough, both pairs; his softer with age but still ridged with file-marks, hers hardening into the same topography, the same map of the trade. Teacher and student, matched like gears.

"I taught you to repair things," he said. "I never meant for you to repair the world. It is too heavy a movement. The parts do not want to fit."

"They fit," Hazel said. "You just have to cut them yourself instead of waiting for someone to sell them to you."

* * *

What happened next happened quickly, because she had known it would need to happen quickly and had rehearsed it against the dark ceiling of her room: the way you rehearse a difficult adjustment before ever touching the tool.

She put her hand flat against his chest — over the pocket where, absurdly, he still carried a broken repeater watch he refused to discard — and she turned him gently toward the boat.

"Go," she said. "Please. Before the gap closes."

"Hazel—"

"If we both stay, we lose everything. If we both go, we lose everything. This is the only arrangement where anything survives." She heard her own voice and did not recognize it; it sounded like Director Voss, or like no one, or like the clock itself, striking hours whether or not anyone wished to hear them. "You carry the message to Lisbon. You find the people Voss wrote to. You tell them what is in the clock, and where, and how to read it — because if I am taken, someone must know the order of the plates, the dating marks, the handwriting system. You are the only other person alive who can read my hand well enough. So you have to live. Do you understand? Living is your part of the work now."

Elias's jaw worked. Below them, Duarte coughed meaninglessly and began untying a line that did not need untying.

"When you taught me," Hazel said, softer, "you told me that a clockmaker's first duty is not to the clock but to the time. The mechanism fails, the parts wear, the whole thing can burn — but the time goes on, and someone must serve it. I am serving the time, Elias. Mine is here. Yours is elsewhere. That is all this is. It isn't courage." Her throat closed. She forced the words through. "I am a coward who is good with her hands, and staying is the only thing my hands know how to do."

"That is not true," he said. "None of that is true." He pulled off one glove — he wore gloves now, old man's gloves, to hide the tremor — and pressed something into her palm. The broken repeater. Its case was warm from his chest. "Slide. Strike. Repeat. You'll fix it when I'm gone, the way you fix everything I leave broken." His voice broke entirely on the last word, and he did not try to mend it. "I promised your mother I'd teach you. I never promised anyone I'd survive you."

"Survive," she said. "That's an order."

For a moment longer he held her shoulders, and she let herself lean into him the way she had at fourteen, when her father died and she had come to the workshop because she had nowhere else to put her grief and Elias had simply kept working, letting her hand him tools until the shaking stopped, and at the end of the day had said, *grief is like a mainspring, it must be housed or it destroys everything near it.* Now she housed it again. She felt it click into place, contained, dangerous, ready to drive something.

Then she stepped back, and Elias Marchetti went down the stone stairs to the slip, and Duarte's hand caught his arm, and the small boat took his weight with a groan of timber, and Hazel stood at the top of the stairs with a broken watch in her fist and watched the sail catch the wind.

He did not look back. She was grateful. She would not have survived him looking back.

* * *

The walk up from the harbor took eleven minutes. She counted them against her heartbeat, the way she timed everything now, the occupational disease of her trade, and by the twelfth minute she was at the observatory door with Voss opening it before she knocked, his face the color of the paper in his hand.

"They came to the house," she said. "I saw the notice on my own door on the way up. Almeida. It's a Portuguese name, they've decided it's a Portuguese name, and Portuguese is close enough to enemy for their purposes." She heard how steady she sounded and marveled at herself distantly, as if listening to a stranger's movement run. "Two days, Voss. The soldier at the corner has orders to bring me in tomorrow afternoon for questioning. Brandt wants me to service the clock under supervision first — he still needs the train timed, God help us, the schedule is sacred."

"Duarte sailed?" Voss asked.

"He sailed."

Voss closed his eyes briefly, and in that brief darkness she saw everything he was carrying: the cellar now empty, the forged maintenance logs stacked in his desk drawer like unexploded shells, the knowledge that he had bargained with a tyranny and lost the bargain and was going to be arrested for it eventually anyway, and had chosen to lose it slowly, on his own terms, buying what time he could for her. He was not a brave man. He had told her so himself, twice, in the tones of a confession. But he had done the arithmetic of decency in the dark, alone, night after night, and the sum had always come out the same, and she thought that maybe that was what courage actually was — not the absence of fear or even the mastery of it, just the same answer, arrived at again and again, until the fear got tired of asking.

"The boat is gone," Voss said. "So you're staying."

"So I'm staying."

"You understand—" He stopped, rubbed his eyes under his spectacles. "You understand what you are choosing. Not martyrdom. Martyrdom is clean. What you are choosing is interrogation, and cells, and possibly nothing worse than years of paperwork, which is its own death. And you are choosing it for a name on a plate of brass that no one may ever read."

"Someone read the old ones," Hazel said. "Whoever cleaned this movement before me found them. Someone always finds them, eventually. Clocks get serviced. That is their nature. They insist on being opened."

* * *

She went up to the clock room.

Morning light came through the tall east windows in long amber bars and lay across the floor like planks of a different, older world. The master clock stood in the center of the room as it had stood for ninety years, taller than two men, its case of black walnut glowing faintly, its pendulum swinging in the long slow breath that governed every bell, every shift, every train, every departure in the town below. Nine hundred names inside it. Nine hundred people struck from the civic ledger and written instead into the heart of the machine that the clerks used to erase them. Somewhere in that glittering architecture of brass and steel was Marta's name. Somewhere was Elias's, in her own steadiest lettering, cut at two in the morning with tears running freely and her burin never once slipping, because grief, properly housed, drives the work instead of destroying it.

And one name unfinished. Ferreira. The plate was seated, polished, waiting behind the third wheel like a held breath.

Hazel set down her bag. She took off her coat. She rolled her sleeves the way she had every morning since she was twelve years old, and she opened the case, and the smell of oil and metal rose around her like a language she had spoken before she could speak any other.

The work took her out of herself, as it always did. Hands moving, loupe fixed to her eye, the world narrowing to the geometry of the barrel and the play of the pallets — and there, in that narrowness, she found the strange peace she had been seeking for weeks. She cut the F first. Then the e, small and round, matching the hand she had invented for the archive, a hand that belonged to no one, that could not be traced to any single apprentice's slant or flourish. The r's, doubled. The i with its dot placed slightly high, deliberately, a maker's mark of absence. The e. The i. The r. The a.

When she finished, the sun had moved to the west windows. The name was complete. Nine hundred and one. The clock's true ledger stood full, and no clerk alive knew how to read it.

She closed the case with the tenderness of tucking in a child, and then she sat down on the floor beneath the pendulum, in the great quiet tick of the machine, and allowed herself, for exactly four minutes — she timed it, she could not help it — to feel the weight of what she had done. Elias on a grey sea somewhere, getting smaller. Voss downstairs forging one last log entry. Marta on a train months ago, her face at the window, finding Hazel in the crowd or not finding her, impossible to say. Her own name on a notice nailed to her own door.

Then the four minutes ended, and Hazel Almeida stood up, and went downstairs to surrender.

* * *

Brandt was not there. A younger soldier was, a boy with chapped hands and a rifle he held like something borrowed, and he seemed almost relieved to have the paperwork in order, almost apologetic as he explained that the Herr Officer would receive her at the station in the morning, that she was to remain at the observatory tonight under watch, that she was to make no alterations to the clock.

"Alterations," Hazel repeated.

"Repairs, madam. Adjustments. Nothing beyond routine maintenance."

"Routine maintenance is scheduled for six tomorrow morning," she said evenly. "I will wind the clock, check the rate, and log it. Nothing else. You may watch me do it if you like."

He flushed and said that would not be necessary, and she understood, with a tired clarity, that she had already begun to lie to them well, and that she would keep lying, and that the lies were now indistinguishable from the truth, because she really would perform the maintenance, and it really would be routine, and none of it would save her, and all of it would keep the clock running.

That night she sat in the clock room with a single lamp, the soldier's shadow occasionally crossing the frosted glass of the door, and she repaired Elias's broken repeater. Slide. Strike. The little hammer fell on the gongs, counting the hours into the dark — eleven, clear and silver, ringing out over a town that did not know it was listening. She set the watch on the sill where moonlight could find it, and she thought: *he is on the water right now. He is alive. The time is correct.*

In the morning they would come for her, and she had a story prepared, maker's marks, trade habit, the dull technical truth stretched thin — and she had no way of knowing whether it would hold. But upstairs the master clock ticked on, carrying nine hundred and one names in its brass heart, keeping perfect time over a town that had erased them, and every hour it struck was a name, and every name was true.

At six o'clock she climbed the stairs, wound the great machine, and heard, far below, the first truck of the morning stop outside the observatory door.
