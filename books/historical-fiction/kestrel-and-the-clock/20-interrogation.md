## 20. Interrogation

They came for her on a Tuesday, in the hour between the noon bell and the afternoon wind, when the light through the observatory windows goes the color of old paper.

Hazel had been expecting it for eleven days — ever since Brandt's soldier had carried the message up the hill: *The Officer requests the clockmaker's presence at the harbor office at fourteen hundred hours.* Requests. The word was a courtesy the army extended to no one else in Kestrel's Reach, and she understood it for what it was: Brandt wanted witnesses of a particular kind when he asked his questions. Not soldiers. A room, a table, and a girl whose hands were famous in this town for being steady.

She had spent those eleven days the way she spent all her days now. She rose before the gulls, climbed the stair into the clock tower while the stars were still dissolving, and wound the master clock. She let down the great driving weights on their steel cables, checked the oil in the pivots, listened with her whole body to the escapement's patient tick — that sound she had come to think of as the town's heartbeat, though she no longer knew whose heart it beat for. Then she descended to the movement chamber, unlocked the barrel housing with the small brass key she kept sewn into her hem, and put her fingers against the hidden plates.

Forty-one names now. She did not need lamplight to find them anymore. Her fingertips knew each one — the shallow V of Marchetti, the doubled t of Torretti, Marta Lindqvist's name cut in letters so fine she had needed three nights to finish it, working by feel because her eyes had blurred. Forty-one names riding inside the great wheelwork, turning twelve times a day as the clock told the town its hours, passing under the lifting pallets, gathering oil and time, invisible to every eye but hers.

She had cleaned her files. She had burned her notebook pages in the stove and scattered the ash. She had rehearsed, in the dark of her cot above the workshop, the sentence she would say if they ever asked her directly: *They are maker's marks. Every clockmaker signs his work.* She had said it aloud until it stopped tasting like a lie.

At half past one she locked the tower door, pocketed the key, and walked down through the town to the harbor.

* * *

Kestrel's Reach in its fifth year of occupation was a town that had learned to walk with its head down. The fishing fleet was gone — commandeered, then sunk, then simply not spoken of. The shops along the front opened at odd hours, shuttered again without pattern, their windows papered over with notices whose ink faded from black to grey to a ghost of itself, layer upon layer, like sediment recording some slow catastrophe. Soldiers were thinner now. Their uniforms had been mended so many times the original cloth survived only at the seams, and the boys wearing them looked, most days, less like conquerors than like apprentices who had been set an impossible task and left alone with the tools.

Hazel noticed these things with the detached precision of her trade. Five years of servicing a clock under an officer's eye had taught her to observe everything and react to nothing; her face had become a dial, smooth and readable only in the ways she permitted.

The harbor office still stood where it always had, though the customs house sign had been taken down and replaced with a board listing curfew hours, cargo regulations, and — new since spring, the letters already weathering — a directive about *the orderly conclusion of administrative matters pending transfer of authority.*

Transfer of authority. Everyone in the queue outside understood what those words meant, even if no one said them. The empire was dying somewhere far to the east, and its clerks here could smell the change in the air the way fishermen smell weather.

Inside, the room had been stripped to its essentials: a table, two chairs, a lamp burning even in daylight because the window had been painted over. A sergeant sat in the corner with a ledger open and a pencil behind his ear, looking, Hazel thought, like nothing so much as a clerk in the ledger room below the observatory, five years younger and five years more tired.

Officer Brandt sat across the table.

He had aged strangely. The occupation had hollowed him — the flesh fallen away from his jaw, the fair hair gone thin — but his uniform remained immaculate, pressed with a rigor that struck Hazel suddenly as its own kind of confession. A man keeping himself sharp against the day he would be called to account for it.

"Fräulein Almeida." He gestured to the chair. "Thank you for coming."

"You requested me, Officer. I came." She sat. Her hands, she noted with something like gratitude, folded themselves in her lap without trembling. Whatever else the years had done, they had trained her hands well.

"Yes." He opened a folder — an actual folder, manila, with a string tie, and she thought absurdly that she had imagined something grander for this moment: rifles, shouting, the whole theatre of accusation. Instead there was a folder, and a lamp, and a sergeant who licked his pencil before writing the date. "You are the keeper of the master clock at the observatory."

"I service it. Daily. I have since 1913."

"You are also," Brandt said, "an apprentice of Elias Marchetti."

The name landed in the room and sat there between them. Hazel had rehearsed for this too. "He taught me my trade when I was fifteen. That was before the requisition. Before any of it."

"Before," Brandt agreed mildly. He turned the folder around and pushed it across the table.

Photographs. She kept her breathing even. Three photographs of the interior of the clock barrel, taken — she could tell from the angle and the smudge of shadow at the frame's edge — during the inspection last winter, the one after which she'd planted the false tool marks implicating old Halloran, dead these four years. The camera had found what eyes had missed. There, in the third print, circled in red grease pencil: a corner of engraved brass, letters caught slantwise in the flash. Not legible. But legible enough to raise the question the circle existed to ask.

"You will tell me," Brandt said, "what these marks are."

"They're maker's marks."

The sentence came out exactly as she had practiced it: flat, bored, faintly offended at being interrupted for this. She let a silence follow it, the kind of silence an expert permits a layman before explaining.

Brandt waited. He was better at waiting than she remembered. Then: "Explain maker's marks to me, Fräulein. As you would explain them to a court."

"Certainly." She unfolded her hands and laid them on the table, palms down, the way she did when demonstrating something to a customer. "Every serious clock is signed inside the movement. It has been so for two centuries. The practice predates your empire's founding, Officer, and it exists because a clock is not furniture — it is a trust. Whoever services it must know who built it, who repaired it, what alterations were made and when. Otherwise every hand that touches the work corrupts the record of it." She tapped the circled photograph with one finger. "That mark follows the convention of the old guild houses. Initials, date code, station number. You'll find similar marks in church turret clocks from here to the capital. Your own inspector wrote in his report — I read it, it was left in the maintenance log — that the movement showed evidence of 'multiple historical interventions consistent with ordinary trade practice.' His words. I can show you the page."

She had not known, walking down the hill, whether she could do this. Now she heard her own voice laying out the technical truth in careful courses, like brick, and understood that she had been building toward this hour for five years — that every morning she had serviced the clock under Brandt's eye had also been rehearsal, that craft and deception had grown into the same muscle.

Brandt studied her. In the corner, the sergeant's pencil scratched.

"Multiple historical interventions," Brandt repeated. "And yet the inspector flagged this photograph for review."

"The inspector," Hazel said, "was a signals technician seconded from a railway unit, and he had never opened a astronomical regulator in his life. With respect, Officer — you have run this town's schedules off my clock for five years. If the movement were compromised, you would have known before your inspector did. The trains ran on time."

It was a terrible thing to say and she said it steadily, because it was true, and because truth was the only material she had left to build with. The trains had run on time. She had kept them running, or she had stopped them when she could, and both of those facts lived in her now like shrapnel, and she would carry them the rest of her life.

Brandt closed the folder. For a long moment he looked at the lamp instead of at her, and Hazel saw — with the same detached precision, the same craftsman's reading of mechanism — the moment when the officer's machinery changed gear.

"How long," he asked quietly, "until the end of the war? By your estimate. You watch the shipping. You hear the wireless before we do, up there on your hill."

"I mend clocks, Officer. I don't forecast."

"Everyone forecasts." He almost smiled; it was the ghost of the expression, worn down to its wire. "My superiors forecast daily, and each forecast contradicts the last. Three weeks ago I received an order to compile a full inventory of all records held in this district, for evacuation. Last week the order was countermanded. This week I am told records of certain categories may be destroyed in place to prevent their capture." He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger, and for a moment he looked like what he perhaps had been before all this: a minor official's son from a provincial town, good at timetables, promoted past his imagination. "I am asking you, the keeper of this district's official time, whether the schedule I am working to still exists."

Hazel looked at him and felt the strangest sensation — not fear, not hatred, though both were present, worn smooth as river stones — but a kind of horrified recognition. He was asking her the question she had asked herself every dawn for five years at the top of the tower: *does the work still mean anything, and who will be held to account for it.*

"I can only tell you what the clock tells me," she said. "It runs. It will go on running after everyone in this room is gone. That is all a clock knows."

The sergeant stopped writing. Brandt sat back. Outside, through the painted-over window, came the sound of a lorry changing gear on the coast road, and beneath it the long wash of the sea, indifferent as it had been before any of them were born.

* * *

He kept her another hour. It was, she understood eventually, not really an interrogation at all — or rather, it was an interrogation conducted in such perfect bureaucratic exhaustion that it had forgotten its purpose halfway through, the way a clock wound down will tick slower and slower and finally stand. He asked about Halloran, and she gave him Halloran: the old man's habits, his tools, the marks he had left in forty years of movements, the fact of his death and burial and the location of his grave, which she had visited once with flowers and once, later, with guilt. He asked about the maintenance logs, and she produced from memory the dates, the faults, the parts replaced, a five-year litany of small honest labors that was also, entirely, a fabric of cover. He asked whether anyone else had access to the tower, and she said Director Voss had, before his arrest, and that the keys were now hers alone, and watched him write that down and understood that Voss's sacrifice was still holding, two years on, a wall between her and the firing squad — or whatever passed for one here, at the end, when nobody had the resources for conviction.

Once, near the end, Brandt set down his pen and asked, not looking at her: "Marchetti. The watchmaker. Do you know where he went?"

"On the boat," Hazel said. "South, I was told. I don't know where he is."

This was true. It was the truest thing she had said all afternoon, and it hurt her throat coming out, because the last word from Elias had come eight months ago through a fish merchant's circuitous kindness — a postcard with no signature, a picture of a harbor she didn't recognize, and on the back, in handwriting disguised as a stranger's: *The watch keeps good time. E.*

"Perhaps," Brandt said, "that is just as well. For him, and for you." He gathered the photographs back into the folder, tied the string, and regarded it for a moment as a man regards a problem he has decided not to solve. Then, in a voice drained of everything — of suspicion, of duty, of belief in the architecture he served — he said: "The matter of the marks will remain under review. Pending clarification of directives from district command."

"And district command?"

"District command," Brandt said, "has stopped answering."

He stood, which meant the interview was over, which meant — she made herself understand it slowly, the way one understands a mechanism by watching its wheels — that she was going to walk out of this room. That the folder would go into a cabinet. That the cabinet would sit in an office that might, within weeks, be someone else's office, or rubble, or a customs house again with its old sign rehung. Justice, if that was the word, would arrive not as a verdict but as a filing error, a folder lost in the shuffle of empires, a question officially left unfinished because no one remaining had the stomach or the orders to finish it.

She had wanted, for five years, to be believed innocent. Walking out through the painted-over daylight, she discovered she felt nothing of triumph at all — only a fatigue so complete it resembled grief, and beneath the fatigue, ticking steadily, the knowledge that the names were still hers to keep. The clock did not care about verdicts. The clock required winding.

At the door Brandt spoke once more, to her back.

"Fräulein Almeida. When they come — whoever comes, whichever flag — the observatory will want a keeper who knows the movement. Someone the records can trust." A pause. "I have written as much in my report."

She did not turn around. She did not thank him. Some debts she would not owe.

* * *

The walk home was long, and she took it slowly, letting the sea wind push at her as if testing her weight.

Halfway up the hill she stopped at the churchyard wall, where she could see the whole town spread below her: the grey harbor, the papered windows, the roofs she had known all her life and barely recognized anymore. And above it all, unmistakable even at this distance, the observatory dome catching the late sun, and beside it the tower, and inside the tower the great wheelwork turning, carrying its secret cargo around and around, twelve times a day, faithful as breath.

Something was different. She stood very still and worked out what it was, the way she worked out any fault — by listening for the note that had changed.

The bells. The quarter bells were late. Not broken — late, by seconds she could feel in her teeth, a drag in the striking train that had not been there that morning.

Someone had been in the tower. Or something had shifted — the cold snap, the settling of the frame, a hundred innocent explanations, and she had learned long ago not to believe in innocent explanations.

Hazel tightened her coat around her, took the hill at a pace she hadn't managed in months, and climbed the tower stairs two at a time, her key already in her hand, her heart going faster than any clock she had ever kept.
