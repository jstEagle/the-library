## 21. The Names Read Aloud

The war ended the way a long illness ends—not with a blow but with a slow lessening, a morning when Hazel woke and realized the shelling she had half-heard in her dreams had not been shelling at all, only the sea working itself against the harbor wall as it always had, indifferent and eternal.

Word came to the observatory before it came anywhere else. It came, fittingly, by clockwork: the wireless in Director Voss's old office—he had been released in the spring, thinner, his hands unsteady, but alive—crackled to life at six in the morning and spoke the armistice in three languages, none of them quite Hazel's, all of them understood. She stood in the doorway with an oil can in one hand and listened until the announcer's voice gave way to music, some trembling string piece from a country that had been a word only, a wound only, for years, and was suddenly again a place where people played violins.

She did not cry. She had thought she would, had imagined this moment through four winters, and she had expected tears the way one expects rain in April. Instead she felt the strange lightness of a weight she had carried so long it had grown into her skeleton, as if someone had reached in and lifted out a stone she'd mistaken for bone. Her hand, still holding the oil can, did not shake. It had stopped shaking somewhere in the third year, when there was no strength left to waste on trembling.

Below her window, the town began to make noise. Not cheering at first—cheering came later, in the afternoon, with wine and flags dug out of attics—but a low murmur, doors opening, voices calling across streets that had been silent at this hour for years because silence was safer. Somewhere a church bell began to ring and could not stop, or would not, and after a moment Hazel recognized its rhythm as wrong, joyful, arrhythmic—the bell-ringer letting his children take turns on the rope.

The master clock, above it all, ticked on. She had kept it running. Whatever else the war had taken from her hands, they had never once let it stop—not for sabotage again, not for shortage of oil, not for grief. She had ground substitute lubricant from olive oil and graphite when the proper stuff ran out. She had filed replacement teeth for the escapement wheel by lamplight while soldiers slept in the gallery above. The clock had become, without her ever deciding it, the axis of her life: everything else revolved around those few hundred pounds of brass and steel, and inside them, the names.

* * *

It was Marta who proposed the reading.

Hazel saw her first from the observatory steps three days after the armistice, coming up the hill through the pines with flour still ghosting her forearms, and for a moment Hazel's legs nearly failed her, because the last time she had seen that face it had been turned away from her, framed in the window of a deportation train, growing smaller. Four years. Four years of Hazel carrying the girl's name against her heart like a splinter too deep to work out, engraving *Marta Okafor* into brass in the second week after the train left, her tears falling on the metal and beading there because water will not wet clean brass, and now here she came up the hill in the morning sun, alive, broadened, her hair covered in the manner of married women, a scar at her temple that had not been there before.

They stood looking at each other on the observatory steps and neither could speak. Then Marta laughed—a sound Hazel had feared she would never hear again, rusty and full—and said, "You've gone gray at the temples, Hazel Almeida," and Hazel said, "And you've gotten old enough to notice," and then they were holding each other in the doorway of the building where time was kept, and the keeping of time, for once, felt like mercy instead of mathematics.

Marta's story, told over the following days in fragments, was not remarkable except in that she had survived it—which is to say it was the story of thousands, told ten thousand times, and every telling both necessary and insufficient. A labor camp in the interior. A farmer who needed hands and asked no questions about papers. The end of things arriving slowly, then all at once, and a walk home of eleven weeks on roads where nobody looked closely at anyone anymore because everyone was walking somewhere.

"I went to the registry office first thing," Marta said. "I wanted my name put back in the ledger. To walk in and say: here I am, write me down." Her mouth twisted. "The clerk was very sorry. The ledger, he says, was damaged in the occupation. Pages missing. He couldn't restore what wasn't there. My whole family's record—gone. As if we'd sprouted from the hillsides like mushrooms, with no history."

"Your name isn't gone," Hazel said quietly.

Marta looked at her.

"It's in the clock."

* * *

They decided together that it should not be done quietly.

There were arguments for quiet. The town was exhausted; half of it was ashamed and the other half frightened, and the two halves overlapped more than anyone would say aloud. There were men on the new town council who had signed requisition receipts with tidy signatures, women who had pointed out neighbors' houses with a tilt of the chin, and all of them would prefer that the war's local history remain unwritten, or written vaguely, in passive voice: mistakes were made, people were taken. A public reading would drag all of it into the light.

"But it was never meant to be hidden forever," Hazel said. She was standing in the clock room with Marta and with Voss, who had come back to the observatory like a man returning to a house he'd been told burned down and found still standing. "Elias and I engraved them so clerks wouldn't find them. That was the point—so the names would survive the clerks. But surviving isn't the same as being read." She thought of Elias, far away, and her chest tightened. "He used to say a watch that runs in a drawer keeps no one's time. A name locked in brass keeps no one's memory. It has to be witnessed."

Voss had aged terribly in four years, but his eyes were unchanged—still the eyes of a man who loved instruments and distrusted himself. "The barrel will have to come out," he said, in the tone he used for calculations. "It can't be read in place. And once it's out—" he spread his hands— "it's out. Everyone will see everything. Every name. Every tool mark. What you did, Hazel, laid open for the whole town."

"Yes," Hazel said. "That's the idea."

He studied her for a long moment. During the occupation she had learned to read him well enough to know when he was bargaining with his conscience; now she watched him lose the bargain, whatever version of it he was attempting. "I'll speak to the council," he said at last. "Sunday. After the service. Let them come up the hill themselves if they want to hear it. We won't drag anyone."

"They'll come," Marta said. "People can't help it. They have to know what was done to them, and what was done beside them. Even the ones who'd rather not." She paused. "Especially those."

* * *

On Sunday the observatory steps had not held so many people since the eclipse of Hazel's childhood, which her mother had described to her so many times she felt she remembered it: the whole town climbing the hill with smoked glasses, strangers sharing bread, the ordinary world briefly suspended. Now they came without smoked glasses, in their best worn clothes, and the crowd overflowed the steps onto the terrace and down the switchback path, faces Hazel had catalogued through years of watching from windows—faces she had seen in bread lines and identity checks and, once, in the terrible columns moving toward the trains.

The new military governor had sent a representative, a young captain with an embarrassed face, who stood to one side holding his cap. Father Anselmo was there, and the schoolteacher, and the widow Serrano whose husband's name was fourth on the barrel. The baker, Marta's father, sat near the front on a chair someone brought him, too old now for stairs, weeping openly and unashamed, as if he had saved up four years of tears for exactly this expenditure.

Inside, the clock room was too small for even a fraction of them, so the great case doors had been carried out to the terrace, set on trestles like tables at a wedding feast, and the movement itself—Hazel's patient, battered, magnificent machine—stood exposed to the sky and the crowd, its pendulum stilled for the first time in living memory.

Hazel stood before it with white gloves on, because Elias had taught her that one touches fine mechanism either bare-handed or gloved but never apologetically, and she would not apologize today. Her heart was going harder than any escapement she had ever regulated.

"We'll begin," she said, and her voice carried farther than she expected, into a hush so complete she could hear the sea below. "The clock was built to keep official time. For four years it kept the time of schedules I will not describe, because everyone here knows them. But clocks are older than governments, and they have always been used to hold other things. When we cleaned this movement, my teacher and I found names inside the case. Refugees from an older purge, hidden there by clockmakers before us. The clock has been an archive longer than it has been an instrument of the state. We only continued the trade."

She reached into the case, past the gearing she knew better than her own hands, and drew out the barrel.

It had taken her and Voss two days to extract it intact, disassembling supports that had not been moved since the last century, sweating over alignments, terrified of a slip. Now she held it up—a cylinder of brass, heavy as sin, dense with engraved script—and turned it slowly in the light.

The crowd made a sound. Not gasping; something lower than that, a collective intake like the sea drawing back before a wave. They could see the writing catch the sun as the barrel turned, lines of names flashing bright and sliding into shadow, bright and shadow, like a lighthouse beam sweeping a dark shore.

"The names struck from the ledger," Hazel said. "All of them. In the order they were struck."

She had rehearsed the reading alone, at night, whispering into the dark clock room so she would not break down in front of the town. It did no good. The first name undid her.

"Serrano, Tomás," she read, and the widow Serrano said, "Here," in a voice like a cracked bell, and answered every name after that with *here*, though she was not the only one—soon others were answering too, a call and response no one had planned, the town answering for its own absent dead, *here, here, he is here, she is here,* and Hazel read through her tears because stopping was impossible now, the names had momentum like the clock itself, and her voice was only the escapement letting them out one by one, measured, irreversible.

Okafor, Marta. And Marta herself, standing in the sunlight, said, "Here," loudly, laughing and crying at once, and her father cried out as if a wound had been closed.

Marchetti, Elias.

Hazel's voice failed on it. She stood with the barrel in her gloved hands and could not speak, and the crowd waited, and the widow Serrano answered for her—"Here"—and then others took it up, uncertainly at first, then firmly, though most of them had barely known the old exile with the foreign name, because by now the town understood the liturgy: no name goes unanswered. Here. He is here. Written in brass. Here.

There were sixty-three names. It took forty minutes to read them. Some belonged to people who had come back, like Marta, and stood among the crowd answering for themselves, which undid the listeners worse than the deaths did. Some belonged to families entirely extinguished, and those were answered by neighbors, by the schoolteacher, by strangers, by Father Anselmo in Latin under his breath. By the end, Hazel's arms ached from the barrel's weight and she would not have put it down for anything in the world.

When it was finished, the young captain stepped forward, cleared his throat, and offered some sentences about records being restored, about a commission, about the empire's regret. People listened politely, the way one listens to weather. Then the crowd did something Hazel had not anticipated: they did not disperse. They lingered around the open clock case as around an altar, touching the brass with fingertips, some of them weeping, one old man simply standing with his palm flat against the metal for a long time, and Hazel understood that she was watching something being rebuilt—not the clock, which had never stopped, but the thing underneath the clock, the fragile agreement that these people belonged to one another and to this place, an agreement that had been shattered and was now, joint by joint, being set back in its pivots.

* * *

The letter came a week later, forwarded through three countries and half a dozen hands, its envelope soft and travel-worn as an old glove.

Hazel took it up the hill unopened and sat in the gallery where she had watched the trains, and opened it there, in the place of worst memory, because she wanted the answer to arrive in that place and overwrite it.

The handwriting was unmistakable—small, upright, economical, the hand of a man who had spent seventy years engraving letters no larger than fleas.

*My dear Hazel,—*

*I have heard, by ways I will not commit to paper, of the Sunday reading. Sixty-three names, they say, and the town answering them like a congregation. I sat on a dock in another country and wept like a boy, and the fishermen let me, because fishermen understand salt water.*

*I am old and my hands are going—I dropped a balance spring last month, which I do not tell you to alarm you but because you will know what it means, and knowing, forgive it. I will not make the journey north again; the sea has had enough of carrying me. So let me write the thing plainly, which I have never managed aloud:*

*You kept your promise. You said you would not let my name be erased, and it is not erased—it is in brass, in daylight, in the mouths of your neighbors. Everything I taught you about the trade, you have surpassed, because I taught you to repair time and you have repaired something larger.*

*Wind the clock daily. Touch the names. Teach whoever comes next.*

*Your teacher, always,*
*Elias Marchetti*

P.S. *I am told my name was read aloud and answered "Here." Correct answer. Wherever I actually am is a detail.*

Hazel laughed until she cried, and cried until she laughed, sitting in the empty gallery with the gulls wheeling below her, and when she finally folded the letter it was spotted dark in places, and she decided the sea air had gotten into it, and knew no one would believe her.

* * *

That evening she stayed late in the clock room. She restarted the pendulum with two fingers, gently, the way one wakes a sleeper, and watched the escapement take up its ancient conversation—tick, and tock, and tick—the beat slightly fast, as always, needing her ear, her touch, her correction. Outside, dusk came down over the town, and lights appeared in windows that had been dark for years, and somewhere below a child was practicing a trumpet very badly and very persistently.

Hazel opened the case and put her hand flat against the barrel, against the sixty-three names, feeling the vibration of the running clock pass through them like pulses.

Then, from the pocket of her apron, she took out the small graver she had carried for four years and never used for this purpose, and set it on the bench beside a blank square of brass she had cut that morning.

There was room in the clock. There was always room. And she had been thinking, all week, about what the town had lost besides its people—about the pages missing from the ledger, the mushroom-people, the ones with no recorded history at all—and about a promise she had made once, in peacetime, that she was beginning to suspect she had never finished making.

The clock ticked. She picked up the graver.
