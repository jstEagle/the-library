## 1. The Last Crossing

There is a particular satisfaction, I have found, in being the person nobody looks at. The chauffeur is a piece of the motorcar to most minds—a uniform with hands—and so I have spent eleven years driving Lady Verity Kestrel across half of England while remaining as unexamined as the upholstery. People say astonishing things in front of a chauffeur. They confess. They scheme. They weep at railway stations and compose lies in the back seat as casually as one composes a shopping list. I have heard cabinet ministers call each other names that would make a fishwife blush, and I have watched Lady Verity, through the rearview mirror, watch them right back with those grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

Being no one is a profession, and I am very good at it.

On the last day of November, 1913, I drove her across the tidal causeway bridge for what would be the final crossing until spring, and I confess I was paying more attention to the water than to her instructions.

"Mind the tide, Edmund," she said anyway, because Lady Verity gave instructions the way other women breathed—automatically, and with the expectation of compliance.

"The tide is out two hours yet, my lady."

"Two hours and eleven minutes. The water does not round up for our convenience." She settled deeper into furs that had probably been old when I was born. "Neither, for that matter, does London."

I said nothing, which is also a professional skill. But the word *London* sat in the car with us like a third passenger.

The bridge itself deserves description, if only because it will be five months before anyone crosses it again. It was not a bridge in the graceful sense—no arches, no elegant span—but a long stone causeway raised just above the sand and shingle, connecting Kestrel's Harbor to the mainland at low tide and drowning entirely beneath six feet of grey water twice a day. In summer it carried carts of tourists and fish and gossip. In winter, when the storms came up the channel like something with intent, the causeway closed, and the harbor house became an island with seven souls on it, or nine this year counting Mrs. Tregowan's cat, who counted himself.

The water on either side lay in long silver ripples today, obedient-looking, the way dangerous things look when they are resting. I knew its rhythm by heart—the way it drained first from the eastern flats in braided channels, the way the gulls walked ahead of the ebb like little landlords inspecting their property. I had memorized that rhythm the way other people memorize psalms. There was something in repetition that comforted me: the same crossing, the same water, the same invisible woman at the wheel. Eleven years of being a fixture, and fixtures do not get hurt.

"You're driving slowly," Verity observed.

"I'm driving carefully."

"You are rehearsing your invisibility, which you mistake for carefulness." She said it without malice; she simply said things. That was the trouble with her. "Look at me a moment."

I looked in the mirror. Sixty-eight years old, and the illness had thinned her to the architecture of herself—sharp cheekbones, sharp eyes, a mouth built for pronouncements. She wore a hat with a heron's feather in it, because Lady Verity believed that dying was no excuse for dressing badly, though she had not yet admitted she was doing either.

"When the post comes tomorrow," she said, "you will bring it to me unopened."

This was new. For eleven years I had sorted her correspondence, because her hands were bad and my discretion was legendary. I read everything. Bills, threats, love letters from three decades of men who should have known better—I read them all, and she knew I read them, and neither of us had ever once acknowledged it aloud. That was the arrangement. That was the marriage, almost, that neither of us had entered into.

"Unopened," I repeated.

"There are letters coming that are not for your eyes." A pause, precise as a metronome. "Yet."

*Yet* is a word Lady Verity uses the way surgeons use scalpels. I put it away in the part of my mind where I kept all her words, and turned back to the road.

* * *

It was halfway across the causeway that I saw the letter.

Not hers—one of mine, or rather, one addressed to me, which amounted to the same rarity. My name—Miss E. Blackwood—written in a solicitor's clerk hand, sitting on the seat beside me where the stationmaster at Penlow must have handed it up while I was checking the tyres. I had driven forty miles with my own name lying there like a lit fuse, and never noticed.

Nobody wrote to me. My mother was dead; my brother thought I was dead in every way that mattered; and the last personal letter I'd received had been a circular advertising corsets, addressed to Miss Blackwood in a hand that hoped.

I wanted, badly, to open it then and there. Instead I drove, because Lady Verity did not tolerate stopped cars on her causeway, and because some small surviving instinct told me that once I read whatever was inside, the world I was driving through would stop being the same world.

The harbor house rose ahead of us against the slate sky—three storeys of dark granite, windows shuttered for winter except the study, where Mrs. Tregowan had already lit a lamp because Lady Verity's moods required illumination. Behind the house the lighthouse stood white and blind in the daytime, and beyond it the channel, where fishing boats were being hauled up for the season. The *Merry Alice* was still out—that would be young Cobb and his father making the last run to Penlow before the weather shut the door on all of us.

We crossed the causeway's highest shelf, where the stones were green with weed, and I felt the familiar small thrill I always felt here: the sensation of driving on the floor of the sea, temporarily permitted. Tomorrow the water would come back and take its road again, and we would be alone with ourselves until April.

Alone, I thought, and glanced at the letter. *Alone* had always meant safe. Alone had meant eleven years of reading history through other people's mail and telling myself I was only a spectator.

"Eyes forward, Miss Blackwood," said Lady Verity mildly.

She never called me Miss Blackwood. I looked at her again, and this time she was looking directly back, and there was something in her face I had not seen in eleven years of looking.

She was measuring me.

* * *

The car was unloaded, garaged, covered. Lady Verity went indoors on Mrs. Tregowan's arm, coughing once, twice—a dry sound she disguised as clearing her throat, though I had been keeping count since September and the count was rising. Then I stood by the motorcar in the yard with my letter, and opened it with fingers that were steadier than they had any right to be.

*Dear Miss Blackwood,* it began, in that tidy clerk hand. *We write at the instruction of Mr. Charles Kestrel of Lincoln's Inn Fields, who has retained this firm in the matter of Lady V. Kestrel's estate...*

Estate. Not *health*, not *wishes*—*estate*. The word has a smell to it, like coin polish and cut flowers at funerals.

*Mr. Kestrel intends to travel to Kestrel's Harbor before the closing of the causeway, accompanied by a stenographer, for the purpose of organizing Lady Kestrel's papers and affairs. We are instructed to inform you, as holder of keys to the household, of the arrival dates enclosed, and to request that no correspondence leave or enter the house save through Mr. Kestrel's office during the period of organization.*

*Yours faithfully,*

*Pentelow & Sons*

I read it three times. The gulls screamed over the chimneys. Somewhere below the causeway the tide had already turned, and was coming back for its road.

Charles Kestrel. The heir. The estranged heir, if village gossip could be credited, which it usually could, being invented by people with nothing better to do than improve on fact. Eleven years I had driven Lady Verity and eleven years I had heard perhaps forty references to her brother's grandson, each colder than a sermon in January. He was coming here. Before the bridge closed—which meant he had timed it, deliberately, to arrive with the tide and be sealed in with her, her papers, and her dying.

And a stenographer. A man whose entire profession was writing down what other people said and making it permanent—or making it disappear, depending on who held his wages.

I folded the letter and put it in my coat pocket, and found that my heart was behaving strangely—not frightened, exactly. Disturbed. The way water is disturbed when something large passes underneath: nothing visible, only a long swell where no swell should be.

For eleven years I had been no one, and it had been warm and safe and small. Now someone in London had written my name on an envelope, and someone else was coming across my sea to organize papers I had been reading in secret for a decade, and Lady Verity—who missed nothing, who forgave less—had called me *Miss Blackwood* as though I were about to become somebody.

The lamp in the study window flickered. Upstairs, faintly, through the shuttered glass, I heard her coughing again, and this time she did not disguise it at all.

I went in to fetch the key to the post room, and to think about how long a winter can be.
