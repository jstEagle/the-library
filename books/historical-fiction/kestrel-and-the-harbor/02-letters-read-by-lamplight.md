## 2. Letters Read by Lamplight

The harbor house in winter is a ship that has forgotten it isn't one. The wind finds every seam, the timbers speak in their sleep, and the whole structure leans—only slightly, only imaginatively—as though listening for orders from the sea. I had three days to learn its language before I would be its prisoner until spring, and I spent them the way I spent everything: usefully, invisibly, sorting other people's words.

The post had come over on the last cart before the bridge closed. Two sacks of it, forwarded from the London office, accumulated since October. Lady Verity Kestrel's correspondence did not pause for tides; it merely slowed, like everything else in her orbit, and gathered.

I set myself up in the morning room with the good lamp, the one with the green glass shade that made every letter look faintly nautical, and began the ritual I knew better than my own prayers. Sort first: parliamentary, charitable, threatening, begging. Then date-stamp what needed dating. Then read.

Yes. Read.

No one had ever told me not to. That was my story and I kept it polished the way sailors keep brass. When I'd taken the position six years ago—a lady novelist recommended by a lady novelist, which is how all respectable conspiracies begin—Lady Verity had said, "You'll need to know everything, Miss Blackwood, because I intend to remember nothing," and had swept out of the room before I could determine whether this was a joke. It was not a joke. It was a job description.

So I read. Petitions from mill girls in Blackburn whose names I knew by heart without ever having seen their faces. A furious four pages from an MP whose name I was sworn to forget. Christmas cards from women who signed themselves only with initials, because initials were safer than names in our year of our Lord 1913. I knew Verity Kestrel through her correspondence the way a doctor knows a patient through a keyhole—intimately, illegitimately, completely.

* * *

It was the third sack, near the bottom, damp where the canvas had met sleet, that held the solicitor's letter. Pritt & Halloway, Lincoln's Inn Fields. I recognized the firm the way one recognizes a smell from childhood—not the content, just the weight of it. Solicitors' letters have a particular fold, crisp as a shut door.

*Dear Lady Kestrel,—We write further to your instructions of the 14th ultimo respecting the estate...*

My instructions. She had instructions. I read on, telling myself I was doing it for her, the way I told myself everything.

*...we are instructed to advise that Mr. Charles Kestrel intends to travel north before the closure of the seasonal passage, accompanied by his legal representative and a stenographer, for the purpose of reviewing and organizing the family papers...*

I set the letter down on the green glass and watched the lamp shine through it, illuminating the watermark like a ghost in the paper itself.

Charles Kestrel. The name existed in my private index of Verity's life under a single cross-reference: *the nephew who does not write.* Sixteen years of correspondence had passed through my hands and never once had his hand appeared among them. Not a birthday card. Not a bill. In an age of estrangement he was remarkable chiefly for his thoroughness.

And now he was coming. Before the bridge closed—which meant within the week—with lawyers and a stenographer, to *organize* the family papers while the current Lady Kestrel still breathed above them.

I want to say I felt alarm. What I actually felt, in the shameful privacy of that lamplit room, was interest. The pure, cold, writerly interest of a woman who has spent her life watching history through other people's windows and has just seen a curtain move in a house she thought empty. Then came the guilt, punctual as rent, and then something worse underneath both: fear. Because if Charles Kestrel organized the papers, he organized my letters too—the ones I'd copied into my private notebooks, the record I'd kept for six years against my own conscience's advice. My invisibility had always depended on no one looking closely at the furniture. He was bringing people whose entire profession was looking closely.

I put the letter in the tray marked URGENT, which is to say I held it under my arm, took the back stairs two at a time, and knocked on Verity's door before my nerve could file an objection.

* * *

She was at the window in her dressing gown, watching the channel, and she did not turn when I entered. That was our arrangement in miniature: I could enter; she needn't acknowledge me. Intimacy without permission, both directions.

"The post," I said. "Something from Pritt & Halloway."

"Read it to me."

"I have the original."

"I know you do. Read it anyway." She turned then, and I saw what I had been refusing to see since we crossed the bridge three days ago. The cough had changed. I am not a medical woman, but I have read enough deathbeds—fictional ones mostly, which is its own education—to know the difference between a cough that is an inconvenience and a cough that is an occupant. This one had moved in. It had unpacked.

I read the letter aloud. My voice behaved beautifully. Inside, I was doing arithmetic: *before the closure of the seasonal passage* meant days. Meant the fishing boat from the mainland, meant strangers in the hall, meant—

"Well," said Verity, when I finished. "The vulture books passage."

"He's your heir."

"He's my brother's son, which is a different animal entirely. An heir inherits. A nephew *arrives*." She crossed to the fire and held her hands to it. They were beautiful hands, still; the ringed, imperious hands that had shaken half the platforms in England. "He'll want the papers tidy before I die. Papers are how the living bury the dead properly, Edmund. You of all people understand that."

"You could refuse him the study."

"On what grounds? That I'm not dead yet?" The laugh became the cough, mid-sound, and I stood there holding the solicitor's letter like a fool while she bent over the fire and fought for air. It went long. Longer than usual. When she straightened, there was blood-bright color high on her cheeks and she waved the letter away from me without looking. "Don't hover. You're a terrible hoverer. You do it silently, which is somehow worse."

"Should I fetch Dr. Parry from the village?"

"Dr. Parry is eighty-one years old and smells of lozenges. What he'll tell me is what I'd tell him." She sat, finally, and looked at me—really looked, which happened perhaps twice a year and always made me feel like a document someone had opened to the wrong page. "You've read ahead in the letter. I can always tell. Your chin does a thing."

"There's more," I admitted. "He's bringing a stenographer."

"A stenographer." She considered this with the expression she reserved for hostile amendments. "Someone to take down the family history as dictated. To get it right before anyone can object to the version." Her eyes moved to the window, to the flat grey channel and the far shore. "Then we haven't much time, you and I, for any version of our own."

"Our own?" I said. But she had turned back to the fire, and whatever sentence she had been constructing—one of those sentences of hers that arrived half-built, letting you furnish the rooms yourself—she abandoned it.

* * *

That night I did not sleep. I sat in my room above the scullery with my notebooks around me like a card game I was losing, and I reread six years of a woman's life in my own handwriting. Here was the spring she'd chained herself to the railings in Manchester and written to me about the weather instead, because the weather was safe and the railings were not. Here was the letter after her second operation, one line long: *Well. Onward.* Here were the mill girls again, and the MP again, and the slow accumulation of a person assembled entirely out of things she'd trusted to paper.

I knew her cough better than her doctor did. I knew which charities she funded anonymously and which grudges she fed openly. I knew she took her tea so strong it was nearly a foodstuff, that she had not cried at her brother's funeral but had gone home and burned his letters, that she was frightened of exactly one thing, and had named it to no one, and I had guessed at it nightly for years without ever once being right.

And she knew—I understood suddenly, sitting there in the small hours with the wind worrying the shutters—that she knew almost nothing true about me. She knew I was competent and quiet and from somewhere unglamorous in Yorkshire. She knew my penmanship. If I died tomorrow, she could not have described my face to a search party.

This is the bargain no one warns you about, when you make yourself useful to greatness. You get the whole of them—all the letters, all the drafts, the crossed-out courage and the uncrossed-out fear—and they get a service. I had chosen it. I chose it daily. There was comfort in it, real comfort, the comfort of being no one in particular in a century that was busy killing particular people.

But the solicitor's letter lay on my table where I'd left it, and its watermark glowed faintly even in the dark, and I thought: *a stenographer.* Someone else was coming whose whole art was listening, whose trade was making the invisible visible one shorthand stroke at a time. And I found that the prospect terrified me in a way the heir did not. Heirs want money. Stenographers want sentences. And I was, in the end, entirely made of sentences.

* * *

In the morning the cough was worse, and the lie about it had grown to fit. Verity came down to breakfast dressed as if for the platform, grey wool and the Kestrel garnets, and announced that we would spend the day preparing the study against invasion.

"We will do nothing of the sort," she corrected herself, halfway through the toast. "You will prepare the study. I will supervise from a chair."

What followed was the strangest day of my service. She directed me to shelve certain boxes high and deep, to lock others in the drawer of the desk whose key she wore on a ribbon at her wrist, and—once, quietly, when the housekeeper had gone to the kitchen—to take a thin bundle of letters tied in bootlace from the bottom of the coal-scuttle cupboard and put them, unread, into my own keeping.

"Unread," she repeated, watching my face with something that was almost amusement. "Can you manage that? For a fortnight? A miracle on the order of the loaves."

"What are they?"

"Insurance," she said. "Mine or yours, depending on how the season goes."

She laughed then—a real laugh, quickly converted by her chest into another of those long, tearing coughs—and pressed her handkerchief to her mouth, and waved me off with the other hand as though I were a servant and not the only person alive who knew what was in those letters' neighborhood. And I went, because that is what I did. I went down to the study with the bundle against my ribs like a smuggled heart, and I shelved and locked and dusted, and through the window I watched the gray water of the channel for the sail of a fishing boat coming north.

The tide tables said three days. The wind, which had begun that evening to carry ice in it, said sooner.

I stood in the study doorway at dusk, the room arranged for burial, the lamplight just catching the spines of forty years of Kestrel papers—and I confess that for the first time in six faithful, invisible years, I wished quite sincerely that the sea would fail to cooperate, that the boat would founder on some technicality, that winter would close around us like a fist and keep them out.

The wind rose. Somewhere down the coast, ice was forming early in the channel.

And three mornings later, while the kettle sang and Verity pretended her cough was a habit rather than a diagnosis, the boy from the village came running up the path with his collar up, shouting that there was a boat in the harbor, and that the gentleman aboard had asked particularly after the lady's *secretary*.

Her secretary. Not her driver. Not her shadow.

Somebody on that boat had been reading my lady's mail—and mine.
