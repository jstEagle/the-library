## 3. The Heir and His Pen

The fishing boat came in on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays are the day the world delivers things you didn't order.

I watched it from the kitchen window while Mrs. Penhale—our cook, our housekeeper, and by winter's end, I suspected, our entire remaining staff—beat a rug against the porch rail with the violence of a woman who had opinions about visitors and no one left to share them with.

"That'll be the nephew," she said, not turning around.

"Mr. Kestrel."

"Him too." Another blow to the rug. Dust rose like a small angry ghost.

Lady Verity had taken her breakfast upstairs and locked the study door behind her, which was her way of saying she knew what was coming and would meet it from behind oak rather than courtesy. I had learned to read Verity's doors the way sailors read weather. This one said: *batten down*.

The boat was one of Tregowan's, the old fisherman's, hired out for the season now that herring paid worse than ferrying solicitors. It came through the harbor mouth sideways, the way everything came here in November, and disgorged its passengers onto the wet steps of the quay: first a trunk, then a man who stepped ashore without waiting for help, then a second man carrying a case the size of a Bible box and looking, even at that distance, apologetic about it.

I went down to meet them because someone had to, and because I had discovered over four years of service that invisibility is not the same as absence. Ghosts do the fetching in this house.

* * *

Charles Kestrel was forty perhaps, with his great-aunt's height and none of her spine. He wore a coat cut in London for a man who intended to be photographed in it, and he surveyed the harbor—the gray water, the shuttered boathouse, the hills already going brown—as a man surveys property he has only just been assured is his.

"This is all of it?" he asked, meaning the house, the village, the headland, possibly the sky.

"All of what, sir?" I asked, because I couldn't help myself.

He looked at me then for the first time, taking in the plain dark coat, the gloves, the notebook I carried out of habit. "You're the secretary."

"I'm Miss Blackwood. I drive Lady Kestrel and manage her correspondence." A pause, measured to the half-second. "Among other things."

"My aunt employs a *chauffeuse*," he said, to no one in particular, in the tone of a man filing a grievance.

"In point of fact she employs a driver," I said. "The motor doesn't notice the difference."

Something flickered across his face—not amusement; Charles did not look like a man accustomed to being amused by servants—but he let it go, which told me something useful: he was here to win, not to quarrel with the help. Men like that are dangerous precisely because they underestimate you. They hand you their whole game while you're carrying their luggage.

The second man had stood back through all of this, rain beading on the shoulders of a coat that had been good five years ago and was honest about it now. He was younger than Charles, thinner, with ink on the side of his right hand the way schoolmasters have chalk dust—a permanent condition, not an accident. When Charles turned away to issue instructions to Tregowan about the trunk, the man looked at me.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have. He didn't glance at me. He didn't look through me, the way four years of gentlemen in motor cars had taught me to expect. He looked *at* me—at my face, at whatever it was doing—and then he inclined his head slightly, the greeting you give an equal whose name you don't yet know.

It had been so long since anyone did that that I nearly checked behind me.

"You'd be Mr. Ashworth," I said.

"The stenographer, yes." His voice was quiet, pitched under Charles's, a voice trained for taking dictation and not interrupting it. "You must be Miss Blackwood. Mr. Kestrel's letters mentioned you."

"Flatteringly?"

"With precision," he said, and there—it was almost a smile, quickly folded away, but I had seen the machinery of it work. "He wrote that you were 'protective of the study.' Underlined twice."

"He does love his pen," I said.

Ashworth looked down at the case in his own hands. "So I'm told."

* * *

Luncheon was a strained performance of civilization. Mrs. Penhale produced a cold chicken that had given its life before learning of the honor awaiting it, and Charles talked about London—about the clubs, the trains, the appalling slowness of correspondence with the north—while Lady Verity sat at the head of the table looking at him the way a cat looks at a dog that has been invited onto its sofa.

"You've come at a difficult season," she said. "The bridge closes Friday. After that we're an island until March."

"So I understand. Which is why I wished to arrive before it did. Time is—" he smiled at his own delicacy— "a consideration, given your health."

"My health," said Verity, "is my own consideration. The rest of you may consider the estate."

"Aunt Verity." He set down his fork with the care of a man laying a card. "There are arrangements to be made. Papers to be put in order. The family history ought properly to be—organized—while there is still someone living who remembers it accurately."

*I* remembered it accurately. I remembered every letter that had passed through her hands for four years, every bill, every threat, every subscription to the Cause, every name scratched out and rewritten. But I passed the potatoes and said nothing, because invisibility has rules, and rule one is: the potatoes come first.

"And who will perform this organizing?" Verity asked.

"Mr. Ashworth. A professional. Discreet, thorough." Charles gestured, and Ashworth, who had been eating in the modest silence of a man aware he was furniture, straightened slightly. "He will require access to the study. And to the ledgers, the correspondence, the—family papers generally."

The clock ticked. Verity's cough ticked with it, once, twice, pressed down behind her napkin like a secret.

"The study," she said pleasantly, "is locked."

"For how long?"

"Why, indefinitely, dear. It's such a comfort, at my age, to have a room of one's own that stays exactly where one leaves it."

I poured the tea and marveled, as I had marveled for four years, at how completely she could weaponize an afternoon. Charles heard a dotty old woman being fond of her clutter. I heard a fortress closing its gate.

* * *

That evening I intercepted the notes. I am not proud of it. I am also not sorry.

They were staying in the blue rooms, the ones that caught whatever light November offered, and after dinner Charles sent Ashworth down to the study door—to test the lock, presumably, or to stand outside it feeling embarrassed. Instead Ashworth went to the window of the drawing room where I was mending a lamp wick, saw me, and—this was the interesting part—did not pretend he hadn't.

Later, a note came up on a tray meant for Verity, addressed to Charles, and I read it in the corridor because it was addressed in a hand I recognized as belonging to a man with permanent ink, and because I have never in my life successfully resisted a sealed letter. Verity made me what I am.

*"Sir,—Regarding scope: I understand the instruction to prepare a full catalogue of Lady Kestrel's papers. I would ask that the catalogue proceed in stages, beginning with the financial records, so that the family narrative may be drafted from settled material. I note your instruction re: correspondence dated prior to 1890 and will act accordingly.—A."*

Which was stenographer for: *you want me to bury something, and I know it.*

And beneath it, in Charles's hand, returned like homework: *"Organize. That is the word. Do the word."*

Organize. The way you organize a drawer. The way you organize a witness list down to nothing.

I put the note back on the tray and stood in the dark corridor feeling the shape of the winter ahead of me: two men in my house, one of them holding a shovel and calling it a pen, and between us a locked door with a dying woman on one side of it and forty years of paper on the other.

* * *

I found Ashworth the next morning in the boathouse, which he had apparently claimed as his territory—the one room in the house without a lock worth picking and therefore, in his arithmetic, safe. He had set up a folding table with the neatness of a man who alphabetizes his regrets: pens in a row, blotting paper squared, the Bible-box case open to reveal typed sheets in careful stacks.

"You're not in the study," I said from the doorway.

"The study is locked."

"You're not breaking the lock either. You could. There are four keys in this house and three people who know where they live."

He set down his pen and considered me again—that same unhurried seeing, which was becoming either a comfort or a threat, I hadn't decided. "Miss Blackwood. May I ask you a professional question?"

"You may ask."

"When a man is hired to write a family history, and the family keeps sending him lists of what the history must contain—" he tapped the typed stack— "at what point does it stop being a history?"

I leaned against the doorframe. Rain worked at the water beyond him, patient as everything else in this place.

"When it starts having an ending before it begins," I said.

He nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed a figure he'd double-checked. "That's what I thought."

We stood there in the smell of tar and old rope, two people paid to handle other people's truths, each trying to determine whether the other was a colleague or a hazard. His eyes went to my hands—I hadn't noticed I'd clenched them—and back to my face, and he waited. Nobody waits, normally. People talk past me, around me, over me. He waited as though what I said next might actually matter.

"The correspondence before 1890," I said carefully. "What does Mr. Kestrel think is in it?"

"I don't know." He said it flatly, honestly, and then, quieter: "But I intend to find out before I organize anything."

Above us, on the cliff path, I heard the front door of the house open and Charles's voice carry down the wind, asking for his aunt, asking for the keys, asking for everything. Ashworth heard it too. Neither of us moved.

"Miss Blackwood," he said, "I think you and I are going to be reading the same book this winter."

"We may disagree about the ending."

"Then we'll take minutes," he said, and this time the smile stayed long enough for me to see it properly, and I understood that the winter had just become a great deal more complicated—and that for the first time in four years, I minded less than I should have.
