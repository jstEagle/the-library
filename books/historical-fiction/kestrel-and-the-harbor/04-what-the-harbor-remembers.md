## 4. What the Harbor Remembers

Edmund

Mr. Ashworth worked the way tide worked—patiently, thoroughly, and with an unsettling indifference to what he disturbed.

I had taken to positioning myself in the study's doorway at odd hours, a teapot or a stack of linen as my excuse, watching him arrange Lady Verity's papers into neat chronological rows. He never asked me to leave. That was the first curious thing about him. Charles Kestrel barked me out of rooms on principle, as though my presence might contaminate the air, but Ashworth simply glanced up when I appeared, gave me a nod of acknowledgment—the kind one gives a colleague—and returned to his work.

The second curious thing was the folders he didn't touch.

He catalogued everything: correspondence from 1909 to 1913, household accounts, the suffrage correspondence that made Charles's jaw tighten whenever it was mentioned aloud. But there was a shelf behind Verity's desk—old ledgers, mostly, spines cracked and salt-damp—and whenever Ashworth reached that section of his circuit, his hands would pause, hover, and move elsewhere. Every time. With the regularity of a heartbeat.

You develop an eye for avoidance when your whole life has been built on it. I knew what a man pretending not to see something looked like, because I had been one in mirrors for thirty years.

"You've skipped the Hollis ledgers twice today," I said, on the fourth morning, mostly to see what would happen to his face.

He did not startle. He set down his pen, squared a page of notes, and looked at me with those gray, unhurried eyes. "Have I?"

"The red ones. 1881 through 1887."

"Those are before my brief," he said mildly. "I'm organizing Lady Verity's current affairs for Mr. Kestrel. The past is past." And then—here was the third curious thing—he added, so quietly I nearly missed it: "Though it does seem to have left rather a lot of paperwork behind."

I retreated before my own face could betray me, which is my singular talent, and went out to drive Verity to the village, where she intended to terrorize the postmistress about the delayed London mails. The harbor road was iced at its edges. The water beyond the seawall lay flat and pewter-colored, keeping its accounts private, the way everything and everyone in this house seemed to do.

Verity coughed twice on the way down and blamed the wind. I pretended to believe her, because that is the arrangement we have never spoken of: she pretends to be well, I pretend to be no one, and between us we maintain a fiction sturdy enough to drive across at low tide.

In the village, while she interrogated the postmistress, I waited by the fish sheds where old Tom Cready sat mending nets with fingers like knotted rope.

"They say the heir's brought his own pen-pusher," he said, without looking up. "Fellow who writes things down."

"He writes things down," I agreed.

"Huh." A knot pulled tight. "Kestrels always did prefer their own version of things written down. My father used to say the only true history of this coast is underwater." He squinted at the horizon. "My mother packed the crates for the Meridian boats back in '82. Before Mrs.—before the old lady's mother took over the counting house."

"Took over?" I said. "I understood the Kestrel ladies kept more to themselves."

Tom's hands stopped. For a moment he looked at me properly, the way you look at someone to determine whether they're safe to drown. Then Verity's voice rang out sharp from the post office, calling my name like a summons, and his hands resumed their work.

"Marguerite," he said, to the net, not to me. "Her name was Marguerite. Ask any Bible in this village but the Kestrel one."

* * *

Ashworth

The stenographer's first rule: record what is said, not what is meant. The second rule, unwritten but absolute: never let them see which one interests you.

I broke the second rule within a week of arriving at Harbor House.

It was the secretary who did it. Miss Blackwood—or Edmund, though no one here called her anything but Blackwood, as if a surname could be a disguise—stood in the doorway of the study every morning with some prop or other, and watched me work with an attention she probably believed was invisible. It was not. She watched the way an auditor watches: looking for the discrepancy, the skipped line, the number that doesn't reconcile.

She found mine by Thursday.

"The red ones. 1881 through 1887." She said it without accusation, almost conversationally, and then left before I could arrange my face—which was fortunate, because my face had stopped taking instructions.

Here is what I should have recorded in my private notebook that night: *Mr. Kestrel's instructions regarding the family papers are specific. Catalogue all correspondence after 1890. Set aside anything earlier for separate collection. Do not read the separate collection.*

Here is what I actually wrote: *Miss B. sees everything. God help whoever she decides to look at properly.*

Charles wanted a history, and he wanted it clean. His instructions were delivered over brandy the second night, with the fire burning low and his voice pitched to the register men use when they're about to make a problem someone else's. "A chronicle, Ashworth. Tasteful. Grandfather's shipping ventures, Grandmother's good works, Aunt Verity's—enthusiasms. Nothing about the counting house years, nothing about the Meridian business, nothing about—" he waved his glass, "—any of that. You'll have full access. You'll know exactly what to leave out, because I'll tell you what to leave out."

"And if Lady Verity objects to the omissions?"

"Aunt Verity will be dead by spring," he said, with the mildness of a weather report. "Dead women don't litigate."

I said nothing, which is my profession. I wrote nothing down, which is my art. And I began, the next morning, to catalogue a great many documents with scrupulous accuracy while systematically not reading a shelf of red-ledgered years—until a woman in the doorway named the exact shape of my evasion out loud.

That afternoon I walked into the village, ostensibly to post letters for Charles. Ostensibly is most of a stenographer's life. I bought tobacco from the chandlery and let the shopkeeper talk, and talk he did, the way coastal people do when they smell a listener from the mainland.

"Cataloguing the old lady's papers, are you?" he said. "Well. Mind how you write it. Last fellow who wrote down the truth about the Kestrels drowned off the Meridian light, and he'd been sailing these waters thirty years."

"Is that so."

"That's so." He wrapped my tobacco with unnecessary care. "Course, drowning's common enough here. Like silence."

On the walk back the tide was turning, and the causeway stones were emerging one by one from the gray water like a sentence being slowly revealed. I stood and watched it happen and thought: *this is what I am doing with Charles's money.* Revealing a sentence stone by stone. Or covering one. At that moment I honestly could not have told you which.

There was one thing I hadn't put in any notebook, public or private. On my third day, moving a stack of Verity's correspondence to dust beneath it, I had dislodged a loose floorboard near the window seat—not hidden, exactly; hidden implies effort—and seen the edge of oilcloth, and beneath the oilcloth, bundles of letters tied in faded ribbon, addressed in a hand I did not recognize, dated 1884.

I had put the board back. I had dusted the sill. I had gone to lunch.

And every day since, when Miss Blackwood appears in the doorway with her teapot and her linens, I wonder whether she knows they're there, and whether she knows that I know, and which of us the letters are waiting for.

* * *

Edmund

That evening I checked the family Bible.

It lived on the hall stand under a glass dome, as Bibles in ambitious houses do—displayed rather than read, faith as furniture. The Kestrel marriages and births marched down its pages in copperplate: Ambrose Kestrel m. Constance Hale, 1878. Verity, born 1880. All serene, all sealed.

But Tom Cready had said *Marguerite*, and Tom Cready had packed crates for boats whose name I had now heard twice—once from a fisherman, once from a ledger spine I wasn't supposed to notice—and twice is a pattern, and patterns are the most dangerous form of hope.

Constance Hale. No Marguerite anywhere. Unless Marguerite had been a middle name, a pet name, a first marriage, a ghost. Unless the copperplate lied. Bibles don't lie; hands do. Someone had dipped a pen and chosen what to write, and choosing is the beginning of every cover-up, and I felt the thrill rise in me like water under a causeway—that hot, shameful delight of a woman who has spent her life reading other people's mail and finally found a letter addressed to *her*, in the only sense that matters: addressed to her attention, her patience, her long apprenticeship in invisibility.

And underneath the thrill, the guilt, cold as the channel. Because wanting a secret is already a kind of betrayal. Verity had let me read her letters for six years on the understanding that I was furniture. Furniture that develops opinions about the family Bible is furniture on its way to the bonfire.

She came down the stairs as I closed the dome, coughing into a handkerchief, and paused at the sight of me standing there.

"Reading scripture, Blackwood?" Her voice was rougher than usual.

"Checking dates," I said.

"Dangerous habit." Her eyes, bright with fever or amusement or both, held mine a moment too long. "Dates have a way of disagreeing with each other in this house. Ask anyone but me."

She went on into the drawing room. Through the study door I could hear Ashworth's pen moving, steady as the tide, recording everything except the red ledgers, and I stood in the hall between the two of them—the woman who chose what to remember and the man paid to choose what to forget—understanding suddenly that the loose floorboard under the window seat had been discovered by at least two other pairs of hands besides mine.

The next morning, Ashworth met my eyes across the breakfast table and said, quietly, "If you ever find yourself checking dates again, Miss Blackwood—you might check the window seat first."

Then Charles rang for more coffee, and we both became furniture again.
