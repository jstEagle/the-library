## 7. The Stenographer's Dilemma

There is a particular kind of silence a man must learn when his profession is to write down what powerful people say. I had mastered it years ago—hands still, eyes lowered, the pen moving only when spoken to. For six days under Charles Kestrel's roof, I had been the most invisible man in that house.

Or so I believed, until I caught Miss Blackwood watching me skip the third folder of the morning and understood that she had been counting my omissions the way a banker counts coins.

But I am ahead of myself. Let me begin where the trouble began, which was at half past seven, with Charles in his dressing gown and a decanter that had no business being open before breakfast.

"Ashworth." He said my name the way one identifies an umbrella. "I want a history written. The family history. Properly done."

"Genealogical, sir?"

"Narrative," he said, pouring. "My aunt is dying—that is not sentiment, it is arithmetic—and when she goes there will be questions. Journalists, cranks, suffragists with too much time and my aunt's mailing list. I want a document. The Kestrels from 1840 to the present. Shipping, philanthropy, the bridge, the school she built to flatter herself. Respectable. Boring. A history so dull no one will ever read past the second page."

"And the material in the study?"

"Organized," he said, with the mild finality of a man closing a drawer on something live. "Curated. You know your work, Ashworth; do it well and there's a position for you in London come spring."

He said it as though offering me promotion. What he was offering was authorship of a lie, typeset in my own careful hand. I wrote *The Kestrels: A Family History* across a fresh page that morning, and beneath it, because even my treachery required structure, a table of contents composed entirely of things I would have to make true by leaving everything else out.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about falsifying history: it is mostly typing. Charles supplied the shape of the story he wanted—the Kestrels as honest merchants raised up by diligence and sea salt—and I supplied the connective tissue. I wrote that Josiah Kestrel, Lady Verity's father, was "a man of exacting commercial ethics." I wrote that the loss of the barque *Providence* in 1884 "was met with transparency and full compensation to all parties."

I knew about the *Providence*. I had known since my second week, when cataloguing Verity's ledgers I found a bundle behind them—not hidden, exactly, but shelved with the deliberate care of a woman who wanted the evidence preserved and did not dare to file it. Letters. A clerk named Thomas Wren, transported for a fraud committed with his signature and none of his profit. Payments. A marriage arranged between scandal and respectability like two halves of a hull.

I also knew that Miss Edmund Blackwood had found them first, because I had watched her find them—or rather, watched her emerge from the study that afternoon wearing the expression of a woman who has touched a wire and felt current. And I had watched Lady Verity follow her in, and heard nothing more, which frightened me worse than anything.

Charles believed he had hired a stenographer. In truth he had hired the only other person in the house who read everything, and neither of us had yet confessed to the other.

* * *

I found her in the boathouse, because by the sixth day I had learned to look for her everywhere, which is its own confession.

Edmund sat on the upturned skiff with her coat buttoned wrong—one button off, the whole line pulled crooked—and a folder open on her knees. Outside, the harbor had begun its winter transformation; thin sheets of ice lapped at the pilings like paper pressed to a wound.

"You've been writing something," she said, without looking up. She had seen my hand, apparently, or my posture, or some tell I hadn't known I carried. Being observed by her was an experience I kept failing to prepare for. "You hold the pen differently when you're inventing. Looser. Guilty."

"Is there a great deal of guilty penmanship in this harbor?"

"Years of it," she said. "Most of it mine."

She looked at me then, and I understood that whatever I decided in the next thirty seconds would decide considerably more than a career. I took the draft from my case and held it out.

"The chapter on 1884," I said. "Read it before you read anything else into me."

She read. I watched her face go through winter—the jaw tightening at *exacting commercial ethics*, the small exhaled curse at *full compensation*. When she finished she set the pages down on the skiff with exaggerated gentleness, as though they might bite.

"It's very good," she said quietly. "That's the obscene part. It reads like truth. Anyone would believe it."

"I believe almost no one will ever check it. That was the specification."

"And you wrote it anyway."

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was a fair question and deserved better than the answer I'd rehearsed. I sat down on the skiff beside her, which put us shoulder to shoulder in the cold dark of the boathouse, smelling of tar and frozen rope.

"Because I have spent nine years being the instrument of other people's versions," I said. "Depositions, board minutes, a peer's memoirs that required the removal of two entire decades. You get good at it. You get paid well for it. You tell yourself the record was already rotten and you're only arranging the furniture." I turned toward her. "And then I came here, and within a fortnight I was skipping folders so that a dead clerk might keep one unburned page somewhere, and lying awake wondering how you could bear to serve that woman and copy her letters and say nothing either."

Her breath caught. "You knew about—"

"Behind the ledgers. Yes. Since November." I let the admission sit in the freezing air between us. "I've known for weeks, Edmund. I read what you'd moved. I said nothing. To him or to you. I don't know which makes me the greater coward."

For a long moment the only sound was the ice clicking against the pilings. Then she laughed—a short, unsteady, entirely unsanctimonious laugh.

"Two cowards in a boathouse," she said. "With one draft of lies and one folder of truth between them. What are we going to do, Mr. Ashworth? We can't both be invisible. There isn't room."

"No," I agreed. "I've checked. The boathouse is quite visible."

"Your draft," she said. "What happens to it if you don't finish it?"

"He finishes it himself, badly, or hires someone worse. It doesn't matter whether I write the lie. It matters whether the truth survives me."

Something changed in her face then—not softening; Edmund did not soften so much as decide. She took my hand, turned it palm up, and laid the folder of Thomas Wren's letters upon it, closing my fingers over the paper like a oath administered.

"Then be the record," she said. "All of it. Even the part where you helped."

I am a man whose trade is exact transcription, and I want it entered accurately that I kissed her first. That she permitted it instantly. That her coat was still buttoned wrong and I corrected it afterward, one button, absurdly tender about it, and that she let me—which I understood to be a larger permission than the kiss.

We were perhaps four seconds into whatever came next when the channel spoke.

Not cracked—*detonated*. The ice that had been creeping across the narrows for a week gave way all at once with a sound like a rifle shot multiplied, a long tearing boom that shuddered through the pilings and set the hanging boat hooks swinging. We broke apart. Edmund was on her feet before I'd finished standing, and by the time we reached the boathouse door the whole gray reach of water was shrugging itself loose, floes grinding and tilting in the dark.

"That's early," she said. Her voice had gone flat and quick. "Weeks early. The ferry won't run on that, but a fishing boat could get out. Or in."

And we both arrived at the same thought simultaneously, because we had become the kind of people who thought in tandem now: Charles had booked passage on the first thing that floated. Whatever he intended to send away from this harbor—papers, witnesses, the last of the record—he could send it weeks sooner than anyone had planned.

"Verity," Edmund said.

She was already running for the house, folder clutched to her chest, and I followed, the draft of the lie fluttering forgotten somewhere behind us in the boathouse dark, and above us the ice kept breaking, louder and louder, like the winter clearing its throat to speak.
