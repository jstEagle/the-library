## 8. Burning Season

There is a particular smell to paper burning in a grate that has never before been asked to burn anything but driftwood and last year's gulls' nests of correspondence. It is sweeter than you'd expect. That's the obscenity of it — the sweetness.

I smelled it from the kitchen passage on the third morning after the ice cracked early across the channel, and I knew before I reached the study door exactly what it was, because I had spent six years learning the grammar of this house and a house tells you everything if you listen. The fire was lit at half past nine instead of evening. The chimney drew with a different note when fed fresh vellum. And Charles Kestrel had put on his good waistcoat for the occasion, which was how I knew he considered it an event.

He looked up when I came in with the coal scuttle I did not need to be carrying. "Good morning, Miss Blackwood."

"Mr. Kestrel." I set the scuttle down as though it had always been my errand. "Lady Verity's breakfast is ready."

"She took tea in her room." He turned back to the grate. In his hands was a folder from the second drawer of the ledger cabinet — I knew the drawer by its sticking hinge — and he fed pages into the flames one at a time, unhurried, a man buttering toast.

"You'll find the drafts are poor fuel," I said. "Salt air. Everything here burns damp."

"Then I shall burn slowly," he said pleasantly, "and get through all of it."

I want it recorded somewhere other than my own conscience that I did not flinch, though every nerve I owned was screaming *take them, run*. Instead I said something about the vicar expecting the household accounts by Friday, and I left the room at a servant's pace while inside me something ran flat out toward the sea.

* * *

Here is what I had, by then: three weeks of watching Ashworth catalogue the study by day and Charles re-catalogue it by night; two letters intercepted out of the morning post — one from a firm of London historians retained, per Charles's instruction, to prepare "a definitive and discreet family record"; one from Mr. Ashworth's own employers confirming his fee and adding, in a postscript that made my blood go cold and hot at once, *"the lady's memory being unreliable, reliance upon documents is discouraged."*

And here is what Verity had, by then: less time than any of us pretended.

She had stopped coming down to the study. She received me in her room now, propped among pillows like some splendid, furious figurehead, her cough arriving in weather systems. She knew what Charles was doing — of course she knew; she had taught him nothing and he had learned it anyway, which is the tragedy of sons — and when I told her, she laughed until the laugh became the cough and the cough became silence.

"He burns what he cannot rewrite," she said at last, wiping her eyes with a hand that shook only slightly more than usual. "His grandfather's trick. My father's trick. It has worked twice before, Miss Blackwood. Why should it fail a third time?"

"Because I am reading faster than he is burning," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment. Whatever passed over her face, she had hidden it behind sixty years of practice, but not quite fast enough. It might have been pride. It might have been pity.

"Then read quickly," she said, "and forgive yourself later. You will need the forgiveness. Everyone who touches this history does."

* * *

The lighthouse stands on the north point, forty minutes' walk from the house along the shingle, and it has been dark and unstaffed since the Trinity House men decommissioned it eleven years ago. The door's lock surrendered years ago. Inside, the spiral stair still held its smell of lamp-oil and cold iron, and the lamp room at the top had one intact pane of glass through which the whole gray harbor arranged itself like a painting nobody had commissioned.

It was not a clever hiding place. It was the only one I had.

I went at dusk, when the household settled into its evening suspicion, and I went carrying what I could: the 1884 letter in which Verity's father described the *Marisol*'s cargo in terms no honest manifest would survive; the deposition of the clerk, Thomas Wren, taken before the inquiry and never entered into it; the settlement papers showing where Wren's silence had gone — a cottage in Whitby, an annuity, and then, two years on, a grave with a very convenient date on it. And the confession itself. The confession. Verity's father's own hand, eight pages, written the winter before he died, naming himself, naming the bribes, naming the marriage that had sealed the Kestrels to the money and the Wrens to the blame.

I wrapped the confession in oilcloth first. I told myself this was because of the damp. It was because it felt like handling a body.

Three trips the first week. Four the second. I learned the stair in the dark, learned which treads groaned, learned to stand in the lamp room with the wind coming through the broken panes and feel, briefly, like a keeper of something again. The pages lived in a fish crate beneath the rusted base of the old lamp assembly, under a layer of netting that smelled honestly of herring and therefore fooled everyone, including, most days, me.

On the fifth trip I found Ashworth waiting at the bottom of the stair.

I nearly went backward off the shingle. He caught my elbow — the first time he had touched me since the boathouse, since the ice cracked and we stepped apart into our separate duties — and said nothing, which was somehow worse than accusation.

"How long," I said.

"Since your second trip. You walk like a woman delivering state secrets, Miss Blackwood. You always have. You just used to deliver them indoors."

"And you've said nothing to Charles."

"I draft his sanitized histories by day." His voice was low and even and it cost him something; I heard the cost. "I am not going to hand him the index of everything he's missed by night."

"Why?" It came out harder than I meant it. "You're paid by the same purse."

"Yes." He looked up at the dark tower, then back at me, and whatever was in his face I could not file it anywhere, it fit no category I owned. "I keep telling myself it's professional habit. A stenographer hates an inaccurate record the way a surgeon hates dirt. But that isn't all of it, and we both know what the rest of it is, and there isn't time tonight to say it properly. Go up. I'll watch the path."

So I climbed, with my heart doing things no historical record would ever contain, and I understood that I had acquired an accomplice, and that accomplices are how invisible people become legible — one witness at a time.

* * *

The village turned the week the burning began in earnest.

I had thought, foolishly, that truth behaves like weather — that once it arrives, everyone gets wet together. But the harbor runs on Kestrel wages. The boatyard, the smokehouse, half the cottages on the front, the very coal in the vicar's grate: all of it flowed downstream from the fortune whose foundations I was hauling up into a lighthouse by night. When Mrs. Tregale, the fisherman's widow, stopped me outside the chapel and said, carefully, kindly, looking past my shoulder at the road, *"Miss Blackwood, my Tom was a Kestrel man thirty years, and whatever's in them boxes, it won't warm anyone's stove come January"* — I understood that neutrality, in a place like this, is not indifference. It is survival wearing a polite face.

Even Mrs. Penhale at the shop weighed my sugar with a new exactness. Even the vicar, dear wavering Mr. Coby, began receiving me with the expression of a man who hoped I would not say anything requiring him to have an opinion.

Only the widow Tregale's daughter, Ruth, sixteen and furious at the world in the way I remembered being, asked me outright, on the harbor wall, in the wind: "Is it true? About the clerk?"

"It's true," I said. "I can prove part of it. I'm trying to prove all of it."

She nodded slowly, looking out at the gray water. "Grandfather said the Wren name was cursed. He wouldn't fish the north ground because of it." She turned to me. "If you need a witness someday, I remember what he said. Word for word."

The record, I thought, climbing home. It keeps itself in strange vessels. Fishermen's granddaughters. Lighthouse crates. Stenographers with inconvenient consciences.

* * *

Charles burned the ledgers on the ninth day.

I know the day precisely because Verity's fever spiked that same afternoon and Dr. Mowbray came over the ice road — the bridge long shut, the channel frozen hard enough now to walk, which meant the mainland had come closer and further away at once — and pronounced what we all already knew in the gentlest available language. Days. Perhaps a fortnight. She sent the doctor away and demanded her writing desk brought up, and dictated codicils in a voice like a saw going through green wood.

That night the study chimney smoked black until two in the morning, and in the morning the second drawer of the ledger cabinet hung open and empty, and the 1880s correspondence — all of it, the *Marisol*, the inquiry, the settlements — was ash in the grate, sifted and stirred so thoroughly that no page corner survived larger than a postage stamp.

But the confession was not there. The confession had been in my crate under a herring net for eleven days, and Charles did not know it existed, because Verity had never told him and I had never told anyone but the dark and one stenographer.

Or so I believed until I made the trip that night and found the crate disturbed. Netting moved. Oilcloth refolded — neatly, carefully, by someone who folded things for a living.

The confession was gone.

I stood in that freezing lamp room with the wind coming through the broken glass and the harbor lying black below, and I did the arithmetic of despair: every copy of the central document in the case against the Kestrel fortune, gone. Burned, or locked in Charles's dispatch box awaiting the London historians. Either way, gone. Three weeks of night walks, of salt-stiff fingers, of rehearsing testimony to the sound of my own heartbeat — and the spine of the thing snapped in my hands.

I did not cry. I want that recorded too. What I did was worse: I sat down on the cold iron of the stair and laughed, quietly, the way Verity laughs, and heard her voice in my own mouth saying *you will need the forgiveness, everyone who touches this history does.*

Footsteps on the shingle. Ashworth, breath smoking, face white in the starlight, taking the ladder two rungs at a time.

"Edmund." He had never used my Christian name; it landed like a dropped lantern. "The confession. Did you—"

"It's gone." My voice sounded far away, borrowed. "Someone took it."

"No." He gripped the rail. "No — I came to tell you. Charles has it. He found your trips out — he's had Ruth Tregale watched since the harbor wall, the girl talked to her mother, her mother mentioned it to the housekeeper — he knows about the lighthouse, Edmund, and he's riding to Whitby tomorrow on the ice road to meet the historians, and he's taking *everything*, and Verity—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "Verity sent for you. Twenty minutes ago. She said—" his voice failed its composure entirely — "she said, *tell Miss Blackwood to bring the record and come quickly, there isn't much night left.*"

Below us the harbor lay frozen solid, white and silent, the closed bridge a black line against the ice. Somewhere beyond it, the season's first ferry waited in its shed like a promise.

We stood a moment longer in the dark tower, the two of us, holding nothing between us but each other.

Then I took up my empty crate, and we went down together into the cold.
