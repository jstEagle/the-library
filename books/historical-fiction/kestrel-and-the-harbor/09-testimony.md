## 9. Testimony

I had driven Lady Verity Kestrel across that tidal bridge for six years, and in all that time I had learned precisely one skill better than driving: how to be unremarkable in a room. I could pour tea without being seen doing it. I could stand at a door's edge until people forgot the door had an edge. I had made a career of being the woman holding the coat, and I had told myself it was dignity, this erasure, the way you might call a small cold room *cozy*.

At seven o'clock on the morning Verity began her last day, I stood in the servants' passage with four rescued pages hidden in my bodice and realized I was about to be remarkable whether I liked it or not.

The ice on the channel had cracked in the night—a long, gun-shot report that had woken the whole house. Old Mrs. Pentreath, our cook, swore it was an omen. I chose to swear it was an invitation.

"You'll want the great hall," Ashworth said quietly when I found him in the corridor, already dressed, already holding his stenographer's case as though he'd been waiting for me since midnight. His eyes were red-rimmed. He had not slept either. "And you'll want witnesses who can't be bought."

"I have a vicar and a fisherman's widow."

"That's a start." He hesitated, and for one absurd moment he looked younger than his years, like a boy caught with contraband. "Edmund. There's something you should know before you do this. Something I should have told you days ago."

"If it's another confession, Mr. Ashworth, I'll ask you to hold it until after. My courage is a finite ration this morning."

"It will keep," he said. But his hand found my elbow—briefly, gravely, the way one steadies another person on a ship's deck—and it did not feel like something that would keep. It felt like ballast.

* * *

The great hall of Harbor House had been built for the kind of family that expected to be listened to: oak rafters, a hearth big enough to roast an ox, portraits of Kestrels glaring down at three centuries of their own good fortune. I had arranged chairs in a half-circle facing the long table, and by half past eight they filled.

Vicar Tregowan came first, smelling of church damp and moral apprehension. He was a decent man, which is to say a frightened one; decency and fear make an uncomfortable fit in a narrow cassock. Mrs. Sorrell arrived next in her widow's black, hands still stiff from gutting fish at dawn, because I had gone to her cottage myself and said, *You knew the clerk's daughter. You buried her mother under the wrong name. Come and watch me put it right.* She came without a word, which from Mrs. Sorrell was a sermon.

Then the household—Mrs. Pentreath, the girls from the scullery, old Hobbs who kept the lamps—all of them standing along the walls because none of them could quite believe they were allowed furniture.

Charles Kestrel came down last, and he came like weather. He had dressed carefully for battle: dark wool, a lawyer's smile borrowed from Mr. Prew, the solicitor who trailed him like a bad smell in good tailoring. Charles looked at the half-circle of chairs, at the vicar, at the widow, and laughed—one short bark with nothing in it but arithmetic.

"What is this? A seance?"

"A record," I said.

My voice came out steady, which surprised me, because inside I was a kettle at full boil. Six years of *Miss Blackwood, will you see to the motor,* six years of being addressed as though furniture might answer if spoken to clearly enough—and here was my voice, out in open air, claiming a room.

"Miss Blackwood." Charles descended the steps slowly, savoring the novelty of my name in his mouth. "Where is my aunt?"

"Upstairs. Dying. Which is why we are doing this today, now, while she can still hear it through the floorboards and still, God willing, be pleased."

Prew murmured something about defamation, about families ruined by servant-girl fictions, and Charles raised a hand like a man halting traffic he intended to drive through himself.

"Let her perform," he said. "The village will forget by spring."

But the village would not forget by spring, and Charles knew it, because the village had sent Mrs. Sorrell, and Mrs. Sorrell sat with her chin lifted and her dead husband's Bible on her knee like a gavel.

I took out the pages.

Four of them survived—the rest were ash in Charles's grate or folded into the dark of the lighthouse lamp room—but four were enough, because four contained the thing itself: the letter of 1884 in Edmund Kestrel's own hand, instructing that the manifest discrepancies be charged to the clerk Thomas Wrenn; the bank draft settling Wrenn's silence; and the marriage settlement that had bound Wrenn's daughter to the Kestrel name and her history to a locked drawer.

I read them aloud.

I read them slowly, because I wanted every syllable to land like a stone in still water, and because my voice shook unless I went slowly, and I refused—I refused—to let them hear it shake. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked, and I thought: *she is listening. Let her listen.*

When I read Thomas Wrenn's name, Mrs. Sorrell closed her eyes. Her mother's name. The name that didn't match the family Bible, the name the harbor had whispered for thirty years and been paid to stop whispering.

"This is a forgery," Charles said, but flatly, without heat, and flatness was a confession. Forgeries are denounced loudly. Truths are denounced quietly, by men calculating costs.

"They're in her hand," I said. "You've spent a week burning them. A man doesn't burn forgeries, Mr. Kestrel. He burns evidence."

"Enough." He crossed to the table and put both palms flat on the oak, leaning into my space, and his voice dropped to the register men use when they believe women understand only threat. "Here is what happens next. This house goes to me. Your position ends at the funeral. Your references—" he smiled "—will describe exactly what you are. A thief. A fantasist. A woman who reads other people's letters. You'll be lucky to get work as a charwoman in a town where nobody knows your name."

And there it was. The whole weight of my invisibility, turned around and swung at me like a plank. He thought he was threatening me. He didn't understand that he was describing the life I had chosen for six years, the safety I had clutched like a hot coal, and that I had already, somewhere in the small hours, opened my hand and let it fall.

"You're quite right," I said. "That is exactly who I was."

The hall went very quiet.

"So you may write it in your references truthfully," I went on, "and then you may add the correction underneath. Miss Edmund Blackwood, reader of other people's letters—and witness. Because everything I read this morning is now entered into the parish record, signed by a vicar of the Church of England and by the granddaughter of Thomas Wrenn, and no burning and no barrister and no reference, however viciously composed, can un-sign them."

Vicar Tregowan's hand trembled so badly over the parish register that Mrs. Pentreath steadied his elbow, but he wrote. He dated it. He blotted it. And when Charles turned on him—"Tregowan, think of the living your church depends upon"—the vicar looked up with the expression of a man stepping off a ledge and said, "I have thought of little else for thirty years, sir, whenever I passed that grave with the wrong name on it. I am done thinking."

Mrs. Sorrell signed next. She pressed so hard the nib nearly tore the page, and I loved her for it.

"Signatures need witnesses," Prew sneered. "Two credible adults, at minimum. Whom do you propose?"

"I propose me," said a voice from the doorway.

Ashworth walked the length of the hall as though it were the most ordinary errand of his career, set his case on the table, and opened it. Inside lay a typescript, pages squared and numbered, ribboned with the neat black lines of a machine kept faithfully fed.

"Stenographic transcript," he said. "Complete correspondence of 1884, transcribed word for word—including the letters Mr. Kestrel burned last Tuesday. I catalogued this collection under the impression I was organizing papers. Instead, apparently, I preserved them." He glanced at me, and the look held weeks of sleepless arithmetic in it. "I also transcribed certain documents Miss Blackwood concealed behind Lady Verity's ledgers, which she believed private. For which I apologize. Mostly."

"You *knew*—" I began.

"After," he said. "And I said nothing, and I destroyed nothing, and I kept a copy against the day someone found her spine. You misplaced yours in the lighthouse, I believe. I retrieved it."

He set the transcript beside the register and signed beneath the others, dating it in his precise draftsman's hand. Then he stood—not beside the table, but beside *me*, shoulder to shoulder, where the whole hall could see it, where the portraits could glare at it.

Charles stared at his own hired pen as though it had grown opinions, which, to be fair, it had. "Ashworth. You are finished in every firm in England."

"Yes," Ashworth said pleasantly. "It's remarkably freeing."

"Ruin," Charles said to the room, gathering Prew with a jerk of his head toward the door, "has a long memory. Longer than harbors."

"Harbors," said Mrs. Sorrell, not looking up from her Bible, "remember everything."

* * *

They left. The door boomed shut behind them, and the hall exhaled, and I discovered that my knees had been locked for the better part of an hour and now proposed to file complaints. Mrs. Pentreath, without ceremony, pushed me into a chair and put brandy in my hand, and Hobbs lit the lamps against the gray morning even though the morning barely deserved them.

Upstairs, faintly, a bell rang. Verity's bell. Once, twice—a weak, imperative summons, the sound of a woman who had commanded rooms for sixty years and did not intend to die politely below stairs to gossip.

Ashworth looked at me. "You shouldn't go alone."

"No," I agreed, tucking the register under my arm with the care of a woman carrying an infant. "She should hear the ending from the beginning."

I climbed the stairs with the parish record against my heart and the whole house listening, and outside the window the ice groaned and split again across the channel—as if the winter itself had decided, ahead of schedule, to let the water through.
