## 1. The Ledger of Small Names

The cold came through the infirmary walls the way water comes through a leaking hull—not all at once, but steadily, finding every seam, every gap in the mortar, until a man forgot what warmth had ever felt like. Gideon Crane had learned to think of the winter of 1813 as a siege. The enemy was not French. The enemy was the frost creeping up the stone floor from the marsh beyond, and the wind that came off it carrying the smell of frozen reeds and old iron, and the fact that there were thirty-one men in his care and fuel for perhaps nine days more.

He moved down the ward with a lamp held low, so its light would not wake the sleepers. That was the first rule of the infirmary: move quietly, see everything, be seen by no one. In five years he had refined the art of being overlooked into something like a craft, the way a joiner refines dovetailing. He wore a nurse's grey smock that seemed deliberately chosen to be forgettable. He spoke only when spoken to, and then in a voice so level and unremarkable that officers' eyes would slide off him mid-sentence. He had once stood at Sergeant Molyneux's shoulder while the colonel gave orders concerning the man's own burial, and the colonel did not once look at him. That, Gideon understood, was not luck. That was discipline.

The lamp found Private Wilkes first—fever case, third bunk from the stove—and moved on. It found young Harker, who had lost three fingers to frostbite and would lose the hand if Gideon could not persuade the surgeon to amputate before the rot climbed past the wrist. It found the empty bunk where Cobb had lain that morning, already stripped and made up, as though no man had ever coughed his life out onto the straw.

And then it found the man in the last bunk against the east wall, who was not asleep, though his eyes were closed, and whose breathing had begun to make a sound Gideon knew too well: a wet, rattling patience, the sound of a body negotiating terms it would not keep.

Private Daniel Oakes. Forty-eighth of the line, though you would search the regimental ledger until your eyes bled and never find him.

Gideon set the lamp on the stool and sat, and Oakes opened his eyes.

"You're still here," Oakes said. His voice was a ruin. The fever had been working through his chest for eleven days now, and it had left him with barely enough wind for four words at a time.

"I'm always here."

"Thought I'd dreamed you." A pause while he gathered breath. "Or that I'd died and you were the thing after."

"Some men have woken and wished worse," Gideon said, and Oakes managed something like a smile.

Gideon put his fingers to the private's wrist. The pulse was quick and thin, a bird's heartbeat, running. He lifted the man's head and let him drink from the cup—barley water, tepid, the last of the honey gone into it two days ago because Gideon had decided this man deserved it. Then he settled him back against the pillow and did the small offices that nobody thanks anybody for: straightened the blanket, tucked it along the cold side of his body, wiped the sweat from his temples with a cloth dampened in melted snow.

Outside, the wind changed pitch and came around the corner of the building with a sound like a door being forced. Somewhere in the officers' quarters, a fiddle was playing. They were celebrating something—the repulse of a raid on the supply train, or merely the survival of another week of winter. The sound came faintly through the wall, bright and meaningless, music for men whose names would all go into the book.

"The major came round today," Oakes said. "While you were at the well."

Gideon's hands did not stop moving. That was the second rule: hands never stop moving. "Did he."

"Asked my name." Oakes laughed, or tried to; it became a cough, and Gideon held him through it, feeling the whole frail cage of the man's ribs shudder against his forearm. When it passed, Oakes lay back, greyer than before. "I told him. Daniel Oakes, forty-eighth. Enlisted at Derby, June of 'nine. He just looked at me. Like I'd spoken in church."

"He wasn't given your name," Gideon said carefully. "That's why."

"No." Oakes stared at the ceiling. "I've worked it out, Nurse. Took me a while—I'm not a clever man—but I've worked it out. There's a page somewhere with my name on it, and someone's crossed it out."

Gideon said nothing. The lamp flame bent in a draught and righted itself.

Five years ago, newly assigned to the 44th after Passchendale's cousin disaster at some nameless farm he still could not say aloud, Gideon Crane had discovered that an army does not only kill men. It also unmakes them. A clerk's error, a quartermaster's grudge, a captain's need to explain away a failed engagement—a man could die twice. Once in the mud, and once in the record. The first death took his body. The second took everything else: his pay owed to a widow, his discharge papers, his grave marker, the proof that he had existed at all between the covers of the great book. And when Gideon had raised the matter—politely, once, in writing—he had been summoned before Captain Ferris and shown, without comment, the file of a nurse at a previous posting whose contract had not been renewed, and whose subsequent employment history was a blank. The message had required no annotation. He had walked back to the infirmary understanding that he had been permitted one question, and that he would not be permitted two.

So he had stopped asking questions. And he had started writing them instead.

In the heel of his left boot, wrapped in oilcloth, Gideon carried a notebook. It was nothing—a cheap thing of grey paper, sewn rather than bound, small enough to sit in a grown man's palm. On its pages, in a hand so cramped it might have been a merchant's inventory, he recorded the erased. Name, rank, place of enlistment, date of death where he knew it, manner of it, and anything a mother or a wife might one day want to know: *had a scar through the left eyebrow; sang tenor; carried a lock of hair in a locket and asked me to send it to Mrs. Anne Trewin, Bodmin.* He had forty-three names now. Forty-four, when Oakes joined them, which would be soon.

Every time ink touched that paper, Gideon committed an offence for which the army had no formal charge and therefore no limit to its informal punishment. He had thought about this more than any man should think about anything. He had imagined the workshop searched, the boot taken, the pages read aloud in a room full of officers while his career—or what the army called a career, which was a different animal entirely—was dismantled around him. He kept the notebook anyway. Not out of courage; he was honest enough with himself to know it was not courage. Courage implied choice, and he had discovered that some things are simply structural, like load-bearing walls. You did not decide whether to hold the building up. You held it, or everything came down including you.

"I want you to write it," Oakes said.

Gideon looked at him.

"My name. Whatever it is you do—the orderlies talk, you know. Thomas saw you once, by the well, writing in the little book. He says you write the names of the dead men." Oakes's breathing hitched. "I don't want the major's book. I want yours."

"Thomas talks too much," Gideon said, which was neither confirmation nor denial, and bought him a moment. But Oakes's eyes stayed on him, fever-bright and terribly patient, and lying to a dying man was a labour Gideon had never acquired the stomach for.

"Yes," he said. "I'll write it."

Oakes exhaled, long and slow, as though a knot inside him had loosened. "Daniel Oakes. Derby. My mother's still living, I think—she'd be at her sister's, near Ashbourne, if she's anywhere. Ellen Oakes. Tell her—" He stopped. Gathered himself. "Tell her I didn't desert. Whatever they wrote. I didn't desert."

"You didn't," Gideon said. "You fell ill in February after the forage action, and you were tended, and you died among men who knew your name."

"That's not how it'll be written."

"It's how it will be remembered."

* * *

The fiddle across the compound played on and then, mercifully, stopped. Gideon made his rounds a second time, dosed Wilkes with willow bark and the dregs of the quinine, checked Harker's bandages, and stood a moment at the window, watching snow come down over the parade ground in long diagonal sheets, erasing the day's tracks, erasing everything, patiently, as though the whole world were a ledger and winter the clerk.

Then he went back to Oakes and sat with him through the small hours, because there was nothing else to be done and because sitting with a dying man is a kind of medicine, the only kind Gideon had never run short of.

Near two in the morning, Oakes began to speak to people who were not there. He argued briefly with his brother. He asked twice for his rifle to be cleaned. At half past three he surfaced, lucid and calm, and said, "It's snowing hard, isn't it. Bad for the wagons." And at ten minutes past five, with the sky outside still black but carrying the faintest suggestion of iron-grey at its hem, he said, very clearly, "Derby," and did not speak again.

Gideon sat with him until the body finished its business, which took most of an hour, and then he closed the man's eyes and folded his arms and pulled the sheet up, and stood in the cold ward with his breath hanging in front of him like something torn out of him.

* * *

By seven the surgeon had pronounced death—glancing at Oakes for less time than he spent brushing ash from his cuff—and by eight the burial detail had come with a stretcher and a blanket and carried him out into the falling snow toward the plot behind the chapel, where the ground was too frozen for proper graves and the dead went down shallow, stacked two deep when the week required it.

No entry in the regimental ledger. No return submitted to the War Office. Gideon watched from the infirmary doorway, hands jammed in his pockets around the heat of nothing, and Major Kettering happened to pass him on the path.

"A bad night, Crane?"

"Quiet, sir."

"Good. Keep it quiet." The major's breath plumed. He glanced at the blanket-wrapped bundle receding down the white path. "We're stretched thin on returns this quarter. The men we have are quite sufficient for the men we have, do you understand me?"

"Perfectly, sir."

Kettering nodded, satisfied, and crunched away, and Gideon stood in the doorway a long moment after, watching the burial detail dwindle to dots and then to smudges and then to nothing, swallowed by the snowfield, and understood with perfect clarity that the major's sentence was the whole architecture of his world in miniature: *the men we have are sufficient for the men we have.* Everything else was arithmetic done in pencil, easily corrected.

* * *

At noon the workshop was quiet. Wilkes slept. Harker dozed with his ruined hand cradled against his chest like something stolen. Orderly Thomas split kindling out back with more noise than skill. And Gideon locked the infirmary door from the inside, wedged a chair under the handle out of habit rather than necessity, and knelt beside the stove.

He pulled off his left boot. He unwrapped the oilcloth. The notebook sat in his palm, warm from his foot, absurdly small, weighing almost nothing—less than a loaf, less than a pistol, less than a man's reputation—and containing, nonetheless, forty-three lives that the Crown had mislaid.

He opened it to a fresh page. He dipped his pen—the stub of a goose quill, cut down until it was nearly impossible to hold, because small pens drew less attention—and waited for his hand to steady.

*Daniel Oakes, private, 48th Foot. Of Derby. Enlisted June 1809. Died of fever, 14th February 1813, at the garrison infirmary. Did not desert.*

His pen hovered.

*Did not desert.* Three words. Three words that could, if the wrong eyes found this page, cost him everything: his post, his liberty, quite possibly his life, because men like Hale and Kettering did not require law to dispose of inconvenient nurses, only opportunity. Every letter he set down was a wager placed against his own neck. He had made this calculation forty-three times before and had never yet refused the stake.

He wrote it. Then, beneath, in letters smaller than the rest:

*Mother, Ellen Oakes, at her sister's near Ashbourne. Last words: "Derby." Tended to the end. Buried behind the garrison chapel, north row, fourth from the wall. I was there. Someone was there.*

Someone was there. He had written some version of those words on nearly every page, and he sometimes thought that if he ever published the book—if such a thing could ever be published, in a kinder country, in a kinder century—that would be its title. Because that was the whole of what a record-keeper could promise the forgotten: not justice, not restitution, only the plain, stubborn fact of witness. *You were not alone at the end. Someone was there, and he wrote it down.*

He blotted the page, wrapped the book, pushed it back into the heel of the boot, and laced it on. Then he sat back against the cold wall and allowed himself thirty seconds—no more—to feel the full weight of what he did, night after night, page after page. Thirty seconds of fear, honestly felt, and then folded away again like linen.

From the yard came the sound of hooves. Two horses, by the weight of it, coming fast through the snow, and a voice calling for the gate to be opened—calling with the particular authority of somebody who expected gates to open for him.

Gideon rose, smoothed his smock, and became unremarkable. Through the frosted pane of the infirmary window he watched a carriage—no, a coach, a gentleman's coach, mud to its windows—draw up before the commandant's quarters, steam rising off the horses like breath off blood. An officer hurried down the steps to meet it. A servant handed a lady down.

She stood a moment in the falling snow, looking up at the garrison with an expression he could not read at that distance and did not try to. Dark-haired, straight-backed, dressed in travelling grey. She was, he would learn within the hour, Miss Eliza Vane, arrived to serve as volunteer nurse in the infirmary, sent—so the commanding officer's compliments ran—at her father's particular request.

Her father's. Gideon turned that phrase over as he unbolted the door, and felt the first faint pressure of something against the far side of his careful life, like a hand testing a lock.

Colonel Ashworth had no reason to send anyone to Gideon's infirmary. Colonel Ashworth had never once, in five years, acknowledged that the infirmary existed.

The lady came up the path through the snow, and Gideon went out to meet her with his face arranged into blankness, and behind his left heel, against his skin, forty-four names rode in silence.
