## 2. What the Door Left Behind

The knock never came. That was the first thing Gideon Crane would remember afterward—not a voice, not footsteps, but the absence of them: the small silence of a door opened by someone who did not wish to be heard, and closed just as carefully, and then the crunch of boots on frost retreating down the lane at a pace that wanted very much to be a run.

He was at the dispensary shelf with his back to the window, counting out laudanum drops into Private Weller's cup—twelve, no more, though Weller had begged for twenty with eyes like a drowning man's—and so he did not see the visitor. He heard only the door latch, the cold breathing in through the gap beneath it, and then, threaded through that cold, a sound thinner than wind.

A cry. Small, furious, unmistakably alive.

Gideon set the cup down. The laudanum trembled faintly on its surface.

The infirmary had been a wool merchant's warehouse once, before the regiment claimed it, and its long low room still smelled of lanolin under the carbolic and the sweet rot of fever—the smell of things meant to be kept warm. Twenty-two cots ran in two rows down its length. Nineteen were occupied tonight; three men coughed in their sleep, one moaned without waking, and the rest breathed in the shallow unison of the exhausted. The fire in the great hearth had been banked to embers because coal cost money and Major Pell did not approve of money spent on men who were going to die anyway. Gideon had learned not to argue the point. He simply waited until Pell's office went dark and then fed the fire himself, scooping from the cellar stores with a shovel and a clear conscience, an economy of conscience being the only kind available to him.

He crossed the room now, past cot after cot, his boots making no more noise than the building itself required. Five years in this place had taught him how to move through it like a draft—present everywhere, noticed nowhere.

The door was oak, iron-banded, older than the war. When he pulled it open the night came in like a slap: January, starless, the courtyard glazed in ice, and the wind off the flats carrying that particular salt-and-rot stink of the marsh that got into wool and would not wash out.

On the step, in a market basket lined with sacking, lay a baby.

Gideon stood quite still. This was his habit in all crises—to become furniture until danger passed—but the baby was not a crisis that could be waited out. It was perhaps three weeks old, red-faced and furious, swaddled against the cold in a quantity of fine white linen that had no business in this courtyard, in this garrison, in this whole grey corner of Lincolnshire. Its fists were balled at its chin. Its cry was thin but disciplined, as if it already understood that loudness invited attention, and attention here was dangerous.

There was a note pinned to the swaddling with a silver pin.

Gideon looked at the note for a long moment without touching it. Then he looked up and down the courtyard—the cookhouse dark, the stable block dark, the gate arch standing open to the lane where the frost held the wheel-ruts of whatever cart or carriage had brought the visitor and gone. No lantern anywhere. Only the wind, working at the eaves.

He lifted the basket inside and shut the door on the night.

* * *

He carried it to the far end of the infirmary, past the last cot, into the little alcove where the linens were stored—a space barely wide enough for a shelf, a stool, and a man's shoulders. There was a candle stub there. He lit it, shielded the flame with his hand, and looked properly at what the door had left him.

The child had stopped crying. It stared up at him with the unfocused, ancient gravity of the very young, its breath quickening, its face working toward another complaint. Gideon had delivered eleven babies in five years—soldiers' wives mostly, in the cottages beyond the wall, because the regiment paid for surgeons and not for midwives and someone had to stand in the gap. He knew what newborns weighed in his hands. He knew the blue at the fingertips that meant hurry. This one was cold but not dangerously so; hungry, certainly; and wrapped, when he parted the outer blanket, in linen so fine it seemed to generate its own light in the candle's reach.

And embroidered at the fold, small enough that only someone handling the child closely would ever find it, was a crest.

A falcon rising. Two stars above it. The thread was silver, real silver, and the cloth beneath it was French in origin, smuggled or prize-taken, worth more than Gideon's yearly wage.

He knew the crest. Everyone at Winterbourne garrison knew it, the way everyone knows the mark burned into the beams of a house where something bad once happened. It flew over the colonel's quarters. It sat stamped in wax atop every order that came down from the great house. Colonel Sir Edmund Ashworth, commanding officer of the 44th, whose wife had died in '09 and whose grief was spoken of in the tones reserved for monuments.

Gideon put the candle down because his hand had begun, very slightly, to shake.

The note was folded twice. He unfolded it. The hand was a servant's hand, schooled and hurried both—looped letters made fast, the ink blotted where the writer had not waited for it to dry.

*Forget this face. Ask nothing. If you are wise, you never saw it.*

No signature. Of course no signature. But the paper was good paper, and the pin was silver, and the linen bore a falcon and two stars, and Gideon Crane had spent five years learning to read documents the way other men read faces. The note said *forget*, which meant someone important. The note said *never saw it*, which meant someone frightened. Important and frightened together made a combination that historically ended badly for people who stood where Gideon was standing.

He looked at the child.

It had found his finger—one of his fingers, swallowed whole in its tiny fist—and gripped it with the astonishing, unreasonable strength of infants, as though holding on were the whole of its understanding and it intended to do the thing thoroughly.

"No," Gideon said quietly, to the child, or to himself, or to the note lying on the shelf like a verdict. "No, you don't."

Private Weller died at half past three.

Gideon had known he would since Tuesday, when the wound in the man's belly had turned the color of tea left standing, and he had done everything a nurse could do, which was to say almost nothing, which was to say he had kept Weller clean and warm and dosed against pain while the man's body conducted its private negotiation with death and lost it slowly. What Gideon had not been able to do was write the man's name down.

That was the part nobody outside these walls ever understood. A man could die in the King's service and not exist—that was the discovery of Gideon's first year, the one that had changed him from a soft-hearted fool into something rarer and more careful. Private Daniel Weller, 44th Foot, had been struck from the muster roll in October for striking a corporal who had struck him first, and a struck man who died was recorded nowhere: not in the burial register, not in the returns, not in the ledger of effects, because a struck man officially owned nothing. He had a mother in Norwich. He had a sweetheart's ribbon knotted round his watch chain. He had a name, and the name was going to vanish at dawn like breath off glass, unless—

Unless somebody wrote it down. Somebody with a notebook and a memory for faces and a talent, cultivated like a garden knife honed nightly, for being invisible.

Gideon waited until the orderly Thomas had finished laying Weller out—Thomas was young, devout, and easily spooked by anything that looked like irregularity—and then waited another hour, until even the fire's crackle seemed asleep. He moved to his own narrow cot in the corner, sat, took off one boot, and drew from inside the boot's shaft a notebook no larger than his palm, bound in calf leather worn soft as cloth.

There were ninety-one names in it. Ninety-two, after tonight.

He dipped his pen. The candle was nearly gone; he worked by feel as much as sight, the letters small and cramped, each one a decision, each decision a risk. Writing a name was nothing, in itself. Ink was cheap. Paper was cheap. But a list was evidence, and evidence of what? That a nurse in the 44th had spent five years documenting, in secret, every soldier the regiment had decided to forget—the flogged men buried under false entries, the deserters shot without trial, the sick shipped home to die unnamed, the poor devils like Weller erased by a clerk's stroke of the pen because erasure was cheaper than justice. If Adjutant Hale ever saw that book, Gideon would not face a court-martial. He would face a ditch behind the drill yard, and the book would burn, and the ninety-one names would go wherever erased names went.

He wrote anyway. He always wrote anyway. He had long ago stopped examining why; the impulse sat below examination, down where the conscience keeps its root.

*Daniel Weller. Private, 44th. Struck off Oct. last for lifting his hand to Cpl. Bryers, who struck him first. Died 14 Jan., of a wound taken at Badajoz that should have killed him quick and didn't. Mother living, Norwich, St. Stephen's parish. Sweetheart's ribbon, green. Played the fiddle badly and constantly, and the men complained, and they played his tunes for a week after he was gone.*

He blew on the ink, folded the book along its worn crease, and slid it back into his boot. Ninety-two.

Then he rose, and went to the linen alcove, and stood looking down at the basket.

The child slept now, finally warm, having eaten a mash of bread soaked in milk that Gideon had coaxed from the cookhouse under pretense of his own supper. She was a girl—he had established this during the feeding, with clinical detachment and a rising sense of doom. She slept with one fist beside her cheek and her breath coming in the small bellows rhythm that is the most honest sound in the world, because an infant cannot lie about being alive.

She should not be here. That was the arithmetic of it, simple as a sergeant's sums. A child bearing the Ashworth crest, deposited at his door by a servant too frightened to knock, accompanied by a note ordering him to un-see her—this was not misfortune. This was the visible edge of something with weight and intention behind it, some failure or scandal or crime committed upstairs in the great house among men who commanded courts-martial the way other men commanded dinner. Whatever story she belonged to, it was a story someone wanted dead, and she was the last living page of it. People who kept such pages did not prosper. Gideon knew this professionally. His entire notebook was proof that records survived men, and men did not survive records.

Every survival instinct he had refined over five years—the stillness, the silence, the practiced art of walking through rooms unnoticed, the boot with its hidden book, the face arranged into mild competent blankness—every instinct said the same thing: carry the basket to the gatehouse at dawn, leave it on the step of the parish beadle, walk away, forget this face.

The instructions were right there. Written down, even. Forget this face.

Gideon reached out and touched the child's cheek with the back of one finger. Her skin was astonishing—warm silk over warm water—and she turned her face toward the touch without waking, the way flowers turn, following warmth because warmth meant life.

"Someone ought to remember you," he said. The words came out rough; he hadn't spoken aloud in hours and hadn't spoken *to* anyone in days. "That's all anybody needs, in the end. Someone willing to say: this one was here."

He thought of Weller laid out under the sheet with his fiddle tunes unrecorded. He thought of ninety-one names and the ditches waiting for men who kept lists. He thought of the falcon stitched in silver thread, and of Sir Edmund Ashworth up at the great house, and of how the powerful always assumed that silence was a country they owned outright.

The child sighed in her sleep. It was a small, satisfied sound, the sound of a creature who has been cold and is no longer cold, and it went through Gideon's chest like a bayonet through wet canvas.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, I'm going to regret this."

But he was already moving—fetching the second blanket, wedging the basket near the hearth's residual warmth where he could see it from his cot, banking the fire higher than regulation allowed. He told himself he would keep her one night. One night, until he understood what she was, until some safer arrangement could be found. One night was caution, not defiance. One night could be explained away, if explanation were ever demanded.

He knew, lying down on his cot with his boots on and his book against his ribs, that he was lying to himself, and he let it stand, because a man who spends five years recording the forgotten learns eventually that forgetting is not a thing that happens to you. It is a thing you agree to do. And somewhere in the dark between the third watch and the fourth, listening to an unregistered child breathe in a room full of unrecorded men, Gideon Crane declined to agree.

* * *

Morning came grey and hard, the frost ferning the insides of the windows. Gideon rose before the garrison bugle, fed the child again—she ate with a determination that heartened him absurdly—and had just settled her, basket and all, behind the screen by the dispensary when Thomas pushed through the main doors with the post-sack and a strange expression.

"Nurse Crane." Thomas was twenty, pious, and constitutionally incapable of concealing a state of mind. Something had rattled him. "There's—there's a lady come, sir. On the London coach."

"A lady." Women arrived at Winterbourne rarely enough that the word alone constituted news. "Sick? Injured?"

"No, sir. That is—" Thomas shifted the sack, wrestling with it. "She asked after the infirmary. Said she'd been sent to nurse. With papers from the colonel's own office, sir, sealed and everything." He lowered his voice, though the nearest sleeper was forty feet away and dreaming of nowhere. "Adjutant Hale brought her up himself. From the gate. Personally, sir. And he was smiling, sir, and you know how it sits wrong when he does that."

Gideon's hands went still on the screen's edge.

Hale, escorting. Hale, smiling. A lady with sealed papers arriving on precisely the morning after a servant had run through frost rather than knock, leaving behind a falcon, two stars, and four written words that Gideon now carried folded in his pocket like a stone.

Behind the screen, with impeccable timing, the child woke and began to cry.

Thomas's eyes went wide. "Good Lord," he said. "What on earth—"

"A cat," Gideon said smoothly, stepping sideways to close the gap in the screen. "Been yowling half the night. I'll have it out before the surgeon rounds."

It was a poor lie, delivered too fast, and Thomas was staring at the screen instead of at him, and the post-sack had slipped entirely from his shoulder to the floor, and none of it mattered—none of it—because across the infirmary's long room the main doors were opening again, letting in a blade of cold light and a tall figure in a grey traveling cloak, and Gideon understood, with the clarity that arrives a heartbeat too late, that the story he had agreed to keep alive had just sent someone to find it.

The woman in the doorway removed one glove, finger by finger, and looked at the cots, and the fire, and the screen—and last of all, longest of all, at him.
