## 3. The Colonel's Daughter

The coach left her at the crossroads, as promised, and Eliza Vane stood alone in the falling dark with two trunks and a letter of introduction she had not been given permission to read twice.

The driver had taken her coin with a grunt and averted eyes. She supposed she would have to grow accustomed to that—to averted eyes, to the particular quality of silence that surrounded her father's name in this place. In London it had been one kind of silence: the drawing-room hush that followed Colonel Sir Reginald Ashworth into any room, the respectful quiet of men calculating advancement and women calculating marriages. Here, at the edge of the fens where the 44th wintered like a knife left out in the rain, the silence was different. It was the silence of men who had seen things they could not speak of, and who had learned that a colonel's daughter was another thing entirely that could not be spoken of.

She pulled her cloak tighter and began the walk. The garrison showed itself by degrees: first the smoke, hanging flat and grey above the low horizon like a wound dressing; then the palisade, black timber silvered with frost; then the gate lanterns, small orange suns swallowed whole by the vast December dusk.

A sentry challenged her. She gave her name and heard him repeat it—*Vane*—with a slight hesitation, the syllable turned over like a coin tested for counterfeit.

"Expected," he said at last. "Infirmary's past the parade ground. They'll take you at the workshop door."

*They'll take you.* As though she were a parcel. She supposed she was.

Her trunk wheels caught in the frozen ruts, and no one came to help her, and she told herself this was freedom. That was the story she had constructed on the coach north, word by careful word: that her father's summons was an opportunity dressed in obligation; that at twenty-six, unmoored by her mother's death and useless in a London season that had long since stopped expecting anything of her, she might make herself useful where usefulness was wanted. Nursing was respectable. Nursing was *needed*. The war ate men faster than the surgeons could mend them, and a woman with steady hands and no prospects could carve out a life at the very edge of what was permitted.

That was the story. But beneath it, in the cellar of her mind where she kept the things she declined to examine, sat the memory of her father's face three weeks ago, when he had come to her aunt's house unannounced and stood dripping in the hall like a man who had ridden through his own storm.

"You will go to the garrison," he had said. Not asked. "There is a matter there requiring discretion."

"What manner of matter?"

He had looked at her then—a long look, and she had understood, even before he spoke, that whatever he was about to say had been rehearsed and found wanting, and that he was ashamed of it, and that his shame frightened her more than anything he might confess.

"A problem," he said. "You will know it when you see it. You will deal with it quietly. You will write to me through the address I give you, and to no one else, and you will sign yourself with your mother's name, not mine. No one at the garrison is to know you are my daughter. No one. Do you understand me, Eliza?"

She had not understood. She understood less now, walking through gates that closed behind her with a sound like a book shutting.

* * *

The infirmary smelled of camphor, lye, and under it all something sweeter and worse that she would learn to ignore—the smell the men brought in from the surgery tent. A woman named Mrs. Harkness, broad as a barn door with forearms like a laundress and eyes that missed nothing, took her letter of introduction, read it twice, and grunted.

"Surgeon-Major wants another pair of hands. You've done ward work?"

"Volunteer work. St Bartholomew's, during the fever year."

"Paid work is different from pious work." Mrs. Harkness handed back the letter without ceremony. "But hands are hands. You'll be on nights within the week if you don't faint."

"I don't faint."

"We'll see." The woman's gaze lingered a moment too long—not hostile, merely cataloguing, the way a quartermaster catalogues stores. "Vane, is it? Any relation to—"

"No relation to anyone," Eliza said, and heard how smoothly the lie came, and disliked herself for it, and filed the smoothness away as evidence of something her father had made her into before she ever left London.

She was given a cell of a room off the drying loft, a straw mattress, a washstand, a hook for her cloak. From the narrow window she could see the parade ground going white with frost, the flag drooping on its pole like a dead man's arm, and beyond the palisade the fen stretching to a horizon the colour of pewter. Somewhere out there were marshes, she'd been told. Men who tried to desert in winter rarely got further than the reed-beds.

She unpacked slowly, listening to the building around her. Every garrison had its own language of sound—boots, doors, coughs, the particular rhythm of men trying to be quiet in a place built for noise. This one sounded wrong somewhere underneath, she thought. Muffled. Like a house where someone was ill and everyone pretended not to hear it.

*A problem,* her father had said. *You will know it when you see it.*

What shape did a problem take, that it required a daughter instead of a soldier? That required her mother's name as a mask?

* * *

Her first evening, she was set to work folding bandages by the great infirmary hearth while Mrs. Harkness made her rounds down the ward. Thirty-two men lay in two ranks of cots, and Eliza counted them without meaning to—an old habit from the fever year, counting the living because the dead could not be counted aloud. Thirty-one men and one empty cot at the end, its blankets already folded back, waiting.

"Who's in the end bed?" she asked an orderly passing with a slop pail—a boy of sixteen or so, thin as a lath, with the startled eyes of a creature hunted.

"That's for—" He stopped himself. Looked over his shoulder. "No one yet, miss."

"It's made up for no one?"

"The beds stay made." And he hurried on, but not before she saw his throat move as he swallowed.

Later, carrying linen to the store-cupboard, she passed the office at the end of the corridor and heard voices through the door—Mrs. Harkness and a man whose voice was dry as paper and precise as a ledger.

"—the new nurse arrives today," the dry voice was saying. "Vane. Unusual name for these parts. Nobody's people. Nobody's history. Convenient."

"Convenient for what, Adjutant Hale?" Mrs. Harkness's voice carried no deference whatsoever, which surprised Eliza, and pleased her obscurely.

"For exactly what it says in the surgeon-major's requisition. Another pair of hands. The ward is stretched. Winter does what winters do." A pause, the sound of papers squared against a desk. "Still, I like to know who comes through the gate. Everyone has a reason for being where they are. Even the ones who think they don't."

Eliza moved on before she could be overheard lingering, her heart beating strangely. *Everyone has a reason.* Was Hale a fool, or worse than a fool? She thought of her father's instruction—that she must arrive masked, anonymous—and felt the first cold touch of real fear. Her disguise was good enough for orderlies and matrons. Whether it was good enough for a man who spoke of reasons the way a fishmonger spoke of weights remained to be seen.

* * *

It was near midnight when she saw Gideon Crane for the first time.

She had risen unable to sleep, drawn down the stairs by a sound she could not name—perhaps the wind, perhaps something else—and found the ward dimly lit by a single shaded lamp. Most of the men slept, breathing in ragged chorus. At the far end, in the chair beside the empty-made-up cot—no, not empty, she saw now, never mind what the orderly had said—a man sat bent over a dying private, holding a cloth wetted with vinegar to lips that cracked and bled with each breath.

The man was not young and not old, thirty perhaps, with the sort of face that would never be handsome and would never be forgotten: sharp planes, a scar through one brow, and eyes that held a stillness she had only ever seen in deep water. His sleeves were rolled to the elbow; his forearms were corded and marked with old burns and older scars. A nurse. She had expected someone soft. Nothing about him was soft except his hands.

"Private Dunmore," he said quietly, without looking up, as though he had known she was there all along. "Fever. Four days. The surgeons are done with him."

"Then shouldn't you call his family, or—"

"He hasn't any. Or rather—" the man's mouth curved, without humour, "—the regiment says he hasn't any. There's a difference between having nothing and being recorded as having nothing. Private Dunmore is learning the difference tonight."

Something in his tone snagged her like a nail in cloth. "The regiment *says*?"

At that he did look up, and she felt the full weight of those still-water eyes pass over her face, patient and thorough, the way a reader reads a page. She had the vertiginous sense of being *understood*—of every concealment she carried being noted, catalogued, and shelved for later consideration, without judgement or alarm.

"You're the new nurse," he said. "Vane."

"Yes."

"London?"

"Yes."

"Hm." He wrung out the vinegar cloth with great economy of movement. "You'll want to sleep. Nights here start early and end late. The ward bell rings at five."

She should have gone. Instead she heard herself ask, "Is there anything I can carry for you? Water, brandy—"

"You can sit with him," Crane said. "For a while, if you like. Dying men like a second pair of hands even when the hands do nothing. It's the doing-nothing that's hardest to learn."

So she sat, on a hard stool in a freezing ward at one in the morning, watching a stranger tend a man the army had decided to forget, and something in her chest ached in a way she could not account for. The private's breathing slowed, and slowed, and there came a pause that went on too long, and Crane's hand rested on the man's wrist, counting a silence.

"There," Crane said softly. Not to her. Perhaps to the dead man. Perhaps to himself.

He performed the small offices of death—closing the eyes, tying the jaw, straightening the limbs—with a gentleness that undid her more than any sermon. When he finished, he drew a small notebook from his breast pocket, and she watched, puzzled, as he wrote something in it by lamplight. He was quick about it, shielding the page with his body, an instinctive gesture, and she looked away as though she had caught him at prayer.

"The bed will need remaking," he said. "Mrs. Harkness will show you tomorrow. We turn the mattresses at dawn."

"Was he married?" Eliza asked. "Private Dunmore?"

"No." A pause. "Yes. It depends which record you consult."

And before she could unpick that impossible sentence, he had thanked her for sitting, banked the fire, and was gone down the dark corridor with the lamp and the notebook, leaving her alone with the folded blankets of a man who both had and had not existed.

* * *

She wrote her first report the next afternoon, after her duties, sealing it with wax stolen from the surgeon-major's desk and signing it *Margaret*, her mother's name, as instructed.

*Margaret* — I am arrived safe. The garrison is poor and the ward full. I have begun work and the people seem honest enough, though there is a watchfulness here I cannot describe — the men guard their silences like coin. I do not yet know what matter you meant. Nothing presents itself. Tell me plainly what I am to look for and I will find it. Your obedient — Margaret

She posted it in the village, four miles' walk through freezing fog, and walked back with the answer forming and reforming in her mind like breath on glass. What matter required a daughter? What problem could not be put to a major, a chaplain, a magistrate? Problems that could be spoken of went to proper channels. Problems that could not—

She thought of her father's face in the hallway. Not stern. *Afraid.* Her father, who had stormed citadels, afraid of something at his own garrison, small enough to hide and heavy enough to bend him.

By the time the palisade rose out of the fog again she had assembled and discarded a dozen possibilities. Debts. A woman. Stolen funds. An officer gone mad and confined. Each theory collapsed under examination, and behind all of them, patient as the fen itself, waited the thing she could not yet see: the true shape of it, wearing the fog.

* * *

Three days into her service, the shape almost showed itself.

She had been sent to the record room for the ward returns—a long low chamber behind the adjutant's offices, lined with shelves of ledgers and pigeonholes stuffed with muster rolls, discharge papers, correspondence. The air was dry and dust-laden, and it smelled of tallow and old ink, and she liked it instantly, the way she had liked her father's library as a child: rooms where lives were pinned down like moths.

She found the returns easily enough. It was the drawer next to them that stopped her—one pigeonhole labelled *Correspondence, Oct.–Dec. 1812*, standing open and half-empty, its contents clearly thinned. On the floor beneath, where someone had dropped or discarded it, lay a scrap of heavy cream-coloured paper, fine-laid stock, such as no garrison used for military business. She picked it up meaning to return it, and froze.

On its corner, half-torn away, was a crest. A falcon rising, above the motto she had known all her life, embroidered on every cushion of her childhood home:

*Sub Alis Umbra.* In the shadow of wings.

The Ashworth crest. Her father's crest—on personal stationery, in a garrison pigeonhole, torn and discarded among records a year old.

She stood very still in the dusty room while the frost ticked in the window frames. Stationery could be explained. Letters were written, forwarded, misfiled; her father corresponded constantly with his officers. It explained nothing and everything, and beneath the explanations her unease shifted, settled, and grew teeth. Whatever the problem was, it had passed through her father's hands. It had been written on paper fine enough for a lady's desk.

She replaced the scrap exactly as she had found it, took the ward returns, and left the record room walking at a pace precisely calibrated not to suggest flight.

In the corridor she passed Adjutant Hale coming the other way. He was a narrow man, grey and neat, with ink-stained cuffs and eyes like counting-houses, and he paused—just a heartbeat longer than courtesy required—as she stepped aside.

"Nurse Vane." His voice was pleasant. Everything about him was pleasant, the way ice is pleasant until it bears weight. "Finding the archive satisfactory?"

"Very orderly, thank you, sir."

"We pride ourselves on order here." The counting-house eyes rested on her face, then dipped briefly to the returns folder under her arm, as though weighing whether it contained anything besides returns. "Order is what separates a regiment from a mob. Loose papers, loose tongues, loose women—" he smiled, showing that the list had been a joke, or that jokes were among the things he kept in ledgers, "—all the same difficulty, in the end. Things out of their proper place."

"I'll remember, sir."

"Do." He moved on, unhurried, and she stood listening to his footsteps fade and understanding that she had just been measured—for what purpose she did not know, against what standard she dreaded to learn.

* * *

That night she could not keep to her cell. She walked the ward at two in the morning, telling herself it was habit from the fever year, knowing it was fear looking for somewhere to put itself down.

The ward was warmest near the hearth, and there she found Gideon Crane again, awake as before, mending linens by firelight with small, exact stitches. He glanced up, and this time he gestured her to the second chair without surprise, as though her presence at two in the morning were simply another item on the ward's inventory.

"You don't sleep," she said.

"Not much. You neither."

"I sleep."

"You walk like a woman who sleeps," he agreed mildly, snipping a thread. "Mrs. Harkness says you're from a charitable inclination and London. She says it as though she believes it, which is interesting, because Mrs. Harkness doesn't believe anything she hasn't buried herself."

"And what do you say?"

Crane considered the question honestly, which unsettled her more than suspicion would have. "I say the ward is short of hands and yours look clean. The rest is nobody's business until somebody makes it so. In my experience, that somebody is usually the adjutant."

The fire popped. Down the ward a man groaned in his sleep and was silent.

"Why does a nurse keep notes?" she asked. "The night we met. You wrote something after Private Dunmore died. The regiment keeps its own records, surely."

The stitches paused—only for a moment, a hitch smaller than a heartbeat, but she had spent a childhood reading her father across dinner tables and she did not miss it.

"The regiment's records," Crane said at last, evenly, "record what the regiment wishes to have happened. I keep a record of what did." He tied off the thread and folded the linen. "Someday, Nurse Vane, someone may ask you the difference between those two things. When they do, you'll already know the answer. Everyone does. They just spend most of their lives pretending otherwise."

"You speak carefully," she said.

"And you listen carefully. We make a dangerous pair by a fire." He rose, gathered his mended linens, and paused. For a moment something moved behind the still water of his eyes—hesitation, or invitation, she could not tell which. "Whatever brought you to the 44th, Nurse Vane—whatever you're looking for in the fens—it's colder here than you're dressed for. People come to garrisons thinking the secrets are far away and small. Secrets live close to the bone in places like this. They have to. Out here, a big secret would freeze before morning."

He bade her goodnight and left her by the dying fire with her heart hammering and her thoughts in disarray.

*He knows,* she thought. *Not what. But that.*

She climbed back to her cell and stood at the narrow window a long while, watching frost feather across the glass. Below, in the yard, a light burned in the infirmary storehouse—someone moving among the stores at three in the morning. She thought of her father's afraid face, of the torn crest in the dusty drawer, of Crane's notebook and his impossible sentence about Private Dunmore, of Hale's pleasant voice enumerating loose things.

*A problem. You will know it when you see it.*

Somewhere in this frozen garrison, close to the bone, her father's shame was breathing. She could feel it now, the way one feels weather changing—a pressure behind the ears, a taste of coming snow. She did not yet know its shape. But it was here, in these wards and corridors, wrapped in wool and silence, and sooner or later it would cry out in the night, and she would know it, and knowing it would change everything.

Below her window, the light in the storehouse went out.

And in the dark, faint and far off—from the infirmary, from behind the wall where the nurses' cells gave onto the drying loft, from some direction she could not fix—she heard a sound she would remember all her life, though she would not understand it for weeks.

A baby, crying. Thin as a wire, brief as a bird's note, gone almost before it began.

She listened until the wind rose and erased it, and told herself she had imagined it, and knew she hadn't.
