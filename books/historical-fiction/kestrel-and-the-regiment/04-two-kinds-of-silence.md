## 4. Two Kinds of Silence

*Gideon*

The new nurse arrived on a Tuesday, in a hired gig with mud to the axles, and Gideon knew within the first quarter hour that she was lying.

She said her name was Eliza Vane. She said she'd come from York, that she'd nursed her mother through a long consumption and found she had a taste for the work. She said the pay was poor and the posting remote and none of it mattered, because a woman must be useful somewhere. All of this she delivered in a level, well-bred voice while unpinning her bonnet as though the infirmary were a drawing room she'd decided to tolerate.

Every word of it was rehearsed. Gideon had spent five years listening to men lie about their ages to get bread, about their wounds to get home, about their courage so they could sleep. He knew the grain of a prepared story the way a carpenter knows warped timber—by feel, by the slight resistance when he pressed it.

He also knew the Ashworth jaw when he saw it. She had the colonel's face worn soft, the way iron wears lace.

"You'll want the cot by the stove," he said. "The other rooms don't hold heat."

"And you'll want me to believe I've chosen that cot," she said, "when in fact you've put every nurse before me in it because it's nearest the door."

Gideon looked at her. She looked back, entirely pleasant, entirely guarded, and something in his chest—the thing he kept locked and inventory-kept like everything else in his life—shifted half an inch against its chains.

"The stove smokes if you feed it green wood," he said. "Welcome to the 44th, Miss Vane."

He hung her cloak on the hook farthest from his own, and told himself the distance was prudence. It was. It had to be.

* * *

*Eliza*

The infirmary smelled of carbolic, tallow, and cold stone, and its keeper moved through it like a man who owned nothing in it, not even his own shadow.

Eliza had met men who hid things before—her father chief among them—but Crane was a different order of concealment. Her father hid behind rank and rhetoric; this man hid behind usefulness. He was always doing something with his hands: wringing linen, grinding chalk, stirring the great pot of gruel that seemed to be perpetually at a simmer. His hands never stopped, and therefore his eyes never had to meet anyone's for longer than courtesy required.

Except they did. She caught him watching her twice the first day—quick, precise assessments, like a surgeon counting a pulse—and both times he looked away before she could return the look, which was itself an admission. Innocent men didn't count their glances.

Her father's letter sat folded against her ribs, its instructions terse even by his standards: *A matter requiring discretion. You will know it when you find it. Bring it to no one but me.* No one had told her what *it* was. She had crossed three counties on the strength of a sentence, telling herself that whatever shame her father was managing, managing shame was what daughters of Colonel Ashworth were for.

In the meantime, she would be a nurse. She knew enough for that—her mother's long dying had taught her fevers, poultices, and the particular patience of watching. And in the meantime, she would watch Mr. Gideon Crane, because whatever her father's problem was, this man's silence had the shape of it.

They circled each other over the infirmary fire those first evenings, after the last lamps were trimmed. He read. She mended. Neither spoke more than the ward's demands, and the not-speaking grew strangely companionable, two animals discovering they were denning in the same winter.

On the third night he looked up from his book—a slim volume she couldn't identify from across the room—and said, "You hold your needle like a fencer."

"I held my brother's side of every argument for ten years. One learns economy."

"He'll have taught you to watch hands, too."

"Among other things." She bit her thread. "Where did you learn, Mr. Crane? To watch?"

He turned back to his page. "At a table where watching was cheaper than talking."

It was the most honest thing either of them had said all week, and it frightened her how much she wanted him to say another.

* * *

*Gideon*

The cat began on Thursday night.

That is to say: the fiction of the cat began then, though the crying itself had started the moment Eliza Vane's gig cleared the garrison gates three days earlier, as though the child had understood she was to be invisible precisely until the one person arrived who might recognize her.

Gideon had managed it before. Mrs. Quill, the washerwoman, took the baby up to the loft above the laundry during the days; at night Gideon kept her in the dispensary behind the laudanum cabinet, where the smell of camphor masked the small human noises. He slept in four-hour fragments and woke at every whimper like a soldier waking to distant guns. Five years of practice had made invisibility a discipline, and discipline, he told himself, did not fail just because a woman with a liar's steady voice now slept forty feet away.

But infants do not keep discipline. On the fourth night, past midnight, Kestrel—for he had begun privately calling her that, the small hawk that hung motionless in bad weather—woke screaming with the colic, and no amount of camphor would hold it.

Boots on the stair. Lamplight.

Gideon was already in the corridor, holding a shovelful of dead coals he had grabbed from the stove for no reason he could later name, wearing the expression of a man interrupted at chores.

"Mr. Crane." Eliza stood wrapped in her blanket, candle raised. "There's a child crying."

"A cat," Gideon said. "Gets under the eaves. The wind funnels it—sounds uncanny."

"In February."

"Cats are indifferent to the calendar."

She listened. The crying rose, cracked, rose again—the unmistakable fury of an empty stomach attached to lungs. Eliza tilted her head with the terrible patience of a woman who had been lied to by experts since childhood.

"That," she said, "is a very large cat."

"Tomcats in these parts grow considerable."

"Mr. Crane." She came down one step. The candle threw her shadow long across the flags. "I was sent here by my father to deal with something. I don't know what. But I know this: whatever is behind that door, my father wants it gone, and you are standing between them. So you may tell me about the cat, or you may tell me the truth, but one of us is going through that door, and I think you would rather it were you."

For a long moment neither moved. The fire ticked in the stove below. Somewhere a patient groaned in his sleep, dreaming of Belgium or of home.

Then Gideon set down the shovel, walked into the dispensary, and brought her out.

She was so small. He had forgotten, in the weeks since the door, how small—fists like hazelnuts, the Ashworth crest stitched crooked into the swaddling where a servant's hurried hand had done it. Eliza looked down at the child, and Gideon watched three expressions cross her face in succession like clouds crossing a field: confusion, recognition, and a grief so sudden and complete that he understood, before any confession was possible, exactly whose blood ran in the bundle.

"She has—" Eliza stopped. Started again. "Who was her mother?"

"A woman whose name isn't written anywhere now," Gideon said carefully. "Not in any record of this regiment."

"And my father sent you a note."

Gideon went still. "How—"

"I know his hand on anything he doesn't sign." She touched the child's cheek with one finger, feather-light. "Forget this face. That's what it said, isn't it. Those words."

"Yes."

Eliza nodded slowly, once, twice, absorbing a blow in installments. Then, without a word about fathers or orders or the letter against her ribs, she rolled up her sleeves.

"There'll be milk in the kitchen," she said. "Warm it. If she's to be a cat, we may as well feed her properly."

* * *

*Eliza*

She fed the child at three in the morning by the banked fire, and fed her again at six, and somewhere between those hours Eliza Vane's life quietly divided into before and after.

The child—she refused, already, to call her *it*, refused the grammar of disposal—gripped her little finger with the whole serious force of her being, and Eliza thought: *my father watched this woman die, or arranged it, or simply let it happen, and then wrote five words.* She thought: *he sent me. Not soldiers. Me.*

That was the part that sat coldest. Not the sin itself—she had grown up adjacent to her father's sins, learning to read them in the pauses of dinner conversation. It was the delegation. Whatever was to be done with this child, he had wanted done gently, deniably, by someone who loved him. He had weaponized her obedience, and she had traveled three counties to be useful.

Crane sat across the hearth, watching her feed the baby with an expression she was beginning to catalog: not softness exactly, but attention, the total attention he gave to everything living. His hands, which never stilled, were still now.

"Why do you keep records?" she asked quietly. "The ones you hide. I've seen the notebook. I'm not sorry—I look for things; it's why I'm here."

He considered refusing. She saw that too—the whole machinery of his caution engage, deliberate, and then, for reasons perhaps unknown to himself, release.

"Because the army writes down what it can use," he said, "and forgets what it can't. Men die and their names go in no ledger, and then there is no widow's pension, no stone, no proof they stood anywhere at all. I write the names down. Where I can, I write what killed them. It's theft, technically. Regimental property, the truth is." A ghost of a smile. "I've stolen five years' worth."

"And if they catch you?"

"They will burn the notebook first and me second, and in between they will ask questions I won't answer." He said it without drama, the way he said everything, an inventory of facts. Then his eyes lifted to hers. "And now you're in it. You understand that. You fed her. There's no version of my father's"—he corrected himself, precise even now—"of *the colonel's* intentions that survives your knowing. Either you're leaving tomorrow to report all this, in which case I'd like time to move her tonight—or you're staying, and we had both better become very good liars."

Eliza looked down at the child asleep against her breast, this small unnamed granddaughter of a great man, breathing in the rhythm of the utterly innocent.

"I've been good at lying since I was nine years old," she said. "It's the family trade. But if we're to do it together, Mr. Crane, we will not do it with cats. Someone will hear her eventually. We need a better story, warmer clothes, and a plan."

"We?"

"You are standing between my father and this child whether you wish it or not. I am apparently the instrument of his wishes." She lifted her chin, and felt—for the first time in her adult life—that the gesture meant something. "So it seems the instrument has chosen its own hand."

Something moved across his face then, quick as a kestrel's shadow, and she recognized it because she felt the same thing and was equally unwilling to name it: relief. The strange, guilty relief of conspirators, which is the first cousin of trust, which is the first cousin of love.

"It's dangerous," he said. "Being near me is going to get more dangerous before it gets less."

"I was raised by Colonel Ashworth," Eliza said. "Dangerous is my native climate."

He almost smiled. Outside, the wind came off the marsh and rattled the shutters, and the child slept on, unaware that two exhausted people had just signed themselves, over a dying fire, into the same losing cause.

* * *

*Gideon*

The next evening, Adjutant Hale came to the infirmary.

Gideon heard the gig before he saw it—the adjutant's matched grays, too fine for a garrison road—and had time to sweep the dispensary, bank the fire, and compose his face into professional blandness before the door opened on a man smelling of bay rum and arithmetic.

"Nurse Crane." Hale removed his gloves finger by finger, taking in the ward with the glance of an auditor. "We haven't spoken properly. I make a point of knowing everyone who keeps... records."

"Everyone keeps records, sir."

"Not like yours." Hale smiled. It was a well-made smile, fitted to his face like a uniform. "I hear you had a disturbance last night. Crying, one of the orderlies said. In the east wing."

Gideon wiped down the table he was wiping, unhurried. "Cat under the eaves. The wind does odd things off the marsh."

"A cat." Hale walked the length of the ward, trailing one glove along the footrails, and paused at the dispensary door. Looked in. The camphor smell rolled out to meet him, thick as fog. "Curious animal, the marsh cat. Appears whenever it's inconvenient." He turned back, and his eyes were pleasant and bottomless. "Well. Cats come and go, Nurse Crane. The wise man asks only what they leave behind." He replaced his gloves. "Give my regards to Miss Vane. Her father speaks of her often. Family is such a—convenient thing, to have records of."

He left. The gig crunched away into the dusk.

Gideon stood a long moment in the doorway, hands steady, heart hammering the way it hadn't hammered even at Waterloo. Then he climbed the stairs to where Eliza sat with the child, and said the only thing that mattered:

"He knows something. We have three days, maybe less. We need a way out of this garrison that no ledger records—and we need it tonight."
