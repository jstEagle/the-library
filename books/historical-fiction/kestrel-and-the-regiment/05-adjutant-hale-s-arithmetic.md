## 5. Adjutant Hale's Arithmetic

Major Nathaniel Hale kept two ledgers on his desk, and only one of them was official.

The first lay open beneath the lamp, its ruled columns waiting for the day's entries: rations drawn, men returned to duty, men buried, ammunition accounted for. It was a clean book, a lawful book, and he wrote in it with the same hand he used for everything—small, upright, without flourish. The second ledger lived in the drawer on his right, locked, and it was not bound at all but folded loose into folios, because paper that could be burned quickly was worth more than paper that looked impressive.

He unlocked the drawer now, though the sentry outside had been changed not ten minutes past and would stand there for two hours without stirring. Habit was its own discipline. Hale believed in discipline the way other men believed in God—impersonally, thoroughly, and with the expectation that it would eventually save him.

The folio contained four items.

The first was a copy of the burial return for a woman named Margaret Sorrel, entered in the parish register of the town below the garrison under the word *fever*, which was true in the sense that all death is a fever. She had been pretty, ambitious, and careless with letters. Two of those qualities had been tolerable. The third had brought her to the attention of people who did not forgive carelessness, and by then it was too late for her and inconvenient for everyone else.

The second item was a letter, unsigned, from an informant in the town who reported that before her death Mrs. Sorrel—no one had ever verified the *Mrs.*, and no one had tried—had been seen walking twice of an evening toward the garrison infirmary, and once carrying a parcel the size of a loaf of bread.

The third item was a single sheet in Colonel Ashworth's own hand, written three weeks ago, containing eleven words: *Resolve the matter entirely. I do not wish to know the method.* Hale had read it four times the night it arrived and once since. It was not, strictly speaking, an order. That was the elegance of it. If matters went wrong, the sheet proved nothing except that the colonel had wished a matter resolved, and matters were always being resolved in winter garrisons—fevers, accidents, debts called in, men lost to ice and drink and their own despairing arithmetic. Ashworth's handwriting was merely the signature of a man who wanted quiet, and half of England wanted quiet; you could not hang a colonel for wanting what everyone wanted.

The fourth item was smaller than the rest and troubled Hale more than all of them combined.

It was a scrap of paper, torn from a cheap penny notebook, found by a laundress in the pocket of a dead private's shirt and sold to the adjutant's clerk for sixpence and silence. On it, in a small careful hand, was written: *Pvt. James Whitlow, b. Sheffield, 1791. Admitted 14 Nov. Died 19 Nov. Wounds to the left thigh, subsequent fever. Mother living, address unknown. He asked twice for his brother.*

Nothing seditious in it. Nothing actionable. A name, dates, a manner of dying, a fragment of grief. And yet Hale had sat with the scrap for the better part of an hour when it first came to him, turning it in his long fingers as a moneylender turns a suspect coin, because the handwriting was familiar. He had seen it before—in the infirmary's daily returns, neat and punctual, the only reports in the garrison that were never late and never wrong. The hand belonged to the head nurse. Crane. Gideon Crane.

*He asked twice for his brother.* Who recorded such a thing? What use was it? It proved nothing, settled nothing, purchased nothing. It existed only so that a dead man's asking would not vanish entire from the world—and that, to Nathaniel Hale, made it the most dangerous object in the garrison.

He understood at once, in the way he understood all things, as a problem of accounts.

A scandal was a debt. That was the first principle, learned young, learned hungry, in a attorney's back office in Chatham where he had copied out writs for six years before buying his commission with money whose origins he had never been asked to explain and never volunteered. Every scandal was a debt owed by someone to someone: a widow owed an explanation, a patron owed discretion, an institution owed the appearance of order. Most debts could be discharged quietly. Payment came in coin, or in silence, or—if necessary—in the removal of creditors.

But this nurse was minting creditors. Every name he set down in his forbidden little book created a claim upon the regiment—a family owed truth, a village owed a son's story, a future inquirer owed a thread to pull. Five years of such entries, if the laundress's scrap was any measure. Five years of small claims accumulating interest in a drawer somewhere in the infirmary. The principal was bad enough. The compounding was intolerable.

Hale dipped his pen and, on the inside cover of the unofficial folio, began a new list.

He headed it with a single letter—*C*—and beneath it wrote nothing at first. He simply looked at the letter and let his mind perform its cold arithmetic, the arithmetic no one had ever taught him because no one had needed to. He had been born knowing how to count lives.

Item: the woman Sorrel, dead. Cost of her silence: already paid. Residual risk: letters. There were letters—he was certain of it, women like her always kept letters, they kept them the way misers kept coins—and letters in the possession of no one were letters that might surface from anywhere. Findings: her lodging had been cleared within a week of her death by persons unknown. Either the letters were destroyed, or they were held. By whom? He wrote: *the child.*

There. He had avoided the word for three weeks, even alone, even here. He wrote it now and watched the ink dry and felt nothing, which he took as confirmation that he was the right man for the work. Others felt things about children. Feeling was a form of inefficiency.

Item: the child itself. Alive—that much the informant chain confirmed, though the chain grew unreliable exactly where it mattered most. Born of a dead woman and fathered by—well. Hale did not permit himself the word *Ashworth* either, even in ink. He wrote instead: *the bloodline,* which was cleaner. A dead mistress was a story. A living child was a *fact*, and facts could inherit. Facts could, thirty years hence, walk into a lawyer's office with a swatch of embroidered linen and a tale and begin the whole ruinous business over again. Scandals died with witnesses; bloodlines did not. The arithmetic was brutal and simple: every year the child lived multiplied the eventual cost. Every year it lived near the garrison multiplied it faster.

Item: Colonel Ashworth himself. Here the calculation turned delicate, because the colonel was simultaneously the cause of the debt and the principal debtor and—not least—the source of Hale's own advancement, his majority bought with recommendations, his place in the regiment secured by the old man's patronage. You did not burn down the house that fed you. But you did, occasionally, open the windows and let the smoke out, and if a little smoke escaped into rooms the owner preferred kept shut, well. Hale had watched Ashworth at the officers' mess these three weeks and catalogued the changes with a bookkeeper's satisfaction: the wine taken earlier each evening, the eyes that went to the window when a woman's laugh carried across the yard, the letter from London answered two days late. The colonel was bleeding confidence. A man bleeding confidence would sign anything that promised him the wound was closed. *Resolve the matter entirely. I do not wish to know the method.* Eleven words, and every one of them a door through which Nathaniel Hale could walk.

And the daughter. He set down *Vane, Miss E.* and paused over it longer than he had paused over anything else on the page.

She had arrived nine days ago with papers that named her a volunteer nurse, recommended by an aunt in Bath, and the papers were flawless, and that was precisely what troubled him, because nothing in the Ashworth family had been flawless in a year. He had made discreet inquiry and learned that Eliza Vane had not spoken to her father in fourteen months; that she had left his roof after a quarrel conducted at such volume that servants in two households still retailed it; that her aunt in Bath had expressed, in a letter to a mutual acquaintance, considerable surprise at learning her niece had gone nursing *to her father's garrison of all places on earth*. Surprise. The aunt had been surprised.

So the daughter had come either at her father's secret summons—which meant she knew something—or against his wishes, which meant she suspected something, and in Hale's experience suspicion in a strong-minded woman was more dangerous than knowledge in a weak one. Knowledge could be bought. Suspicion had to be managed. He had observed her four times in the infirmary yard: composed, watchful, her gaze going over the building the way a purchaser's gaze goes over a horse—checking the legs, the teeth, the hidden faults. Twice her attention had lingered on Crane.

Hale drew a line beneath the name and moved to the last entry, the one he had saved for last because it required the most thought, and the least feeling.

*Crane, Gideon. Head nurse, infirmary.*

What did one write about a man like that? Hale had interviewed him once, formally, over discrepancies in the autumn stores—discrepancies Hale himself had manufactured, to see how the nurse behaved under examination. Crane had been perfect. Not evasive, not nervous, not clever. Perfect: every figure produced instantly, every question answered plainly, and behind the plain answers a stillness like the stillness of deep water. Men who lied gave themselves away with excess. Crane gave away nothing at all, and nothing, sustained for twenty minutes under the eyes of the adjutant, was its own confession. An innocent man has small reactions—a flicker of indignation, a rush to defend himself. Crane had offered Hale the smooth surface of a pond, and somewhere beneath it, Hale was now nearly certain, lay five years of stolen names.

Including, perhaps, names that touched the matter at hand. Had Sorrel come to the infirmary in her last months? Had she given birth there, in the west room, in the night, with only nurses to witness? If so, Crane knew the child existed. Crane knew its face. Crane might even hold something in writing—of course he would hold something in writing; a man who records dying privates' last words would record a birth—and somewhere in that hidden book, between a Sheffield boy asking for his brother and some other mother's son coughing out his life, there might be a line that read: *Delivered this night, a daughter, to Mrs. S.—*

The scandal was not the child. Hale had understood this from the beginning, and understanding it made him, he believed, uniquely qualified among the colonel's circle to end the affair properly. The scandal was the *record*. England ran on records—baptisms, burials, commissions, debts—and a record in the wrong hands was a loaded pistol laid in a hallway where any passing servant might pick it up. A bastard child unrecorded was a rumor, and rumors starve. A bastard child written down in a nurse's notebook, dated, witnessed, described—was evidence, and evidence does not starve; it waits.

So the arithmetic resolved itself at last into its final terms, and Hale wrote them out in his small upright hand, because writing clarified everything:

One: locate the child. Confirm its condition and custody.
Two: locate the mother's papers. Secure or destroy.
Three: neutralize the record-keeper—not by violence, violence was the recourse of men who had run out of methods, but by law, which was inexhaustible. A nurse who falsified returns, who kept unauthorized documents, who harbored—yes, harbored was good—an infant of unknown provenance inside a military infirmary: such a man was not a victim awaiting violence but a culprit awaiting process. One did not kill Crane. One arraigned him. The regiment had courts for exactly this purpose, and courts produced paperwork, and paperwork buried a man deeper than any grave while leaving the diggers entirely clean.
Four: the daughter Vane. To be watched, not touched. A woman could be retrieved from sentiment; women came back to their families when the alternative grew frightening enough.
Five—

His pen stopped.

Five was the child again, returned to the bottom of the list because it could not be crossed off until everything above it was done, and because Hale's mind, reaching the end of its columns, had arrived at the sum and found it disagreeable. Everything else on the list could be handled with paper. The child could not. Paper did not unmake a person. Sooner or later the matter would come down to what the trade called, euphemistically, *removal*, and removal of that particular article required either great cruelty or great distance, and Hale told himself—as he had told himself for thirty years, in Chatham, in Portugal, in a hundred ledgers of other people's lives—that he preferred distance. Things arranged at distance. Men hired at distance. Conscience, such as it was, preserved at distance, folded up like the folio in the drawer, burnable at a moment's notice.

Outside, the wind came off the marsh and pressed against the shutters, and somewhere across the yard a dog began to bark, high and urgent, and stopped abruptly as though corrected.

Hale listened. The garrison at night was a body breathing, and he knew its sounds the way a physician knows a pulse, and this particular silence after the barking was not quite the ordinary silence. He waited a full minute, pen suspended, and heard nothing further, and logged the moment anyway, because he had learned never to waste an anomaly. Anomalies were entries too. Somewhere in this fortress of routines, something had disturbed a dog near the infirmary side of the yard, at an hour when honest men were asleep.

He closed the folio. Locked the drawer. Sat a moment longer with his hands flat on the official ledger, feeling the peculiar pleasure that always followed the completion of a list—the sense of a world reduced to items, items to methods, methods to costs, all of it payable, all of it manageable, nothing exempt from counting.

Then he added one final line to the official book, in his fairest clerk's hand, an entry so mundane that no court-martial would ever look at it twice: *Stores inspection, infirmary, Tuesday next. All departments to render full account of papers and provisions.*

Full account. Let the nurse explain his storeroom. Let him open his cupboards and his drawers and his careful little arrangements under the adjutant's eye, and let Hale watch that smooth pond of a face for the ripple—the flicker, the fraction of a delay—when the inspection came near whatever shelf held five years of names.

Across the yard, in the dark beyond the dog's correction, a shutter that should have been latched swung once against its frame and was stilled by a hand he could not see.

Hale blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, doing sums.

* * *

Tuesday was five days off. He gave it three, privately, in his own arithmetic—for plans, like debts, always came due early—and he was almost exactly right, though the thing that surfaced first was not what he expected, and it came not from his informants in the town nor from his clerk nor from the laundress and her sixpences, but from the youngest orderly in the infirmary, a boy named Thomas with frightened eyes and gambling debts, who appeared at the adjutant's office door on Friday morning pale as whey and asked, in the voice of a man selling his own soul at a discount, whether Major Hale was the officer to whom one might report irregularities of a medical nature.

Hale set down his pen, inclined his head with perfect courtesy, and indicated the chair.

Everyone, sooner or later, came to sell.
