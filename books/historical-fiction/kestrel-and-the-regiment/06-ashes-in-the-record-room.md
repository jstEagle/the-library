## 6. Ashes in the Record Room

The record room smelled wrong before Gideon ever opened the door.

He had tended that room the way other men tended a chapel—five years of small devotions, straightening the shelves of muster rolls, oiling the hinges so they would not cry out at midnight when he came to check a spelling or a date of enlistment. He knew its smells the way a mother knows her child's breath: candle tallow, damp stone, old glue, the dry sweetness of paper aging in the dark. On a Tuesday morning in the third week of December, climbing the narrow stair with a tray of gruel for Private Weller, he stopped on the landing because the air above him carried something that did not belong.

Smoke. Not woodsmoke from the kitchen chimneys, which lay over the garrison every morning like a grey blanket. This was thinner, sharper, with an undertone of char—he knew it intimately, the smell of paper burning, of ink curling off the page like a living thing trying to escape the flame.

He set the tray down on the windowsill and stood very still, listening. The record room above him was silent. The stair was empty. But the smell hung there, fresh, not yet dispersed by any draught.

Someone had burned paper. Recently. In his room.

Gideon Crane had survived five years in the 44th by making a discipline of stillness. When the surgeons raged, he was calm. When the officers blundered, he was invisible. When a man died badly and no one else would hold his hand, Gideon held it without trembling. Stillness was not passivity; it was armor, worn so long he had forgotten the shape of his own body beneath it.

But standing on that landing, smelling the smoke of someone else's fire, he felt the armor crack along a seam he had not known was there.

He climbed the last steps quietly, out of habit, though whoever had been there was long gone. The door to the record room stood ajar—an abomination. It was always locked; he kept the key himself, hung on the same nail as the infirmary keys, and it had never left that nail except to turn a lock. Now the door leaned open three inches, breathing cold air into the corridor, and inside—

Inside, the room had been searched.

Not ransacked. That was what chilled him most. Nothing thrown, nothing toppled. The shelves stood neat as parade lines. But Gideon's eye was a nurse's eye, trained to notice when a bandage had been rewrapped by hands other than the ones that first bound it—the small betrayals of order. A bundle of discharge papers sat half an inch proud of its neighbours. The regimental history, a book no one had opened since Waterloo, lay with its spine turned slightly toward the window instead of the wall. And on the great table where the clerks copied their fair pages, a scatter of ash had been brushed toward one corner—not cleaned away, merely tidied, as though the sweeper had run out of time or conscience.

Gideon closed the door behind him and set his back against it.

The ash. He crossed to the table and looked down into the corner they had tried to make tidy. Among the grey flakes were fragments too stubborn for the flame: the corner of a printed form, bearing part of a stamp—*44th Regiment of Foot*—and, caught in the crease of the table itself, a sliver of paper no bigger than his thumbnail, carrying three letters in a clerk's careful copperplate: *—ora.*

Marie Elena D'Oravay. The name the colonel's mistress had gone by in garrison, before she went by nothing at all. Gideon had seen it once, months ago, on a requisition for lodging signed by the quartermaster—a small administrative ghost, the kind of thing that survives in archives the way footprints survive in snow, telling you someone walked here once whether or not anyone meant to remember it.

He had thought of that requisition more than once since the night the child arrived at his door. Thought of it the way a man thinks of a loose tooth: touching it, wincing, unable to leave it alone. Because names in records are dangerous things. A woman who existed in the quartermaster's books might have a child who existed somewhere too, if anyone cared to look—and Gideon was keeping exactly the kind of man who looked.

Now someone else had looked. Someone with a key, or the authority to take one. Someone who had found the loose papers—the unbound leaves, the duplicates, the requisitions and letters and stray returns that accumulate in any archive like silt in a river bend—and had carried them out to some yard or brazier and fed them to a fire, then come back and put the room right again, believing that tidiness was the same as absence.

They had missed the sliver in the crease. They had missed, perhaps, other things. But they would not always miss them.

Gideon stood in the cold room with the dead woman's name under his thumb and understood, with the flat certainty of a surgeon who feels the wound before he sees it, that the fire in this corner was aimed at him.

* * *

He worked the rest of the morning as though nothing had changed, because everything about staying alive in the 44th depended on nothing changing. He fed Weller his gruel and held the basin when the morphine would not quite hold back the pain. He changed dressings, argued gently with the apothecary over quantities of laudanum, wrote up the day's admissions in his round, unremarkable hand. His hands did not shake. He made sure of it, watched them the way a man watches a horse he suspects of shying.

But beneath the routine his mind moved fast and cold, laying out the situation the way he laid out instruments before an operation—each danger named, each counted.

First: who. Not Hale himself; the adjutant's hands never touched actual work, and the ash had been brushed by someone hurried and frightened. A clerk, then, or an orderly, sent by Hale or by the colonel. It hardly mattered which. What mattered was that the order had been given, and orders of that kind were not given once. Men who burn evidence do not stop burning when the first fire gutters. They go looking for embers they missed.

Second: what else might burn. His mind went, inevitably and instantly, to the ledger.

It lay where it had lain for five years, sewn into the ticking of his cot mattress, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. Sixty-three names now. Sixty-three men the regiment had decided not to remember—deserters whose desertions were actually deaths, privates struck through by clerical convenience, wounded left off the returns so a battalion could report itself stronger than God had made it. Every name entered in his own hand, dated, witnessed where witness was possible. Five years of nights with a stub of pencil and his heart hammering, writing while the ward slept.

He had always known, in the abstract, that the ledger was dangerous. Knowledge was dangerous; ink was dangerous. But he had told himself, in the way of careful men, that his pages were hidden and therefore safe—that secrecy was a kind of shelter.

Standing over the swept ash, he understood the lie of it. Secrecy was not shelter. Secrecy was only delay. If Hale was hunting every scrap of paper connected to Marie Elena D'Oravay, how long before the search widened? How long before a frightened orderly remembered that Nurse Crane kept late hours, wrote often, asked questions no nurse needed to ask? How long before the cot was searched—not casually, as the record room had been searched, but thoroughly, with the methodical patience of men who had all the time in the world and a prisoner who had none?

And worse—worse than burning, because burning was quick—the thought arrived and would not leave: the ledger was not only evidence *of* him. It was evidence *against* others. Sixty-three families of omissions, each pointing upward to an officer who had signed a false return or a clerk who had obeyed one. In Hale's hands, the ledger was not a memorial. It was a lever. A list of men who could be squeezed, and a demonstration that Gideon Crane was a man who collected such lists.

He had thought he was keeping the dead. He saw now that he had been keeping hostages.

Third: the child.

Here his counting faltered, the way his voice sometimes faltered over the last prayers of a dying man. Everything else—the record room, the ledger, even his own neck—he could weigh like a surgeon weighing a limb: what must come off, what could be saved. But the child was not arithmetic. The child was Mary asleep in the linen closet behind a false panel, breathing that astonishingly small breath, her fist curled against her cheek as though the whole armed world were a thing she had decided to ignore.

They would burn the workshop. He had heard the phrase in his mind since the night the note came—*forget this face*—and he had thought it melodrama, the overheated imagination of a man who had seen too much death to calibrate his fears. He no longer thought so. Men who burned a dead woman's requisitions would not hesitate at a storehouse. Men who erased names from ledgers would not hesitate to erase witnesses. The fire in the record room's corner was not housekeeping.

It was a rehearsal.

* * *

In the afternoon he made himself go out to the yards, because a man who stays indoors after a discovery invites question, and question was the one currency he could not afford. Snow had begun again, fine as sifted flour, dusting the parade ground where a punishment detail was drilling. He walked to the well and back, nodded to Sergeant Bray, let the cold pack itself into his lungs, and looked—casually, a nurse stretching his legs—at the garrison's fires.

The cookhouse, banked and ordinary. The smithy, roaring at legitimate work. And behind the quartermaster's stores, half-hidden by a snow-buried cart, a black scar in the snow where something had burned in a bucket or a brazier—paper ash, drifted over but not extinguished, and among it a button. He did not go closer. He had seen enough. A regimental button, brass, the kind issued to clerks and orderlies. Someone had knelt there in the snow, feeding the fire page by page, and dropped evidence of their own in their hurry, and this too reassured Gideon in a grim, sideways fashion: the men sent to do this work were afraid. Afraid men made mistakes.

Fear, he thought, walking back, is the only ally I have that does not know it is mine.

Because he was afraid too—but five years of nursing had taught him the difference between fear that scatters and fear that focuses. In the wards he had watched brave men and cowards, and learned that the difference between them was rarely in the blood and usually in the task. A man given something to do with his terror—a tourniquet to tie, a door to hold—became steady as scripture. Gideon gave himself the task now, deliberately, the way he would assign a dressing change.

The ledger could not stay in the cot. Not tonight, not tomorrow night, not with the archive already smoking. Nor could it be destroyed—he would sooner have cut off his own hand; those sixty-three names were the only funerals some of those men would ever have, and he would not unring that bell. It would have to move. Somewhere outside the garrison walls. Somewhere Hale's tidy searches would not think to look until it was too late to matter.

And the child—his eyes went, without permission, to the infirmary roofline, the thin ribbon of chimney smoke rising from the ward where Mary slept twenty feet from the linen closet—needed what the ledger needed. A road. A name that was not hers. An adult willing to carry her past the checkpoints of other people's convenience.

He did not yet know who. The thought of strangers turned his stomach; the thought of anyone inside the garrison turned it further, because the button in the snow said plainly that the garrison leaked. Trust was a wound in a place like this, and wounds admitted infection.

But the problem had a shape now, and shape was mercy. All morning the dread had been a fog, total and suffocating. Now it condensed into tasks. Move the ledger. Watch the clerks. Learn who had the key besides himself. Keep Mary quiet, keep Mary warm, keep Mary—his mind supplied the word before he could stop it—*mine*, and he let it stand, because denying it had become more exhausting than admitting it.

* * *

Evening came down blue and bitter. In the ward the stove ticked, and Weller slept propped upright because lying flat drowned him slowly, and the orderlies moved on quiet feet among the beds. Gideon ate his supper standing at the dispensary counter, and it was there, ladling weak tea, that Thomas found him—young Thomas, seventeen and hollow-cheeked, who had been jumpy as a sparrow for a week and whose debts to the sutler Gideon had quietly covered twice without saying so.

'Beg pardon, Mr Crane.' The boy's eyes went everywhere except Gideon's face. 'Adjutant's clerk was asking after you. At supper. Asking when you finished in the record room, like.'

Gideon poured the tea with a steady hand and felt the floor tilt gently beneath his certainty.

'Asking when I *finished*?'

'Said there was papers wanted checking. Said the adjutant means to do a full inventory of the archive. New year, new order, he said.' Thomas swallowed. 'Mr Crane—they're not going to do away with the old records, are they? Only my mum's got letters from my da, from Spain, and if they burn—'

'Hush.' Gideon heard his own voice, low and even, the voice he used over wounds. 'No one will burn your father's letters. Give me your tray, boy, and get to the ward.'

Thomas went, relieved of the conversation if not the fear, and Gideon stood alone at the counter with the tea going cold in his fist.

A full inventory. Of course. Hale—or whoever directed Hale—had burned the loose papers and now wanted a pretext to go through everything bound and shelved, page by page, under the respectable banner of administration. And when the inventory reached the infirmary's returns, when some clerk cross-checked the admission books against the muster rolls, they would find discrepancies. Small ones. Deliberate ones. Sixty-three of them, each in a hand they would eventually recognize.

The net was not closing. It was already closed, and he had been swimming inside it thinking the water was the sea.

Gideon set down the cup. Through the dispensary's small window he could see across the yard to the quartermaster's stores, and beyond them the eastern wall, and beyond the wall, faint in the last light, the flat dark line of the marsh where the frozen channels braided through the reeds—a country without roads, without ledgers, without adjutants. A country that kept no records at all.

Five years he had spent believing that paper could protect the forgotten—that ink was a fortress. Tonight he had watched a fortress burn in a bucket behind the quartermaster's shed. Paper protected no one. Paper only testified, and testimony required a living tongue, and tongues required lungs, and lungs required that a man first survive the winter.

Very well. He would save both. The names and the child. He would find a way to spirit the ledger out from under the inventory's nose, and he would find a pair of trustworthy arms for Mary, and he would do it before the clerks' careful fingers walked shelf by shelf to the truth. He owed the dead their sixty-three names. He owed the living one small face he had been ordered to forget.

He was still standing at the window, marshalling the plan like a line of stretcher-bearers, when Eliza Vane came into the dispensary doorway with a candle, her shawl dusted with snow, and said, without preamble:

'Mr Crane. There's smoke in the east passage, and it isn't the stove.' Her eyes were level and very awake. 'And I don't think you'll believe me when I tell you what I found scorched on the floor beside it.'
