## 7. A Name for the Child

Eliza had learned in her first week at the garrison that night vigils were less about nursing than about listening. The infirmary after midnight was a country of small sounds: the tick of the stove cooling, the groan of frost working into the mortar, the wet rattle of Private Dunn's breathing from the far cot. She had been sent here by her father with instructions that were no instructions at all—*go and be useful, Lizzie; there is a matter I will speak of when you are settled*—and settled she was, three weeks now, and still he had not spoken it. So she listened instead. To the ward. And to Gideon Crane.

He was an oddity among nurses, and she had catalogued his oddities the way one catalogues symptoms: the way his eyes moved to a door before they moved to a face; the way he answered questions a half-beat late, as though translating them out of some private language; the way he never wrote anything where anyone could see him write it. She had seen him once, in the dispensary, slide a scrap of paper inside his coat with the speed of a card sharp, and she had said nothing, because she had her own silences and knew the weight of them.

Tonight the ward slept, or performed sleep. Eliza sat by the fire with a shawl around her shoulders and mending in her lap that she had not touched for an hour. The wind had come up off the marsh with a thin whine in it, pressing at the shutters like something wanting in.

And beneath the wind—there. Faint. A cry.

Not a cat. She had heard Gideon tell Hale's clerk it was a cat, twice now, and had watched the lie sit on him like an ill-fitting uniform. Cats did not sound indignant. Cats did not fall silent the instant footsteps passed and resume the moment they withdrew, with the timing of a creature that had learned discretion. Cats did not cry from behind the door of the linen store.

She set down the mending. Her heart was behaving foolishly, beating hard over nothing—a cat, a store-room, a man who lied badly about animals—and yet she crossed the ward on feet she kept deliberately quiet, as if her body had decided before her mind consented.

The linen store was cold and dark and smelled of starch and, faintly, of milk gone sour. In the corner, behind a screen made of an old blanket hung over a chair, there was a crate lined with a soldier's grey shirt, and in the crate, a baby.

The child regarded her with an expression of profound grievance, cheeks flushed, fists raised, having evidently exhausted herself crying and started again on principle. Dark hair stood up from her head in a cowlick that no amount of wind could have improved.

"Well," Eliza said, and the word came out soft and stupid. "Well."

She looked behind her at the door, at the dark rectangle of the ward beyond it, at the whole apparatus of duty and obedience that had carried her through twenty-four years like a boat carries cargo. *Find and dispose of a problem,* her father's letter had not quite said. *A delicate matter. You will understand when you see it.* She understood now. She understood with a clarity that felt like falling.

The problem had a face the size of her palm and a temper like a gunpowder store.

She lifted the child out of the crate. Warmth against her chest, a furious little heartbeat going like a wren's. The baby stopped mid-cry, considered this new arrangement, and grabbed a fistful of Eliza's collar with the decisiveness of someone annexing territory.

"I ought to fetch my father at once," Eliza told her conversationally. "That is what a dutiful daughter would do."

The baby blew a bubble.

"Quite," Eliza agreed, and sat down on the upturned pail, and began, with hands that shook only slightly, to unlace her bodice.

* * *

Gideon knew within a minute that someone was in his linen store, because he knew every sound the infirmary made the way other men knew their own pulse, and the sound it made tonight was Eliza Vane singing.

Badly. Under her breath. Something half-remembered, a nursery fragment trailing off whenever she forgot the tune. He stood in the doorway of the ward with a candle in his hand and five years of survival instinct screaming at him to walk back to his cot, and he did not walk back to his cot.

He had known this moment would come since the night the child arrived. Every morning since, waking in the grey dark, he had counted the days like a man counting steps to a gallows. Three weeks. He'd had three weeks more than he deserved, and in those weeks something had happened to him that he refused to name even privately: the child had become *his*, in the way that nothing had been his since before the Peninsula—the 3 a.m. feedings, the particular smell of her head, the ferocious concentration she brought to the project of one day holding up her own weight. He had built, without meaning to, a small architecture of love in a place designed for dying. And now Eliza Vane, the colonel's daughter, sent by God-knew-what hand, was sitting in his store-room feeding that architecture at her breast.

He pushed open the door.

She looked up. She did not startle, which he would remember later; she looked at him the way you look at a person you have been expecting, and she put a finger to her lips, though the child was already asleep, mouth slack at her chest, one fist still knotted in fabric.

"Three weeks," Eliza whispered. "You kept her three weeks, Mr. Crane, and told everyone it was a cat."

"A very large cat," Gideon said.

Something happened to her face then—not quite a smile; something more dangerous than a smile, the beginning of laughter held in check by circumstance—and Gideon felt the floor of his careful life tilt.

"Sit down," she said. "You look like a man awaiting sentence. I'm not giving you up. I'm asking why."

So he told her. Not everything—he gave her the shape of it rather than the substance: a dead mother, a swaddling cloth with a crest on it, a note instructing him to forget a face he saw every day. He watched her listen, watched the colour leave her and return, watched her arrive somewhere in the middle of his account and stop walking.

"The crest," she said. "Ashworth?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. He saw her take the fact the way soldiers take a wound—quietly at first, the full damage not yet assessed.

"My father," she said at last, "sent me here to find something. He would not say what." She looked down at the sleeping child, and Gideon watched her do arithmetic that cost her visibly. "She's my—"

"Don't say it." His voice came out harder than he intended. "Don't say it anywhere but inside your own skull. This garrison has walls with ears on both sides of them, Miss Vane, and I've survived five years by being nobody who knows anything. If you're going to be part of this, you must be nobody too."

"And if I'm not going to be part of it?" But her arms had already tightened around the child as she said it, betraying her completely, and they both heard it.

"Then put her down," Gideon said, "and walk out, and by tomorrow I'll have moved her, and by Friday there'll be no her at all, so far as you know. That's the mercy of not knowing. It can be taken away again."

Eliza Vane looked at him across the cold store-room, firelight from the ward guttering in the doorway, and Gideon thought: *now she goes*. Any sensible woman goes.

"Her name is Kestrel," she said.

"She hasn't got a name."

"She has now. I've just given her one." She shifted the baby against her shoulder. "There's a bird on the marsh road in the mornings. It hangs in the air over the reeds without moving, wings beating against the wind that ought to knock it flat. My governess said it was unnatural. I thought it was the bravest thing I ever saw." She met his eyes. "Kestrel."

Gideon found he could not speak for a moment. Five years of names written in secret, hundreds of them, men erased and inked quietly back into existence—and never once had he been asked to give a name, only to save them. This was different. This was the thing itself, unhidden, spoken aloud between two people in a room where saying it aloud was nearly a death warrant.

"Kestrel," he repeated, testing it. It fit. Names did that, sometimes, when they were true.

"It needs witnessing," Eliza said. "Christenings need witnesses, don't they? Otherwise it isn't real."

"There's no church in this."

"There's a fire, and a child, and two liars. It will do."

* * *

They did it properly, insofar as anything done at two in the morning in a linen store can be proper. Gideon fetched water in a chipped basin. Eliza laid the child on the folded grey shirt—the same cloth she had been abandoned in, the Ashworth crest turned discreetly inward—and dipped her fingers and touched the small forehead while the baby slept on, untroubled by liturgy, indifferent to consequence.

"Kestrel," Eliza said. "Daughter of nobody official, christened by conspirators, witnessed by God if He's watching, which at this hour I doubt."

"Amen," said Gideon, and meant it more than he had ever meant the word in a chapel.

Afterward they sat either side of the basin like pilgrims at a shrine, and the strangeness of their situation settled over them gently, the way snow settles—no violence in it, just accumulation. Two people who had spent weeks circling each other with careful lies suddenly had no lies left worth keeping. What remained was simpler and far more perilous.

"You should know what you've joined," Gideon said. "Whoever left her here knows where she is. Whoever wants her forgotten will eventually come and make certain of the forgetting. When that happens, everyone in this building is in the fire's path. I want you to hear me say it plainly, because you grew up safe, and safe people imagine danger arrives with trumpets. It doesn't. It arrives with paperwork."

"My father is paperwork," Eliza said, and there was something in her voice he hadn't heard before—not bitterness exactly; grief's dry cousin. "I've spent my life being managed by him. Sent here, sent there. Be useful, Lizzie. Be discreet, Lizzie. I always assumed the discretion was about Mother, or money, or some scandal too dull to interest me." She drew her knees up, chin on them, looking at the sleeping child. "It was about her. All along it was about a baby in a box."

"You couldn't have known."

"No. But I know now, and I find—" She stopped. Started again. "I find I am not my father's daughter in the way that matters. I hope you can forgive the observation."

"Miss Vane," Gideon said, "in the last hour you have fed an contraband infant, named her after a bird, and baptized her with tap water over a laundry basin. I think we're well past forgiveness. I think we're in conspiracy."

She laughed then—really laughed, low and helpless, one hand over her mouth to keep the ward asleep—and Gideon watched her laugh and understood with a sensation like vertigo that he was in terrible trouble of a kind no ledger had prepared him for. He had survived five years by wanting nothing. Wanting nothing, no one could take anything from you. He looked at Eliza Vane's face in the guttering light, at the child asleep on the shirt that named her blood, and knew the era of wanting nothing had ended at some precise, unrecoverable moment, probably the moment she had said *a very large cat indeed* without saying it at all.

The baby woke and began to fuss, and Eliza gathered her up without thinking, and the gesture was so natural, so entirely hers already, that Gideon had to look away at the wall.

"She'll need feeding again before dawn," Eliza said practically. "And you've been getting up for every one alone, haven't you? Don't lie. You have the look of a man who sleeps in instalments."

"There's a goat at Marsh Farm," Gideon admitted. "The widow takes payment in nursing her husband's leg. It's worked so far."

"It stops working tomorrow. I'll take the night vigils. No one questions a nurse sitting up with the fevered ward." She stood, rocking the child, and paused at the door. "Mr. Crane. Gideon. Whatever comes for her—it will come soon, won't it?"

"Yes."

"And you'll stand with me?"

"That," Gideon said, "is the only thing I've been certain of since the day she arrived."

She held his gaze a moment longer than safety allowed, and went.

* * *

Alone, Gideon took out the ledger—his true ledger, the one sewn into the ticking of his cot—and added nothing, because there was nothing to add. Kestrel would never appear in any regimental record. She existed now only in two memories and a basin of cold water, and perhaps that was the safest archive in England.

But he sat a while longer by the dying fire all the same, and let himself imagine, briefly, recklessly, an entry in a different book altogether: *Kestrel, born in winter, loved by two people who had no right to her and every obligation.*

Then he heard, through the wall, the faint sound of Eliza singing to the child—the same broken nursery tune, slightly better this time—and below it, outside, the crunch of boots on frozen gravel that was far too deliberate for a sentry's round.

Boots, stopping at the infirmary door. A pause. Then three slow knocks, and Adjutant Hale's voice, pleasant as a ledger line:

"Nurse Crane. You're awake. Good. We should talk about cats."
