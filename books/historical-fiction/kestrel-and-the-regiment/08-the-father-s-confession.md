## 8. The Father's Confession

The summons came by a corporal who would not meet her eyes.

"Colonel Ashworth requests Miss Vane at his quarters. Nine o'clock, miss. After the officers' mess clears."

*Requests.* Her father had never requested anything in his life; he had summoned, ordered, expected, and been obeyed. The word choice was its own message—this was to appear official, proper, a daughter visiting a parent in the ordinary course of things. Which meant that whatever he intended to say to her could not be said where the garrison might overhear it.

Eliza dressed with more care than she had given any toilette since leaving Tunbridge Wells. The grey wool that made her look plain and respectable. Her hair pinned severely. If he meant to use her, she would at least arrive as herself and not as an instrument already polished for his hand.

The walk across the parade ground took four minutes. The frost had come down hard that evening, and her boots rang on the frozen ruts like small struck bells. She counted them—one for every lie she suspected she was about to hear—and stopped counting when she ran out of fingers.

His quarters occupied the corner of the officers' wing: two rooms and a study that smelled of pipe smoke, brandy, and the particular loneliness of men who had spent forty years making other men stand at attention. A batman admitted her and withdrew without being told. Her father stood at the window with his back to the door, and for a moment—a long, strange moment—he did not turn around.

He had aged.

She had seen him last at Michaelmas, barely eleven months past, and he had been straight-backed then, vigorous, his voice carrying across a dinner table with the easy authority of a man who had never once doubted his right to occupy any room he entered. Now his shoulders had a settled stoop, and when he finally turned, the candlelight found lines around his mouth she did not remember drawing there.

"Liza." He said it softly. Not *Elizabeth*, which he used when she displeased him. Not *Miss Vane*. The old nursery name, deployed like a coin slipped to a beggar.

"Papa."

"You'll take a glass of wine." It was not a question. He poured two glasses himself, waving off the decanter on the sideboard as though servants' hands might contaminate what passed between them, and gave her the better of the two. Another message. Whatever came next was to be bought with small kindnesses.

She did not sit when he gestured to the chair. She remained standing, and after a moment his mouth tightened—the only sign that he noticed.

"You've been at the infirmary how long now? A fortnight?"

"A fortnight yesterday."

"And you find the work tolerable? Crane keeps an orderly establishment?"

"He does." She chose each word the way one crosses ice. "He is a remarkable nurse. Better than any surgeon's mate I've seen. The men trust him."

"Mm." Her father drank, watching her over the rim of his glass. "And in your nursing duties—in the ordinary course of the wards—have you encountered anything... irregular? Anything out of place?"

There it was. The question beneath all his questions these five days, since Corporal Hale's man had first met her coach at the crossroads with instructions. *Find the problem, Miss Vane. Your father requires discretion and speed.*

"I have seen gangrene," Eliza said. "I have seen fever, and amputation, and men who weep for mothers they will not see again. I have seen a great deal that is irregular, Papa. You will have to be more precise."

Something flickered in his face—pride, perhaps, or irritation at having raised a daughter who could fence. He set down his glass and crossed to the desk, and for the first time she saw the papers laid out there with such deliberate neatness that they must have been arranged and rearranged a dozen times before her arrival.

"Do you know," he said, not looking at her, "how a man's sins find him? They do not march up to the gate with colours flying. They seep. They come through the drains and under the doors. They wait years, patient as water, until one day—" His hand hovered over the papers without touching them. "One day you find the whole floor is wet and you cannot recall when it began."

"Papa—"

"A woman named Sarah Whitcombe died in November." He said the name flatly, quickly, the way a man pulls a tooth. "In lodgings in Deptford. Fever of the lungs. There was a child."

*There was a child.*

Eliza heard the words and felt them land somewhere below thought, in the body, the way a fall lands before the mind has understood the staircase. She kept her voice level. "What has this to do with me?"

Her father turned from the desk at last, and what was in his face was so raw and so old that her heart lurched despite everything—pity, pure and useless, for whatever had been eating him alive in this room before she ever arrived.

"Everything," he said. "It has everything to do with you, because you are going to bring that child to me."

* * *

The fire had burned low. Neither of them had moved to feed it.

She had made him tell it all. That was the thing she would remember afterward—not the facts themselves but the labour of extracting them, the way he circled and stalled and reached for the brandy twice, the way a man under interrogation will give up a street before he gives up a name. But she had learned interrogation in the sickroom too: patience, silence, the terrible gentle question asked at exactly the right moment. And Colonel Ashworth, who had faced French columns and mutinous companies and the War Office itself without flinching, could not withstand his daughter sitting very still by the dying fire, asking nothing, waiting for everything.

Sarah Whitcombe had been nineteen years old when he met her. A milliner's assistant, pretty in a way he described as "modest," which Eliza understood to mean *easily overwhelmed*. He had kept her three years, first in rooms in Woolwich, then in a cottage near the arsenal, then—when promotion came and prudence tightened its grip—in smaller rooms, farther out, visited less often. There had been promises. Of course there had been promises. There were always promises; that was the currency men of his generation carried instead of honour.

"She was content," he said, and it sounded like a prayer he was begging her to confirm. "She told me she was content."

"Were there letters?" Eliza asked quietly. "Did you write to her?"

A long silence. "A gentleman doesn't put such things on paper."

"No," Eliza said. "A prudent man doesn't. A kind man might."

He winced as though she had struck him, and she watched him reach for the discipline that had carried him through Breda and Corunna, watched him assemble his face piece by piece into something resembling command.

"The child was born in September," he went on, faster now, wanting it done. "A girl. Sarah took a chill—lying-in, they call it, the danger of it—and by November she was dead. The woman who attended her wrote to..." He stopped. Started again. "Someone wrote to me. Informing me of the circumstances. Demanding—there was talk of parish records, of settlement examinations, of establishing paternity through the Overseers—"

"So you sent someone to collect the baby."

"To resolve the matter," he said sharply, and then, catching her expression, quieter: "To protect my family. My name. Elizabeth, do you understand what a scandal of this nature would do? Thirty-four years of service. My pension. Your brother's prospects—I have a granddaughter, apparently, and I am also a colonel of the 44th, and I cannot be both. The world does not permit it."

"The world," Eliza said, "permits whatever powerful men agree not to see."

He looked at her for a long moment then—really looked, the way he had not done since she was a girl—and she saw something move behind his eyes that frightened her more than anger would have. Calculation. He was weighing her, the way he weighed terrain, the way he weighed casualties.

"The child is at Crane's infirmary," he said.

It was not a question, but she answered it anyway, because lying to him now seemed suddenly obscene. "Yes."

"How do you know?"

"Because I have held her, Papa. Because I fed her last night at two o'clock in the morning while Gideon Crane slept in his chair beside the stove, and because she has—" Her voice betrayed her, cracked on the words, and she hated herself for it. "She has your eyes. God help her, she has the Ashworth grey eyes, and no one at that infirmary needs a note or a crest to know whose blood she carries."

The silence that followed was the longest yet. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, a sentry called the hour.

"Crane knows," her father said at last, very softly. "Crane knows, and he has not reported the presence of an infant in a military infirmary to anyone. Do you know what that makes him, Elizabeth?"

"A decent man."

"It makes him a thief holding stolen property. It makes him a blackmailer, whether he has demanded anything or not. A man who possesses a dangerous fact about a colonel of the Crown, unreported, undocumented—that man is a threat to the security of this regiment. And threats to the security of the regiment are dealt with. Firmly. Quietly." He said it as if reciting from a manual. Perhaps he was. "Which is why you will bring her to me. Tonight. Tomorrow at the latest. Alone, without Crane's knowledge, without a word to anyone. You will place her in my care and return to your duties and speak of this to no one as long as you live."

"And then what happens to her?"

"She will be provided for."

"That is not what I asked."

"Elizabeth—"

"*That is not what I asked.*"

Her own voice startled her—loud in the shuttered room, ragged at the edges. She heard it echo and thought: *so this is what it sounds like, breaking with someone.* Not a snap but a tearing, slow, fabric giving way seam by seam while the wearer pretends not to notice.

Her father stood very straight now, the stoop gone, the colonel fully present, and when he spoke his voice had the flatness she remembered from her childhood punishments—the tone that preceded loss of allowance, confinement to rooms, exile to an aunt in Cheltenham.

"You forget yourself."

"No, Papa. For the first time in twenty-six years, I believe I am finding myself, and it is the most frightening thing that has ever happened to me." She rose. Her legs were unsteady and she locked her knees against it. "You ask me to bring you a motherless infant, alone, secretly, and you will not tell me what becomes of her afterward. You ask me to help make a human being disappear the way you made her mother disappear—rooms farther out, visits less often, no letters, nothing on paper. Well. Sarah Whitcombe died in Deptford in November, and everyone who loved her is conveniently gone, and now there is only a baby between you and your pension. So I am asking you plainly, and I will stand here until you answer me plainly: what happens to her when I put her in your arms?"

For one moment—one unguarded instant—her father's face opened, and she saw the truth there, saw it as clearly as she had ever seen anything: he did not know. He had not decided. Some part of him, some buried remainder of the man who had once promised a milliner's assistant the world, wanted desperately to hold his son's—or rather his own—child and weep. And another part of him, the larger part, the part that had been promoted thirty-four years running, had already drafted the answer in the cold arithmetic of reputation, and the answer was a carriage to a distant village, a paid couple, a name that was not Ashworth, and a life conducted entirely off the record—or failing that—

He closed his face. That was the horror of it, she thought. Not the sin, but the ease with which the door shut on it.

"She will be *provided for*," he said again, and now the words sounded like what they were: a sentence handed down. "And you will bring her to me, Elizabeth, because you are my daughter, because you owe me obedience, and because—" and here he played his last card, and played it without hesitation, which told her everything about how far he had fallen, "—because if you do not, others will do it for you. Men less gentle than I am. Men whose methods I cannot always govern. Adjutant Hale, for one. Do you imagine I dispatched you here merely to *nurse*? I sent you because you are the clean instrument available to me. Be the clean instrument, Liza. It is the kindest version of this night that exists."

*The kindest version of this night.* She turned the phrase over and found nothing inside it but threat.

"Yes, Papa," she heard herself say, in a voice borrowed from her girlhood, docile and empty. "I understand."

Relief loosened his shoulders. He believed her. That was the final wound—that after everything, he still believed she was his to command, that twenty-six years of filial duty constituted a debt he could call due whenever the ledger grew inconvenient. He crossed the room and, wonder of wonders, kissed her forehead, his lips dry and papery against her skin, smelling of brandy and camphor.

"Tomorrow evening," he murmured. "After dark. The north postern; the sentry there is mine. Bring her wrapped so she cannot be seen."

"Yes, Papa."

At the door she paused, her hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"Her name," she said, "is Kestrel. We gave it to her ourselves, because a kestrel hovers against the wind, Papa—it holds itself steady in the gale where other birds must land. I thought you should know, since you will not ask."

She heard him inhale to speak and left before he could.

* * *

The frost had thickened. She crossed the parade ground fast, not feeling the cold, her mind running like a spilled reel—*the north postern, the sentry is mine, men less gentle than I am, Hale, Hale, Hale*—and it was only halfway back to the infirmary that she realized she was no longer afraid of her father at all.

She was afraid for Gideon. Because her father had said *others will do it for you*, and he had named Hale, and Hale had been seen twice this week walking the lane behind the workshop with his hands clasped behind his back, counting windows.

And because tomorrow after dark, someone was expecting a baby carried alone through the north postern—and Eliza Vane had just discovered that she knew another road entirely, south, through the marsh, where the fog came up at dusk so thick that even riders sent by adjutants could not follow it.

She reached the infirmary door and stopped, breath smoking in the lamplight, and before she could raise her hand to knock, the door opened. Gideon stood in the frame with the lamp behind him, awake, dressed, a look on his face that told her he already knew something was wrong.

"Miss Vane," he said quietly. "You'd better come in. There's been a fire—at the record room. And I think we are past the time for telling each other half of anything."

She stepped inside, and the door closed behind her on the cold.
