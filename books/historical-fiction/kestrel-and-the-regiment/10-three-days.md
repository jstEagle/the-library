## 10. Three Days

*Gideon*

The first of the three days began with lice and ended nearly at the gallows, and in between Gideon peeled forty potatoes and changed eleven dressings and smiled at Adjutant Hale as though his chest were not a drum.

Morning roll-call came at seven, breath steaming in the yard like the whole garrison were cattle brought to market. Gideon stood in his place among the infirmary staff, hands folded, face arranged into the mild blankness that had kept him alive for five years. Hale walked the line slowly, cane tapping the frozen mud, and stopped in front of him for one heartbeat longer than courtesy required.

"You look tired, Crane."

"The fever ward was restless, sir."

"Fever wards often are." The cane tapped once against Gideon's boot—a small sound, almost affectionate, and entirely a warning. "Three days is a generous interval. I should hate to see it wasted."

Hale moved on. Gideon did not exhale until the adjutant's grey coat disappeared into the officers' mess, and even then he gave the exhalation only in part, holding the rest of it in his body like coin he could not afford to spend.

The morning passed in ordinary labours, and this was the discipline of it: to do the ordinary things *well*. A nurse planning flight who neglected his rounds was a nurse someone would remember. So Gideon bathed Private Callum's rotting foot and packed it with comfrey, and he listened to Mrs. Harkness complain about the price of soap, and he laughed—actually laughed—at orderly Peters's impression of the chaplain, because a man whose laughter rang false would be noticed by people paid to notice.

In the afternoon, carrying a basket of soiled linens toward the wash-house as cover, he detoured through the reed path behind the stables and walked the first mile of the marsh route with his eyes working like a clerk's.

The frost had hardened the ground, which was good for wheels but bad for hoofprints—every cart track would be printed in the mud like an accusation. He noted where the path forked at the drowned willow; noted that the left branch, though it looked the surer going, ran within sight of the ford watch-post for a quarter mile. Right branch, then. Longer, wetter, but screened by rushes tall enough to hide a woman standing upright.

He crouched at the waterline, pretending to rinse linen, and studied the ford itself. Low water after three days without rain—the stones showing dark and bare, crossable at a walk. But the watch-post's fire was visible from the crossing, which meant the crossing was visible from the watch-post, and no amount of fog he might pray for could be entered on a schedule.

There was a third way. It came to him as he watched a heron lift out of the shallows on wings like a folded banner: the old peat-cutters' causeway two miles north, half-drowned, passable only at low tide, known to perhaps six living men—and to Gideon only because a dying sapper had described it to him last spring, drawing it on the sheet with a finger too weak to hold a pencil. The sapper's name was in the ledger. It seemed suddenly right that the man should go on helping after death, when no one had helped him enough while he lived.

Low tide came at dusk on the third day. That fixed everything. Dusk on the third day, the causeway, one cart.

Gideon rinsed the linen until his fingers ached, then walked back through the garrison gate past the sentry, who nodded at him the way one nods at furniture.

That evening, the near thing came.

He was in the dispensary, measuring out laudanum for the fever ward—a legitimate task, a necessary task—but tucked inside the cabinet's shadowed corner lay the bundle he had been assembling all day: a change of clothes for the child sized for growth, a tin of the milk-thickening mixture, Eliza's travelling cloak smuggled out of the linen store fold by fold across four separate trips. He was counting drops into a glass when the door opened without a knock and Sergeant Bray filled it, holding a requisition chit.

"Laudanum. Two ounces. Adjutant's orders."

"Two ounces is a poisoner's purchase," Gideon said mildly, writing the entry. "Your colonel must have a great many headaches."

"Adjutant's orders," Bray repeated, and his eyes went past Gideon's shoulder to the cabinet—to the corner—to the cloak's edge, just visible, dark wool against dark wood.

Gideon did not turn around. Turning would confirm it. He finished the entry, corked the bottle, weighed both ounces into a paper twist with a steady hand, and held it out. "Sign the register."

Bray signed. His eyes lingered one second more on the cabinet, and then he took his poison and left, boots ringing down the corridor.

Gideon stood very still for a long moment. Then he moved the cloak to a different hiding place, deeper, inside a trussed mattress awaiting repair, and wrote in the margin of his memory, in letters that did not need ink: *Bray has seen something. Whether he knows what he saw is the question on which our lives now turn.*

*Eliza*

Eliza had spent her whole life learning to lie to one man, and it was astonishing how well the skill generalised.

Her father believed she had come to the garrison out of filial duty and would carry out whatever obscure errand he intended. Hale believed she was a decorative nuisance, a colonel's daughter playing at nursing until the cold drove her home. The other nurses believed she was what she appeared. Only Gideon knew she was none of these things, and Gideon, she was learning, could be trusted with a truth the way other men could be trusted with a rifle—he would keep it clean, keep it oiled, keep it pointed away from you until you asked for it.

She maintained her routines with a rigour that surprised even herself. She sat with the burned men of the old Welsh company and read them the shipping news from a month-old paper because it was the least martial reading in the infirmary, and they loved her for the ordinariness of it. She took her meals in the mess. She let Lieutenant Sallow bore her about hunting dogs at dinner, and smiled in the correct places, and thought, all the while, *by Friday I will never smile at anything in this county again.*

The plan came to her in pieces, passed in fragments that would mean nothing to any listener: Gideon mentioning at the wash-line that "the tide serves late on Thursday"; herself answering Mrs. Harkness, loudly, that she had always wanted to see the marsh flowers before they went to seed. Flowers in January. Mrs. Harkness laughed at town-bred ignorance and thought nothing further, and that night Eliza walked the reed path alone under a scud of cloud, memorising the route, feeling with her boots for the firm ground the way she had once felt for the truth about her father—tentatively, dreading what she'd find.

She practised the story until it lived in her mouth like her own teeth. A cousin in York, widowed, unwell, requiring companionship through the spring. She had told Mrs. Haskett at the mess. She had told the chaplain. By the second telling she found she could produce a small moist grief at will, and despised how easily it came, and told it again anyway.

The child was the hard part—not the keeping of her secret, which Eliza had done now for two nights, but the leaving of her in the laundry basket during the day, wrapped and dosed with just enough poppy syrup to sleep, hidden among sheets that smelled of lye while Eliza made conversation ten yards away. Every hour she spent being convincingly dull at a bedside was an hour the baby spent alone in woven darkness, and every time Eliza lifted the blanket to check that small breath still stirred it, her heart performed the same terrible trick: relief and guilt in one contraction, indistinguishable.

On the second night she took the vigil again. The infirmary long ward slept in its rows of moans and silence. Kestrel—for they had named her now, privately, the name passed between Eliza and Gideon like contraband—Kestrel woke at two and would not settle, and worked herself up from fretting to crying in the rising, hopeless way of infants, and Eliza paced the dispensary with her, whispering nonsense, feeling the panic climb her spine with each cry. The walls here were lath and plaster. Sound carried to the corridor, the corridor to the officers' wing.

The door opened.

She turned with the baby already moving behind her, one practiced sweep of the arm, into the shadow of the linen press—and Sergeant Bray stood in the doorway with a lantern, blinking, obviously summoned from the mess by the noise.

"Nurse Vane? You're wanted—there's a man come in with a gut wound, they said fetch the—"

"I'm coming." Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "Give me a moment to compose myself; it won't do to meet blood with a flushed face, the men gossip."

Bray hesitated. The lantern light swung. Behind Eliza, in the black seam beside the linen press, Kestrel hiccupped—one small, wet, unmistakable sound—and Eliza talked straight over it, louder, stepping forward into the light to fill the room with herself. "A gut wound, you say? Then we've no time for my complexion. Lead on, Sergeant."

And Bray, who had perhaps seen nothing, or perhaps seen everything and not yet decided what it was worth, lowered the lantern and led on.

She handed the case to the duty surgeon's assistant and did her work, and her hands did not shake until it was over, and then they shook so badly she had to sit down in the corridor among the smell of lamp oil and blood and laugh once, silently, at the size of the thing she had undertaken. Three days. Two now. One small person's entire life balanced on her ability to seem boring.

She went back at four and found Kestrel asleep, fist curled at her cheek, utterly unaware. Eliza put her lips to the small warm head and made a promise there in the dark, with no witness but the pressing linens: *I will get you out. Whatever it costs me, whoever I stop being to manage it—I will get you out.*

*Thomas*

Orderly Thomas Prewitt knew exactly how much a man's silence cost, because he had been buying his own by the week since September.

Eight pounds he owed to the loan-shark who drank at the Bell and Anchor beyond the palisade, and eight pounds was four months' pay, and the interest grew like the fever ward grew, quietly and then all at once. When the gentleman had approached him after chapel—fine coat, soft voice, questions about the infirmary and its comings and goings—Thomas had told himself it was harmless. Who was hurt by answers? What was a man supposed to do, ruin himself over principle? Principle didn't eat.

So he had answered questions. Small ones. Whether Nurse Crane kept to his room at night. Whether the new nurse, Miss Vane, received visitors. Whether the storehouse lock was old.

And then the gentleman had smiled in a way that closed like a trap and said, *good, very good, and if you see anything regarding carts or absent persons, there'll be five more pounds in it,* and Thomas had understood, in the slow cold way of understanding terrible arithmetic, that he was not the gentleman's informant so much as the gentleman's instrument, and that instruments got used and then broken.

Meanwhile, the others were letting him in.

It had happened on Monday, in the woodshed, all of them together—Crane, Miss Vane, Peters, old Mrs. Harkness, even quiet Corporal Dunn from the far ward—and Thomas had stood among them with his heart hammering while Crane laid out the plan in a voice low and even as a psalm. The causeway. The tide. The cart loaded with turnips over the false floor. Each man's part named plainly, no drama, as if arranging shifts.

"And you, Thomas," Crane had said, looking at him with those calm grey eyes, "you'll drive the milk cart to the north gate at four as usual, and see nothing, and say nothing, which I know you can do because you've been doing it beautifully for some time."

Thomas had gone hot all over. *Some time.* Did Crane know? Could he know? No—no, it was only Crane's manner of speaking, weightless words that landed heavy. Thomas had nodded and promised, and meant it, and hated how much he meant it, because meaning it meant he was the kind of man who could betray it.

For two days he swung between poles like a bell. At night he lay in the orderlies' dorm listening to Peters breathe three feet away—one of the conspirators now, sworn over a shared pipe—and imagined the fine-coated gentleman's voice: *anything about carts, Prewitt, anything at all.* Five pounds. Eight pounds plus interest. A man couldn't hang for debt, but a man could surely hang for what Thomas was now inside of. Helping a stolen child flee a colonel. Obstructing Adjutant Hale, who had removed men before—everyone whispered it, no one proved it.

Wednesday evening, he cracked—not all the way, but enough. He was carrying slops to the pit behind the cook-house when the gentleman fell into step beside him, smelling of bay rum and money.

"Well, Prewitt?"

"Nothing," Thomas said. "There's nothing."

The pause lasted exactly long enough.

"That's a pity." Coins clicked in a gloved palm. "Because I heard the infirmary bought a false-bottomed cart from Wheelwright's on Tuesday. Curious purchase, for an establishment that doesn't move goods. Curiouser still that Wheelwright says a young orderly carried the deposit."

Thomas's whole body went to water. The slop bucket rang against his knee. He heard himself say—"That was for—milk. We buy milk."

"In January. In a false-bottomed cart." The smile again. "Five pounds, Prewitt. Ten. And forgiveness of the other debt, if the cart leaves and I hear of it beforehand. Or—" the voice dropped to something almost kind— "I mention your borrowing to Sergeant Bray, who mentions it to the paymaster, and you're dismissed for debt and imprisoned for conspiracy, because you've just implicated yourself in the milk story, haven't you? Conspiracies require liars, and liars require remembering their lines."

Thomas stood in the freezing dark after the gentleman left, and looked at the garrison lights, and understood that whichever way he moved next, someone in those lit windows would pay for it.

He went and found Crane.

He found him in the dispensary doing inventory and stood in the doorway unable to speak, and Crane looked up and set down the ledger—*the* ledger, Thomas realised, the famous forbidden one, lying open on the desk as casually as a prayer book—and waited.

"I spoke to a man," Thomas said. His voice came out in pieces. "After chapel. Weeks ago. I answered questions. About you. About the storehouse. And tonight he—Crane, he knows about the cart. He offered me money to tell when it goes, and threats if I don't, and I couldn't—" He stopped, swallowed. "You should change everything. You should leave me out of it. You shouldn't trust me with another thing."

Crane was quiet for the space of three heartbeats. Then he said, "Thank you for telling me," and it wasn't absolution, it was simply accurate, and somehow that was worse and better than anything Thomas had braced for.

"How late?" Crane asked. "When did he want to know?"

"He said—if the cart leaves. Beforehand."

"Then we have until it moves." Crane closed the ledger and his face did something Thomas had never seen it do: it stopped hiding. Underneath five years of mildness there was stone. "Go back to your dormitory, Thomas. Walk slowly. If anyone asks, you've been sick. From tomorrow you'll know nothing, because I'll make certain there's nothing left to know—the plan you betrayed will stop existing tonight, and the one that replaces it, no one will tell you."

Thomas nodded, and turned to go, and at the door Crane called softly after him:

"Prewitt. You did the harder thing just now. Most men never do it at all."

Thomas walked slowly back across the yard as ordered, and it was true that he was sick, in the way Crane had prescribed, and it was also true that somewhere under the fear a small coal had been lit that no loan-shark would ever put out.

*Gideon*

The second day ended in rehearsal and the third began with the plan rebuilt from rubble.

They abandoned the false-bottomed cart entirely—Wheelwright's was compromised, and possibly Wheelwright. In its place: Mrs. Harkness's own gig, the one she used for market, loaded openly with her fortnightly shopping as if she herself were bound for the Bell and Anchor road, which she would be, loudly and verifiably, driven by Peters, seen by everyone. Half a plan built to be seen. The real cargo would travel differently.

At midday Gideon carried a hamper of "boiled linens" out the north gate on the milk cart's usual run—Thomas driving, silent, staring ahead, knowing nothing now except the road. The hamper held not linens but Kestrel, dosed and sleeping, packed between warm stones and folded blankets, and Gideon walked beside the cart with his heart in his ears the entire four miles, certain at every wheel-turn that a hand would fall on his shoulder. Nothing fell. They transferred the hamper to the peat-cutter's hut at the causeway head, where Eliza waited in borrowed clothes with her hair pinned under a man's cap, looking so unlike a colonel's daughter that Gideon's chest ached with admiration and terror in equal measure.

She would wait out the hours until dusk with the child, in the hut, alone. There was no other arrangement that held together. They stood a moment facing each other over the hamper while the wind combed the rushes, and everything they had not said filled the space between them like floodwater.

"Dusk," Gideon said. "If I'm not at the causeway by full dark, take the right-hand fork and don't wait."

"Don't say that."

"It's the plan. Plans are promises made to people we may disappoint. Say you understand."

"I understand," Eliza said, and did not say she understood, in the way women mean it, that plans are also prayers, and she gripped his hand once, hard, and pushed the hut door shut between them.

He made the return trip by a different path, arriving at the garrison's south post at the changing of the watch, dirty, plausible, empty-handed.

Sentry Hobbes waved him through—and then, as Gideon passed, called after him.

"Beg pardon, Nurse Crane. Sergeant Bray's asking after you. Says there's a matter for the adjutant." Hobbes shifted his musket, apologetic. "Says it's urgent. Says you're to come to the guardroom directly."

Across the yard, beyond the guardroom windows, lamplight burned in Adjutant Hale's office. Gideon could see the silhouette of a man at a desk, unhurried, writing.

Dusk was four hours away. The tide would serve at dusk, and only at dusk, and the causeway would drown after it, and somewhere in a peat-cutter's hut a child slept who had been erased before she could even speak.

Gideon turned toward the guardroom, arranging his face into mild blankness, and began composing, sentence by sentence, the most important lie of his life.
