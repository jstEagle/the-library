## 11. The Informer

Thomas Pell had learned to count before he had learned to be brave, and in the nineteen years of his life the two skills had never once been useful together.

He counted now, sitting on an upturned bucket behind the infirmary storehouse, his hands cupped around a stub of tallow candle that he dared not light. Eleven pounds, four shillings, and sixpence. That was what he owed to a man named Corrigan who kept a ledger of his own—the kind that did not forgive, the kind that sent two quiet gentlemen to stand at the edge of a lamplit street and simply wait, saying nothing, until the debt spoke for itself. Thomas owed them since summer. He had paid three shillings since Michaelmas. The interest grew like something alive, something with teeth, and every night he lay on his pallet in the orderlies' quarters doing sums until the numbers became faces and the faces became hands.

Eleven pounds was more than a year of his wages.

The child had come to the infirmary in November. Thomas had carried water for her, fetched clean linen, held her once—only once—when Mrs. Rowley's rheumatism made her fingers useless, and the baby had gripped his thumb with a strength that astonished him, as though she were already refusing to let go of the world. She had eyes the colour of strong tea. She had no name yet that anyone would say aloud, though he had heard Nurse Crane, in a careless hour, murmur *kestrel* over her like a password.

Thomas told himself, in those first weeks, that he knew nothing. That was the trick of surviving at the garrison: you knew nothing carefully, deliberately, the way Mr. Crane knew everything quietly. You kept your head down and your mouth shut and your debts somewhere else.

But then came the marsh.

* * *

It began, as most dangerous things did at the workshop, with Gideon Crane being reasonable.

Thomas had been mending the hinge on the storehouse door—a task assigned because his hands shook less when they were busy—when Crane appeared beside him with a lantern and an air of discussing nothing in particular.

"The cart route through the reed-beds," Crane said. "You know it."

"Everyone knows it."

"Not the ford at low water. Not which path drowns a horse in February." Crane set the lantern down on the sill. His burned hands were bandaged still, the right one swathed so that he could barely hold the lantern's ring, and yet he held it steady enough. "I need someone who knows the ground well enough to walk it blindfolded. Someone young. Someone no officer would think to question."

Thomas's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. "Mr. Crane—"

"You'd drive the second cart. Empty. A decoy, if it comes to that—but I don't believe it will come to that." Crane looked at him, and there was nothing in his face but patience, which was somehow worse than anger. "I wouldn't ask if there were anyone else. The others are watched too closely. You, Thomas—they barely see you. It's a cruel thing I'm saying, and I'm saying it anyway, because a child's life is weighing in the scale."

That was the trouble with Gideon Crane. He never lied to you about the cost. He only asked you to pay it.

Thomas said yes. He heard himself say it from a great distance, as though the word belonged to some braver cousin of his. Yes, he knew the ford. Yes, he could be ready. And all the while, in the ledger behind his eyes, Corrigan's neat hand was adding figures, and the two quiet gentlemen were standing at the edge of the lamplight, and eleven pounds four and six sat on his chest like a stone.

For six days he helped plan the escape of a baby whose thumb-grip he still felt in his sleep, and every night the arithmetic got louder.

* * *

Hale found him, of course. Or rather—Hale arranged to be found by him, which was the same thing done more elegantly.

It was Thursday evening. Thomas had been sent to the adjutant's office with the weekly returns, a bundle of papers tied in string, and he stood in the doorway of that cold, orderly room feeling the way he always felt in offices: like dirt that had learned to walk. Adjutant Hale sat behind his desk without looking up, pen moving in small precise strokes, and Thomas waited, and counted—one, two, three—because counting steadied him.

"Pell," Hale said at last, without raising his eyes. "Set them down."

Thomas set them down.

"You have brothers, I believe. In Nottingham." Hale finished his stroke, blotted it, and looked up. His face was pleasant the way a frozen pond is pleasant. "A weaver and a currier. Good trades. Steady work, if a man can keep steady."

"I—yes, sir."

"And your mother keeps the little shop by the bridge. Widow Pell's. Candles and thread." Hale folded his hands. "Corrigan is a client of mine, you know. Gambling debts, mostly. He speaks to me of certain accounts from time to time. Yours has been mentioned."

The room did not change—same grey walls, same smell of ink and wet wool—but Thomas felt it tilt, the way a boat tilts when something large moves beneath it.

"Eleven pounds," Hale said softly. "Four shillings. Sixpence." He drew a slip of paper from a drawer and laid it on the desk, face up. It took Thomas a moment to understand that it was a note of hand, drawn and signed, discharging the debt entire. "Debts are a form of noise, Pell. I deal in silence. You may buy yours."

Thomas's mouth was dry as flour. "What would you want me to tell you, sir?"

"Nothing." Hale rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the parade ground where sleet was beginning to stipple the mud. "Nothing at all. I want you to listen. Nurses talk. Orderlies carry things. When Crane moves—I don't know when, and I don't need to know how—you will send me a message. A chalk mark on the pump handle will do. That is the whole of your duty. One mark of chalk, and your mother's shop stays open, and Corrigan's gentlemen find other business." He turned back, and his smile was thin as paper. "Or you may refuse. Men do. And afterwards their families write them letters wondering why fortune has turned so strange."

Thomas stood very still. He thought of the baby's fist around his thumb. He thought of his mother's cracked hands dipping wicks, of Ned in the weaving shed coughing lint into his handkerchief.

He thought: *it's only a mark. It isn't the cart. It isn't the child. It doesn't stop anything.*

That was the sentence his mind built for him, brick by brick, because a man cannot betray himself all at once; he can only mislay himself a piece at a time.

"All right," he said.

Hale picked up the note of hand and pressed it into Thomas's palm, folding his fingers over it almost gently. "Wise boy. Chalk on the pump. Nothing more." And then, as Thomas reached the door: "Pell. If Crane suspects you—if anything delays him—remember that I'll know it was you either way. Best be sure of what you're sure of."

Outside, the sleet had thickened. Thomas walked back to the workshop through the freezing dusk with the note of hand burning in his pocket like a coal, and told himself the lie again, over and over, the way one tells any lie that must last: *a mark of chalk isn't a door closing. It's only weather.*

* * *

He did not mark the pump that night. Nor the next.

He told himself he was waiting—that Crane might delay, that the whole scheme might dissolve, that God or chance might relieve him of the choice. He scrubbed floors. He carried the baby's washing. He avoided Gideon Crane's eyes the way a man avoids a mirror after doing wrong, and Crane, gentle fool, mistook his avoidance for nerves about the journey and pressed a hunk of bread and cheese on him with a word about keeping one's strength up.

On Saturday night, Thomas heard the date.

He was stacking bowls in the scullery when Mrs. Rowley said to Eliza Vane—not loudly, not secretly, just with the flat confidence of women speaking in their own kitchen—"Tuesday, then. Fog comes off the marsh early Tuesday. He'll want the tide."

Tuesday. Two days.

That night Thomas lay awake doing sums again, but this time the figures were different. This time he counted the hours between Saturday and Tuesday, and the miles between the garrison and the ford, and the speed at which a troop of dragoons could ride, and the number of chalk marks required on a pump handle to make a message true. And beneath the sums ran another count, small and stubborn, that would not be silenced: one child, eyes like strong tea, grip like iron, asleep somewhere above his head.

If he marked the pump Sunday morning, the riders would be at the marsh by Monday night. They would take the road positions first—he knew how Hale thought now, oh yes, he had learned the shape of that mind in one cold office—and the carts would roll into a country of closed doors.

If he marked the pump, Kestrel did not reach the ford.

And if he did not mark it—

His mother's face. Ned's cough. Corrigan's gentlemen standing under the lamp, patient as furniture.

Near dawn he got up, dressed in the dark, and walked out into the yard with a stub of chalk in his fist, and stood before the pump for a long time while the frost ate through his boots. Then he went back inside without marking it, and lay down, and did not sleep, and understood with perfect clarity that he had not decided anything at all. He had only postponed. Postponement, he was learning, was the coward's version of courage and cost exactly the same.

He marked the pump at noon on Sunday, when the yard was empty and his hand could pretend it was someone else's.

One stroke of white chalk on the black iron. It took half a second. Nineteen years of being nobody had taught him that harm done quickly feels lighter than harm imagined slowly—and that was the cruelest lesson of his life, learned in half a second, unlearnable forever after.

By evening his soul had caught up with his hand, and the weight arrived all at once.

* * *

He confessed on Monday, in the hour before the fog, because he could not carry it into Tuesday.

Crane was in the dispensary, cataloguing bottles by candlelight, his burned hands moving with careful clumsiness among the glass. Thomas came in, closed the door, leaned against it, and found that his voice had gone.

"Sit down, Thomas," Crane said mildly, not looking up. "Whatever it is, sit first."

"I marked the pump." The words came out in a rush, ugly and graceless, like a bucket of foul water thrown all at once. "Sunday noon. Hale—Corrigan—my mother's shop, my brother, they knew everything, sir, they had the debt written out, eleven pounds, and he gave me the paper, signed, and I took it, I took it and I marked the pump, and Mrs. Rowley said Tuesday, and the dragoons will ride tonight or at first light, they'll know the roads, they'll be ahead of you—" His legs gave and he sat, hard, on the flagstones, and put his face in his hands. "I'm sorry. God help me, I'm sorry. Take the money back, tell Hale it was me, say I lied, I'll confess it, I'll be flogged, only not the child, sir, please, not the child."

Silence. The candle flame bent and straightened.

When Thomas finally raised his head, Gideon Crane was looking at him, and his face was terrible—not with rage, Thomas saw, but with grief, the deep exhausted grief of a man who has long expected exactly this from the world and hates the world for proving him right.

"Which pump," Crane said quietly.

"The—the yard pump. One stroke, sir, just one, I didn't say anything else, I never told them the route, I swear it on my mother—"

"You've told them everything that matters. A date and a departure is a map." Crane closed the catalogue. His bandaged hands rested on it, and Thomas watched him breathe, twice, three times, watched him fold the fury away into whatever iron drawer he kept it in. "How many hours since noon yesterday?"

"Nineteen. Twenty, maybe."

"Then the riders are already saddled." Crane rose. Through the frosted pane, the yard beyond was turning the colour of dirty wool—the fog coming off the marsh early, a day ahead of Mrs. Rowley's prediction, as though even the weather had been bought. "They won't wait for Tuesday now. Why should they? They'll close the roads tonight and search every cart at leisure, and call it routine."

"What do we do?" Thomas whispered.

Crane looked at him for a long moment. Thomas braced himself for contempt and found something worse: fairness.

"You'll drive the decoy cart," Crane said. "As planned. Down the north road, slowly, visibly, and you'll be stopped, and questioned, and you will have nothing on the cart and nothing in your mouth, because if you give them one name more than they have, Thomas, I will not be able to save you from what Hale does to men who are useful twice." He took the candle and moved to the door. "You bought your family cheaply. Now spend yourself dearly. It's the only coin left to you."

He paused, hand on the latch.

"And Thomas. Thank you for telling me. Most men wouldn't have."

Then he was gone into the fog, and Thomas sat alone among the bottles, listening to the workshop wake around him—the rattle of pails, a baby crying briefly and being hushed—and understood that the window was closing even as the shutters opened, that somewhere in the grey a troop horse was stamping, that everything now depended on fog, and nerve, and a sympathetic stranger at a ford.

He got up. He went to harness the decoy cart.

It was the least, and the most, he had to give.
