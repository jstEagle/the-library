## 12. Fire at the Workshop

The night before the fire, the frost came down hard enough to crack the water buckets in the yard, and I remember thinking—as I broke the ice in the horse trough with my elbow so Thomas could fill the kettle—that cold was the worst thing winter could do to us.

I had not yet learned what a lie that was.

The storehouse stood apart from the infirmary proper, a long low building of timber and tar-paper that had been a granary before the regiment claimed it. We kept everything in it that kept men alive: lint and laudanum, splints and salt-pork, the good linen Mrs. Petch hoarded like a miser, crates of bark and oil of turpentine, and in the far corner behind the charcoal barrels, locked in a soldier's campaign chest that had followed me from Lisbon to Toulouse, five years of names in a notebook bound in calfskin gone soft as chamois from handling.

I want to say I carried the ledger on my body at all times. I do not. A man who keeps a secret learns that the safest place for a thing is the place no one would think to look twice, and a nurse's storehouse is full of chests. Hale knew there was a ledger—I had made certain he believed he knew—but he did not know where it slept, and for three days since his offer I had let him wonder, because a man who is wondering is a man who is waiting, and waiting bought us hours.

We spent those hours the way mice spend the nights beneath a floor where owls hunt: quietly, industriously, and always listening. Eliza had drawn the marsh route on the back of a laundry list—her hand small and precise, marking the ford, the peat-cutters' track, the ruined byre where a cart could stand unseen until moonset. Marten the farrier had agreed to lend a cob and a cart if the loan happened after dark and without his name attached to it. And Kestrel—the name still felt new in my mouth, like a coin from a country I'd never visited—slept through all of it in the linen press behind the fever ward, breathing her small breaths, utterly unaware that she was the arithmetic around which every adult in the building was now calculating their lives.

I checked on her last thing, as I had done every night since the press became her cradle. She had worked one hand free of her blanket and lay with her fist curled against her cheek like a boxer awaiting the bell. Her face in the candle-glow was the face the note had ordered me to forget. *Forget this face.* I had looked at it now for weeks. I intended to go on looking at it for years, somewhere Hale's writ did not run, and the ferocity of that intention frightened me more than any fire could have.

That is what I was thinking as I pinched out the candle. Not smoke. Not sparks. Just a child's fist, and a road north, and three days that were already two.

The fire came an hour before dawn, when the watch is drowsiest and the frost lies thickest and no sane man expects flame.

* * *

Thomas woke me, or rather his screaming did—a sound I had heard from him only once before, at Badajoz, when we opened a doorway we should not have opened. I came out of sleep with my hand already reaching for the instruments on the table, which tells you something about how a battlefield nurse sleeps, and ran barefoot into the corridor where the light was all wrong.

It was orange. Winter dawn in Yorkshire is grey as pewter; this light was the colour of the inside of a furnace, and it came through the east windows in long quivering blades, and it was dancing.

"The storehouse," Thomas gasped. He was in his shirt, hair wild, hands shaking so badly he could not manage the door latch. "Sir—Mr. Crane—the storehouse—it's—it's gone up all at once, all at once, sir, that's not right, fires start slow—"

He was right, and we both knew what it meant. A fire that starts all at once has been started by something other than accident. Tar. Spirits. Oil of turpentine, poured generous along the south wall while four men stood by with lit lanterns and orders they were pleased to follow.

By the time I reached the yard, half the garrison's servants were spilling out of the barracks in nightclothes, faces lit from below like actors in some infernal theatre, and the storehouse was fully alight. The heat reached me across thirty feet of frozen mud, a wall of it, pressing against my chest like a great warm palm. Above the roofline the flames stood up straight in the windless air, forty feet of them, roaring so steadily it sounded less like burning than like the building itself had found its voice at last and had a great deal to say.

And standing at the edge of the light, exactly far enough away to be seen by anyone who cared to look, were two dragoons in adjutant's escort uniform, arms folded, watching with the placid attention of men supervising drainage works.

Nobody moved toward them. Nobody moved at all, at first. That is the thing about terror in a community—it does not scatter people like panic does; it freezes them into a tableau, each face turned toward the fire, each mind running the same sum: *if they will burn our stores, what else will they burn?*

Mrs. Petch arrived beside me with a bucket that held, absurdly, dishwater. She hurled it at the flames anyway. It rose off the rim and turned to steam before it landed.

"It's no use," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me, because inside I was already past grief into something colder and more useful. "Let it burn."

Then I remembered the chest, and stopped being a bystander.

* * *

There are moments when a man acts and does not understand his own action until afterward, when he reconstructs it like a surgeon reconstructing a battle wound from the shape of the scar. I know I crossed the yard. I know I went in through the side door rather than the south entrance, because the south wall was a sheet of flame and the side door, though already smoking at its frame, still stood. I know the heat inside hit me like a hand shoving me backward, and I leaned into it the way you lean uphill.

The smoke lay along the ceiling in a black tide, dropping as I watched, thickening. Behind me someone—Eliza, I would learn, though at the time the voice was only a scream among screams—shouted my name, or a word that might have been my name eaten alive by the roar.

The far corner. Charcoal barrels. The campaign chest.

The intervening twenty feet contained, as far as my eyes could tell between blinks, nothing solid—only heat made visible, the air itself shimmering like water over a summer road, and the crates going up one by one around me with soft concussive pops as their contents caught. A crate of lamp oil burst as I passed it. I felt the flash on the left side of my face and did not stop to learn whether it had taken skin. There would be time to hurt later. Later is the nurse's whole religion: defer the pain, do the next thing, the next thing, the next.

The chest was hot to the touch but not yet burning—the tar-paper roof above it had held longer than the rest, some accident of draught or angle, God or chance being careless with their favours. The lock was brass and had heated enough to brand. I did not search for the key. I hit the lock with the iron poker that stood by the charcoal barrels, once, twice, and on the third stroke it sheared, and I hauled the lid up and pulled out the calfskin book with both hands and shoved it inside my shirt against my chest, and then the roof beam directly overhead cracked like a musket shot and came down in fire across the place where I had knelt.

Afterward, everyone in the yard agreed on what they saw: a man staggering out of the burning storehouse wreathed in smoke, coat smouldering, moving with terrible purpose, and going down on his knees in the mud ten yards from the door. What they did not see, because my body covered it, was the book. Even Mrs. Petch, who helped strip off my scorched coat, saw only a nurse's under-shirt blackened with sweat and soot, and a man cradling his own burned hands against his stomach and moaning—not, she thought, from the burns.

She was wrong about that. The burns I could bear. It was the knowledge that I had gone into that fire for paper—five years of names, yes, lives rescued from oblivion, work I would have sworn was holy—and that I would do it again, that frightened me. A man who will walk into fire for a book has decided what he is willing to be. There is no walking back from it. Hale understood that about me before I did. Perhaps it was the only thing he ever understood correctly.

* * *

They laid me on the trestle in the fever ward, which smelled of smoke now even though the fire had never touched this building—the wind had shifted and carried the stink of our destruction across the yard like a message delivered to every window. Eliza cleaned my hands. Neither of us spoke for a long time. Her fingers were steady, better than steady, surgical, unwrapping the salved lint and winding the linen with the economy of someone who had done dressings all her life and was grateful, just then, to have a task that required nothing but her hands.

My palms had taken the worst of it—the left worse than the right, where the beam's heat had come through the chest lid as I shielded the book. The skin had gone that waxy white that means deep burn, blistering at the edges in ropes of yellow. I looked at them lying in her lap like something disembodied, these hands that had written five years of small names, and I thought, with a lucidity that felt almost like laughter: *Hale cannot demand the ledger from me tomorrow. I cannot hold a pen.*

"Say it," Eliza said quietly.

"He's burned our stores. Lint, bandages, medicine, a winter of provisions—in the middle of a frost that kills the old and the wounded first." I heard my own voice as though from a distance, very calm. "Men will die of this. Not soldiers, not even—he'll have killed more men tonight than the French managed all autumn, and no one will call it murder, because he did it with fire and paperwork instead of powder."

"Yes."

"And he'll call it an accident."

"He already has. Thomas heard two of the garrison servants repeating it in the yard—*careless with the lamp oil*, they're saying. By noon it will be the truth, because everyone will have agreed to it." She tied off the bandage, tucked the end neatly, and sat back. In the grey light coming through the ward's small window her face was pale and set, and older than her twenty-three years. "Gideon. He wasn't trying to find your book. He didn't search the ashes. He wanted you to see it burn and understand that everything you've built can be taken from you whenever he chooses. It was never about the stores."

"No. It was a sentence, punctuated in fire." I flexed my bandaged fingers, testing, and pain climbed my forearms like ivy, fast and total. "It says: *the next building will have people in it.*"

She was silent. Through the wall, muffled, we could hear the settlement of the storehouse—timbers going down one after another, each collapse a soft boom, the sound of our future being measured out in ash. Somewhere in that wreck was the good linen. The laudanum. The splints. Two hundred pounds' worth of a workshop's life, and beside it, unburned because it had never been in the chest at all, the thing Hale actually wanted, sleeping against my ribs inside my shirt, warm from my own blood-heat.

"You should give it to him," Eliza said.

I looked at her.

"After what's happened. After tonight." Her jaw was tight but her eyes did not flinch. "You said yourself he'd kill more men this way than the war would. Every day you keep it, he has a reason. Give him the book and take the bargain—three days, the cart, Kestrel out through the marsh—and let it be finished."

For a moment I hated her, which is the tribute love pays to fear. Then the hatred passed through and left the truth standing behind it, which was that she was right, and had been right since the night she found the baby at my chest and fed her instead of reporting her. Eliza Vane had never once mistaken sentiment for strategy. It was I who kept confusing the ledger with the living.

"There's something you don't know," I said slowly. "Something I haven't told anyone. The ledger isn't only names. It's dated entries. Places. Orders witnessed. There are pages in it that name the officer who sold the forage contracts at Toulouse, and pages that record what was said in the courtyard at San Sebastián when the wounded were refused wagons, and—" I drew a breath, and the breath hurt, and not from the burns. "There is a page that mentions a woman of Ashford Row. A woman who died last October. With dates. With the crest on letters I saw carried to the colonel's quarters. If Hale takes that book, he doesn't destroy evidence, Eliza. He acquires it. Leverage over half the staff of this garrison, and over your father most of all. He will never burn it. He'll use it."

The last beam fell outside, and a column of sparks climbed past the window like a flight of burning birds, hung against the dark, and went out.

"So we don't give it to him," Eliza said at last. Her voice had changed—flatter, faster, the voice of a woman arriving at a decision she had been circling for days. "And we don't wait for his three days to run out either, because tonight was his answer to your stalling. He's done negotiating." She stood, smoothing her apron, and for a moment the firelight through the window laid a bright bar across her eyes. "Tomorrow, while they're still counting the cost of their bonfire, we put Kestrel in that cart and we go. And as for the book—"

She looked down at my bandaged hands, and something moved across her face that was not tenderness exactly; it was fiercer than tenderness, and colder, and it made me understand for the first time whose daughter she was, and how much of the colonel had gone into the making of her, and how differently it had set.

"As for the book," she said, "you don't need hands to hide a thing in plain sight. You only need someone the adjutant has already stopped looking at. Get well quickly, Gideon Crane. We have until dawn."

Outside, the storehouse settled into its own ashes, and the frost began, very gently, to fall like snow.
