## 13. What Eliza Chose

The colonel's quarters smelled of wax and cold iron, the way a church smells of incense—everything arranged to make you forget it was only a room.

Eliza stood on the worn square of carpet before her father's desk and watched him read Hale's latest dispatch. Colonel Ashworth read the way he did everything: slowly, with the patience of a man who had never once been interrupted by consequence. His signet ring caught the lamplight. The same ring she had held as a girl when he lifted her onto his horse, the same ring that had sealed orders sending men into French fire at Talavera while he dined two miles behind the lines.

She had loved that ring once. She catalogued the fact with a strange calm, the way one catalogues the contents of a house before burning it.

"You've been at the infirmary every evening," her father said, without looking up. "Hale notes it."

"Hale notes everything. That is his profession."

"Eliza." He set the paper down and folded his hands over it, and there—he was looking at her now with those grey eyes she had inherited, eyes that had always seemed to her the very image of honour because they were all she knew of it. "The matter I sent you here to resolve. Have you found it?"

*The matter.* Not the child. Not the infant left in winter at a nurse's door with her mother three weeks dead in a rented room where no one had thought to light a fire. He could not even say the word. She understood then that this was how he had survived thirty years of soldiering—not courage, but vocabulary. A man who refused certain words could refuse certain deeds along with them.

"I have found her," Eliza said.

Something moved in his face—hope and dread in equal measure, which told her everything before she spoke again. He had hoped the child was already gone. Buried, or given to some parish far south, or simply vanished into England's enormous indifference. He had hoped his daughter would arrive carrying the solution like a wrapped parcel and hand it over without asking what was inside.

"And?" he said.

"And she has blue eyes. She cries at dusk. She has a birthmark on her left wrist shaped rather like a comma, as if God began writing something about her and stopped." Eliza heard her own voice and did not recognise its steadiness. "She will be one year old in March. Her mother named her Mary, in the ledger of the parish where she was born, though no one has said that name aloud since."

The silence that followed had texture. It pressed against the windows. Somewhere in the garrison a drum called the evening watch, and her father let the sound pass entirely through him before he answered.

"Bring her to me," Ashworth said quietly. "Alone. Not to the infirmary—here. There is a family in York, respectable people, childless. They will want for nothing. The girl will have a name, an education, a life. It is the best possible arrangement, Eliza, and it requires nothing from you but obedience for one more week."

"One more week," she repeated.

"You have always been obedient. It is your great virtue." He smiled—the smile he used at mess dinners, warm and terrible. "You are your mother's daughter in that."

Her mother. Dead eleven years, and still he reached for her the way a man reaches for a coin he knows is spent. Eliza looked at her father—at the ring, the hands, the careful grey eyes—and felt the last cable connecting them part, not with a snap but with a long quiet giving-way, like ice leaving the riverbank in spring.

"No," she said.

He blinked. In thirty-eight years of life she had never once said the word to him plainly. She said it again, tasting it. "No, Father. I will not bring her to you. I will not bring her anywhere near you. And if your adjutant's men come to the infirmary for her, they will find me standing in front of her cot, and we will discover together whether Captain Hale is willing to explain to a court why a colonel's daughter died defending an infant whose existence you deny."

"Eliza—" He was on his feet now, and for the first time in her memory her father looked his age. "You do not understand what is at stake."

"I understand precisely." Her voice did not rise. That surprised her; she had expected to shout. Instead it came out low and clear, almost gentle, and somehow that was worse—for him. "A woman is dead. You ruined her when you were a captain and she was nobody, and when she grew inconvenient you let her grow poor instead, and when she died you sent servants instead of flowers, and when she left a living child you sent *me*. Because I am the thing you spend when you cannot be seen spending yourself."

The words came out of her whole, fully formed, like stones pulled from a river she had been walking beside all her life without looking down.

"I renounce it," she said. "The allowance. The house in Bath. Your name, if names can be renounced—I will take my mother's, Vane, which at least belonged to a woman who chose it. Keep your money, Father. Buy yourself a quieter conscience somewhere."

"Think carefully—" 

"I have thought carefully every evening for six weeks, walking back from the infirmary in the dark. That is where I did my thinking, sir. Beside her cot." She took a breath. "You asked me once, when I was small, what I wanted to be when I grew up. I said *useful*. You laughed and said that was not a profession. But it is, Father. It is the only one I have, and I mean to practise it where it counts."

She turned toward the door. Behind her, her father's voice cracked across the room like a whip finding its mark too late:

"If you leave this garrison with that child, you will never enter my house again."

Eliza paused with her hand on the latch. She did not turn around.

"That," she said, "is the first honest offer you have made me."

And she went out into the blue February evening, and the door closed on the colonel of the 44th, alone with his wax and his iron and the ruin of his arithmetic.

* * *

Gideon was mending the fire when she came in.

She watched him from the doorway a moment—this was becoming habit, this watching, and she no longer pretended otherwise. He knelt at the hearth with his sleeves rolled, feeding split wood to the flames with the economy of a man who had learned never to waste anything: fuel, words, movement, years. The light moved over his hands. Burned hands now, bandaged since the fire at the storehouse, yet still deft, still gentle, turning each stick so the flame would catch quickest. Even pain he managed thriftily.

Behind him, in the little alcove behind the drug cabinet where a cot had been improvised from a stretcher and two crates, Kestrel slept with one fist curled against her cheek and her blanket rucked to her chin. Eliza's chest did the thing it always did now when she looked at that sleeping face—a slow, painful loosening, like a knot untying in a place knots had no business being.

"She ate the whole bowl," Gideon said, without turning. He had known her footsteps; of course he had. "Then she argued with Thomas about the spoon. I believe she won."

"She takes after her—"

She stopped. The sentence had nowhere legal to go. *Her mother. Her grandfather. Anyone.* The child's history was a country everyone agreed not to map.

Gideon rose, dusting his palms, and turned, and saw her face, and whatever was written there made him go very still. He was good at reading faces—it was half his profession, triage by expression, sorting the dying from the merely frightened—but she suspected that with her he did not need the skill. He simply looked, and waited. He had a gift for waiting that most men mistook for passivity. She had learned better. His stillness was the stillness of deep water.

"I have resigned my post," Eliza said. "As your assistant nurse. As my father's daughter. As all of it, effectively."

"Resigned." He tested the word.

"Renounced. Whichever is the stronger." She came forward into the firelight and sat on the bench across from him, and found her hands would not be still, so she pressed them flat against her skirts. "Gideon, I need to ask you something, and I need you to let me finish before you answer, because you are a man who answers carefully and I cannot bear careful just now."

He inclined his head. Sat down opposite her. Between them the fire ticked and settled.

"In three days you mean to give Hale your ledger," she said. He did not flinch, which confirmed what she had guessed; his face changed so little that only someone watching for it would catch the shadow crossing it. "You mean to trade five years of your life for her safe passage. I know. Thomas told me—he tells me everything now, out of guilt, poor boy, like a leaking barrel. And I know you intend to stay behind. To face whatever comes. You've arranged it all in that quiet way of yours, the cart, the marsh road, the ford at Millbeck, and nowhere in any of it is there a place for you, because you have decided you are the price."

"Eliza—"

"I said let me finish." Her voice shook once and steadied. "I am not here to argue the plan. Your plan is good. Your plan is the only reason either of us sleeps. But you have made one error in it, and the error is me. You have assumed that because I am her blood—or near enough to her blood to satisfy the law, which cares nothing for truth and much for lineage—you have assumed I should go with her because it is *proper*. A woman for the child. A lady for the story anyone might ask later. You have assumed I am going because it is the sensible arrangement."

"Are you not?"

"No," Eliza said. "I am going because I love her."

The words fell into the room and neither of them moved to retrieve them. The fire popped. In her alcove, Kestrel sighed in her sleep, a small contented sound, utterly ignorant of adjutants and bargains and the cost of things.

"Not the way her mother loved her, which I cannot speak to," Eliza went on, softer now. "Not the way duty loves, which checks the ledger first. I mean the plain, unsanctioned, inconvenient kind. I have known her six weeks and she is stitched into me somewhere below argument. When she cries at dusk my arms ache before my ears have finished hearing it. So I am not asking you to send me, Gideon. I am telling you that I will carry her out of this place myself—with you or over you, though I would vastly prefer with—and I will not hand her to anyone else's arms at any ford or ferry, and I will teach her, in Yorkshire or the Hebrides or wherever we land, that a man kept her alive when every powerful person in England wanted her erased. That is my condition. That is my *price*, if you like. I have learned the word from your adjutant."

Gideon was silent a long moment. The firelight worked in his eyes, and she saw him doing what he always did: weighing, counting, the arithmetic of survival that had kept him alive through five years of stolen ink. She watched him try to find the flaw in her resolve and fail, and something in his face eased—not surrender; relief. As though she had taken from him a weight he had not admitted to carrying.

"She will need you," he said at last, roughly. "That is the truth of it. I told myself she needed a woman. It was easier than the other truth."

"Which is?"

"That I need you," Gideon said. "And that needing is not a reason to risk a child's life on a fog-bound marsh, so I put it away. I have become expert at putting things away. It is how the ledger got written."

Eliza's heart performed a manoeuvre she could not have described to a surgeon. "Careful," she whispered. "I warned you about careful."

"I know what you asked me to promise—that I'd let you finish. You've finished." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the bandaged hands hung between them like documents. "So here is my answer, uncared-for. I love you, Eliza Vane. I have loved you since the night you lied to Captain Hale about a cat. It was a bad lie and you told it beautifully, and I thought: there is a person who will lie badly for the right things. I have waited my whole life to meet one."

She laughed, and the laugh broke halfway and became something else, wet and helpless. "This is a terrible proposal. No future in it. No promise of reunion. By summer you may be in a cell, or transported, or—"

"—or nothing. Or I walk north in the autumn and knock on a door." He smiled, and it was the rare smile, the one that cost him, which was why she treasured it. "I promise nothing except this: I have finished writing down the names of the forgotten. I will not add ours to any ledger. Ours will be remembered the ordinary way. By showing up."

"The ordinary way," she repeated, and crossed the space between the benches and took his burned hands in hers, gently, the way she had once dressed a wound on his forearm and known, with a certainty that frightened her, that she would dress his wounds for the rest of her life if he allowed it. "Then that is our vow. Terribly witnessed. One cat."

"One sleeping cat," Gideon corrected, nodding toward the alcove, where Kestrel had opened one blue eye at the sound of their voices, considered the matter, and closed it again, secure—in the way of children who cannot yet know a thing and know it anyway—that the voices arguing softly over her cot were arguing *for* her, and would be there when she woke.

Outside, across the frozen parade ground, a lantern bobbed between the storehouse ruins: Hale's men, walking their new vigil. Three days. Perhaps fewer.

Eliza tightened her fingers around Gideon's and watched the light move along the wall.

"Three days," she said. "Teach me the marsh road by moonlight. We'll need to know it blindfolded."

And far off in the officers' wing, a door opened and closed—Adjutant Hale, working late over his lists, adding and subtracting the value of lives—and neither of them looked toward the sound, because looking was over, and the leaving had begun.
