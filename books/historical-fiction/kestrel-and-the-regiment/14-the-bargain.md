## 14. The Bargain

The fire in the record room had been dead for two days, but the smell of it still hung in the stone—a wet char that got into the throat and stayed there. Gideon stood at Adjutant Hale's door at half past seven in the evening, his hands wrapped in clean linen bandages to the wrist, and waited to be acknowledged.

He had chosen the hour deliberately. Too late for the officers' mess, too early for the night rounds. The corridor outside Hale's office was empty except for a sentry who had learned, over the past weeks, not to see Gideon Crane coming or going. There were men in every garrison who made themselves useful precisely by being unremarkable, and Gideon had spent five years perfecting the craft. Tonight he meant to be remarkable once, and only once.

The door opened without a summons.

"Crane." Hale's voice was mild, which was how Gideon knew the man had been expecting him. Hale was never surprised by anything he had arranged himself. "You look terrible."

"I've burned my hands, sir."

"So I heard. The storehouse fire was careless work. My compliments to whoever set it—they'll need steadier hands next time." He stepped back from the doorway, an invitation that was also a sentence. "Come in. Mind the rug. It came from Brussels and I'd rather it didn't smell of liniment."

* * *

The office was warm in the way of rooms where a man spends money on his own comfort without shame. A brazier glowed behind Hale's chair. On the desk lay papers squared with geometric precision: requisitions, returns, a half-written letter. And beneath the letter, just visible at the corner, a page in a cramped hand that Gideon recognized as his own.

A page torn from his ledger.

His stomach turned over slowly, like a cart finding its axle. So Thomas had given him more than the escape route—Hale had already been inside the workshop, already handled the book itself, or part of it. The thought that any of those names had sat on this desk, under this man's thumb, was worse than the fire had been.

"You've been reading," Gideon said.

"I've been auditing." Hale settled into his chair and folded his hands. He was not a large man; he did not need to be. "Five years of unauthorized records, Crane. Names of men whose discharges were processed under other designations. Deaths entered as desertions. Desertions entered as transfers. Do you know what this is, legally speaking?"

"Property of the regiment, I expect you'll say."

"Theft of regimental property. Falsification of official documents. Contempt of the colonel's command. Sedition, if I'm feeling imaginative, and I often am." Hale tapped the torn page. "And every one of these entries is a small accusation against officers whose careers depend on the accuracy of the ledgers they ordered kept inaccurate. You've spent five years building a case against half the staff of this garrison, and you did it in a hand so small it could hide under a thumbnail. I almost admire it."

Gideon said nothing. Silence was the first tool of his trade—he had learned it in operating tents, standing over boys who begged him to tell them whether they would live, when the honest answer was written all over his face. Silence bought time. Time bought choices.

Hale let the quiet stretch, then smiled thinly. He had expected pleading and was pleased not to get it; Gideon could see the pleasure register and file itself away.

"But here's what interests me," Hale went on. "It isn't the book. The book is paper. Paper burns—it demonstrated that aptly two nights ago. What interests me is the girl."

The brazier ticked. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed.

"There is no girl, sir."

"There is a child," Hale corrected softly, "of an age and a coloring that certain people find inconvenient, currently hidden in your infirmary behind a fiction about stray cats. Don't. Please don't. It insults us both." He rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the parade ground going blue with dusk. "Colonel Ashworth wants her brought to him quietly. I want her brought to me at all. And you want—I'm guessing at the last one, but I've watched you for weeks—you want her gone. Out of this garrison, out of this county, out of every ledger including mine. Am I close?"

Gideon's bandaged hands ached. The burn salve Eliza had applied that morning had worn off, and underneath the linen his skin pulled tight with every pulse. He thought about the feel of the child asleep against his shoulder, her breath quick and warm as a bird's, and he made his face into nothing at all.

"What are you offering?" he said.

Hale turned from the window. For the first time, something genuine moved behind his eyes—the flicker of a man who has done arithmetic all evening and finally found the column that balances.

"A bargain," Hale said. "I'm delighted you asked."

* * *

They negotiated for an hour, and Gideon understood before the first ten minutes passed that the negotiation was theater. Hale had decided what he would grant before Gideon walked through the door; everything after was calibration, the adjutant measuring exactly how much resistance Gideon could afford and granting concessions just generous enough to keep him at the table. But theater required an audience that believed, and so Gideon performed belief, and Hale performed magnanimity, and between them they built a contract neither intended to honor in quite the same way.

The terms, as Hale dictated them to himself in a fair hand while Gideon watched:

The ledger—all of it, every page, five years of names—to be delivered into Hale's possession complete and undamaged. In exchange, one cart, one driver, and one passenger of Gideon's naming would pass the north gate unmolested, with papers of travel made out under a name of Gideon's choosing. No pursuit for a period of forty-eight hours.

"One passenger," Gideon said.

"One. The child cannot drive."

"The child travels with the woman. One driver, one nurse, one infant. That's a cartload by any honest count."

"That's three bodies by mine." Hale's pen hovered. "Very well. Three. But hear the rest, Crane, because the rest is where men like you always stumble." He wrote as he spoke. "You remain. You surrender yourself tomorrow morning, before the cart leaves, publicly enough that your surrender explains any search that follows. You will stand court-martial on the property charges. You will say nothing in your defense about the child, the woman, or the terms of this agreement, because there were no terms, because a man who steals from his regiment does not bargain. And if the child is ever found—if she surfaces in any parish registry, any school roll, any wedding announcement under any name whatsoever—then every page of that ledger goes into a stove, and you go before a firing squad on charges I will invent for the occasion. Do you follow?"

"I follow."

"You're agreeing to destroy yourself."

"I'm agreeing to stop being useful to you," Gideon said. "There's a difference, but I take your meaning."

Hale studied him for a long moment, and Gideon met the study without flinching, though his heart was going hard against his ribs. This was the moment the whole thing balanced on—not the terms, which were worthless, but Hale's read of him. Hale needed to believe that Gideon valued the ledger above his own skin, that pride in the work would make him compliant, that a man who had spent five years writing names would trade anything rather than see the names die. It was even true. Gideon did value the ledger above his own skin. He simply valued one unrecorded life above the ledger, and Hale, who counted everything, had no column for that.

"You understand," Hale said at last, almost gently, "that I intend to hang you."

"I assumed."

"And you came anyway."

"The cart leaves either way," Gideon said. "The only question is whether it leaves through a gate that's watching for it."

Something shifted in Hale's expression—not respect, because Gideon doubted the man was built for it, but the narrow professional satisfaction of a clerk who finds the accounts add up. Hale dipped his pen, dated the paper, and slid it across the desk.

"For your records," he said, with the ghost of a smile. "Though I imagine you keep better ones."

Gideon did not touch it. "I'll want it witnessed. Your sergeant-major, perhaps. Or do bargains in this garrison travel on trust?"

"Bargains here travel on leverage." Hale withdrew the paper again, unhurried. "You have none, which is why we're able to do business so quickly. Forty-eight hours begins when I say it begins. Deliver the ledger to my office by midnight tomorrow. After that, the price changes."

"It changes to what?"

"To whatever I decide it is." Hale stood, ending the audience the way he ended everything: by making further discussion pointless. "Good evening, Crane. Give my regards to the cat."

* * *

The corridor outside was cold enough to hurt after the brazier-warmth of the office, and Gideon stood a moment in it, letting the chill close over him like water. His hands throbbed inside their bandages. Down the corridor, lamplight leaked under the infirmary door, and he knew without looking who kept the vigil beyond it—who was sitting up with the child, humming some tuneless thing she'd deny ever humming, guarding a secret that had started as her father's shame and become, somewhere along the way, hers.

He walked back through the dark with the terms turning over in his mind, and he did not permit himself the comfort of believing them. Hale would honor the gate for exactly as long as it took to confirm the ledger was complete. Then riders would go out—quietly, in twos and threes, the way Hale did everything—and the marsh road and the ford and every lane between here and the coast would sprout watchers. Gideon had seen the man work a problem before. Hale did not chase things. He closed doors ahead of them and waited.

Which meant the forty-eight hours was a lie, and the cart had to move at the first gray edge of morning, before Hale's confirmation, before his riders, before the doors began closing. It meant the ledger had to be copied tonight—or most of it, the pages that mattered, the names that would outlive whatever stove Hale fed the original into. It meant Eliza would have to leave without knowing whether the man who loved her would ever follow, and Gideon would have to tell her so with his hands bandaged and his voice level, and watch her face do whatever her face would do.

And it meant that somewhere in the next six hours, with fingers that would hardly hold a pen, he had to write faster than he had ever written in his life.

He stopped at the infirmary door and rested his forehead briefly against the wood. From inside came the small, astonishing sound of the child laughing—at what, at nothing, at the candle flame—and then Eliza's voice, low, telling her something he couldn't make out. A story, maybe. A name.

Kestrel. The bird that hovers against the wind.

Gideon closed his eyes. Five years of names in a notebook, and it had come down to this arithmetic, simple and obscene: all of them, for one of them. He would have made the trade a hundred times over. He would simply never forgive the world for requiring it.

He pushed open the door and went in to tell her, and behind him, far across the parade ground, a light kindled in the window of the adjutant's office—still burning, still patient, still counting.
