## 15. Cart Through the Marsh

The fog came off the marsh like something breathing.

Eliza sat on the driver's bench of a cart that smelled of turnips and old canvas, with Kestrel tucked against her chest beneath her cloak, and she could not see ten feet in any direction. The world had been reduced to grey wool: the horse's rump, the wet spine of the seat, the pale oval of Thomas's face where he walked at the horse's head with the lantern shuttered to a sliver. Everything else was mist, and reed, and the small black water running somewhere below them that she could hear but not see.

*They were never going to let us go.* The thought kept returning, patient as tide. Hale's word was worth exactly what it cost him to give, which was nothing, and Gideon knew it—Gideon, who had handed over five years of his own hand's work with the steadiness of a man laying a card he'd already lost with, and then looked at her across the workshop table and said only, *Go before first light. Don't take the road. Take the marsh path, and don't stop at the sound of hooves until you're past the ford.*

She had wanted to say a hundred things. She had said almost none of them. There is a kind of farewell that cannot survive being spoken aloud, so they had left it unspoken, and now it rode with her on the bench like a third passenger, heavy as iron.

Kestrel stirred and made a small sound—not crying, just a question—and Eliza pressed her lips to the downy crown beneath the bonnet. *Hush, my heart. Hush.* The child had been fed, changed, wrapped in two blankets and the swaddling cloth with its Ashworth crest, which Eliza had turned inward so the embroidery faced the baby's body rather than the world. If they were stopped, let no one see the crest. Let no one see anything at all.

The horse's shoes made soft sucking sounds in the mud. Somewhere ahead, a curlew cried, long and descending, and was not answered.

"Ford's a mile yet," Thomas murmured. He was nineteen and had betrayed them all nine days ago and confessed it weeping with his forehead against the doorframe, and Gideon had not struck him, had only said, *Then you'll carry us out instead,* and Thomas had been carrying ever since—with intelligence, with maps drawn from memory, now with this shuttered lantern and his whole trembling future walking at the end of a plough-horse's bridle. "The riders went by the north road, miss. Sergeant Willet told me himself. But Hale keeps men at the ford regardless. Always has. Toll-keepers, he calls them."

"How many?"

"Two. Maybe three when the pay waggon comes through." He paused. "One of them's Dragoon Bellhouse."

Eliza knew the name dimly—a big quiet man who came to the infirmary in autumn with a cough and had sat very still while Gideon lanced something on his neck, and had said afterward, in his slow way, that his mother died of the same fever the soldiers died of, the ones whose names never got read out. She remembered thinking then that grief made strange allies.

"Will he stop us?"

"I don't know, miss." Thomas's voice cracked on it. "Nobody knows what Bellhouse will do. That's rather the thing about him."

The fog thickened. The reeds rose up on either side, taller than a mounted man, whispering against each other with a dry paper sound, and Eliza understood suddenly why Gideon had chosen this route—not for speed, because the cart crawled—but because here the world itself conspired toward concealment. On the road there were milestones and witnesses. Here there was nothing but water and fog, neither of which took orders from Adjutant Hale.

The natural world, she thought, had no interest in any of them. The reeds would stand here after every name in Gideon's ledger was forgotten and every officer of the 44th was under the ground. The curlew would cry. The tide would come in over the flats and erase all tracks equally, the guilty and the innocent alike. There was an odd comfort in it. Men like Hale believed themselves the authors of everything; the marsh did not even bother to disagree. It simply went on being indifferent, and in its indifference was a kind of shelter.

Kestrel's small fist emerged from the blanket and closed around Eliza's finger, and held on with astonishing strength for someone three months old.

"Not long now," Eliza whispered. "We're going north, where the wind comes off the hills instead of the water. You'll like the wind. You were named for a bird that fights it."

* * *

They heard the ford before they saw it—the change in the water's voice, from a mutter among the reed-beds to a broader, flatter sound, the noise of a river spread wide and shallow over gravel. Then the fog thinned for a moment, torn by some current of air, and Eliza saw it: a grey ribbon of water perhaps forty feet across, a line of stepping-stone shallows marked with willow wands, and on the far bank, catching the light of their own fire, two shapes in dragoon red.

Thomas stopped walking. The horse stopped. For a moment nobody breathed.

"One of those is Bellhouse," Thomas said. "The other one I don't know. He's the one worries me. Young. Eager." Eager men, Eliza had learned, were the dangerous ones; they performed vigilance the way other men performed courage, loudly and for an audience.

"What do we do?"

"We do what carts do, miss. We cross. Slow. Boring. A woman taking a sick infant to a cousin in the fen villages, per your story." He swallowed. "If it goes wrong—get down behind the horse and run back into the reeds. They won't follow deep into the beds without a guide. Men drown in there every winter."

"And you?"

"I'm an orderly with a stolen lantern," Thomas said, and managed something that was nearly a smile. "I've been dead since the day I talked to Hale. Everything now is profit."

He clicked his tongue at the horse and they moved forward, out of the reeds, onto the shingle at the water's edge, and Eliza arranged her face into what she hoped was weary, unremarkable patience—the face of nobody, of a nurse, of a woman whose whole life fit inside a cart of turnips.

The young dragoon stepped out of the firelight to meet them, musket sloped but hands near it. "Halt. Where from?"

"The garrison infirmary," Eliza said. Her voice came out level, and she was quietly astonished by it. "I'm taking the colonel's—taking a sick child to her aunt at Marling Fen. Fevered since Tuesday. The surgeon's orders."

"Surgeon's orders." The young man said it as if tasting it. "Name?"

She gave the false name they had rehearsed—Vane, her mother's maiden name, the one identity her father could not easily contradict—and watched suspicion move across the boy's face like weather. He was building his career out of moments like this one; she could see it. Somewhere behind him, Adjutant Hale had described a woman and a child, or perhaps had not, had only said *watch the ford*, and here was raw material for a report, a commendation, a step up the ladder built of other people's bodies.

The horse shifted. Under the canvas, among the turnips, Kestrel slept. Eliza willed sleep into her like warmth, like breath itself. *Stay down. Stay down, my heart.*

"Open the cart."

"She's sleeping, sir, and the cold—"

"Open the cart."

And then the second shape rose from beside the fire, unhurried, and Eliza's heart slammed once and went quiet. Bellhouse was bigger than she remembered, red coat straining at the shoulders, face unreadable in the firelight. He crossed the shingle, looked at the cart, looked at Eliza—looked at her, she thought later, the way he must have looked at the fever-maps of his childhood village, seeing a pattern he already knew—and said to the young dragoon:

"That'll be the infirmary cart right enough. I saw it loaded yesterday evening. Turnips for the fen garrisons." He spat into the water. "You want to search every turnip cart on the marsh road, lad, you'll be at it till spring."

"This isn't the marsh road."

"No." Bellhouse scratched under his chin. "It isn't. Which is why nobody but me and you and the eels are here to care." He came closer, and for one suspended moment Eliza looked directly into his face, and he looked directly back, and between them passed something that had no words in it at all—an entire transaction concluded in silence, priced and paid. His mother had died of the fever. His mother's name would have been in a ledger like Gideon Crane's, if anyone had troubled to keep one.

"Wave them through," Bellhouse said, turning back to the fire. "Child's sick. Standing about in river fog arguing jurisdiction—that's how infants die, and then there's paperwork, and the adjutant hates paperwork."

The young dragoon stood a moment longer, ambition and obedience wrestling openly in his young face. Then Bellhouse added, mildly, without looking round, "Unless you'd like me to tell Captain Ross why you delayed a medical convoy. He's particular about that."

That settled it. The boy stepped aside, sulking, and waved them forward with two fingers.

* * *

The water came up over the wheels halfway to the hubs, shockingly loud after the hush, and Eliza gripped the bench and stared straight ahead and did not look at either dragoon. The horse picked its way through the shallows with maddening calm, hooves rolling the gravel, the current pushing gently at the cart's flank like a large indifferent animal deciding whether to bother. Halfway across, a wheel dropped into a hole and the whole cart lurched, and Kestrel woke—

—and cried. One thin, furious, unmistakably infant cry into the fog.

Everything in Eliza locked. The young dragoon's head came up. Bellhouse, back at the fire, did not move at all.

"Babies," Bellhouse said to the flames, conversationally, to no one. "Marsh is full of 'em. Eels too. All manner of things get born out here."

The young dragoon hesitated—and in that hesitation Eliza found her voice again, pitched high with practiced irritation: "Nearly there, my lamb, nearly there, poor mite's frozen half through—" and it was such a complete, such a boring picture, a nurse and a squalling charge, that the boy lost interest in front of her eyes, visibly filing it away as nothing, as beneath a report.

Then the far bank took them, and the reeds closed behind, and the ford was gone into the fog as though it had never existed.

Eliza did not believe she had stopped shaking until a mile later, when she noticed her hands were still on the bench and her knuckles ached from gripping it. Thomas walked beside the wheel and said nothing for a long time, and then said, "Bellhouse knew."

"Yes."

"He knew and he let us pass. That's a hanging offence, miss. For him, I mean. If it comes back on him."

"Then it doesn't come back on him," Eliza said. "Not ever. We are no one, going nowhere, in a cart of turnips." But she filed Bellhouse away with the growing inventory of people she owed—Gideon, Thomas, a silent dragoon by a fire—and thought that her father's world ran on debts of money and rank, and that hers, the new one, seemed to run on debts of quite another kind, and that she preferred the terms.

Behind them, faint through the fog, came a sound that turned her blood to ice water: hoofbeats. Several horses, moving fast, on the north road—parallel to them, heading for the crossing upstream where the water ran deeper but a rider could swim it.

Thomas froze, listening. "That'll be Hale's relief riding for the ford," he whispered. "Changing the watch early. Or—" he didn't finish. Or checking. Or sent because someone at the garrison had counted carts, or asked questions, or because Hale had never intended the bargain and had decided three days' grace was three days too many.

The hoofbeats grew, passed opposite them through the reeds perhaps two hundred yards distant, close enough that Eliza could hear tack creaking and a rider laugh at something—and then, blessedly, continued on toward the ford they had already left behind. Toward Bellhouse, and the eager boy, and an empty stretch of shingle.

"They'll learn we crossed," she whispered.

"Aye. In an hour, maybe two. When Bellhouse tells them, they'll learn nothing at all." Thomas's grin in the greying light was fierce and terrified and wonderful. "But after that, miss, they'll have dogs, and dogs don't bargain. We need the coach road by dark, and the York mail at Harrowgate Cross by morning, and between here and there is eight miles of marsh with our names written on none of it."

Eliza pulled the cloak tighter around Kestrel, who had gone back to sleep as though the world were a reasonable place, and looked north, where the fog was thinning at last and the low hills were beginning to surface like the backs of swimming animals.

"Then drive," she said.
