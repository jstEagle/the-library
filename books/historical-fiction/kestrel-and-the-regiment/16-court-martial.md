## 16. Court-Martial

They held the trial in the drill hall because the guardroom was too small for what Hale intended to be a spectacle.

I understood the arithmetic of it the moment I was marched in. The long room with its high windows and its scarred floorboards, the trestle table draped in regimental colour, the seven officers arranged behind it like a jury of portraits come down off a wall to pass judgement on one of the servants. Word had gone round. Orderlies stood at the back. Nurses who could be spared from the wards lined the wall beneath the windows, and among them I saw faces I had bandaged, hands I had held steady while they shook with fever. They were not there for Hale's spectacle. They were there for mine, though they did not yet know there would be one.

The charges were read in a voice like a man reciting a shopping list: theft of regimental property—paper, ink, a ledger belonging to the adjutant's office—and falsification of records, being the wilful insertion of names into documents not authorised to receive them.

That last phrase almost made me smile. *Names.* As though names were vermin. As though Private Daniel Hobb of the light company had crawled unbidden onto a page and would not be swept off.

"Prisoner, you will stand."

I stood. My hands were still wrapped in linen where the fire had taken them, two days before, when I had reached into the burning storehouse and pulled my ledger out of the jaws of the flames. The surgeon had dressed them without asking questions; surgeons know which questions cost money. Under the linen my palms throbbed in time with my pulse, and every time the pain came I thought: *good*. Pain was proof the hands still worked. I might need them to work one last time.

Colonel Ashworth presided. He sat slightly apart from the other officers, as though his chair had been moved an inch to the left of theirs, and he did not look at me. That was its own testimony. A man can only avoid another man's eyes if he knows precisely where they are.

Hale conducted the prosecution himself. Of course he did. He wore his best coat and the expression of a man doing an unpleasant duty thoroughly, and he began—as I knew he would—with the ledger.

* * *

They had it on the table between us, wrapped in a cloth like evidence of plague, which in a sense it was. Five years of small names. Hale unwrapped it with two fingers, delicately, letting the officers see the cover, the swollen spine, the water-and-smoke-stained corners.

"This book," Hale said, "was found in the prisoner's possession after the destruction of the infirmary storehouse—a fire, gentlemen, of highly convenient timing. It contains—" he consulted a paper he did not need to consult, "—some three hundred and eleven entries. Names of soldiers. Dates of death. Circumstances. None of it authorised. None of it sanctioned by this regiment or by the Army establishment." He closed the ledger and laid his palm flat upon it, a man claiming territory. "The prisoner will tell you he was keeping a record. I put it to the court that he was keeping a weapon."

He turned to me. "Crane. You admit this is your hand?"

"It is my hand," I said.

"You admit you copied these names from regimental materials without authority?"

"I wrote them from memory," I said, "mostly. Some from papers that were going to be burned anyway. I considered that less theft than rescue."

A sound moved along the back wall—not quite laughter, not quite breath, quickly suppressed. Hale let it die before he answered.

"Rescue," he repeated. "Of what? Paper?"

"Of men," I said. "Private Samuel Ockham, drowned in the canal last March, struck from the rolls as a deserter so his mother wouldn't draw his pension. Private Daniel Hobb, dead of gaol fever in the guardhouse, recorded as transferred. Corporal Reyes, whose name appears nowhere because he died before his enlistment papers were filed and no officer thought that worth correcting." I looked down the row of faces behind the table. "Your court has been asked to believe that writing these men's names down is a crime. I would like the transcript to show that I do not believe it."

"Prisoner will confine himself to answering questions."

"Then ask me whether I wrote the names, sir, and I will answer yes."

Ashworth's head came up at that—just slightly, just enough. He had heard something in my voice, I think, that he had not expected. Perhaps he had expected a man begging.

Hale asked his questions for the better part of an hour. Where had I obtained the information. Who else had access to the book. Whether any officer had assisted me—I noted the careful plural, the net thrown wide, and I understood that Hale hoped less to convict me than to catch someone larger in the same drag-net. Eliza's name he did not say. Not yet. He was saving her, the way a man saves his best card, and every hour he didn't play it told me the child and the cart were still moving north through the marsh fog, and that he wanted me to sweat a while longer first.

To every question bearing on them, I gave him silence or misdirection. Had I acted alone? I acted alone. Did anyone assist me in removing property from the storehouse before the fire? The fire took what it took. Was there a child? I have nursed many children born to this garrison, sir; the parish register will show the baptisms.

He grew tired of me the way a whetstone grows tired of a blade that won't take an edge. And then, because he was thorough, he changed tactics.

"The court has heard the prisoner describe himself as a keeper of records," Hale said, turning to the officers. "Let us examine the quality of his record-keeping." He opened the ledger at a marked place. "Entry the fourteenth. Read it, Crane, if your hands permit."

My hands permitted. They hurt abominably, holding the book open, but pain has never been much of an argument against me. I read the entry: name, company, date, cause of death, and beneath it the small line I added to every record—the line that had begun all this, five years ago, when a dying private named Whitlock had gripped my wrist and said *tell someone I was here.*

"'Remembered by G. Crane,'" I finished. "'Who was present.'"

"You were present at a great many deaths, Mr. Crane."

"That is the nature of nursing, sir."

"And you considered yourself entitled to make official what the regiment had chosen not to record." Hale took the ledger back from me gently, almost reverently, and I saw then—he wanted this book. He had always wanted this book. Not destroyed: *possessed*. A list of grievances is leverage, in the right hands. "You understand that a record is an instrument of authority? That whoever keeps the roll decides who existed?"

"Yes," I said. "I learned that some years ago. I've been trying to give the deciding back to the men themselves ever since."

Silence, then. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. One of the younger officers, a major with a face like a peeled apple, shifted in his chair and looked at the table. Another studied the ceiling beams. Only Hale and Ashworth kept their eyes forward, the adjutant satisfied, the colonel grey.

It was Ashworth who spoke next, and his voice was rough, as if the words had to be planed smooth on their way out.

"The prisoner will state—for the record—whether any member of this garrison, military or civilian, aided him in compiling this document."

And there it was. The question he had to ask, and the answer he had to pray for.

I looked at him straight for the first time since they'd marched me in. He had aged since the autumn; grief does its work even on men who deserve it, though more slowly. His daughter was forty miles north by now, God willing, in a cart with a false bottom and a baby named for a bird, and everything I loved in this world was moving away from me at the speed of a tired horse.

"No member of this garrison aided me," I said clearly, into the hush. "Every name in this book I carry alone."

* * *

They adjourned at noon and reconvened at two, and in the interval I sat in the guardroom with a sentry who pretended to study the wall and slipped me, once, half a hunk of bread and cheese, saying nothing. Kindness has gotten very quiet in this garrison. That is how I knew it was still alive.

When court resumed, Hale played the card I had been waiting for.

"We come now to the matter of the infant," he said. "A child, gentlemen, discovered concealed within the infirmary premises during the period of the prisoner's charge over it. Unregistered. Unbaptised—at least in any parish we can locate. The prisoner will inform the court of the child's parentage."

"I don't know it," I said.

"You were seen. You were *heard*, Crane. An infant crying in a building where no infant should be, night after night, while you told respectable women it was a cat."

"A cat," I agreed, "with uncommon lungs."

Someone at the back of the hall failed entirely to suppress a laugh. Hale's jaw tightened, and I understood I had given the orderlies and nurses at the wall a gift: permission to find this ridiculous. Ridicule is a poor man's armour, but it is armour.

"The child's parentage," Hale pressed, "or the court will draw its own conclusions."

"Draw them, sir," I said. "You'll have to write them down to keep them. And we've established, haven't we, how much this regiment values what gets written down."

That landed harder than I intended—harder on Ashworth than on Hale. The colonel's hand, resting on the table, curled slowly into a fist and stayed there, and I watched a man conduct a war inside himself in the space of four seconds. He wanted his granddaughter safe. He wanted his shame buried. He wanted Hale stopped. Three wants, and only room in his chest for one, and I judged—which is to say, I gambled—that shame was losing.

"Enough," Ashworth said. "The child is not before this court. Theft and falsification are. Proceed to those, Mr. Hale, or close your case."

For one naked moment the adjutant and the colonel looked at each other across the draped table, and the whole hall saw the crack in the edifice. Then Hale bowed, very slightly, and closed his case.

* * *

They gave me the afternoon to prepare my defence, which was their way of offering me the chance to grovel by morning. I spent it sitting on my cot with my bandaged hands in my lap, rehearsing.

Not a defence. A reading.

Because here is what I had understood in the night watches, lying awake in the guardroom with the fire's smell still in my nose: they were going to win. Hale had the court, the charges, the machinery. I would be convicted; the only negotiation left was over the sentence. But a court-martial, I had learned from watching five years of them, produces one thing that outlives every verdict.

A transcript.

A clerk sat at the little desk beside the table, quill going from the first word to the last, taking down everything spoken aloud in that hall, because the proceedings must be preserved for the Judge Advocate's office in London. Every word. Everything entered into that record became part of the regiment's permanent archive—the same archive, mind, from which loose pages had lately been burning.

They had taught me the value of records by spending five years showing me what they erased. Now I would teach them what happens when the eraser itself becomes a page.

* * *

On the second day, when Hale rested and the president invited my defence, I stood, and I did not call witnesses and I did not plead mitigation. I took the wrapped ledger from the table—no one stopped me; in the strange etiquette of a trial, the evidence belongs briefly to everyone—and I unwrapped it, and I turned to the first page.

"What I have to say to this court," I said, "is already written down. It seems only fair, since I am charged with falsifying records, that I put before the court a true one. I will read selected entries. The clerk will take them down. After today, they will exist in your archive under your seal, and no fire in this garrison will ever again be able to claim them."

"Prisoner," the president began, "this is highly irregular—"

"With respect, sir, so am I."

Hale was on his feet. "The prosecution objects. This is theatre."

"It is testimony," I said. "Entry the first. Private Thomas Whitlock, 44th Foot, died the twelfth of January, 1809, of wounds received at the river crossing, having served six years and been twice commended by an officer who did not attend his burial. Remembered by G. Crane, who was present."

"Objection—"

"Overruled," said Colonel Ashworth.

I looked up. So did half the hall. The colonel's face was stone, but his voice had not wavered, and behind him the apple-faced major opened his mouth, met Ashworth's eye, and thought better of it.

I read on.

"Entry the ninth. Private Samuel Ockham, drowned, wrongly returned as deserter; recommend restoration of name to the rolls and payment to his mother, Anne Ockham of Derby, whose address is in the entry." Quill scratching. "Entry the twenty-second. Corporal Miguel Reyes, died before enlistment filed; served eight months; buried under a stone marked only with a number. Entry the fifty-first. Private James Dole—"

"This court will not sit here and be made a repository—" Hale started.

"It will, Mr. Hale," Ashworth said quietly, "because I instruct it to. Let the man read. If the names are false, the transcript will expose him. If they are true—" he paused, and something in him settled, some long-carried weight shifting to a different shelf, "—then the court ought to hear them. All of it, Mr. Crane. Proceed."

I proceeded. For two hours I read them: the drowned, the fevered, the flogged men who died of the flogging and were recorded as having died of flux, the boy of fifteen whose age nobody had ever officially known. I read causes and dates and the small human details I had hoarded against exactly this hour—that Ockham sang, that Dole had a crooked smile from a healed jaw, that Reyes wrote letters home in Spanish that no one could post. The hall went very still. Nurses wept openly at the wall. The clerk's quill flew and flew, and once he stopped to flex his cramped fingers and a sergeant-major near him said, quietly, "Go on, son, get it all."

At the end I closed the book.

"That is my defence," I said. "Three hundred and eleven men whom this regiment decided had never existed. I existed alongside them, and I wrote what I saw. If writing truth is falsification, then I am guilty, and I will be guilty of it again tomorrow. But whatever sentence this court passes, it cannot pass on them anymore. As of this morning, gentlemen, they are in the record. In yours. Signed, sealed, and witnessed by every soul in this hall."

I set the ledger down on the table between Hale and the colonel, and I stood before my judges with my burned hands at my sides, and for the first time in five years I felt completely, perfectly silent inside—not the silence of hiding, but the silence after a bell has done ringing.

Ashworth stared at the book. Then at me. And I watched him arrive, alone in front of the whole garrison, at the place I had been waiting for him: the understanding that the ledger on the table was no longer the only copy, and that the copy which mattered now wore his own regimental seal.

"The court will deliberate," he said hoarsely, and rose.

Behind him, unnoticed by nearly everyone, Adjutant Hale had gone very pale—pale, and calculating, and afraid—for he alone in that hall seemed to fully grasp what had just walked out of the room: a transcript, bound for London, carrying three hundred and eleven ghosts with his own signature scattered through their suppression like fingerprints on a knife.

He caught my eye as the guards took my arms. What he said was nothing. What his face said was: *you have no idea what I will do to undo this.*

As it happened, I had a fairly precise idea. I only wondered—being escorted out into the cold afternoon, past the nurses who touched my sleeves as I passed—whether Colonel Ashworth, summoning his carriage with a hand that visibly trembled, had begun to wonder the same thing about himself.
