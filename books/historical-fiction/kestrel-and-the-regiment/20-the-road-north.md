## 20. The Road North

The village was called Thwaitesby, and it had the good manners not to ask questions.

Eliza learned this within the first week, when she carried Kestrel—two years old then, round-faced and solemn as an owl—into the parish church on a Sunday morning and every head turned, considered her, and turned away again with the particular courtesy of a community that had decided she was nobody worth the trouble of gossip. A widow, they concluded. Mrs. Vane—no, she had given that up; *Mrs. Hart*, she said, when the vicar's wife pressed her gently for a name. A widow of the war, come north from somewhere unpleasant, with no family worth mentioning.

It was remarkable, how easily a woman could be erased if she did it herself. She thought often of Gideon's ledger, of the names he'd rescued from exactly this kind of quiet disappearance, and understood at last why he did it. Erasure was not violence. It was simply what happened when no one wrote you down.

She rented the cottage at the end of Mill Lane because it leaned slightly eastward, like a man listening, and because the landlady, Mrs. Ackroyd, was deaf enough in one ear that half of everything could be safely said twice without consequence. The cottage had two rooms downstairs and one above, a garden gone wild with rosemary, and a view across the beck to the moors, which began abruptly where the fields gave up and climbed away into purple-brown nothing until they met the sky. Eliza had lived her whole life in country made by men—parterres, terraces, avenues of limes planted in obedient ranks—and she found she loved the moors precisely because no one had ever gotten the better of them.

Kestrel called them *the big grass*. It was, Eliza decided, as accurate a name as any cartographer's.

* * *

That first winter was hard in ways Eliza had never been permitted to find hard before. In her father's house, want had been an abstraction, something that happened in ledgers and pamphlets and other people's parishes. Now it was the price of coal, counted out in farthings; now it was darning her own stockings by candlelight until her eyes watered; now it was stretching a shoulder of mutton across five days and pretending, brightly, that Kestrel preferred barley broth to anything else in the world.

But there were compensations so fierce they frightened her. When Kestrel fell asleep against her ribs each night, breathing in the small bellows-rhythm of a child who has never once been afraid, Eliza would lie awake listening and think: *this is mine.* Not the colonel's daughter's. Not anyone's duty or arrangement or shame. Hers. Bought with a cart through the marsh and a father's name abandoned on the road behind her, and worth every farthing of the price.

Money arrived, at first, in ways she chose not to examine too closely—a draft drawn on a York bank, sent quarterly, unsigned, in a clerk's careful hand. She knew whose conscience funded it. She hated taking it and took it anyway, because Kestrel needed shoes, and pride, she had learned, was a luxury item like any other. But she wrote to no one, acknowledged nothing, and spent it with the grim efficiency of a quartermaster provisioning a siege.

By spring she had established herself. Mrs. Hart took in sewing—the officers' wives at Thwaitesby Hall required alterations, and Eliza's needlework was finer than her station suggested, which occasioned some mild speculation that she might be gentry fallen low, a theory she encouraged because it excused her odd manners. She kept bees. She taught herself the names of the moorland birds from a secondhand book, reading aloud to Kestrel by the fire: curlew, lapwing, snipe, merlin. The child absorbed them with the indiscriminate appetite of the very young, pointing at everything that flew and demanding its name, and Eliza gave them gladly, all except one.

Because Kestrel did not yet know her own story. And the day was coming—Eliza felt it approaching the way one feels weather in an old wound—when the questions would arrive.

They came in autumn.

* * *

She was four now, Kestrel. Quick-eyed, dark-haired, with a stubborn set to her small chin that Eliza refused to identify as familiar, knowing perfectly well whose jawline it echoed. She talked constantly, having decided sometime that summer that words were the finest invention ever devised and ought to be employed without pause. She narrated her breakfast. She interrogated the bees through the window. She asked, with the relentless precision of childhood, why things were the way they were, and Eliza discovered that most of the answers adults give children are simply smaller lies wearing honest faces.

"Why do we live here?" Kestrel asked one evening, legs swinging from her stool, watching Eliza knead bread. "And not with Grandmama?"

"We don't have a grandmama, my love."

"Everybody has grandmamas."

"Not everybody."

"Did she die?"

"Yes." This was true enough; the old countess had died the winter before, and Eliza had learned of it from a newspaper in the York coach, feeling nothing she could name and much that she couldn't.

"Did my papa die?"

The flour went suddenly loud under Eliza's hands. She kept kneading. "What put that in your head?"

"Mary Ackroyd says widows have dead papas. Or dead husbands. I couldn't remember which kind we are."

Eliza laughed—helplessly, really—and wiped her hands and crouched down to the child's level, and saw the question still waiting underneath the joke, patient as stone. Children always had one question underneath all their others. She had learned to hear it.

"You remember," Eliza said carefully, "that I told you some stories are like seeds. They need to stay in the ground a while before they can grow into something you can look at."

"I'm not little," Kestrel said, with dignity. "I'm nearly grown."

"You are entirely grown. Nearly six feet of grown, I should think, by the way you eat." She caught the girl's hands. "When you're bigger, I'll tell you everything. Every single thing. That is a promise, and I don't make promises I mean to break."

"Is Papa in the story?"

Eliza held very still. Outside, a wind was coming up off the moors, moving the heather in long silver strokes.

"The story has a great many people in it," she said. "Some of them were good, and some of them were frightened, and some of them were both at once, which is more common than you'd think. And one of them—" Her voice caught, just slightly, on the present tense she hadn't used aloud in two years. "*Is* very brave, and writes things down so they're remembered, and if you are patient, you will know him."

"Him," Kestrel repeated, triumphant, latching onto the pronoun like a kestrel onto a vole. "You said him."

"I said many things. Go and fetch the butter dish before your interrogation bankrupts me entirely."

The child went, satisfied for the moment, and Eliza stood alone in the warm flour-smelling kitchen and let herself, briefly, feel the whole weight of the distance south. Two years. No word. She had known there would be none—that silence was the shape safety took—but knowing a thing and living inside it were different countries, and she had been living in the second one a long time.

* * *

The village settled around them the way silt settles in a harbor. Kestrel went to dame-school and came home with chalk dust on her nose and a Yorkshire accent thickening month by month, so that sometimes she said words Eliza barely recognized and had to be translated to by Mrs. Ackroyd, who found the entire situation delightful. Eliza sewed, kept her bees, walked the moors in all weathers because the wind was the only thing that ever fully emptied her mind. She learned to shoot a rabbit snare and to swear fluently and quietly in the manner of farmers' wives, and once, when a drover's bullock blundered into her garden and began demolishing the rosemary, she seized it by the ring in its nose and hauled it out bodily, an act witnessed by three villagers, after which no one in Thwaitesby ever doubted her again about anything.

She was, she realized one bright cold morning on the ridge above the beck, happy. Not content—content implied completion, and she was not complete; there was a Gideon-shaped absence in her that she had stopped apologizing for—but happy in the way of a plant grown in a crack, rooted in almost nothing, blooming anyway.

It was the waiting that had changed. In the first year, waiting had been a hunger. In the second, it had become a discipline. Now, entering the third, she found it had transformed again, into something she had no better word for than faith—not certainty, never that, but a settled conviction beneath the doubt, the way bedrock lies beneath soil that freezes and thaws. He had traded five years of his life's work for Kestrel's life. A man who did that was not a man who forgot. Whatever had happened to him after the cart went through the marsh—and she had imagined every possibility a hundred times, prison, rope, a nameless grave, worse—he would come north if breath remained in him, or he would send word, or he would die trying, and she had arranged her heart around all three possibilities like rooms in a house.

Most days she lived in the first room.

* * *

The letter came in March, at the end of a week of sleet, when the beck ran high and brown and the whole village smelled of wet wool and coal smoke.

Mrs. Ackroyd brought it up the lane herself, which was unusual—post generally waited at the shop until collected—and she was holding it oddly, between finger and thumb, the way one holds something that might be alive.

"Came wi' t' carrier from Otley," she said. "No direction but you. No wax. T' lad said there were no franking nor owt. Just this." She peered at Eliza over the envelope with her one good eye, bright with unembarrassed curiosity. "No name on it neither."

"Thank you, Mrs. Ackroyd."

"Aye." She lingered. "Well. If it's bad news, you'll want tea. If it's good news, you'll want tea. I'll put kettle on regardless." And she departed down the lane at her unhurried pace, leaving Eliza standing in the doorway with the letter in her hands.

It was cheap paper, the kind sold in stationers' shops in any market town. No crest. No address beyond *Mrs. Hart, Mill Lane, Thwaitesby*, written in a hand she did not recognize—or rather, in a hand she recognized too well and could not place, being neither the clerk's copperplate of the quarterly drafts nor any hand from her former life. Slightly cramped. Pressed hard into the paper, as though the writer had been unaccustomed to writing lately, or writing much, or writing at all.

Her heart performed a slow, painful maneuver against her ribs.

Inside the envelope there was no letter. There was a single sheet, blank on both sides, folded twice—and pressed flat between the folds, a feather.

Barred and pointed, rufous and dark, longer than her hand. A primary flight feather. She knew it instantly and completely, the way one knows a face across a crowded church: a kestrel's wing feather, taken lawfully or otherwise from a bird that hovered against the wind over some moor or downland while she had been going about her ordinary days.

Eliza stood in the doorway a long time. The sleet ticked against the eaves. Somewhere behind her in the cottage, Kestrel was singing to herself, tunelessly, a song of her own composition involving bees and the unfairness of bedtime.

There was no signature. There was no date. There was nothing at all except the feather, and the handwriting on the envelope, and the fact—which Eliza clutched to her chest like a coal against the cold—that only one person in all of England knew what that bird meant to them. Only one person had stood beside her on a night vigil in a freezing infirmary while a hungry infant screamed, and watched her point to a painting in an old bird book and say *that one, that hovering one, that's what she'll be.*

He was alive. He had written. He had sent nothing else—no address, no plea, no promise—and she understood why, understood it down to the bone, because whatever shadow still followed him, whatever sentence or suspicion or pursuit, he would not risk putting one incriminating word on paper. He had sent the one message that could not be read by any adjutant, opened by any magistrate, entered into any record.

*A feather means: I am coming. A feather means: wait for me. A feather means: I remember everything, including you.*

Or it meant none of those things, whispered the cold voice that lived in the back of her skull, the voice that sounded disconcertingly like her father. It might mean goodbye. It might mean a man settling accounts before dying. It might mean—

"Who's it from?" Kestrel had appeared at her side, silent as a cat for once, staring up at the feather with narrowed eyes. "That's a big one. Is it off a hawk?"

"It's off a kestrel."

The girl's whole face changed. "Off *my* bird? My namesake bird?" She reached out and touched the barbs with one reverent fingertip. "Where did it come from?"

Eliza looked down at her daughter—at the stubborn chin, the quick dark eyes, the small hand resting on the feather as though it already belonged to her, as though it had been waiting for her these four years—and heard herself say, steadily, though her heart was hammering like a drum at a regimental review:

"It came from the south, my love. From someone who knows our story." She closed her hand gently around the feather, feather and small fingers together. "And I think—I truly think—it means he's coming to tell it to us himself."

Far to the south, past the high brown line of the moors, a road ran down out of the hills toward everything she had left behind. Eliza stood in her doorway and looked at it for a long moment, the sleet melting on her shoulders, the feather burning in her closed fist like a brand.

Then she turned back into the warmth, to the kettle Mrs. Ackroyd was no doubt already filling, and began, without quite admitting it to herself, to count the days.
