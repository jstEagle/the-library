## 21. Names Read Aloud

London smelled of coal smoke and wet stone, and Gideon Crane had been in it four days before he worked up the courage to buy a newspaper.

It was not the price that stopped him—two pence was nothing against what remained in his pocket after a fortnight of coaches and cheap beds. It was that buying it made him part of the event. As long as he had not read it, the thing existed at one remove, a rumor he had heard in a Dover coffee-room from a man who swore his cousin in the city had seen it with his own eyes: *the 44th's forgotten dead, printed whole.* Rumors could be wrong. Rumors could be taken back. Ink on newsprint could not.

He bought it anyway, from a barefoot boy outside Cheapside who did not look at him twice, and he carried it folded under his arm for two streets like contraband before finding a bench in St Paul's churchyard where the pigeons and the clerks would leave a man alone.

The paper was *The Examiner*. The date was the third of November, 1814. And there, below an editorial on parliamentary reform, occupying nearly a full column, was a heading set in type so plain and so permanent that his hands began to shake before he read a word of it:

**THE ROLL OF THE FORGOTTEN — Being a True List of Soldiers of H.M. 44th Regiment of Foot, Died in Service, Omitted from the Regimental Records. Communicated by a Nurse of the Same Regiment.**

*Communicated by a nurse of the same regiment.*

Not his name. He had insisted on that, in the two letters he had posted from Canterbury to an address Eliza had given him months ago—a correspondent of hers, a man named Hobday who wrote about debtors' prisons and the corn laws and had, she said, no fear of colonels. *Print the names, never mine,* Gideon had written. *The names are the point. I am only the hand.*

Hobday had honored it. But the phrase still landed in Gideon's chest like a musket ball: *a nurse of the same regiment.* Five years of writing by tallow-light while men slept and bled around him, five years of hiding a stitched notebook inside a mattress ticking, and here it all was in three inches of column, anonymous as rain.

He began to read.

*Private James Corry, died of fever, December 1809, Badajoz. Entered in no ledger. Wife unknown, said to be living in Colchester.*

Corry. He remembered Corry perfectly—the smell of vinegar sponges, the way the man had wept without sound, ashamed of it, asking whether anyone would write to his mother because he had not dared send his address home since he'd been flogged for a lost boot. Gideon had promised him someone would remember. He had not told the dying man how he intended to keep that promise.

*Sapper William Dree, killed by fall of earth, siege works, June 1810. No record. Pay owed to no claimant.*

Dree, whose name the paymaster had struck through with a flourish because a dead sapper owed the regiment more than the regiment owed him.

Page by page, column by column, the list unspooled—ninety-one names, though the count had grown past a hundred and forty in his ledger by the end, and Hobday had printed only those Gideon could vouch for absolutely, with dates and witnesses where witnesses had existed. Some entries were only a name and a wound. Some were small histories: a man who could carve anything out of horn, a fifer who had smuggled a canary through three campaigns, a private called Samuels who had gone into the breach at Ciudad Rodrigo carrying another man's pack because the other man's feet were frozen.

Gideon read until the light went thin and grey, and then he read again, and the second reading was worse than the first, because by then he understood what he was looking at.

It was not his work anymore. That was the strange mercy of it. For five years the ledger had lived in his head and under his mattress, a secret that weighed like a stone and could kill him like one. Now it hung in the air of every coffee-house in London. A clerk in Cornhill was reading it over his pie. A printer's apprentice was setting a pirated copy for a rival sheet. The names had escaped him, the way a bird escapes a hand, and they were beyond anyone's reach now—beyond Hale's, beyond the colonel's, beyond the court-martial that had tried to make them vanish twice over, once by omission and once by fire.

A woman walking past with a basket gave him an odd look, and he realized his face was wet.

He wiped it with his cuff, folded the newspaper with exaggerated care, as if it were a wounded man's shirt, and sat a while longer watching the pigeons walk their tight circles around the cathedral stones.

* * *

The coffee-house off Paternoster Row was the kind of place where journalists, printers, and half-paid clerks argued politics at a volume that suggested they expected Parliament to hear them. Gideon chose it deliberately. If you wanted to learn what a thing meant in this city, you did not go to Whitehall; you went to where the ink was mixed.

He took a corner table, ordered coffee he did not want, and listened.

"—ninety-one names, and Hale's people are saying forgery, but you don't forge a list of nobodies, that's the beauty of it—" A thin man with ink-stained cuffs was addressing the room at large. "There's no profit in a forged private. Who'd bother?"

"They're saying the nurse stole regimental property," someone offered.

"They're saying a great many things." This came from a heavyset man behind the counter, who thumped down a fresh pot among the cups. "They're also saying the court-martial transcript is being sold in pamphlet form in three shops I could name, and that Colonel Ashworth stood up in front of God and the deputy judge advocate and acknowledged a child. You can call ninety-one families liars if you've a mind, Mr. Snell, but I'd not care to be the man who does it in print."

"Where is the nurse, then?" Snell demanded. "That's what I want to know. Hobday won't say. Says he doesn't know. Man writes letters like scripture and claims he doesn't know his own source."

"Maybe he doesn't."

"Maybe he's protecting him. Or her." Snell leaned back, warming to his theme. "Because whoever it is—if the list is true, and I believe it's true—then the army owes pensions, and widows owe thanks, and the Duke himself owes an explanation. There's a man or a woman in England today who did more for the common soldier than the entire Horse Guards has done in my lifetime, and nobody knows their face."

Gideon lifted his cup and drank his bitter coffee and said nothing, and the silence fit him like an old coat.

He thought of Private Corry's mother in Colchester, if she still lived. He thought of the widow of Sapper Dree, if there was one, who might now stand before a magistrate with a printed column in her fist and say *this was my husband, and he was owed eleven months' pay.* The names would do that. The names had always been the point. He had understood it dimly, the first cold night he had written a dying man's name against orders, and he understood it plainly now: a record is not a memorial. A record is a lever. You put it under the world and something moves.

He left coins on the table and slipped out while Snell was still holding forth, and no one marked the quiet man in the darned coat who left before his coffee was cool.

* * *

He had planned to leave London the next morning. Instead he stayed three days more, because there was one more thing to do, and it required daylight and nerve.

The office of *The Examiner* was up two flights of a narrow staircase that smelled of lamp oil and hot lead. Gideon climbed it on the fourth morning with his hooded eyes open and his heart going like a drummer-boy's, and asked a boy at a desk littered with proofs whether Mr. Hobday was within.

"He's at the printer's," the boy said. "Back by noon, likely. Will you wait? Have you something for him?"

"I have nothing for him," Gideon said truthfully. "I only wanted—" He stopped. What had he wanted? To see the man who had carried his secret ink into public daylight? To say thank you, which would mean explaining why, which would mean unmasking himself in a room full of journalists? "Tell him the nurse of the 44th has read the list. Tell him the dates are right. That's all."

The boy looked at him oddly—not with recognition, for how could he, but with the particular attention of someone sensing weight in an ordinary sentence. "You'll want to tell him yourself," a voice said from the stairs.

Gideon turned.

Hobday was coming up the last steps, a broad man in his fifties with flour-white temples and the unhurried eyes of someone who had spent thirty years deciding what the public could bear to know. He looked at Gideon's burned hands first—he noticed everything, Gideon realized; that was the trade—and then at his face, and something in his expression settled, like a scale coming to rest.

"The hands," Hobday said quietly. "From the storehouse fire. It was in the transcript. You pulled your papers out of a burning building."

Gideon said nothing.

"You needn't say anything," Hobday said. "I've been reading faces for a living longer than you've been keeping ledgers. Come inside before the whole street hears."

The office he shared with another writer was small and chaotic, smelling of tobacco, and he shut the door on the noise of the stairs and stood regarding Gideon with his arms folded, not in suspicion but in something like wonder.

"Ninety-one names," Hobday said. "Do you know what's happened since we printed? The War Office has opened a review of the 44th's records. Two MPs have asked questions in the House. There's a fund being subscribed for the Colchester widow—your Corry's woman—by readers who never knew him. And Adjutant Hale—" he smiled without warmth, "Adjutant Hale has discovered that his patrons prefer their scandals in the past tense. He's been removed from the garrison staff pending inquiry. They're calling it the Kestrel affair, in some quarters. Do you know why?"

"Eliza named her for a bird," Gideon said. His voice came out rough. "The kestrel hovers against the wind. She said it was the only creature that stays put in a gale by refusing to fly with it."

"A good omen for your cause." Hobday unfolded his arms and gestured to a chair. "Sit down, man, you look like you'll fall down. Now. I'm going to ask you once, and then I'll never ask again, and I'll go to my grave denying I ever guessed. But I think you should hear it said aloud, at least once, by someone who isn't a magistrate or a journalist hunting a name." He leaned forward. "Thank you. On behalf of ninety-one dead men and everyone who loved them. Thank you."

Gideon sat down slowly, because his knees had informed him they were finished with standing. He looked at his own hands, the burn-scarred right and the steady left, and found that for the first time in five years there was nothing in them to hide.

"She chose well," he said at last. "When she sent you the pages. I hope you'll tell her so."

"I imagine she knows," said Hobday. "Wives generally do."

* * *

He walked north out of London the following dawn, on foot, because he had decided somewhere in the night that he wanted the last miles of this affair to pass slowly enough to feel.

The road was already busy—carts of hay, drovers with geese, a mail coach thundering past with its lamps still lit—and Gideon moved among them unnoticed, a lean man of forty-odd with a knapsack, a stick, and ruined hands, indistinguishable from ten thousand other men walking away from something. That was the whole art of his life, and he practiced it now without bitterness. Vindication, he had learned this week, was a meal best eaten standing up, in small portions, in company that didn't know you. Anything more tasted of vanity, and vanity got people killed. It had nearly gotten a child killed. It had gotten Thomas the orderly a guilt he'd carry longer than any sentence, and it had gotten Colonel Ashworth a career's worth of ruin delivered in a single afternoon of honesty.

Purpose completed. That was what he felt, mostly—not triumph, not even relief exactly, but completion, the way a long march feels when the ground finally stops rising. Ninety-one names in print, safe forever from every match in England. A girl alive in the world under a new name, hovering against whatever winds came. Nothing left in his pockets worth stealing and nothing left in his chest worth burning.

Except.

He stopped at the top of Highgate Hill and looked back once at London, a brown smudge under its own smoke, and allowed himself the smallest accounting. Debts owed: one to a woman who had carried his pages to a press with her father's name in her mouth and her inheritance in her fists. One to a child who would grow up asking questions. He had answered every name in the ledger except those two, and those two could not be paid by post.

The letter from Eliza had reached him in Canterbury nine days ago—no signature, only a line in her level, unhurried hand: *The village is called Marske-in-Cleveland. North Yorkshire. The child asks questions now, and I have run out of answers that are not lies. There is a feather pressed inside this letter. You will understand what it means, or you will not come, and either way we are well.*

He had understood. The kestrel feather, flat and barred and perfect between the pages, was not a message. It was a door, left unlatched.

Gideon turned his back on the city and set his face to the north country, and the road took him the way roads do—without ceremony, without trumpets, at the pace of a man who has finally stopped running in either direction. By evening the smudge of London was gone entirely and the land opened into hedged fields and woodsmoke, and he thought, with something dangerously close to happiness, that he would need to buy a new notebook in York. Blank, this time. Or nearly blank.

Two hundred and forty miles lay between him and a cottage in Cleveland, and he meant to walk every one of them, arriving at dusk, knocking—or perhaps, if the door opened before he knocked, not needing to knock at all.
