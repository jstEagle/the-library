## 1. The Weight of Other People's Words

The letters arrived on Tuesdays and Fridays, and on those days Rafael Underhill rose before the fire had learned its business and sat down at the small table in the back passage with the post spread out before them like a hand of cards no one had asked them to deal.

There was an order to it. There was always an order. Bills to the left, correspondence for Mrs. Marlowe's committees in the middle, personal letters stacked face-down at the right where they could be dealt with last and held longest. The lamp threw its circle of yellow across the table, and beyond the circle the passage was dark and smelled of beeswax and cold stone, and Rafael read.

They read everything. That was the truth of it, though no one had ever asked them to and no one would ever be told. They read the bills from the ironmonger and the seed merchant, because a person ought to know what a household owed and to whom. They read the circulars from the Lancashire federation and the folded newspapers cut into columns by some distant clerk. And they read the letters — all the letters — with the particular attention of someone who had once gone eleven years without being able to read a word of anything, and who had never since been able to let printed language pass through their hands unexamined, the way a man who has known hunger cannot bear to see bread thrown out.

This morning there were nine pieces. Rafael sorted them with quick fingers, sliding the knife under each flap, smoothing each page flat against the wood.

*Dear Mrs. Marlowe,* began the first, *I heard you speak in Keswick in the spring and I have not slept properly since.* Rafael's mouth curved. They read on: a millworker's wife, three children, a husband who called her a fool for wanting the vote and then wept when she described the meeting anyway. She wanted pamphlets. She wanted twenty pamphlets, and could Mrs. Marlowe say whether it was true that women in New Zealand had voted now for twenty years, because her husband said it wasn't true and she couldn't prove him wrong without proof.

Rafael set that letter aside to answer first. Not to write — that was Edith's hand, Edith's privilege — but to mark it with a slip of paper: *NZ confirmed. Send pamphlets. Invite her to the December meeting if she'll come this far.* The rest of the machinery would follow. It always did.

The second letter was from a vicar, declining to host a meeting on grounds of parish harmony. Rafael translated the sentence as it truly meant itself: he was frightened, and his wife had not yet finished overruling him, and there might be room to return in Lent. They filed it under *revisitable* rather than *refused*. Edith had taught them the categories without ever quite admitting she'd done so, and Rafael kept them in their head the way other servants kept recipes.

The third letter made them stop.

It was addressed in a woman's cramped, urgent hand, and inside was a single page that had clearly been written twice, the second attempt written over the ghost of the first. A husband at the front — well, not at the front yet, but in training at Aldershot, and due to embark. A quarrel before he left. Words she wished unsaid, and no way to unsay them by post because his company had moved and the regimental office would only forward letters, not find men. Could Mrs. Marlowe — who knew people, who had spoken at a rally where a colonel's sister had been present —

Rafael read it twice through, holding the page steady in the lamplight while the wind tested the shutters outside. Then they did what they always did with these letters. They read it once more slowly, committing the shape of it to memory — the name of the regiment, the maiden name of the writer, the particular phrase about the quarrel, which was *I told him he'd be sorry when I stood at his grave, and God forgive me I think of it every night* — so that when Edith dictated a reply, Rafael would know whether the reply honored the woman who had written. Edith's replies always did. But Rafael checked anyway. It was not distrust. It was the habit of a person whose whole life was conducted in other people's blind spots.

They had learned to read at nineteen, in a boathouse belonging to a house not unlike this one, from a man who taught with the patience of someone who found the whole enterprise hilarious — letters as jokes, words as contraband, spelling as a card trick performed for an audience of one. Rafael had been a very fast student, because they had understood immediately what they were being handed. Not literacy exactly. Keys. A ring of keys to every locked door in the world, and the world none the wiser.

That had been five years ago. Five years, and the keys had never left their pocket, and no one in this valley — not Edith, not Cook, not the gardener Hobbes who fancied himself observant — had ever seen Rafael look at a printed page longer than a servant reasonably must. When Edith read aloud at meetings, Rafael listened with their head tilted, attentive as a spaniel. When a newspaper lay open in the kitchen, Rafael turned pages at intervals without appearing to take anything in. The performance was flawless now. It had cost them something to make it flawless. Some nights they missed being able to simply sit with a book where anyone could see, the way other people breathed air.

But invisibility was a trade, and Rafael had paid for it gladly once, and the price only came due in moments like this one: alone at a lamplit table with a stranger's grief unfolded before them, wanting desperately to tell someone, *I have read your letter four times and I am carrying it with you now, you are not alone in the dark with this.*

Instead they folded the page along its original creases, slid it home, and wrote on a slip: *Regimental adjutant owes E.M. a favor from the Harrowby dinner. Remind her.*

Outside, the frost was doing its slow white arithmetic on the garden. October, and the mornings had turned the color of pewter, and the horses' breath hung about their heads like small ghosts reluctant to leave. Rafael banked the fire, trimmed the lamp, carried the sorted post upstairs on a salver, and knocked on the study door with the two-knuckle rap that meant *post* and nothing else, because everything else between them was said aloud or not at all.

*

Edith Marlowe received her correspondence the way a general receives dispatches: standing, sleeves pushed up, already dressed for battle at seven in the morning in a house where no one had asked her to fight.

"Bad news travels first," she said, taking the topmost letter. "Vicar Hensley?"

"Declines. Cites parish harmony."

"Parish cowardice." She tossed it toward the desk; Rafael caught it midair and filed it in the *revisitable* stack without breaking stride, and Edith gave them the look she always gave them at that moment — the flicker of amusement, quickly shuttered, that suggested she suspected her driver and general factotum was cleverer than the position required and had decided, deliberately, never to investigate further. "And the rest?"

"A woman whose husband's gone to Aldershot. There's a reply needed today if it's to catch the afternoon collection."

"Read me her chief points."

So Rafael recited them — the quarrel, the unsendable letter, the plea — watching Edith's face harden from briskness into something older and harder and truer. This was, Rafael thought not for the first time, the strangest part of their strange employment: that they stood here performing illiteracy while handing Edith Marlowe the entire contents of the post in perfect summary, and she never once looked at them and saw it. Or perhaps she saw it and chose, like Rafael, not to investigate. Two people keeping the same secret from opposite sides of it, for opposite reasons.

"She thinks she's cursed the marriage with her own mouth," Edith said. "Tell her — no, I'll tell her myself tonight. Sit down, Underhill, and take this properly." She was already pulling paper toward her. "We will draft it now, before the day eats us both."

Rafael sat, pen ready, and for half an hour the study held only the sound of Edith's voice shaping comfort out of fury — because that was her gift, Rafael had long ago decided; every kind thing she did was forged out of anger at the world that made kindness necessary — and the scratch of Rafael's pen keeping pace. Their handwriting was careful, upright, entirely unremarkable. A secretary's hand. Nobody's hand. They had built it stroke by stroke in that same boathouse until their old writing, the wild slanted scrawl of a self-taught person, had vanished beneath it like a body under fresh snow.

"There," Edith said at last. "Now. The motor at ten, the W.I. at half past, and if Mrs. Fenn has put her hand up again to ask whether votes for women means votes for her sister-in-law's cat, I want you positioned where you can glare at her."

"I don't glare, ma'am. I wait."

"You do worse than glare. You *wait* at people." Edith pulled on her jacket. "It's unsettling. Keep it up."

Rafael went down to bring the car around, and the cold met them in the yard like a wall with opinions. Overhead the sky was the flat grey-white that meant the year was turning its face toward winter, and down the valley, faint as a pencil line, the river showed between the bare trees — low and sluggish now, running toward the bridge at Thackwell that was the valley's one thread to the world. Rafael looked at it longer than they meant to. The county engineers had been sniffing at the bridge piers since summer. Men had come with instruments and long faces. There was talk, in the village shop, of closure. Of repairs that would run clear through to spring.

An island, some of the shop-talkers had said, laughing. We'll be an island all winter.

Rafael climbed into the driver's seat and found their hands were not entirely steady, and could not have said why, and put it down to the cold.

*

The W.I. meeting ran long, as meetings did when half the attendees had come for the cause and half for the spectacle of Edith Marlowe refusing to be interrupted. Rafael waited in the motor by the chapel hall, cap tipped forward, hands loose on the wheel, wearing the particular stillness that had taken years to perfect — the stillness of furniture, of scenery, of a thing that sees everything and is seen by nothing.

From the hall came the murmur and swell of Edith's voice, rising at the turns. Through the propped door Rafael caught fragments: *—half the population—* and *—taxed without voice, governed without consent—*, the familiar liturgy. Around the edges moved the audience: hats nodding, a girl of fourteen near the back taking notes in a pencil stub with the ferocity of a war correspondent. Nell Quist, the ferryman's daughter. Rafael had noticed her before. Children who watched that closely usually had reasons.

A woman beside the door leaned to her neighbor and murmured, not quietly enough, "Her driver, now. That one gives me the shivers. Sits there like a waxwork. Never says a word."

"Writes enough," the neighbor said. "My Bert saw the letters she sends off. Neat as print."

"Someone writes them *for* her, I expect."

"Mm. Well. These educated ones."

Rafael sat perfectly still and let the words pass through them like weather. That was the discipline: not flinching. The first time it happened — a farmer's wife saying to their face, kindly, *you're lucky, girl, to find work you can manage* — Rafael had nearly given themselves away by the effort of not laughing. Now it was nothing. Now it was Tuesday.

Lucky. Yes. They were lucky, and they knew precisely the exchange rate of luck: every letter read in secret bought a little safety, and every secret spent a little solitude. They belonged to this household the way a keystone belongs to an arch — load-bearing, essential, invisible. They belonged to the movement the way a shadow belongs to whatever stands in the light. And they belonged nowhere at all, really, nowhere their name was known, nowhere their actual self — the reading self, the remembering self, the self that had once, in another life by a lake in high summer, laughed out loud in front of another human being as an equal — could go and put down its bags.

The lake. That was a country they didn't visit anymore. It had sunlight in it, and a voice with a foreign accent making fun of English spelling, and a promise sworn on a warm night with the seriousness of drunkards and the permanence, it had turned out, of scripture. Rafael kept that country behind a closed door in themselves and did not often knock.

The hall emptied. Hats streamed past the motor, voices chattering about resolutions and rationals and whether Mrs. Fenn's cat question had been answered to anyone's satisfaction. Then Edith herself, coat buttoned, cheeks bright with the peculiar satisfaction of a fight enjoyed, and Rafael started the engine before she reached it because that too was part of the performance — competence so smooth it looked like telepathy, service so seamless it required no acknowledgment, and therefore no scrutiny.

"Well," Edith said, settling into the seat, "Mrs. Fenn has withdrawn the cat question until spring, which we shall all regret, and Nell Quist asked me whether girls of fourteen might form a junior league, which we shall not." She glanced sideways. "You heard all that, I suppose."

"I hear most things, ma'am."

"Mm." Edith looked out at the road unspooling between the bare hedges, toward the river, toward the bridge standing grey in the distance with its warning notices freshly pasted. "They're going to close it, you know. The engineer told Hobbes. First hard frost, and up goes the barrier, and this valley becomes a snow globe until April." She said it lightly, but Rafael heard the fury under it, the same fury that lived under everything she did: *they will take even our roads away while we have no vote to object with.* "Half my winter tour is on the far side of that river. Half my correspondence comes through Thackwell. If it closes—"

"The post would slow," Rafael agreed. "Twice weekly, perhaps."

"Twice weekly." Edith shook her head. "A valley full of women waking up, and a post bag twice a week. We'll manage. We always manage." She was quiet a moment, then, almost to herself: "Funny thing, letters. Whole lives crossing rivers in paper coats. You never think about it until the river closes."

Rafael kept their eyes on the road and their hands easy on the wheel and said nothing at all, which was the correct answer to every question they could not afford to answer honestly. The bridge grew larger in the windshield, its stone arches squatting over the thinning water, and Rafael looked at it and felt — absurdly, prematurely, as though some part of them had read next week's post — the cold weight of dread arrive early, wrapped in ordinary October light.

That evening there would be letters again. There were always letters. And Rafael would sit down at the lamplit table in the dark passage and open every one of them, because that was the work, and the work was the keyring, and the keyring was the loneliness, all of it the same thing worn under different names.

They drove Mrs. Marlowe home through the failing light, and the frost came down over the valley like a held breath, and five days later, when the barrier finally went up across the Thackwell bridge, the first post to crawl through the slowed delivery included one envelope that stopped Rafael's heart where they stood — not addressed to Mrs. Marlowe at all, but to R. Underhill, Esq., in a handwriting they would have known anywhere in the world, alive or dead, drowned or burned.
