## 2. A Promise Made in Sunlight

Five years before the bridge closed — before the frost came down like a verdict and the valley turned into a sealed envelope — there was a lake.

Rafael Underhill would remember it later as a place made entirely of light: light off the water in long silver shingles, light through the birch leaves cut into coins, light pooling in the bottom of a rowboat so that everything they carried that summer seemed to float. It was the summer of 1908, and Rafael was twenty-two and driving for a family named Halloway who had rented the last house on the eastern shore, a great rambling white thing with a porch like an open mouth and a boathouse painted the green of bottle glass. Rafael's work was easy there. Mr. Halloway drank. Mrs. Halloway read novels with her back to the lake. The children were away at their grandmother's, and so Rafael rose early, polished a car nobody wanted to ride in, and then had whole days like found money, unspent, unplanned, theirs alone.

They spent those days learning the shape of their own freedom, cautiously, the way you test ice at the edge of a pond.

Rafael had been many people by then, though all of them wore the same face. A scullery girl in a hotel town, until the mistress of the house discovered the girl could read and grew frightened of it, as though literacy were a kind of theft. An errant boy in a city where no one looked twice if you bound yourself flat and lowered your voice and answered to a name that fit like borrowed boots. A chauffeur, which was the best of all the costumes, because a chauffeur belonged to a machine, and machines asked nothing of you but competence. Rafael had taught themselves to read at eleven out of a salvaged primer and a newspaper blown against a fence, and had never once told anyone, because reading was the one possession that could not be replaced if it were taken, and so it must be hidden as other people hid jewelry.

The lake knew none of this. The lake only reflected them.

* * *

Anton Varga came into it by water.

Rafael was lying in the bottom of the borrowed rowboat, half asleep, letting the hull drift along the reeds, when a voice called across the water — accented, amused, carrying a melody in it even when the words were ordinary.

"You are going to drown like that. Flat on your back. Very peaceful. They will write it on your stone: *Died As They Intended.*"

Rafael sat up too fast and rocked the boat. Standing on the end of the Quist dock — even then a Quist, even then that family held the water — was a young man in shirtsleeves rolled to the elbow, holding his shoes in one hand and a sheaf of drawings in the other. He had dark hair going grey early at the temples, though he couldn't have been thirty, and a face that seemed permanently on the verge of telling you something excellent.

"I'm not going to drown," Rafael said. "I'm thinking."

"Thinking is done better sitting up." He stepped down onto the swim ladder without asking, without ceremony, as though the boat had been sent for him personally, and folded his long frame into the stern. "Row us out. I am Anton Varga. I am building the new weir at the north inlet, or I am supposed to be. Instead I have walked to the dock to escape my own surveyors, who love me very much and talk constantly."

"Rafael Underhill," Rafael said, and took up the oars, because it was easier than explaining why a chauffeur was lying in a drifting rowboat in the middle of a working morning.

"Underhill." Anton tried the name the way he seemed to try everything, turning it over, weighing it. "Good name. Honest. Like a door that opens."

They rowed out to where the water went green-black and cold, and dropped the oars, and drifted. And Anton talked — about the weir, about hydraulics, about Vienna and Trieste and a bridge he'd helped rebuild in the mountains that sang in certain winds, a low humming note the villagers said was haunted and he said was resonance, and how he'd argued with the priest about it and they'd ended up drinking together and agreeing it didn't matter which of them was right, since both explanations were beautiful. He talked with his hands. He drew in the air. Once he took the pencil from behind his ear and sketched on the back of one of his drawings — quick, sure lines, a profile emerging from nothing — and Rafael realized, with a lurch like the boat shifting, that the profile was their own.

"What are you doing?" they asked, too sharply.

Anton turned the paper around without embarrassment. "You listen with your whole face," he said. "Like the lake is being explained to you and you intend to check my figures. I wanted to see if I could catch it." He studied the sketch, then Rafael, then offered the page across the gunwale. "I could not. But it is a good attempt. Keep it."

Rafael should have refused. Everything in their training — five years of becoming invisible, of never accepting, never owing — said refuse. Instead they took the paper, and heard themselves say, "I didn't know I did that. Listened with my face."

"You do everything with your whole attention. It is rare. Most people are only half anywhere." Anton leaned back against the thwart and put his face up to the sun like a man being paid for it. "So. Underhill. What do you do, besides think horizontally?"

"I drive," Rafael said. "For the Halloways."

"And before?"

"Before," Rafael said, and let it hang there, the way they'd learned to let things hang, a small polite wall.

Most people respected the wall. Anton looked at it, and smiled slowly, and said, "Ah. A locked room. Good. I love locked rooms. I will not pick it today." He tilted his head back. "But someday you will tell me what is behind it, and I promise you I will not be shocked. I have seen a great deal of Europe and most of what people hide is only grief wearing a coat."

* * *

That was how the summer began: on the water, or on the dock after the workmen left, or walking the shore path at dusk while the bats stitched the air overhead. Anton was lodging at the inn in the village, three miles off, and would appear at odd hours with bread and cheese and questions. He treated Rafael — and this was the thing, the thing that undid all their careful architecture — he treated Rafael as an equal without ever appearing to notice he was doing anything unusual. He argued with them. He asked their opinion on the weir's siting and listened to the answer as though Rafael might know something about water, which, having grown up in river country, they did. When Rafael quoted something they'd read, some line of poetry salvaged years ago and kept like contraband, Anton did not ask, astonished, *where did you learn that?* He only said, "Yes — exactly," and argued about whether the poet was right.

Rafael kept waiting for the trick. There was no trick. That was almost unbearable. Kindness freely given was far harder to hide from than suspicion; suspicion confirmed your secret mattered, but kindness suggested you might survive its discovery.

One evening in late July, Mrs. Halloway gave one of her porch parties, lanterns strung in the maples, a gramophone winding down mid-waltz again and again. Rafael stood at the edge of the gravel in their good livery, hands folded, invisible in plain sight the way only servants can be. And Anton arrived with the engineer crowd, saw them across the whole glittering lawn, and crossed it directly, ignoring everyone more important on the way.

"There you are," he said, as though Rafael were the reason for the party. "Come. The lake is doing something at night I want to show you. You can drive me, yes? Tell them it is a mechanical emergency."

"It isn't an emergency."

"All the best ones aren't," Anton said, already walking.

* * *

He took them to the north inlet, where the weir's timbers were half-built, and made them kill the engine and the lamps and simply listen. In the dark the incomplete structure hummed — very low, below music, felt in the ribs more than heard — as the evening current pushed through the gaps in the scaffolding.

"Do you hear? It is already singing and it is not even finished," Anton said softly. "This is why I do this work. Somewhere under every ugly thing — the politics, the borders, the men in offices — there is a note like that. The true structure. Everything else is scaffolding." He laughed at himself, gently. "Listen to me. One glass of wine and I am a philosopher."

"You've had four glasses," Rafael said.

"Four glasses of philosophy, then." He sat down on a stack of planking, and Rafael, after a moment, sat beside him, and the dark made it possible to say things that lamplight would have prevented.

Perhaps it was the wine. Perhaps it was the singing water. Later Rafael would believe it was neither — that Anton had been waiting all summer for exactly this darkness, patient as an engineer, letting the load come onto the structure gradually so nothing cracked.

"My turn to guess at the locked room," Anton said. "No — don't tense. I won't open it. I'll just say what I think is behind the door, and you may throw me in the river." He waited. The weir hummed. "You were not born to hold a steering wheel for the Halloways. You were born for books, and someone taught you that books were not for you, and you learned them anyway in secret, and now you live a double life where your mind belongs to you and your hands belong to your employers. And you are lonelier than any person I have ever met, and you have gotten so good at the disguise that sometimes you fool even yourself."

The silence afterward was very long. A fish rose somewhere and slapped the water flat.

"The priest in the mountain village," Rafael said finally, "the one who thought the bridge was haunted. Did you tell him he was wrong?"

"Never."

"That's how you're right about the door," Rafael said. "You don't have to open it to know what's inside."

And then, because the dark was safe and the water was singing and Anton Varga had guessed nearly everything without cruelty, Rafael told him anyway. Not everything — never everything — but enough. The primer against the fence. The mistress who'd caught them reading and turned them out with a month's wages and a reference that praised their discretion. The names shed along the way like coats. The sentence they had constructed for themselves: *competent, quiet, no history*, a servant-shaped life built deliberately around the fact that a person with no history could not be traced through it.

"I'm not ashamed of reading," Rafael said. "I'm ashamed of nothing. I just know what happens when the wrong person finds out you're not what you seem. People need everyone to stay in the place they were put. Move one thing and they'll spend years moving it back."

"Or burning it," Anton said quietly. "Yes. I know something of this." He drew a breath. "My turn, then. Fair is fair. My name is Anton Varga, and in the Empire where I was born, that name has become the wrong answer to a question that should never be asked. My father edited a newspaper in a language the government does not care for. He is in prison for it. Not for anything he wrote — for the language itself. For the crime of existing on the wrong side of a border drawn in a room in Berlin by men who have never seen our river." His voice stayed level, but his hand had found a loose bolt on the planking and was turning it, turning it, a small repetitive violence. "I send money home when I can. I write letters that say nothing, because letters are read. And I have learned, as you have learned, that a man can be made illegal by paperwork. Not by anything he has done. By ink."

The weir sang beneath them. Rafael reached over — surprising themselves more than Anton — and stilled the hand worrying the bolt.

"We are the same," Anton said, "you and I. We are both hiding the true structure behind scaffolding."

"Yes," Rafael said. "Though yours may yet come down legally. Mine never existed at all."

"No," Anton said. "Yours existed. It is only unwitnessed." He turned to look at them, and in the dark his face was mostly voice. "That is worse, I think. To have lived unrecorded. Listen to me, Underhill. Here is what I have decided tonight, and I am sober enough to mean it and drunk enough to say it."

* * *

What he proposed was simple, the way all enormous things are simple in the saying.

"A vow. Between us. Not to God, not to the state — to each other, which is the only authority I trust anymore." He held up one finger. "If either of us ever truly needs help — not wants it, *needs* it, needs it the way a drowning man needs a rope — and asks the other, the other answers. No questions. No arithmetic. No *is this wise, is this safe, what will it cost.* You call, I come. I call, you come. Whatever the papers say we are by then, whatever the borders have done overnight."

"And if the cost is terrible?" Rafael asked.

"Then we pay it," Anton said, "because we will have agreed to pay it in advance, in sunlight, when we were free to choose. That is the whole point of a vow, Underhill. It is a decision made early, so that later, in the dark, when you are afraid and clever and full of reasons, the decision is already made and you cannot unmake it. Do you see? Promises are debts we incur on purpose, at a fair rate, in daylight."

Rafael sat with it. Everything they had built — the invisibility, the untraceable life, the walls — said refuse. A debt was a hook. A hook meant exposure, meant the wrong person finding the letter, the ledger entry, the truth. Five years of careful nothing, undone by one word: *yes.*

But the water sang. And the alternative, Rafael understood suddenly, was to go on living unwitnessed — competent, quiet, no history — until no one remained alive who had seen the true structure at all.

"If I ask," Rafael said slowly, "it will be because I have no other door. And I will hate asking. I want you to know that in advance. Hate it like poison."

"Good," Anton said. "Hate it. But ask."

They clasped hands over the space between them — Anton's grip warm and absolute, an engineer's grip, built for bearing loads — and spoke the words each in turn, plainly, without ceremony, because ceremony was for people whose promises needed decoration. *If you ever need me and ask, I will answer. No questions.* The weir hummed the low note underneath it, the true structure, the sound of a thing not yet finished that already held.

"There," Anton said, releasing their hand. "Now you are witnessed, Underhill. Remember that, on the bad nights. Someone knows you exist."

They drove back along the dark shore road with the lamps lit and neither of them speaking, and at the fork by the village Anton got out, put on his shoes standing in the dust, and paused with one hand on the door of the car.

"You'll be gone by September," he said. "Halloways always leave. So will I, probably — contracts end. Doesn't matter. Distance is only another border, and we've agreed to ignore those." He smiled, and for a moment the smile was not charming at all, but grave. "Take care of the sketch. Proof of witness."

Then he was walking up the inn path, whistling something in a mode Rafael didn't recognize, and the summer went on toward August, and neither of them knew — how could they know — that promises made in sunlight keep their interest accruing quietly through the dark years, and that debts of this kind always, always come due in winter.

Five summers later, on the first hard frost of the year, on the same day the county nailed its notices to the closing bridge, a letter would arrive at Marlowe House addressed in that same handwriting — thinner now, hurried, the loops pulled tight as wire — and Rafael Underhill would stand in the hall holding it, feeling the old vow wake like a limb gone to sleep, pins and needles, blood returning.

But that was five winters away. That night there was only the lake, and the road, and a man whistling up an unfamiliar scale, and the strange new weight in Rafael's chest of being, for the first time in their life, owed to.
