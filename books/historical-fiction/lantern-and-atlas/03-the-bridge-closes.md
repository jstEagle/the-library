## 3. The Bridge Closes

The frost came in the night like a thief who wanted to be caught, leaving its signature silvered across every pane of Marlowe House and every blade of grass in the paddock. Rafael saw it first from the kitchen window at half past six, kettle in hand, breath fogging the glass, and understood before anyone said a word: the bridge would close today.

You could feel it in the iron of the railings. You could smell it — that clean, mineral cold that meant the river's edges had gone stiff and the county men would be out with their lanterns and their paint pots, stenciling CLOSED across the planks before the church bells rang for morning service.

Rafael set the kettle down and did not hurry. Hurrying, they had learned long ago, told people you had something to hurry about. They banked the range, laid Edith's tray, and let the house wake around them at its own pace: Marta clattering up from the basement room she shared with the laundry baskets, old Hendricks coughing his way out toward the glasshouses with a coat thrown over his nightshirt because he believed frost was a personal enemy he could shame into retreat.

By seven the whole valley knew what Rafael had known by lamplight. The bridge over the Alder was closed for repairs — new pilings, new decking, four months of work if the weather held mild, six or seven if it didn't. The notice went up nailed to the tollhouse door, and Mr. Quist read it aloud twice to the little crowd that gathered, though most of them could read it themselves and only wanted to hear it said by someone whose living depended on the water.

Four months. Perhaps seven. The valley sealed at both ends by hills that gave way to nothing but forestry tracks and one road over the shoulder of Corry Fell that no motorcar had ever survived, and now sealed below by a river without a bridge until spring.

An island, then. A green and grey and soon-to-be-white island, with two hundred souls on it who would spend the winter discovering exactly how much they had ever needed the world beyond the water.

Rafael carried Edith's tray upstairs and told her the news along with the weather.

"Good," Edith Marlowe said, not looking up from her notes. She was fifty-one years old and wrote her speeches standing at the mantel when she was angry and sitting at the desk when she was merely determined; this morning she sat, which meant she was working rather than raging. "That settles it. No more dashing back and forth to Ashcombe for a month of committee meetings that accomplish nothing but bruised feelings. We'll hold the valley instead. There are four hundred women between here and the tollhouse who've never once been asked what they think about anything, and I intend to ask them all before the ice breaks."

"You'll need a hall," Rafael said.

"I'll need a hall, coal, a pianist, and someone to fetch me to eleven villages' worth of parlors before Christmas." She looked up then, and her eyes — quick, grey, missing less than people assumed — moved over Rafael's face as if checking it against some private ledger. "You're quiet this morning."

"The frost woke me early."

"Mm." Edith returned to her pages. "Well. The republic will not be won by women who lie abed listening to the weather. Toast, please, and tell Mrs. Fearn we'll want soup at midday. If the valley's to be an island, it may as well be a well-fed one."

Rafael went downstairs past the portraits of Marlowe men who had never once driven anywhere themselves, and thought: *an island*. And thought, in the same unbidden breath that had haunted them since dawn: *the post*.

* * *

Mrs. Fearn took the news the way she took all news, personally.

"Closed," she said, hands stilling over her pastry board for the first time in Rafael's memory. "Closed till spring. And how am I to get sugar, may I ask? How am I to get flour in any quantity, or dried fruit, or — " she lowered her voice as though the very word might summon him, " — tea? The grocer's van comes over that bridge, Rafael. Everything comes over that bridge."

"Everything will keep coming over that bridge until the water freezes," Rafael said mildly. "Then it will come over the ice, the way it always does. Quist ran sleds last time they did repairs, in '08."

"In '08 I was a younger woman with a younger woman's patience." But she was rolling again, the crisis absorbed into the dough, and Rafael escaped while the escape was good.

Marta was less philosophical than either of them, being seventeen and in love with a boy from the far side of the river, a cooper's apprentice named Josef whom Rafael had met precisely once and who now, as of this morning, might as well have been on the moon. Marta stood at the scullery window with her chin in her hands, looking at the distant line of the bridge works where tiny figures in caps moved among the timbers.

"They say the ferry runs Sundays only, once the ice goes bad," she said.

"They say right. It'll run when it can and not when it can't."

"He was going to come to the harvest dance." Marta said it the way other people announced deaths.

"There'll be another dance."

"There won't be another Josef." But she smiled despite herself, because Rafael delivered even comfort dry as kindling, and went back to her blacking.

Hendricks, when informed, said only that the roses would want mulching earlier this year and that the county had been letting that bridge rot since Queen Victoria's day and it served them right, and returned to his war against the frost with the satisfaction of a man whose entire world extended, conveniently, to the garden wall.

It was Mrs. Fearn who voiced the thing nobody else would say aloud, that evening, over the staff supper of ham and boiled potatoes. She said it with her fork halfway to her mouth, struck suddenly by the thought:

"And the post. Who's thought about the post?"

Everyone had thought about the post. But nobody said so except Rafael.

"Twice weekly now," they said. "Tuesdays and Fridays. The van comes to the far bank and Quist rows the bags across. Same as '08."

"All Miss Edith's letters." Mrs. Fearn shook her head. "All that society work. However will she manage, boxed in like this?"

"She'll manage," Rafael said. "She'll manage the whole valley into managing, you'll see."

And it was true, and everyone at the table knew it was true, and the conversation moved on to whether the ice would be thick enough for skating by Advent. But underneath the talk, beneath the clink of forks and the wind testing the shutters, Rafael sat with the one thing they had not said: that the post would slow to Tuesdays and Fridays, and that every letter that came into the valley would pass first through the sorting basket in Edith Marlowe's study, and then through Rafael Underhill's hands, and that for four months, perhaps seven, the whole world would arrive in this house at the rate of two days a week.

Two days a week in. Two days a week out. Whatever else the winter meant, it meant that everything Rafael was — everything hidden in the margins, everything folded small and tucked behind a servant's competent blankness — would have nowhere to go and nothing coming to meet it.

They should have felt only relief at that. Instead, washing the supper dishes while Mrs. Fearn planned her stores and Marta hummed something mournful, Rafael felt the peculiar ache they had never learned a name for: the feeling of a door closing that you had never quite dared to open.

* * *

The letter came on the third evening after the closure, which was a Friday, which meant it had crossed the river in Quist's boat in a hard drizzle and arrived at Marlowe House damp at both corners, wrapped in oilcloth with the rest of the bag.

It was Rafael's habit — established over three years and never once remarked upon — to carry the post directly to the study, sort it on the leather-topped desk, and leave the neat stacks where Edith could reach them with her morning coffee: correspondence on the left, bills in the center, the suffrage circulars and federation bulletins on the right, and anything requiring an answer flagged with a slip of paper in Rafael's small, upright hand. Edith called it *your little customs office*, and laughed, and never asked why Rafael read everything, because the answer was obvious: somebody had to know what the letters said in order to know where they belonged, and Edith's secretaries were all in London, and Rafael Underhill was the only pair of literate hands in the house below stairs.

That was the story. That had always been the story. It held because Edith did not look too closely at it, and because Rafael had built it to bear exactly that much weight and no more.

So on Friday evening, lamp lit, rain ticking at the dark window, Rafael slit the envelopes with the bone-handled knife and worked through the bag in order: a bill from the Ashcombe seed merchant, a circular about a bazaar in Keswick, two letters from federation women whose handwriting Rafael knew as well as their causes, a note from the vicar's wife accepting an invitation to speak on Thursday —

— and then a plain envelope, brownish, cheap paper, postmarked from a market town Rafael had never heard of in a county Rafael had never had reason to visit, addressed in blue-black ink in a slanting, impatient, entirely familiar hand:

**R. UNDERHILL**
**MARLOWE HOUSE**
**ALDER VALE**

Not *Miss Edith Marlowe*. Not *The Secretary*. Not *Household*.

*R. Underhill.* Their own name, spelled out in letters they would have known anywhere in the world — the letters that had once covered forty pages of an engineering notebook on a lakeside dock, drawn diagrams of bridges that could span anything, written out the names of stars in two languages because Anton Varga claimed you hadn't really learned a country until you knew what its poets called its sky.

Rafael put down the knife very carefully, as though it might otherwise do something on its own initiative.

The study was warm. The rain continued. Down the hall, faintly, Marta was singing to herself in the kitchen, and Mrs. Fearn was telling her to mind the milk, and the whole ordinary machinery of the house went on turning, ignorant, while Rafael Underhill sat at their mistress's desk holding an envelope with their own name on it and feeling the temperature of their blood drop degree by degree.

No one wrote to R. Underhill. In three years, no one had written to R. Underhill. Rafael's existence in the world's records was a pencil mark, a wage entry, a name on a driver's license obtained through a solicitor in Carlisle who had asked no questions because Rafael paid well and looked honest. Letters were things that happened to other people. Letters were things Rafael read, sorted, filed, and forgot.

This letter had crossed the river in Quist's boat. This letter had been carried up the drive in the postman's bag. This letter had passed through the village office, where — and Rafael's mind seized on the fact with cold precision — there was a ledger. A ledger in which incoming mail was entered by name and addressee, because the valley's postmistress was a careful woman and because the world had lately become a place where careful women were encouraged to be carefuler still. The papers had been full of it all autumn: registration orders, restriction orders, lists of names, categories of people who had been neighbors and workmen and husbands for thirty years and were now, by Act of Parliament, something else. *Enemy aliens,* the headlines said, in type as tall as your thumb.

Anton Varga was Austrian. Had been Austrian five years ago too, on a summer lake with the sun going down behind the fells, and nobody had thought it mattered, least of all Anton himself, who had laughed and said England would never make war on the country that taught him his engineering, that he was a citizen of bridges, that borders were for maps and maps were for people who lacked imagination.

That was before August. Before the names in the newspapers. Before the shopkeepers in Ashcombe took down the photographs of their German-born sons-in-law.

Rafael looked at the envelope a long moment. Then they turned it over, and broke the seal, and read it standing at the window with the lamp turned low, the way they read everything worth hiding.

The handwriting had changed. That was the first wound — smaller than the words, somehow, and sharper. Five years ago the letters had marched; now they limped, crowded, written fast on whatever paper came to hand. The date at the top was three weeks old. Three weeks in the post, and then however many more in the slowed bags, because the bridge had closed and the world arrived twice a week now, and a man who needed an answer in three weeks had written to a valley that had just become an island.

*Dear R,* the letter began, and even the salutation was a message — no *Rafael*, no endearment, nothing that a censor or a curious postmistress could catch at. Then:

*I will not insult you by pretending this is a social letter. I am dismissed. My papers were taken in September under the new orders — you will have read about the orders, everyone has read about the orders — and I am required to report to a police station ninety miles from my own bed, which is very funny if you know me, as I have never in my life managed ninety miles in a straight line.*

*I am staying — I will not write where — with people who are kind at considerable risk to themselves. But kindness runs out, R, as you better than anyone must know, and mine is running low, and the police are becoming interested in men who move about. I have thought for six weeks of every person I have ever known, and made a list, and your name is the only name left on it.*

*You will remember a promise. I remember it every hour. I made it on a dock with my shoes off, which shows you how seriously I took it, and I take it more seriously now than I have ever taken anything. If you cannot answer, burn this and forgive me, and know that I have never blamed you for being careful, even when I teased you for it. Careful people survive. I was never careful, and see where it has brought me.*

*If you can answer —*

The rest of the sentence was one word. Rafael would carry it for the rest of their life, the way one carries shrapnel: present, invisible, aching at the change of weather.

*If you can answer, then answer.*

There was no address. There was no signature — or rather there was a single letter, *A*, inked large and slightly crooked, the way Anton signed diagrams he was proud of. There was a postmark, and a date, and the fact that the letter existed at all, which was itself a confession that somewhere in the next valley a man with the wrong passport was hiding in a stranger's house waiting for the winter mail like a drowning man watching the shore for lamps.

Rafael read it twice. Then they folded it along its original creases, and stood a moment longer at the window, watching the rain come down over a valley that was now, officially, cut off from everywhere — and did the arithmetic they would do for weeks afterward, lying awake, staring at the ceiling of their room above the coach house.

The ledger at the post office. *R. Underhill, Marlowe House.* Constable Brine, newly arrived, newly ambitious, spending his dull autumn making lists. Two postal days a week, meaning every letter scrutinized twice as hard for being twice as rare. A household whose mistress was the most conspicuous woman in three parishes. And somewhere across the frozen river, a promise made in sunlight five years ago, coming due in the dark with interest.

Downstairs, Mrs. Fearn called that the range wanted banking. Marta's song had stopped. The house was settling into sleep the way it did every night, board by board, trustingly.

Rafael slid the letter back into its envelope, carried it to the study grate, and fed it to the fire piece by piece, holding each corner with the poker until the blue-black ink curled and went white. It was the second letter they had ever burned, and the first had been their own.

Then they washed the ash down, trimmed the lamp, and stood in the dark study doing sums.

*If you can answer, then answer.*

The fire ticked. The river, somewhere out beyond the gardens, ran cold and rising under a bridge that no longer carried anyone anywhere.

Rafael Underhill blew out the lamp and went to find a map.
