## 4. Enemy Alien

The house had a particular silence at half past eleven at night, and Rafael knew all its varieties. There was the silence of the house asleep, which was soft and total, a silence with weight to it like snowfall. There was the silence of Edith working late, which was not silent at all really — the scratch of her pen traveling down the corridor like a small animal, the occasional cough, the creak of the third stair when she went for more tea. And there was this: the silence of a letter burning a hole in a servant's pocket.

Rafael stood in their narrow room at the top of the back stairs with the door bolted and the lamp turned low, and did not take the letter out.

They had carried it through the whole of the evening. Through supper, where Marta had complained about the price of dripping and Cook had agreed at length. Through the hour of mending Edith had insisted on after dinner, Rafael kneeling by the grate to feed the fire while Edith paced behind them, drafting a speech aloud about the franchise and municipal responsibility, her voice rising into the rafters with all the confidence of a woman who had never once in her life been unable to say what she meant. Through the goodnight, the candle carried upstairs, the familiar ritual of it all.

And now the letter lay against Rafael's ribs like a second heart, and the house was properly asleep, and there was no excuse left.

They took it out at last and held it in the lamplight without opening it. Ordinary paper, ordinary envelope, bought cheaply by the look of it, the kind sold in bundles at village shops. The address was written in a hand that Rafael had spent five years trying not to think about and thought about every day anyway. *R. Underhill, The Marlowe House.* Not care of Mrs. Marlowe. Not care of anyone. Their own name, spelled out in that slanting, impatient script that crossed its t's like a man fencing.

Anton Varga knew their name. That alone was dangerous. Half the valley believed Rafael Underhill could barely manage their own signature; the other half had never considered the question at all. A driver was furniture that moved. Furniture did not receive letters.

But Anton had always known exactly what Rafael was. That had been the terror and the miracle of him from the first hour on the lakeshore.

Rafael sat down on the edge of the bed, worked a thumbnail under the flap — no knife, they were too careful for knife-marks on an envelope that might need to look unopened — and drew out two thin sheets folded in thirds.

*My friend,* the letter began, and even those two words undid something in them, because Anton wrote English as he spoke it, extravagantly, with flourishes, and because *my friend* was a claim, and claims could be evidence.

*I write quickly and I burn nothing, which you will understand is unlike me. You will have seen the orders in the papers, or Mrs. Marlowe will have told you of them, since she tells everyone everything and God bless her for it.*

He was right. Edith had told them. Three weeks ago she had read aloud at breakfast from the register of enemy aliens, her voice clipped with contempt, listing the new restrictions as though reading a menu she found insulting: aliens registered, fingerprinted, forbidden to travel beyond five miles of their registered address, forbidden this, forbidden that, subject to internment if the authorities saw fit. She had spoken of it as an outrage to principle, one injustice among the many she kept in a tidy row inside herself, and Rafael had nodded and poured the coffee and felt the floor tilt very slightly beneath their chair, because somewhere out in the world, attached to none of those names, was a man who called them *my friend.*

*The works let me go the week the order came down. Fourteen years an engineer, Rafael. I built the turbine hall at Kessel with my own hands and signed my name in their book fourteen times, and on Monday last a boy younger than my boots handed me a printed notice and walked me to the gate while the others watched from the windows. My landlady was kind. Her son was not. The constable came twice, then three times, and each time his questions were larger. So I have done what you would expect of me, which is the foolish thing, and come here.*

Here. Rafael turned the page and the word hung there, pointing backward at wherever the letter had been posted from, and Rafael understood with a cold sliding sensation that Anton was not careful anymore. Fear had eaten his caution. He had put a return direction on the envelope.

*The next valley over. The mill village — you'll know the one; there is only the one worth knowing. I am lodging above a cooper's shop under a name that is nearly mine, which is the best sort of lie. It holds for now. It will not hold long. They are doing the rounds there as they are everywhere, and a man with my accent and no work papers is a coin jingling in a poor man's pocket.*

Then the handwriting changed. It narrowed, pressed harder into the paper, the pen dragged rather than drawn.

*Rafael. I swore once, by water and by sunlight both, that if ever either of us needed the other and asked, the answer would be yes, and there would be no questions asked. I have not forgotten it. I have tried to forget it — do you understand? I have lain awake these six weeks telling myself that a promise made by a river in summer does not bind a man in winter, that you will have your own life there, your own dangers I know nothing of, that I have no right. All true. And still I am writing this letter, so we may dispense with the argument.*

*I do not ask you to come to me. I ask only this: tell me if the road through your valley is watched. Tell me if a man might cross it alive before the spring. One line back, addressed any way you like, and I will ask nothing else. If you cannot answer, burn this and hate me if you must, but do not carry it. Carrying things is heavier than answering them. I remember how you carry.*

*Your debtors' arithmetic aside, I remain, in sunlight and out of it,
Anton*

Rafael read it four times. Then they sat very still and listened to the house breathe around them — the settling of a beam over the kitchen, wind testing the shutters, far off on the frozen river the boom and groan of ice shifting, a sound like a door closing in the floor of the world.

*Do not carry it. Carrying things is heavier than answering them. I remember how you carry.*

Five years, and he remembered. Rafael had told him perhaps six things about themselves in the whole of that summer, and he had kept every one as though they were coins.

They rose, took the candle to the hearth in the corner of the room, knelt, and fed the letter to the small heap of banked coals. The paper caught slowly, reluctantly, the way paper does when the fire is low, and Rafael watched the words go black-edged and curl — *my friend*, *fourteen years an engineer*, *the next valley over* — and only when the second sheet had gone entirely did they realize their mistake.

The envelope remained in their hand. The envelope with the return direction on it.

Rafael looked at it for a long moment. Then they tore it deliberately into quarters, then eighths, and gave the pieces to the fire one at a time, waiting for each to catch before offering the next, until there was nothing anywhere in the room but ash and the smell of scorched paper and the knowledge, which no fire would take, sitting fully formed behind their eyes:

*The next valley over. The mill village. Above the cooper's shop.*

They had promised not to carry it. They had burned it, as instructed. And they had disobeyed him anyway, because the letter had gone into them the way letters went into Rafael, permanently, and because some part of them — the part that had been awake since the moment the postmistress's boy handed over that envelope with a curious look and said, *This one's got a name on it I don't know* — had already begun, without asking permission, to make plans.

* * *

Sleep did not come. Rafael did not expect it to. Instead they lay flat on their back under the blankets with the lamp out and performed, methodically, the arithmetic that had become the night's real occupation.

Item: a letter had arrived for R. Underhill. Letters arriving required hands. The post came up-valley twice weekly now that the bridge was closed — Tuesday and Saturday, the carrier's cart, and before that the postmistress, who read addresses the way other people read newspapers, thoroughly and with commentary. The carrier's boy had made a remark. Small. Forgettable, probably. Probably.

Probably was a currency Rafael had never been able to afford.

Item: the valley was sealed. This had seemed in October a mere inconvenience — Edith had shrugged, Cook had fretted over her stores, Rafael had privately calculated the post delays and thought nothing worse. Now it cut both ways like everything in the world. No bridge meant no easy road out for a fugitive. No bridge also meant Constable Brine — and Rafael had met his type before, ambition wearing a uniform, boredom sharpened into industry — had nowhere to be except here, among them, inventing work. A sealed valley was a jar with a lid screwed down, and men like Brine loved nothing so much as shaking jars.

Item: Anton Varga was hiding eleven miles away as the crow flew, perhaps fifteen by the road, in a valley equally sealed on its side of the river, wanting only — he claimed — to know whether the road through was passable. But Rafael had read enough letters in this house to read between lines written by any hand. *Tell me if a man might cross it alive before the spring* was not a question about roads. It was a man asking, in the only voice pride permitted him, whether he could come.

And item, the largest, the one Rafael kept circling back to as though walking a wall's edge in the dark: the promise.

They had sworn it, both of them, on a June evening five years gone, standing hip-deep in lake shallows gone silver with dusk, drunk on nothing stronger than confession. Rafael could summon the whole of it still, sense by sense: the heat coming off the rocks, Anton's shirt sleeves rolled, the way he'd said *I will tell you a true thing if you'll trade me one* — and how Rafael, who traded in no truths with anyone, had heard themselves answer *done* before caution could arrive. What Anton had traded Rafael never repeated. What Rafael had traded — the literacy, the self-taught hours by candle stub, the name that wasn't quite the one they'd been born under, the whole constructed self revealed like a watch opened to its gears — Anton had received without flinching, without pity, without a single question. And then he'd said: *Then here is my true thing. If ever you need me and ask, I come. No questions. And I ask the same, and I will almost certainly never use it, because I am too proud, but I want it to exist. Promises are debts, Rafael. I like owing something to someone who can't be robbed of it.*

No questions. That had been the shape of the vow. And the question now, the one turning in Rafael's chest like a key in a stubborn lock, was whether the vow survived its own conditions — whether a promise made in sunlight bound them in winter, in wartime, when the man across the table of the world had been reclassified by Act of Parliament from engineer to enemy, from neighbor to alien, by virtue of nothing except a border drawn through the middle of his life.

Edith would have an answer. Edith had answers the way other houses had mice; you couldn't keep them out. *Justice that stops at the border of convenience isn't justice, it's merchandise* — Rafael could hear her saying it, had heard her say versions of it a hundred times from a hundred platforms. And yet Rafael had watched the same woman that very afternoon sign a household circular, without reading past the first line, pledging local vigilance against enemy sympathizers. She signed such things the way she initialed invoices, briskly, the abstract swallowing the particular. It had not occurred to Edith Marlowe, Rafael was nearly certain, that her own household might contain anything requiring vigilance. That blindness was Edith's great luxury and Rafael's great shield, and Rafael had rested their whole quiet life upon it.

Until tonight.

They turned the arithmetic over once more, the way you turn a purse over to count what remains. The risks, enumerated: discovery of the letter (burned, but the postmistress remembered), discovery of correspondence (none possible now without new danger), discovery of concealment of a person (that was the abyss; that was the one with no bottom). Against it, set out on the other side of the ledger with terrible clarity: a single human being, cold, unemployed, hunted by paperwork, who had once looked at Rafael Underhill and seen a person instead of a position. Who had asked for one line back. Who had said *do not carry it* because he knew Rafael well enough to know they would carry it regardless.

It was not, Rafael thought, staring into the dark ceiling, even truly a decision yet. It was only fear, and fear was honest arithmetic, and honest arithmetic had a way of resolving itself into resolve if you let it run long enough. Somewhere in that process — they could feel it happening, could almost timestamp it, sometime between the second counting of the risks and the fourth replaying of *I remember how you carry* — the fear had stopped being a wall and started being a fuel. Not courage. Courage was a story people told afterward. This was colder and more practical: the simple recognition that there existed no version of the future in which Rafael Underhill sent back silence and then lived comfortably with it. Every other risk was survivable. That one was not.

A line back. Addressed any way you like.

Addressed any way you like. Rafael's mind, which had spent years learning every crack and culvert of this valley, every shortcut, every closed door and unlocked window, began — without any decision having been made, or perhaps with one having been made somewhere below the level of announcement — to work the problem. The post was watched, or might be. The road was watched, or would be. But the river was frozen, and frozen rivers belonged to no registry, and there were people on the river who were sixteen years old and bored and sharp-eyed and utterly invisible to men like Brine.

Nell Quist, ferryman's daughter, who took laundry in at the big house twice a week and had once, catching Rafael watching her race the ice floes in spring, grinned and said, *You lot see everything from the drive, don't you? I can always tell.*

Someone who noticed who noticed. That was worth considering.

The ice boomed again out on the river, long and resonant, and Rafael found they were no longer lying rigid but simply lying, breath slowed, body finally convinced by the argument the mind had concluded. Outside, the frost thickened on the glass in ferns and feathers. In another valley, in a room above a cooper's shop, a man named Anton was awake too — Rafael was sure of it, sure the way one is sure of weather — doing his own sums, waiting on a silence that would tell him everything.

Tomorrow there would be laundry to bring in.

Rafael closed their eyes, and just before sleep took them at last, the last thought was neither fear nor resolve nor memory but something small and practical and final, a sentence laid down like a cornerstone:

*Tuesday. The Quist girl comes Tuesday.*
