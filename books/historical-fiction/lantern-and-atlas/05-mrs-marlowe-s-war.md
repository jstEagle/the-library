## 5. Mrs. Marlowe's War

Edith Marlowe had planned the winter tour down to the quarter hour, and watching the calendar on her study wall die town by town was, she reflected, a particular species of torture invented by a county council that had never once been asked to wait for anything.

The bridge had closed on the first hard frost of November — closed with a cheerful printed notice pinned to the tollhouse door, *until spring*, as though a season were a reasonable unit of apology. She had gone down to look at it herself, of course. She always went and looked at things herself; it was the principle of the thing. The workmen had been polite. The foreman had shown her the cracks in the masonry with the pride of a man displaying his children's drawings, and she had stood in the cold wind off the river calculating, to the day, how many meetings she would miss.

Forty-one. Forty-one engagements between now and thaw, from Millbrook down through the southern towns where the movement had only just begun to put down roots — halls booked, branches organized, women who had written to her in careful hands saying *we have heard you speak of things we did not know could be spoken.* And all of it sealed behind a broken bridge like a letter no one would carry.

"It's an outrage," said her sister-in-law on the telephone, a instrument whose line crackled so badly that every conversation sounded like a conspiracy already. "The council could expedite it if they wanted. They expedite things for men."

"They expedite everything for men," Edith said. "That is rather the whole of my grievance, Alice."

She rang off and stood at the window. Below, on the gravel sweep, Rafael Underhill was washing the motor car in weather no sane person washed a car in, sleeves rolled despite the cold, moving with that unhurried economy Edith had long since stopped noticing and lately begun to notice again. Something had changed in them these past weeks. She couldn't name it precisely. A watchfulness, perhaps. Or the opposite of watchfulness — a person listening for something behind every sound.

She put it out of her mind. There was work, and there was always work.

* * *

If the valley would not let her go out to the war, she decided, then the war would come into the valley. It was not a consolation; it was a strategy, and she made the distinction aloud at least twice a day until her household stopped flinching at it.

The trapped valley suited her purpose better than she first admitted even to herself. Here were four hundred households cut off from the county seat, from newspapers more than three days stale, from any hall large enough to hold a proper meeting — and here, consequently, were women who had nothing to do all winter but think, and talk to one another, and discover that their thoughts were not so very different after all.

She began in her own drawing room, which she converted each Thursday into something between a salon and a barracks. The first week, six women came. By the second Thursday there were eleven, standing shoulder to shoulder among the good chairs because there weren't enough chairs and nobody would sit anyway once the speaking started. The milliner's widow. The doctor's wife, who surprised everyone including herself. Two sisters from the dairy farms above Harrow Bend who had walked four miles through frost fog and arrived pink-faced and defiant, as though the walk itself were the argument they'd come to make.

"You cannot march on London from here," said Mrs. Petch, the doctor's wife, on the third Thursday, in the tone of a woman stating a physical law.

"We are not marching on London," Edith said. "London is not the point. London is a symbol, and symbols are cheap. The point is that Mrs. Hobbes in this room cannot own the shop she has run for eleven years because her dead husband's will was drawn by a solicitor who never imagined she might have opinions. The point is that the girls at the mill work sixty hours for wages that would embarrass a charity board, and when they complain they are told it is temporary, everything is temporary, except their situation." She let the silence do a moment's work. "A valley shut in for the winter is simply a country in miniature, ladies. We have been governed without consent for rather longer than one season. We are merely the first to be able to prove it on a map."

It was, she knew, a fine speech. She had given finer, to larger rooms, but never to a room that needed it more, and she watched the two dairy sisters exchange a look that meant a new branch would be founded before Christmas.

What she did not see — what she was constitutionally unable to see, though years later she would catalogue the missed signals with the rigor of an audit — was Rafael standing in the doorway with the tea tray, or passing through with logs for the fire, present and invisible in the way servants become furniture to people who believe themselves observant.

Rafael heard every word. That much Edith knew, in the dim, unexamined way one knows the cook knows your letters. But knowing a servant hears justice is different from wondering what a servant needs of it.

* * *

She threw herself at the winter the way she threw herself at everything: with timetables.

By the first week of December she had established a rotating schedule of cottage meetings across the valley — here the dissenting chapel vestry, there a farm kitchen lent for the evening by a woman whose husband believed she was at choir practice. She drafted a newsletter, two foolscap pages set by the printer's boy in the village, and named it with deliberate provocation *The Sealed Valley*. She wrote to the national office requesting literature and received back a parcel of pamphlets so mild she nearly posted them into the fire, and wrote again, sharper.

Her correspondence swelled, and with it her dependence on Rafael grew daily. Letters came twice a week now instead of six days, mounded in the post office like snow against a fence, and Rafael sorted them, carried them up on the salver, took her dictated replies away in that tidy handwriting of theirs — far too fine a hand for their station, Edith had thought once, and had the thought and dismissed it in the same breath, the way one dismisses a great many convenient things.

One evening in mid-December she found Rafael in the small office off the hall, at the writing desk she had given over to them, sitting before a half-finished reply with the pen lifted and not moving. Not resting. Suspended. Rafael's eyes were on the paper but Edith, entering quietly, saw that they were not reading it. They were somewhere else entire — some other valley, some other winter.

"Rafael?"

The pen came down. The composure reassembled itself so smoothly that anyone else would have doubted what they'd seen. "Beg pardon, ma'am. The Henderson account — I want the phrasing right."

"The phrasing," Edith said, "can wait for a kettle." She stayed in the doorway, studying them. Underhill was twenty-eight, perhaps thirty; had driven for her three years without one wrong turning, one late arrival, one borrowed pound or loose word. Competence like that was its own kind of wall. She had respected the wall and never once asked what it enclosed. "You've been elsewhere this fortnight. Everywhere I send you, you arrive perfectly and leave early. You stand at windows. You burned your hand on the kettle Tuesday and didn't notice for a full minute."

Rafael looked down at the hand in question, wrapped now in a strip of linen, and said nothing, which from Rafael was a paragraph.

"You're homesick," Edith concluded, with the satisfaction of a woman fitting the last piece into a puzzle she had assembled entirely from her own assumptions. "I should have seen it. This business of the bridge — shut in like this, and the post slowed to nothing, and everyone's people beyond reach. You have family somewhere, I expect. People you'd be seeing at Christmas, in a normal year."

Something crossed Rafael's face — quickly, but not quickly enough, and Edith would spend many nights afterward trying to reconstruct it: not grief exactly, and not guilt, but the specific expression of a person offered the wrong key and having to decide whether to pretend the lock is broken.

"Something like that, ma'am," Rafael said. "Yes. There are— people I'd want to reach, if the roads were open."

"Well." Edith softened, and meant it. "Write to them regardless. The post goes twice a week; it goes slowly; it goes all the same. That is the whole genius of the postal service, and why I defend it in print more often than my readers appreciate. Whatever it is you're carrying about—" she gestured at the younger woman's stillness, the held quality of her, "—it will keep better written down. Trust me. I have made a profession of it."

"Yes, ma'am," said Rafael, and Edith chose, comfortably, to hear agreement.

She went upstairs pleased with herself. She had noticed. She had inquired. She had dispensed sensible counsel. It did not occur to her — it would not occur to her for another month, and then with a force that rearranged her understanding of the past three years — that Rafael had just told her the absolute truth, and that she had heard it as small talk.

* * *

Christmas came and went inside the seal. Edith organized a benefit for the mill girls' heating fund and spoke at it standing on the village hall's billiard table because the stage was too small, and it was reported in the southern papers — arriving nine days late — as *plucky*. She answered every letter. She drilled the new Harrow Bend branch on procedure until its secretary could take minutes in her sleep. She was, by every measure she kept for herself, winning the winter.

And yet.

There were moments, folding the pamphlets, reviewing the subscription list of *The Sealed Valley*, when a thought surfaced that she pushed down like a cork in water: that she knew the names of four hundred women in this valley and could tell you the grievances of a hundred of them, and could not tell you one true fact about the person who drove her motor car, managed her household accounts, and read every letter that entered this house before she did.

She knew Rafael's file, so to speak: references checked, character vouched, wages agreed. She knew Rafael's competence the way she knew the reliability of the boiler. What she did not know was where Underhill had learned that too-fine handwriting, or why a person of such evident intelligence had spent three years fetching hems and minding a radiator, or what lay behind the careful flatness with which they answered any question touching their own history.

Once — only once — she had tried. In October, before the frost, over the accounts. "You write better than my bank manager, Underhill. How does a chauffeur come by a hand like that?"

"Self-taught, ma'am," Rafael had said, with a smile that closed the subject like a gate, and Edith had let it close, because the accounts balanced and the car ran and curiosity about one's servants is a luxury the comfortable cannot afford and the uncomfortable eventually pay for.

Now she stood at her study window on the last dark morning of December, watching Rafael walk down toward the lane to meet the post cart — walking too fast, scanning the road before the road was visible — and felt the cork bob up again. The girl moved like someone expecting news. Like someone standing trial in absentia, in a courtroom Edith could not see.

Absurd, she told herself. Loyal as a spaniel, that one. Homesick, probably. Lonely, certainly — loneliness was the occupational disease of service, and wasn't that precisely what her whole argument came down to, the invisible labor, the unseen lives? She fought for women who were unseen. She had said so forty times this winter.

The thought paused. Turned. Looked at her.

She did not follow it further. There was a branch meeting at two, and a draft to finish, and injustice was easier to fight at a lectern than at a breakfast table.

Down the lane, the post cart appeared out of the white morning, and Rafael quickened — not toward it, Edith noticed, but almost away from it, a small instinctive recoil, quickly mastered, before going forward to meet whatever the saddlebags carried.

Edith watched until the lane bent and took them both from view, and turned back to her desk, and did not think about it again for eleven days — by which time Constable Brine would be standing in her hall asking questions about the Marlowe mail, and looking, as he asked them, directly at Rafael.
