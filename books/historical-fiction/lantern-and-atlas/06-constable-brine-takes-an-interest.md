## 6. Constable Brine Takes an Interest

The first frost had come and gone without ceremony, laying its silver hand over the valley one night in early November and refusing to lift by morning. Rafael noticed it the way they noticed everything — from the driver's seat, hands gloved on the wheel of the Marlowe motorcar, breath ghosting up past their collar. The hedges along the Millbrook road wore white seams. The paddock grass crunched underfoot when Edith crossed it to complain about the state of the lane, which she did every morning now with the vigor of a woman who needed something to be wrong about.

"It's an outrage," she said, settling into her seat with a rustle of wool. "A valley full of people sealed in like jam because the county can't be bothered to pour concrete before winter."

"They've promised March," Rafael said.

"The county promises everything and delivers gravel." She snapped her gloves taut. "To the post office, Rafael. If I'm to be imprisoned in my own valley, I intend to write furious letters about it while I wait."

Rafael drove. That was the shape of most days: drive, carry, sort, read. The bridge closure had slowed the post to twice weekly — Tuesdays and Fridays, weather permitting — so that the mail now arrived in dense, breathing bundles, sacks of it, as though the whole valley's withheld words came at once like water released from a dam. And each Tuesday and Friday evening, after the household retired, Rafael sat in the small room off the kitchen with the good lamp lit and read every letter addressed to Edith Marlowe before carrying it up to her tray in the morning.

They told themselves it was prudence. It was, in part. A woman in Edith's position made enemies; a servant who was careless could get her hurt. But prudence was only the coat the hunger wore underneath. Rafael had been illiterate until fifteen — had been *nothing* until fifteen, a nameless scrap of the workhouse floor — and the first words they had ever owned were ones no one knew they could read. Reading other people's letters was theft, perhaps. It was also the closest thing to possession Rafael had ever permitted themselves.

That Friday, the post brought the usual harvest: three circulars, a bill from the draper in Tarnford, an envelope smelling faintly of lavender from some cousin in the south, and a stiff card from the County Federation confirming a date for a lecture that could no longer be given, the speaker being sealed in the valley like jam.

Rafael read them all twice, filed them in the walnut sorting box, and thought nothing more of any of it until Sunday, when Constable Brine came to call.

* * *

Brine had been on the village constabulary for eleven months, long enough to learn everyone's name and short enough to still find the posting an insult. He was thirty or thereabouts, pink-faced, with a moustache groomed to a point of such aggressive tidiness that Rafael privately suspected he trimmed it with the same precision he applied to his ambitions. Before the war, he'd have moved on to a town constabulary by spring. Now the war had handed men like Brine a gift wrapped in khaki paper: a valley suddenly full of legitimate suspicions, a population legally obligated to present itself for counting.

He began with the Kesslers.

Everyone knew it before anyone said it. Old Kessler had run the clock-and-watch shop on Fore Street for twenty-two years; his son Heinrich had been born in the valley, in the room above the shop, and had never slept a night outside England in his life. On Monday the shop was closed. On Tuesday Brine was seen coming out of it with a ledger under his arm and his expression arranged into the particular gravity of a man who has found exactly what he went in looking for, whatever that happened to be.

By Wednesday, the word *register* was being spoken in shops and over gates. Aliens of German origin, Brine explained to whoever asked, were required to present themselves. Names, addresses, places of birth, particulars of employment. It was all perfectly routine, he said, in a tone calibrated to make routine sound like a favor he was doing you.

Edith received this news at breakfast and received it, Rafael noted, at arm's length.

"Well," she said, buttering toast with the deliberation she used on opponents she hadn't yet decided whether to fight or convert. "One can't argue with the necessity of knowing who is in the country. Half of Europe is marching under false pretenses."

"Heinrich Kessler mended Mrs. Prewitt's watch chain for nothing," said Marta from the doorway, the teapot in her hands and something dangerous in her voice. She was nineteen and had not yet learned to keep her face for the upstairs rooms. "He's done my mother's clock twice. He's never been to Germany except once, when he was nine, to see his grandmother die."

"Which is precisely why he has nothing to fear from a register," Edith said, in the reasonable voice she used when she was aware, somewhere beneath the reasonableness, that she was losing an argument. "If a man is innocent, a list does him no harm."

Marta set the teapot down harder than a teapot ought to be set down and left the room. Edith looked at the door, then at her toast, and ate the toast.

Rafael poured the tea and said nothing. This was the discipline of their life: the pouring, the saying nothing, the perfect surface. But underneath, where they kept the margins, a small cold arithmetic had already begun. Because Rafael had read the papers too — every word since August, folded inside Edith's copies of *The Times* and *Votes for Women* — and Rafael knew that lists were never only lists. A list was a net. And nets did not ask what a man had done; they asked what a man was called.

And there was a letter sitting in the grate of their memory, not two weeks old, that Rafael had fed to the kitchen fire page by page.

* * *

Brine came to Marlowe House on Thursday afternoon, while Edith was out walking the estate with her sister-in-law and the light was going thin and blue over the river.

Rafael answered the door themselves, which was unusual — normally Marta took the door — but the timing was bad and Rafael had a housekeeper's instinct for intercepting things, honed over years of standing between Edith Marlowe and the world's less pleasant deliveries.

"Constable." They kept their voice level, unhurried, faintly bored. Boredom was armor. Fear was a scent, and dogs and constables both caught it.

"Underhill." Brine consulted a small notebook he didn't appear to need. "Mrs. Marlowe in?"

"Her ladyship's walked down to the home farm. Shall I say you called?"

"I'll catch her another time." He didn't move. The hallway behind Rafael was dim and smelled of beeswax and last night's fire. Somewhere upstairs a clock ticked with the patience of furniture. "Actually, while I'm here. You handle the household's post, I understand."

"I see it sorted, sir."

"Sorted." Brine smiled. His teeth were very even. "You'd know, then, what passes through. Letters in, letters out. For the household generally."

"There's the usual. Bills. Circulars. Her ladyship's federation correspondence — there's a deal of that, as you'll imagine." Rafael let a small dry note enter their voice, the note of a man mildly wearied by his employer's enthusiasms. It was a performance they had rehearsed for years without knowing it: the competent, incurious servant, slightly superior about the mistress's politics, loyal in the dull way servants are presumed loyal. "More of it than bills, some weeks."

"And anything out of the ordinary?"

"Out of the ordinary, sir?"

Brine's eyes were pale and quick and they went over Rafael's face the way a man goes over a lock, testing the tumblers. "Letters from abroad. Foreign stamps. Anything in a language that isn't English. Given the circumstances."

"The circumstances," Rafael repeated, gently, as though tasting the phrase.

"The war, Underhill." Impatience flickered through the affability, and Rafael understood suddenly how much the man wanted this — wanted a find, a catch, something to carry to the county superintendent with both hands. "There are people in this valley whose loyalty is owed elsewhere. My job is to know who they are and what moves through the post. You'd be surprised what a fellow sees, doing this work." He tapped the notebook against his leg. "Names come up. Questions get asked. Best thing a man can do is cooperate plainly. Nothing to hide, nothing to fear — that's how it works now."

"That's how it always worked, sir, far as I ever saw."

It was the right answer, delivered flatly, with just enough of the servant's shrug in it. Rafael watched it land. Brine wanted resistance; resistance would justify him. What he got instead was mildness, a man agreeing about the weather.

"So nothing foreign comes through here. No correspondence from the continent. Nothing you couldn't read yourself."

"No, sir." A pause, measured to the hair. "I couldn't say I read her ladyship's letters, sir. But nothing's come with foreign stamps on it since the summer, that I'd swear to."

All technically true. The letter had borne an English postmark, forwarded twice, the sender having learned caution the way the desperate learn everything — quickly, and too late. Rafael held the truth up like a lampshade over the dark and let Brine look at the light.

"Mm." Brine wrote something in the notebook anyway. Men like him always wrote something; ink was how they convinced themselves the day had produced evidence. "How long have you been with Mrs. Marlowe?"

"Six years, come spring."

"Before that?"

"Various. Service in Tarnford. Before that—" Rafael let the pause hang a half-second, then supplied the lie they had worn smooth as a river stone: "—the north. Work."

"The north," Brine repeated, without inflection, writing again. "Family local, are they?"

"Gone, sir."

Something moved behind Brine's pink composure — not sympathy; curiosity, the professional kind, sniffing at a door that stood ajar. Rafael felt the draft of it on their neck and did the only thing there was to do: stepped back from the threshold and widened the opening, offering the house, the hall, a cup of tea, the whole dull theater of service. "Shall I fetch her ladyship after all? She won't forgive me if she learns you called and was turned away."

"No need." Brine pocketed the notebook. At the steps he paused, turned back with the air of a man remembering a courtesy, and dealt the actual blow as though it were small talk. "You'll want to collect your own post promptly, mind. There's a letter sat unclaimed for you at the office near a fortnight now. Quist mentioned it when I was in about the register — girl's sharp, keeps her ledgers better than her father does. Unclaimed post draws attention these days, Underhill. Everything draws attention these days." He settled his cap. "Good afternoon."

He went down the drive whistling something tuneless. Rafael stood in the doorway with the cold coming in around their ankles and the hallway clock ticking behind them, and let the sentence finish arriving.

*A letter sat unclaimed for you.*

They had known, of course. Some part of them had known since the night they burned Anton's pages and watched the ash curl — known that a letter did not simply vanish because it was destroyed, that the postal world kept its own accounts, that somewhere in a canvas sack in the Quists' front room lay an envelope addressed in a hand Rafael knew better than their own, waiting like a landmine dressed as correspondence. They had told themselves the post office ledger was a private clutter, that Nell Quist was fourteen and incurious, that no one in a valley of four hundred souls would trouble to cross-reference a chauffeur's mail.

But Brine troubled. Troubling was his profession and his appetite, and he had just been handed, by a girl with sharp eyes and tidy columns, exactly the thread he'd want to pull.

* * *

That night Rafael did not sleep. They lay in the narrow room over the carriage house with the moon laying a hard white stripe across the floorboards, and they did sums.

Item: a letter, addressed to R. Underhill, arrived — when? Nine days ago? Twelve? — entered in the Quist ledger in Nell's upright hand. Unclaimed.

Item: Constable Brine, who had begun asking the valley's households about their post, now possessed that item, or would by Monday when he went in to copy his registers.

Item: R. Underhill, who had no family, no correspondents, no history that any record in the county could produce, and whose entire constructed self depended on being a man to whom no letter had ever come.

Item: the promise. *If either ever needs help and asks, the other answers, no questions.* Spoken across five years and a lake's width of summer light, and answered — Rafael had answered, God help them — in flame and ash in the kitchen grate.

The arithmetic was ugly and it came out the same every way they ran it. If they claimed the letter, they claimed a connection to a man whose name was now, by order of the Crown, a category of suspicion. If they disclaimed it — misdelivered, unknown to me, sir — they invited Brine to open it, and Brine would open it, and even if the envelope held nothing but Anton's careful coded pleasantries, the address alone would put Rafael on a list, and lists, Rafael had understood since Monday's argument over toast, were nets.

There was a third arithmetic, the one they did not want to do, and lying in the moonlight they made themselves do it anyway. Anton was in the next valley. Anton had written asking — what? Rafael had burned the letter too fast, that first terrible night, and had retained only fragments: *dismissed*, *cannot draw wages*, *if you remember*, *no questions*. Fragments were not a plan. If Anton needed shelter, the ice road was the only way in now that the bridge was shut, and the ice road was a month from bearing weight, and between here and there stood a constable collecting names in a notebook with the diligence of a man building a case one entry at a time.

Every road forward ran through Brine's ledger. And Brine's ledger already had Rafael's name in it once.

Rafael rose before dawn, dressed, and stood a long time at the window watching the river. It ran dark between its frost-whited banks, unhurried, indifferent, carrying nothing now but cold. In six weeks it would begin to freeze at the edges. In eight, if the winter held, a man might walk across it in the dark.

First, then. First the letter. First the small, stupid, unavoidable errand that could not be avoided, because the longer it sat unclaimed the more it looked like exactly what it was.

Tuesday was the next post day. Rafael spent Monday in a state of performed normalcy so complete it exhausted them — drove Edith to the Kesslers' to offer, stiffly, the loan of her patronage for Heinrich's case (she had woken, apparently, to some portion of her conscience); ate lunch; polished the car's brasses within an inch of their lives. And on Tuesday morning, with the first sledge of the season jingling somewhere down the Millbrook road and the sky the color of dirty wool, Rafael walked into the Quists' post office with three of Edith's outgoing letters in one hand and their heart beating somewhere up around their ears.

Nell Quist looked up from behind the counter. She was small and freckled and had her father's heavy brows and none of his vagueness, and her eyes went to Rafael's face and then away, and something passed across her expression — quickly, professionally — that Rafael would spend the rest of the winter learning to read.

"Morning, Mr. Underhill," she said. "Post for the house is ready whenever your mistress wants it." She reached below the counter, unhurried, and set one envelope on the worn wood, face down, apart from the rest, the way a shopgirl sets aside an item already sold.

"There's been one here for you," said Nell Quist, fourteen years old, keeper of ledgers, "near three weeks. I told the constable I couldn't rightly hand out another person's post without proper claiming." She slid it across. "Proper claiming," she repeated, very quietly, and looked at the door.
