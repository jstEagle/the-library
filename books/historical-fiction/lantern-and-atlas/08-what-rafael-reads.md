## 8. What Rafael Reads

The winter mail came on Tuesdays and Fridays, and by the second week of December, Rafael had begun to dread both days.

Not because of Anton's letter — that was burned, its ashes long since rinsed down the drain of the scullery sink — but because every bag Nell Quist's father ferried across the frozen river brought more paper into a house where Rafael's hands were supposed to be merely useful. Sort. Deliver. Place the bills on the estate ledger side of the desk and the personal correspondence in the lacquer tray with the mother-of-pearl inlay. It was a simple choreography, performed a thousand times, invisible as breathing.

Except that Rafael read everything.

They had explained it to themselves years ago, in the beginning: *I must know what she's agreeing to before she signs. I must know which debts are urgent. A secretary who cannot read her correspondence cannot serve her.* All true, all defensible, and all beside the point. Edith Marlowe had never once asked Rafael to open a letter marked private. Rafael simply did it, the way some people drink, or pray — an appetite that felt too much like need to be called a habit.

Tonight the study fire was low and the lamp turned down to its economical blue flame, the one Edith never used because it flattered no one's complexion but suited Rafael's purposes exactly: enough light to read by, not enough to read *by*, should anyone pause at the door. Rafael sat with the day's tray and began, as always, with the vicar's wife.

*My dear Mrs. Marlowe,* — so far, so ordinary — *I write in confidence, and beg you will burn this as you value our friendship. You will have heard that my husband has taken against the Cause, and that is his right as head of our household. What you will not have heard is that I have sold my grandmother's opal brooch to fund the branch society, and told him it went for repairs to the church clock. I am forty-six years old and I have never lied to him before, and I find I am very good at it.*

Rafael read it twice. The handwriting was upright and careful until the last lines, where it slanted, hurried, as though the words had gotten ahead of their courage.

There was nothing to do with such a letter but file it under the society papers where Mr. Halloway would never look, and add its author to the list of women whose subscriptions were paid from mysterious sources. The list had eleven names now. Rafael kept it in their own head, nowhere written — a private census of the valley's quiet rebels.

* * *

The debt notices came next, and there the reading was less a pleasure than an arithmetic. Farmer Dill's second notice from the agricultural bank, red-stamped. The ironmonger's final warning to the Hendersons, who had bought a coffin on credit in October and could not stop buying since — grief made extravagant, Rafael had learned, watching households come apart along the seams of their ledgers. And one from a clothier in Keswick addressed to young Lieutenant Fairholme's mother, dunning her for his dress uniform alterations, and Rafael set that one gently aside to be paid out of the household account without Edith being troubled, because some mercies required only a pen stroke and silence.

It was the third letter in the tray that stopped Rafael's hands.

No return address. The postmark smudged. Addressed in a round, uneducated hand to *Mrs. Marlowe or whoever opens her letters, if God puts it in your heart.* Rafael almost put it down there. Almost. But the hand trembled even through the ink, and trembling handwriting had a claim on them that propriety did not.

*Mrs. Marlowe,—*

*I know you are a great lady for the vote and perhaps too busy for a plain woman's trouble. My Thomas joined up in September with the territorials and is somewhere in France, they will not say where. He is seventeen. I told the recruiting man he was eighteen because he begged me and because the army feeds a lad better than I can this winter, and now I lie awake every night knowing I signed him over with my own mouth.*

*I have written him eleven letters. He answers maybe one in three, and short, and cheerful, and I know cheerfulness when it is being done to me, Mrs. Marlowe, I have done it myself at a deathbed. Last letter he said the mud is the worst part. Not the shelling. The mud. What kind of place is it where a mother's whole comfort hangs on whether her boy lies about the mud?*

*I enclose his last note. Tell me if you think he is well. Truly well. You read newspapers, you know things. Do not spare me.*

The enclosed note was folded small and soft as cloth, having been carried and unfolded many times.

*Dear Mam,—*

*Coping on grand. Mud everywhere like porridge, we laugh about it. Fred Kelsall got a parcel with a plum duff in it and shared round. Don't send socks, the ones you sent were lovely but the post eats parcels. Kiss the little ones. Your loving son, Thomas.*

Rafael sat for a long while with the two letters lying together on the desk, the mother's fear and the son's mercy, and understood that she would never get what she asked for. There was no truth in that note to find. *Coping on grand.* Seventeen years old, doing cheerfulness at a deathbed — the deathbed being his own, probably, and the mud real, and the plum duff possibly invented whole to give her something sweet to hold.

She had asked Rafael — asked Mrs. Marlowe — to tell her if he was truly well. And the honest answer was that nobody knew, and that the letter itself was proof of how far a family could drift from its own truth across three hundred miles of censored line.

Rafael drafted the reply four times. *Your Thomas writes with an easy hand and a full heart* — true, and useless. Finally they settled on: *He is plainly thinking of you in every line. Boys his age protect their mothers the way soldiers protect a position; his cheerfulness is not evidence of danger concealed but of love, held in the only way he can hold it. Write to him of ordinary things. Ordinary things are what they are fighting to keep.* It was not quite a lie. It was the best thing Rafael owned that wasn't true, and they sealed it with the Marlowe franking stamp and felt the weight of the misuse settle somewhere behind the breastbone.

* * *

Friday's mail brought the love letters, which were Rafael's secret shame and secret joy.

The district nurse, Miss Braithwaite, wrote weekly to a married man in Penrith whose wife was, everyone said kindly, not quite right in her mind and had not been for nine years. The letters crossed each other in the post like birds migrating in opposite weather. His arrived first that morning, and Rafael read it standing by the window with the frost ferning up the glass behind them.

*Dearest Ruthie,—*

*Tuesday again. I count the days to Tuesdays the way other men count pay. Ellen had a better night. Dr. Craig says her mind is quieter this season, which is his way of saying nothing, but I choose to take comfort, as one does. I have been thinking about the summer we took the boat to Ullswater and you wore your hair loose and the wind had its way with it, and you laughed at me for being solemn about scenery. I am still solemn about scenery, Ruthie. I am still solemn about you.*

*I know what we agreed. I know the terms. But terms are for business, and this is not business, whatever we call it. If anything happened to me — I am forty-one and fat and unlikely, but say the boiler burst — you would learn it from a stranger. That seems to me a wrong I have no right to do you. So: the enclosed paper tells you where my papers are, and what I would want. Burn it if it frightens you. Keep it if you ever loved me half as much as I—*

The rest was crossed out and rewritten beneath in a smaller hand: *as I do you. Yours entirely, J.B.*

Rafael filed it in the tray for delivery up the lane, and thought: *he has made her a widow in advance, in writing.* And thought: *she will keep the paper. Of course she will keep the paper.* And thought, with a sudden hot uncomfortable clarity: *I have no right to any of this. Every letter I read is a room I walk into uninvited, and I keep walking, because the alternative is my own rooms, my own silence, my own single chair.*

But then — Rafael was honest with themselves even about dishonesty — that was not why they read either. They read because Ruthie Braithwaite would carry this letter against her heart all day on her rounds and no one else in the world would ever know that the brisk, competent nurse who set broken collarbones without flinching was loved *entirely*, in writing, with a boiler-burst clause. Someone ought to know. Knowing was a kind of witness. Rafael had been invisible so long that witnessing had become the shape their soul took.

* * *

That afternoon, driving Edith back from the Hendersons' — she had gone to argue with the ironmonger's manager about the debt and lost gracefully, which was her least favorite way to lose — Rafael held the reins and let her talk, which was most of the skill of driving Mrs. Marlowe.

"—and the man looked straight past me to Henderson himself, as if the money were his to discuss, as if Mrs. Henderson hadn't signed every order in that house since the funeral. That's the whole war in miniature, Underhill. Half the population rendered furniture." She pulled her gloves off finger by finger, glaring at the hedgerows. "We'll have a meeting of the branch Thursday. The Hallam sisters will host. And I want the circulars drafted tonight — short, plain, no Latin quotations this time, I don't care how much it pleases me."

"Yes, ma'am."

"You're quiet lately." Her eyes came around sideways, sharp as a billhook under the wool. "Not homesick? It's a hard thing, a valley sealed in. Some minds need the road."

Rafael considered the question with the same care they gave the letters. "Not homesick, ma'am."

"Good." Satisfied, misreading, already elsewhere. "Thursday, then. And Underhill — the Hallams' father reads the post before they do, I'd stake the motor on it. Get the circulars worded so they can be read aloud at his table without hanging anyone."

*I'd stake the motor on it.* Rafael turned the horses through the gate and thought about how casually she said it, how universally assumed: that fathers read daughters' post, that husbands read wives', that employers' letters passed under servants' eyes at their peril and servants' letters existed mostly as rumors. The whole valley was a lattice of opened envelopes. Nobody's words traveled in peace. And yet everyone wrote, endlessly, urgently, because the alternative to being read wrongly was not being read at all.

* * *

The last item Friday was a thin envelope addressed in pencil, from the Hallam sisters to Edith, confirming Thursday. On the back, upside down and crammed into the margin where a censor might not think to look — where a father certainly wouldn't — was a single added line in different ink:

*R. says the coast road is watched now. Tell no one. E.*

Rafael read it three times by the blue flame, and the cold that moved through them had nothing to do with the frost on the window.

*E* was Eleanor Hallam, twenty-two, who sang in the choir and subscribed to the Cause with egg money. *R.* — Rafael did not know who R. was. Could be a brother. Could be a sweetheart. Could be a cousin in Whitehaven with opinions about the coast road. It did not matter who R. was. What mattered was that Eleanor Hallam was running messages of some kind, small ones, frightened ones, and hiding them in the margins of suffrage circulars, and had chosen those circulars — chosen *this house*, chosen *Edith Marlowe's mail* — as the safest channel in the valley.

Because everyone knew Mrs. Marlowe's post was watched by the Cause's friends and ignored by its enemies. Because a lady's letters passed unexamined. Because—

Because someone out there had reasoned their way to the same conclusion Rafael lived inside every day: that the margins of respectable paper were the safest country in England.

Rafael held the envelope over the lamp a moment longer than duty required, then placed it in the tray, delivered it upstairs with the evening tea, and stood a moment in the corridor listening to Edith's pen scratching away at her speech for Thursday — a speech about the franchise, about justice, about the clean bright future — while below stairs Marta argued cheerfully with the gardener about whether the Germans ate breakfast differently from Christians, and out past the ice-rimed gardens the river lay locked white under the moon, and somewhere beyond it, in the next valley, a man was waiting on a promise.

Everyone living double lives, Rafael thought, climbing back down to their room. The vicar's wife and her brooch. Nurse Braithwaite and her widower-in-advance. Thomas lying about the mud, his mother lying to the recruiting sergeant, Eleanor Hallam penciling warnings into margins, Marta laughing about breakfast so as not to say what she thought about loyalty. The valley was a sackful of sealed letters, all of them steamed open by somebody, all of them containing something other than what the address promised.

Rafael had believed their own secret was a stone in the shoe, a private limp. Tonight it felt more like a uniform. Everyone in the valley wore one; theirs merely fit better than most.

They banked the fire, wound the clock, and took out the stub of pencil and the scrap of paper on which, for six days now, they had been sketching in tiny careful strokes the geography of the next valley over: roads, the works where Anton had been employed, the farms a strong swimmer might reach if the ferry were watched. Six days of silence since the burned letter. Either Anton was still there, waiting, or he had moved and the promise had gone cold on the far side of the river, and there was no way to learn which except—

Except that Rafael knew how messages traveled now. Knew it the way they knew everything else worth knowing: from other people's mail.

The Hallam sisters hosted the meeting Thursday. Eleanor Hallam ran messages along the coast. And between the Marlowe household and the coast lay the river, and along the river lay the Quists' ferry steps, where the ferryman's daughter handled every parcel that crossed the water and looked, to all appearances, like nobody at all.

Rafael put the scrap of map away under the loose brick behind the stove and lay in the dark composing, mentally, the wording of a circular — short, plain, readable aloud at a suspicious father's table without hanging anyone — with a sentence hidden in its margins that only a certain kind of reader would ever find.

Thursday, then. Two days.
