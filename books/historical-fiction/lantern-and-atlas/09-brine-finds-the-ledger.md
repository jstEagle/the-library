## 9. Brine Finds the Ledger

The frost had come into the house itself by the second week of December. It lived in the corners of the windows like a tenant nobody had invited, and it made lace of the panes in the scullery each morning before Marta arrived to salt the step. Rafael knew all its habits now: which doors swelled in their frames, which pipe in the east wall would knock at four in the morning like a creditor with a grudge, which rooms Edith Marlowe would tolerate being cold and which she would not.

Edith tolerated cold everywhere but her study. So it was that Rafael stood at the study hearth that Tuesday morning, feeding the fire log by log while Edith sat wrapped in a shawl she pretended was for fashion, dictating letters to women who could not yet vote for the women who might one day listen to them.

"The meeting at the chapel must be moved to Thursday," Edith said, not looking up from her notes. "Half the valley is at market on Thursday, and I want them passing the door."

"They pass the door anyway," Rafael said. "You've made sure of it."

"That's what posters are for." She glanced up then, and something crossed her face — a small crease of assessment, the look of a woman doing sums she hadn't meant to start. "You're thin, Underhill. Thinner than you were in November. Are you sleeping?"

"Like the dead," Rafael said, which was true if the dead spent their nights staring at ceilings and rehearsing lies they hadn't yet been asked to tell.

The letter from Anton was ash these three weeks gone. Rafael had burned it in this very grate, piece by piece, holding each corner with a fork until the flames took the handwriting out of the world entirely — and still Rafael could recite it. *You said no questions. I am asking none. Only that you remember.* The words lived behind Rafael's teeth like a stone that had been swallowed wrong and could go neither up nor down.

The fire caught properly. Rafael dusted bark off their hands and went out into the hall, where the day's post lay in a wire basket — twice weekly now since the bridge closed, a thin trickle of the world arriving over the hills by carrier's cart. Rafael sorted it standing, as always: bills first, then the circulars, then the two letters with London postmarks that meant the movement was thinking about Edith again.

It was while sorting that the knock came. Not the soft double rap of a neighbor. A single flat blow, official as a stamp.

Rafael opened the door with their face already arranged into mild, serviceable blankness — the face of a person whose whole biography is *may I help you* — and found Constable Brine standing on the step with his collar turned up against the wind and a ledger under his arm.

"Underhill," he said. He knew the name. That was the first thing Rafael noticed, and the first thing to be afraid of. "Got a moment?"

"For you, Constable, several," Rafael said, and held the door wide.

* * *

Brine came into the hall the way a draught comes in — without apology, finding every gap. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a constable's habit of standing where he could see more than one doorway at once, and he had the ledger with him the way other men carried Bibles: openly, but meaning to use it.

It was the post office ledger. Rafael knew it at once — the canvas-bound book with its marbled spine, in which every item of mail passing through the valley was entered in the postmistress's upright hand. Rafael had seen it perhaps forty times across the counter at Quist & Son, had watched Nell's father run his finger down its columns. It had never seemed a dangerous object. Books rarely do until someone reads them.

"Mild morning," Brine observed, unwinding his scarf with theatrical patience. "For December."

"It comes and goes."

"It does." He let the silence sit. He was good at silences; Rafael gave him credit for that. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard complained under Marta's feet, and the kitchen smelled of onions and bay, and the whole ordinary architecture of the morning stood around them pretending nothing was happening. "I'll come to it, then, since you're a busy man. We've been registering the foreign-born. You'll have seen the notices."

"I've sorted the notices," Rafael said. "Into the circulars."

"And your name comes up." Brine opened the ledger onto the hall table with the care of a man laying out evidence at an inquest. His finger — blunt, clean, nail bitten at the corner — travelled down a column. "Underhill. R. No place of birth recorded at the census, which is its own kind of interesting. And here—" The finger stopped. Tapped. Twice. "A letter. Received the twenty-ninth of November. Addressed to R. Underhill, care of Mrs. E. Marlowe. Never claimed."

The hall was very warm from the study fire, and Rafael felt the warmth leave them entirely, as though the body had decided heat was a luxury it could no longer fund. *Dread arriving wrapped in ordinary paper* — that had been Rafael's thought when the letter came, and now the paper was gone but the dread had stayed, patient, and here was its representative on earth with a ledger under his arm.

"A circular," Rafael said.

Brine looked up slowly. He had grey eyes, unremarkable, the sort of eyes that would never be remembered at a party and would never forget anything they saw. "A circular."

"Misdelivered. The carriers get confused when the bridge closes — new routes, relief men who don't know the houses. It was some advertisement or other. Farm machinery, I believe, or a patent medicine. I set it aside meaning to return it and then forgot it entirely." Rafael shrugged, and the shrug cost more than any speech ever had. "Paper's paper, Constable. Most of it isn't worth claiming."

"You didn't open it?"

"Circulars don't need opening. You can read the gist through the envelope if you hold them to the light." Rafael smiled — a servant's smile, faintly apologetic, inviting the constable to share in the joke of a world full of useless mail. "If I'd known it was going to interest the police, I'd have burned it with the rest of the waste."

That last word left Rafael's mouth and hung there, and Rafael heard too late exactly how it sounded. *Burned.* Brine heard it too. Something moved behind the grey eyes, slow as ice shifting on a river — not certainty, but the shape of a question assembling itself out of parts.

"Burned," Brine repeated pleasantly.

"The grate gets everything eventually. It's the only system this house trusts."

They regarded one another across the ledger. Outside, wind moved through the bare elms with a sound like water, and down the drive the world lay white and silent, sealed off at both ends by frozen river and closed bridge. An island. Rafael had thought of the valley as an island often enough these past weeks, but had somehow always been the islander, not the thing the tide was coming in for.

"You know," Brine said, closing the ledger but keeping his hand flat upon it, as if it might otherwise escape, "most people, when I ask them a plain question, give me a plain answer and we're done in a minute. But every so often I meet somebody who answers like a man playing chess. Each answer covers another square. It's quite impressive, really. You can always spot it because it takes longer than lying would."

"I wouldn't know, Constable. I'm not much of a chess player."

"No," Brine agreed mildly. "But I'd wager you're a reader. There's not many servants keep their own correspondence, in my experience. Not many can." He picked up the ledger, tucked it back under his arm with the familiarity of long possession. "Where did you learn your letters, Underhill?"

"In service. The children needed practising on and the governess got tired."

"That's a tidy answer, too."

"It's a true one."

"I'm glad to hear it." He wound the scarf back around his neck, unhurried, a man establishing that time was his to spend. Then, at the door, with his hand on the latch, he turned — and dropped the pleasantness entirely, letting it fall away like the scarf might have fallen, so that for a moment Rafael saw the thing underneath, which was not anger but appetite, quiet and certain and fed daily. "Here's what I think. I think there was a letter. I think it wasn't farm machinery. I think you know that, and I think you know why the postmark mattered, and I think the reason you've gone thin as a lath since November has less to do with winter than you'd have me believe. I can't prove any of it. Yet. But this valley's shut up tight as a drum till spring, and drums are my business, so to speak. I'll be watching, Underhill. The mail. The lanes. The ice, when it bears. You'd be surprised what a man sees when he stands still long enough."

He let himself out. The latch clicked with the smallness of a very large thing concluding, for now.

* * *

Rafael stood in the hall until the cold where Brine had been faded back into ordinary warmth, and then began, with great care, to sort the post that remained in the basket, because hands that stopped moving invited questions and hands at work answered none. Bills. Circulars. The London letters, squared and tapped into alignment. The ordinary liturgy of other people's lives.

"You can stop now," said Edith Marlowe from the study doorway. "I've watched you square those same letters three times. If you square them again they'll turn back into pulp."

Rafael looked up. Edith stood with her shawl clutched at her throat and her reading glasses pushed up into her greying hair, and her expression was one Rafael had never seen directed at them before. Not the brisk fondness of an employer. Not the half-attention of a woman surrounded by people paid to agree with her. This was something sharper and more interested — the look she turned on a witness at a meeting who had just said something that contradicted the pamphlets.

"How long," Rafael asked, "have you been standing there?"

"Long enough." She came out into the hall proper, and Rafael braced for the questions — where, whom, what have you brought into my house — but Edith Marlowe, whatever her faults, had never in six years asked a servant to account for themselves as a servant. She lowered herself onto the hall bench, the one beneath the mirror, and patted the place beside her with the imperiousness of a woman who had never once been refused a seat by anyone. "Sit down, Underhill. You've gone the colour of the wallpaper."

"I'm all right."

"You're not, but sitting costs nothing." Rafael sat. Up close, Edith smelled of ink and eucalyptus, and her hands, folded in her lap, were spotted and steady. For a moment neither spoke. In the study the fire popped; somewhere far off a crow said one syllable and thought better of it. "That man," Edith said at last, "has been in my hall twice this month without once asking whether I mind. I begin to understand how the Germans feel about our side of the North Sea." She paused. "He thinks you're hiding something."

"He thinks a great deal. Thinking's free."

"Yes, well. I've built a career on it, so I won't sneer at the practice." She turned her head and looked at Rafael directly, and there it was again — the new look, the stranger's look, except that it wasn't unkind. It was, if anything, the opposite: the look of a woman discovering that a person she had counted among the furniture of her life had an interior, a past, a set of weights and measures all their own. "Six years you've driven that car and read me my letters and never once told me a thing about yourself that wasn't useful. I noticed, you know. I told myself it was discretion, and that discretion was a virtue, and then I stopped thinking about it, because I am a selfish woman with a war on and a country that won't let me vote, and it was easier to be grateful than curious."

"That's not—"

"Don't interrupt a confession; it's rude." Her mouth quirked, briefly, and settled. "Here's what matters to me, and I'll say it plainly because I haven't the patience for anything else. Whatever that man is hunting — whoever wrote to you in November, whatever promise or trouble you're carrying around like a hot coal — I don't want it served to me by the constable on my doorstep. If there's a thing I ought to know about what happens under my roof, I'd rather hear it from you, badly told, than from him, well told. Do you understand the difference?"

Rafael understood it perfectly. It was the difference between a door opened from inside and one broken down, and no one had ever offered Rafael the former — not once, not ever, in a life spent passing through other people's houses by the tradesman's entrance of their trust.

"I understand," Rafael said carefully, "that you're offering."

"I'm offering," Edith agreed, "and I'm not taking the answer today. Today you'll drive me to the chapel at two, and you'll stand at the back looking disapproving, which you do beautifully, and we'll say nothing further. But Underhill—" She rose, gathering her shawl, and paused at the study door, and did not look back, which made it easier for both of them, and was itself a kindness of a very Edith kind. "When you're ready to tell me, remember that I have spent my whole life listening to men explain that inconvenient truths are best kept from women for their own protection. I have developed opinions on the subject."

She went in and closed the door, and Rafael sat alone in the hall with the mirror above the bench showing a figure that looked, in the dull silver, remarkably composed for someone whose foundations had just shifted.

Exposure, Rafael had always imagined, would arrive like the ice breaking — one great crack, then the plunge. Instead it had come by inches, a constable's finger on a ledger line, an employer's new eyes, the slow discovery that invisibility was a garment with holes in it that winter found easily. The chill of it settled now into the bones and stayed.

And beneath the chill, colder and clearer, the arithmetic resumed itself, because there was nothing else to do with a mind at four in the morning or in a hall at noon. Brine had no proof — only a line in a ledger and a word that had slipped. But Brine had the mail, and the lanes, and the ice when it bore, and Rafael had a man hidden in the next valley with the thaw coming and a promise outstanding, and between those two inventories the winter stretched, white and watchful, longer than it had ever been.

At two o'clock, precisely, Rafael brought the car to the door. Edith came out with her satchel of pamphlets and her chin set at the angle that meant the chapel would regret hosting her, and as she passed she laid one gloved hand a moment on Rafael's sleeve — the briefest pressure, asking nothing, promising nothing, changing everything.

"Thursday," she said, as though resuming an old conversation. "We'll see who passes the door."

Rafael drove her down the white lane toward the village, toward the chapel, toward the watching constable and the island's narrowing shores, and tried not to feel how completely the season had turned against them — or how little, all things considered, they minded being seen.
