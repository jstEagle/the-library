## 10. Edith's Question

Edith Marlowe had spent forty-seven years learning to read people the way she read parliamentary reports — closely, skeptically, and with an eye for the sentence that didn't belong. It was a skill her father had called impertinence, her late husband had called intuition, and the newspapers, when they bothered to notice her at all, had called shrillness. She knew it was none of those things. It was attention. Attention was the whole of her method: attention to the woman in the third row of a meeting who hadn't clapped, attention to the alderman whose hands went still on his papers when she mentioned the wages bill, attention to what was *not* said.

And for three days now, Rafael Underhill had not said a great many things.

They drove her as competently as ever — better, if anything, as though competence were a wall being built higher by the hour. They answered questions before she asked them. They kept the motorcar's lamps trimmed and the chains on the wheels and the radiator blanketed against the frost, and they spoke when spoken to in exactly the voice of a person who had nothing whatsoever to hide. Which was precisely the trouble. Edith had employed enough servants in her life to know that no one was uniformly agreeable unless they were working very hard at it. Rafael had been working hard at it since the morning Constable Brine's boots had come up the drive.

She watched them now across the length of her study, where they stood sorting the week's mail into her trays — correspondence, bills, the movement's circulars, the pitiful trickle the twice-weekly post still managed to bring up the frozen valley. Their hands moved with the economy of someone who had done this ten thousand times. The lamplight caught the edge of their jaw, the small scar above the left eyebrow that Edith had never asked about. Ten years in her service, and she could not have said where that scar came from. The thought arrived with an unfamiliar sting. She knew the birthdays of her gardener's children. She did not know whether Rafael Underhill had anyone at all.

"Mrs. Marlowe." Rafael held up an envelope, its corner creased from travel. "This one's been opened. Not badly — steamed, I'd say, and resealed. Do you want it logged?"

Edith set down her pen. "Who from?"

"The county federation. Subscription renewal." A pause so slight it might have been nothing. "Brine's work, most likely."

"You think he opens my mail."

"I think," Rafael said, sliding the envelope into the correspondence tray with perfect neutrality, "that a man with more ambition than evidence tends to start with envelopes because they can't answer back."

It was exactly the sort of dry observation Edith privately relished, and it annoyed her profoundly, because it was also a deflection. She let the silence stretch until Rafael glanced up — met her eyes, held them a half-second too long, and looked back down at the letters as if they were suddenly fascinating.

"Rafael," Edith said. "Sit down, please."

"I've the post to finish, ma'am."

"The post will keep. It has kept all winter; it can keep another quarter hour." She gestured at the chair opposite the desk, and after a moment — a hesitation she had never once seen in them — Rafael sat. Perched, rather, on the front third of it, spine straight, hands folded, a servant summoned for instructions.

Edith came around the desk and did not sit behind it. That was a magistrate's arrangement, a schoolroom's, and she wanted neither. She leaned against the desk's near corner instead, arms folded, and studied the top of Rafael's head until they lifted their chin with something very like defiance.

"I'm going to ask you a question," Edith said, "and I want you to understand before I ask it that I am not asking as your employer."

"No, ma'am?"

"I am asking as a woman who has spent twenty years being told to mind her own business by men who were minding everyone else's, and who has decided she will not do the same to you." She let that sit. "Who are you, Rafael? Truly."

The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere upstairs Marta was beating a rug with methodical violence. Rafael's face did the thing that faces did when they were deciding — a flicker of calculation behind the eyes, quick as a card sharp's shuffle — and then, remarkably, the calculation stopped. Something underneath it surfaced, tired and old and careful.

"You noticed the letter," Rafael said.

"I notice everything. It's my only talent." Edith tilted her head. "The letter from the postal ledger. The one Brine waved about this morning like a trout he'd caught. You told him it was a misdelivered circular."

"It was a reasonable explanation."

"It was a *performance*. And a good one — he swallowed it the way a pike swallows a spinner, all appetite and no examination. But you looked, afterward, as though you'd swallowed something yourself." Edith unfolded her arms. "I am not going to hand you over to that man. I want that understood first, and then I want the truth, or as much of it as you choose to give me. I'll take partial. I've built a movement on partial truths dragged into daylight one inch at a time."

For a long moment Rafael said nothing at all. Then they rose, crossed to the window, and checked the drive — a habit, Edith realized, not a nervous tic. Checking had simply become the shape of their life.

* * *

"When I was fourteen," Rafael said, still facing the window, "I couldn't read."

Edith kept her voice level. "And now you sort my correspondence faster than my last two secretaries combined."

"Now I read everything. Newspapers, contracts, other people's letters." A ghost of movement at the corner of their mouth — not quite a smile. "That last is the part you're circling, ma'am, so let me spare us both the orbit. Yes. I read your letters. All of them, these ten years, before they reach you. Not from malice. From hunger, at first — a girl who taught herself her letters off shop signs and hymn books doesn't stop being hungry just because she's got a position — and then from habit, and then—" They stopped. Started again. "Then because some of them needed reading. There was a letter two years ago from your cousin Harold that I burned unopened because I could feel through the paper what it was, and you were preparing for the Bath rally, and it would have taken the legs out from under you for a fortnight."

Edith's breath caught. Harold. The loan. She had learned of it eventually, from a solicitor's follow-up, and had wondered at the strange mercy of never seeing the first cruel draft. "You decided for me."

"I did. It was presumptuous and I'd likely do it again." Rafael turned from the window at last. "That's the smallest of it. You asked who I am. Here is who I am: nobody, born nowhere, on documents that say otherwise. My mother was a scullery maid who died of the fever when I was eleven, and after that I made myself up out of whatever was lying about — a name here, a reference there, a parish that didn't look too hard at a quiet child who worked hard and asked nothing. I taught myself to write a fair hand copying funeral notices. I taught myself figures keeping a publican's books under a name that wasn't mine. Everything I am, Mrs. Marlowe, is either self-invented or borrowed, and none of it would survive ten minutes of a determined inquiry." Their voice stayed even, but Edith heard the wire pulled taut beneath it. "Until this winter, nobody determined had any reason to inquire."

"And now there's a letter in the ledger," Edith said quietly. "In your name. R. Underhill."

Something moved through Rafael's face then — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Grief wearing fear's coat.

"Yes," they said.

"From whom?"

Rafael stood very still. Edith watched them weigh it, watched the scales working behind their eyes, and understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that whatever this was, it was not embarrassment over a false résumé. A forged past could be survived. This was something loved.

"You don't have to tell me," she said — and found, saying it, that she meant it and didn't mean it, both at once, which was the condition of her entire life lately.

"If I don't tell you," Rafael said, "you'll spend the night constructing it anyway, and you'll construct it wrong, and you'll be careful with me in ways that will make the household whisper." A breath. "Anton Varga. He's an engineer. We met five summers ago — he was home from abroad, visiting the lakes, and we had six weeks of talking until dawn and I have never—"

The sentence stopped. Rafael looked at the fire instead of at her, and Edith, who had buried a husband and grieved a love or two besides, recognized the grammar of the unfinished sentence perfectly.

"He wrote to me," Rafael went on, steadier. "Because he had no one else. He's been dismissed from his works — his own works, the ones he designed the machinery for — because of where he was born. They've declared him an enemy alien, Mrs. Marlowe. Anton. The man once walked four miles through rain to return a farmer's lost tools because leaving them in a field offended his idea of order. He's hiding in the next valley with no work, no papers worth having, and every neighbor he's known for fifteen years looking at him as though the Kaiser were feeding him at table." Their jaw tightened. "A border moved. That's all. Politicians drew a line on a map in a room he's never seen, and overnight his whole life became evidence against him."

The fire settled. Edith found she had come off the desk without noticing, drawn a step closer, her hands clasped hard enough to whiten the knuckles.

"You answered the letter," she said. It was not a question.

"I burned it and answered it," Rafael said. "Through channels I won't describe, because if you don't know, you can't be accused of knowing. But yes. Years ago — that summer at the lakes — we made each other a promise. If either of us ever needed help and asked for it, the other would answer. No questions." A pause. "He's asked. So I'm answering. That's the whole of it, ma'am, and I know how it looks, and I know what Brine would do with it if he had it, and I'm telling you anyway because you asked me plainly and because—" Here Rafael finally faltered, just slightly, the composure shifting like ice underfoot. "Because you fight for women who are legally invisible, and I have been legally invisible my entire life, and I thought perhaps you of all people would understand that a person can be entirely loyal to a country that has never once written their name down anywhere except its ledgers of suspicion."

* * *

Edith turned away and walked to the window, needing the motion, needing to look at something that asked nothing of her.

Below, the valley lay white and silent under its rime, the river a dull pewter seam stitched between the hills, the closed bridge a gray skeleton at its narrowest point. An island, the cook called it, mournfully, every time the post failed to come. Trapped. Edith had laughed at the word a month ago. Now she stood looking out at the frozen fields and understood that she had been living in a sealed room all along and calling it a view.

Her whole life, she had trusted documents. Papers were her weapons: petitions with ten thousand signatures, pamphlets, the legal arguments she paid barristers to sharpen. Paper was how the powerless proved they existed. And here was Rafael — ten years of flawless service, a person she had recommended to two other households without hesitation — admitting that every paper bearing their name was fiction, and asking her to believe that the truth underneath was worth more than the forgery.

It should have been difficult. It was astonishingly easy. That was what frightened her.

Because the arithmetic did not stop at Rafael. If she sheltered this — if she let it pass — then she was doing precisely what she had spent twenty years condemning: deciding which people deserved the law's protection based on whether she liked them. Principle did not bend to affection. Principle was the thing you kept when affection ran out. And yet. And yet Anton Varga, an enemy alien on paper and a returning lost tools in fact. And yet Rafael, standing in her study with their whole invented self laid open, waiting to be judged.

There was also, and she was honest enough to face it, the cold practical voice — the movement's voice, the treasurer's voice. A scandal would cost them everything. Branches would close. The tour, the spring campaign, the slow respectable accumulation of allies among people who supported votes for women so long as no one inconvenient actually got hurt. One harbored foreigner discovered in her stable loft and forty years of respectability would burn faster than a pamphlet in a constable's grate.

But if she turned Rafael out — if she so much as hinted to Brine — she would be announcing to herself, forever, that all of it had been performance. The speeches, the platform, the banner. Justice in the abstract, revoked at the first knock on her own door.

"Ma'am." Rafael's voice behind her, carefully empty. "I can see this puts you in an impossible position. If you want my resignation, I'll write it tonight and be gone before the post comes. You'll never have to explain me."

Edith turned around.

"Sit down, Rafael," she said, "before you fall down. You've been holding yourself like a fence post for three days and it's exhausting to watch." She waited until they obeyed — really sat this time, back against the chair, as if the permission had removed a prop. "I'm not accepting a resignation I intend to refuse. Hear me clearly, because I'll only say this once, and then we will never speak of it again unless it becomes necessary."

Rafael looked at her.

"I don't approve of what you're doing," Edith said. "I don't know the whole of it and I don't want to know, and I want you to understand that my ignorance is a decision, not an oversight. What I do know is this: there is a man in the next valley who committed no crime except being born on the wrong side of a line some chancellors moved for their convenience, and this country is currently treating him as guilty of everything. I have spent my life arguing that a person's rights must not depend on the accident of their classification. It would be obscene of me to make an exception the moment the classification becomes *enemy* instead of *female*." She drew a breath, and the words came out quieter than she intended. "So I know nothing. But if a person were to need warm meals, and a roof that doesn't leak, and a household that asks no questions — well. This house has fed stranger causes than yours."

The silence that followed was the longest of the winter. Rafael's eyes were bright and wet and they did not permit a single tear to fall, and Edith respected them enormously for it, and respected them almost more when their voice came out rough anyway.

"Thank you," they said. Inadequate words. They both knew it.

"Don't thank me. Thank me by being careful." Edith returned to her desk, took up her pen, and found her hand steady enough to use it. "And Rafael — the constable will be back. Men like Brine always come back; boredom is patient. Whatever channels you're using, use them cleaner. And whatever comes next—" She paused over the blotter, not looking up. "Whatever comes next, you will not face it alone in this house. Is that understood?"

"Yes, ma'am," Rafael said softly.

Edith bent to her correspondence. Behind her, the door opened and closed with its usual quiet click, and the house resumed its ordinary sounds, and she stared at the subscription renewal from the county federation — steamed open, resealed, handled by ambitious little men — and thought about borders, and lines, and all the maps drawn by people who had never once stood on the ground they divided.

She had told Rafael the truth: she wanted to know nothing. But lying awake that night, listening to the wind come down the valley from the direction of the bridge, Edith Marlowe admitted to herself that knowledge was already finding her whether she willed it or not — because that afternoon, while Rafael saw to the motorcar, she had gone out to the river path for her constitutional and seen, far across the frozen fields, the Quist girl standing on the landing stage, staring up at the Marlowe laundry line with the fixed attention of a telegraph operator reading signals.

And the shirts, she had noted idly at the time, hung in a pattern that made no earthly sense for drying.
