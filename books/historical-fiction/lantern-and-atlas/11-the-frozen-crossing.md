## 11. The Frozen Crossing

The word came on a Tuesday, in a way that looked like nothing at all.

Rafael had gone down to the Quist house with a parcel of mending for Mrs. Quist's rheumatic sister — an errand Edith had invented without knowing she was inventing it, one of the small mercies of a busy household — and Nell had met them at the gate, cheeks whipped red by the wind, hands jammed in the pockets of a coat two winters outgrown.

"Your lantern," Nell said, not looking at them, looking instead at the river as though it were the river that interested her. "Third hook from the left. You hung it wrong last night."

"I hung it exactly right."

"Well." Nell picked at a splinter of fence rail. "There's a man who'd say you hung it wrong. He says the glass is dirty and needs washing soon. Before month's end, he says, or there won't be any point hanging it at all."

The parcel went suddenly heavy in Rafael's hands. *Before month's end.* Nine days.

"Tell your father," Rafael said, "that I'll bring the vinegar myself."

Nell nodded once, sharp as a bird taking a crumb, and turned back to the frozen garden as if the conversation had never happened. It hadn't, in any way that could be proved. That was the whole art of it now: conversations that had never happened, letters that had never existed, a man who had never been anything but a smudge of lantern light on the far shore where the ferry sat up on blocks for winter.

Rafael walked home along the towpath with the wind pushing at their back and their heart going like a trapped bird against the cage of their ribs.

*He must move.* That was what the message meant, unwrapped from its laundry-line language. Anton had sent it through whatever chain he'd stitched together in the next valley — a peddler, a cousin of a cousin, some thread of the vast invisible web that people like him spun because they were no longer permitted roads or railways or names in ledgers. And the meaning beneath the meaning was plain enough that Rafael didn't need it said aloud: *they are close to me. The searching has come near.*

Nine days. And between Anton and safety lay half a mile of river that pretended, in this hard cold, to be land.

* * *

They went out at midnight three nights later, Rafael and Nell, and Rafael would carry the sound of that night for the rest of their life: the particular silence of a valley under snow, so complete that their own pulse seemed an intrusion, and beneath everything, if you stopped walking and listened, the river talking to itself under the ice.

Not a sound a person could ignore, once they'd learned to hear it. A long slow groan, then a tick, then a hiss like something breathing in its sleep. Half a mile of water moving beneath what only looked like ground.

"You're sure about the moon," Rafael whispered.

"It sets past two. We've got till then." Nell walked ahead with the certainty of someone who had grown up on this bank, who knew the river the way other children knew a grandmother's temper. She carried a hazel stick and used it like a blind woman's cane, tapping ahead of every step. "We go where the ice is white. Never grey. Grey means it's thinned over current. And never, never where you see the dark feathering — that's water sitting on top."

"And where does the white lead?"

"Straight across, if we're lucky. My da says the channel shifts every year. Some years the deep current runs under the boathouse and you couldn't pay him to cross. This year—" She tapped, listened, tapped again. "This year he thinks it runs closer to the willow bar. Which is why we're testing the middle and not the edges."

They started from the slipway below the boathouse, where the shallows froze earliest and thickest, and stepped off the land onto the river.

It held. Of course it held — six weeks of the hardest frost anyone could remember had made the millpond solid enough for skaters and the slower reaches nearly as firm. But *nearly* was the word that lived in Rafael's chest like a splinter, and every step forward drove it deeper. The ice spoke under their boots. Not cracking, exactly. Settling. A sound like a great door easing in its frame, and every time it came Rafael's stomach dropped through the floor of the world and hung somewhere over black running water.

Nell didn't flinch. Rafael watched her small figure ahead, hood up, stick tap-tap-tapping, and understood that fear was a thing you could carry without showing, the same as you carried a letter unopened or a name you never said. The girl was fourteen and had more composure than most magistrates.

Halfway across, the ice changed color under them.

It happened between one step and the next — white going to a pale bruised grey, the snow cover thinning as though the river had exhaled upward through it. Nell stopped so abruptly that Rafael nearly walked into her.

"Don't move," she breathed.

They stood still. Beneath their feet, faintly, came the sound — the true sound this time, not settling but *working*, a thin creak that traveled outward from Nell's boot heels like ripples made of noise.

"The feathering," Rafael murmured. "Is that—"

"No. Look." Nell pointed with her stick. Off to the left, maybe thirty yards, the surface showed a pattern like fern leaves drawn in ink, black veins spidering under milky glass. "That's what we don't touch. We've hit the seam where the current comes close. We go around it — slow, weight low, like this." She sank into a crouch and began to shuffle sideways, spreading herself wide, a strange crablike progress that Rafael copied, feeling absurd and terrified and grateful all at once. Low to the ice, arms out, each foot placed like a word chosen carefully in a letter that might be read by the wrong eyes.

Twenty feet of grey. It took a hundred years, or four minutes; Rafael lost track of time entirely, aware only of the moon sliding lower behind the western ridge, of Nell's breath smoking in short quick puffs, of the river's voice rising just slightly, as if amused.

Then white again. Firm again. The far bank rose ahead, dark willows fringed with frost, and beyond them the fields of the next valley where a man was hiding in somebody's hay barn, listening for dogs and boots.

Nell stood upright, and let out a breath that shook only a little.

"There," she said. "You can tell him. White from the slipway, round the seam at the willow bar, white again to the far side. But—" and here she turned, and in the blue darkness her face was older than fourteen, much older, old as the river itself — "only until the thaw starts. Da says the frost breaks in the first warm spell after the new year, and when it goes, it goes fast. Ice you could drive a cart on Monday will be lace by Thursday. So it's month's end or never."

"Month's end," Rafael repeated. It sounded impossible. It sounded like the only possible thing.

They stood a moment longer on the enemy's shore, two figures on a frozen river at midnight, conspirators by the simple act of standing there. Then Nell touched Rafael's sleeve.

"We should go back while the moon's still useful. And Mr. Underhill?" She almost never called them that. "When you bring him — don't stop in the middle. Whatever you hear. The ice talks terrible but mostly it's lying. Only stop if I stop."

"When did you get to be the expert on everything?"

"Born on it," Nell said, and grinned, sudden and bright, and for a second she was a child again, and Rafael loved her fiercely, hopelessly, the way you love people you have no right to endanger. Which, they realized, walking back across the river in the failing moonlight, was precisely what they were doing. Had been doing since the first lantern went crooked on its hook.

* * *

The arithmetic kept Rafael awake, as it had every night since the letter burned. They had become good at arithmetic these past weeks — risk summed in columns, probabilities carried over. If Anton crossed at month's end and was not seen: one family sheltering a stranger indefinitely, in a valley where Constable Brine had begun keeping his little book of foreign names. If Anton crossed and was seen at a distance: a story, a rumor, perhaps survivable. If Anton fell through: that column Rafael refused to enter in the ledger, though it wrote itself anyway at three in the morning.

And if Anton did not cross — if they waited past month's end, past the frost, past the widening of the search — then the promise made in sunlight five years ago at the lake became a lie, and Rafael would have to go on being the person who let it be a lie, forever, in the mirror, in margins of other people's letters.

There was no invisible way back. Rafael understood that now, lying in the dark above the stables, listening to the wind test the shutters. Invisibility had been their whole craft — the servant who saw everything and was seen as furniture, the reader of letters who left no fingerprints. But the moment Nell had strung her first signal shirt on the line, the moment Rafael had hung a lantern crooked on purpose, the moment they had walked out onto the river tonight and learned its one safe road — the craft had broken. They had put their weight on the world, and the world had creaked, and now the world knew where they stood.

Strangely, that was not entirely terror. Underneath the fear, cold and clean as the river under its ice, ran something steadier. Purpose has that texture, Rafael thought. Terror is a flood; purpose is the same water, harnessed, turning a wheel.

Month's end. Nine days minus three. Six days, now, to arrange everything: a night Edith was safely occupied — though how much longer Edith could be counted as uninvolved, Rafael no longer knew; she had looked at them differently since the business with Brine and the ledger, a look with questions folded inside it. A cart or no cart. Blankets, brandy, dry clothes waiting in the boathouse. A story ready in case of lanterns on the bank. And Nell at the middle of the river, small and steady as a keel, saying *don't stop unless I stop.*

Six days also to do the ordinary work of being R. Underhill, driver and secretary and nobody: brush the motor, sort the post, sit at the kitchen table among people who trusted them, and smile.

That was the part that had begun to hurt. Rafael had spent years perfecting the art of belonging nowhere, and now, too late, they belonged — to a girl with a hazel stick, to a cook who saved them the ends of the lemon cake, to a household whose cause they had caught like a fever, to a woman upstairs whose faith in law and fairness was about to collide with the lawless thing sleeping in Rafael's chest. Every secret is a debt, Anton had told them once, laughing at the lake. *And debts come due in winter.*

Downstairs, early, Rafael found the kitchen already warm and Marta scrubbing potatoes with unusual violence, her mouth set in a line that meant trouble at the shop again. Cook was muttering over the paper. Somewhere above, Edith's typewriter was already going, hammering away at the tour that couldn't happen, the meetings that could, the war of argument she intended to wage on a valley that was beginning, Rafael feared, to fight back with uglier weapons than argument.

"Morning, Mr. Underhill," Marta said, not quite kindly, and Rafael heard underneath it the question the whole valley was asking itself now of everyone it met: *which side are you on, and how would I know?*

"Morning, Marta," Rafael said, and reached for the kettle, and performed the ordinary morning of an ordinary person, while six days away a river waited with its one white road across its black heart, and a man with a familiar handwriting crouched in a barn counting the same six days backward toward ruin.

Outside, the frost had written its own message on the window: whole ferns of it, feathered and fine, beautiful and doomed. Rafael pressed one gloved finger against the coldest pane and watched a single clear drop form, run, and fall — the first small defeat of winter, or the first warning of spring, depending entirely on which side of the ice you were standing on.

Six days. There was so much to do, and only one of them could afford to fail at any of it.
