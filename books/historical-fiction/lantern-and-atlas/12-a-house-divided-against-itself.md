## 12. A House Divided Against Itself

Marta Hessel had been peeling potatoes for the Marlowe household for three years, and she had learned that a kitchen told you everything a great house tried to hide. The dining room showed you the family the Marlowes wished to be; the drawing room showed you the family they performed for company. But the kitchen — the kitchen showed you the truth, steaming and salted, before it ever went upstairs on a covered dish.

The truth that November morning was that Mrs. Beckett, the cook, had stopped humming.

For three years Marta had worked beside the woman's humming — hymns mostly, sometimes music-hall songs she pretended to disapprove of — and now the only sound was the knife against the board, and beneath it the wireless-less silence of a valley sealed off by snow, waiting for a bridge that would not open until spring.

"You've heard, then," Marta said.

"Heard what?" Mrs. Beckett did not look up. Her knife kept its rhythm. "That we're an island until April? That the post comes Tuesdays and Fridays if the roads allow? I'd heard."

"About Mr. Prewitt's shop."

The knife paused — half a beat, no more — and resumed.

"What about it," said Mrs. Beckett, in the voice of a woman who already knew and wanted very much not to discuss it, which was, Marta had learned, the same voice the cook used for dead birds found in the larder and other facts that could not be helped.

"Nell Quist told me at the gate this morning. He's put a card in the window. *No orders taken from persons of foreign origin.* His own words. Nell read it out to me twice because I didn't want to believe her the first time."

Mrs. Beckett set down her knife at last and looked up. She was a broad woman with forearms like a blacksmith and a face that gave away nothing she did not choose to give away, and just now she chose to give away worry.

"That's Prewitt's business," she said. "Prewitt's ruin, more like, when folk see how he carries on. You'll still go, of course."

"I go every Thursday."

"And are you going to stand in his queue and let him look at you and say nothing?"

Marta looked down at her hands, which had gone pale around the peeler. "My name is Hessel," she said. "My mother came from Bremen when she was nineteen. My father's English as Sunday bread. What would he see, Mrs. Beckett? What does he think he sees?"

"He thinks he sees the war," Mrs. Beckett said quietly. "That's all any of them see now. It doesn't matter what's actually in front of them." She took up the knife again. "Go on your Thursday. Hold your head up. And if he says anything to you, you come home and tell me, and I'll speak to Mrs. Marlowe, who will make his ears ring for a week. She's done it to better men than Prewitt."

It was a kindness, and Marta took it like one, folding it away. But kindness was not the same as safety, and both of them knew it.

* * *

The argument began at supper — staff supper, half past eight, the long deal table scrubbed yellow by generations of elbows — and it began, as most arguments in that house began, with Tom the gardener saying something he should have kept behind his teeth.

"All I'm saying," Tom said, spearing a potato, "is that you can't blame people for being careful. There's a war on. My brother's been in the trenches since September, and he writes me it's not like the papers say. It's worse. So if a man wants to be careful about who he sells his bread to—"

"A man," said Nell Quist, who had delivered the evening post and stayed, as she always contrived to stay, on the excuse of warming herself, "wants to be careful about whose money he takes, is what you mean. So long as it spends the same."

"I didn't ask your opinion, girl."

"No," Nell agreed cheerfully. She was fourteen and built out of angles and impudence, the ferryman's daughter with river ice in her veins instead of blood, and she was sitting at the edge of the staff table with her boots dangling because nobody had ever successfully made her leave. "You never do. That's why I give it free."

"Nell," Mrs. Beckett said, in warning.

"Well, but it's daft, isn't it?" Nell leaned forward. "Half this valley is foreign-born if you go back far enough. Old Man Hedley's grandmother was Dutch. The Thorpes came over from France two generations back — that's why they spell their name with a *p*. Is Mr. Prewitt going to put cards in the window against all of them? Where does he stop?"

"He stops where the government tells him to stop," Tom said. "There's registers now. There's orders in council. Germans are enemy aliens, that's the law of it, and my brother is getting shelled for our side, so I don't see—"

"Marta's mother was German," Nell said, and there was a silence like a dropped glass.

Marta kept her eyes on her plate. She had known it would come to this eventually; she had felt the whole autumn gathering toward it the way the sky gathers toward thunder, in every conversation about the war that stopped when she entered the room, in Mrs. Beckett's new habit of reading the newspaper aloud and then putting it face-down.

"She's dead," Marta said at last, to her plate. "My mother. Eleven years. She's buried in the churchyard at St. Aidan's under a stone with a German word cut into it because my father wanted her own prayer on it, in her own tongue, and the vicar allowed it then." She looked up. "Is she careful about too, Tom? Should we turn her out of consecrated ground while we're at it?"

Tom went red from collar to hairline. "That's not — I never meant—"

"You meant it generally," Marta said. "Everyone means it generally. It's only when it lands on someone that anyone notices it landed."

"It's not the same," Tom insisted, but his voice had lost its footing. "You're not the same. Your father's English. You were born here. Nobody's saying anything about you."

"Nobody's saying anything about me," Marta repeated. "No. They're saying it about persons of foreign origin, in Mr. Prewitt's window, and about enemy aliens in the paper, and about *them* at the market, and everybody knows exactly who they mean and it's never anybody standing right in front of them. Until it is."

She had not meant to raise her voice. She heard it crack on the last word and hated herself for it, and then decided, sitting there with her fists in her apron, that she would not hate herself for it after all. Some things deserved a cracked voice.

Across the table, Rafael Underhill sat very still.

They were all used to Rafael's stillness — the driver had a way of being present without taking up space, quiet as furniture, always at the edge of things with an eye on everything — but Marta had lately begun to suspect that the stillness was not emptiness. It was attention. Rafael listened the way other people spoke: completely, giving the whole of themselves to it. Tonight that gaze moved from Marta to Tom to Nell and back again, unhurried, and Marta had the sudden uncanny feeling of being understood from the inside — of having her anger received somewhere and weighed and found reasonable.

"There's a difference," Tom was saying, weaker now, "between a person and a government. Nobody sensible holds a person responsible for a government."

"Do you know," Nell said, "I think that's the first sensible thing you've said all winter, Tom Garrow, and you said it by accident."

"I'll thank you to—"

"And yet Mr. Prewitt's holding every person responsible. Every one with a foreign grandmother or a foreign name or a foreign way of crossing themselves. It's not governments queuing at his shop. It's women. With baskets. With children hanging off them. Mrs. Aldous couldn't get served Tuesday — she's lived here fifty years, her husband drove the mill wheel — and she stood in that queue and he looked through her like glass and served the woman behind her. Fifty years, Tom. How many years makes a person yours?"

Nobody answered that. The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere overhead the house creaked, settling its old bones against the cold, and Marta thought: upstairs Mrs. Marlowe is writing speeches about justice with a fountain pen, and down here we're doing the same work with potato peelings and raised voices, and neither of us can reach the other.

Except — she glanced at Rafael — perhaps one of us can.

* * *

The trouble with Marta's cousin Josef was not that he had done anything. It was that he existed, loudly, in a body born in Baden, and that he had made the mistake, on the second Saturday of December, of walking past the George and Dragon at closing time.

He came to the kitchen door two days later with a split lip and a story he told badly, in his broken English, with his cap wrung in his hands — Josef, who had laid stone walls in this valley for nine years, who had carried Marta on his shoulders at the fair when she was six, who sent half his wages to their aunt in the old country and was proud of it.

"Some men," he said. "From the public house. They asked was I registered. I said yes, yes, I have registered at the constabulary, it is done, I have the paper. They said let us see the paper. I reached in my coat—" He demonstrated reaching into his coat, and his hand shook doing it, and Marta understood suddenly what the reaching must have looked like to men full of beer and war news. "Then there was pushing. Then—" He touched his lip, as if surprised to find it still hurt. "A man said, *go home.* And I stood in the road and I thought, but I am home. This is eleven years of home. And I did not say it, because my English goes small when I am frightened, so I said nothing, and they laughed at me for saying nothing."

Mrs. Beckett fed him tea and bread and dripping without a word, the way she fed everyone, and Marta sat across from her cousin and felt something harden in her chest into a shape she recognized from history books and church sermons and had never expected to feel from the inside.

Rage. Cold, patient, documented rage.

"Constable Brine," Josef said. "He came to my lodgings Monday. Asking questions. Who visits me. Where I walk. He wrote it all in a little book." Josef smiled, and it was the saddest thing Marta had seen all year. "I thought, my book will be very short. I walk to work. I walk to church. I eat."

"He's got a little book for everyone now," Nell said darkly, from the corner where she'd installed herself. "Or wishes he did."

"You hush," said Mrs. Beckett.

But Josef's visit changed the temperature of the kitchen, the way a door left open changes it. Afterward nobody argued anymore — not really. Tom, to his credit, came round within a week, showing up at the scullery door with a sack of his own potatoes, grown himself, muttering that Prewitt could keep his flour, that his mother's people had come from Ireland anyway and wasn't that foreign enough for anybody. Mrs. Beckett started sending food out the back door in covered baskets and forbade all questions about where it went. Even the housemaid Elsie, who feared her own shadow, took to walking partway home with Marta on her evenings out, saying it was on her way, which it was not.

The valley was dividing itself, street by street and table by table, into the kind of people who asked questions and the kind who answered them — and everyone, Marta saw, was choosing sides whether they meant to or not, by silence, by baskets, by where they walked and with whom. It was the war itself, in miniature: the same lines drawn, the same terrible arithmetic of who counts as us. Only here the trenches ran through queues at the grocer's and along the frozen lane to church, and the ammunition was talk.

And through all of it, Rafael watched, and listened, and said almost nothing — except once.

It was near midnight, a week before Christmas, and Marta came down for a hot brick for her feet and found Rafael alone at the kitchen table with a single candle and a letter, and Rafael did not startle, did not hide it — only sat looking at the fire with the letter face-down under one hand, and said, softly, as if continuing a conversation they'd been having for years:

"Do you know what I can't stop thinking about, Marta? That everyone in this valley is living two lives just now. Every single one. What they say and what they mean. Who they are and who it's safe to be. Even Brine, I expect." A pause. The fire settled. "You'd think that would separate people. Mostly it does the opposite, if you know how to look. Two lives recognize each other."

Marta stood in the doorway with her brick cooling in her hands and understood that she had just been trusted with something enormous and unnamed, and that Rafael was lonelier than any person she had ever met, and braver, and that these two facts were the same fact.

"Whose letter is it?" she asked.

Rafael smiled — the rare one, the real one, gone as fast as it came. "Someone I made a promise to," they said, "a long time ago, in sunlight. And I'm afraid the bill for it is coming due."

Two nights later, Nell Quist appeared at the kitchen door past dark, cheeks scarlet with cold, breathless in a way that had nothing to do with running — though she had run — and said, low, to the room in general and Rafael in particular:

"Lantern signal from across the water. Three flashes, then two. It's time."
