## 13. The Night of the Lanterns

The church social began at seven, as church socials always did, with the certainty of tide. By half past, lanterns had been strung along the vicarage fence in a wavering line of yellow — forty of them at least, glass chimneys glowing through the early dark, and more carried about the green on poles by children who ran shrieking between the tombola stall and the cocoa table as though the war were a rumor from another county entirely.

Rafael stood at the edge of the green in their good coat and watched the light.

It was Edith's idea, or half of it was. She did not know what the lanterns would be for — could not know, though Rafael suspected she guessed at the shape of it the way one guesses at a shape behind frosted glass. When Rafael had mentioned that a demonstration might be useful, some public gathering to remind the valley that the Marlowe household still held its head up after Constable Brine's searches, Edith had seized the notion with both hands. *A social,* she'd said. *Nothing political. Nothing he can pin a seditious word to. Lanterns, music, Mrs. Aldous's choir. Let him watch us sing.* And then she had looked at Rafael a moment too long, and added, quietly: *And whatever else needs doing on such a crowded, well-lit night — do it.*

So here they all were: the whole valley gathered on the green like moths pinned to a board, every eye fixed on sugar buns and a raffle for a goose, while two men prepared to walk across a frozen river under the constable's nose.

The ice road was three hundred yards of black water pretending to be ground. Nell Quist had walked it twice this week with a hazel pole, sounding it the way her father sounded the ferry channel, and had reported back in laundry-line code — two white sheets and a blue apron, meaning *sound and safe, but thin at the middle* — that the crossing held if one kept to the line of the old ford stakes, where the current slowed beneath the willows. The thaw had begun its slow argument with the winter; the days had turned soft enough that the snow on the banks went rotten by noon and refroze at dusk into a crust that would bear a man's weight only if he moved like a penitent, slowly and spread wide.

Tonight, then. The last cold night before the warm front the ferryman swore was coming down from the west. After tonight there would be no ice, and no Anton either — arrested at dawn wherever he crouched in the next valley over, because Nell's last signal had said plainly: *he must move now.*

Rafael checked their pocket watch by feel. Half past seven. The choir was tuning inside the parish hall; through the open windows came the sound of Mrs. Aldous beating time with a hymnbook and losing the argument. In twenty minutes the whole congregation would be drawn out onto the green for the lantern procession around the boundary of the village — every light in the valley moving together in one slow, glittering circuit, so that anyone watching from the bridge approach would see nothing but a river of flame and faces.

Anyone watching would see exactly what they were meant to see.

Rafael turned and went down through the churchyard, past the leaning stones, out along the towpath where the dark closed over them like water closing over a dropped coin.

* * *

Anton Varga was waiting under the willows at the old ford stakes, and for a moment Rafael could not find him at all — which was itself a kind of answer. Five years ago, Anton had been a man you located by his laugh, a big-shouldered engineer who filled any room he stood in, who had once waded fully clothed into the lake at Midsummer to prove some point about buoyancy and come out crowing like a boy. The man under the willows was a shadow that flinched when a twig cracked.

"Here," Rafael said softly, and saw the shadow resolve into a coat too large for its owner, a face gone sharp at the cheekbones, a beard grown rough and grey-flecked where there had been smooth dark jaw five summers ago. "Anton."

"You came." The voice was hoarse, as though it had not been used much lately, or used mostly for lying low. "I told myself you wouldn't. I made myself believe it, so that—" He stopped. His hands, when they came up out of his pockets, were shaking, and he looked at them as if they belonged to a stranger. "So that it wouldn't kill me when you didn't."

"I said I would."

"Yes." Anton laughed, and it was a terrible small sound. "Yes, you did. You were the only person I ever knew who did exactly what they said. Do you remember—"

"The lake," Rafael said. "You promised to teach me to swim and instead you nearly drowned us both."

"And you never let me forget it." Something of the old warmth moved across the ruined face, brief as a match struck in wind. Then it guttered. He looked back over his shoulder at the far bank, at the black seam of trees beyond the frozen fields, and his voice dropped. "There were men at the works, Rafa. Men I'd drunk beer with for six years. They stopped meeting my eye in September. By November one of them had written my name on a list. A list. My name, spelled wrong, on a list, and that was the end of everything — my lodgings, my wage, my standing in the world — because of a piece of paper signed by men in London who've never heard of the town where I learned my trade." He swallowed. "They took my tools. Did I write that? They took my tools, Rafa. Twenty years of a man's hands, confiscated."

Rafael remembered the letter, read by lamplight and burned page by page in the grate, the ink curling off the paper like something alive dying. *I invoke the promise. I have nowhere else. Forgive me for asking.*

"No questions," Rafael said now, as they'd said it on the lakeshore five years ago. "That was the vow. Come."

They went down onto the ice together.

* * *

The first ten steps were the worst, and then they were simply the first ten steps, and after that there were no steps that were not the worst.

The river underfoot was not white but black, glazed and faintly luminous, and through it — when Rafael dared look down, which was rarely — the slow shapes of weeds bent in the current like drowned dancers. The crust crackled in long sighing reports that traveled away under the surface in both directions, as though the whole river were one vast pane of stressed glass and they were walking on the lid of the world. Nell had taught them the trick of it: weight low, feet apart, the hazel pole carried crosswise so that if the ice gave, its width might catch across the gap like a bridge of one's own. *Don't run even if it sings at you,* the girl had said, fourteen years old and matter-of-fact as a coroner. *Ice singing is just ice talking. It's silence you want to worry about.*

The ice sang the whole way across.

Behind them and above them, on the rise of the green, the lantern procession had begun. Rafael felt it rather than saw it — a brightening of the sky's lower hem, a wash of gold reflected up off the clouds — and then, turning carefully, terribly, on the black glass, they saw it entire: the whole village come out of the hall in a slow ribbon of light, forty lanterns and more swinging on their poles, the choir's hymn carrying thin and strange across the frozen fields, the children's voices rising above the old people's like sparks off a fire. The light moved along the boundary of the village at the pace of a funeral, and every yard of it drew eyes that would otherwise have been scanning the river, and every voice raised in *Abide With Me* was a door slammed in the face of inquiry.

Edith had done this. Edith, who did not know what she was covering, had covered it with everything she had.

"She'll hang for it someday," Anton murmured, watching the lights. "This woman of yours. Mark me. Anyone who stands up in times like these gets hanged eventually — or knighted, if the times change fast enough." He shivered inside the borrowed coat. "Which way?"

"Straight out. Keep to my left shoulder and don't stop for anything, not even if the ice speaks."

The middle of the river found them, as Nell had promised it would, thinner than anywhere else. The singing underfoot changed key — a higher, hollower note, the note of ice stretched near its limit — and Rafael felt the crust flex beneath one boot with a sensation like standing on the skin of a drum. The hazel pole went out ahead, tapping, testing. Ten feet. Fifteen. Under the willows' far shadow the current muttered audibly through a dark seam of open water no wider than a coffin, and they stepped over it as one steps over a sleeping thing, Anton's breath coming fast and ragged, Rafael's own heart hammering so hard it seemed impossible the ice couldn't hear it.

And there — midway, in the exact middle of the black river, with the lantern-light of England glimmering behind and the darkness of the next valley waiting ahead — Rafael understood that they had already crossed.

Not the river. The other thing. There was a line, invisible and absolute, running through the middle of every life, and Rafael had spent thirty years walking along the near side of theirs — reading other people's letters, keeping other people's secrets, belonging everywhere and nowhere, invisible by craft and by choice. All of it had been preparation for this and none of it had been survivable without this: the moment when invisibility ended and the self stepped forward onto cracking ground with no pole long enough and no shore close enough, committed wholly to the far side of whatever waited. Brine's ledger. The hearing that would surely come. The fine, or the prison, or worse. Every step from here was testimony.

*Rafa.* Only Anton called them that anymore. The name hung in the frozen air between the two shores like a lantern of its own.

"We're past the deep channel," Rafael said aloud, because saying it made it true. "The rest holds. Walk."

* * *

The far bank took them in like a mother taking in a prodigal — mud and dead reeds and the boathouse looming black against the slightly less black sky, its door swollen shut with frost until Rafael put a shoulder to it and the wood gave with a groan that seemed loud enough to wake the county.

Inside, the dark smelled of tar and rope and winters. Nell was there already — of course she was, she had rowed the long way round the weir with her father an hour before, and now she stood holding a storm lantern shuttered to a slit, and when she opened it a hand's width her face came up out of the dark, sharp and grinning and absurdly young.

"Told you it'd hold," she whispered.

"You told me it was *thin at the middle,*" Rafael whispered back.

"Well. It was. And you're not at the middle now, are you?" She thrust a bundle at Anton — bread, cheese, a flask of tea sweetened with the cook's rationed sugar, smuggled out under Marta's silent arrangement. "Eat. Dad's got the punt ready at the cut below the mill; we go when the moon's behind the ridge, hour yet. He says to tell you—" she faltered for the first time, suddenly aware of the size of what she was carrying in her mouth, "—he says there's room at his sister's place over the water, and work on the locks come spring, if a man keeps his head down and his hands busy."

Anton sat down heavily on an upturned skiff with the bundle in both hands and looked at it for a long moment without eating, and then he looked at Rafael, and then at Nell, and Rafael watched something move across his ruined face that had not moved across it in months — the expression of a man realizing he has not been abandoned by the world entire.

"A driver," he said unsteadily. "A post-reading, letter-burning, law-breaking chauffeur. And now a child who runs messages across a frozen river and a ferryman who risks his boat." He laughed, wet-eyed, hopeless, grateful. "I asked one person for help. How did I get an army?"

"You didn't ask them," Rafael said. "That's how. People choose. It counts double, choosing."

Outside, faint and far across the fields, the hymn had ended. The lantern procession would be circling back toward the parish hall now, the lights gathering home one by one, the valley folding itself back into ordinary sleep. Somewhere on the bridge approach, Constable Brine was watching the last of it, finding nothing, going to bed unsatisfied. Tomorrow there would be questions. Tomorrow there might be a knock at the Marlowe door, a search, a charge laid against a chauffeur who had lied to an officer of the Crown about a misdelivered circular. Tomorrow the promise would begin collecting its interest.

But tonight there was bread and cheese in a fugitive's hands, and a girl humming tunelessly over a shuttered lantern, and a punt waiting at the mill cut, and the river between them and the middle of it already walked.

Anton ate. Nell kept the watch. Rafael stood at the boathouse door with the cold coming through the boards, looking back across the black ice at the distant glow of the village windows, and counted the hours until dawn — knowing that when dawn came, they would have to walk back across that same thinning ice alone, into the valley, into the light, into whatever Brine had set moving while the lanterns burned.

From the ridge, the moon slipped behind the cloud. Nell touched Rafael's arm.

"Dad says now," she whispered.
