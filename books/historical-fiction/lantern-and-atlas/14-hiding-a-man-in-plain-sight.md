## 14. Hiding a Man in Plain Sight

### Nell

Nell Quist had learned the river before she'd learned her letters, and she trusted it more.

She sat on the boathouse roof with her knees drawn up and her father's field glasses balanced on her knee, watching the white road of the frozen river run from the Marlowe boathouse to the bend at Hollis Point. The ice house stood behind her, half-sunk into the shaded bank like something the estate had tried to forget — which was precisely why Rafael had chosen it. Its door faced away from the house, screened by black pines, and its thick walls of packed sawdust and stone kept out more than heat. They kept out sound. Nell had tested that herself, standing inside with the door shut while Marta dropped a tin pail on the stones outside. She'd heard nothing but her own blood.

From up here she could see everything that mattered: the lane to the village, the bare spine of the ridge where Constable Brine liked to walk his rounds, the smoke rising thin and domestic from the Marlowe kitchen chimney. And she could see the laundry.

The laundry line ran from the Quist porch down to the willow at the water's edge, and this morning her mother had hung, without knowing why, two white sheets and a pillowcase in an order Nell herself had pinned out the night before. Two whites and a pale thing meant *nothing moving on your side.* If Brine came down toward the boathouse, she would hang the red flannel. If she wanted Rafael to come to the ferry landing after dark, the lantern would go in the north window instead of the south.

Her mother thought the arrangement was Nell's own fussy housekeeping. Her father thought nothing at all; he drank his tea, mended his nets, and asked no questions, which was its own kind of knowledge. Nell had decided weeks ago, standing on the ice with Rafael's hand gripping her sleeve, that some kinds of knowing were better kept in the body than spoken aloud. The river knew things it never said.

Below her, the boathouse door opened a hand's width and Rafael's face appeared — just the eyes above a scarf, scanning the bank the way Nell had taught them, left to right, then the ridge, then back. She rapped twice on the roof with her knuckle. *All clear.* The door closed again.

They had worked out the whole grammar of it over three nights in the boathouse, speaking low, their breath fogging between them. Rafael drew the signals in frost on the planking with a fingertip and wiped each one away as soon as Nell had it by heart. Nell had expected to be given small tasks, child's work, and had prepared herself to be insulted about it. Instead Rafael had looked at her across the lantern light with an expression she couldn't name at first, and later understood: it was the look of someone being *relied upon*, perhaps for the first time being the one doing the relying.

"You see what I can't," Rafael had said. "I'm in the house. You're on the water. Whatever you say, I'll believe."

Nobody had ever told Nell Quist that her word would be believed before. She had gone home that night and lain awake under the eaves feeling taller than she was.

Now she swung the field glasses up and swept the ridge out of habit. A figure moved along it — dark coat, unhurried gait, pausing at intervals to look down at the valley. Brine. Her stomach did its cold drop and settled. He walked the ridge most mornings now; it meant nothing new. But she watched him all the same, marking where he stopped, how long he looked at the boathouse shore, whether his head turned toward the pines that hid the ice house door.

He stopped once. Longer than usual. Long enough that Nell counted.

Then he walked on, and the ridge was empty, and Nell slid down off the boathouse roof, dropped the last four feet to the frozen shingle, and went to change the signal — not to red flannel, because he hadn't come down, but to something new she invented on the spot: the pillowcase hung inside out. *He looked. Nothing yet. But he looked.*

Rafael would understand. Rafael understood everything written in cloth these days.

### Marta

Marta carried the bread in the bottom of a basket of mending, under a folded tablecloth, beneath two cold roasted potatoes that were there for appearance's sake.

This was the part she hated — not the danger of it, though her hands sweated against the basket handle every single time, but the *lying*. She had been raised in a household where lying to the mistress of a house was a sin ranked somewhere near theft, and here she was doing it daily, cheerfully, to Mrs. Marlowe's face, who only ever said "thank you, Marta" and returned to her papers. It made Marta feel like a cracked jug carrying water: sound enough until you looked closely at where the seam ran.

"It's the linens from the east rooms," she told Cook, sailing through the kitchen with her basket held at the practiced angle of a woman with nothing in it. "And I've the mending to sort by the laundry stove, if that's all right."

Cook grunted without looking up from her pastry. Whether Cook suspected anything, Marta genuinely could not say. Cook had gone very quiet since the bridge closed, quiet in the way of a woman taking inventory of her household and filing things away. But she also, every second day without fail, set aside the heel ends of the loaves and a crock of dripping, and said only, "For the birds, Marta. The poor birds, starving in this cold." And her eyes, when she said it, stayed fixed on her work with terrible concentration.

The birds got fed well that winter. So did the man in the ice house.

Marta took the outside path, past the walled garden where old Havelock turned the frozen beds, past the glasshouse with its panes furred in frost-ferns. She did not look toward the house. Looking at the house was what a guilty person did. She looked at the mending in her basket, at the gray sky, at her own boots, and she walked the pace of a woman mildly bored.

The pine screen swallowed her, and then she was at the ice house door, and she knocked the little pattern Rafael had taught her — two, pause, three — with one knuckle so softly it was more intention than sound.

The bolt drew back. Warmish air, smelling of straw and lamp oil and something else, something human and close-lived, breathed out at her.

"You're early," Rafael said. "She'll notice you're gone from the linen room."

"I told you, I've got it arranged." Marta slipped inside and set the basket down on the upturned crate that served as a table. "It's Tuesday. Tuesdays the mending takes till the bell. Nobody counts a girl on a Tuesday."

Anton Varga sat where he always sat, on the cot against the sawdust-packed wall, wrapped in a blanket that had once, Marta gathered, belonged to the late Mr. Marlowe. He was thinner than when they'd carried him in ten days ago, gaunt in the way that happened to a man who ate carefully because the food might stop. He rose when she entered, which he didn't need to do, and thanked her, which he also didn't need to do, and both of those not-needing had made her cry once already and she had resolved not to let it happen again.

"There's bread," she said briskly, unpacking. "There's dripping. There's — Rafael, there's a letter."

Rafael went still the way a pointer goes still.

"From my cousin Josef," Marta said. "In Millbrook. He writes me regular." She produced it from her bodice. "Only this time he says the shopkeepers there have put notices in the window. *No foreigners served.* And his landlady gave him notice at Christmas, and he can't get work because his name is on some list now, some registry, and—" Her voice did the thing she hated, wobbling. She pressed on. "He asks could there be work here. In this valley. He asks me to find out if it's safe here."

The silence in the ice house had a texture to it. Straw, lamplight, the faint drip of melt somewhere in the dark inner wall. Anton had stopped moving entirely, and he was looking at the letter in Marta's hand the way a man looks at his own future arriving in someone else's envelope.

"He cannot come here," Anton said quietly. His English was careful and accented and beautiful. "You know that, Marta. One is already too many."

"I know it," Marta said fiercely. "Don't you think I know it? I lie to my mistress's face every day of my life now, and I was raised better, and I'd do worse for family. That's what I'm telling you. That's what I'm telling *both* of you." She rounded on Rafael. "You read everyone's letters. Read mine. Tell me what I write back to him, because I don't know. I don't know what's true anymore except that he's my cousin and he's hungry."

Rafael took the letter. Their eyes moved down it once, twice, and Marta watched them do the thing they did — the reading that wasn't just reading, the weighing of every sentence against every other sentence, against the whole valley, against whatever private atlas Rafael carried behind those steady eyes.

"We tell him the truth," Rafael said at last. "We tell him it isn't safe here. We tell him what we know of Millbrook, and we ask Nell's father whether any boats run west of the locks in thaw." They folded the letter along its crease and handed it back. "We don't promise him safety, Marta. We promise him we'll look. That's all anyone honest can promise."

"That's nothing," Marta said.

"No," said Anton, from the cot, with the ghost of a smile. "That is everything. That is exactly the promise that brought me here."

Marta repacked the basket with the empty crock and the lie about the linens ready on her tongue, and at the door she paused, because Anton had touched her sleeve.

"The birds," he said. "Tell Cook the birds thank her."

Marta went out into the cold fast, before her face could do anything embarrassing, and walked back toward the house rehearsing her Tuesday lies, and finding, to her astonishment, that they came easier than they used to — not because they weighed less, but because she had finally learned what they were buying.

### Havelock

Havelock had gardened at Marlowe's for thirty-one years, through two mistresses, one flood, and the great blizzard of '08, and he had survived all of it on a single principle: a gardener sees everything and remarks on nothing.

He remarked on nothing now, either. He simply adjusted his rounds.

The east path, which passed within thirty feet of the ice house door, had developed an urgent need for gravel in December, and needed it again in January, and Havelock trundled his barrow along it two or three times a week, spreading gravel that required no spreading, pausing to prune the pines — which needed no pruning — and letting his eyes do their slow, patient, professional survey of the ground. Footprints in snow. Cigarette ends (he found none; whoever it was didn't smoke, or was too careful). The particular flattened look of ground that had been walked on often by someone trying not to leave sign.

He'd known within three days. A man doesn't keep bees for forty years without learning to notice traffic around a hive.

He had said nothing to Rafael. He had said nothing to anyone. But he had noticed that the padlock on the ice house, rusted shut since the old mistress died, now opened freely, and he had left a tin of oil beside it one morning without comment. He had noticed the straw in the loft going down faster than one person should use it, and he had ordered a fresh bale from Simmonds' yard and charged it to bed maintenance without a flicker. He had noticed that Underhill now burned the household paper rubbish personally in the small grate rather than sending it out with the bins, and he approved of the thoroughness, having done exactly the same thing himself in the war year for reasons that were nobody's business.

On Thursday afternoon, spreading his phantom gravel, he heard the church bells begin across the valley — the half-hour — and a moment later the ice house door opened in the shadow of the pines and the man came out.

Havelock kept his eyes on his pruning. But he saw him: tall, stooped now, wrapped in a coat too big for him, standing in the strip of weak sun with his face turned up like a plant that had been kept in a cellar. Ten days underground, and the man stood in daylight for four minutes at a stretch, that was the ration, and Havelock timed him without meaning to, out of pure professional habit, and felt something move in his old chest when the man stayed five minutes. Six. Standing in the sun with his eyes shut.

Starving men and sun-starved men, Havelock thought, are the same problem. You feed them slowly and you give them light by degrees.

When the figure slipped back into the dark of the doorway, Havelock straightened up, rubbed his hands, and addressed the nearest pine in a low voice, the way he talked to all growing things.

"Ground's soft under the north side of the ice house, come spring. Drainage wants seeing to." A pause. Snip of the shears. "Man could get a bit of digging done in there, if he took an interest in spade work. Good exercise. Keeps a body warm."

He didn't look around. He didn't need to. He had seen, in the edge of his vision, the door open a crack, and he had chosen, with thirty-one years of professional discipline, to be examining a bud.

He finished his gravel, trundled his barrow back along the east path, and paused once at the corner of the walled garden where he could see, far off, the figure of Constable Brine standing at the top of the ridge road, looking down at the valley the way crows look at lambing fields.

Havelock looked back at him, unhurried, unremarkable, a gardener regarding weather. Then he went in to his supper.

* * *

That night, the household performed itself as it did every night. Edith Marlowe at her desk upstairs, the scratch of her pen audible through the study door like a small persistent insect. Cook producing dinner. Marta laying and clearing. Rafael driving the car to the station approach and back for no reason anyone questioned, keeping the engine warm, keeping the habit of movement alive. And underneath the performance, running beneath the floorboards of the house like a hidden stream, the other household: the signals on the line, the heel-ends of bread, the phantom gravel, the oil on the padlock, the crock labeled FOR THE BIRDS.

None of them knew the whole. Nell knew the river and the signals but not what was said inside the ice house. Marta knew the food and the letters but not the ice road or the timing. Havelock knew the traffic and told himself he knew nothing at all. Even Rafael, who knew most, lay awake at night cataloguing what they did *not* know — whether Brine had informants in the village, whether the post office ledger had been copied, whether Marta's cousin's letters were being opened in Millbrook as surely as Rafael opened everyone's letters here.

It should have frightened them apart, such a machine built of separate parts. Instead it held them together, the way a net holds: each knot ignorant of the rope's whole length, trusting the tension of the others.

On Friday morning, Nell hung out the washing in a wind straight off the mountains, and pinned to the line, among the ordinary whites, a single red flannel — not the alarm signal, but a variant, one she'd devised alone at her window the night before: *man on the ridge again, and this time he has a dog.*

By noon the flannel was in. By dusk, Rafael stood at the nursery window with a candle, watching the dark mass of the ice house against the riverbank, and found that they were no longer counting the days until the thaw.

They were counting the hours until Brine did.
