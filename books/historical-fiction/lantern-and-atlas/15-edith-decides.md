## 15. Edith Decides

Edith Marlowe had built a life on the principle that one should never spy on the people one loved, and she had broken it by half past nine on a Tuesday evening in February.

She told herself it was not spying. She had merely noticed — and noticing was her profession, more or less; she had noticed her way through fifteen years of meetings where men told her women did not want the vote, had noticed the flicker in a chancellor's eye that meant he could be moved and the set of another's mouth that meant he could not — and what she had noticed was that Rafael Underhill had been lying to her for three weeks.

Not with words. Rafael's words were as careful and correct as ever: *the coal delivery's short again, ma'am; I've written to the merchant; the roads past Hollow Bridge are drifted; shall I take the trap or shall we walk.* It was Rafael's hands she had begun to distrust, those quiet competent hands that sorted her correspondence every evening, and above all it was the hours those hands kept. Three times this fortnight Rafael had gone out after supper with a lantern and a bundle under one arm, saying only *an errand, ma'am,* and each time had come back an hour later smelling of river cold and woodsmoke, cheeks scoured pink, eyes carefully empty of everything.

Edith knew what careful emptiness looked like. She wore it herself at her sister's dinner table when the talk turned to the war and someone said something unforgivable about the Germans and she smiled and passed the potatoes because a quarrel there would accomplish nothing but indigestion. Careful emptiness was not nothing. It was everything, packed down hard.

So on Tuesday evening, when Rafael took the lantern down from its hook for the fourth time, Edith put on her heaviest coat and followed.

The night was bitter, the kind of clear black cold that made the stars seem personal. Snow squeaked underfoot. Rafael went down the drive at a steady pace, lantern swinging, and Edith kept forty yards behind, close enough to follow the light, far enough that her own dark coat made her a rumor rather than a presence. She felt absurd. She was fifty-one years old and a woman who had been dragged out of a horse trough at Trafalgar Square by two policemen, and here she was skulking after her own chauffeur like a character in a shilling shocker.

But she had watched Rafael Underhill for two years now — watched them read her letters before delivering them, which she had always assumed was diligence and now wondered about; watched them sit in on the edge of her meetings, taking no notes and missing nothing; watched them absorb the whole household's moods like blotting paper and give back nothing but competence. And she had thought, until recently, that she knew everything worth knowing about Rafael Underhill. That was precisely what she had liked about them: their blankness, their reliability, the sense of a person without entanglements, without history, without needs of their own. A perfect instrument. She had even said so once, at a committee dinner, and had seen Rafael standing behind her chair with the coats, and had felt, briefly, ashamed, and had buried the feeling under the next agenda item.

Constable Brine's visit last week had unburied it.

*Your Mr. Underhill — your Miss Underhill — whatever we're to call them — received a letter in December they never claimed.* Brine had stood in her front hall breathing officialdom all over her hat stand, enjoying himself. *Misdelivered circular, they said. Circulars aren't hand-addressed, Mrs. Marlowe. I'd take it kindly if you'd keep an eye on your household's comings and goings. There's a war on.*

She had promised nothing. But she had looked at Rafael differently since, and Rafael had known it, and the knowing sat between them at breakfast like an unpaid bill.

Now Rafael left the drive at the boathouse path, and Edith's stomach tightened, because the boathouse path led to the river, and the river was ice, and beyond the river lay the next valley, and in the next valley, according to three weeks of overheard kitchen conversation and one very specific remark from Nell Quist at the ferry landing, something was hidden.

Rafael did not go to the boathouse. They turned along the bank instead, toward the old ice house — the great brick vault from her father-in-law's day, half-sunk in the hillside, that hadn't held ice since before the motorcar. Edith stopped at the top of the slope and let the lantern light shrink ahead of her, and watched Rafael knock on the ice house door in a pattern — two, pause, three, pause, one — and watched the door open on darkness, and saw, silhouetted for one instant against Rafael's lantern, the shape of a man.

Tall. Gaunt. A blanket around his shoulders like a shawl. He reached out and touched Rafael's arm, just the arm, briefly, the way one touches a thing to be sure it is real, and Rafael went inside, and the door closed, and Edith stood in the snow with her heart going like a triphammer.

She knew who it was. God help her, she knew immediately, the way one knows the answer to a riddle once it is spoken. The Austrian engineer. Not Austrian — Hungarian, or Czech, or whatever the Empire had decided to call itself this decade; Rafael had mentioned him exactly once in two years, in the neutral tone reserved for dangerous subjects: *a friend from before, ma'am, an engineer, he went home in '09.* Anton Varga. And Varga was now, per the register Constable Brine carried around like a prayer book, an enemy alien — dismissed from the works at Corbridge, ordered to report weekly to police sixty miles away across a closed bridge, liable to internment at a stroke. A man with no legal way to exist anywhere in England, hiding in a brick cellar forty yards from her own property, warmed and fed by her own chauffeur.

Edith stood in the cold for a long time.

* * *

She did not confront anyone that night. She walked back to the house by the field path, boots crunching, mind moving with terrible clarity, the clarity she usually felt only in the last minute before stepping onto a platform before a hostile crowd.

Here is what she knew, laid out like exhibits:

Harboring an enemy alien was a crime. Not a metaphorical crime, not a matter of opinion — a prosecutable offense, and worse, a *newspaper* offense. If it came out that the suffragist Mrs. Marlowe, whose movement was already accused of being unpatriotic, pro-German, soft, hysterical, *foreign-tainted* — if it came out that she had sheltered an enemy national in her own ice house while English boys froze in Flanders, the cause would bleed. Not die — she refused to think in terms of dying — but bleed, publicly, at the worst possible moment, handing every heckler in Yorkshire a gift-wrapped accusation. She could see the headlines. She could see Sylvia's people distancing themselves. She could see the WSPU's patriotic wing pointing at her as proof of everything they'd renounced. Twenty years of work, and it would be undone not by her error but by her kindness, which was somehow the crueler mechanism.

And here is what else she knew:

That the man in the ice house had done nothing but exist on the wrong side of a treaty line drawn by men in rooms in Vienna and Berlin and London, none of whom would ever be hungry in a cellar in Yorkshire. That "enemy alien" was a phrase constructed to make a category of person into a problem, and she had spent her whole life refusing precisely that move when it was made about women — *they are not citizens, they are a class, they are a question, they are somebody else's to decide.* That if she turned him in, she would be safe, respectable, and finished in her own estimation forever.

She stood at her bedroom window with her coat still on, looking down at the white lawn, and let the two truths fight it out.

The pragmatist in her — and she honored the pragmatist; the pragmatist had won the vote in Western Australia and kept the movement solvent through every famine year — the pragmatist said: *you are not responsible for this. Rafael brought this into your house without your knowledge. You owe the man nothing. Report it quietly, distance yourself firmly, manage the narrative, protect the greater good. One man against the franchise for millions. Any decent general knows which side of that arithmetic to stand on.*

And the other voice — the younger one, the one that had first gotten up on a crate in a market square with her heart hammering and had understood in that moment that respectability was a currency and she was going to spend every coin of it — that voice said: *you have spent twenty years telling audiences that justice is not a matter of convenience. You have said, in public, in print, that the measure of a principle is what it costs you. Do you mean it, Edith, or was it only ever rhetoric aimed at other people's consciences?*

She thought of her husband, dead eleven years, who had believed in her causes the way one believes in weather — real, impressive, not personally actionable. She thought of the mothers in the valley whose letters she had signed her name to, petitioning for their sons. She thought of Marta, the kitchen girl, who had gone silent and watchful since the shopkeeper in the village had put a card in his window: NO FOREIGNERS SERVED HERE. She thought of Rafael's face at the top of the stairs yesterday, worn thin as paper.

Then she thought about the arithmetic honestly, because she owed it that much. She was not a sentimentalist. Sentiment was what happened to movements when they got tired.

If she helped, and it stayed hidden, the cost was her complicity and her sleep. If she helped and it came out, the cost might be catastrophic — to the movement, to Rafael, to herself. If she turned him in, the cost was certain, immediate, and internal: she would become a person who had handed a starving man to a constable to protect her lecture bookings.

Provisionally — she held the word in front of her like a shield — provisionally, the third cost was the only one she could not pay and remain Edith Marlowe.

Very well then. But she would do it her way. Not Rafael's way, all lantern-light and hope and no contingency. If she was in, she was in as a commander, and commanders planned retreats.

* * *

She waited two days, because arriving too quickly at the ice house would tell Rafael she'd followed them, and she wanted — needed — the confrontation to happen on ground she chose. She used the interval to prepare. She inventoried the ice house's stores by proxy, sending the gardener to check the old sacking "before the thaw ruins it." She wrote two letters: one to her solicitor in London asking, in deliberately abstract terms, what the law actually said about persons who gave food and shelter to registered enemy aliens, and what the realistic penalties were; and one to a journalist friend, unsent, held in her desk drawer, outlining the story she would tell *if* it broke — the story in which she had discovered the man herself and, pending inquiry, extended Christian charity to a destitute civilian, which was legal, defensible, and nearly true. She hated herself a little for writing it. She wrote it anyway. Principle and pragmatism could share a bed if neither slept too soundly.

On Thursday evening she intercepted Rafael in the corridor outside the study, where Rafael was carrying in the decanter and the post.

"Set that down," Edith said. "Come in and sit. Not there — the chair. Sit properly. We are going to have a conversation."

Rafael set down the tray. Edith watched the transformation happen in real time: the shoulders settling, the face arranging itself into pleasant blankness, the servant assembling over the person like water closing over a stone. It was, she thought, a remarkable performance. It had fooled her for two years.

"I know," Edith said.

Rafael did not move. "Ma'am?"

"Do not *ma'am* me. Sit down." Rafael sat, on the edge of the chair, hands loose on knees, and Edith realized with a small cold shock that Rafael was preparing to deny everything, calmly, indefinitely, and probably successfully. "I followed you Tuesday night. To the ice house. I saw the door open. I saw him."

The blankness held for three full seconds. Then something in Rafael's face cracked — not dramatically; a hairline fracture, a shift in the eyes, the look of a person hearing ice groan beneath them. "Mrs. Marlowe—"

"His name is Varga. Anton Varga. He's the engineer you knew before '09, and he is on the police register as an enemy alien, and he has been living in my father-in-law's ice house for some weeks, and you have been feeding him." She kept her voice level, almost conversational; volume was for platforms. "I am not going to ask you whether it's true. I watched it with my own eyes. What I am going to ask you is what you intend, because as of Tuesday night it is also my problem, and I do not manage problems by pretending they don't exist."

Rafael's hands had stopped being loose. "Are you going to report it?"

It was not a defiant question. That was what undid her a little. It was asked the way one asks a doctor — braced, past arguing, wanting only to know.

"No," Edith said, and watched relief move through Rafael like weather. "Don't thank me yet. Listen to me. What you are doing is illegal. What I propose to allow is illegal. I want you to understand that I know it, that I am choosing it with my eyes open, and that I will not have it done badly." She stood and went to the window; the lawn was blue with dusk. "Brine has the postal ledger. He has a letter with your name on it that you claim never existed. He watches this house. He has posted a man near the bridge approach — yes, I know that too; the gardener drinks at the King's Arms and has ears. Every day this continues, the weight on that scale increases. So we are going to assume he finds him. We are going to work backward from catastrophe. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, Mrs. Marlowe."

"And stop *Mrs. Marlowe*-ing me while I'm trying to think." She turned back. "Tell me about him. Everything. How long he can stay hidden, what he needs, how sick he is — he looked half-starved through a door at forty yards — and how you propose to get him out of this valley before the thaw takes away the ice road and leaves us only a bridge with a constable on it."

For the first time in two years, Edith watched the servant fall entirely away from Rafael Underhill's face, and what stood underneath was not blank at all. It was exhausted, grateful, terrified, and fiercely intelligent, and she thought: *there you are. Where have you been?*

"He has to move within days," Rafael said. "The ice is going. He can't cross at the bridge — it's watched. And Mrs. Marlowe—" a breath, the first wobble in the voice, "I haven't got a plan that works."

"Of course you haven't," Edith said. "You've had loyalty, which is a different commodity entirely. Fortunately I have spent twenty years organizing people who had no legal right to do what they were doing anyway. Sit still. I'm going to think out loud, and you are going to interrupt only when you have a better idea, which I anticipate will be often."

Rafael almost smiled. Almost.

"One condition," Edith added, and held up a finger before Rafael could speak. "When this ends — however it ends — you will tell me the rest. Your name, your history, all of it that bears on this. I am not lending my reputation to a story I only half know. Do we understand each other?"

"We do," Rafael said quietly.

"Good." Edith drew the curtain against the dusk and lit the lamp on her desk, and the small bright circle of it steadied her the way it always had. "Then let us begin with the bridge, the constable, and every way in and out of this valley — because I suspect, Rafael, that we are going to need all of them, and soon."

Down the hall, the hall clock struck seven. Somewhere toward the river, faint under the wind, she fancied she could hear the ice talking to itself — groaning, shifting, counting down the days.
