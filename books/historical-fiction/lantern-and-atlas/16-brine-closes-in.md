## 16. Brine Closes In

Rafael found the corner of the letter on a Tuesday, three days too late.

They had burned Anton's first letter in the drawing room grate back in November, watched the paper curl and go to ash, stirred the embers with a poker until nothing remained but grey — or so they had believed. But Edith's maids were not diligent about the grates, and the drawing room fire was laid but seldom lit, and so the fragment had sat through weeks of cold mornings, wedged where the iron grate met the brick, waiting like something buried in a peat bog with all its secrets intact.

Rafael saw it when they went to lay the kindling: a triangle of paper no larger than a thumbnail, scorched brown at its edges, and along one edge — unmistakable even now, even faded — the neat, slanted hand that Rafael knew better than their own.

They did not touch it at first. They stood very still, the kindling balanced in their arms, and looked at it the way one looks at a snake sunning itself on a step.

Then Brine's knock came at the front door, and there was no time to be anything but calm.

* * *

Constable Brine had a new coat for winter, better than his old one, and he wore it the way small men wear promotions. He stood in the Marlowe hall stamping snow from his boots and apologizing without meaning it, saying he was obliged to make a routine survey of the households in the valley, nothing personal, Mrs. Marlowe would understand, matters being what they were.

Edith understood nothing of the sort, and said so with a smile that could have cut glass.

"A survey of *what*, Constable? Our spoons?"

"Of persons, madam." He produced a notebook, and Rafael, standing by the coat rack with their arms full of Edith's wraps, watched him open it and watched the pages fall naturally — too naturally — to a place partway through, where something had already been written. "Persons of foreign origin. It's a home office matter now. Everyone's been very cooperative."

"Everyone," Edith repeated. "How comforting for everyone."

He turned to Rafael then, and Rafael gave him the face they had spent a lifetime perfecting: pleasant, slightly vacant, the face of a person whose thoughts rarely traveled further than the next errand.

"Underhill, isn't it," Brine said. Not a question. "You handle the post here."

"Some of it," Rafael said. "Mrs. Marlowe gets a great deal. I sort it."

"And your own post?"

"I don't get much." A small shrug, self-deprecating. "Circulars, mostly. The seed catalogues come late since the bridge closed."

Brine wrote something down. Rafael could not see what, and hated not knowing more than they hated almost anything else in that moment. The pencil moved four times, perhaps five. Four or five words about R. Underhill, who sorted the mail, who got circulars, who mentioned seed catalogues as though catalogues were the whole of a life.

"The thing about the post," Brine said pleasantly, still writing, "is that it's a record. People forget that. Every letter that comes through the valley, there's a line in the ledger at the post office. Date, name, addressee. Paper remembers, Mr. Underhill. People are surprised how well paper remembers."

"I wouldn't know," said Rafael, who knew exactly how well paper remembered; who had built an entire hidden history out of other people's paper; who had read the valley's whole heart through its envelopes for years and left no line anywhere in any ledger under any name that could be traced back further than three years, which was as far back as Rafael Underhill existed, which was precisely the problem.

"Well." Brine snapped the notebook shut. He put out a hand and Edith, with magnificent reluctance, shook it. "Thank you both. We'll be making the rounds of the lower village this week. Door to door. Just routine." He settled his new coat on his shoulders. "Cold work, but someone has to do it. That's what people forget about order, Mrs. Marlowe. Someone always has to do it."

"Yes," Edith said coolly. "I've often noticed that the someone is never you."

The door closed behind him. Edith stood a moment in the hall, then looked at Rafael — that new look she had been giving them since the business of the misdelivered letter, the look of a woman recalculating a sum she'd thought she'd finished. She said only, "Seed catalogues," with a faint arch of her brow, and went up to her study.

When she was gone Rafael knelt at the grate and picked the scorched corner out of the cold ashes with two fingers, and folded it small, and put it in the box of Edith's correspondence where it would sit inside a sealed envelope addressed to nobody, hidden inside the one category of paper in the house that no one dared open but Rafael.

Paper remembers. So do I, Constable.

* * *

The door-to-door searches began Thursday and were, in the beginning, almost comically bad. Brine had deputized two men from the village — the Henderson brothers, who ran the quarry cart and had opinions about foreigners that began and ended with whatever Brine told them — and the three of them went up and down the lanes knocking on doors with the air of men doing something official and hoping no one would ask for the document that made it so. They looked in pantries. They counted heads at supper tables. At the Kessler farm they made old Mr. Kessler recite the date of his naturalization papers twice, and then made him fetch the papers themselves from a tin box under the bed, and read them aloud in the kitchen while Mrs. Kessler wept quietly into her apron over nothing, over papers, over the fact of having to prove forty years of plowing in the rain.

Rafael heard all of it secondhand, in the kitchen, from Marta, whose cousin lived down in the lower village and whose reports came each evening with less breath and less color in them.

"They took Mr. Ostrowski's name off the church roll," Marta said, kneading bread with a violence the dough had done nothing to deserve. "Off the *church roll*. Father Ambrose wouldn't do it, so they wrote it in their book anyway, like writing it makes it true. And Hedy says—" She stopped. Pressed her palms flat into the dough. "Hedy says they asked about me. Here. Whether anyone foreign works up at the big house."

"What did you tell them?" Rafael asked, though Marta had told them nothing, obviously; Marta needed this place the way a drowning swimmer needs a riverbank.

"That you're English as Sunday roast," Marta said, and laughed, and the laugh had a crack down the middle of it.

Rafael noted the crack and filed it away with all the other fragile things they were keeping track of: Nell Quist's lantern schedule; the ice house stores, now enough for ten days; the thaw line creeping up the river's edge a finger's width each afternoon; Brine's notebook; the ledger at the post office; the scorched corner folded inside an envelope addressed to nobody.

Everything was holding. Everything was also, Rafael understood, one pulled thread from unraveling, and there were more threads every day.

* * *

Nell was caught on Saturday.

Rafael learned of it the way bad news travels in a valley — faster than the news itself, arriving as a feeling before it arrived as fact. Cook came into the scullery white around the mouth and said, "That Quist girl's been stopped on the ferry road," and Rafael set down the harness they were cleaning and walked out the back door and across the frozen garden at a pace carefully calibrated to look like nothing at all.

By the time Rafael reached the bend above the ferry road, Nell was standing in the trampled snow beside Brine and one of the Henderson brothers, and there was a basket at her feet, and the basket had spilled.

It was, on reflection, a masterpiece. Rafael saw it all in one glance from the rise: the basket overturned just so, the cabbages rolling free, and scattered among them — this was the genius of it — a dozen wrapped parcels of cooked meat and bread, greasy paper splitting in the cold, and Nell's face arranged into an expression of such profound fourteen-year-old outrage that it deserved its own frame.

"—every Saturday since Michaelmas," she was saying, in the wounded, carrying voice of a girl who has been wronged by the entire world, "and Dad says if I can't keep the trap lines straight it's no use him teaching me, and now you've gone and trod half of it into the snow—"

"These parcels," Brine said. He crouched, prodded one of the meat packages with his stick, and did not pick it up, because picking it up would mean taking evidence seriously and the evidence was a cabbage-adjacent bundle of cold mutton. "This is a deal of food for trap lines."

"It's bait, isn't it!" Nell flared. "Foxes don't eat cabbage! You want a fox to come to a trap you put meat on it, everyone knows that, my brother could tell you and he's eleven!" She rounded on the Henderson brother. "Did you have to tread on the good ones? Those were the leg-of-mutton scraps. Foxes won't look twice at shin."

"There's a deal of meat going missing in this valley," Brine said slowly, and Rafael, watching from the rise, felt the shape of the danger and admired, helplessly, how fast the girl moved inside it.

"Well, it's not foxes taking the hens, if that's what's rattled you," Nell said scornfully. "It's a marten. Dad's seen its prints. You'd know a marten print if you knew anything about anything, but there, I suppose that's asking." She bent and began furiously gathering cabbages. "Are you going to fine me for spilling my own baskets on my own road, or am I allowed to go? Because the traps want seeing before dark and it'll be your fault about the foxes, all of it, and I'll say so."

There was a silence in which Brine looked at her, and Nell looked back with the flat, unbroken stare of someone who has absolutely nothing to hide because she has buried it too deep to find herself. Rafael had seen constables break grown men with that silence. It slid off the girl like water off ice.

Brine straightened. "Get your cabbages," he said. "And stay off the river path after dark. There's been talk of lights."

"Lights," Nell repeated, with a superb teenage contempt for the entire subject of light. "It's lanterns at the church social, isn't it. Everyone knows about the lanterns." She hoisted the basket, dumped the surviving bundles back in among the cabbages — the marten story, the trap-line story, the whole architecture of it reassembling itself around the truth like bone around marrow — and trudged past Brine and past the Henderson brother and up the hill, muttering about men and officials and ruined mutton in a voice calculated to carry.

She passed within six feet of Rafael's vantage and did not glance up, did not flicker, did not so much as change her breathing. Only when she was well past, only in the swing of her shoulders, was there the smallest loosening — the release of a held thing — and then she was gone around the hedge, and Rafael stood in the cold and let out their own breath very slowly.

Bait for the traps. Leg-of-mutton scraps, because foxes won't look twice at shin. The food for a hidden man, explained in broad daylight to a constable's face, and explained so completely, so indignantly, so *boringly*, that the truth had simply drowned in the volume of the lie.

Rafael climbed back over the garden wall and went to check the ice house stores, and to halve them, starting tonight. Ten days' provisions drawn down to five, spread thinner, carried in smaller bundles, by different hands, at different hours. The clock was ticking now; Rafael could hear it everywhere — in the thaw line on the river, in Brine's notebook, in Marta's cracking laugh, in the ledger's patient lines.

* * *

Sunday after church, Rafael drove Edith along the river road for the air of it, and saw the new thing at once.

At the near end of the closed bridge — the great skeleton of it standing dark over the ice, its planks stacked and its chains strung — a sentry box had appeared. Rough boards, tarpaulin roof, and a brazier glowing at its mouth, and beside the brazier a man in a constable's coat with a rifle leaned against the box wall, warming his hands.

Rafael slowed the car. There was no reason not to; slowing was natural, curious, harmless. The man lifted a hand — not waving, merely acknowledging, the way a lighthouse acknowledges a ship — and Rafael nodded back and drove on, and did not let their eyes linger on the bridge, on the ice road running beneath it, on the pale ribbon of frozen river that was the only way across and was now, as of some hour between Saturday night and Sunday morning, under a gun.

"He's new," Edith said, craning to look back. "That's not Brine."

"No."

"A posted sentry. On a *closed bridge*. Whatever for?" Her voice was light, conversational, and beneath it Rafael heard the other Edith, the quick one, assembling facts. "Unless," she went on, still lightly, "someone believes something crosses the river that oughtn't to. Smuggling, perhaps. Or people."

"People cross where there's a gap in the law," Rafael said, keeping their eyes on the horses' backs, their voice on the flat side of neutral. "That's what gaps are for."

Edith was quiet for a long moment. The runners hissed on the packed snow. Ahead the valley opened out, white to the ridgeline, beautiful the way a trap is beautiful when the snow covers the jaws.

"When I was in Hollow Vale in the spring," she said at last, "they arrested a woman for feeding strikers. Not violence. Not conspiracy. *Feeding* them. Soup." Her hands were quiet in her lap, gloved, composed. "The magistrate asked her why, and she said, because they were hungry and I had a pot. He sentenced her to a month. And the whole courtroom thought she was the mad one, and I thought—" She stopped. Looked out at the river, at the sentry's brazier glowing small and red against the blue dusk. "I thought, there is the whole question of the age, in one pot of soup."

"Yes, ma'am," Rafael said.

They turned for home. Behind them the sentry box burned its little fire at the throat of the valley, and Rafael drove into the dusk doing arithmetic again — five days of stores, one thawing river, one watching man, one girl with a genius for lies, one cook's assistant with a cousin in the lower village and a crack down the middle of her courage — and arrived, somewhere past the third mile, at a number they did not like at all.

That night, Rafael wrote nothing down. But lying awake in the cold dark, listening to the wind work at the shutters, they heard it plainly: the clock, ticking. And beneath it, fainter, another sound they couldn't yet name — footsteps coming up the drive of some future morning, and Marta's voice, high and frightened, saying a word Rafael had taught the whole household never to say.

*Ice house.*
