## 17. The Betrayal

Marta Havel had learned early that fear lived in the body before it ever reached the mouth. It lived in her hands when she was nine years old and her father's print shop in Brno was raided, and it lived in them now, six years later and an ocean away, as she scrubbed the Marlowe kitchen floor until the boards bleached pale under her brush. Fear was a thing you could scrub at all morning without ever lifting it out of the grain.

She had been thinking about her cousin Josef since Tuesday. Since Constable Brine had stood in the shop doorway on the high street with his notebook open, asking Mr. Pell his questions in that soft voice of his, the one that made everything sound reasonable. *And your staff, Mr. Pell — any foreign-born? You'd want to be registering them, of course. Nothing personal in it. Just order.* And Mr. Pell, whose livelihood sat in Brine's hands like an egg, had hesitated only a moment before saying, *My girl Marta's Czech, but she's been here since she was thirteen. Practically local.* As if Marta were a cheese that had aged into respectability. And Brine had written something down anyway, because writing things down was what he did, and then he'd said, almost kindly, *There's a list coming, Mr. Pell. Names go on lists, or names come off them. A man in your position wants to think about which.*

Josef's name would not be on any good list. Josef, who had come over two summers ago to work the orchards, who sent half his wages home to their aunt in Prague, who laughed too loud and sang German drinking songs he'd learned from German workmen because songs didn't have nationalities until men like Brine decided they did. Josef, whom Brine had already stopped twice on the road, whom the fishmonger had refused to serve last week with the whole queue watching, nobody saying a word because nobody dared.

*You can help him,* Brine had told her yesterday, appearing at the scullery door while she hung washing, so quietly she hadn't heard the gate. He'd held up his notebook like a hymnal. *People who cooperate find cooperation goes both ways. A cousin with a clean sheet. A work permit extended. Small things, Miss Havel. I'm a small man for small favors.* He had smiled when he said it, and the smile had been the worst part, because it was friendly, because he clearly believed himself generous.

She had said she had nothing to tell him. She had said it in her careful second-language voice, flat and slow, and he had nodded and left, and she had stood in the yard with a wet sheet in her arms and known — the way animals know weather — that he would come back, and that next time he would not leave empty.

* * *

The trouble with the Marlowe household was that it had grown a secret, and secrets were heavy, and Marta carried everything heavily these days.

She had known about the ice house for eleven days. Not because anyone had told her — Rafael Underhill told nobody anything; Marta suspected Rafael even dreamed behind locked doors — but because Marta counted. She counted the bread, she counted the candles, she counted the spoons, because counting was how a girl with a fourth-grade education kept herself valuable in a country that measured people by their papers. And eleven days ago the count had gone wrong. Two eggs short at breakfast, blamed on the hens. A heel of ham gone that Mrs. Fenn the cook blamed on her own memory. And Rafael, who took tea alone in the estate office most evenings, now walked out after dark toward the orchard carrying something flat under their coat, and came back lighter than they'd gone.

Marta had followed once. Only once, guiltily, her boots wrapped in sacking, down past the frozen raspberry canes to the old ice house with its sod roof and its door swollen shut for a decade — except the door wasn't swollen shut anymore, and lamplight leaked through the seams like the building had swallowed a star.

She hadn't needed to see the face. There was only one reason for a face to be hidden in an ice house in February, in a valley where Constable Brine read the newspaper clippings about enemy aliens aloud in the pub as though reciting scripture. She had gone back to the kitchen and finished the supper bread and said nothing, and every day since she had added a crust here, a spoonful there, an uncounted egg, feeding a stranger she'd never seen because Rafael Underhill had fed her family's name into silence often enough — covering her broken English to callers, telling Mrs. Fenn the dropped crockery had been Marta's own clumsiness — and debts between quiet people were paid in kind or not paid at all.

Now she stood at the kitchen table Thursday evening with her hands flat on the flour-dusted wood, and through the window she could see Brine's bicycle leaning against the garden wall, and Brine himself sitting in Mr. Pell's shop across the lane — no, not Pell's; he'd moved on; he was talking to the ferryman now, Quist, at the bottom of the lane, notebook open, and little Nell Quist standing in the ferry-house doorway with her arms crossed like a woman three times her age.

He was working down the lane. House by house. And everyone knew where a search ended when it started at the top of the village: the big house was always last, and always biggest.

*Marta,* said Mrs. Fenn behind her, *the bread. You've let the loaf sit.*

*Sorry, Mrs. Fenn.* Her voice came out steady. That was the trick she'd learned at nine years old: the body could shake all it wanted as long as the voice stayed level.

Rafael came through the service door at that moment, shaking snow off their shoulders, and paused, and looked at her — really looked, the way they sometimes did, as if reading a letter addressed to someone else — and Marta understood with sick certainty that if she meant to break, she had minutes to do it in, and no matter which way she broke, something would die: Josef's freedom, or the man in the ice house, or the person she wanted to believe she was.

* * *

Brine caught her Friday morning at the well.

Not at the house — never at the house; he was too clever for witnesses — but at the village well, where she'd been sent with the yoke and buckets, where the path was screened by the alder hedge and a girl could be talked to privately. He was leaning against the stone as though he'd been waiting for water himself, and perhaps he had, for an hour, in the cold.

*Miss Havel.* He touched his cap. Up close his face was younger than his manner, fair-skinned, unremarkable, the kind of face that would look correct in a photograph labeled *Constable Brine, 1913*, and be forgotten. *Cold morning for fetching.*

*Always cold mornings for fetching.*

*That's the trade.* He let the silence sit, then said, conversationally, *Your cousin. Josef. Orchard contract runs out end of March, doesn't it? And renewals are suspended for — certain categories, just now.* He turned his notebook idly, not opening it. Showing her it existed. *I could see my way to recommending an exception. Family man's wages still going home to Prague, that counts for something. Stability. Roots.* He smiled his friendly smile. *Of course, if something should come to light later — something you knew and didn't mention — well. Then it's not one name on a list, is it. It's two. Maybe three. Deportation's a hard crossing this time of year, they tell me. The holding camp at Hull is worse.*

The bucket rope creaked in her hands. Somewhere beyond the hedge a cart went by, and the world continued being ordinary, which felt obscene.

*I don't know anything,* she said.

*I know.* Gently. Almost tenderly. *But you're an observant girl. I've noticed that. The big house has a lot of ground, and disused buildings, and servants who see everything and say little. If anything — anything at all — seemed unusual to you. Food moving where it shouldn't. Lights where there's no call for them. A person who isn't a person yet.* He closed his hand around the notebook, tucking it away like a finished meal. *You'd be doing the right thing, Miss Havel. For your cousin. For your country, such as it is. For yourself.*

He tipped his cap again and walked off down the frozen lane, and Marta stood at the well until the rope froze to her palms, and the water in the buckets filmed with ice, and somewhere inside her a door that had held against six years of pressure swung quietly open.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape: Josef was real. She had held his letters. She knew the shape of his handwriting, the aunt's name at the top of every page, the exact sum he sent home. The man in the ice house was a rumor she had confirmed with her own eyes only as a shape behind a lamp seam — a stranger, an enemy alien, a word from the newspapers. Love was supposed to choose the known quantity. Everyone said so. Her mother had crossed an ocean on that principle.

She told herself this all the way up the drive. She told it to herself as she set down the buckets in the scullery and took off the yoke and stood in the warm bread-smelling dark of the kitchen corridor. And then she thought of Rafael's face in the lamplight, patient and unreadable, and of the lanterns she had seen — though she'd never told a soul — strung along the riverbank on some evenings like a language, and of Edith Marlowe at the winter meeting saying in her clear carrying voice, *Justice that stops at the border of convenience is not justice at all, it is accounting* — and Marta understood, with a clarity that arrived far too late to be useful, exactly which direction she was walking, and that she had been walking it since Brine first touched his cap.

She had already spoken. That was the horror of it, the thing she would carry longer than anything else: she hadn't waited for the well. Wednesday, two days ago, Mrs. Fenn had asked why the stores ran strange, and Marta — flustered, guarding the secret badly — had said the gardener took extra to the ice house for *storing apples*. And Mrs. Fenn had repeated it to the butcher's boy, and the butcher's boy was a gossip, and gossip traveled downhill faster than water, and Constable Brine drank at the pub where the butcher's boy delivered.

She hadn't broken this morning. She had broken days ago, carelessly, in her own kitchen, without knowing it. Brine's visit to the well hadn't been fishing. It had been confirmation, courtesy, a signature collected on a debt already incurred.

Marta put her hands over her face and stood very still in the corridor, and then she wiped them on her apron, and went to find Rafael.

* * *

She found them in the stable, harnessing the motor car though no errand was ordered — Rafael always found work for their hands when their mind was elsewhere — and the words came out of Marta in the wrong order, in her broken English, in Czech once when English failed entirely, and Rafael went very still against the horse's flank with the buckle half-fastened, and listened the way they did everything: completely.

*The ice house,* Marta said again, when she'd finished. *He knows. Or he'll know by tonight. The story about the apples — I said it — I didn't think —* and here her voice finally cracked, the level instrument failing at last. *I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I didn't mean to give him anything and I gave him everything, and I came to tell you because you have to move him, tonight, now, before —*

Rafael set the buckle. Finished it. Their hands did not shake; Marta watched them deliberately not shake, and understood that Rafael's calm was not the absence of fear but fear mastered daily, like her own scrubbed floors.

*When will he act?* Rafael asked quietly.

*Tomorrow. Dawn, I think. He likes dawn — he said once a man taken at dawn hasn't time to arrange his face.* She swallowed. *He'll bring men. Quiet ones. He won't want a scene at the big house — not with Mrs. Marlowe's name in the county papers every week.*

*A scene.* Something moved behind Rafael's eyes, some rapid interior arithmetic that Marta could almost see running — risk weighed against risk, route against route — and then Rafael looked at her, fully, and Marta braced for the judgment she had earned.

It didn't come.

*You ran here first,* Rafael said. *Across the yard, in this cold, straight to me. Brine offered you your cousin and you came here anyway.*

*I came here too late.*

*You came.* Rafael took the harness off the hook and began, methodically, unbuckling what they'd just fastened, converting the car from readiness back to innocence. *Go back to the kitchen, Marta. Count your spoons. Be seen. If anyone asks, we spoke about boot polish.* They paused, and their voice gentled by a degree Marta had never heard in it. *What you did was human. What you're doing now is brave. They aren't the same thing, and the second one matters more tonight.*

Marta stood a moment longer. Then she said, in Czech, because some things could only be said in the language her mother had prayed in: *I will help. Whatever comes. Tell me what to do and I will do it, and God can judge the rest.*

Rafael, who understood more Czech than they'd ever admitted, nodded once.

* * *

They did not sleep, most of them. Word moved through the household the way warmth moves through a wall — slowly, invisibly, by conduction. Marta saw Nell Quist slip through the side gate at dusk, breathless, and leave again twenty minutes later at a run. She saw the gardener take the long-handled lantern down from the tack room peg and oil it without being told. She saw Mrs. Fenn bake bread at midnight and ask no questions about where it was going, which was its own kind of eloquence. And she saw Edith Marlowe's study window burn late, and a shadow cross it twice, and wondered what the lady of the house knew and when she had chosen to know it.

At four in the morning, Marta stood at her attic window with the curtain drawn back two inches, and watched figures move down through the orchard — Rafael, the gardener, a thin stooped shape between them that walked like a man relearning walking — heading not for the river but for the boathouse, and she pressed her forehead to the freezing glass and whispered into her own reflection, *Be quick, be quick, be quick.*

At five, the figures came back up. Three of them. The thin shape was not among them.

At half past five, the sky over the eastern hills went the color of a healing bruise, and Marta heard — she was sure she heard, though it might have been her own blood — a motorcar on the lane below, traveling fast, and stopping at the lodge gates.

Brine liked dawn. A man taken at dawn hadn't time to arrange his face.

But the ice house, when the constable's men reached it an hour later, swinging their lanterns across a sod roof silvered with frost, contained nothing but old straw, the ghost smell of a stranger, and one item laid deliberately on the stone shelf where the ice used to pack: a folded scrap of paper, weighted with a river pebble, on which was written in a small, unhurried hand:

*You are late. He was never here. Ask yourself who told you he was, and what they were protecting.*

Which was a lie, of course — he had been there, for weeks, fed by uncounted eggs and smuggled bread — but it was a lie built like a door, and behind it Marta could hear, faintly, terribly, the sound of Brine beginning to wonder.
