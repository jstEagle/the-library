## 18. Running Out of River

The knock came before the sun did.

Rafael was already awake — had been awake since three, lying in the narrow bed in the attic room, listening to the river. That was the thing no one warned you about keeping a secret the size of a man: you began to hear everything. The wind working at the shutter. The house settling its old bones. And beneath it all, under the frost-hardened ground and past the boathouse and out along the black channel of open water where the ice had given up weeks ago, the sound of the river moving. It had a voice in March. A low, restless mutter, like a crowd gathering outside a building where something inevitable was happening.

The knock was Marta's. Rafael knew it by its rhythm — two quick, one hesitant, the way she always knocked as though apologizing for the door existing. Rafael pulled on trousers and a sweater over their nightshirt, took the stairs down through the sleeping house two at a time, and opened the kitchen door on a girl gray-faced in the blue dark, her coat buttoned wrong, her breath coming in white rags.

"You have to get him out," Marta said. "He knows."

* * *

They stood at the kitchen table while the sky behind the window went from ink to slate, and Marta told it in pieces, her hands flat on the wood as if holding the table down would hold herself together. Brine had come to her cousin's shop the evening before. Not to arrest — worse than that. To sit. To buy tobacco he didn't use and talk about how hard it must be, in these times, for people with family across the water, and how a person's troubles could be lifted entirely if they helped the Crown with certain small matters. He had mentioned Marta by name. He had mentioned that he knew she went walking some evenings with a basket heavier than an evening walk required.

"I didn't tell him anything," Marta said, and then her face did something terrible and honest, crumpling in the middle. "Not yet. But he's going to come back tonight, and Rafa, I don't — when he sits across from me and talks about my cousin going to an internment camp, I don't know what I'll do. I don't know what I am." She looked up. "You have to assume he knows. You have to assume it's tomorrow morning, or the morning after. Not Sunday. Not next week."

Rafael poured her tea she didn't drink and did not let themself think about forgiveness yet. Forgiveness was a luxury for later hours. Later days, if there were later days.

"Go back before the household wakes," they said. "Take the back lane. If anyone asks, you couldn't sleep and went to see the sun come up over the water. That's not even a lie; girls do it."

"And you?"

"I'm going to do arithmetic," Rafael said.

Marta almost smiled at that — a broken little curve, gone at once. "You're always doing arithmetic." She left her tea untouched and went out through the scullery, and Rafael stood alone in the kitchen watching the light strengthen over the frozen garden, doing arithmetic.

It was this: Anton had been in the ice house eleven days. Eleven days of Nell's watch schedule, of Marta's smuggled bread and cold chicken, of the gardener Hobbs laying straw over the footprints with a rake and never once asking why. Eleven days was already nine days longer than prudent. And now the margin was gone. If Brine knew — and Rafael had learned the hard way to budget for the worst case, because hope made sloppy bookkeeping — then the search could come at any hour. Today. This very morning, perhaps, once Brine assembled his men.

And the alternatives? The ice road, their careful midnight discovery of weeks ago, the route Rafael had walked twice with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light — that was finished. The thaw had been working at it for a fortnight. Yesterday afternoon Rafael had walked out to look, telling the household they were posting letters, and had stood on the bank and watched the river eat its own winter. Great plates of ice rode downstream like slow rafts. Where the crossing had been, there was open gray water in three places, and the remaining ice had gone the color of old candle tallow, rotten through, holding nothing but its own memory of being solid.

The bridge, then. The bridge with Brine's man posted at the approach — Rafael had seen him Tuesday, a young constable with a stove-pipe stove beside him, checking carts, checking faces, checking papers. The bridge was impossible.

The ice was impossible.

Rafael put both hands flat on the table the way Marta had, as though the truth might be pinned there like a moth, and found that the mind, cornered, does not go quiet. It goes clear. Somewhere in the last hour before dawn, standing in the cold kitchen while the house above slept on its secrets, Rafael stopped looking for a hidden way and started looking at the impossible one straight on.

Brine's man watched the bridge for a smuggler. For a figure at night, a cart with a false bottom, a bundle moved under cover. What no one watched for — what would be madness — was a sick workman driven openly across the bridge in broad daylight, in the motorcar of a well-known suffragist, bound for the doctor in Millbrook. Madness hid itself in plain sight precisely because it was mad. The whole weight of the lie would rest not on concealment but on nerve: on whether Constable Brine, who suspected everything and could prove nothing, dared stop a lady's car on a public road without cause, in front of witnesses, on the strength of a suspicion he couldn't name.

It rested, too, on Edith Marlowe.

That was the part that made Rafael's hands go still. Because it wasn't enough to dress Anton in Hobbs's oldest coat and put a muffler over his gaunt face and mumble about fever. Fever excused a passenger; it did not excuse a driver crossing into the next county in wartime with a foreign-born man beside them. But Edith — Edith with her speaking tour strangled by the closed bridge, Edith who had been chafing like a hawk on a short chain all winter — Edith could give them the one thing no disguise could manufacture: a reason. She had been invited, weeks ago, to address the Millbrook women's guild, and had declined with bitterness because of the checkpoint. If she un-declined. If she announced, loudly, that she would speak in Millbrook on Saturday and would brook no obstruction of her lawful travel — and if Rafael drove her, and if a sick workman happened to be conveyed the same direction rather than leave him unattended in the village —

No one stops a car carrying Mrs. Marlowe to a public meeting without making a scene. And Brine, Rafael thought, could not afford a scene. Scenes traveled. Scenes got written down. An ambitious man's superiors read about scenes.

It was a terrible plan. It was the only plan. Rafael noticed that both things were true and felt, unexpectedly, calm — the specific calm of a gambler pushing the entire stake to the center of the table, when the alternative is losing slowly instead.

* * *

Anton took the news sitting on a crate in the ice house, sawdust drifting down through the gaps in the plank ceiling, his breath and the block of ice beside him producing nearly the same amount of cold.

"A workman," he repeated. His English was still good after all these months of disuse, but slower, each word set down carefully like crockery. "I have not been a workman since—" He gestured vaguely, a gesture that contained engineering offices, lakeside summers, a whole vanished continent of before. "I do not know how to be a workman."

"Then be a dying one," Rafael said. "You won't have to do anything but slump."

He laughed — a dry thread of sound — and then pressed his hands against his knees and was quiet a long moment. Up close, in the gray light through the vent, the change in him was harder to ignore than ever. Five years ago on the lakeshore he had been all gesture and appetite, a man who took up more room than his body occupied. Now the body had shrunk toward the center of him and the room he took up was almost none at all. Only the eyes were the same. Dark, quick, still capable of catching fire sideways at a joke.

"Rafael." He used their name the way he always had, all three syllables, giving each its due. "If this fails, it is not only me. They will take you too. The girl. The maid. The old gardener." He shook his head. "A promise should not cost this much."

"The promise doesn't cost anything," Rafael said. "The times charge extra." They crouched so their eyes were level with his, sawdust biting into their knee. "Listen to me. Tomorrow morning you are Joseph Kessler, a laborer off the Hartwell farm, taken bad with pleurisy. Mrs. Marlowe is conveying you to Doctor Pringle in Millbrook because Dr. Hartley here is useless and you can't be left to the charity ward. You will cough if anyone looks at you. Can you cough?"

"I have been living in an ice house for eleven days," Anton said. "Madam, I can cough."

"Good." Rafael held his gaze. "And whatever happens at that checkpoint — however long the silence goes, whatever Brine says or does not say — you stay inside the sickness. You don't understand English questions. You moan. Do you hear me? Even if it goes wrong. Especially if it goes wrong. Everything I'm going to bet tomorrow depends on them believing you're too far gone to matter."

"And if they believe it," Anton said softly, "you are still the one who drove me. They will come for you."

"Yes," Rafael said.

It hung between them, plain as a lamp. Anton reached out and gripped Rafael's wrist, hard, the engineer's grip, precise even in ruin. "Five years ago I made you promise to answer," he said. "I did not know I was asking for this."

"Nobody ever does," Rafael said. "That's what makes it a promise instead of a favor."

* * *

Edith heard the whole of it in her study with the door shut and the fire burned down to embers, and Rafael told her all of it — not the partial truths of weeks past, not the managed portions, but everything: the letter, the promise, the ice road, the ice house, the conspiracy of laundry lines and bread crusts that had grown around Anton like ivy around a wall. It took twenty minutes. Edith interrupted only once, near the beginning, with a single raised hand and the word "continue," which was somehow more frightening than shouting would have been.

When Rafael finished, the clock on the mantel had ticked through a full minute of silence, and Edith sat with her fingers steepled beneath her chin, looking at the dead fire.

"You have been running a resistance operation out of my own household," she said at last, "for eleven days. Under my roof. Alongside my cause. Without a word to me."

"Yes, ma'am."

"I have spent a season lecturing this valley on courage," Edith said, "and I was the last person in it to be trusted with any." There was no self-pity in it. It was said like a surgeon noting the location of a tumor. She turned and fixed Rafael with the look she used on hecklers at meetings — the look that could hold a hall of four hundred men without raising her voice. "Why tell me now?"

"Because I need you," Rafael said. "Because tomorrow I'm going to drive a lie past a policeman, and the lie only works if it's wearing your coat. And because Marta may already have broken, and if Brine comes for Anton before we move, I'd rather you hear it from me than from an arrest report."

Something shifted in Edith's face at that — not softening exactly. Recalculation. Rafael had spent three years learning to read her employer the way they read letters, and what they saw cross her features now was the collision of two convictions she had never before been forced to weigh against each other: the movement, which was her life's architecture, and the principle underneath it, which was its foundation. Harboring Varga could stain everything she'd built. Turning him out would hollow it.

"You understand," Edith said slowly, "that if I am seen to shield an enemy alien, every newspaper that has ever called us unpatriotic will call it proof. They will say the vote is a foreign infection. They will use him to beat us for a decade."

"Yes."

"And you ask anyway."

"I'm not asking you to shield him," Rafael said. "I'm asking you to give a speech. What Brine decides it means is his own arithmetic." They heard their own voice steady in the cold room and marveled faintly at it. "You taught me that, ma'am. Whether you meant to or not. You stand where you have a right to stand, and say what you have a right to say, and you make the man with the stick explain himself first."

Edith was silent a moment longer. Then she rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the garden going silver at its edges with the new light.

"There's a meeting of the Millbrook guild," she said, to the glass rather than to Rafael. "Saturday. I wrote them in January that I could not come, owing to the closure of the bridge. I shall write again today to say that I find, on reflection, that I can." She turned. Her jaw was set in the particular line it took before platform appearances, before arrests, before every confrontation Rafael had witnessed from the driver's seat. "Ten o'clock. We will leave at half past eight sharp, and I will instruct them to expect a motorcar party of three. Mrs. Marlowe, her secretary—" a flicker of a glance at Rafael, the future tense of the word *secretary* passing unremarked between them— "and a sick farmhand bound for the doctor. All perfectly true, incidentally. I intend to say all of it to Brine's face if he asks. I have found," and here something dry and almost merry surfaced in her voice, "that the most efficient lies contain no falsehoods whatsoever."

"He'll suspect," Rafael said. "He suspects everything."

"Of course he'll suspect. Suspicion is free. Proof costs warrants, and warrants require a magistrate, and magistrates require reasons, and I will spend the entire journey giving him nothing but reasons not to." Edith came back to the desk and began, briskly, pulling paper toward her — two letters, one to the Millbrook guild, one that she paused over before writing, addressed to the county superintendent of police, informing him, in her most courteous and devastating prose, that she wished to confirm her lawful passage across the bridge on Saturday morning and trusted there would be no interference with ordinary civilian travel. "There," she said, sanding it. "Now if Brine touches that car, he does it in front of his own superior officer's morning post."

She looked up at Rafael then, and for a moment the general dissolved and it was simply a woman in a cold study, tired, afraid in the way she permitted herself to be afraid only when no one was supposed to be watching.

"Rafael," she said. "If this goes wrong, you will be charged. Possibly imprisoned. Certainly dismissed, disgraced, marked. You know that."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you're going anyway."

"The river's melting," Rafael said. "After that there's nowhere left to run to. So I thought I'd try somewhere worth arriving."

Edith held their eyes a heartbeat longer. Whatever passed between them passed wholly, and needed no minutes recorded.

"Half past eight," she said. "Get some sleep. You'll want your hands steady tomorrow."

* * *

Rafael did not sleep. They went down through the waking household — past Marta scrubbing a step that was already clean, past Hobbs raking gravel that needed no raking, everyone industrious with the particular industry of conspirators on the eve — and out along the river path to look one last time at the water.

The ice was going. Great leads of open black water ran where Rafael had walked in moonlight five weeks ago, and the remaining floes ground against one another in the current with a sound like distant furniture being moved. Behind them, up the valley, a church bell rang the hour. Ahead, across the water, lay Millbrook, the bridge, the stove-pipe stove, the young constable, the whole apparatus of the law arranged against a borrowed coat and a speech and a lie with no falsehoods in it.

Rafael stood on the bank a long time, listening to the river dismantle the winter, and felt the strange calm settle over them completely at last — not peace, nothing so kind as peace, but the terrible clarity of a person who has run out of hidden roads and chosen, freely, the visible one. Tomorrow they would drive straight at the thing they feared, in daylight, in full view, carrying everything they loved in a single motorcar.

Somewhere upstream a lantern moved along the far bank. Nell, at her post, signaling the evening's all's-well with three slow flashes, the old laundry-line code translated to light.

Rafael raised their own lantern and answered: three slow flashes back. All's well. One more night.

Then, quickly, before the fear could find a foothold: a fourth flash. New code, invented on the spot, heart hammering as they sent it glittering across the black water.
