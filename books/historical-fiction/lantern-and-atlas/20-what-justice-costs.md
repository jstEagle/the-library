## 20. What Justice Costs

Rafael did not watch the crossing from the bank. They watched it from the middle of it.

That was the difference between the night of the lanterns and this grey, exhausted morning — on the first crossing Rafael had been a guide, all purpose and no feeling, counting cracks in the ice like a rosary. Now there was nothing left to do but stand beside Anton at the halfway mark where the river ran deepest and darkest, and feel the whole enterprise tremble under their boots like a held breath.

The thaw had come early and come hungry. Three days of warm wind off the southern hills had softened the snow into slush and turned the ice the color of old pewter, shot through with veins of black water that widened as they walked. Every few yards the river spoke to them — a groan, a tick, a long sighing crack that traveled beneath their feet like something turning over in its sleep. Nell Quist went ahead with a hazel pole, testing each step twice, her breath steaming, her braid stiff with frost. She was fourteen years old and she moved across a dying ice-road with more certainty than any constable in the county moved through his ledgers, and Rafael loved her for it with a fierce, frightened love.

"Here," she said, near the far shore where the current ran fast under the shelf. "Lightly now. Like you're stepping on eggs."

Anton Varga stepped like a man made of eggs. He had lost weight in the ice house that no amount of Marta's smuggled bread could disguise; his coat hung on him the way coats hang on scarecrows, and his hands shook — from cold, mostly, but not entirely. Five months ago he had been declared an enemy alien by men who had never seen his face. Two days ago he had nearly been arrested in a boathouse. He was thirty-six years old and he was crossing a frozen river before dawn to become, officially, nobody at all: no papers, no name that any government would honor, a man made entirely out of other people's promises.

And yet when he reached the halfway mark he stopped, against every sane instinct, and looked back at the valley — at the dark bulk of Marlowe house with its one lit window, at the smoke standing up straight from the kitchen chimney, at the pale seam of the closed bridge upstream — and his face did something complicated and young.

"I used to swim here," he said. "Do you remember? The summer of the regatta. You swore the water was warm and it was a lie."

"It was a useful lie," Rafael said. Their voice came out strange, flat, careful. They had been rehearsing this moment for weeks without knowing it, and now that it had arrived they found they had prepared nothing at all. "You got in, didn't you?"

"You pushed me."

"I encouraged you."

Nell stamped her boot impatiently, and beyond her, on the far bank, a lantern swung twice — side to side, side to side — the signal they had agreed on. Quist the ferryman stood among the alders with his boat on his shoulder where the ice ended and open water began, waiting for the man he had never met because his daughter told him to wait, which was, Rafael reflected, how most of the decent things in this war had probably gotten done.

They crossed the last twenty yards quickly, badly, half-running, and then the shore was under their feet and the boat was down on the shingle and the world had narrowed to rope and gunwale and the small black water lapping at the bow.

There was no time. That was the mercy of it. There were perhaps four minutes of grey dark left, and Brine's man posted somewhere up the road, and so everything that needed saying had to be said in the language of hands.

Anton took Rafael's face in his shaking ones. His palms smelled of creosote and ice-house rot and, faintly, impossibly, of the lake in summer — or Rafael imagined it did, which amounted to the same thing.

"The promise," he said. "It's paid."

"It's not paid," Rafael said. "Promises like that don't get paid. They get renewed." They pressed their forehead to his, briefly, hard, the way you'd press a seal into wax. "You'll write. Careless-looking letters, about weather. I'll know what they mean."

"I will write about weather," Anton said gravely, "for the rest of my life."

"And I'll read them all. It's what I do."

He laughed — one syllable, cracked down the middle — and then Nell shoved him over the gunwale and into the stern sheets and her father pushed off, oars biting, and the boat slid out onto the open channel into the last of the darkness, and became a shape, and became a suggestion, and became nothing.

Rafael stood on the foreign bank until Nell tugged their sleeve and said, in her matter-of-fact way, "We've still got to get *us* across," and the two of them went back over the dying ice in the full light of morning, slowly, with all the fear they hadn't had time for before.

* * *

The walk back up the valley took an hour and cost Rafael ten years.

By daylight the river was obscene — black leads everywhere, the ice sagging in great blistered plates, meltwater chuckling in the holes like idiots. Twice they detoured far out onto the shelving shallows where the ice was thicker, and once Nell went through to her knee and hauled herself out swearing with a fluency she must have learned from the ferryman, and they wrapped her legs in Rafael's coat and kept moving because stopping meant thinking.

At the top of the field above the boathouse they parted. Nell would go home along the towpath, muddy and explainable — a girl out poking sticks at ice, as girls do. Rafael would go back through the wicket gate and up the drive, past the gardener's bothy where old Hobbes touched his cap without meeting their eye, which meant he knew, which meant everyone knew, which meant the conspiracy of small kindnesses was now also a conspiracy of silence, and silence is a thing that gets sold.

Edith Marlowe was waiting in the hall.

She stood with her back to the tall window in her traveling suit — the good grey one she wore to speak in places that required being taken seriously — and her arms were folded, and she had clearly been standing there for some time. Behind her the morning lay across the lawn like a dropped sheet.

"Well?" she said.

"Gone," Rafael said. "Past the channel. His father-in-all-but-name has him." A beat. "Nell fell in up to the knee. She's fine. She swore magnificently."

Edith nodded once, twice, a machine working. Then, unexpectedly, she crossed the hall and gripped Rafael by both shoulders, hard enough to leave impressions, and looked at them the way she looked at hostile crowds before she began — measuring, deciding, committing.

"You look," she said, "like something the cat brought back and disowned. Go eat. Sleep if your conscience permits it, which I doubt." She released them, turned away, and then turned back, and her voice dropped into a register Rafael had only heard her use on platforms. "Underhill. Whatever comes of this — and I am not naive enough to think nothing will — I chose it. Say that to yourself tonight, whatever else you say. *She chose it.*"

Rafael said it to themselves that night, lying dressed on top of the blanket in the narrow room above the stables, listening to the wind work at the shutters. *She chose it.* It helped the way a coal helps a man freezing in an open field: locally, briefly, insufficiently.

Because sleep would not come, Rafael got up and did the honest thing, the only ritual that had ever settled them, and sat down at the little table by the lamp with the day's correspondence — three bills, a circular from the Lancashire federation, a letter from Edith's sister in Manchester full of family news that wasn't really family news but strategy wearing a floral collar. They read each one twice, the way they had read ten thousand letters in this valley, holding other people's words up to the light, and understood, with a calm that felt almost like happiness, that these might be the last letters they ever sorted as a free person.

They were right, but they were wrong about the timing. The knock came the next afternoon.

* * *

Constable Brine did not bring handcuffs. He brought a notebook, and somehow that was worse.

Rafael saw the party come up the drive from the scullery window: Brine in his best coat, which fit him badly across the shoulders as though he'd grown into ambition since it was tailored; a sergeant from the county town trailing him with the apologetic gait of men assigned to other men's grudges; and behind them both, uninvited and unbothered, Mrs. Hoskins from the post office, clutching her ledger to her chest like a hymnbook. Rafael had time to wipe their hands, take off their apron, fold it over a chair — a servant's reflexes, tidying even the end of the world — and go open the door.

"Miss Underhill." Brine removed his hat. Up close his face had the raw, scrubbed look of a man who had slept badly for a week, and there was something new in it that Rafael took a moment to identify: not triumph. Hunger still unsatisfied. "I'll be plain, because I know you appreciate plainness. On Tuesday last, a prisoner escaped lawful detention in this valley and was conveyed across the river. Persons unknown assisted him. I have statements placing a motorcar on the bridge road that morning bearing this house."

"What a coincidence," Rafael said, "that we own the only motorcar in the valley."

"Don't." He said it quietly, and the quietness was worse than shouting. "I searched your grate in the servants' hall while you were out driving Tuesday. You keep a clean grate, Miss Underhill. Very clean. Except for one corner of paper burned at the edge, with writing on it. Foreign writing. Accented characters. I have it in an envelope at the station." He opened the notebook, though he plainly knew the words by heart; he wanted to watch them land. "Obstruction of officers in the execution of their duty. False statements made to a constable, namely that a certain letter was a misdelivered circular. And aiding and abetting, at minimum, the flight of one Anton Varga, registered enemy alien. That last one goes to a military tribunal, not to the magistrates. You understand what that means better than you're pretending to."

Behind him, Mrs. Hoskins would not meet Rafael's eye. Her thumb was pressed white against the ledger's spine. She had shown him the entry — R. Underhill, one letter, unclaimed — weeks ago, and she had hated doing it, and she hated doing this, and none of it had changed anything, which was the whole lesson of the war in one postmistress's face.

"I see," Rafael said.

"Do you?" Brine closed the notebook. For one moment the hunger in him flickered into something almost like appeal, which was the most frightening thing yet. "Run. That's my professional advice, given off the record. You've got until the evening train connection at least, and I can't swear what I'd write in my report if the stable were empty tomorrow. You've been useful to this house. Be useful to yourself."

And there it was — the door, held open, exactly wide enough.

Rafael thought of running with a completeness that surprised them. Not dramatically — no midnight flight over the fields with Nell's father waiting — but the small, available version: a bag packed tonight, the motorcar legitimately on the road, a sister in Manchester, a name that could be shortened or lengthened as their mother's had been, a life of competent invisibility in some city where nobody sorted anyone's mail. They had lived invisible for thirty years. Invisibility was a warm coat. They knew every button of it.

But they thought too of Anton writing about weather for the rest of his life, and how those letters would need someone on this end who existed — whose name appeared, whose testimony counted, whose word could be weighed against a constable's report in a room where truth was, for once, the whole business at hand. If Rafael vanished, then everything Brine alleged became merely allegation, and Anton remained forever a fugitive invented by suspicion. If Rafael stayed and spoke, then the crossing became a fact with a witness attached — awkward, expensive, but *true*, and true things are heavier than convenient things and eventually settle lower.

Also — and Rafael was honest enough, alone inside themselves, to admit it — there was this: they had spent a lifetime as the margins of other people's pages. Here, at last, was a sentence that could not be written except in their own hand.

"No," Rafael said. "Thank you. But no."

Brine stared at them. "You understand what you're refusing."

"I've had a great deal of practice understanding things," Rafael said, "without anyone asking whether I did."

From the stair behind them came the click of Edith Marlowe's heels, descending at the pace she reserved for chairmen who had interrupted her. Brine squared himself. What followed was conducted in the hall, in formal tones, before witnesses: the charges recited, the summons delivered — to appear before the magistrates at the county town on the fourteenth, bail allowed upon sureties, the matter of the tribunal deferred pending examination. Edith stood with her hands folded and answered him in sentences like iron railings, and signed the surety paper with a flourish that dared him to comment, and showed him out with the courtesy of a woman closing a lid on something.

When the door was shut she did not turn around at once. She stood with her hand flat on the wood, and Rafael watched her shoulders rise and fall twice, gathering herself the way she gathered crowds.

"The fourteenth," she said at last. "Eleven days."

"Yes, ma'am."

"They'll ask you questions designed so that every honest answer sounds like guilt."

"I know."

"And you mean to answer them honestly anyway." It was not a question. She turned. Her face was doing what her face did when she was afraid for something and had decided to be angry on its behalf instead. "Underhill, listen to me. I have spent fifteen years learning how courtrooms and hearing rooms swallow women who tell the truth badly. Truth needs an advocate or it dies of tone of voice. Let me—"

"Ma'am," Rafael said, gently, and it was the first time in eight years they had interrupted her, and they were both startled enough that neither moved. "You can advocate for me. But no one can testify for me. That's rather the point. It's the first thing in my life I can't do from the margins."

Edith looked at them for a long moment. Something shifted behind her eyes — the recalculation of a strategist discovering that the person she had protected had been, all along, protecting her right back, and did not require it anymore.

"Then we prepare," she said finally, briskly, already climbing back into her cause the way other people climbed into coats. "Statements. Chronology. Names of character witnesses — Marta will speak, and the Quist girl, and half this valley owes you letters it doesn't know you read kindly. We shall make their courtroom so inconvenient that they'll wish they'd never—" She stopped herself. Drew breath. "Underhill. Eleven days. Are you afraid?"

Rafael considered it honestly, the way they read a letter — all of it, even the lines between.

"Yes," they said. "But I've been afraid since October, ma'am, and it turns out fear is survivable if you keep walking. I crossed the river twice on Tuesday."

Something in Edith's face broke open, just slightly, and re-formed into the expression Rafael had seen her wear only after the best of her rallies — the one that meant *this is why*.

* * *

That night the household gathered in the kitchen without being asked, the way families do at wakes and weddings, and nobody named which of the two this was. Marta put a dish on the table that used the good butter. Hobbes came in from the bothy with his collar still damp and sat closer to the stove than dignity allowed. Nell arrived through the scullery window because doors had ceased to be her medium, ate three rolls, and announced that she had worked out eleven different ways to smuggle messages into a courtroom if it came to that, and was talked down to four.

Rafael sat among them with their hands around a cooling cup and let it all happen — the arguments about whether Marta's cousin should be named as a witness, Hobbes's theory that Brine would be promoted out of spite regardless of outcome, Nell's demand to know, cross-examiner-style, whether Rafael was nervous — and understood that this was the payment coming due, and this was also the change it purchased. They had bought a man's freedom with their own visibility. The bill was presented in a constable's notebook. The change came back in butter and bad jokes and a fourteen-year-old rehearsing perjury at the kitchen table, and Rafael found, to their astonishment, that they were rich.

Later, alone, they set the lamp on the sill above the stable yard — angled toward the river, hooded twice, a private signal in a code of one. Somewhere beyond the dark hills, in a country that would not appear on any atlas for years, a man was eating a meal he hadn't cooked and sleeping behind a door he could lock, and if he happened to look out and see nothing, well.

Weather, Rafael thought. He'll write to me about the weather.

Eleven days. On the fourteenth they would stand up in a room full of strangers and say their name — the whole of it, the true spelling, the mother-tongue vowels and all — out loud, for the record, for the first time since they were nine years old. Their hands were steady when they pictured it. That either meant courage or meant that the part of them that feared had finally been outvoted.

Upstairs, Edith's lamp still burned. Down the drive, unseen, a constable's pipe glowed and faded at the bridge approach, patient as arithmetic.

Rafael trimmed the wick, sat back down at the table, and began — in a fair hand, on good paper, addressed to the clerk to the magistrates — to draft the truth.
