## 1. The Weight of Letters

There is an art to reading another person's mail, and I have mastered it the way a blind man masters a familiar hallway — not through ambition, but through necessity, repeated nightly until shame wore smooth as a stair rail.

The kitchen of Number 14 Corvane Row has one lamp I am allowed to use after eleven, the small brass one with the cracked chimney that sits on the third shelf beside the flour. Miss Marsh believes I take my supper at that hour. In a sense I do. My supper is her correspondence.

I want it understood that I did not begin as a thief of envelopes. When I took the position two years ago — driver, odd-hands, and general furniture — I sorted the post the way any competent servant does: bills here, invitations there, the crank letters and marriage proposals into the drawer that Miss Marsh calls, with a certain grim satisfaction, *the lunatics' cabinet*. But a man cannot handle forty letters a week without learning to read at a glance. And a glance, gentlemen, is a gateway drug.

By lamplight before dawn, the kitchen becomes a country with one citizen. The range ticks as it cools. Rain works at the window with patient fingers. And I sit with a stack of other people's secrets and learn Adeline Marsh the way her biographers never will — not from her speeches, which are armor, but from her mail, which is skin.

* * *

Tonight's haul is instructive. A bill from Halloran & Sons for printing three thousand pamphlets, marked *overdue* in red so aggressive it looks like it was applied with a bayonet. An invitation to the Halloway garden fête, written in the hand of a woman who clearly believes suffragists should be admitted to society the way one admits a mild smell — with windows opened. Two letters from northern mill towns, both beginning *Dear Madam, I hesitate to write*, which in my experience means the writer hesitated for exactly the length of one paragraph and then told everything.

And one letter, postmarked Leeds, that I read twice because its grammar was so furious it nearly set the lamp alight. A weaver named Mrs. Praed reports that her branch's funds were collected by a gentleman organizer who then failed to forward them, and she names this gentleman, and the name is one I have seen on invitation cards in the hall.

I copy such things down sometimes. Not all — I am not a magistrate — only the ones with weight. Weight is my profession, or my affliction. Everything in my life sorts itself by what can be carried.

A suitcase, for instance, can be carried forty yards at a brisk walk before the arms complain. A trunk requires two men or a trolley and witnesses. A secret weighs nothing at all in the hand and everything everywhere else; I carry several, and they have shaped my shoulders more than the driving has. As for a life — well. I carried mine out of Antwerp in 1908 inside a coat that was not mine, and I have been carrying it ever since, and it has never gotten lighter, only more familiar, the way a bad shoe molds itself to a wrong foot.

You will want to know why a man reads his employer's mail instead of minding his own. I have prepared answers for this question, none of them true. The true answer is uglier and simpler: I am invisible, and invisibility is hungry. A man whom no one sees must see everything or go mad from the nothing that sees him back.

No one sees Rafael Voss. I have tested this. Shopkeepers look through me when Miss Marsh sends me to collect her hats. Constables wave me past checkpoints without checking papers, because a man in a driver's coat with grease under his nails is weather, not a person. At the suffrage rallies, I stand at the motor with my cap in my hands while ten thousand people watch Adeline Marsh on her platform, and not one of those ten thousand could describe me an hour later. Once, a pickpocket worked my own pocket directly in front of me and apologized to me when I caught his wrist, apologizing as if he'd bumped into a hatstand.

So I read the mail. It is how I attend the world.

* * *

From the correspondence, assembled over months like a scholar reconstructing a dead language, I know these things:

That Miss Marsh's left shoe pinches, because her sister-by-marriage wrote asking after her health and mentioned the limp Adeline conceals on platforms. That she gives away most of what she earns and lies about it in her account books, inflating expenses, because charity offends her more than poverty does — poverty being merely unjust, whereas charity requires her to be thanked.

That her brother Tomas wrote to her every fortnight until two years ago, and then stopped, and that the letter announcing his death was written not by his wife but by a solicitor whose hand I recognized later on a bill of lading in the shipping office window. I remember thinking, at nineteen minutes past six on a Tuesday evening, *that is an odd firm to conduct grief through*. Then I drove her to a meeting in Bermondsey and she spoke for forty minutes on the franchise and never stumbled once, and I decided some thoughts are better abandoned at the roadside like a body.

That she receives, on average, nine threats a month, files them alphabetically in the lunatics' cabinet, and reads them aloud to herself in a flat voice when she thinks the house is asleep, the way other people do crosswords.

And that she is lonelier than I am, though she would deny it with a speech that would raise funds. This I know from the gaps in the mail, not its contents — from the fact that no letter in two years has asked after *her*, only after the Cause, which is a fine mistress but a poor correspondent. People write to the movement. Nobody writes to the woman.

I find this unbearable, and I mention it to no one, least of all her, since mentioning it would require being visible.

* * *

At twenty minutes to five the rain stopped, which meant the streets would be greasy and the Millgate Bridge cobbles treacherous, and I put down Mrs. Praed's furious letter — copied, filed in the lining of my own coat, where I keep the weighty ones — and went out to the motor.

The city before dawn belongs to men like me. Bakers, drivers, sweepers, the crews who hose the gutters: the invisible trades, all of us fluent in the same silence. We nod to each other the way ghosts might nod in a haunted house, acknowledging the shared condition. The gas lamps along Corvane Row were going down one by one, each extinguishment a small surrender of the night.

I checked the tires, the water, the fuel. I do this in the dark now, by feel, which surprises people who assume a chauffeur needs light. Darkness is my native script.

Miss Marsh keeps a schedule that would kill a horse. She rises at six, writes until eight, takes meetings until she drops, and sleeps like a woman falling downstairs — abruptly and with damage. Her world arrives to her in paper: petitions, pamphlets, threats, bills, the occasional love letter from some earnest clerk, which she answers kindly and keeps in a separate drawer, unfiled, which I confess I found more moving than anything else in two years of surveillance. Even generals keep souvenirs.

Today's schedule, reconstructed from yesterday's telephone calls and the diary she leaves open in the hall as if secrecy were a vulgar foreign custom: the Hallorans' printing dispute at ten, the platform at Guildhall at two, and Councilor Bright coming to dinner at seven, an engagement she had circled twice, which in Adeline Marsh's private hieroglyphics meant either pleasure or war.

Bright. His letters are the only ones I read standing up. There is something about his hand — fat, assured, the ink laid down like mortar money — that makes me want to check the exits. He writes *my dear Adeline* in a way that uses affection as an invoice. I have no evidence against Councilor Bright except the evidence of my spine, which has been right about men before, notably in Antwerp, where it kept me alive while better men than me became paperwork.

But that is tomorrow's problem, and tomorrow's problems, unlike tonight's letters, weigh exactly what they weigh.

I came back in, hung my coat on its hook — the coat with its hidden seam, its paper cargo, its accumulated biography of other people's troubles — and put the kettle on, because at six sharp I would hear her door above, and at half past she would descend with her collar already fighting her, and she would say *good morning, Voss* in the voice of a woman addressing a fixture, and I would say *ma'am* in the voice of a fixture agreeing, and the day would proceed on rails neither of us examines.

It is a good arrangement. I want that recorded. I eat at her table's edge, I drive her roads, I read her enemies' hearts by way of their envelopes, and in exchange I am permitted to exist adjacent to a person who burns. Most men warm themselves at smaller fires.

The kettle sang. Above me, a floorboard shifted — early, even for her — and I straightened my collar out of pure animal habit, though there was no one to see.

Then the knocker sounded at the service entrance. Three raps, soft, deliberate, wrong in every particular: nobody knocks at the kitchen door at ten past six except debt collectors and policemen, and neither of those knocks softly.

I opened it on a man I had never seen, soaked through past the dignity of rain, gaunt as a Lenten sermon, holding one hand inside his coat as if warming it — or guarding it. He looked at my face the way no one had looked at it in years, actually looking, seeing me, which alone should have warned me.

"You're Voss," he said. Not a question. "She'll be down soon. I haven't got long." He swallowed, and his eyes went past me to the stairs, to the light rising behind me, and back. "I've brought a letter from Tomas."

The name went through me like cold water through cloth. Tomas Marsh had been dead two years. I knew the date of it. I had driven her to the funeral.

And yet the man in the doorway said it again, quieter, with the terrible confidence of someone holding proof:

"From Tomas."
