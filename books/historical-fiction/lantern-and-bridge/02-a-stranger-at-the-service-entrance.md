## 2. A Stranger at the Service Entrance

Rain in this city does not fall so much as it settles in, the way a debt settles in. By the third day of it, the whole street had gone the color of old pewter, and I had stopped pretending I would make my evening rounds dry.

I was in the kitchen at Marsh House because that is where I am when I am not needed elsewhere, and being not-needed is a country I hold citizenship in. Cook had left a pot of barley soup and instructions nobody had asked for. The clock over the pantry door ticked with the smugness of clocks that have never been wrong about anything except how much time a man has.

The knock came at the service entrance, which tells you something already. Nobody important knocks at the service entrance. Important people are announced, heralded, carried up on cushions of other people's anticipation. A knock at the service entrance is the sound of a man who has decided you are the most powerful person available to him, and there is a particular weight to being somebody's most powerful person available. I have carried heavier things, but not many.

I opened the door on a rain-soaked ruin of a man.

He was tall the way famine is tall—all vertical ambition, no substance behind it—and the rain had gotten into him so thoroughly that his coat hung like something he'd stolen from a scarecrow and regretted already. His face had the gray pallor of a man who has been cold for weeks rather than minutes. But it was his eyes I noted first, because eyes are the part of a stranger you cannot mend with a fire and a bowl of soup. His were doing arithmetic. Desperate men's eyes usually do one of two things: they beg, or they calculate. His were calculating, which meant he still believed he had something to trade.

"You're the driver," he said. Not a question. "Voss."

"Miss Marsh isn't receiving."

"Good," he said. "It's you I've come for." And then he said the sentence that turned my ordinary wet evening into whatever the rest of my life would be divided against: "I carry a letter from Tomas Marsh."

Now. There are moments when the mind refuses its own conclusions, when it stands at the door of a fact and declines to admit it, like a butler turning away a caller. Tomas Marsh had been dead two years. Dead officially, dead in the newspapers, dead in the black-edged card Miss Adeline had answered two hundred times with her own hand while I sat outside the mourning house in the motorcar, engine ticking, watching her reflection in the rear glass hold itself together by main strength. I knew what his death had cost her because I had watched the bill come due in her shoulders, in the way she stopped asking me to take the long road past the harbor.

So when this drowned creature said *a letter from Tomas Marsh*, my mind did its butler act. But my hands—my hands are more honest than my mind, trained by years of reading mail that was never addressed to me—they took the oilcloth packet from him before I had decided anything.

"I don't know what you mean to me or anyone," I said, which was true and also a lie of omission large enough to park a motorcar in.

"It isn't for you to know. It's for you to keep safe until she comes home." He looked past me into the kitchen with the particular hunger of a man who has slept under hedges. "Voss. Please. There are men looking for me. They look like gentlemen. That's the worst thing about them."

Here is an inventory of everything I knew in that moment: the handwriting on the packet, visible through the fold, was looped and slanted in a way I had seen once before, on the letters Miss Adeline kept in the drawer of her writing desk, the ones she reread the first year after the funeral and then locked away. I knew it the way a man knows a tune he heard through a wall. And here is what I did with that knowledge: nothing sensible.

* * *

You must understand something about me before you judge what happened next. I have spent my life learning how not to be seen. A driver learns it; a driver is furniture with hands. Servants, drivers, porters, the men who stand at doors—we are the wallpaper of the important world, and I had made such a study of invisibility that I could walk through a room of my own acquaintances and leave no impression whatsoever. It is a useful skill. It is also, I can now report, a kind of illness. You practice vanishing long enough and you begin to believe you deserve it.

A stranger claiming letters from the dead should have been sent away. Sent away, or fetched the police, or at minimum made to stand in the rain another ten minutes while I thought clearly. Instead I stood aside and let him in, because some part of me—the part that reads other people's letters by lamplight, the part that has been starving politely for years on secondhand lives—wanted the answer more than it wanted safety.

"In," I said. "Through the wash-house. Don't touch anything."

He nearly wept at the word *don't touch anything*, the way a man does when rules mean someone still expects him to be a person who follows them.

I put him in the wash-house off the scullery, where the copper boiler still held heat from the morning washing and the steam fogged the little window. I gave him soup because Cook's instructions, though unsolicited, had been emphatic. He ate like a man relearning the procedure. Between spoonfuls he talked, low, the way men talk when they have been silent too long and silence has started tasting like blood.

"They watch the front of the house," he said. "They've watched it since Tuesday. I circled through the mews twice. You didn't see me because I'm—" and here he almost smiled, "—apparently good at not being seen too. We're a guild, you and I."

"We are not a guild," I said.

"No. Well." He held out his cup for more soup, having apparently decided that a man who feeds you will not immediately betray you, which shows you how far hunger simplifies philosophy. "My name is Nils. Nils Ferro. I was deck crew on the *Corvidene*. That's where I met Mr. Tomas, before."

"Before he died," I said, testing the sentence like ice on a pond.

Nils looked at me for a long moment, and his calculating eyes did their sums, and he arrived at some figure he liked, because he said, quietly: "Before he stopped dying, Voss. Which is a different matter entirely, and one you'll want to hear about, only not here and not tonight. Tonight you'll just keep the letter and keep your mouth shut and decide whether you're the sort of man who carries things for living people or only buries them for dead ones."

That was cruel, and accurate, which is the worst combination a sentence can have.

* * *

I want to tell you about the handwriting, because the handwriting is where dread became fascination, and fascination is far more dangerous.

Tomas Marsh had written his sister perhaps forty letters in his life. Miss Adeline kept every one. In the early days of my employment—I had been with the household eight months when he died—she used to read them aloud sometimes in the motorcar without meaning to, fragments, laughing at some joke of his phrasing, and so I absorbed the hand of him the way you absorb weather: the tall ascenders, the way his *r* trailed off unfinished, as if he always expected to be interrupted, the strange flat crossbar on his *t* like a shelf holding something up.

The packet in my hands had all of it. Every trait. And more than that, it had the small private economy of a man writing to family: the fold done in thirds with the crease pressed sharp, the direction written on the reverse in the same hand instead of trusting it to a clerk. You cannot counterfeit intimacy of that particular kind. Forgery copies shapes. It cannot copy habit.

Unless, said the voice of cold sense in me, unless the forger had studied those same letters in that same drawer, and had hands patient enough, and reasons terrible enough.

I sat at the kitchen table with the packet unopened in front of me—for I had no right to open it, and both of us at that table, if you counted Nils at the wash-house door, knew exactly what kind of right I claimed anyway—and I listened to the rain and the clock and the small sounds of a starving man warming himself in my landlady's washtub territory.

Miss Adeline was due back at nine. A committee dinner in Hampstead. She would come in smelling of other people's cigar smoke, tired in the specific way she was tired after nights spent being charming to men who called her *madam* in the tone they reserved for weather. She would hang her coat herself, refusing help on principle. She would say good night to me the way she always did—*thank you, Mr. Voss, that will be all*—the formal distance of a woman determined not to need anyone, and I would drive the car around back and sit in it a while before going in, as I always did, because those thirty seconds are the closest thing to privacy either of us owns.

And tomorrow, or perhaps tonight, if the letter broke her the way I feared it might, everything about that liturgy would end.

Because here is what I had already understood, sitting there with the dead man's handwriting under my thumb: whoever Nils Ferro was, whatever the letter contained, it had not been sent through any post. It had been carried by hand, across whatever distance, by a man frightened enough to sleep rough and circle through mews twice. Letters travel safely all the time. It is only letters that can hurt someone who require a courier willing to drown for them.

And the men who watched the house since Tuesday—whoever they were, however gentlemanly their outlines—they were watching for the letter, or for Nils, or for the connection between the two. Which meant Miss Adeline was already inside some machinery she hadn't seen yet, turning slowly, all its teeth oiled and patient.

I folded the packet into the inner pocket of my coat, against my chest, where I carry things that matter. It weighed nothing. Documents weigh nothing; it is what they do afterward that has mass.

Nine o'clock. Headlamps swinging through the gate, throwing the rain into brief golden disorder.

I went out to bring the car around, and behind me in the dark of the wash-house, Nils Ferro whispered—so low I nearly missed it, meant for himself more than me:

"She doesn't know I'm alive either. God help us both when she finds out what her brother became."

The kitchen door closed. The motorcar coughed awake. And I drove around to the front of Marsh House to collect my employer, carrying a dead man's handwriting against my heart, and rehearsing, for the first time in my careful invisible life, the impossible art of telling the truth to someone who outranks me in every way that matters.
