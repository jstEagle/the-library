## 3. What the Dead Write

I knew something was wrong before I knew what it was, because Rafael Voss has only two speeds—stillness and less stillness—and when he came into my study that evening he achieved a third: a kind of hovering, like a moth that had mistaken a lamp for the moon and couldn't decide whether to be ashamed.

"Mrs. Marsh," he said. "There's a matter."

"A matter." Rafael does not waste words on pleasantries, so a noun as vague as *matter* was practically a speech.

"In the kitchen."

"In the kitchen," I repeated, setting down my pen. "Is the matter bleeding? On fire? Do we have a pig in the house?"

"No, ma'am." A pause so long I could hear the clock in the hall deciding whether to bother ticking. "It's about your brother."

I will tell you honestly what I thought in that first instant, because a woman in my position learns to inventory her own cowardice: I thought, *the bill collectors again*. Tomas had died owing money to half of London, or rather, having died owing it, the debts had become my inheritance alongside his handwriting, which we never could read, and his laugh, which we always could. Two years is long enough that grief becomes furniture. You stop bumping into it. But someone knocking at the door asking to be paid for a wreath—well. That knocks the furniture over.

"Show me," I said.

* * *

The man in my kitchen was not a debt collector. Debt collectors come dry. This one sat where Rafael pointed him—on the stool by the stove, the servant's seat, though he didn't seem to notice or mind—and dripped. His coat had given up its waterproofing sometime around the reign of Victoria. His face had the particular gauntness of a man who has been eating irregularly on purpose, because regular meals require an address.

He stood when I entered, swayed, sat back down.

"Mrs. Marsh." His voice was gravel that had been rained on. "Nils Ferren. I knew your brother. In—" He stopped, glanced at Rafael, and something passed between the two men that I could not read and did not enjoy. "In Antwerp," he finished.

"Tomas died in Antwerp," I said. "Two years ago this March."

"Yes, ma'am." Nils reached into his coat with the care of a man who had already been shot once over what he carried. The envelope he laid on the table was rain-stained but dry inside; he had kept it against his chest, I'd realize later, the way other men keep a Bible or a photograph. "Not everything that dies stays dead. Some things just get buried shallow."

Rafael made a small sound—not quite a cough, not quite a warning—and I picked up the letter.

I would know that hand anywhere. It hadn't improved with age or distance; if anything, death had done wonders for Tomas's penmanship, which had gone from a drunken scrawl to merely a hurried one, the letters of a man writing fast because being caught writing was worse than being caught doing anything else.

*Addie.*

Nobody else had ever called me that. My mother, dead these twenty years. And Tomas.

*By the time you read this I'll either be coming to you or gone somewhere you can't follow, and I'm sorry for both. What I told you happened in Antwerp wasn't true. There was no fever, no accident, no body for you to bury—we buried a name, that's all. I died so that some other men could go on living comfortably, because they wanted me dead and I preferred the paperwork version.*

*Here is what matters, and I need you to believe it even though you'll want to burn this letter instead: Harrowgate, Vane, and yes—Bright. Their money isn't theirs. It comes from Kessler & Rath, through three accounts I kept the books for, and I can prove every pound of it. They bought your opponents the way other men buy coal—by the ton, delivered quietly. Everything you've built, Addie, they've built a counterfeit of it beside you, and they mean to use it to bury you at the moment you're closest to winning.*

*I have the ledgers. Pages of them. Names, dates, amounts. They know I have them. That's why I stay dead.*

*Nils will explain. Trust the driver—he reads everything anyway, and he's still here.*

I put the letter down. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I had expected them to shake. Perhaps shock is just the body's way of declining to participate.

"Councilor Bright," I said, "is godfather to my niece."

"Yes, ma'am," said Nils.

"He gave the address at my mother's grave."

"I wouldn't know about that, ma'am."

"He—" I stopped. There was nothing to finish that sentence with that wouldn't sound like a woman arguing with a letter. I looked at Rafael instead, standing by the door with his cap in his hands, wearing the expression of a man who has read a thing out of duty and now must watch its owner read it out of catastrophe.

"You read this," I said to him.

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you let me walk down here blind."

"It wasn't mine to summarize, Mrs. Marsh." Which was Rafael for *I wasn't going to be the one who killed your brother twice.*

* * *

I went back to the study and lit the lamp and read the letter four more times, and each time the same sentence rose up out of the page like a stone in shallow water: *they mean to use it to bury you at the moment you're closest to winning.*

We are close. I have given eleven years to this—the meetings in church basements, the deputations that end in police lines, the newspaper editors who print our names with quotation marks around *ladies*. Eleven years, and in six weeks the Reform Committee votes, and Bright has promised me—promised me, with his hand on my shoulder, smelling of brandy and good intentions—that this time the home rule clause would reach the floor.

And the whole while he was taking Kessler money through accounts kept by my brother.

I did not cry. I want that recorded somewhere, in case anyone writes about this later: I did not cry. What I did was worse—I laughed, once, briefly, at the sheer administrative thoroughness of it. They had forged a death certificate. They had let me stand at a graveside in the rain while a vicar who had never met Tomas spoke about a life cut short. Someone had chosen the flowers. Someone had paid for them. And the man being mourned had presumably been a mile away, counting his escape money, and had either not watched or—

No. I won't wonder whether he watched. That way lies madness, and I haven't the schedule for it.

What I felt, underneath the fury, in the flooded cellar of myself where I keep the things I don't spend time on, was something smaller and more humiliating: *he chose them over me.* Not the blackmailers—those he chose against, obviously, at the cost of everything. He chose silence over me. Two years of my writing letters to a dead man's solicitor. Two years of paying for a grave. Two years of being the poor brave sister, the one who kept working after the loss, which had done more for my public sympathy than any speech I ever gave—and God forgive me, I had used his death that way, and it had worked, and now it turned out the death was a fiction I'd been unknowingly performing in, a widow in a play I never auditioned for.

My grief had been currency. Someone had counterfeited it.

When I came back to the kitchen, Nils had been fed—bread, cold mutton, tea—which meant Rafael had made a decision without consulting me, which meant Rafael had decided the man would live. I found this oddly reassuring. Rafael's judgments are like his silences: rare, and usually correct.

"He needs to sleep," Rafael said. "There's a room off the coach house."

"He needs to talk," I said. "Then he can sleep. Mr. Ferren—the ledgers. Where are they?"

"Close," Nils said. "Not close enough to find and close enough to move. That's deliberate, ma'am. Your brother's rules. Never keep the proof where you sleep." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "But there's a ship. Thursday tide, the *Meridian*, bound for Rotterdam. There's a man aboard, a journalist named Verhoeven, who'll print what England won't. Tomas wants the pages on that ship."

"Then give them to me and I'll take them to the newspapers myself."

The look Nils and Rafael exchanged this time was longer, and I understood it entirely, and I hated both of them for it.

"Ma'am," Nils said gently, the way one speaks to the recently bereaved, which I suppose I technically was again, "you're followed everywhere. Every constable on your street knows your hat. If Bright's men learn those pages exist, they'll come through walls to burn them, and they'll do it legally, mostly. No. They have to travel quiet. They have to travel"—he gestured at Rafael, at the cap, at the whole gray invisible architecture of the man—"like he travels."

"They have to travel," I translated slowly, "with Rafael."

"With somebody like Mr. Voss, yes, ma'am."

I looked at my driver. He stood there being unremarkable, which is his gift; I have watched him park outside a hall full of hostile reporters for three hours and afterward no one could describe him beyond *some fellow*. I had never once wondered what he thought about anything. Now I needed him to think about everything—routes, checkpoints, tides, the specific weight of paper versus the patience of policemen—and the needing sat in my chest like a swallowed coin.

I am not good at needing people. I have practiced at it the way one practices the violin without a bow, all the posture and none of the sound. My whole life is built so that the needing goes one direction: hundreds of women need me, and I need the cause, and the cause needs nothing so small as a person.

"Thursday," I said. "Three days."

"There'll be checkpoints," Rafael said. Quietly, the way he says everything, but I heard something new under it—a wire pulled taut. "Millgate Bridge will have the police post. And the quay."

"Can it be done?"

He considered the question with the seriousness other men reserve for scripture.

"It can be attempted, Mrs. Marsh."

"That is not what I asked."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

He left me at the foot of the stairs, and I climbed toward bed knowing I wouldn't sleep, and at the landing I stopped, because through the window I could see the coach house, and one light burning in it, and the shadow crossing it—my driver, awake at midnight, already planning.

Tomas's postscript came back to me. *Trust the driver—he reads everything anyway, and he's still here.*

Still here. As opposed to whom? As opposed to my brother, who faked his own death rather than fight; as opposed to Bright, who never faked anything except friendship. Still here, sweeping the step, reading my mail, keeping my secrets from me until keeping them became cruelty.

"Well, Rafael," I said to the window, to the shadow, to nobody. "Let's see what the dead have taught you."

Down in the coach house, the light moved, and did not go out.
