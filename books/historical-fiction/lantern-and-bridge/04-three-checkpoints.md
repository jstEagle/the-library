## 4. Three Checkpoints

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that happens in the mind of a man who has spent his life carrying things for other people. Most men count money or hours. I count weight, distance, and the probability of being noticed. It is a grim little ledger, but it has kept me employed and mostly unbroken for thirty-one years.

By the time the rain stopped on Thursday evening, I had already measured the letter twice—once by length and breadth, once by what it would cost us if it were found. Four pages of Tomas Marsh's handwriting describing where bodies were buried, some of them metaphorical, none of them his. Adeline had read it three times, gone very pale in the way that made her look more like her portraits, and then said the only sensible thing anyone had said all week:

"It has to reach the *Corinthia* before she sails."

The *Corinthia* carried mail and freight to Amsterdam, and aboard her, per arrangement with a Dutch journalist whose name Nils would not speak above a whisper, the evidence would be printed where no English court could smother it. She sailed Saturday morning, with the tide. Which gave us one day and one night to move four pages across a city that had recently developed an appetite for stopping us.

I did not say any of this aloud. Instead I did what I always do: I took out my notebook, drew a line down the middle of a page, and began making lists. Adeline watched me do it with an expression I have learned to read as respect delivered secondhand—she approved of the notebook but would sooner die than say so.

"The route," she said. "Walk me through it."

So I did. And because this chapter of my life deserves an honest accounting, I will walk you through it too, the way I walked them through it that night in her study, with the curtains drawn and the fire banked low and Nils Ostrand dripping nervously onto a chair he seemed afraid might report him.

* * *

"Three checkpoints," I said, and tapped the map. "If it were two, I'd call it difficult. Three means someone designed this deliberately. Someone wants anything moving toward the harbor to pass through three separate pairs of hands."

The first tap landed on Millgate Bridge, the iron span over the river where the road narrows to a single lane each way. "Police post. Two constables on days, one on nights, rotated weekly so they don't make friends with smugglers. There's a lamp at the north end and a hut, which means warmth, which means whoever's posted there will be standing in the doorway rather than walking the bridge. They stop carts when the mood takes them—market days especially, looking for untaxed goods. They don't stop drivers much, because drivers are furniture." I looked up. "That's not self-pity. That's tactical intelligence."

Adeline almost smiled. Almost. She had been doing that thing all evening where grief and fury took turns holding her face, and I suspected the smile was losing more often than not.

"You know the constables?" she asked.

"I know the one who'll be there tomorrow night. Constable Hobbs. He knows my face because I drive past him forty times a week and once helped him wrestle a runaway horse off the bridge. He thinks I'm a decent, dull man. This is the most valuable professional relationship I have."

Nils shifted in his chair. He was a long, grey sort of man, the kind whose body seemed to apologize for taking up space, though his hands never quite stopped moving. "And if Hobbs is replaced? Rotations change."

"Then we adapt. That's what the list is for." I moved my finger south along the river. "Second checkpoint. The shipping office on Cordwainer Quay. Not police—worse. Private guards hired by Halloran & Vye, which is the shipping concern owned by cousins of the men Tomas says are funding our respected opposition. The guards answer to nobody with a uniform, which means no paperwork, no procedure, and no superior to complain to if you get a broken wrist. They search freight going out because Halloran & Vye have been burned before—someone shipped documents against them once, years ago, and they've been jumpy ever since."

"That would be the time Tomas's friends tried it before," Adeline said quietly.

Everyone looked at her. She was sitting very straight, turning her mother's ring around her finger, and I understood she had just done something painful: she had connected her brother's silence to a history she'd only half-known about.

"Possibly," I said, because certainty wasn't mine to give. "Either way, they search. But here's their weakness—they search cargo, not people. A guard who pokes through crates feels thorough. A guard who pats down a driver feels like a criminal. Class is a wonderful thing if you're willing to exploit it, and after thirty-one years of being on the receiving end, I am nothing if not willing."

Third tap: the customs shed at the end of the quay, squatting beside the water like a grey toad. "Customs officers. Actual law, actual authority, actual forms. You'd think that makes them the safest checkpoint, because lawful men follow rules. It doesn't. It makes them the most dangerous, because they're the only ones who can legally hold you indefinitely while they 'verify' something. And—" I hesitated, then decided they needed the whole ledger. "And Nils is known to at least one of them."

Nils went still, which for him was louder than shouting.

"How known?" Adeline asked.

"I came through in March," Nils said, staring at the table. "They questioned me. Wrote my name down. Officer named Prue. He has a memory like a flypaper. If he sees me within a mile of that quay—"

"Then you won't be within a mile of that quay," I said. "Not at the shed. We'll plan accordingly."

I could feel Adeline watching me as I wrote, and I knew what she was seeing: not the driver, not the courier, but something she hadn't budgeted for. A man building a machine out of timings and human weaknesses. It occurs to me now that she may have found it frightening. Good. Fear keeps people from volunteering for things they shouldn't.

* * *

We argued for two hours. I want that recorded, because afterward, when people told the story, it became Rafael the inevitable, Rafael who never doubted. In truth I laid out four plans and dismantled three of them myself before anyone else could.

Plan One: send the pages by ordinary post to Amsterdam. Dismantled in under a minute—mail to foreign destinations was already being opened, Tomas's own letter warned of it, and besides, the *Corinthia*'s mail bag would be sealed at the shipping office. Checkpoint Two would swallow it whole.

Plan Two: hire a stranger to carry it. Dismantled by Adeline, who said, with terrible flatness, "Anyone whose price we can pay, Bright's men can double." Hearing Councilor Bright's name come out of her mouth like that—no honorific, no warmth left—told me more about how her week was going than anything else.

Plan Three: split the pages among several couriers. I killed this one myself. Four pages, four chances to fail, and the failure of any one page ruined all of them. Also, I confess, a less noble reason: I did not want to share the job. Some men hoard gold. I hoard responsibility. It's cheaper and you always know where it's kept.

Which left Plan Four, the ugly one. I carry it myself, sewn into something boring, driving my normal rounds, crossing all three checkpoints in a single night run timed to put me at the quay within an hour of the tide. No accomplices visible. Nils kept far away from the customs shed and preferably indoors, sedated if necessary—I was only half joking about that.

"It should be someone expendable," Nils said, into the silence after I finished. He meant himself. To his credit—and I will defend this man later, when others condemn him—he believed it. "You're worth ten of me to her."

"Nobody in this room is expendable," Adeline said, in the voice she used on crowds of ten thousand. Then, softer, to me, in the voice she used on perhaps one person a decade: "Is it truly possible? All three?"

I looked at the map. The bridge, the office, the shed. Three gates, three kinds of men, three ways to fail. And underneath the fear, somewhere in the old machinery of me, a cold clean feeling was settling into place—the feeling I get when a load is balanced just right, when every strap is tight and the weight sits exactly where it should. Planning is the only prayer I've ever believed in.

"Yes," I said. "Possible. Not safe. Those are different words, and I want everyone clear on the difference."

"They're different words," Adeline agreed. "I've spent twenty years explaining that to newspapers."

It was nearly midnight when Nils finally left, slipping out through the garden gate like a man rehearsing for a life of it. I walked back around to the mews, and stood in the wet dark next to the motorcar, and let myself do the thing I never do in front of witnesses: I counted what I might lose.

My situation. My rounds, my routines, the small warm invisibility I had built like a nest out of other people's indifference. Possibly my liberty—customs men can invent charges the way spiders invent webs, effortlessly and without much originality. Possibly more than liberty; Halloran & Vye's men did not strike me as the type to stop at a broken wrist.

Against which: four pages. The truth about a dead man alive, and living men who deserved the word instead.

I got out my notebook one last time and wrote the schedule in full, hour by hour, and beneath it a line I did not show anyone:

*Three doors. One pair of hands. Mine.*

Then I went in to check the tire repair kits in the boot, because tomorrow I would need a convincing reason to be carrying them, and a man's cover story starts with his luggage.

I did not sleep much. Planners rarely do; the map runs behind the eyes all night, gates opening and shutting, and somewhere past three in the morning I realized there was a flaw in my beautiful schedule—a small one, a scheduling seam, the kind of flaw that seems trivial until it isn't.

Hobbs's shift at Millgate ended at eleven. The shipping office guards changed at midnight. And the customs shed—that was where the timing ran thinnest, where I would arrive with minutes, not hours, in hand, relying on everything going to plan.

And nothing, in my considerable experience, ever goes entirely to plan.

Which is why, when I heard the garden gate click again at half past four in the morning—a sound I had catalogued hours earlier as Nils leaving, and had not expected to hear reversed—I set down the notebook, picked up the poker, and went to see which part of my arithmetic had just walked back through the door.
