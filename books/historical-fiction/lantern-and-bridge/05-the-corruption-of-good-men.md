## 5. The Corruption of Good Men

I had rehearsed cruelty in the carriage the whole way to Bright's office. Not the doing of it—the enduring of it. There is a difference, and I have built a life on knowing the difference between the wound one inflicts and the wound one accepts with a straight spine.

Rafael drove me. He did not ask where we were going, which was his particular genius; he simply knew, the way a dog knows before the thunder does. When I told him—*Councilor Bright, Chancery Row, and wait for me, and if I am more than an hour, come in without knocking*—his eyes found mine in the rearview glass for exactly one second longer than a driver's eyes should.

"An hour," he said, as though confirming a delivery address.

"Less, if he disappoints me. He has never disappointed me before." I heard how that sounded even as I said it, and I despised myself for the fondness still lodged in my voice like a stone in a boot.

Tomas's letter sat against my ribs, folded into eighths inside my glove. I had read it so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. *My death was purchased, Addie. Not by you, not for you. By men whose names you would know, whose hands you have shaken, whose money you may have taken without knowing its smell.* My brother wrote blackmail the way he once wrote Christmas lists—cheerfully, exhaustively, with an accountant's precision. Four names. Two subscriptions to party funds. One line that I had read perhaps forty times: *Ask Bright about the Harrow Lane accounts. Watch his left hand when you do.*

I did not know why the left hand. Tomas had always trusted details over explanations.

* * *

Chancery Row kept its respectability the way old men keep their hair—thinly, and with visible effort. Bright's office smelled of beeswax and cigar, the two smells of English power, and he rose from behind his desk with both arms open as though I were a returning daughter rather than a woman who had once thrown a milk jug at him over the question of whether the movement should endorse temperance.

We had lost the jug argument. I mention this so it is understood that Bright wins things, quietly, and that I have a history of mistaking his charm for agreement.

"Adeline." He took my hands. His grip was warm and dry and entirely calibrated. "You look exhausted. The campaign?"

"The campaign." I let him settle me into the leather chair across from him. On the wall behind him hung a photograph of the two of us at the Keswick rally, 1909, both of us mid-laugh, youngish and certain. I looked at that photograph the way one looks at a receipt for something one can no longer afford. "Edmund, I've come about money."

"Money." He sat back down, folding his hands over his waistcoat, and smiled the smile he used on donors' widows. "You've never once come to me about money in eleven years. You consider it beneath us both."

"I consider asking for it beneath us. Asking about it is another matter entirely." I unfolded the glove on my lap, though I did not take out the letter. Its presence was enough. It had become, in three days, less a document than an organ—one more thing inside me that beat. "The Harrow Lane accounts. The winter subvention. Two thousand pounds that arrived in November through a solicitor named Pell, and another eight hundred in February through no solicitor at all, just a bank draft with a name I cannot find anywhere in the register of companies."

Something happened in the room. Nothing one could point to. The fire did not go out; the clock did not stop. But Bright's face performed warmth while his eyes went elsewhere—to the door, to the window, to some internal ledger—and I understood that I was watching a man count exits.

"You've been talking to journalists," he said lightly. "They put these fancies in your head."

"I've been talking to my brother."

The pause lasted half a heartbeat. If I had not been watching for it—if I had not spent thirty years learning to read men who thought themselves unreadable—I would have missed the way his left hand lifted from his knee, touched the desk blotter, and flattened there. *Watch his left hand when you do.*

"Tomas is dead," Bright said gently. Gently! As though gentleness were a coat he could put on over arithmetic.

"Yes," I said. "He writes anyway. It's a family failing. We never learned to leave things buried—not causes, not brothers, not questions."

"Adeline." He came around the desk. This was a technique of his, I realized—not moving toward me but repositioning himself between me and the photograph on the wall, as if our younger laughing selves might testify against him. "Whatever you've been shown, whoever showed it to you—they want to hurt the movement through you. You know how these men operate. A forged letter, a manufactured scandal, timed to the by-election—"

"His handwriting, Edmund. I changed his nappies before I changed the world. I know his hand the way you know your own signature."

"Then someone practiced it." He said it smoothly. That was the horror of it—he said everything smoothly, each lie fitted to the last like joinery, and only his eyes worked independently, flicking from my face to my gloves to the window to the clock. Calculating. I had seen those same eyes over subscription ledgers a hundred times and called it prudence. "You are tired, and grieving twice—once for him, once for what they're making you believe. Let me send you home. Let me look into this quietly. Quietly, Adeline. That's all I ask."

"There," I said. "You've done it again."

"Done what?"

"That. *Let me.* Let me look, let me handle, let me protect. Eleven years I thought it was kindness." I stood. The chair legs made a small ugly sound on the floorboards. "The two thousand pounds, Edmund. Where did it come from?"

"Concerned gentlemen."

"Name one."

"Names are confidential in matters of—"

"—matters of blackmail, yes, I've read the letter, I know precisely what matters they're confidential in." My voice had gone very level, which anyone who knows me knows is worse than shouting. "Your friends bought my brother's death. They staged it—there was a body, Edmund, there was a funeral, I spoke at it—and then they held the fact of his survival over him like a knife, and they used the arrangement to fund the very men we campaign against. Halloran's committee. The men who keep the question of the vote safely dead in committee every session. Our enemies drink from the same purse as our friends, and you—" I stopped. I had meant to say *you knew*, but the truth was worse and I could see it in his face, in the terrible patience of it: he hadn't known. He had suspected. And suspicion, given enough years and enough comfort, becomes a kind of policy.

"You didn't know," I said slowly. "But you didn't want to."

Bright looked at me then—really looked, the calculation dropping away for one unguarded second—and what was underneath was not guilt. It was fatigue. That frightened me more than guilt would have. Guilt implies a standard broken.

"Adeline," he said quietly, "do you know how much the winter shelter cost? The one you insisted on, the one for the women turned out of the mills? Nine hundred pounds. Do you know where nine hundred pounds comes from, in this country, when women aren't permitted to earn it honestly? It comes from men. All of it. Every shilling of every march and every pamphlet and every lawyer's fee when they arrest us—it comes out of the pockets of men like me, and men worse than me, and the only question worth asking is whether the hands that give it are clean or merely washed." He spread those hands, palms up—an old conjurer's gesture, showing them empty. "You never asked where the money came from in eleven years. Why begin now, when the asking costs more than the not-knowing ever did?"

There it was. The knife, offered handle-first, and I had no defense except the truth, which was that he was right and being right made it filthier.

"Because I didn't know there was anything to ask," I said. "That's the answer, Edmund. Ignorance isn't innocence, but it isn't complicity either. Complicity starts the moment one chooses not to look." I took the letter from my glove—not to give it to him, only to hold it, the way one holds a torch in a dark room to see how much darkness there is. "And I am looking now."

His left hand moved again. Toward the letter. Quick, flat, almost gentle—a man reaching to steady a candle—and I stepped back before I understood I'd decided to.

"Don't," I said.

"Adeline—"

"If that hand touches this letter, I will scream this building down, and unlike you, Edmund, I have nothing left to lose by noise." I folded it away. My heart was going like a girl's and my voice stayed level, and I remember thinking, absurdly, that Rafael would have noted both facts without comment and filed them somewhere useful. "The ship sails tomorrow morning. I don't expect you to understand what's on it. I only ask that whatever you do next, you do it remembering that I know your face, and your hand, and your signature, and your solici—your *concerned gentlemen*. Goodbye, Edmund."

I walked out. He did not follow me to the door, which told me one thing, and the silence behind me, which told me another.

* * *

Rafael had the car warm and the engine running, and he watched me come down the steps of Chancery Row the way he watches everything—with the total attention of a man who expects the world to require catching.

"Well?" he said, when I was inside.

"He'll warn them." I said it aloud and heard how true it was, the way one hears a bell after it has struck. "Not tonight. Tonight he'll sit in his office and tell himself he's protecting the movement, and by ten o'clock he'll have convinced himself, and then he'll make one telephone call, very reasonably, to a concerned gentleman." I looked at the letter in my lap, then at the rain starting on the windscreen, each drop catching the streetlamp like a small lit thing. "We have until his conscience finishes its arithmetic, Rafael. Perhaps four hours."

Rafael said nothing for a long moment. Then he put the car into gear, and in the mirror his eyes met mine, calm as deep water.

"Then we shouldn't waste them planning politely," he said, and drove us toward Millgate Bridge.
