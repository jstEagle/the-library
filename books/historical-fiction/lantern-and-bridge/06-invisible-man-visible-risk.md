## 6. Invisible Man, Visible Risk

There is a particular kind of arithmetic a man learns when he has spent his life being overlooked. A constable's glance lasts one and a half seconds. A doorman's lasts less. A woman of quality looking through you to the motorcar behind you: half a second, if that. I had spent thirty-one years collecting these numbers the way other men collect stamps or debts, and I had never once imagined they would be worth anything.

Now they were worth a ship.

* * *

The morning after we mapped the route, I rose at four and did what I always did: I loaded the delivery rounds. Adeline's correspondence went into the leather satchel — the innocent mail, invitations and committee minutes and one frankly unhinged sonnet from a gentleman in Leeds who had mistaken her pamphlets for a courtship. On top of it, wrapped in oilcloth, I laid the packet. Tomas Marsh's letters. The ledger pages Nils had carried across half of Europe sewn into the lining of a coat he no longer owned, having traded it for bread somewhere outside Hamburg. The photographs. Enough paper to hang three councilmen, a shipping magnate, and very likely me.

It weighed almost nothing. Four hundred grams, give or take. I weighed everything now; it was becoming a habit I suspected would outlive the week.

"You're taking it *out*?" Nils hissed from the doorway. He had slept — if that was the word — in the coach house, on a mattress that had last known service when Queen Victoria was alive. In daylight he looked worse than he had by lamplight, which I would not have believed possible. Gaunt is a polite word for it. He looked like a man the earth had tried to reclaim once already and would be coming back for.

"I'm taking a copy," I said.

"There is no time for copies—"

"There is time for exactly one thing, and that is doing this correctly." I wound the oilcloth tight. "If I'm stopped tomorrow and they take the packet, your brother-in-law's evidence dies with my morning. So today the packet rides with me on an ordinary round, in an ordinary satchel, among ordinary letters, and I learn where it fits and where it doesn't. Tomorrow there will be no learning. Only doing."

He did not like it. Everything about Nils disliked everything about the plan; fear had eaten his patience down to the quick. But he had no better idea, and more importantly, no license, no face anyone in this city knew, and no talent for stillness. He was a man built for running. Unfortunately, running was precisely what would kill us.

* * *

The tire repair kit was my own invention, and I was absurdly proud of it.

The motor required maintenance the way all motors do, and I had cultivated over four years a reputation as a fellow who fussed. Patches, vulcanizing rubber, a hand pump, chalk, spare valves — the kit lived in a wooden box under the rear seat, and no one in the history of motoring had ever wanted to look inside another man's tire repair kit. It is the most boring object ever devised. Boredom, I have found, is the best lock in the world.

I cut the ledger pages into quarters. Folded, each quarter sat flat against the underside of the box's lid, beneath a glued layer of felt that already lived there legitimately, protecting the tools from rattling. A man could open the box, count the patches, find nothing but the honest debris of bad roads. Reassembling the pages afterward would take twenty minutes with the numbering Tomas had thoughtfully provided, because of course a blackmailed engineer turned fugitive numbers his evidence. Men like Tomas trust systems. It is why they are so easy to destroy and so difficult to silence.

The coat was simpler. My driving coat, the grey one, had a lining loose at the left shoulder since the winter before last. I had never mended it because a mended coat gets noticed by the woman who pays for the mending, and questions are heavier than paper. Now I unpicked six inches of seam myself, laid the photographs flat against the canvas, and stitched them back in with the big clumsy stitches of a man who hates sewing. If someone searched me, they would find a coat. The stitches were ugly enough to be true.

"Again," I told Nils, and walked the route of Millgate Bridge checkpoint while he played constable, badly.

"Stop the motor," he said, in an accent that put him somewhere between Prussia and parody.

"You wouldn't say it like that."

"How would you know? You've never been stopped."

*I've never been noticed,* I didn't say. There was no need to be cruel. He was frightened enough for both of us and then some.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being invisible: it works only while you agree with it.

For thirty-one years I had agreed. I had agreed so thoroughly that I sometimes wondered whether I cast reflections at all. Shopgirls served the person behind me. Waiters refilled the glass next to mine. Once, at a funeral — not a fictional one, a real one, my mother's — the priest asked me twice whether I was family or hired staff, and I said staff both times because it was easier than explaining that family was the wrong category entirely.

Invisibility is a trade. You give up being seen, and in exchange you go everywhere. Through service doors. Past police posts. Into rooms where decisions are made about people who will never know you stood in the corner holding the coats. Adeline paid me wages, but the truth is she paid me in access — she let me read her world, and I let her believe she was alone in it. It was the closest thing to intimacy either of us could afford.

Now the trade had changed its terms. The same invisibility that would carry the packet past three checkpoints would carry it there *silently*. Whatever happened at the harbor, Adeline would learn of it the way she learned of weather. And if I were taken, she would learn nothing at all until the men who took me came for her.

That was the cage part. The weapon came free.

* * *

By Thursday I knew the route the way I know the shape of my own hands.

Millgate Bridge at half five in the morning: Constable Peabody, who took his tea at the tollhouse at ten past and could be counted on to inspect a vehicle for exactly as long as it took his tea to cool. He knew my face — knew it the way you know a lamppost, which is the correct amount. Shipping office yard at six: two private guards, Hartley and the young one whose name nobody had bothered to learn, including possibly himself. Their interest began and ended with whether you had business inside the fence. Deliveries for the *Corinthia* would be waved to the quay side. Customs shed at the quay itself: the true teeth of it. Two officers, one of them thorough, one of them cold, and a table, and a habit of opening boxes.

"The customs shed is where it dies," Nils said. We sat in the coach house among the smell of hay and petrol, and he turned the fake mourning wreath over in his hands like it might bite him. White lilies, wired onto a frame, the card reading *In deepest sorrow, from colleagues at the Northern Rubber Works.* Inside the wreath's hollow back, flat as a communion wafer, the whole packet, rolled. It was, I maintained, beautiful.

"It doesn't die there. It dies if you come with me."

"We should split it—"

"We should do nothing of the kind." I took the wreath away from him before he crushed it. "Split the evidence and you split the risk in half and the chance of success into quarters. One man, one delivery, one story. The story is: I am delivering funeral flowers to the *Corinthia* for the widow of a fireman lost at Hull, sent by his workmates, poor souls, and here is the chit from the undertaker, and would the officer like to look, because look all you like, it's lilies and wire."

"And if they search you anyway?"

"Then they search a man carrying flowers." I met his eyes. "They'll only search harder if the man carrying flowers is sweating at them in an accent they can't place. You stay here. That is not a request."

He stood up too fast, knocking over the lamp oil can, and for a moment we both watched the dark spread of it across the floorboards, and neither of us moved to wipe it up.

"They killed a man in Rotterdam," he said quietly. "A clerk who helped Tomas. They found him in the harbor with his pockets full of stones, and the newspapers wrote *suicide*, and everyone believed it because it is easier to believe a man despaired than that he was murdered by committee." His hands were shaking. "You talk about checkpoints like they're doors, Rafael. They're mouths."

I knelt and wiped up the oil myself, which gave me something to do with my face.

"I know what they are," I said. "I've been driving past their sort for years. The difference between us, Nils, is that I've spent my whole life being something people look straight through, and I intend to spend tomorrow being exactly that, on purpose, one last time. You've spent your life being looked *at*. Stay home where nobody's looking."

He didn't answer. That should have worried me more than it did.

* * *

Friday evening, Adeline came down to the coach house. She stood in the doorway with her arms crossed — her thinking posture, the one she used at meetings when some lordly opponent was digging his own grave — and watched me load the wreath into a shallow crate of straw.

"If it goes wrong," she said, "you tell them the truth."

I looked up. "The truth?"

"That you're delivering flowers. Rafael." She came closer, and her voice dropped to the register she saved for things that mattered. "If they take you, you know nothing about Tomas, nothing about me, nothing about any of it. You are a driver with a delivery and a bad memory for other people's business. Promise me."

"You've never once wanted me to promise to know less."

"This once." Her jaw was set. I knew that set. It was the same jaw that had held firm through hecklers and thrown stones and her father's disinheritance, and I understood suddenly that she was not asking me to be safe. She was asking to be spared. There is a difference, and it is the width of a heart.

"Tomorrow night," I said, "I'll drink tea in your kitchen and tell you it went badly or tell you it went well, and either way I'll know nothing except flowers."

She held my eyes a moment longer than was comfortable, which from Adeline was practically an embrace, and left.

Nils did not leave. Nils paced. And around eleven, when I checked on him before bed, I found the coach house door open, the night rain blowing in, and his mattress empty — his coat gone from its hook, though mercifully not the wreath, which sat in its crate exactly where I'd left it, white lilies gleaming in the lantern light like a small patient ghost.

He had gone out into the dark with his shaking hands and his Rotterdam stories, and God alone knew what mouth he meant to feed.

I took the lantern and went after him, and the first place I thought to look was the road that led to Millgate Bridge.
