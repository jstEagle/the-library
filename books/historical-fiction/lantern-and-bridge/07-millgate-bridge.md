## 7. Millgate Bridge

The dead are useful, if you know how to employ them. The living ask questions; the dead only require flowers.

I learned this at half past four in the morning, kneeling on the kitchen floor of Mrs. Peale's boarding house with a wreath of boxwood and white chrysanthemums spread across my knees like a halo awaiting a saint who would never arrive. Nils sat against the stove watching me work the needle through the wreath's burlap backing, and every few minutes he asked the same question in a different key.

"What if they search it?"

"Then we're finished."

"What if they *weigh* it?"

"Then we're finished more slowly."

He did not laugh. I had not expected him to. Fear had eaten him down to the wiring — two years of being a dead man will do that, hollow you out until there's nothing left but the flinch. I'd seen the same architecture in soldiers, in debtors, in a housemaid once who had stolen a brooch and carried it under her tongue for a week. The guilty carry themselves differently. They walk like men crossing ice.

I, by contrast, have always walked like a man crossing a floor he owns. This is not courage. It is practice. When you are invisible long enough, you stop checking whether anyone sees you, because no one ever does.

The evidence — Tomas Marsh's ledger pages, three letters in his own hand, and the photograph of Councilor Bright shaking hands with a man whose face belonged in a police gazette — now lay flat inside the wreath's frame, sewn between two layers of oiled canvas in packets thin as communion wafers. A wreath for a funeral. That was the beauty of the thing: grief is the one cargo no constable wants to inspect. Search a man's suitcase and you're doing your duty. Search his mourning wreath and you're kicking a crutch.

The funeral was fictional. The deceased, one Elias Grange of Harrow Lane, had been invented at eleven o'clock the previous night, complete with an age (sixty-one), a cause (the lungs), and a grieving nephew (myself). Mrs. Peale, who asked nothing and saw everything, had supplied the chrysanthemums from her own garden and a length of black crepe from a hatband she claimed never to have liked.

"You'll want ribbon," she'd said, winding it round my hat. "Funerals run on ribbon."

* * *

We came at Millgate Bridge from the north, on foot, because a motorcar is a confession. Rafael Voss drives for Miss Adeline Marsh; Rafael Voss walking at dawn with a wreath in his arms is simply Rafael Voss, undertaker's errand, a man so unremarkable that even describing him now you are already forgetting his coat.

The bridge is iron and stone, older than either of us, spanning the Millwater where it runs grey and quick toward the harbor. There is a police post at the far end — a wooden kiosk, a brazier, and Constable Hobbes, whom I knew the way one knows a dog that has bitten you twice: formally, respectfully, and with a clear memory of teeth.

Hobbes had stopped me twice before. Once over a tail-lamp, once because Adeline had spoken sharply to a magistrate and the magistrate wanted reminding that constables exist. Both times he had looked at my face too long, the way men do when they cannot place a servant and find the failure irritating. He knew my face the way you know a face seen through steam. Not enough to name it. Enough to slow it.

"He knows me," I told Nils as we approached the last bend. "That's better and worse than a stranger. Better: he'll wave us through rather than do paperwork. Worse: he'll wave us through while looking at us the whole time. Your job is to be a mourner. Grief keeps its eyes down. Do you understand grief?"

"I have grief," Nils said.

"You have terror. They look similar but terror sweats. Keep your hands in the wreath. Touch the flowers. Mourners fidget with flowers — it gives the sorrow somewhere to go."

The fog was coming off the water in ropes. Good weather for ghosts. Somewhere below us a barge coughed upstream, and the gulls were conducting their usual argument about ownership of the morning.

Hobbes stepped out of the kiosk at twenty yards, silhouetted against his brazier, and I felt the old familiar sensation of becoming visible — the specific, physical drop of it, like a lift beginning its descent. Twenty years of invisibility, and it takes one constable and one wreath to undo it.

"Morning," said Hobbes.

"Morning, Constable." Grief keeps its voice low. So does respect. Convenient.

"Early for a procession."

"Elias Grange didn't choose the hour, sir. The family wants him in the ground before the heat comes." I let the wreath tilt slightly, chrysanthemums nodding. White flowers, dawn light, crepe. Hobbes's eyes went soft the way they do when a man is offered a role he knows how to play. Every constable has attended funerals. Every constable has a mother in the ground.

"Who's passed, then?"

"Uncle." I let my voice roughen on the word, and it required very little acting; I thought of Adeline asleep across the city with raiders' boot-prints still on her carpet, and the roughness arrived on schedule.

Hobbes nodded. Then, because the world is not a well-made play, his gaze slid past my shoulder. "Your friend crying or laughing? Hard to tell at this distance."

Nils was smiling.

It was the worst smile I have ever seen on a human face — the grin of a man whose body, having exhausted trembling, had moved on to its final indignity. His hands were not in the wreath. His hands were at his sides, opening and closing, and his eyes were fixed on the kiosk, on the brazier, on the telephone wire running from the post toward the shipping office, cataloguing exits that did not exist.

"Grief sits strangely on some men," I said. "He was Uncle Elias's caretaker these last years. Took it hard."

"Took it hard," Hobbes repeated. He took one step closer. The fog thinned between us as if clearing a stage. "Hold up. Haven't I stopped you before? Motorcar, blue, near the Corn Exchange—"

"You did, sir. Tail-lamp. You were kind enough to let me fix it myself."

Something eased in his face — the small vanity of being remembered kindly. "You fixed it, right enough." He glanced at Nils again, and the smile was still there, fixed and wrong as a painted mask left out in rain. "Your friend looks like a man who's about to be sick."

"He's about to bury the only man who paid him," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and true, and Hobbes laughed — one bark, apologetic — and stepped back.

"Go on, then. Tell the family the force sends condolences."

"Generous of you, Constable."

"Cheaper than flowers."

And then we were past the kiosk, past the brazier's smell of coke and rain, onto the far span where the fog swallowed us, and I counted forty steps before I allowed myself breath. Forty steps, and behind us Hobbes had already turned back to his fire, already forgetting, because I am forgettable, because that has always been my gift—

Nils sat down on the roadway.

Just sat down, right there on the wet cobbles of the bridge, wreath still clutched to his chest like a life-ring, laughing — actually laughing now, high and cracked, the sound bouncing off the iron girders. "He waved us through. He *waved*—"

"Get up." 

"We *did* it. Do you understand? Two years I couldn't cross a street without—" 

"Get *up*." I hauled him by the collar, and he came up swaying, and I put my face close to his and kept my voice under the gulls. "You nearly ruined it with your mouth and then you tried to ruin it with your spine. If you sit down again I will leave you on this bridge for the tide, and I will explain to the fish why you deserve them. We have two checkpoints left. That was the easy one. Hobbes is a man; men can be grieved at. The shipping office employs men who are paid not to be."

Nils wiped his face with both hands. Under the fear, briefly, I saw something else — shame, maybe, or its cousin, gratitude. "How did you do that?" he asked. "Stand there and lie to a policeman with a dead man's papers in your arms?"

I adjusted the wreath on my arm and started walking.

"It's not lying," I said. "It's carrying. I've carried things all my life. Suitcases, secrets, other people's tempers. The weight never changes. Only the reason for it."

He followed. Behind us the fog closed over Millgate Bridge, and ahead the harbor was showing itself in pieces — masts first, then cranes, then the dark shoulder of the *Corvus* at berth nine, taking on stores for the morning tide.

We had perhaps four hours. I remember thinking, with the terrible lightness that comes after danger instead of before it, that the worst was behind us.

I want it recorded that I have rarely been so wrong.

Because when we came down onto the quay, past the customs shed with its shuttered windows, a man was leaning against the shed door as if he owned it — and he did own it, or rented it from those who did — and he was reading a telegram by matchlight, and even at fifty yards, even in fog, I recognized the slope of his shoulders.

Councilor Bright's man. And he was not reading the telegram idly.

He was reading it, folding it away, and looking directly at us.
