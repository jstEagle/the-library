## 9. Morning Tide

The tide does not wait for anyone, which is the only honest thing about the sea. Everything else that morning—the fog, the customs men, Nils's trembling hands—was a lie told slowly.

We came down to the quay at half past four, when the lamps still burned and the gulls were arguing over nothing. The packet rode against my ribs, sewn into the lining of my coat in three flat sections, the way a man carries a Bible he doesn't believe in but can't put down. I had slept two hours on a chair. My body felt borrowed and poorly returned.

Nils walked six paces behind me, as instructed. He had already violated this instruction twice by walking eight paces behind, then four, then stopping entirely to be sick into the harbor. Fear is a load like any other. Some men carry it well; Nils carried it the way a donkey carries stairs.

"Walk," I told him quietly. "Just walk. You are a man going to work."

"I am a man who has been dead two years."

"Then you are the healthiest corpse in England. Keep walking."

The *Corvidene* sat low in the water at berth eleven, taking on her last crates. Captain Aldous had been paid—Adeline insisted on paying, because a favor can be revoked but a debt is binding—and all that remained was for the packet to cross forty feet of open quay, pass the customs shed, go up the gangway, and disappear into a ship's papers until it surfaced in Lisbon under another man's name.

Forty feet. I have crossed forty feet ten thousand times without anyone looking at me. That is my gift. I am the man at the edge of every photograph, the driver whose face is a hat brim, the servant whom gentlemen discuss secrets in front of because I am furniture that drives.

But the customs shed had three lamps lit where there should have been one, and three men where there should have been two, and one of them was holding a piece of paper up to the lamplight the way you hold a photograph of someone you've been paid to find.

I knew before I knew. Some knowledge arrives in the body first—a coldness along the spine, a tightening behind the knees—and the mind only catches up later, panting, saying *yes, all right, I agree.*

They had Nils's face on paper. And Nils, dead these two years, was walking toward them at a funeral pace.

* * *

Let me explain what I had planned, so you may properly appreciate what went wrong.

Plan: Nils and I separate at the fish market. Nils goes nowhere—he sits in the churchyard of St. Anselm's, among people genuinely dead, the safest congregation in the city—and waits. I take the packet aboard alone, because the packet matters and Nils does not. The men hunting Nils get Nils or they get nothing, but they do not get the truth, and Adeline does not hang.

Simple. Clean. The kind of plan a careful man makes.

What happened instead: at the mouth of the fish market, Nils grabbed my sleeve and hissed, "They'll be watching the churchyard. They know I think in churchyards."

"You've never thought anywhere else."

"Rafael." His fingers were ice through my sleeve. "If they stop me and search me and find nothing, fine. But if they follow *you*—"

"They won't follow me. Nobody follows me. It is my single talent."

But he would not let go, and there was no time, and I made the error that careful men make: I let a frightened man argue me out of arithmetic. We would cross together, he said. Two men going to work. He would carry the manifest roll—innocuous, his own shipping papers from a name that wasn't quite his—and I would carry the coat, and we would be through the shed in ninety seconds.

Ninety seconds. Remember that number. It will come back bleeding.

* * *

The quay smelled of tar, fish blood, and the particular damp-iron smell of dawn. The customs shed stood between us and the gangway like a tooth in a keyhole. Three men. The one with the paper—I would learn later his name was Hollis, and that he was not customs at all, which explained the quality of his boots—looked up as we approached, and I watched his eyes do a terrible thing.

They slid past me. Of course they did. I am Rafael Voss, invisible man, and even men hunting a ghost do not see the driver.

Then they found Nils.

It took perhaps four seconds. Hollis looked at the paper. Looked at Nils. Looked at the paper again, tilting it, comparing a dead man's likeness to a living man's exhaustion. And Nils—who had one line to say, one lie, *wrong man, sir, common face*—opened his mouth and ran.

He did not run well. Frightened men never run well; they run loudly. A crate went over. A rope coil caught his ankle. And the second customs man came off his stool with surprising speed for a man built like a mooring post, and suddenly the whole checkpoint was a single knot of violence with Nils at the center of it, screaming things in two languages.

Nobody was watching the gangway. Everybody was watching the fight.

This was my moment. Walk through. Forty feet. Board. Done. Every rational instinct said *go now,* and I want it recorded that I understood this perfectly and clearly, the way you understand the depth of water when you are drowning in it.

But Nils turned his head, mid-struggle, and looked at me. Not screaming for help. Just looking. Asking the question with his eyes: *was I right to trust the invisible man, or are you furniture after all?*

I dropped the lantern.

Not at them—at the ground beside the shed, where the previous night's rain had floated a skin of spilled lamp oil off the barge work into a slow rainbow puddle that any sensible man would have noticed and none had. Glass burst. Oil caught with a soft, obscene *whump*, and flame stood up along the shed wall like a thing that had been waiting all night for permission.

The quay became a fairground. Men shouted. The mooring post abandoned Nils for the fire. Hollis hesitated one precious second between the burning shed and the fleeing dead man, and I used that second to put my shoulder into Nils's back and shove him toward the fish market, away from me, away from everything.

"Go," I said. "St. Anselm's. Dig your own grave and lie in it."

He went. God help me, he actually went.

And I turned and walked the forty feet with fire climbing the shed wall behind me and the tide turning beneath the pier posts, and I handed the manifest roll to the mate of the *Corvidene*, and from inside my coat lining, calmly, like posting a letter, I passed him the three flat sections of Tomas Marsh's truth.

"Aldous knows," I said. "Sail with the tide. Don't wait for anyone."

"Anyone?" the mate said, and looked at my face, which I suppose had finally become visible.

"Anyone," I said.

The gangway rose. The hawsers came loose. Somewhere behind me a man shouted my description—*dark coat, driver's build, the quiet one*—which is the finest review I have ever received, though the notice came late.

I could have run. Millgate Bridge was twenty minutes north, and the fire would occupy them till dawn, and I might have melted into the city that had held me all my life the way water holds a stone.

Instead I stood on the quay and watched the *Corvidene* take the ebb, her lights small and certain, carrying Adeline's brother out of the hands of the men who had buried him alive—carrying, though no soul aboard knew it yet, the end of Councilor Bright and every gentleman who had bought a movement and thought himself safe.

I watched her go the way you watch a door close on the last warm room in the house. Not running cost me nothing in those moments. It cost everything in the next ones.

A hand took my collar. Another took my elbow, expertly, the way you handle livestock. I did not struggle; struggling is for men who expect to win. Hollis came around the corner of the burning shed with his fine boots wet to the lace, and he looked at me for a long moment with the expression of a bookkeeper finding a sum that wouldn't resolve.

"The quiet one," he said. "Well. Let's see how quiet."

* * *

They asked questions in the customs shed, which still smelled of scorched timber and my own mistake. When questions failed—they were always going to fail; I had decided in the first minute that I would be a very dull stone—they used other methods, and I learned that morning what I had always suspected: that invisibility is a property of other people's attention, and once it is stripped away, you are only meat with a memory.

I will not catalogue it. Enough to say that by the time the light came up gray over the harbor, I had given them a name (false), an address (burned), and an employer (Mrs. Ada Finch, laundress, deceased eleven years). I gave them these slowly, painfully, one splinter at a time, because every minute I spent being dull was a minute the *Corvidene* spent becoming sea instead of harbor.

The tide went out. I felt it go the way you feel a bone set—something leaving that must leave, something right that hurts. By seven o'clock she was hull-down in the fog, and the truth was beyond any man in that shed, including me.

Hollis worked me again after that, angrier, and I remember thinking with a strange floating clarity: *they cannot take back the tide.* They could break every bone I owned and they would be breaking the bones of a man who had already won. Pain is a currency. I have never had money. It was pleasant, in a bitter way, to finally spend something.

Near the end—it was near the end because I could hear gulls distinctly again, having lost and regained consciousness—I heard Hollis outside the shed door, speaking low and fast to someone. Speaking the name *Marsh*. Speaking the name *Bright*. Saying the words *the woman will know by evening; we go to the house.*

And I laughed. I couldn't help it. It cost me two ribs to laugh, and it was worth every one.

Because he said *we go to the house* as though it were a threat, as though Adeline Marsh—whom I had watched face hecklers, thrown bottles, and the full contempt of Parliament with nothing but posture and principle—would be found unready. He did not know her. He did not know that she had spent last night burning decoy papers in her own grate while constables hammered at her door, feeding her movement to the flames page by page to buy me time.

He thought he was going to catch a woman. He was going to find a lantern.

They left me on the floor of the shed with the fire's aftermath drifting through the boards and the tide far out and gray. I lay there counting my working parts, and found most of them protesting but reporting for duty, and I thought: *Millgate. Twenty minutes north. She'll come by the bridge road, if she comes.*

She would come. I had sewn her brother's truth into a stranger's ship and paid for it in blood on a customs floor, and none of it—not one stitch, not one drop—meant anything unless she chose to publish. That was the bargain I had made silently, somewhere between the lantern and the gangway, without asking her leave: *I will carry it if you will light it.*

Footsteps outside. Keys. The door swung open on gray light and cold air, and a customs officer I'd never seen before looked down at me with distaste and said the strangest sentence of my life:

"Get up, Mr. Voss. There's a lady outside insisting on your release, and she's brought a solicitor, a journalist, and what appears to be half the morning edition."
