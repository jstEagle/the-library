## 1. The Weight of Names

The water came in at four in the afternoon and again at nine, faithful as a bad husband. I learned its schedule the way other women learned prayer times, and by my second week at the dressing station I had raised the supply crates on bricks, moved the operating table's feet onto overturned mess tins, and developed a theory — unproven but deeply held — that the river and the staff colonel were conspiring, each determined to take up permanent residence where they were not wanted.

"Water's in," said Private Enfield from the doorway, holding his boots in one hand and his trouser legs in the other, as if the flood were something he could politely step over rather than something that had already soaked him to the knee.

"Thank you, Enfield. Put them on the table with the others."

He set his boots beside the row of boots already drying on the operating table, because that was how we did things now. Boots went on the table. Bandages went on the shelf above the water line. Patients went on pallets stacked on still more bricks, arranged in tiers like a bakery display, so that the men nearest the floor could look up at the men near the ceiling and draw what comfort they could from the fact that someone, at least, was drier.

I have a method for measuring the world, though I would not call it a philosophy, because philosophies are things you choose and this was simply what grief had left me. I measure by what can be carried. A man can carry a rifle and forty pounds more before his back gives out. A woman can carry a child, or a kit, or a ledger — not all three. A town can carry its living; it cannot carry its dead, and so the dead stay where they fall and the living learn to walk past them without turning their heads, which is its own kind of weight, distributed.

Everything I own fits into two canvas bags. Everything I know worth knowing fits into a book the size of a hymnal, bound in grey cloth, which I keep inside my coat against my ribs, where the officers believe I keep either sentimentality or a spare corset. They are wrong on both counts. It is an accounting.

"Sister Oyelaran," said Sister Marguerite from the doorway of the side ward, and her voice had that particular flatness I had learned to distrust. "Captain Boileau has been round again."

"And?"

"The list is posted." She handed me the paper. She handles papers the way other nurses handle bedpans — necessary, unpleasant, to be done quickly and without comment.

I read it. The evacuation manifest for Thursday: thirty-one names. Wounded only, priority by severity. No civilians. There was a line at the bottom, printed neatly in the captain's hand: *Civilians to be processed separately per directive.*

"Processed separately," I repeated. It is remarkable how much cruelty you can fold into two words if your handwriting is tidy enough.

"He struck six names yesterday," Marguerite said. "The Dubois family. The old schoolmaster. He said there wasn't room."

"There was room. I helped load the barge myself."

"Yes," she said, and looked at me with those pale eyes that see everything and approve of almost nothing, least of all themselves. "There was room."

* * *

Here is what the officers' lists do not say.

They do not say that the town of Saint-Aubin-sur-Brume holds, at my last count — and mine is the last count, because no one else is counting — some four hundred souls beyond the garrison. They do not say that when the thaw comes, and it will come, the Brume will rise eleven feet over its banks and drown the low quarter where those four hundred mostly live, because the low quarter is where you live when the high quarter is full of officers. They do not say that the bridge, that beautiful grey spine of iron across the valley, is the single road out, and that it is already groaning under the weight of guns it was never built to carry.

And they do not say their names.

That is the omission that keeps me at the ledger every night after the lamps are trimmed and the moaning has settled into sleep. Not the numbers — numbers are easy, numbers are how governments eat. The names.

So I sat that evening on an upturned crate with my feet in a basin because the water had come in early, unfaithful even to its own schedule, and I opened the grey book, and I wrote.

*Mireille Dubois, laundress, age 34. Children: Étienne, 7; Sylvie, 4. Husband missing since August, presumed Verdun. Struck from manifest Oct. 9. Reason given: none.*

*Henri Peloquin, schoolmaster, age 61. Lame left leg (old wound, 1870). Struck from manifest Oct. 9. Reason given: "not priority." He taught half the officers in this garrison to read, which they may reflect upon when they are older.*

*Amara,_age 9._ No surname she knows. Found in the ruins of the mill road in July, sitting beside a cart, counting backwards from one hundred. Has counted backwards from one hundred every night since. Under my protection.*

I wrote her name and stopped, because my pen had gone still in that way pens do when the hand behind them understands something before the mind consents to. Then I finished it. Amara, age 9. And beneath, in smaller letters, as if smallness might hide it from whoever someday read this book: *mine now.*

* * *

You will want to know about the grief. Everyone does, eventually, though they ask around it the way you walk around a flooded cobblestone — you know the hole is there but you'd rather not test its depth.

My grief is three years old and wears a private's uniform. It has no name I speak aloud. Before the war I was a woman who kept accounts for my father's import house, which is to say I understood that everything has a price and most prices are lies told in a respectable hand. The war taught me the rest of arithmetic. I lost — no. I will not say *lost*, the way the officers say it, as though the dead were gloves. I will say: the war took, in one afternoon outside Ypres, fourteen men whose names I knew, because I had dressed every one of their wounds at some point in the preceding month and a nurse who dresses your wounds learns your name whether she means to or not.

Fourteen names, and not one of them appeared in the official casualty report I saw afterward. Clerical error, they said. Fog of war, they said. I have come to believe the fog of war is manufactured, in bulk, by clerks, and that it descends precisely over the places where somebody's son died inconveniently.

Afterward I bought this grey notebook in a bombed-out stationer's in Poperinghe, paying far too much for it, because I had decided — in the way one decides such things, suddenly and forever — that if the army would not carry names, then I would. If their lists grew shorter, mine would grow longer. It is not defiance in any grand sense. Defiance implies the possibility of victory. This is closer to spite, and spite, I find, keeps better than hope in a wet climate.

* * *

"Are you writing your memoirs?" Enfield asked. He had come to change the dressing on his hand, which he believed I did not know he tore open himself whenever he feared being sent back up the line. I played along. It kept him coming to me instead of the trench, and a self-inflicted wound in the hand is a poor trade for one in the chest, but it is the only trade some men have left.

"I am writing the truth," I said. "It's shorter."

"Shorter than what?"

"Than everything they're writing instead."

He considered this while I unwrapped his hand. Underneath, the cut was healing cleanly, which meant he would need a fresh one soon. I felt the familiar sick turn in my stomach — complicity, they would call it, if complicity ran both directions, if anyone ever prosecuted the small mercies.

"You know what my mum does?" he said. "Kept books for a fishmonger in Leeds. Says a ledger never lies but a ledger-keeper can. Is that right?"

"Your mother is a philosopher," I said. "Ask her what happens when a ledger-keeper refuses."

"Refuses what?"

"Refuses to lie."

Enfield thought about it with his whole face, which is a thing young men can still do, their faces not yet having learned to be furniture. "Dunno," he said. "Probably gets sacked."

"Probably," I agreed, and wrapped his hand, and thought: *probably gets shot*, in this war's honest currency, and bandaged on anyway.

* * *

At nine o'clock the water came again, punctual after all, and with it Corporal Vasseur's adjutant — not Vasseur himself, Vasseur never came below the rank of rain — bearing the revised casualty list for the colonel's signature. I watched the adjutant pick his way between the pallet tiers, holding the document high like a man crossing a stream with dry trousers in mind. Priority, in this station, was measured in inches of wool.

"Sister. The list for Colonel Vasseur."

"Set it anywhere that isn't wet."

He looked around. Everything was wet, or within splashing distance of wet, or dreaming of becoming wet. He set it on the operating table, next to the boots, which struck me as the truest filing system in France: the official record, shelved among the boots of men whose feet were in it.

When he had gone I picked it up. Forty-one names. Yesterday's list had carried fifty-three. The day before, sixty-eight. The lists were growing shorter, you see, not because fewer men were wounded but because the definition of *wounded enough to count* was contracting, quietly, the way ice contracts — until it cracks. A man with a belly wound and three days of life in him no longer made the list; he made, instead, a note in a margin, and margins, unlike ledgers, burn easily.

I stood a long moment with the two documents, theirs and mine. Theirs: shrinking, typed, signed. Mine: growing, handwritten, unsigned, warm from riding against my ribs all day like a second heart doing double duty.

Marguerite appeared at my elbow with a lamp, and its light fell across the open grey book, and for one moment neither of us breathed.

"You should be careful with that," she said at last.

"Should I?"

"There was a woman at Sedan," Marguerite said, in the voice she used for stories she had promised herself she'd never tell, "kept a diary. Wrote down what the retreat actually looked like. An officer found it. You know what he charged her with?"

"Bad timing."

"Defeatism." She turned the lamp so the light moved off my book and back onto the ward, onto the sleeping tiers of men. "She was fortunate. They only burned the diary."

"And if they'd burned her?"

Marguerite's face did something complicated and settled on nothing. "Then there would have been one fewer witness," she said, "and the report would have read *no irregularities observed*." She paused at the door. "Careful, Theo. Accuracy is a luxury. I've seen what it costs."

"Cheaper than forgetting," I said.

"No," said Marguerite, gently, terribly. "Forgetting is free. That's why everyone can afford it."

She left me alone with my lamp and my book and the sound of the river, which had begun, very faintly, to speak in the new voice it uses under the ice — that low grinding mutter, like a large animal turning over in its sleep, dreaming of spring.

I wrote three more names by lamplight. The lamplight is important. Amara will tell you why, if you let her start on lanterns, which you should never do unless you have an hour and a warm heart.

*Josephine Aymard, midwife. Delivered half the low quarter, including, I suspect, several officers officially born elsewhere. Refused passage twice on grounds of "essential service," as if delivering babies in a flooding town were a post one abandons.*

*Two brothers, names uncertain — Kowalski? Kovac? — Polish laborers attached to the quarry detail. No one speaks for them. I will speak for them. Spelling approximate, existence not.*

*One hundred and twelve unaccounted as of tonight.*

One hundred and twelve. I looked at the number a long time. Numbers are easy; numbers are how governments eat. But somewhere in that figure was a woman named Mireille Dubois teaching her daughter to sew by touch because the candles were requisitioned, and an old man reciting declensions to children who would soon have no school and possibly no teacher, and a girl on a cot in the next room counting backwards from one hundred because forwards leads to tomorrow, and tomorrow is where the floods live.

I closed the book. I put it back against my ribs, where it belonged, and stood to make my rounds, lifting the lamp tier by tier, checking breathing the way you check a pulse in the dark — by faith, mostly.

On the third tier, Amara was awake. Of course she was. Her lips were moving.

"—ninety-seven, ninety-six, ninety-five—" She saw me and stopped, caught, in the way of children who have been caught at nothing and know it.

"Can't sleep?" I asked, though we both knew the question was a fiction we maintained for politeness, like calling the water in the ward *damp*.

"Ninety-four," she said. "I'm going down instead of up tonight. Down feels slower."

"Does it?"

"Slower is safer." She said it with the serene authority of a nine-year-old dispensing cosmology, and pulled the blanket to her chin. "Ninety-three."

I stood there in the lamplight, holding a book full of names against my heart, listening to a child count her way down toward morning, and I understood — with the plain, cold clarity that comes only after the lamps are trimmed — that whatever the officers' lists did, however short they grew, there would always be one more name than they could strike.

Because I would write it.

Because someone had to measure the world by what could be carried, and carry it.

"Ninety-two," said Amara. "Ninety-one. Ninety—"

Outside, far down the valley, the ice on the river shifted and boomed, once, like a door closing somewhere in a very large house.
