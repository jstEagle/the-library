## 2. Orders Come at Dusk

The orders arrived the way most terrible things arrived at Saint-Aubin: late, damp, and signed by someone who had never seen the river.

Major Fenn brought them himself, which was unusual. Usually such documents came down the chain like a coin passed hand to hand, growing less legible at every palm, until they reached me as a smudged instruction to do something I had already done three days earlier. But Fenn came in person at dusk, standing in the doorway of the dressing station with his cap in one hand and a leather folder in the other, wearing the particular expression of a man delivering news he has rehearsed being sorry about.

"Theo." He used my first name, which meant either good tidings or terrible ones. With Fenn it had always been difficult to tell whether familiarity was affection or an attempt to soften the blow by pretending we were friends. "Have you a moment?"

I had forty-one moments, all of them occupied. A gunner from the ridge with a thigh wound I'd repacked twice that afternoon; two civilians from the flooded quarter with the grey, listless cough that meant bad water; a boy of perhaps sixteen who had lied about his age so successfully that even his own regiment believed him, lying now on a cot and asking every ten minutes whether his hand was still attached. It was. I told him so each time, and each time he seemed surprised.

"For you," I said, "I have several."

He came in anyway, stepping carefully around the buckets — we caught the roof's leaks now with an arrangement of pails that had grown so elaborate I thought of it as a second patient requiring constant attention — and he read the orders aloud in the low, level voice men use when they want the words to sound smaller than they are.

The bridge would close for repairs within three weeks. The seasonal thaw was expected early this year; the engineers' estimates put the valley under water by the second week of the thaw, and the front — the word *front* doing its usual work of making chaos sound architectural — could not be supplied across a drowned causeway. One final barge would make the crossing before the chains went up. After that, Saint-Aubin would be what it had been before the war made it a noun on maps: a town on the wrong side of a river, waiting.

"Three weeks," I said.

"Twenty-two days," said Fenn, who had counted. Men like Fenn always counted; it was how they avoided feeling. "The barge takes forty. Priority goes to—"

"I know what priority goes to." Priority went to officers first, then men whose paperwork described them as essential, then anyone whose name appeared on a list compiled by other men whose names would appear on earlier lists. The system was elegant in the way a guillotine is elegant. Everything about it moved in one direction, smoothly, and ended at a basket.

Fenn put the folder down on my supply table, next to the basin and the roll of gut suture, and I noticed he wouldn't quite look at the ledger drawer beside it. Everyone knew I kept notes. Nobody had ever asked to see them, which I had come to understand was not tolerance but superstition — the same instinct that keeps soldiers from whistling in the trenches. As long as no one looked too closely at what was actually happening here, it remained, officially, nothing.

"I'll see the manifests posted tonight," he said.

"You do that."

He hesitated in the doorway. For a moment something almost human moved behind his moustache — some memory, perhaps, that he had been a boy once, or that he had a mother, or that the coughing woman in the corner bed had a name he'd walked past four times without learning. Then the folder clicked shut and the officer reassembled himself around the man, the way it always happened, bone by bone, and he was gone into the dusk.

I stood a while with the paper. Orders are strange objects; they weigh almost nothing and yet you can feel them in the room afterward, like weather changing. Outside, the river ran brown and swollen between its banks, carrying branches, fence posts, a door — just a door, no house attached, turning slowly end over end as though looking for its frame. I watched it go under the bridge and thought: twenty-two days, and then that bridge is a rumor, and everyone the lists forgot is on the wrong side of a rumor.

I took out my ledger.

* * *

You must understand what the ledger is, because the officers' reports will not tell you and eventually, if there is any justice, someone will ask.

The army counts men. That is its whole genius — counting, sorting, moving numbers from one column to another until the columns themselves become the war and nobody remembers there were hands attached. A wounded soldier arrives at my station and becomes a diagnosis, a regiment, a serial number stenciled on a stretcher. A civilian arrives and becomes, in the official arithmetic, a rounding error. When the convoys leave, the manifests record what left. Nothing records what stayed. Nothing records who fell between the door and the cot, or who came in already past saving, or who was never admitted at all because the beds were full and the major on duty that week had opinions about triage that owed more to logistics than to mercy.

So I write them down. Name where I can get one — and you would be astonished how often a dying man finds breath for his name and his village and his mother's maiden name, as though the body knows which accounts must be settled before it closes. Where I cannot get a name, I write what I have: the blue scarf she wore; the boy with the burned left hand who called everyone *maman*; the old ferryman who gave his boat away and died two days later as though his work in the world had concluded and he saw no reason to linger. Descriptions, wounds, dates, the hour if I knew it. It is not literature. Marguerite has called it a psalter, but Marguerite is generous with metaphors the way saints are generous with miracles. It is a ledger. Things in it are owed.

That evening I added four names while the lamp guttered and the pails filled and emptied beneath the leaks. A gunner's mate, Delacourt, who died at seven-forty with someone else's rosary wound around his fist — I wrote down the rosary too, in case anyone ever came asking, because people do come asking, eventually, when the guns stop. The woman with the cough, whose name was Perrin and who had been a schoolteacher in the low quarter and who taught me, between fits, the proper plural of a Latin word I had been misusing for years. Two boys from the labor battalion whose names I got wrong the first time and had to cross out, which I hate doing, because crossed-out names look like erased men.

When I finished, I sat with the book open on my knees and listened to the river.

Here is the confession underneath all of it, the one I keep even from myself most nights: I began this ledger out of spite. My brother and I did not speak — six years of silence, and the silence had a shape I carried in my chest like a swallowed stone — and the first name I ever recorded, back in spring, I recorded because some staff captain had pronounced a man *unaccounted for*, meaning *not worth accounting for*, and I wanted, furiously, pettily, to prove somebody wrong. Grief does that. It looks for a job. Denied the person it belongs to, it hires itself out to strangers.

But spite is a poor fuel; it burns hot and leaves ash. Somewhere between spring and the first frost, the ledger stopped being an accusation and became a promise. These people existed. They drank from cups and complained about the coffee and had opinions about the weather and loved specific, ridiculous things — one man's horror of beets, another's habit of humming opera during amputation, badly, off-key, with real devotion. The reports would forget all of it. Somebody had to remember, and there was no queue forming for the position.

Now the orders said: twenty-two days. Now the orders said: forty berths. And I stood at my table doing the arithmetic that Fenn had already done and filed away in his leather folder, and the sum kept coming out the same, and the same was not a number I could live inside.

* * *

The manifests went up on the door of the town hall at nine o'clock, and I went to watch them read, because a nurse learns to stand where the grief will be and arrive before it does. It saves time later.

They posted the lists under lantern light, two sheets of flimsy paper pinned to the board beside the bread ration notices and a faded appeal for war bonds, as though salvation were one more civic announcement between the price of flour and somebody's lost dog. A crowd gathered the way crowds do in wartime — quickly, silently, with the terrible patience of people accustomed to disappointment arriving on schedule.

And then the reading began, and I watched the striking-through.

An adjutant did it with a pencil, right there against the board, working down the civilian petitions as their bearers stepped forward. I want to be fair; fairness is a discipline like any other, and I practice it even when it tastes like iron. The adjutant was not cruel. He was tired, and frightened in the way staff men are frightened, which is to say of forms filled incorrectly. Each petitioner came with their story — the laundress with a daughter prone to croup; the clock-winder's widow whose son was simple and could not be left; the brewer offering, with magnificent misjudgment, to pay. And the adjutant heard each one, nodded, consulted his regulations, and drew his pencil through the name.

Strike. Strike. Strike.

It did not take long. That was the worst of it. Two hundred souls, give or take — I had counted mine, I always counted mine — and the striking-through of them took less time than the evening Angelus. The pencil moved with the small apologetic scratch of ordinary administration, and the crowd stood watching their own names being subtracted, and nobody shouted. That is what I cannot make anyone understand who was not there. There was no riot. There was only the sound of graphite on paper and, after a while, the river.

A woman near me began quietly folding her petition into smaller and smaller squares, as though she could put the refusal somewhere it couldn't follow her. An old man saluted the adjutant. Saluted him. The adjutant, to his credit or his shame, saluted back.

I found Sister Marguerite at the edge of the lamplight, her great frame still as a buttress. She had not bothered to bring her petition. We had discussed it; she had one legitimate claim through the order she served, and she had already decided, in that maddening way of hers, that decisions about herself were the least interesting decisions available.

"Well," she said. "There it is."

"There it is."

"They've forgotten Madame Aubry's girl entirely. Not struck through — never written down."

"I know."

"And the Delacourt family, up the hill. Four of them. The pencil missed them because the clerk transposed their street."

"Marguerite."

"I'm only observing," she said, "that your book is more accurate than theirs." And then, because she is Marguerite, and because she sees straight through me and through herself and through most of the century, she added, softly: "Accuracy is going to get you into trouble, Théodora. It always does. Pray only that it gets you into the useful kind."

Below us, the crowd was dispersing into the dark, each family carrying home its subtraction. The lanterns along the quay swung in the wet wind, and beyond them the bridge stood black against blacker water, and I thought about what a bridge actually is — not wood and iron but a promise, a sentence the two banks say to each other — and about how soon that sentence would be taken out of the town's mouth mid-word.

Marguerite squeezed my arm once and went in to her prayers. I stayed.

I stood on the quay until the lamps burned low, doing sums. Forty berths. Two hundred names in my book, and the book itself uncounted, and the town besides — the ones with petitions refused, the ones too proud to petition, the ones the clerks never heard of at all. Against that arithmetic, the orders offered me a folder, a pencil line, and twenty-two days.

Dread is a curious material. Held wrong, it shatters you. Held right, it sets, like mortar, and you find you can build with it.

I walked back to the dressing station past the sleeping houses, and at the corner by the bakery — dark now, the ovens cold because the grain had gone to the front — a small figure was waiting for me in the doorway, exactly where she was forbidden to wait after dark, holding our lantern with both hands the way she held everything, as though it might be taken from her at any moment and needed defending.

Amara. Who had no petition on any manifest, no claim, no line even to be struck through. Who counted seconds under her breath when the shells came close, and who had, without anyone appointing her, become the fixed point my world turned around.

She lifted the lantern when she saw me, and the light caught her face, and every sum I had done on the quay rearranged itself into a single, unsolvable equation with her name at the center of it.

"You're late," she said. "I counted four hundred and twelve seconds."

"Then I owe you four hundred and twelve apologies."

"Four hundred and eleven," she said gravely. "I dropped the count once. At three hundred and six." She took my hand in her cold small fingers, and together we went in toward the light, and above us, faint through the clouds, the tower clock struck the hour — a sound that had marked this town's evenings for two hundred years, and that I understood now, with a clarity that felt like cold water, was counting down alongside everything else.

Three weeks. Forty berths. One child asleep against my shoulder by midnight, still counting in her dreams — I could feel the numbers moving her lips.

I lay awake in the store room and let the dread finish setting. Then I got up, lit the lamp, and opened the ledger to a blank page.

At the top, in a hand steadier than I expected, I wrote: *What can be carried.*
