## 3. The Girl Who Counted Backwards

Amara counted backwards from one hundred the way other children said their prayers — not because anyone had taught her to, but because the war had left a hole where instruction should have been and she had filled it herself, as children do, with whatever was lying about. In her case: numbers.

I discovered this in July, my first week at the dressing station by the mill, when she was still only a shape under a table, folded up among the flour sacks with her knees drawn to her chin, counting in a voice so steady it frightened me more than screaming would have.

"Ninety-eight. Ninety-seven. Ninety-six."

Screaming I knew what to do with. Screaming meant morphine, meant holding a hand down, meaning saying *you're all right, you're all right* until the words wore grooves into both of us. But a child who announced the passage of seconds as though she were personally responsible for keeping time when the town clock could not be trusted — that required invention.

"Eighty-nine," she said, when I knelt down to look under the table. She did not stop. She did not look away either; she looked at me the way soldiers looked at a new nurse arriving on a ward, assessing whether I was likely to make things worse or merely fail to make them better.

"What happens at zero?" I asked her.

She considered this seriously, the way she considered everything, with the gravity of a much older person trapped in a body that still had to be lifted onto chairs. "Then I start again."

"And if you don't?"

"If I don't count, then things happen that I can't count." She said it as if explaining arithmetic to someone slow. "Counted things stay where you put them."

I have treated men who lost legs and men who lost brothers and men who lost the ability to say either word without weeping, and none of them ever said anything truer than that girl under the table. Counted things stay where you put them. Everything else runs.

Her name was Amara Diallo, though the second half took me another month to learn, because the first half was all anyone ever bothered to write down. Her mother had worked the river trade — a trader, a translator, depending on whom you asked, which in 1917 usually amounted to the same profession with different paperwork. When the shelling came through the low quarter in June, the mother did not come out, and the daughter did, and somewhere between those two facts the town's collective memory misplaced her entirely. The officers' lists had no column for her. I would later understand, in the way one understands a machine only after it has taken off a finger, exactly how convenient that absence was for everyone who did not have to look at her.

But in July I only understood that there was a child under a table counting backwards, and that the nuns' annex was full, and that my quarters above the dispensary had a floor, and that floors are indifferent to how many people sleep on them.

"You can't stay here," I told her that first night, which was true, and then made up a bed for her anyway, which was truer.

* * *

By October she had opinions about everything. About how I folded bandages (wrongly, too much crease), about Sister Marguerite's soup (a punishment visited on the innocent), about the correct order in which one ought to light a lantern (wick trimmed before oil, never after — "so the light doesn't stutter, because stuttering lights lie"). She had appointed herself my quality inspector, my conscience, and my alarm clock, since the tower clock had begun losing minutes the way the army lost men: quietly, and with no one willing to say the total aloud.

The lantern-lighting began in September, when the evenings started arriving early and uninvited. It was Amara who turned it into ceremony. Every dusk, at the window above the dispensary, we lit the lamp together — she did the wick, I struck the match, because she had decided this division of labor was correct and I had long ago stopped arguing with decisions that cost me nothing and gave her the world slightly rearranged into something she could hold.

"Why do we do it the same every night?" I asked her once, mostly to hear what she'd say.

"So the dark knows we expected it." She set the glass chimney back with two hands, careful as a surgeon. "It comes anyway. But it knows."

Wry as I like to think myself, I had no answer for that, so I said "quite right," and pulled the curtain, and the room became the small yellow country that belonged to the two of us and no one else.

Inside that country there were laws. Supper was eaten at the little table with the wobbly leg shimmed with a folded list of casualties — I did not tell her what the paper was; she never asked what it was; some fictions are load-bearing on both sides. After supper, homework of a sort: I read aloud, she followed with one finger, and whenever my voice stumbled over a word she counted silently — I could see her lips move — until I found it again, as though she were holding my place in line. At eight o'clock, prayers or no prayers (she declined prayers, having conducted her own private negotiations with God in June and found His terms unacceptable), she counted herself to sleep. Not from one hundred. From three hundred now. The war kept lengthening her numbers.

"They're longer than everyone else's," she informed me one night, with something dangerously close to pride. "My counts. Papa's were longest of all. He went to a thousand."

"Where did he learn to need a thousand?"

"He didn't need them. He just liked knowing where he was inside the number." She yawned. "Like you and your book."

I kept very still. "What book is that?"

"The book you write in when you think I'm asleep." She turned over. "You press too hard with your pen. It sounds like a little man walking."

I want it recorded that I did not lie to her. I simply failed to speak, which in October felt like an achievement rather than a debt. There would come a night, I already suspected, when failing to speak would not be enough, and I would have to choose between truth and kindness in front of that serious face. Every adult I respected had eventually had to make that choice. Most of them chose wrong, in my opinion, but then most adults I respected were officers.

* * *

Here is what the ledger held that autumn, pressed hard enough to sound like footsteps: names. That was all it was, at first, and perhaps that is all it ever was — but names, written down by an ordinary hand, in ink that will not wash out, have always seemed to me the most stubborn form of prayer available to a person who has given up on the kneeling kind.

The officers kept their lists, and their lists grew shorter each week, which they reported as progress. Men evacuated: such a number. Effectives remaining: such a number. It was arithmetic performed from a great height, from a basket suspended under a balloon, where individual faces resolve into pleasing patterns. Down at ground level, where I lived, the arithmetic refused to cooperate. A shell fell on the rue Basse and the report recorded four casualties; I dressed eleven, and buried two whose names nobody collected. A labor company passed through and thirty of its men went onto nobody's roll at all, because their battalion existed on paper somewhere between a clerical error and a policy. Refugees from upriver arrived with names in languages the adjutant could not spell and therefore, by a logic I have never been able to reproduce without nausea, did not record.

So I wrote them. In the ledger I bought from Odilon the bargeman — a fat account book, ruled for grain shipments, its previous career visible in faded columns of tonnage — I entered the omitted. Name, age, wound or condition, date, where they slept. It took me an hour some nights. It made the pen sound like a little man walking. And it changed nothing in the world except this: it moved those people from the country of the uncounted into the country of the counted, and counted things, as a nine-year-old once explained to me under a table, stay where you put them.

Amara knew about the ledger the way rivers know about rain — not as information but as weather. She had heard the pen. She had seen me writing by lamplight after our lamp-country was supposed to be closed for the night. Once, waking from a nightmare in early October, she found me mid-entry and climbed into my lap without a word and watched me finish the line — *Bakary Diop, 34, shattered ankle, stable, mill cellar, third row* — and then she said:

"Is that a name?"

"It is."

"Put his age next to it. You always forget ages." She tapped the paper. "Ages matter. If you only have a name, you don't know how many years it is."

I put his age next to it. From then on she checked my work, when she caught me at it, with the mild tyranny of a proofreader who has never once been wrong. She did not know what the ledger was for. Neither, in any honest sense, did I. It was not going to be read by headquarters. It was not going to save anyone. It was a woman pressing too hard with her pen against the general shortening of lists, and a child adding ages because ages mattered, and if you had told either of us in October that the ledger would become worth fighting over, we would have laughed — she with her whole body, I with the guarded half-smile I had worn since 1911.

* * *

The nights were when the fear got in. Days were manageable; days had work, and work is a wall you can stand behind. But at eight o'clock the lamp went up in our yellow country and the guns downriver went on with their slow argument, and sometimes the floor shivered, and Amara's counts got faster, and I learned to sit very close and breathe slowly so she had a rhythm outside her own head to borrow.

One such night — the twelfth of October, because I am the sort of person who dates her terrors — a shell landed close enough to shift the shutters, and instead of counting she went silent. Silence from Amara was a sound like a clock stopping. I put down the pen.

"I'm here," I said.

"I know." Her voice was flat and careful, a soldier's voice. "I'm not counting tonight."

"Why not?"

"Because if I count to the end of the number and the house is still here, then the number saved us. And then I have to keep using the number forever." She stared at the lamp flame. "Marguerite says God does that. Makes you keep doing things so He'll keep doing His things. I don't want to be responsible for the house."

Nine years old. I sat with that sentence the way you sit with a wounded man whose wound you cannot close — knowing that everything kind you might say would be a lie, and that silence, honestly kept, is sometimes the only clean garment in the wardrobe. In the end I said: "The house stands because of its stones. The numbers are just how we remember standing."

She thought about it. "Whose memory? Mine or yours?"

"Both. That's why there's two of us."

That satisfied her. It did not satisfy me, because I knew — the way I knew the thaw would come, the way I knew lists shorten — that sooner or later there would be a night when one of us could not be present for the remembering, and the other would find out what memory weighs alone. But that knowledge belonged to a later hour. That night there was lamp-oil, and a wick trimmed before lighting so the flame stood straight and told no lies, and a child's head heavy against my ribs, and outside the dark arrived as scheduled and found us expecting it.

* * *

Near the end of October she asked me the question I had been rehearsing against for three months without knowing it.

"Theo. Why haven't they sent me anywhere?"

We were doing letters. I had begun teaching her the alphabet that week — French letters, proper ones, because her mother's schooling had been interrupted and the girl read by sight and guesswork, which is a magnificent talent and a dangerous foundation. She traced an *R* in the dust on the table and waited, patient as stone, for my answer. Children ask their questions at the worst moments on purpose; I am convinced of it now. They know the adults are least armored when their hands are full.

"There's nowhere to send you yet," I said, which was true.

"There's north. There's the barges. Marguerite says people go on barges." She drew the R again, deeper. "Nobody's put me on one."

"The lists—"

"I know about the lists." Flatly, the way one says *I know about the war*. "I'm not on any of them. I've looked." She had looked. Of course she had looked; she could find her own name in a world of print the way water finds a drain. "Marguerite says someone has to claim you. For the barge. Someone official has to say you're theirs." She blew the dust-R away. "Are you mine, Theo? Officially?"

And there it was: the choice between truth and kindness, arriving not someday but now, with my hands full of dust.

I could have said yes. One word, and she would have slept well, and the word would have weighed nothing at bedtime and everything afterward, because manifests are read by clerks, and clerks do not recognize love as a legal category, and the day would come when an officer would look at that child and see no column for her. I could also have said *not yet*, which was honest and cruel, or *it's complicated*, which is what cowards say in every language ever invented.

I set down the rag. I took her hands, both of them, and put them flat on the table where the R had been.

"Officially," I said, "there is paper. Paper is a thing men argue about. Unofficially — which is the part that survives the arguing — you are mine and I am yours, and I have thought so since July, and I will be putting your name where I put things I intend to keep. That, I promise you, exists whether or not any clerk agrees."

She studied my face for a long moment, conducting her assessment, the new-nurse inspection, deciding whether I made things better or merely failed to make them worse.

"All right," she said at last. "But write the age next to it. Ages matter."

That night, after she counted herself down from three hundred and under, I opened the ledger and turned past the casualties to the last blank page, and I wrote at the top of it, pressing hard enough for a little man to walk across the millpond of the night:

*Amara Diallo, nine years. Claimed.*

Below it, because honesty is a habit once it takes: *Claimed back.*

The lamp burned steady. Outside, far downriver, the bridge carried its traffic in the dark, unaware — as I was, as we all were — that its days of carrying anything were numbered, and that somewhere in the offices where lists were shortened, a figure was already being assigned to the space where her name should have gone, and finding it empty, and moving on.
