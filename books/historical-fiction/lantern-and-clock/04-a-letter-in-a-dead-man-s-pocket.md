## 4. A Letter in a Dead Man's Pocket

The courier came in on a stretcher at half past four, though I only knew the hour because Sister Marguerite said it aloud, the tower clock having died sometime in October and no one with authority enough to mourn it. He was young — twenty, perhaps, or twenty-one — with the particular paleness of a man who has been bleeding inward for some hours and whose body has begun to conduct its own quiet negotiations.

"Found him by the mill road," said the corporal who'd carried him. "Motorcycle went into the ditch. Don't know how long he lay there."

Don't know how long. That phrase had become the town's whole liturgy. We didn't know how long the bridge would hold, how long the thaw would wait, how long the officers' lists would go on being shorter than the truth. I had stopped asking how long of anyone above the rank of captain, because the answer was always *long enough for it to be someone else's problem.*

I set my lamp close and did what I do. That is the whole of nursing, when you strip away the ceremony: you put your hands where the trouble is, and you keep them there.

* * *

He was conscious by fits. A wound through the thigh, missed the artery by the width of a prayer but torn a vein besides, and the cold of the ditch had done its work on him before I ever saw his face. His lips had gone the color of the river in November. I gave him morinol — morphine, I mean; Amara's mispronunciation had colonized my head months ago and refused to be evicted — and warmed blood to my palms by rubbing them like a washerwoman, which is the oldest and least glamorous trick in the trade.

"Hold still," I told him. "You're among friends. Well. Among nurses. Close enough."

He tried to laugh and it turned into a cough that frightened both of us. When it passed, his hand came up and closed around my wrist with surprising strength, the way dying men do, as if the living might be anchored in place by grip alone.

"The bag," he said. "The dispatch bag."

"It's here." The corporal had set it under the cot. Standard practice: everything a courier carried belonged, technically, to the army, and technically we were supposed to send such things along unopened. Technically. "You'll deliver it yourself when you're mended."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way men do when they are doing arithmetic about their own insides and getting an answer they don't care for. He knew. They usually know before we admit it. There's a look a man gets when he has finished negotiating with his body and lost, and no amount of cheer in a nurse's voice will renegotiate terms.

"Sister." His voice dropped. "Listen. The letter. The one from Captain Rell. Not the official packet. The one underneath."

"There's nothing underneath," I said. "I haven't touched anything."

"There will be." He swallowed. "There's a second letter. Folded inside the first. It isn't — it isn't dispatch business." His eyes were very clear now, the morphine having burned off the fog and left only the urgency. "It concerns the bridge."

Everything in this town concerned the bridge. The bridge was our weather, our calendar, our god.

"You'll want to burn it after," he said. "Or you won't. But someone should know it existed. Someone who writes things down." And then his gaze sharpened on me with a strange, feverish attention. "You're the one who keeps the book. The other sister said. You write down everyone."

I didn't answer that. My ledger lived under a loose floorboard beneath the linen shelf, and precisely four people knew of it: myself, Marguerite, God, and — since September — Amara, who had found it while hunting for candles and could not be persuaded to pretend otherwise.

"I write down everyone," I said carefully, "who comes through my doors."

"Then write this down too." He pulled me closer, and his breath was hot against my ear, smelling of iron and ditch water. "The closure isn't weather. Or not only weather. Tell them the thaw is real, yes — but the schedule, the *schedule* was signed weeks early. Before the engineers' report. Somebody wanted the last crossing moved up. Somebody wanted certain people on the wrong side of the water when the valley floods."

My hands kept working. That is the mercy of trained hands — they continue their business while the rest of you is busy falling down a staircase somewhere.

"Who wanted?" I asked.

He shook against the pillow, a small motion that cost him. "Names I don't have. Only the shape of it. Rell was furious. He said — he said the manifest lists were drawn up *before* the casualty projections. Do you understand? They decided who was leaving before they counted who was stranded."

I understood. Understanding arrived in my chest like a swallowed stone.

"And the letter—"

"Underneath the official one. Rell wrote it to his wife. He didn't trust the pouch. He trusted the courier, and the courier is—" He smiled, and it was a terrible young smile, wry as my own thoughts but without any of the armor. "Well. Here I am."

I bent over the wound again. There was little left to do; there is often little left to do, and one does it anyway, because the alternative is standing with empty hands, and empty hands are unbearable.

"What do I call you?" I asked.

"Bram." A pause. "Corporal Bram Visser. If it matters."

"It matters," I said, and meant it more than he could know. Everything I did these days was a quarrel with people who thought names were optional.

* * *

He lasted two hours. Morphine makes the going gentle, and I talked to him while I worked, the way I talk to all of them — nonsense mostly, the price of bread, the insolence of officers, the color of the sky that morning, which had been the gray of old dishwater. He answered sometimes. Near the end he answered less, and his hand stayed closed around my wrist until it wasn't holding anymore, just resting, and then not even resting.

Marguerite came and stood at the foot of the cot. She had a gift for arriving exactly when a nurse needed another human being who understood that silence is also a procedure.

"Well?" she said softly.

"He's gone."

"I saw." She glanced at the dispatch bag. "They'll send for that within the hour. You know the rule."

"I know the rule," I said.

She knew me better than that rule. She stood there a moment longer, wringing her hands in her apron in the old way, and then she said, very quietly, "Theo. Whatever you're about to do — think about whether knowing is heavier than not knowing."

"When has knowing ever been optional for me?"

"Never," she said. "That's precisely what frightens me."

And she went back to her ward, because there were eleven other men and none of them cared a fig for my moral architecture.

* * *

The official letter was three pages of military boilerplate: route confirmation, seal numbers, acknowledgment requests. I read it standing by the window, where the light was best and where I could see the road in case anyone in authority chose that moment to come asking.

Then I unfolded the second letter.

It was written in a different hand — cramped, private, the handwriting of a man writing fast to someone who knew all his abbreviations. No salutation beyond *my dearest*, no signature beyond an initial. A husband's letter, then, never meant for any eyes but one. Which makes what follows neither theft nor espionage, whatever a tribunal might say. A widow's letter delivered late is still a widow's letter.

I copy the heart of it here, from memory, because memory is the only vault I have never seen broken into:

*My dearest — the crossing schedule is signed and sealed and it was signed six days before the engineers filed their report, which means the report was decoration. Verlac pushed it through. He has interests on the north bank I will not put in writing even to you, but understand this: the low quarter is not an oversight. The omission lists were circulated. Certain names were marked to remain — laborers, debtors, the stateless, anyone whose disappearance will be called regrettable rather than deliberate. I have seen the marking list. I cannot stop it. I can only tell you that if the barge goes without them, it will not be tragedy. It will be policy wearing tragedy's coat.*

*Burn this.*

I did not burn it.

I sat down on the stool by the dead courier's cot, with his hand still half-curled on the blanket, and I read the paragraph four times, and each time the word *marked* rang in my skull like the bell the clock tower used to ring before it died.

Marked. As in inventory. As in the ledger columns officers kept — except their ledger ran opposite to mine. Where I recorded the omitted so they could not vanish quietly, someone above me had recorded them so they *would* vanish, on schedule, with paperwork to prove it was nobody's fault.

I thought of Amara. Stateless — the word applied to her like a coat two sizes too small. An orphan of nowhere in particular, no papers, no patron, a child whose entire legal existence fit inside my insistence that she existed at all. On any marking list drawn up by men who sorted humanity into *regrettable* and *deliberate*, she would be in the first column before the ink dried.

I thought of Samson — wherever Samson was — a laborer's name if ever there was one.

I thought of the two hundred souls I had entered in my ledger while the official rolls grew shorter and neater, and I understood, with a clarity that felt physically like cold water, that those two omissions were not the same omission. Mine was defiance. Theirs was design.

The corporal came for the dispatch bag at ten past five, exactly as Marguerite predicted. I handed him the official packet with both hands and a face I have practiced in mirrors: pleasant, vacant, faintly tired. He asked if the courier had said anything.

"He asked for water," I said. "He asked twice about his mother."

Both true. Neither relevant. I am learning that the most effective lie is simply a smaller truth standing in front of a larger one, the way a screen of trees hides a road.

When he was gone I took the second letter to the stove — and here I must confess something I have never confessed to paper before: I stood there perhaps a full minute, burning-adjacent, weighing the thing in my hand. Bram had said *someone who writes things down.* Rell had said *burn this.* And every instinct of self-preservation, that modest animal which lives just below the ribs, said: you are a nurse in a town about to be cut in half by a flood and a war, and you are holding the one document that important men would drown you to retrieve.

But self-preservation has never been my strongest instinct. Curiosity outranks it. So does spite. So does the memory of every name in my ledger that appeared on no list anywhere.

So I did not burn it.

I folded it small, and that night, after the ward slept, I opened the floorboard beneath the linen shelf and laid it flat against the back of my ledger, ink to ink, one record pressed to another, and closed the board over both. Then I sat a while in the dark with my hand resting on the wood, listening to the wind work at the shutters, feeling the shape of what I had learned settle over the ordinary furniture of my life like snow over a field — hiding nothing, only making every fence and furrow strange.

The thaw was coming regardless. Rivers don't take orders. But men had taken the river's side, and hastened it, and chosen in advance who would be standing in the valley when the water arrived.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would begin a second column in the ledger. Not the omitted — I knew the omitted. A new column: the *marked*. Names I would gather as best I could from work rosters, debt books, the parish rolls of the stateless. If someone intended to lose these people, then these people would acquire, beginning tonight, a clerk of their own.

I blew out the lamp. In the next room, faintly, I heard it — the small, patient whisper of Amara counting backwards from one hundred in her sleep, as she does, down toward zero, toward morning, toward whatever the day would bring.

And the day brought, with the morning convoy, a squad of mud-gray men in labor battalion blue, and at the front of them, taller than the years between us had any right to leave him —

Samson.
