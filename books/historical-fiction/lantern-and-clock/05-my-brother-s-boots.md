## 5. My Brother's Boots

The boots arrived before the man.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only religion I have left. On a grey Tuesday in November — the clock in the tower had stopped by then, but the orderlies still pretended to know the days — a supply wagon ground into the dressing-station yard trailing mud like a bridal train, and out of it climbed forty men of the 3rd Labour Battalion, and every single one of them wore boots. Good boots. English leather, double-stitched, the kind that outlasts the feet inside them.

I noticed because our patients had stopped having boots. Boots were the first thing requisitioned when men went back up the line; they were issued to the next man down. Feet, we kept. Boots circulated. It was one of the war's better jokes, if you liked your humour the way you liked your coffee: bitter, repeated, and never quite hot enough.

And I noticed because I had bought those particular boots. Six years ago, in a shop on a street that has since been renamed twice, for a brother who was leaving for a country I refused to name aloud.

Samson Oyelaran stepped down from the wagon last, which I later learned was habit rather than humility — he always let the others go first so he could be sure no one fell behind. He was broader than I remembered. Greyer at the temples, though he was thirty-one and had no right. He wore the boots like an accusation.

For a long moment neither of us moved. The yard went on around us — stretcher-bearers swearing at a wheel-axle, Sister Marguerite counting morphine vials under her breath like a rosary, Amara nowhere in sight, thank God, because she notices everything and asks about it after.

"You've got mud on your collar," Samson said finally.

"You've got my boots," I said.

And there it was. Six years of silence, two continents of grievance, one betrayal whose ownership we had never agreed upon — compressed into eleven words, delivered across a yard full of dying men, as though we were arguing over a borrowed umbrella.

He crossed the distance between us. I will give him that; he has never once made me cross a room first. He smelled of engine oil and river water and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, of the soap Mama used to buy, the brown kind, the kind that came wrapped in newspaper. Grief does that. It hides in the smell of things and waits years for its moment.

"Theo." My name in his mouth sounded older than either of us.

"Corporal Oyelaran," I said, because I am not always the better person, whatever Marguerite hopes. "You're late."

* * *

They put his battalion to work dredging the drainage channel behind the ward and reinforcing the embankment where the river had begun to eye the low quarter the way a cat eyes a goldfish bowl. The officers called them pioneers, which is what one calls men who are given shovels instead of rifles so that no one has to write home about their deaths.

The labour battalions were not on anyone's evacuation manifests. I checked. Of course I checked. I had begun checking everything against my ledger the way a suspicious wife checks pockets, and the 3rd Labour appeared nowhere — not on Colonel Vasseur's rolls, not on the barge schedules, not in the margins where someone might have pencilled *later*. They were, administratively speaking, part of the landscape. Like the bridge. Like the flood that was coming to drown us all.

I did not tell Samson this on the first day. Or the second. I told him about the ward, about Marguerite, about nothing. We spoke the way strangers speak at funerals — carefully, in present tense, about the deceased.

On the third evening he found me by the lantern post. Amara had gone to bed in the linen closet we'd converted, and the yard was quiet except for the river doing its slow arithmetic below the embankment.

"She counts backwards," Samson said. "The little one. I heard her through the wall. From a hundred. She says it keeps the bad numbers out."

"It works," I said. "Don't knock it until you've tried it."

"What number do you count from?"

"I don't count. I write. Same principle, worse handwriting."

He almost smiled. It was a narrow thing, that smile, hemmed in on both sides by everything we hadn't said, but it got through. Then he sat down on the mounting block beside me, and the boots caught the lantern light, and I thought: six years. Six years since Lagos. Six years since the letter I wrote him that he never answered, or answered too late, or answered in a way I decided not to read — memory has helpfully blurred the sequence, the way it blurs all wounds that would otherwise be fatal.

"You kept them," I said. "All this time."

"They're good boots."

"That's not what I asked."

He turned one ankle, considering the leather, the stitching, the whole archaeology of the thing. "You bought them the week before you left for France. You said — " he paused, and I could hear him choosing between honesty and mercy, a negotiation I recognised because I conduct it daily — "you said we'd need to walk a long way apart, so we might as well walk well-shod. I thought it was a joke. It took me about four years to understand it wasn't."

"It wasn't."

"Theo." He said it gently, the way one says it to a patient before bad news. "You know I didn't take the money."

There it was. Laid on the mounting block between us like a scalpel on a tray.

I looked at the river. The river looked at nothing; rivers are excellent at that. "You signed the confession," I said. "Whatever you didn't take, you signed for it."

"Because the alternative was you signing it. And you were nineteen, and it would have ended your nursing candidacy before it started, and Papa would have — " He stopped. Recalculated. "It was the only trade available. My reputation for yours. I made it. I've never once regretted making it. What I regret is that you let me."

"*Let* you?" Something hot rose in my throat, six years old and perfectly preserved. "I stood in that office and said it wasn't mine and nobody — not Papa, not the matron, not you — nobody looked at me. You sat there with your head down like a man at his own trial and you *confessed*, Samson. What was I supposed to do? Overrule you? With what authority? A nineteen-year-old girl's word against her brother's signature?"

"Yes," he said simply. "That's exactly what you were supposed to do. And when you didn't, I understood something about you that I've spent six years trying to un-understand."

"Which is?"

"That you'd rather carry a thing yourself than owe anyone the carrying." He looked at me then, straight on, and his eyes were Mama's eyes, which felt like being ambushed by a photograph. "So I stopped writing. Because every letter I sent would have been a debt, and you'd have burned it unread."

I wanted to say: I read them. Every one. I have them in a tin under my cot, and I can recite the opening lines of each, and I have hated you efficiently and completely since the spring of 1912, and hatred, it turns out, is just love with nowhere to put itself.

What I said was: "Your bootlaces are done wrong. Cross underneath, not over. You'll ruin the eyelets."

He looked down. He laughed — one short, startled bark, the laugh of a man discovering the floor has been removed from an argument he'd furnished for six years. "God. You're insufferable."

"It runs in the family."

* * *

From the pocket journal of Corporal S. Oyelaran, 3rd Labour Battalion, undated but written, I judge, sometime in his second week — I did not see these pages until much later; I include them here because a ledger should be complete, and because some accounts can only be settled in another man's hand:

*Dear Adaeze,*

*I found her.*

*You'll want more than that, so: she is thinner, and louder, and exactly the same. She runs a dressing station on the river like a woman running a country nobody voted for. She has adopted a child who counts backwards, quarrels daily with a French nun who appears to love her, and keeps some private book she hides inside a hymnal, which fooled me for precisely four seconds, though I had the sense not to say so.*

*She hasn't forgiven me. She has done something more confusing: she has filed the grievance somewhere accessible and continues to consult it. She criticised my bootlaces within ten minutes of our first real conversation. Our sister, Adaeze. Six years of silence and her opening move was laces.*

*I think that means the door is open. I think it also means she'll die before she walks through it herself, so I may have to carry her, and she will hate that, and I will do it anyway, because I am her brother and some offices you hold for life whether the incumbent approves or not.*

*The work here is mud and timber and being invisible. The engineers treat us decently. The staff officers look through us as if we were weather. There is talk — the men talk, the bargemen talk — about the bridge closing and who goes and who stays, and the way the talk goes silent when an officer passes tells me which column we're in.*

*I won't write that to her yet. One catastrophe per letter. That was Mama's rule and I keep it the way I keep the boots: badly, but permanently.*

*Tell Papa — no. Tell him I'm well, and leave the rest. Some ledgers he and I will settle ourselves or not at all.*

*Your son, your brother,*
*S.*

*P.S. — She measures everything by what can be carried. Did I mention that? A nurse weighs a man's chances by how many arms it takes to lift him. She weighs her own life the same way. I intend to find out what she's decided she cannot carry, and then I intend to make it heavier until she puts it down.*

* * *

He started appearing at the ward in off-hours, ostensibly to repair things. This is a lie men tell; Samson repaired nothing that needed repairing and much that didn't. He rehung a door that hung fine. He fixed the pump handle that Marguerite had already fixed, and the two of them argued about washers with the genuine pleasure of people who have found a safe subject. He whittled Amara a top from a piece of barge pine, and she spun it while counting backwards from a hundred, and declared that it kept the bad numbers out even better than counting alone, which Samson received like a knighthood.

I watched him with her and felt something rearrange itself in my chest, painful, like a bone setting. Here was the man I had built a fortress against, sitting on a crate teaching a nine-year-old orphan to whistle through a blade of grass, and the fortress had not so much fallen as been quietly discovered to have been built on his land all along.

"You're good at that," I said, one evening, nodding at the grass-whistle lesson.

"I had practice. Someone used to follow me around demanding I teach her things. Insufferable child. Terrible at whistling."

"She improved."

"So I hear." He set down the knife. "Theo. The battalion. Nobody's said it to our faces, so I'll say it plainly: we're not on the barge lists. Any of them."

"I know."

"How long have you known?"

"Since the day you arrived. I checked the manifests the same night."

He absorbed this. Outside, the river muttered against the pilings, keeping its own books. "And you didn't say anything."

"I was deciding how."

"Deciding *how*," he repeated, and shook his head, half anger, half something warmer that he was working hard to disguise. "Six years, and you're still — " he gestured at me, at all of me, the whole stubborn architecture — "still trying to carry it alone. Two hundred people on that book of yours, a child, a nun, a drowning town, and now your brother, and you stand there calculating how to break it to me like I'm a patient with a bad prognosis."

"You *are* a patient with a bad prognosis. Everyone here is. That's rather the situation."

"And your answer?"

"My answer," I said, "is that I'm working on it."

"Work faster." He stood, and picked up his coat, and paused at the door with his hand on the frame — the same doorway, I realised, he'd walked through the first evening, wearing my boots, carrying six years of unsaid things like sandbags against a rising tide. "The money, Theo. In case it matters. There was never any missing money. Papa invented the shortage to force one of us out — he'd decided the practice couldn't support two heirs, and you were the one with prospects worth protecting. I worked it out two years after I signed. I never told you because — "

"Because it would have been a debt," I finished.

"Because it would have meant you signed away your brother for a fiction." His voice was level, which cost him; I could see the cost in his hands. "I wanted to spare you that arithmetic."

The lantern between us guttered and steadied. Somewhere across the yard, Amara's counting drifted faintly through the wall — eighty-seven, eighty-six — keeping the bad numbers out, keeping all of us in.

"Your boots," I said at last, because I could not say the other thing, not yet, maybe not ever, but soon, I promised myself, soon, when the water gave me leave. "Lace them properly tomorrow."

He went out into the dark. I sat a long while with the ledger hidden in its hymnal, listening to the river rise, and I understood that the hardest entry I would ever make had nothing to do with names at all.

It was near midnight when a runner brought word that Colonel Vasseur required the senior nurse at headquarters at dawn. He wished, the runner said, to discuss *berths*.

The word sat in the room like ice in a glass.

Eighty-five, Amara counted, beyond the wall. Eighty-four.
