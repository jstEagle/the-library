## 6. Colonel Vasseur's Arithmetic

There is a particular pleasure in a clean ledger, and I have never been ashamed of it. Most men find arithmetic cold. I find it merciful. Grief is infinite; numbers are not. A number can be held, compared, dismissed. Two hundred souls is an abstraction. Two hundred is also precisely the number of stretcher-bearers I do not have, divided by the tonnage of grain I cannot spare, multiplied by the weeks until the thaw, and when you finish the sum there is nothing left over for sentiment.

I was at my table in the customs house by six o'clock, which by then was four o'clock by the tower clock, which had begun to lose time the way losing men lose their names — gradually, then all at once. The stove ticked. My adjutant, Lieutenant Perrin, a decent boy whom I intended to preserve from knowing too much, poured my coffee and laid out the morning's papers: requisitions, casualty returns, the manifest projections for the evacuation barges.

Three barges remained before the bridge closed for repairs ahead of the thaw. The last of them would carry forty. I wrote *40* on the corner of the projection sheet and looked at it for some time, the way one looks at a hole in a floor one must eventually cross.

The town held, by my best estimate — that is, by Nurse Oyelaran's estimate, since her figures ran consistently higher than mine and were, annoyingly, consistently correct — something under nine hundred persons requiring removal. Combatants first, obviously; then essential personnel; then civilians with labor value; then the rest. The rest was where arithmetic became philosophy.

"Sir," Perrin said, "the engineers' report on the bridge."

"Later."

He set it down anyway, because he was decent rather than obedient, and slid out. I read it with my coffee going cold. The central span could hold barge traffic perhaps three more weeks if the water stayed low. If the thaw came early — and the rivermen said it would, they always say it will, and half the time they are right — the valley would flood to the second-story line within forty-eight hours of the first warm rain. The low quarter first. The hospital last, being built on slightly higher ground by someone with more foresight than urgency.

I made a small mark beside the figure for the low quarter's population. It is important to make marks. A mark converts a person into a datum, and a datum can be weighed against another datum without anyone weeping at the table.

* * *

Here is what the ledger in my head contained that morning, and here is what no one else at this headquarters knew in full:

The evacuation schedule was not set by me. It reached me from division, and division received it from the ministry, and somewhere above the ministry sat men whose names I knew only as initials on signal flimsies. The bridge closure was dated three weeks earlier than engineering necessity required. I had checked. I am a man who checks. The repair window was fiction; the thaw was real, but the timetable served the thaw rather than outrunning it.

Why? Because manifests are political documents. Because certain names in this town — union men from the ropewalk, a schoolteacher who wrote letters the censors flagged twice, families with the wrong surnames from across the river — were more useful stranded than saved. Stranded men petition. Stranded men can be arrested afterward at leisure, quietly, without trains and witnesses. Evacuation is a form of amnesty, and someone upstairs had decided against amnesty for two hundred-odd inconvenient persons, most of them women, children, and men with damaged legs.

I want to be clear about what I did with this knowledge, because clarity is the only virtue remaining to me. I did not object. I signed the schedules. I adjusted my own projections so that the official rolls came out tidy — forty berths on the last barge, priority categories filled, remainder "deferred pending secondary transport" — knowing full well that secondary transport was a phrase meaning *the flood*. And I told myself the story that all men of my rank tell themselves, which is that the machine is larger than the man, and a colonel who refuses his signature merely gets replaced by a colonel who signs faster, and the two hundred drown either way while the refuser drowns with them. All true. Truth has never once stopped anything.

But I kept one private column in my mental ledger, and it was this: the marsh road.

* * *

There is an overland route south through the marshes. An old drover's causeway, raised stone, running from the tannery lane to the high ground past the eel fisheries, passable only at low water and only at night, unless one wished to be observed — by whom, I had never determined, and for three years I had not asked, because asking questions is how men become casualties of their own curiosity.

I had walked it once, in the spring of '15, with a survey party mapping fallback positions. Captain Deschamps had called it a goat track and refused to log it. That refusal was the single act of bureaucratic negligence for which I have ever been grateful, because an unlogged road does not appear on any map division holds, and therefore does not appear in any order concerning who may leave this valley and who may drown in it.

The causeway was my secret in the way that a loaded rifle in a nursery is a secret. I had done nothing with it. I told myself I was keeping it in reserve — for wounded officers, for the garrison's withdrawal, for some contingency in which my own name appeared on the list of those worth moving. The reserve had a fine moral sound. It amounted to this: I knew the way out, and the two hundred did not, and every week I did not speak of it, the difference between us compounded like interest.

That morning I drew the causeway on the back of Perrin's engineers' report, freehand, from memory, and looked at my own hand doing it, and wondered — not for the first time, but with new precision — at what point a man stops deciding things and simply watches himself decide them.

Then I burned the sketch in the stove, because I was not yet ready to be the kind of man who acts, only the kind who knows he could.

* * *

Perrin returned at ten with the day's real business: the berth allocations for the final barge. This is the document that would matter, though none of us knew yet which days mattered; the war teaches you that all days look alike until one of them doesn't.

"The senior nursing sister gets a guaranteed berth," Perrin said. "Division's standing instruction. Medical continuity of care for the transit." He said it in the flat recitation of a man handing over something unpleasant. He had learned that from me. I noted the debt.

"Nurse Oyelaran," I said. Not Sister Marguerite — Marguerite held formal rank, but division's instruction specified senior *field* nurse, and Oyelaran had the field time. I had watched her at the dressing station in October, working by lantern light with the water rising over her boot soles, writing in that private book of hers between patients. Yes, I knew about the book. I have eyes; I lack the energy to close them.

She was a problem, that woman, and I mean the word with something adjacent to respect. Her counts ran high. Her counts included people the official rolls excluded — civilians treated in the ward, labor battalion men listed as materiel, the orphan she kept fed out of her own ration. She recorded them in a green ledger she believed was hidden. It was hidden adequately, which is different. And a record of omissions is a record of the omissions' authors, and the authors sit at desks above mine with initials instead of names.

"You're frowning at the name, sir," Perrin said.

"I am calculating, Lieutenant. There is a difference."

There was not, much. Here was the calculation: give Oyelaran her guaranteed berth, and she left with her book, and her book surfaced someday in a war office or a newspaper, and the initials upstairs had questions, and the questions traveled downward along the chain of signature to the last hand that touched the paper. Mine. Refuse her the berth, and I manufactured a martyr with documentation.

Or — the third option, which arrived in my head fully formed, the way the worst options do, wearing its best clothes: offer her a trade. Two berths, hers and one of her choosing, in exchange for the ledger surrendered to me for destruction. She would take it. People always believe they will be the exception to the rule that everyone can be bought, and the currency need not be money; safety is finer coin. She had the child. Everyone in this town knew about the child. A woman with a child does not negotiate from principle; she negotiates from bedtime.

I instructed Perrin to draft the allocation with Oyelaran's berth confirmed and one supplementary, "at the nurse's discretion, medical staff category." Perrin wrote it down without question, which is the tragedy of lieutenants: obedience looks identical to trust.

"And the marsh road?" he asked, at the door, casually, the way junior officers ask questions they have been told not to ask. "Deschamps used to say—"

"Deschamps is at division now," I said, "where roads that don't exist stay that way. Thank you, Lieutenant."

He went. I sat with my cooling coffee and the burned smell of the sketch still in the room, and I performed the exercise I perform each evening, which is the only confession available to a man of my construction: I added the columns.

Forty berths. Nine hundred souls. Two hundred omitted. One secret road. One nurse with a book. One child, age approximately nine, unrecorded anywhere, which meant that officially she did not exist, which meant that officially she could not be evacuated, which meant that the woman I intended to blackmail carried precisely the leverage I required and would know it the moment I spoke.

Acceptable losses, per the projections I would sign by afternoon: two hundred and eleven, plus variance. I wrote the figure, and beneath it, in my own private shorthand that no censor would read, I wrote the truer figure: *two hundred and eleven, minus whatever courage costs.*

Because that is the entire balance sheet, gentlemen, and I was honest enough even then to see the entry I could not control. Arithmetic assumes the numbers stay where you put them. Numbers do not march at night. Numbers do not keep books of their own. Somewhere across the flooded square a woman was writing names I had subtracted, and a child was learning them by heart, and a labor battalion of men I had logged as tonnage was stacked within walking distance of my secret road.

I signed the projections. The pen moved smoothly; pens always do; that is how the worst things travel.

That evening the tower clock stopped altogether, or so Perrin reported — the whole town suddenly without a shared hour, everyone's time private now, divergent, unreliable. I wound my own watch out of habit and heard it ticking against the silence like a small stubborn animal.

It was around then that the runner came in with the labor battalion muster sheets — and there, third page, in ink that had smudged, was a name I had not seen in six years and had no wish to see again, written in a hand I recognized instantly, because I had once taught that hand to form its letters properly, on a slate, in another country, before either of us understood what arithmetic cost.

Oyelaran. S.

I put the muster sheets face-down on the table and sat for a long moment in the ticking dark, doing the one calculation I had spent three years avoiding.
