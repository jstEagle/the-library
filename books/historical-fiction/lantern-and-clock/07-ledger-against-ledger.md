## 7. Ledger Against Ledger

The official rolls arrived on a Tuesday, though I only knew it was Tuesday because Sister Marguerite said so and because the bread was the stale kind that always came the day after the fresh. The war had taken most things from us — mirrors, men, the notion of an ending — but it had left the calendar intact, out of what I can only assume was spite.

The rolls came in a canvas satchel carried by a corporal so young he still apologized when he stepped over legs. He set them on my desk in the dressing station's back room, which had once been a pantry and still smelled faintly of onions, as if the onions themselves had refused to be conscripted.

"Compliments of Captain Delorme," he said. "He asks that discrepancies be reported to him directly."

"Discrepancies," I repeated, tasting the word. It was a good word, a clean word, the sort of word a man uses when he wants to believe the mess is merely clerical.

"Yes, miss. Discrepancies."

I thanked him and watched him go, stepping carefully over a sleeping gunner who would lose three toes to frostbite by Friday, and then I sat down with the satchel and felt something in my chest do a thing I cannot better describe than *lean forward*.

Here is what you must understand about me, and I confess it without shame: I am a woman who trusts paper. Not people — people lie for excellent reasons and terrible ones and sometimes for no reason at all, the way rivers run. But paper is patient. Paper does not know it is being interrogated. A list cannot look at you with the eyes of a man who has already decided what the world contains. And so I spread the official rolls across the pantry table like a fortune-teller laying out cards, except my cards were stamped and initialed and contained, I suspected, considerably fewer fortunes.

My own ledger sat beside them in its oilcloth wrapper. I had bought it in June from a stationer in Amiens, a fat black thing with a marbled cover, the kind of book meant for grocers' inventories or the accounts of a modest mill. Fourteen francs. It seemed absurd even then, paying fourteen francs for what would become the most dangerous object in the valley, but stationers do not sell books labeled *for recording what officers prefer not to count*, and one makes do.

* * *

The method was simple, and simplicity was all it had to recommend it, because everything else about the work was misery.

Column one: the official roll. Names, ranks where applicable, villages where the clerks had bothered. Column two: my ledger. The same, plus the ones the clerks hadn't bothered with — the civilians treated in the dressing station's east aisle, the laborers hurt unloading barges, the old woman from the tannery road whose name I never learned and whom I had entered, shamefacedly, as *Woman, tannery road, burned hands, survived*. Column three: the difference.

I worked by lamplight after the ward settled, which meant I worked between ten at night and whenever my own hands began to shake too badly to be trusted with someone's name. There is a particular horror in misspelling a man into oblivion. I checked each entry twice, then a third time if the ink looked doubtful, and I developed a system of small marks in the margin — a tick for confirmed twice, a cross for uncertain, a small circle for the dead, because I could not bring myself to strike anyone through entirely. Striking through seemed like a second dying.

For four nights I did this work, and the numbers grew under my hands like something alive, and I confess there were moments — terrible moments, moments I have not told the priest about because I suspect he would give me absolution and I am not sure I want it — when I *enjoyed* it. When the arithmetic clicked into place and another omitted soul stood up out of the bureaucratic fog and became a line of ink, a fact, a person who had existed whether or not Captain Delorme's superiors wished him to, I felt something adjacent to triumph. As if I were smuggling them across a border one at a time, folded flat between pages.

Then came the fifth night, and the fifth night gave me the total, and the total was two hundred and eleven.

Two hundred and eleven souls in this river valley — wounded, sheltering, working, hiding, breathing — whom the official rolls did not account for. Not wounded-and-sent-back, not missing-presumed, not any of the soft categories armies use to file away what inconveniences them. Simply *absent from the record*, as if the floodwater of the spring thaw were being rehearsed in advance on paper. Two hundred and eleven people who could stand on the bridge with their hands raised and be, officially, nobody.

I put down my pen. My hand had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the pantry's chill.

You must imagine what two hundred and eleven looks like when you know the faces. It is not a number. Numbers are what the Colonel uses; I have watched him do his sums through the ward like a man counting spoons before guests arrive. Two hundred and eleven is Madame Fouché and her bad hip. It is the twins from the mill who carry stretchers for rations. It is little Émile the errand boy who is nine years old and lies about it to seem useful, the way children do, the way Amara does not because Amara counts instead of lying. Two hundred and eleven is forty-one barges' worth of berths that will never come, is a number that walks and eats and prays and will, when the thaw comes and the valley drowns on schedule, drown with it unless somebody with a stamp says otherwise.

And nobody with a stamp was going to say otherwise, because the stamps had made these lists, and the lists had been made by subtraction.

* * *

Sister Marguerite found me at dawn, or rather dawn found us both and she happened upon me sitting exactly where the dark had left me, the ledger open under my hands and the official rolls stacked like a headstone.

She is not tall, Marguerite. She has the compact permanence of a church pew, and her hands, which have done thirty years of other people's washing and lifting and holding-down, are the gentlest brutal instruments I know. She looked at the table. She looked at my face. She is very good at looking at faces; it is most of her profession.

"You've finished it," she said. Not a question.

"Two hundred and eleven."

She did not say anything. She took the kettle off the shelf — the pantry still had its hook for it — and set water to heat on the spirit stove, because Marguerite believes every catastrophe deserves tea, and some of them deserve two cups. Then she sat down across from me and read the ledger the way she reads everything, thoroughly and without mercy, and I watched her finger move down the columns and I waited for her to say what I had been needing someone to say for five nights.

What she said was: "Burn it."

I laughed. I couldn't help it; it came out of me like a cork. "Burn it. Marguerite, there are two hundred and eleven—"

"I know how many there are." She closed the ledger softly, softly, the way she closes the eyes of the men we fail. "I counted them months ago. In my head, because I don't write mine down. You think you're the only one who keeps tally? I have been counting the omitted since the retreat last spring. I carry them" — she touched her temple — "here, where no inspection can find them. And let me tell you what I have learned, Theo Oyelaran, since you insist on learning everything the hard way: accuracy is dangerous."

"The truth is dangerous?"

"The *truth* is inconvenient." She poured the water, and the smell of cheap tea rose up and mixed with the onion ghost of the pantry. "Accuracy is worse. Truth is a general thing, a weather thing — everyone agrees it rains, no one is blamed for the rain. But your book here—" she laid one hand flat on the oilcloth "—your book is *specific*. It has dates. It has wounds described by location and hour. It has, I see, the notation that on the fourth of October, forty civilian casualties were carried past the triage point and entered nowhere, while the same day's official report records, what does it record, let me look—"

"You don't need to—"

"—records *twelve treated, minor*." She looked up at me, and her eyes, which are usually the color of forgiveness, were the color of flint that morning. "That is not truth, child. That is evidence."

"It's names."

"It's a noose with names written on it, which is worse, because they'll hang you with your own handwriting and call it efficiency. Theo. Listen to me. I have been in service to this army longer than you have been alive, and I will tell you the one thing I know for certain: the men who make lists like Delorme's lists do not fear being wrong. They fear being *documented*. The moment your ledger exists on paper, it stops being a kindness to two hundred and eleven people and becomes a weapon pointed at powerful men, and powerful men respond to weapons, and their response generally arrives by artillery."

"They're going to drown anyway," I said. My voice sounded far away to me, thin as the tea. "When the thaw comes, when the front goes, the low quarter floods and nobody evacuates people who aren't on a manifest. That's not conjecture, Marguerite, that's arithmetic. Their arithmetic. If I stay quiet, the ledger kills them just as surely as if I'd drowned them myself, only slower and with better manners."

"And if you speak?" She leaned forward. "If you take this to Delorme, or above Delorme, do you know what happens? Nothing. Nothing happens, first. That's how you'll know it's been received. Then questions. Then an inspection. Then perhaps your posting changes, and the ledger sits in a drawer in some office in Rouen until the ink fades, and in the spring the water comes up over the tannery road exactly as you say, and the only difference is that you won't be here to hold anybody's hand."

The stove ticked. Somewhere beyond the wall, in the ward, a man asked for his mother in a language none of us spoke, and one of the orderlies soothed him in French, which is the army's way of saying *close enough*.

"There's a middle path," I said finally.

"There is always a middle path. It is usually paved with good intentions and mined at both ends."

"Someone should know. Outside the valley. Someone who isn't stampable. If the names exist somewhere the army can't reach—"

Marguerite sighed then, long and slow, the sigh of a woman watching a younger version of herself walk toward a cliff with excellent posture. She wrapped her hands around her cup.

"Do you know what I did before the war, in the Congo station?" she said. "No. Of course you don't; I don't talk about it, which should tell you enough. I will say only this once, and then we will drink our tea and never speak of it again: I once kept a book too. Small one. Accurate. And I learned that the men I thought would be ashamed were instead *grateful* — grateful to have the shape of their cruelty confirmed in writing, grateful for the tidiness of it. Accuracy doesn't shame power, Theo. Power reads accuracy the way a surgeon reads an X-ray: *ah, yes, there is the break, now hand me the saw.*"

"That's defeatism."

"That's experience." She drank. "Keep your names, if you must. Keep them in your skull like I keep mine, where they weigh nothing and hang no one. But do not — I am begging you, and I have not begged for anything since 1914 — do not let that book leave this room, and do not let anyone see you loving it, because the day someone realizes what it is, it stops being yours and starts being leverage, and there are men in this valley who collect leverage the way other men collect stamps."

I looked at the ledger. Under the lamplight the ink had gone brownish, the brown of dried blood or old varnish, and the two hundred and eleven lay inside it like passengers asleep in a burning house, and I understood — with the vertiginous lurch of a woman standing on a bridge and feeling the rail hum — that both of us were right. That keeping it was danger and destroying it was betrayal and carrying it in my skull was impossible, because my skull already held too much and leaked at night into dreams I paid for with the morning.

"Who else knows?" Marguerite asked quietly.

"No one. Only you."

"Only me." She considered this. "And the girl? Does the girl know?"

"Amara thinks it's my prayer book."

Marguerite almost smiled. "Children believe what we need them to believe. God grant she keeps believing it." She stood, gathered her skirts, paused at the door with her hand on the latch. "One more thing, and then I'm on shift. If you ever do find your middle path — the one that's mined at both ends — you come to me before you walk it. Not after. I've buried enough people who consulted me afterward."

The door closed behind her. The stove ticked down toward silence. And I sat alone in the onion-smelling dark with two hundred and eleven lives bound in marbled board and fourteen francs' worth of consequence, doing the arithmetic that Marguerite's warning had opened in me like a wound: not *should* the ledger survive, but *who else wants it to* — and why, when I turned that question over, did the answer feel less like arithmetic and more like a trap being measured for my size?

Because it was that same morning, folding the ledger back into its oilcloth, that I heard the orderlies whispering at the ward door: Colonel Vasseur had arrived early, unannounced, and was asking — by name — for me.
