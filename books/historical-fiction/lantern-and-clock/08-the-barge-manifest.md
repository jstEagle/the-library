## 8. The Barge Manifest

The paper arrived at nine in the morning, which I considered a personal insult. Important papers come at dusk or dawn, delivered by officers with faces like folded laundry; they do not come at nine in the morning while you are elbow-deep in someone else's blood and trying to remember whether you boiled the last of the instruments or merely wished them clean.

Captain Delorme stood in the doorway of the dressing station holding it — a single sheet, typed, with a stamp so red it looked freshly slapped.

"Sister Oyelaran."

"Captain." I did not remove my elbows from their work. Private Coignet, whose shoulder I was repacking, had earned the right to my full attention by virtue of having only the one shoulder left worth attending to.

"You'll want to read this when you're… finished."

"I'll want to read it now, actually, since you're standing there radiating importance like a stove." I held out a hand, dripping as it was. "Paper dries. Gangrene doesn't wait for tidy hands."

He gave me the sheet. He was young enough that being spoken to sharply still startled him rather than bored him, and I envied him that the way one envies anyone anything in the winter of 1917: briefly, uselessly, and with a small private shame.

The document was headed *EVACUATION — FINAL PHASE*, and beneath the heading, in the language of men who have learned to make abandonment sound like logistics, it announced that the barge *Perseverance* — a name chosen by someone with a sense of humor I could have done without — would depart on the last day before the bridge closed for seasonal repair. She would carry forty souls. Forty. The town held, by my own careful count, something north of six hundred people the army had not managed to notice yet, but who's counting?

I was counting. That was rather the whole trouble with me.

"The medical allocation," Delorme said, and here he cleared his throat, "includes one guaranteed berth for the senior nurse on station. That's you."

"Is it," I said. Not a question. A place to stand while the floor moved.

"Seniority is seniority. HQ was explicit." He shifted his weight. "Congratulations are presumably in order."

"Presumably." I looked down at Private Coignet, who had the grace to pretend his hearing had been damaged along with everything else in the region of his collarbone. "You may tell HQ that Sister Oyelaran is honored beyond expression and will consider her answer with the gravity it deserves."

Which was a lie, because I had already considered it, in the way one considers a falling object: instantly, entirely, and with no hope of unseeing it.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being offered salvation: it arrives with arithmetic attached.

Forty berths. One of them mine, if I wanted it, and wanting it required nothing more than signing my name to a manifest — the same kind of manifest, I noted, that had been shrinking all autumn like a wool sweater in a hot wash. Every week the official lists grew shorter and my ledger grew longer, and the difference between those two documents was made entirely of people.

I spent the afternoon doing sums I didn't want to do, in the corner of the ward where the lamp reached and the dying couldn't overhear.

Forty berths. Subtract crew, though whether the crew counted against the forty was a question I decided not to ask, because if they did, the number of saved souls dropped to something obscene. Subtract whatever officers' favorites had already been penciled in — there were always penciled names; I had seen the manifests, and the pencils of headquarters were busier than its conscience. Call it thirty places for a town of six hundred unnoticed people. Thirty.

And one of those thirty had my name on it in advance. Guaranteed. Senior nurse, years of service, letters of commendation filed away by men who thought paper was the same thing as memory. For the first time in my professional life, my competence had become dangerous — not to me, but to everyone whose claim was weaker than mine and stronger than almost everyone's.

Because Amara had no claim at all.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was the only prayer I knew how to say. Amara was not on any roll. She had never been on any roll. She had appeared at the edge of our latrine trench in June, nine years old, carrying a smaller child's shoes and no smaller child, and she had simply refused, through some mechanism of will stronger than any regulation, to be sent anywhere. There was no paperwork making her mine. No baptismal record the army would honor, no village register that hadn't burned, no status the clerks could enter into a column. In the eyes of the manifest she did not exist, and I had spent half a year keeping her alive precisely inside that blindness, feeding her from my ration, teaching her letters under the blanket at night — invisible, invisible, invisible.

Invisibility, it turns out, is a currency that spends very badly when boats are being loaded.

A guaranteed berth for me. Nothing for her. And somewhere across the compound, in a barracks he shared with forty other men of the 214th Labor Company, my brother Samson — six years of silence, three months of awkward truce — was slated to stay behind with his battalion, because labor battalions were slated to stay behind, that being what labor battalions were *for*, in the arithmetic of men who never carried anything heavier than a pen.

I measured things by what could be carried. It was how I kept myself sane in a world that had stopped weighing human beings on the same scales as shells and hay. And what I was being asked to carry across the river was myself. Just myself. Forty kilograms of nurse with steady hands and a ledger sewn into the lining of her coat, and the implicit promise — stamped, typed, red as a wound — that this was enough. That saving the person holding the book was the same as saving the book.

It isn't. I have known this my whole life and I have never once managed to act like it.

* * *

Sister Marguerite found me at the manifest board that evening.

The board had been erected outside the commissary that afternoon — a sheet of plywood painted white, and on it, in an officer's careful hand, the sailing date, the berth count, and the beginning of a list. Eleven names chalked already. I recognized none of them, which meant I recognized all of them immediately for what they were: staff officers' clerks, a quartermaster's cousin, two men from the postal detachment whose only wounds were bureaucratic. The chalking of names is a ceremony, and like all ceremonies it tells you exactly who is performing it and nothing at all about who it claims to honor.

Marguerite stood beside me with her hands folded into her apron the way she did when she disapproved of something and hadn't yet decided whether to say so. She was sixty, give or take a war, and she had the particular stillness of women who had outlived three sets of rules and expected to outlive these too.

"You've seen your name," she said. It wasn't a question either. Everyone had seen my name; Delorme had told the canteen before he'd told me.

"I've seen it."

"And?"

"And I'm admiring the calligraphy."

"Theo." She used my given name the way a surgeon uses a scalpel — rarely, and only when necessary. "Take the berth."

I looked at her. Snow was starting, the fat, indifferent flakes of early winter, settling on the plywood and melting against the chalked names until the letters ran slightly, so that Lieutenant Aubert became Lieutenant Aube, dawn, which struck me as either an omen or an editorial comment depending on one's theology.

"Say the rest of it," I said.

"There's no rest of it. Take the berth. You're offered it, it's yours, and a drowned martyr is of use to no one. I've watched brave women drown themselves in other people's emergencies for thirty years and let me tell you what it accomplishes: a shorter line at the ward and a nicer paragraph in someone's report." Her voice stayed level, which was how I knew the speech had been rehearsed. Marguerite rehearsed nothing she felt and everything she feared. "The girl goes to the convent at Clairfontaine when the town falls back. They take children. It's arranged, it's survivable, and you can send for her after."

"After," I repeated. The word tasted like the river smelled.

"Yes. After."

"Marguerite, the last time this army said *after* to civilians, there were four hundred of them waiting at the depot and two wagons. I wrote their names. All four hundred. I have the book." I tapped my coat where the ledger lived, sewn flat against my ribs like a second heart, which was anatomically inaccurate but emotionally exact. "After is where this army keeps the people it has decided not to save. After is a filing category."

She was quiet for a moment. Snow gathered on her wimple and she did not brush it off, and I understood suddenly that she was older than I had been letting myself see, that the war had been eating her the whole time I'd been congratulating myself on noticing my own appetite.

"She has no claim," Marguerite said finally. "You know it. I know it. The manifest clerk knows it best of all. If you give her your berth, they'll refuse it — a child with no papers, no rank, no name on any list? They'll refuse it and then they'll watch you to see what you do next, because they'll assume you intend something worse."

"They'd be right."

"Theo—"

"They'd be entirely right," I said, "and that is the first honest feeling I've had since the paper came at nine o'clock this morning, which, I'll remind you, is an uncivilized hour for damnation."

* * *

Amara was waiting for me at the lantern post when I came off shift.

We had a ritual, she and I, dating from the summer nights when the shelling kept regular hours and she kept irregular terror: at dusk we lit the lantern on the post outside the schoolhouse where she slept in the supply room, and while I struck the match she counted backwards from one hundred, and if we reached zero before the flame caught, something terrible was permitted to be true, and if the lantern won the race, it wasn't. The system had no theological merit whatsoever. It worked every single time.

That night she was counting before I even reached her. "Quatre-vingt-dix-sept, quatre-vingt-seize…" — she had learned French numbers from the soldiers and English numbers from me, and she mixed them the way she mixed everything, promiscuously and without apology, a little girl assembled from salvage.

"Ninety-one. Ninety." The match flared. The wick took. "Zero," she said, satisfied, and looked up at me with that face of hers — sharp-chinned, solemn, missing one front tooth and pretending not to miss it. "You're late. Late means thinking."

"Late means mud," I said. "The mud between the ward and here has opinions tonight."

"You're thinking anyway. Your mouth does a shape when you think. Like this." She made a shape. It looked like a woman biting down on a coin.

"Insolent child."

"Your child," she said, matter-of-factly, and took my hand, and we walked, and the lantern swung its small yellow sovereignty over the snow, and I thought: *the manifest says forty, and this hand weighs nothing, nothing at all, less than my kit, less than my boots, and there is no column on any form in any language in which this hand appears.*

"The barge," she said, in the tone she reserved for words she'd heard adults use and decided to try on. "The big boat. Odile. Am I on the boat?"

The question arrived the way shells arrive: you hear it coming long enough to know there is nowhere to go.

There are lies that are kindnesses and lies that are cowardice and lies that are simply load-bearing walls in the house of surviving a winter with a child, and I did not yet know which kind I needed, so I bought time the way I always did, with procedure.

"Why do you ask?"

"Claudette says the boat takes important people. Nurses and captains." Claudette was eleven and knew everything incorrectly. "Are you going on the boat?"

"The boat," I said, "hasn't sailed yet."

"That's not an answer. That's the shape your mouth makes when you don't answer."

Nine years old, and she had already mapped my evasions better than I had. Somewhere in the dark behind us the bridge groaned under a transport convoy, chains and timber complaining like an old man asked to carry furniture one more time, and I stood in the cold holding a child's hand and a guaranteed berth in the same grip, and understood that the two objects had begun, quietly and irrevocably, to change places — that the thing I owned and the thing I loved had entered negotiations, and only one of them could win.

"Amara," I said. "Count for me again. Backwards. Slowly."

She gave me the look — the one that said she knew exactly what I was doing, allowing me my rituals as one allows an old woman her rosary — and began. "Cent, quatre-vingt-dix-neuf…"

I listened to the numbers fall away like snow, each one a second further from the paper at headquarters, each one a second the barge had not yet sailed, the bridge had not yet closed, the thaw had not yet come to drown the valley and everyone the officers' lists had failed to mention. Time, for once, was on the side of counting. We had three weeks. Perhaps.

At zero, the lantern was still burning, and Amara smiled her gap-toothed smile, and I walked her to the supply-room door and kissed the top of her head and told her, in a voice I kept level through sheer professional discipline, to sleep well.

Then I stood alone in the snow and admitted what I had known since nine o'clock that morning, since the red stamp, since Delorme's throat-clearing congratulations:

There was no version of the manifest, in any hand, in any ink, in any world I intended to live in, that contained my name and not hers.

Which left the interesting question — the one I would need Samson awake and angry for, the one that had begun pacing around inside me like something with claws — of exactly how far past the law a nurse was prepared to walk to correct a piece of paper.

I put my hand against my ribs, where the ledger lay sleeping, and went in out of the cold to find out.
