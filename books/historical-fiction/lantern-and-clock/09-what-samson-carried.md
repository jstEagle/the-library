## 9. What Samson Carried

The battalion arrived at Saint-Aubin-sur-Meuse on a Tuesday, though I only knew it was Tuesday because a sergeant told me, and I only believed him because he had no reason to lie about anything so useless.

Six years. Six years since my sister had spoken to me, and now here we both stood in a river town that smelled of iodine and mud and the particular sweetness of things going wrong in large quantities, and she looked at me across the duckboards as if I were a bill that had come due at an inconvenient hour.

"You've got grey in your beard," she said.

"You've got blood on your apron," I said. "We've both been busy."

That was Theo. That was always Theo. Other people wept or embraced or made speeches; my sister took inventory. She had been measuring the world by what could be carried since before I knew there was a word for it, and I understood, standing there with a shovel on my shoulder and six years of silence between us, that she was weighing me — deciding what I was worth hauling, whether I was cargo or ballast.

I did not blame her for it. You will understand why in a moment. Or you won't, because you are not her, and neither am I, thank God.

* * *

The labor battalion was lodged in the tannery, which the army had requisitioned and then apparently regretted, because no amount of official paper could make the building stop smelling of its former profession. Forty men on straw pallets, most of them colonial troops who had crossed an ocean to dig trenches for men who could not pronounce their names, and all of them, within a week of arrival, in love with my sister.

I say this without jealousy, or mostly without it. She worked the dressing station near the bridge like a woman keeping a shop open at the end of the world, and the men came through her hands — the wounded, the sick, the ones who simply needed someone to look at them and not flinch — and they came out the other side calling her *Maman Théo* in four languages. She pretended this annoyed her. She kept a list of their names in a little black book she carried everywhere, and I saw her writing in it by lamplight, and when I asked what it was she closed it with a snap and said, "Accounts."

My sister has always kept accounts. This is important too. Hold onto it.

The work itself was simple and endless: shore up the quays before the thaw, reinforce the bridge approaches, haul timber and pump water and dig latrines deeper than vanity required. The engineers talked about the seasonal flood the way priests talk about Judgment Day — with certainty, and with a private hope that they'd be transferred before it arrived. The Meuse, they said, would come up out of its banks like something summoned, and the valley would drown, and everyone's careful arithmetic about how long the front could hold would be done for them by the weather.

"And us?" I asked Sergeant Okonkwo one evening, watching him clean his boots with the thoroughness of a man who had decided his boots were the only thing in Europe he could trust. "When the water comes?"

He didn't look up. "Labor stays," he said. "Labor always stays."

I laughed, because the alternative was to think about it, and thinking was a luxury I had been rationing since 1911.

* * *

Here is the thing I have never told anyone except a priest who died before he could be disappointed in me, and now, apparently, you:

In 1911, in the dispensary at the mission hospital where Theo was training and I was doing clerical work while I convinced myself I had a future, someone stole sixty francs from the matron's lockbox. Not a fortune. Enough. Enough that the person responsible would have been dismissed, disgraced, sent home to Lagos with a stain that no letter of recommendation could scrub out — and Theo was three months from her certification, three months from being the first Oyelaran to hold papers that said she could heal things officially, and I found her that night in the dispensary white as paper with the empty box in her lap, and I knew.

I knew the way you know your own handwriting.

She had taken it. I never learned why — I have my theories, all of them involving some patient whose need outran the hospital's charity, because that is Theo entire: she would steal for the omitted before she'd ask permission from the counted — but the why stopped mattering the moment I heard boots in the corridor. There was no time for discussion. There was only time for me to step forward when the matron demanded to know who had opened the box, and say, "I did."

Why? Because she was going to be brilliant and I was going to be adequate, and the world has room for adequate men who've been dismissed for pilfering but very little room for brilliant women who've been dismissed for the same, and because she was my little sister and I had taught her to swim by throwing her in the river, which tells you everything about my methods and nothing good about my judgment.

She fought me on it. Quietly, later, furiously — the way she fights everything, like weather. She wrote to me for two years afterward. Then she stopped. I let her stop. A man who has confessed to a theft he didn't commit learns to live inside other people's conclusions about him; it seemed fitting to live inside hers too. She believed I had done it. Her silence said so. And some black and stupid part of me thought: good. Let her believe it. If she ever suspected the truth, she'd owe me, and Theo would rather cut off her own hand than owe anyone — especially me, especially for the thing that theft cost her conscience every time she looked at my name.

Guilt inverted. That's what I'm telling you. I wore her crime like a coat for six years and called it brotherhood, and then I resented her for letting me wear it, which is the kind of mathematics that makes priests drink.

And now here we were in Saint-Aubin-sur-Meuse, and she was handing me a mug of tea thin enough to read scripture through, and asking about my boots, and I was carrying the confession around in my chest like shrapnel near the heart, waiting for the weather or the war to bring it to the surface.

* * *

It came up sooner than the flood did.

A Thursday. Rain turning to sleet with the indecision of a season that couldn't commit. I'd carried a requisition chit to the dressing station — timber, nails, the usual begging — and stayed after to watch her work, because watching Theo work was the closest thing to church I'd attended in six years. She moved among the cots like a woman dealing cards, and every man she touched straightened slightly, the way plants lean toward light without knowing they're doing it.

There was a boy on the end cot, nineteen maybe, feverish, with a wound in his thigh that had gone bad. He was talking to the ceiling about his mother's garden. Theo held his hand through the worst of it, and when it was over she closed his eyes, and then she opened the black ledger and wrote his name in it, slowly, the letters formed like they mattered.

"The officers' list won't carry him," she said, without looking up. "They've got him down as 'walked wounded, returned to unit.' He'll be reported a deserter by spring."

"Then write him twice," I said.

Her pen stopped. She looked at me then — really looked, the inventory look — and something shifted behind her eyes like ice shifting on a river.

"You know," she said. "About the book."

"I know you keep accounts nobody else keeps. I know that used to get you into trouble." I set down the chit. "I know it still does."

"The trouble hasn't caught up with me yet."

"It will. Trouble in this army moves slower than the mail but it arrives just as angry." I should have left it there. Any sensible man would have left it there. But the boy's name was drying on the page and the rain was coming down and my sister was standing in a room full of men she'd failed to save, keeping a record of her own failures like penance, and six years of silence suddenly weighed more than the confession did.

"Theo. About 1911—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and fast, a board slammed over a hole.

"—there's something you ought to—"

"*Don't.*" And now she wasn't looking at me at all; she was looking at the ledger, at the boy's name, at forty other names below it, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, the voice she probably used on dying men because it was the only register they could hear. "Whatever it is, whatever speech you've rehearsed on the march here, whatever absolution you've decided you need — I don't want it. Do you understand? I built a life on top of what happened in that dispensary. It has load-bearing walls. You do not get to come back after six years and renovate."

I stood there holding my hat like a man at a funeral who's forgotten which grave he's standing beside.

"You think I don't know?" she went on, quieter now, terrible now. "You think a woman keeps a secret ledger of the uncounted for three years and can't recognize one more name that doesn't appear on any official list? I have known since the week you left Lagos, Samson. I have known every day since, in every year you didn't write, and I have never once forgiven myself for letting you carry it, and I will discuss it with you never. Is that clear? Never is a schedule I can keep."

Well. There it is. Six years of silence and it turned out we hadn't been silent at all; we'd been having the same conversation in two languages, hers made of absence and mine of cowardice, and neither of us had heard a word.

I sat down on an ammunition crate because my legs had opinions my dignity couldn't override. Somewhere down the ward another man began to cough, and Theo moved toward the sound the way water moves downhill, because that was her nature, and then stopped, halfway, her back to me.

"Why did you take the blame?" she asked the wall. "Truly. Not the noble version. The true one."

And because I had spent six years being adequate, I gave her the true one. "Because you were going to matter," I said, "and I wasn't. And because I threw you in the river when you were five and you nearly drowned, and I have been trying to keep you above water ever since, badly, and that was the one time I managed it."

She stood very still. The lamp guttered. Down the ward, the coughing man waited for her, and the war waited outside, and somewhere beyond the bridge the river waited too, patient as arithmetic.

"You idiot," she said, and her voice broke on the second syllable, just once, and then she was gone down the row of cots with her sleeves rolled up, healing what she could reach and recording the rest, and I sat on the ammunition crate feeling lighter and heavier than I had in six years simultaneously, which I believe is the technical definition of absolution.

* * *

But — because I am my sister's brother, and accuracy is our family curse — I have to report the rest of it honestly.

Three days later, the battalion orders came through. Posted on the tannery door, typed, stamped, indifferent: labor companies designated essential to quay works through the thaw period. No rotation. No relief. Every man in that room could read the sentence between the sentences: *you are the part of the machine that stays in the flooding basement.*

Okonkwo read it twice, folded it, and put it in the pocket where he kept his boots' laces at night so no one would steal them. Around him the men went quiet in the particular way of men doing sums they don't like.

I found Theo that evening on the quay, where she stood looking at the bridge — the only way out of the valley, already wearing its winter chains of repair scaffolding — and I watched her do the thing she does, the inventory, the weighing of what can be carried. I watched her look at the barge moored downstream, and at the soldiers counting berths on a manifest, and I saw the exact moment the arithmetic in her head produced an answer she hated.

"They're leaving people behind," she said, when she knew I was there. "On purpose. Names off the lists. Whole categories of names." She turned to me, and her face in the lamplight was the face of a woman standing at the edge of a decision, and I recognized it, God help me, because I'd seen it in a dispensary in 1911 with an empty lockbox in her lap.

"Theo," I said carefully, "whatever you're calculating—"

"How many berths does a labor battalion rate, Samson?"

"None," I said. "You know that."

"Yes," she said softly. "I know."

Downriver, the town began to light its lanterns for the missing — little flames along the water's edge, dozens of them, the whole town turning out its small fires against the dark — and Amara came running along the quay with the lantern she and Theo lit each night, calling my sister's name, and Theo's face did something complicated and tender that no official list would ever record.

I had given up one thing for her once, and it had cost us six years. Watching her watch that barge, I understood with a sinking, familiar certainty that she was about to ask me to give up something larger, and that worse — far worse — she was about to ask me to accept something instead.

She picked up the child's lantern. She looked at me over the flame.

"Walk with me," my sister said. "We need to talk about the manifest."
