## 10. Lanterns on the Water

There is a particular arithmetic to grief that no officer has ever learned to do in his head. The quartermasters can tell you to the half-pound how much biscuit a battalion consumes in a week. The surgeons can estimate blood loss by the color of a man's gums. But ask any of them how many people a town can lose before it starts lighting fires for them on the water, and they will look at you as though you'd asked them to weigh a rumor.

The answer, if you're wondering, is forty-three.

Forty-three was the number I had entered in my ledger under the second week of November — names the official rolls did not carry, people who had walked out one door of the world without troubling to sign the visitors' book on their way. A schoolteacher named Hélène Marchal who had gone to the market on the far bank and simply never come back across. Two brothers from the tannery whose barge had been requisitioned and then, apparently, misplaced. An entire family named Duret, five of them, last seen walking toward the bridge with everything they owned in a handcart, before the shelling closed the road for two days. When the road opened again there was no handcart, no family, no witnesses willing to have been present.

The town did not hold a funeral for any of them. Funerals require bodies, and bodies require paperwork, and paperwork in the winter of 1917 required a signature from an officer who had already signed away his attention to more fashionable losses. So the town did what towns do when the official machinery of mourning declines to notice them. It invented its own.

I heard about it first from Marguerite, who mentioned it in the offhand way she mentioned everything that mattered, as though she were commenting on the weather so that the weather wouldn't take offense.

"They'll be on the quay at dusk," she said, folding bandages with the fierce precision of a woman who believed creases could hold back death if only you made them sharp enough. "Lanterns. For the missing."

"Whose idea?"

"Does it matter?" She smoothed a fold. "The baker's wife started it. She lost a nephew in October — no confirmation, nothing. Just stopped receiving letters." Marguerite's hands went still for a moment, then resumed. "She put a lantern on the water with his name pasted to the side. Then the widow Corbeil did the same for her son. Then it was everyone."

"And the commandant allows this? A hundred little boats carrying names he's declared don't exist?"

Marguerite looked at me over the bandage she was strangling. "Theo. You of all people should know that a name doesn't need permission to float."

* * *

Amara heard about it from the other children, which meant she heard a version of it that was seventy percent invention and thirty percent wonder, and she came to me at the dressing station with her eyes lit like the inside of a paper shade.

"The whole river," she said. "All the way to the bend. And you write the name on the lantern so they know where to come home to."

"That's not quite—" I began, and then stopped, because correcting her would have required me to say aloud what lanterns for the missing actually meant, and I found I was not willing. "Where did you hear this?"

"Pierre. His aunt is making one for his cousin. Everyone's making one." She said *everyone* the way children say it, meaning: *and if I am excluded from everyone I will die of it.* Then, more quietly, in the voice she saved for things that frightened her: "Can we make one? For the ones in your book?"

And there it was. The trap I hadn't seen being set, sprung neatly by a nine-year-old with flour on her sleeve.

Because here is what you must understand about my ledger: Amara knew it existed. She had watched me write in it by lamplight all autumn. In chapter thirteen of our domestic life together — if our lives were a book, which God forbid, since the pacing is terrible — she had even begun learning her letters from it, sounding out the names like psalms, though that came later than this night. What she did not know, because I had never told her and she had never thought to ask, was that the ledger was not a memorial. It was an accusation. Every name in it was a name some officer had decided was not worth accounting, and I kept it the way you keep evidence of a crime — not to mourn the dead but to indict the living.

A lantern, though. A lantern says *we remember you.* A lantern says *rest.* My book said none of those things. My book said *someone will answer for this.*

"I don't think—" I started.

"Please." She took my hand, the one with the ink stain that never washed out anymore, and held it against her cheek. "They're ours. Nobody else knows them. If we don't put them on the water, who will?"

*Who will.* As if I could stand on the quay and watch the baker's wife send her nephew's name drifting downriver while the Duret family — all five, and the handcart — sat unlit in my breast pocket.

"All right," I said. "But we'll need paper that doesn't mind getting wet."

* * *

We made eleven lanterns. That was the number of names I judged could be released without breaking anything — names of the dead beyond recovery, the truly vanished, the ones I had privately given up on weeks ago and simply hadn't crossed out, because crossing out was a thing I couldn't make my hand do. The schoolteacher Marchal went in. The tannery brothers. Old man Ferro, who had drowned in the flooded cellar of his own shop while the town argued about whose jurisdiction the cellar fell under.

Samson helped, which surprised me. He had been in town three weeks by then, attached to a labor battalion that repaired the pontoon sections of the bridge, and we had achieved the peculiar sibling arrangement of speaking daily while saying nothing — six years of silence compressed into pleasantries, like a letter folded too many times. But that afternoon he sat on the floor of the station's back room cutting cardboard squares with his bayonet — a tool I never asked why he still carried — and folding them into boats with a neatness that suggested a childhood spent doing exactly this.

"You always folded them wrong," he said, not looking up. "Yours always sank by the church steps."

"Mine sank because you pushed them under."

"One of them." He creased a fold with his thumbnail. "The rest sank on their own merit."

It was the closest thing to a joke he had made since arriving, and I let it sit between us without examining it, the way you let a coin lie where it falls until you're ready to know whose it is. Amara laughed — a real laugh, unguarded — and something in Samson's face shifted at the sound, some bolt loosening that he immediately pretended he hadn't noticed.

Eleven lanterns, and Amara lettered every name herself in her careful, enormous capitals, asking me to spell each one twice, three times, her tongue between her teeth. She treated each name with the gravity of a priest handling relics, and I stood by watching her and understood, with a sensation like a stitch pulling, that I had built this. That whatever tenderness existed in that room had been assembled by me out of scraps, the way Samson assembled boats, and that it could be torn apart by one shell, one fever, one honest answer to one child's question.

* * *

By dusk the quay was black with people.

I want to get the scene right, because scenes like it are why I keep writing at all, and also because I will never see it again except through my own unreliable remembering. The river ran low and slow under the first hard frost, brown-green, carrying ice scum at the edges. The bridge stood dark above it, its chains newly hung for the closing — I didn't yet know how soon, nobody did, though Vasseur presumably did, Vasseur always did — and along the stone quay the town had gathered in whatever it owned that was warm. Coats over nightdresses. A fur stole that had clearly begun life as a rug. The baker's wife in her good dress, which she had worn to three funerals and zero celebrations and which had now found its true purpose.

There were hundreds of lanterns. More than the missing, of course — grief is not proportional, it is extravagant; people sent lanterns for cousins of cousins, for the crew of a boat none of them had seen, for a dog, in one case, which I include in this account because the child who launched it was entirely sincere and the dog had probably been a very good dog.

The lights went onto the water one and two at a time, and then in dozens, and the current took them and arranged them — this is the part I cannot say without sounding like the sort of novelist I despise, so I will say it plainly and let you make of it what you like: the current arranged them into a line. Not straight. Rivers don't do straight. But a procession, a loose glittering file of small flames moving downstream toward the bend, toward the dark, toward whatever country lies past the reach of officers' lists. Nobody spoke. Four hundred people on a quay in total silence except the water and the wind in the bridge chains, which rang faintly, like a struck glass that won't finish deciding.

Marguerite stood beside me with her hands folded. Samson was behind us, having drifted there without admitting he wanted to be close. Odilon the bargeman was there too, moored nearby, watching from his deck with his cap in his hands — a man who ferried the living for a living and looked, that evening, like a ferryman for the other trade considering a career change.

Amara crouched at the water's edge with our eleven lanterns in a crate, waiting for the right moment. I don't know what she judged the right moment to be. Children have instincts for ceremony that adults spend their whole lives failing to relearn.

She launched them one at a time. Each name spoken aloud first, in her clear carry-everything voice: "*Hélène Marchal.* *Antoine Ferro.* *Luc and Émile Bassong.*" On down the list, and the crowd nearest us turned and listened, and several people mouthed the names after her, and one woman — the tannery brothers' mother, I learned later — reached out and touched the water as each brother's lantern passed.

Then Amara stood, wiped her hands on her coat, and looked up at me with the question.

I saw it forming. I had perhaps four seconds to intervene and I spent them doing nothing, because I am a coward in precisely the ways that matter most.

"Theo." Her hand found mine. "Do the names float?"

And here is the moment the whole chapter turns on, so attend carefully, because I nearly didn't.

What she meant, in the physics of a nine-year-old: does the name travel with the lantern? Does it stay lit? Does it arrive wherever the river goes, so the missing can read it and know they were counted?

What I knew, in the physics of a nurse who kept a secret ledger: the lanterns would burn for twenty minutes and drown within the hour. The paper would soak through at the seams and the flames would go out one by one somewhere past the bend, and by morning the banks would be strung with wet cardboard and guttered candles and the names would be pulp. Nothing floats forever. Nothing floats for long. The river is not a messenger service; the river is a conveyor belt for the drowned and a mirror for everyone standing on the bank pretending otherwise.

Every other question she had ever asked me, I had answered honestly or deflected honestly. Where do wounded men go who leave on the barges — downriver. Will Pierre's cousin come home — I don't know. Are you writing me down in your book too — yes, first page, before anyone, though I've spelled your surname wrong twice and corrected it once. Honesty was the load-bearing wall of what we had. She had lost her actual family to a war that lied to everyone; the single thing I could offer her that the war could not take was that I did not lie.

"Theo?" she said again. "Do they float? All the way?"

"Yes," I said.

The word came out smooth and warm and complete, a finished thing, and I hated it instantly and have never unsaid it. She smiled — the whole face, the gap-toothed, certain smile — and turned back to the water, satisfied, and the current carried eleven small lies past the bridge chains toward the bend, and I stood on the quay having committed my first act of deliberate deception against the person I loved most in the world, for her sake, and understood in my bones what Marguerite had been trying to tell me for months: that love is not the opposite of the lie. Love is frequently the lie's only customer.

Samson's hand settled on my shoulder. He had heard. Of course he had heard; he had spent six years perfecting the art of hearing everything and repeating nothing.

"Heavy," he said quietly. Not a question. A diagnosis.

"It's the first one," I said. "I imagine they weigh less after practice."

"They don't." He squeezed once and removed his hand. "You just learn to carry them in the other pocket."

We stayed until the last light went around the bend. Marguerite crossed herself. Odilon put his cap back on, which among bargemen is a formal declaration that the day is over and the thinking has begun. And far up the quay, apart from the crowd, under the new-hung chains of the bridge that everyone pretended not to count the links of, stood Colonel Vasseur in his immaculate greatcoat, watching the lanterns go, and I might have thought him a mourner like the rest of us except that he was not watching the water at all.

He was watching me.

He held the look for exactly as long as it takes to make certain it's returned, tipped his head — a courtesy, or a summons — and turned away into the dark toward the command post.

Amara tugged my sleeve. "Can we come again next week? When there are more names?"

"There may not be more names," I said, which was the second lie of the evening, and cost considerably less.

"There are always more names," she said placidly, taking my hand, and led me home.
