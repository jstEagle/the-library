## 11. The Colonel's Offer

He came at the hour when the dressing station smelled most like itself — carbolic and wet wool and the ghost of yesterday's chloroform — which is to say he came at the worst possible hour, because every hour in February was the worst possible hour.

Colonel Vasseur did not announce himself. Men of his rank never did; they simply appeared, the way weather appears, already inside your tent before you've noticed the wind change. I looked up from a splint I was tying off on a Belgian farmhand's forearm and found him standing at the end of the ward with his hands clasped behind him, surveying my patients the way a man surveys livestock he is considering selling.

"Sister Oyelaran," he said. He pronounced it correctly, which unsettled me more than any mispronunciation could have. Most officers said *Oy-lar-an* as though my name were a sneeze they were trying not to catch.

"Colonel." I finished the splint. The farmhand watched Vasseur with the particular stillness of men who have learned that rank moves through wards like frost through a pipe, cracking whatever holds water. "If you're here about the requisition forms, they went up Tuesday."

"I'm not here about forms." He walked the length of the ward without hurrying, pausing at each cot just long enough to read the diagnosis card pinned at its head. He was a compact man, Vasseur, gray at the temples and gray in the way of his whole person, as if color had been requisitioned elsewhere and he had made do. His boots were polished to a shine that seemed almost indecent in a place where we scrubbed blood out of floorboards twice daily and lost. "Walk with me, please."

"Three of these men need turning within the hour."

"Then you have an hour." And he turned and walked out into the slush, certain — and this certainty was, I will admit, a kind of genius — that I would follow.

I did follow. That is the thing about temptation nobody warns you about: it doesn't look like temptation. It looks like a colonel's back, and a request phrased as an order, and the knowledge that refusing costs more than complying ever could. Temptation wears a uniform and carries a walking stick and steps carefully around the puddles so that its trousers stay pressed.

* * *

We walked toward the river along the hospital path, past the laundry lines where sheets froze stiff as boards, past the chapel whose bell had been melted for shells and whose bell tower now stood empty like a mouth missing a tooth. The bridge lay ahead of us in the gray light, all iron bones and timber decking, hung with chains that would go up in eleven days. Eleven days. I had begun measuring time the way Amara measured it, in counts and small units — eleven days was two hundred sixty-four turns of the ward, or thirty-three lamp fillings, or one hundred and fifty-four dressings if the wounded kept arriving at their current generosity.

"The barge," Vasseur said, "the final one — departs on the fourteenth. You are aware."

"I am aware."

"You are listed first among the medical personnel. Senior nurse, three years' service at this station, commendations." He recited it flatly, a man reading someone else's inventory. "You have a guaranteed berth, Sister. There are perhaps six hundred people in this town who would trade a finger for what you hold in your hand right now."

I said nothing. There is a discipline to silence; Marguerite taught it to me years ago. *When a man offers you something and names no price, Sister, he has named the price. He is simply waiting to see if you'll pay it without being told.*

"The manifest closes Friday," Vasseur continued. "Forty souls. Thirty-one are already assigned. Your berth makes thirty-two."

"And the other eight?" I asked, though I knew. The eight were the ones being argued over by men with worse arithmetic than his.

"The other eight," he said, "are why I am here."

The river ran brown and slow beneath the bridge, carrying ice in plates. Somewhere upstream a barge horn sounded, long and low, like an animal that had given up pretending to be anything but tired. I thought of Amara at the station, counting backwards from one hundred while she waited for the lantern-lighting hour, her lips moving around numbers the way other children moved around prayers.

"Here is my offer." Vasseur stopped at the foot of the bridge ramp. He did not look at me; he looked at the water, which I understood later was his way of granting both of us the fiction that we were discussing logistics rather than sins. "Two berths. Yours, and one more — assigned to anyone you name, civilian or military, regardless of category. A child, for instance. Or a brother in a labor battalion." 

He let that sit. He knew about Samson. Of course he knew about Samson; Vasseur knew the town down to the number of rats in its granaries.

"In exchange," he said, "you surrender your ledger."

* * *

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only currency left to me after what I nearly did. So: for the space of perhaps five heartbeats — and I counted them, because I had learned counting from a nine-year-old and it steadies me the way prayer steadies better women — I wanted to say yes.

Five heartbeats. Two berths. Amara across the water before the thaw, fed, housed, registered, official. Samson pulled out of a labor battalion slated to stay behind and drowned in mud until the war forgot him. Myself beside them, alive, useful somewhere else. All the love I carried, carried safely to the far bank, in exchange for a book of names.

It is astonishing how reasonable it sounded, how *administrative*. That is the trick of men like Vasseur. They do not ask you to sell your soul; they ask you to complete a form, and the soul is transferred in the margin somewhere, in small print, between two stamps.

"How do you know about the ledger?" I asked.

"Sister." Almost gentle. "You are a careful woman, but you are not invisible. You write at night by a shaded lantern. You keep the book in the false bottom of your instrument case. You consult it against the admission rolls when you believe no one is watching. I command this sector. Invisible things are, professionally speaking, my specialty."

Every hair on my arms rose beneath two layers of wool. The instrument case. It had been under my cot not six hours ago, and would be again tonight, and he had just told me — casually, the way one mentions the weather — exactly where to send the men who would come for it if I refused.

"What is in it," I said, "is none of your business."

"On the contrary." He finally turned from the river to face me, and his eyes were the color of the water, which is to say they carried everything and reflected nothing. "What is in it is precisely my business. Two hundred and seven names, Sister, absent from the official rolls. Wounded who never appeared in casualty reports. Civilians sheltered without authorization. A family of five from the Ruelle district who were never admitted anywhere because admitting them would have required explaining why their street was shelled after curfew." He paused. "You have documented, in your own hand, dated and witnessed, a series of omissions that trace upward through this headquarters to Paris. Do you understand? You haven't been keeping a diary. You've been keeping evidence."

The wind came off the river and I felt it pass straight through me, cold finding every seam. Two hundred and seven. I had counted them last Tuesday, alone, by lamplight, running my finger down the columns the way a miser counts coins, and the number had frightened me then too — not because it was large but because every line was a person, and persons do not compress.

"If those names reach an inquiry," Vasseur said, "they reach it attached to the officers who omitted them. Some of those officers are above me. One of them is likely to be a minister within two years. They will not fall alone. They fall with their staffs, their clerks, their sector commanders — everyone whose signature touches those pages." He adjusted one glove, a small precise motion. "Including, incidentally, mine. I signed the transit orders that emptied the Ruelle district. I did not order the shelling. Signatures, however, are not interested in such distinctions, and neither are tribunals."

"So this is self-preservation."

"This is *arithmetic*," he said, and for the first time something moved behind his face, some brief tectonic shift far below the surface. "Two hundred and seven names in your ledger versus several thousand people moved, fed, and evacuated efficiently because the officers in question remain at their posts instead of awaiting trial in Paris. Weigh it, Sister. You are a woman who weighs things. I have watched you weigh them all winter."

And God forgive me, I weighed it. Standing at the foot of that bridge with my hands going numb in my pockets, I actually performed the calculation he asked of me, because he was right that I was a woman who weighed things, and the scales were right there, familiar as cutlery. Two hundred and seven names against the machinery of the entire evacuation. What was paper against people?

But that was exactly the lie, wasn't it. The ledger wasn't paper. The ledger *was* people — the only place they existed at all, since the army had un-existed them with a pen. Burn the ledger and the two hundred and seven didn't become safer; they became nothing. They became the space where names should have been. And the machinery Vasseur wanted to protect — I had seen its output laid out on cots every morning since October. Efficient machinery. Very tidy. It produced exactly the omissions I had spent the winter recording.

"No," I said.

Vasseur regarded me without surprise. Perhaps he had expected this; perhaps the offer had never really been an offer at all, only an experiment, or a warning delivered in the grammar of commerce. Perhaps he needed my refusal on record — somewhere in his own private ledger — to prove he had tried diplomacy before sending the men with the instrument cases.

"You understand," he said quietly, "what happens on the fourteenth if the manifest closes without your cooperation. Forty berths. Thirty-two assigned. Eight discretionary. I control the eight. After Friday, I control the list, and you control nothing but your conscience, which I am told is heavy and travels poorly."

"My brother—"

"Is a labor battalion private with no claim whatsoever. The girl is not even a legal resident of this town; she appears on no register anywhere in France. As far as the administration is concerned, Sister, neither of them exists." He smiled then, thinly, and it was the smile that told me the truth of him — that he derived no pleasure from any of this, which somehow made it worse. Pleasure can be bargained with. Competence cannot. "You of all people should appreciate the irony. You spend a year writing down the names the army omits, and the army returns the favor."

He saluted. I did not return it; my hands had become two objects at the ends of my arms, foreign consulates of some frozen nation. He turned and walked up the bridge ramp, his stick clicking on the timbers, and stopped halfway, and spoke without looking back.

"The offer stands until Friday noon. Consider that the book buys two lives you love, and burns afterward anyway. Everyone burns it eventually, one way or another. Paper is patient, but not that patient." A pause. "Good afternoon, Sister Oyelaran."

* * *

I did not go back to the ward immediately. I walked to the end of the pier where Odilon's barge was moored, and I stood there in the failing light watching the water shove ice against the pilings, and I let myself feel the full weight of what he had offered, because feeling it was the only way to be sure I wouldn't take it later, in a weak moment, at three in the morning when the ward was quiet and the lantern low.

Two berths. Amara's face when she saw the far shore. Samson's laugh — I remembered it, barely, from before the silence, a big unhinged sound that embarrassed him. My own survival, which I had not once put on the scale in all these months and which now sat there, plain and greedy, asking to be counted.

Against it: two hundred and seven lines in my own hand. Madame Feraud, who had gone deaf from the shelling but hummed anyway. Little Émile Cours, fourteen, who lied about his age twice and got away with it once. The Ruelle family, all five, entered in my book on the ninth of December with a note in the margin that said simply *no one else wrote them down.*

No one else wrote them down.

That was the whole argument, in the end. Not principle, not courage — I felt brave for none of it; I felt sick and cold and small. Just that sentence, repeating itself like Amara counting backwards: *no one else wrote them down.* If I traded the book, the two berths would float across the water carrying everything I loved, and behind me the two hundred and seven would dissolve back into the convenient fog the officers had manufactured for them, and the next town, and the next thaw, would proceed exactly as before, with tidier paperwork.

Temptation wearing a uniform had come to my door, and it had offered me a fair price, and the price was fair, and that was precisely how I knew to refuse it. The devil doesn't lowball. The devil knows your market value down to the sou.

I went back to the ward. I turned my three men, changed two dressings, drank tea that had achieved a flavor best described as regret. And when the corridor had been empty for a full quarter hour, I took the ledger from the false bottom of my instrument case — Vasseur's information sitting in my head like a splinter — and I copied it. Every name, every date, every marginal note, in a hand smaller than my usual one, onto loose pages torn from the back of a hymnal. The original stayed where it had always been, because a man who tells you where your treasure is wants you to leave it there.

The copies went somewhere else entirely. And that night, when Amara lit our lantern at the station window and asked, as she always asked, "How many sleeps until the boat?" — I said, "Nine," and taught her a new hymn verse to count to, one I had chosen that afternoon for reasons she will understand someday, when she is older, and reads what I hid inside it.

Friday noon, then. Two days. And Colonel Vasseur's patience, I suspected, was a great deal shorter than his manners.
