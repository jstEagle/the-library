## 12. Marguerite's Confession

Sister Marguerite did not drink. In the eleven months I had known her — first as a legend in a wimple, then as a superior who corrected my bandaging and my grammar in equal measure — I had never once seen her take anything stronger than chicory coffee. So when she appeared at my elbow that evening holding two cups of something that smelled like it had been distilled from a barn fire, I understood, before she said a word, that the day had frightened her.

Or that she had frightened herself.

"Drink," she said, pressing one cup into my hands. "It's medicinal."

"What does it cure?"

"Memory, if you're lucky."

The ward behind us had finally gone quiet. Vasseur's offer sat in my chest like an unexploded shell — *two berths, yours and one more, for the ledger* — and I had spent the hours since turning it over the way one turns over a coin found on the floor of a latrine, wondering whose it was and what it had bought last. Marguerite steered me out the side door into the small yard where we hung linens that never dried properly, and stood us both under the beam of my own lantern, which I had hung there weeks ago because Amara liked to see it from her corner window.

She looked at the lantern for a long moment.

"You light it every night," she said.

"Amara counts the seconds while I do. It's our ritual. She says counting keeps the dark honest."

"And does it?"

"I've stopped asking whether anything works, Sister. I only ask whether it's kind."

Something moved across her face then, quick as a rat under floorboards, and I realized I had said exactly the wrong thing — or exactly the right one — to open whatever door she had come to open. She drank from her cup, grimaced, drank again.

"The colonel spoke to you today."

It wasn't a question. Word traveled through that hospital the way infection traveled through a wound: silently, along lines no one could see, until it surfaced somewhere fatal.

"He made me an offer," I admitted. "I haven't taken it."

"No," she said. "You wouldn't. Not yet." She turned the cup in her thick, capable hands — hands I had watched pull men back from death so many times I had stopped thinking of them as hands at all and thought of them as weather, as tide, as force of nature. "I want to tell you about the winter of 1914, Theo. Before you decide anything. Because I was offered something once too, and I took it, and I have been carrying it the way you carry that ledger. Except mine weighs more every year instead of less."

I should have gone inside. I should have claimed fatigue, claimed a patient, claimed any of the hundred small lies nurses keep pre-wrapped in their pockets for precisely such occasions. Instead I leaned against the drying post and waited, because some part of me — the part that wrote names in a secret book against all orders — knew that what was coming had been coming for eleven months, and that Marguerite had chosen tonight, tonight of all nights, because Vasseur's offer had shown her a mirror and she could not bear what stood in it.

* * *

"In the autumn of 1914," she began, "I was at a clearing station outside Reims. You'll have heard of it — no. No, you won't. Nobody has. That's rather the point. It was a converted tannery, stone floors, drains that worked, which sounds like nothing until you've worked somewhere the drains don't. We had ninety beds and room for sixty. By October we had two hundred men on those floors, stacked like cordwood, and the convoys kept arriving, and the officers kept asking me how many I could hold, and I kept saying numbers, because I was thirty-four years old and believed that numbers were the truth-tellers. Numbers don't lie. That's what I'd been raised on. God sees every sparrow fall; arithmetic sees every man."

She laughed then, a sound I had never heard from her, dry and terrible, like paper burning slowly.

"Do you know when I learned that arithmetic lies? It doesn't lie the way people lie. It lies by omission. It tells you everything except what the numbers *are*. Ninety beds. Two hundred men. Sixty that can be nursed. Those are just figures until someone walks down the rows with a piece of chalk."

My hands went cold around the cup. I thought of Vasseur's finger moving down the manifest, striking civilian names. I thought of my ledger, upstairs in its hymnal, full of chalk-marks that had been human beings.

"There was a major," Marguerite said. "A decent enough man, which is the worst kind, because decent men follow orders with a clear conscience. He came to me and said: Sister, the next convoy cannot land here. We are sending you forty of the hopeless cases. You will make them comfortable. And he said it kindly. He put his hand on my shoulder. And then he handed me the list and left me alone with it, because the decision of *who* — that was never going to be his sin. They always leave that part to us. Did you notice that? The ones who give the orders never choose the faces. They know better than to look."

"Were they dying?" I asked. "The forty?"

"Some. Not all. That's what no one understands, what I didn't understand until the chalk was in my hand. Hopeless is a word with a sliding bottom. A man with a shattered hip is hopeless in October when the beds are needed for chest wounds. In February he's walking with a cane. I marked twenty-two men with a cross and eighteen with a circle — cross for comfortable, circle for comfort plus, meaning morphine when they asked for it — and the major took my paper away, and the convoy came, and the forty were laid in the far bay where the stoves didn't reach, and I told myself I had chosen mercifully. I had. Given the choices available. That is the sentence I have repeated to myself for three years, Theo. *Given the choices available.* Do you hear how it sounds? Like a key that fits no lock. You can turn it forever. Nothing opens."

The lantern above us guttered and steadied. Somewhere across the river, the pumps thumped on — the engineers fighting the water table that rose a little every week, patient as sin.

"Two of them lived," she said quietly. "Out of the forty. A boy with a belly wound who should have died but was too stubborn, and a sergeant who lost the whole of one leg and most of the other's foot. They lived because a young orderly named Marguerite Aubry disobeyed her own chalk marks. I stole food from the staff stores for them. I smuggled the sergeant's chart so his circle read cross and his cross read circle. I lied to the major's face twice a day for five weeks." She looked up at me, and her eyes in the lantern light were the flattest grey I had ever seen, the grey of a river before ice. "And here is what I learned, lying for them: that I could do it. That choosing who dies is not a wall you crash into once and are broken against. It is a door. And once it has opened for you, it is never locked again. Every choice after is easier. You tell yourself you're being realistic. Pragmatic. Kind, even — kindness is the best disguise cruelty ever wore. By 1916 I was triaging without flinching. I could look at a boy of nineteen doing his breathing and say *circle* or *cross* between heartbeats. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I became good at it. That's the horror. Not that I fell. That I got *good*."

"Sister—"

"Let me finish." Her voice cracked like a whip and then, immediately, softened, because even her anger had been trained into usefulness. "No. Forgive me. But let me finish, because I have said this to no living soul and I find I cannot stop now that the cork is out."

She drained her cup, set it upside-down on the drying post — an old field habit, keeping grit out — and folded her hands.

"When your ledger began. Last summer. I saw it. You think I didn't — Theo, I have eyes in this place, I knew within a fortnight that you were writing down every soul the manifests dropped. And I said nothing. I want you to understand why I said nothing. It wasn't sentiment. It wasn't admiration, though God knows it was partly that, watching you commit your tidy little treason every evening by lamplight. I said nothing because I wanted to see whether you would become what I became. Whether the book would stay a book, or turn into a chalk."

"It's names," I said. "Only names. I don't use it to choose anyone."

"Don't you?" she asked gently, and the gentleness cut deeper than the whip had. "When the barge berth came open and the lists were drawn, did you consult it? When you decide who gets the fresh dressings and who waits till morning, is the book silent? When Odilon's price comes due — and it will come due, girl, bargemen smell leverage the way cats smell fish — will you weigh those names? Of course you will. You must. A record is a scale. The moment you write the second name, you have built a machine for comparing lives. The question was never whether you'd use it. The question is whether you'll still be the person holding it when it's heavier than you are."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth to argue, and found that I was arguing with myself instead, because hadn't I done exactly that, just that afternoon? Hadn't I sat with Vasseur's offer and calculated — actually calculated, with actual sums — how many lives a saved ledger might buy against the one berth surrendered? I had run the arithmetic of my brother against the arithmetic of strangers and called it moral seriousness. Marguerite looked at my face and saw the calculation still hanging there, and nodded slowly, as if I had confessed aloud.

"There it is," she said. "There's the door, standing open. He showed you two berths, didn't he. Vasseur. Yours and one more."

"How did you—"

"Because it's always two. One for you, so you survive to be grateful, and one to spend, so you learn the taste of spending. That was my major's trick too. He let me save the boy with the belly wound. Officially. On paper, my disobedience became my initiative, and he commended me for it, and I understood that I had been paid. That's how they bind you, the ones who give the orders. Not with threats. With purchases. Every mercy they permit becomes your debt, and every debt teaches you that mercy is currency, and once mercy is currency you will start making change." She took my cup from my numb fingers and set it beside hers, upside-down, and gripped my hands in both of hers, and her grip was as strong as I remembered from the days she had held men down while their bones were set. "Theo Oyelaran. Listen to me. I am fifty-seven years old. I have outlived everyone I chose and everyone I declined to choose, and at night they stand in one line, all of them, crosses and circles together, and they do not accuse me. That's the unbearable part. They just wait. As if I owed them something. Which I do. And the interest compounds."

"What are you asking me to do?"

"I'm asking you to burn it."

The word landed like a dropped instrument tray. "Burn the ledger?"

"Burn it. Or sink it. Or give it to the river with your blessing. Two hundred names, and every one of them is a hook in your flesh, and Vasseur knows it, and he will reel you in name by name, berth by berth, until one morning you wake up and realize you're him — a decent woman with a clear conscience and a piece of chalk." Her voice broke on the last words, and she let it break; she had apparently decided, sometime in the yard, that hiding things from me had finished its usefulness. "I begged my major for those forty men. I prayed. I would have traded my own life for theirs and he knew it and he gave me two berths' worth of mercy instead, and I took them, and I have never once, in three years, slept through a night entire. Don't take his berths, child. Whatever you do. Don't let him teach you the price of things."

* * *

I walked back through the ward slowly, past the sleeping shapes, past Amara's corner where her breath rose and fell in counts — I could tell, even in the dark, that she counted herself toward sleep, four in, six out, the way I'd taught her against nightmares — and I sat down at my table and opened the hymnal and looked at the ledger lying inside it like a heart inside a ribcage.

Marguerite was wrong. I believed she was wrong. The book was not a chalk; the book was the opposite of a chalk; the book existed because someone had refused to let the crosses and circles be the whole truth. Names written down were not weights. They were witnesses.

And yet my thumb moved over the pages and I caught myself doing it again — weighing. *This name for that berth. These two hundred against my one brother.* Marguerite had not warned me against the book, I realized. She had warned me against becoming fluent. Against the ease. Against the morning — surely coming, surely already half-arrived — when I would mark a circle or a cross between heartbeats and feel nothing at all.

Outside, across the yard, I heard her door close. Then, a minute later, open again. Then footsteps — hers, unhurried, heavy — crossing the flagstones to the linen post, where my lantern still burned.

She stood beneath it a long moment. I thought she meant to snuff it, and I was already rising to stop her, when I saw what she actually did: she reached up and adjusted the wick, trimming the flame brighter, steadying it against the wind off the river. Then she went back inside.

In the morning she said nothing about any of it, and corrected my grammar as usual.

But that same afternoon a runner brought word through the mud and the drizzle that Colonel Vasseur wished to see me at my earliest convenience regarding "administrative matters pertaining to evacuation priorities" — and pinned to the note, in his neat clerk's hand, was a revised manifest for the final barge.

Forty names.

With room, the covering letter noted, for discussion.
