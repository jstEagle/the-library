## 13. Amara Learns the Alphabet

Theo says I learned my letters in a week, which is a lie, and I know it is a lie because I was there and it took eleven days. But Theo rounds things down when the truth is too long to carry, the way some men round down the number of times they were frightened and say they were only ever cold.

I am nine years old and I can write my name and thirty-seven other names besides, and I will tell you how that happened, because it is the best thing that has ever been made out of nothing since the nuns turned fish stew into Friday dinner.

* * *

It began on a Tuesday, or what Theo called Tuesday, because the clock in the tower had stopped and so days were whatever Theo said they were. The dressing station smelled of carbolic and wet wool, and outside the water in the street had gone down enough that you could hear the cobblestones again, ticking as the current moved small stones against each other. I liked that sound. It was like someone else counting.

Theo sat at the little table we had made from a door laid across two crates, and she had her ledger open in front of her, and she was doing the thing where she pressed her thumb flat against the page before she wrote, as if she were asking the paper's permission.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up. Theo always knows when people are looking at her. She says it comes from having six years of brothers, which is funny if you know about Samson, and I did not yet, so I just laughed politely.

"I want to learn," I said.

"Learn what?"

"Whatever's in there."

Her pen stopped. She looked up at me over the top of the page the way she looks over things, like the page was a wall and I was on the other side of it. "Amara."

"It isn't fair," I said, "that everyone in this room has a name written down except me, and even mine is only spoken. Spoken names blow away. Written ones stay."

That is not something a stupid person says, and Theo knew it, and I watched her decide not to be surprised by me. She does that. She decides things quickly and then never un-decides them, which is either a virtue or a disease, I haven't worked out which.

"Come here," she said.

* * *

Here is the problem with learning letters: most books for children are about cats. There were no cats in this town by November, living or printed, and the one primer in the convent cupboard started with *A is for Apple*, and I have eaten two apples in my entire life, both in June, both stolen, and neither of them looked like the fat red letter A in the book. The book's apple was lying. Books lie all the time; that is why Theo checks them.

So Theo closed the primer and said, "We'll use something better," and she opened the ledger.

Now. I want to say clearly that I did not understand then what the ledger was. I thought it was a list of patients, like Sister Marguerite kept, only messier and with more crossing-out. I did not know that the officers' lists were shorter than hers, or why. Children find out things sideways, like catching a ball meant for somebody else. What I knew was this: every page had names at the top, in Theo's slanted hand, and under the names were little marks like bird tracks, and beside the names sometimes a word — *ward three*, *fever*, *sent on*, and once, memorably, *snored like artillery*, which Theo let stand because she has a rule that even dying men get to be described honestly.

"This," Theo said, tapping the first name, "is Auguste. Aww-GOOST. And Auguste begins with—"

"A," I said, because the primer had taught me that much at least, even if its apples were liars.

"Good. Say it again."

"Auguste. A."

"And this one—" her finger slid down the column "—is Béatrice. B."

"Béatrice. B."

"And here, Camille. C."

And so it went, down the page, and here is what happened, and I did not plan it but it happened anyway: the letters stopped being letters. They became the men themselves. The A leaned like Auguste leaned, tired on his left side. The B had Béatrice's double fussiness, two round worries instead of one. When we reached Édouard, whose É carried an accent like a splint propping it upright, I laughed out loud and Theo said, "What?" and I said, "He's wearing a hat," and Theo put down her pen and looked at me for a long moment, and then she laughed too — a real laugh, low and startled out of her, the kind she makes maybe once a month — and she said, "Yes. He is. God help him, he is."

After that we could not stop. We went through the whole ward alphabetizing everyone. Jules was a J, obviously, all hook and swagger. Henri's H was two soldiers standing back to back. Marguerite got an M with mountains in it. Even Theo got done eventually — T, tall and crossbarred, standing in the middle of everything holding everybody up — though when I told her that, she went quiet and wrote a line in the margin and did not read it aloud.

* * *

At night we lit the lantern.

This is our ritual and I will describe it properly because rituals deserve proper description, that is practically their job. There is a lamp with a glass chimney that Theo rescued from a flooded café, and every evening at what we called dusk, since official dusk had resigned along with the clock, we set it in the window of the room behind the dispensary where we sleep. Theo strikes the match. I turn the wick. You have to turn it exactly right — too low and the flame sulks, too high and it smokes and cries black tears down the glass. Then we say the words. Theo says, "For anyone coming home late." I say, "And for anyone who hasn't come home yet." And then we eat whatever there is, which lately is chicory coffee and bread that could stop a bullet, and after eating, lessons.

Lessons happened by lantern light because the days were short and the work was long and Theo's free hours came only after the lamps in the wards went down. So there we'd sit, the two of us, our shadows huge on the far wall, and she would run her finger down the ledger and I would follow, saying the names like prayers, which is what Sister Marguerite said they sounded like when she heard us through the wall.

"Adetunji," I would say. "A. Baptiste. B. Celestine. C. Delphine. D."

"Slower," Theo said, the second night. "Names aren't a race. If you rush them, you drop them."

I did not tell her that rushing was the point. Rushing is my trick. When the shelling got close last autumn, I counted backwards from a hundred as fast as I could, because a hundred arrived sooner that way, and fear cannot survive arithmetic — Theo told me that, or I decided it myself, and either way it is true. But names are different from numbers. Numbers you spend. Names you keep. So I slowed down. I gave each one its whole weight, the way Theo's pen gave each one its ink, and I found that if I said them slowly enough, in order, the sound of them made a shape in the dark bigger than any shell could fill.

By the fourth night I could recite the A's and B's without the book. By the sixth night, half the page. Theo tested me while she rolled bandages, tossing names across the room like scraps: "First letter of Fernand?" "F!" "Spell Odilon?" "O-D-I-L-O-N, and his O is rounder than the others because he's a bargeman and everything about a bargeman is round." Theo snorted so hard she rolled a bandage crooked and had to do it again, and I counted that among my great victories.

* * *

One night I asked her about the crossings-out.

Some names in the ledger had a single line drawn through them, neat, careful, like the name itself hadn't done anything wrong but had merely been finished. I had noticed them for days and not asked, the way you don't ask about the empty bed at the end of ward two, but that night the lantern flame did its sulk-and-recover trick, and the crossed-out name nearest to me was Célestine, who used to sing in the evenings until she couldn't, and I asked.

Theo put down her pen.

She has a way of going still before she answers hard questions, like the answer is heavy goods arriving by rail and she must make sure the platform is clear. Then she said, "Those are the ones I carried as far as I could."

"Were they angry? That you stopped?"

"No," she said. "They were past anger. They were — light. That's what nobody tells you. At the end, people get light. As if the illness burns off everything extra and leaves just the part that was always really them." She drew the lantern closer, and the flame stood up straight in the glass, and I saw that her eyes were doing the thing where they shine but don't spill. "Célestine asked me to write her name down before she went. She said, 'If I'm written somewhere, I went somewhere.'"

"So the crossing-out isn't erasing," I said. I remember being very sure of this, the way you're sure of things at nine o'clock by lantern light. "It's delivering."

Theo looked at me for a long time. Longer than the question needed. Outside, far off, something went over and came down, and we both listened to it arrive, and neither of us flinched, because we were busy with more important business, which was each other.

"Yes," she said finally. "It's delivering. Write that down. You're literate now."

I wrote it in my copybook, in pencil, pressing hard so it would last: *A line through a name is not erasing. It is delivering.* Underneath, because I am honest, I added: *(spelled deliverng, corrected by T.O.)*

* * *

Sister Marguerite caught us at it on the ninth day. She came into the back room on some errand of linens and stood in the doorway watching me recite, hands folded into her apron, and when I finished the D's she said, "Who taught you to memorize like that?"

"Theo. We're doing psalms."

"We are not doing psalms," Theo said, not looking up from her charts.

"It's the same thing," I said. "Words in a row that you keep in your body so you can't lose them. That's psalms. Sister Marguerite, tell her."

Marguerite smiled the smile she keeps for when Theo is out-argued and doesn't know it yet. "The child has the theology of it better than most seminarians," she said. "Though she should be learning catechism."

"She is learning catechism," Theo said. "Hers just has surnames."

Marguerite stayed a while after that. She quizzed me herself, briskly, like an inspector, and I passed, and then she grew quieter and quieter, and at the door she stopped and said to Theo, in a voice pitched for adults but I have excellent ears, "You know what you're doing, don't you. You're making sure it survives you. Whatever happens."

Theo didn't answer. But later, when Marguerite had gone, I saw her turn back to the first page of the ledger and go over certain letters — the ones I had learned worst — pressing harder, darkening them, as if ink depth might be a kind of volume and she wanted the names loud enough to shout through anything.

I pretended not to see. There is a lot of pretending not to see, in wartime. I am very good at it. I hate that I am good at it.

* * *

On the eleventh day, Theo gave me a pencil of my own.

It was a stub, half-swallowed by its own history, teeth-marked at the end by persons unknown, and I loved it more than I have loved most humans. She also gave me four pages torn from the back of an old account book, blank ones, and she said, "You've watched long enough. Tonight you write."

My hand shook so badly the first A came out like a seismograph reading. The second was worse. I said a rude word I had learned from a corporal from Marseille, and Theo said gravely, "Also spelled with four letters, well done," and then she put her hand over mine — her hand is warm and smells of carbolic and lamp oil, which is the smell I mean when I use the word *home*, though I don't explain that to people — and she guided the third attempt, and the third one was a true A, an Adetunji of an A, and something went off inside my chest that was not a shell and was better than anything the sky had ever dropped.

By midnight I had copied six names. My six favorites, chosen with the ruthless editorial judgment of a nine-year-old: Adetunji, because he gave me raisins; Béatrice, because her braid was longer than my whole arm; Célestine, delivered; Delphine, because she promised me her boots if she went home first and I intended to hold her to it; Édouard, hat and all; and Fernand, who owed me a song.

Theo read them over. She did not correct much. She said, "These are good," and then, quieter, almost to the ledger rather than to me, "Better than you know."

"Why?"

"Because," she said, and trimmed the wick, and the room went soft and gold, "paper burns, and ink runs, and officers lose lists, and bridges close. Memory is the only archive with no address for the shells to find." She tapped my forehead with one finger, gently, right between the eyes. "Congratulations, Amara. As of tonight you are a public building."

I slept that night with the pages under my pillow and the stub of pencil in my fist, and I dreamed in letters. In the dream all the names stood up off the page and walked in alphabetical order across the flooded square, and the water held them up, and nobody sank, and I woke up happy, actually happy, the kind of happy that feels borrowed and must therefore be spent fast.

* * *

I spent it the next morning, which is how I found out the thing I probably shouldn't know, but I will get to that.

I had gone down to the quay to practice — big letters need big air, and the quay was empty at that hour except for gulls and one barge — and I was chalking the alphabet on the mooring stones when two men came along the waterline, arguing low. One was Odilon, the round bargeman, smelling of tar and secrets as usual. The other I didn't know, a lean man in river clothes with mud dried halfway up his boots, which was strange, because the paths that muddy were not the road paths; they were marsh paths, and nobody sane walked the marshes.

I kept chalking. E. F. G. Ears open.

"—only at low water," the muddy man was saying, "and only till the thaw swells it. After that it's a drowned garden path. One night's crossing, Odilon, maybe two, and then it's gone till summer."

"And who tells the officer class about this garden path?"

"Nobody. That's the point of it."

Odilon grunted. Gulls screamed. I chalked an H with two careful soldiers standing back to back and did not turn around, and I did not run, because running makes your shoulders talk.

"One night," Odilon said. "Everything comes down to one night, in this town. Bridges. Barges. Lives." A pause, and then, lower, "There's a nurse here keeps lists. If those lists ever met my boat—"

The rest went under the sound of a train whistle across the river, and by the time the whistle died they had walked on, and I was left alone on the quay with my chalk dust and my heart going like the tower clock used to, before it stopped.

I looked down at my letters. Twenty-six of them, lined up on the stone, innocent as Sunday school.

Then I picked up the chalk, and on the biggest stone, where the water would reach it first, I drew one more H — and inside it, small, hidden in the crossbar, a little winding path. Just in case letters can keep secrets too.

I ran home to Theo, and my pages under my pillow, and my psalm of names, and I did not tell her. Not yet. Some knowledge is too heavy for morning.

But all that evening, while we lit the lantern and said our lines and recited the D's together, one word kept marching around inside my head in muddy boots, keeping time with my counting:

*Marsh. M-A-R-S-H. Marsh.*
