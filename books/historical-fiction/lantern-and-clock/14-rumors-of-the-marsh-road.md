## 14. Rumors of the Marsh Road

Odilon the bargeman smelled of pitch, river mud, and the particular despair of men who have outlived three governments. I mean that as a compliment. In the winter of 1917, despair was a form of expertise, and I had learned to trust the professionals.

I found him where I always found him, in the lee of his half-sunk barge *La Vérité* — he had told me once she was named ironically, though he refused to say ironic against what — mending nets he would never cast and drinking coffee that could have caulked a hull. The afternoon was the color of dishwater, and the river moved beneath the ice-fringe with the slow, muscular patience of something that intended to win eventually. Rivers always do. It is their only virtue and their entire personality.

"Nurse Oyelaran." He didn't look up. Odilon never looked up when he greeted you; it saved wear on his neck, which had opinions.

"Bargeman."

"You want something."

"I want coffee."

"You want something," he repeated, "and you're willing to drink my coffee to get it, which tells me how badly you want it." He poured anyway, into a tin cup that had survived more wars than either of us. "Officers don't come down here anymore. Officers used to come down here when they needed the water moved for them. Now the water is going to do its own moving, and they've stopped pretending it's theirs to command."

"The thaw," I said.

"The thaw," he agreed, with the satisfaction of a man confirming the diagnosis he'd made first.

Here is what nobody says aloud in that town: we are all of us living inside a clock that someone else wound. The bridge closes in two weeks. The barge takes forty souls and no more. And somewhere south of us, winter is holding several feet of meltwater like a debt collector holds a ledger, waiting for the warm days to come collect. The engineers say the low quarter floods by late February. The low quarter contains most of the people whose names exist only because I wrote them down.

I drank the coffee. It tasted like a punishment for something. I asked for more.

* * *

You should understand something about hope, as it is practiced in a war town. Real hope is not the thing in the chaplain's sermons, all wings and trumpets. Real hope arrives sideways, wearing work clothes, smelling of pitch, and it usually begins with a complaint.

"Bridge closes," Odilon said, working a knot loose with his thumbnail. "And after the bridge closes, everyone stands on the north bank like cattle at a gate, waiting for the army to remember them. The army will not remember them. The army has a list, and your list is not the army's list." He glanced up then, briefly — one of his rare expenditures — to see if I knew what he knew about my ledger. I gave him nothing. We were both fluent in nothing. "So the clever ones start asking about other ways."

"Are there other ways?"

"Is that an official question?"

"Nothing about me has been official since 1915."

He set down his net. That was how I knew I'd gotten somewhere worth going. When Odilon kept his hands busy, he was passing time. When Odilon's hands went still, he was deciding the shape of the rest of somebody's life.

"There is the marsh road," he said.

I waited. Waiting is a nursing skill; you learn it holding forceps and bad news.

"My grandfather drove salt across the Marais d'Amont before there was a bridge. Before there was a town on this side worth bridging. There's a causeway under the reeds — stone, old as anything, laid when men still believed in things that last. It runs from the drowned mill south of the tannery, four kilometers through the marsh, and comes up on the high ground past Saint-Aubin. In high water it's ten centimeters under and kills anyone who walks it. In low water—" he turned his cup, watching the coffee settle like sediment in a life, "—in low water it's ankle-deep over stone, and a column can cross it in one night if they keep quiet and keep moving."

My heart did something unprofessional. I made it sit back down.

"Why does nobody speak of this?"

"Because nobody speaks to bargemen. Because the army mapped this valley from the air and saw reeds, and reeds look the same over stone as over drown-you-mud. Because the officers who might care are busy counting berths on a barge." He picked the net back up. "And because low water ends. That causeway holds only while the river is starving. First hard thaw, first warm rain off the southern hills, and the marsh drinks everything and asks for more. You'd have a window. One night, maybe two, between the frost fixing the ground and the melt undoing it. Somewhere in the next month, and I couldn't tell you which night until three days before, because the river doesn't publish schedules."

"One night," I said. "Two hundred people. Some of them children. Some of them stretcher cases."

"Now you see why it stays a rumor." His voice flattened. "A rumor is kind. A plan that fails is a massacre with paperwork."

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the tannery wall, and I am not ashamed to say I counted my heartbeats the whole way, which is Amara's habit rubbing off on me. She counts backwards from a hundred when the shelling comes close, and forwards through her letters when it doesn't, and lately she counts everything else too — stairs, spoons, the buttons on Sister Marguerite's cuff — as if the world were a debt that could be managed if only the arithmetic were done attentively enough. Some nights I think she is right, and some nights I think she has simply found the gentlest available madness, which puts her ahead of most adults I know.

The marsh road. Four kilometers of stone under reeds, open for a single night somewhere in the next few weeks, visible on no map I'd ever seen, known only to a bargeman with a grudge against geography and a dead grandfather.

Hope, I have said, arrives sideways. It also arrives with a deadline, which is what separates it from mere wishing. A wish can wait. Hope is a door already swinging shut, and you either run at it or watch it close and spend the rest of your life describing the room on the other side.

I decided, somewhere between the tannery and the convent steps, to run.

* * *

That evening I did three illegal things, in ascending order of consequence.

First, I took paper from the stores without requisitioning it — a crime so small the quartermaster commits larger ones before breakfast, but I note it for completeness. Second, I drew a map by lamplight in our cell behind the ward, working from memory: the drowned mill, the tannery outfall, the line of pollards Odilon had described marking the causeway's spine like stitches in a wound. Third — and this is the one that would interest Colonel Vasseur, should he ever learn of it — I began a second ledger.

Not to replace the first. The first is sacred and stays sewn into the hymnal where it has lived since the inspection scare, sleeping beside "Abide With Me" like a wolf in a sheepfold. No. This second book was for the road itself. Every fact Odilon gave me, every measurement, every guess dressed up honestly as a guess. Depth of water by date. Width of the path where the reeds thin. The two places where Odilon said the stone gives way to corduroy logging — old timber road-bed — which would float free in rising water and take an ankle with it. Distances measured in the only units that matter on a night march: minutes at a wounded man's pace, which is roughly half a healthy child's pace, which is roughly a third of what fear tells you yours is.

Amara sat on my cot with her knees drawn up, watching me draw.

"That's not a real place," she said. "There's nothing there. It's just lines."

"It's a very real place. It just hasn't happened yet."

"How do you map something that hasn't happened?"

I thought about that longer than she expected me to. Nine-year-olds have a genius for asking the questions adults have agreed to stop asking.

"Carefully," I said finally. "And then you walk it, and correct the map."

She considered this, nodded with terrible gravity, and pointed at the squiggle I'd drawn for the drowned mill. "That looks like a snail."

"The mill has earned the right to be drawn however I like. It died for its country."

She laughed — that sudden, whole-body laugh of hers that makes the ward's exhausted orderlies smile despite themselves — and then, because laughter is expensive currency these days, she spent it quickly and went back to counting her buttons. Forty-one. She has forty-one buttons in her tin, collected from four countries' uniforms and one civilian coat, and she knows each one's story, or believes she does, which amounts to the same thing in a child's economy.

I worked until the lamp oil ran low. The map grew a second page, then a third. Water depths. Moon phases — I copied them from the almanac in the convent library, because a night crossing wants a moon, but not too much moon, and February's moons were falling on the wrong days, of course they were, the sky being as indifferent a bureaucrat as any major. Wind direction, because Odilon said the reeds hiss when wind moves them and go silent when men do, and a silent gap in a windy marsh reads like a shout. Where the columns could rest. Where they could not afford to.

By midnight I had a document that, discovered, would earn me a firing squad or a commendation depending entirely on how the spring went. I folded it into the false bottom of my sewing box, under thread the color of surrender.

Then I blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to Amara breathe and thinking about deadlines.

* * *

Three days later I went back to Odilon with bread — real bread, hoarded shamelessly — and a list of questions long enough to make him laugh, which I suspect was the bread's true purpose.

We talked for two hours. He drew in the mud with a stick, and I committed mud to paper afterward, walking a slow circuit of the tannery wall so my stopping-and-starting would look like the pacing of a tired nurse rather than the surveying of a conspirator. He showed me, from the barge's deck, where the reed line broke — invisible to me, obvious to him, the way a sailor sees weather in a sky that means nothing to the rest of us. He told me about the cold. The causeway stones hold frost, he said; men who stand still on them lose feet. Keep them moving. Sing if you must, quietly. His grandfather's salt-drivers sang. He even gave me the song, four bars of something old and flat and practical, a song with no beauty in it whatsoever, which is how you know it was built for survival.

"What do you want for this?" I asked him, finally. Because Odilon does nothing gratis, and I preferred to learn the price before it learned me.

He was quiet for a while, mending his eternal net.

"When the crossing comes," he said, "someone has to walk it first. Alone. To read the water — tell true depth from a drowning dressed as a puddle. Someone with a lantern and good sense and no family waiting, so that if the causeway eats her, the loss is bearable."

"*Her.*"

"Your boots are sturdier than mine and your sense of your own importance is smaller. Grandfather always said the first walker should be someone the marsh gains nothing by eating." He tied off a knot. "Besides. You're the one keeping the names. A keeper of names should know the ground the names will stand on."

I told him I would think about it. I lied. I had already thought about it; I simply hadn't confessed yet.

Walking back, I passed the bridge — the bridge, with its ice-stained arches and its new chains coiled at the approaches like snakes bought and paid for — and the sentries stamped and steamed, and beyond it the barge that would carry forty sat black on the water. Two hundred names in my ledger. One berth I held and could not in conscience use. One road through the reeds that existed for perhaps a single night.

Everything now was arithmetic, and I hated arithmetic, and I did it anyway, all the way up the convent steps: two hundred walkers, four abreast, wounded pace, four kilometers. Call it six hours with the stragglers. One night. One window of low water that Odilon promised he could smell coming three days out, the way sailors smell storms.

Which meant that before the window opened, I had to teach two hundred frightened people to walk quietly in a dark marsh in winter, following a woman with a lantern and a hand-drawn map, without a single officer noticing.

I went in to supper feeling almost lighthearted. Sister Marguerite took one look at my face and put down her spoon.

"You've found something," she said.

"I've found a rumor."

"Rumors are how God tests bookkeepers." But she was watching me with those eyes of hers that had triaged whole wards and knew exactly which fires were worth feeding. "Whatever it is — and I don't want to know — tell me one thing. Does it need help?"

"It needs everything," I said. "It needs a miracle with a timetable."

"Good," said Marguerite, and picked her spoon back up. "Those are the only prayers I still believe in."

I slept that night the way I imagine sappers sleep — lightly, happily, on top of a great deal of explosive — and woke before dawn with the map already unfolding in my head. There was one more piece the plan required, one I had been circling for days the way the tongue circles a broken tooth.

Samson. My brother, with his labor battalion slated to stay behind, with his six years of silence and his boots that had walked blame for me across half of France. If there was to be a night march, there would be rope-work, raft-work, heavy lifting in black water. There would be a use for a strong man with something to prove and nowhere left to spend it.

I found him at the lumber yard at first light, breath smoking, hands already moving. He smiled when he saw me — that guarded half-smile of his, love wearing armor.

"Theo. Early for visiting hours."

"Samson," I said. "I need to talk to you about a road that isn't on any map. And about a berth on the last barge — the one I'm owed." I watched his face begin, already, to refuse. "Hear me out before you argue. Please. Just hear me out."
