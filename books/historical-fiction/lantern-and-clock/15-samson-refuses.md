## 15. Samson Refuses

My sister came to find me at the timber yard on a Tuesday, though I only knew it was Tuesday because Odilon the bargeman had told me so while we pried nails out of a drowned crate. Time in this town had gone soft since the clock tower stopped — the whole valley moved by rumor now, by the level of lamp oil and the itch of unshaved chins — but Odilon kept count the way some men keep grudges, out of habit and spite, and I trusted him for both.

Theo picked her way across the sawdust in those army boots she'd worn down at the heel, and I knew before she opened her mouth that this was not a social call. My sister has never in her life made a social call. She arrives with purpose or she doesn't arrive at all, and when she was a girl that purpose was usually tattling on me, and lately it had been saving people, which is tattling's nobler cousin.

"They posted the manifest," she said.

"I heard."

"Forty berths. The barge goes Saturday, and after that the bridge chains shut until — " She didn't finish. Nobody finished that sentence anymore; everyone in town had learned to leave it hanging like laundry nobody wanted to bring in.

"I heard," I said again, because it was true. A labor battalion hears everything. We are the furniture of the army — men talk over us, around us, directly through us — and they forget that furniture collects what falls on it. The barge leaves Saturday. The battalion stays. Those two facts had been sitting in my chest all morning like swallowed stones, smooth and cold and perfectly familiar.

"Theo." I set down the hammer. "Say whatever you came to say."

She looked at the crate, at Odilon, who had suddenly discovered an urgent interest in the far end of the yard. Coward, I thought fondly. Then she looked at me, and my sister's eyes have always been the most honest part of her face, which is a terrible thing to say about a woman who keeps a secret ledger, but there you are.

"There's one berth left," she said. "A real one. Senior staff allocation — mine. I can transfer it. I've already worked out how. It transfers to family, Samson, and whether either of us likes the paperwork or not, you are that."

The sawdust seemed to shift under my boots.

"You're offering me your seat," I said.

"I'm offering a seat. Whose it started as doesn't matter."

"It matters," I said, "enormously."

* * *

Let me tell you something about pride, since apparently this is the chapter where I'm required to be honest about mine. Pride is what a man builds when he has nothing else to build. Our father died with two hands, one Bible, and a house that leaked, and he left us his pride like an inheritance neither of us could refuse — Theo wore hers quiet, folded into her spine, and I wore mine loud, out on my shoulders where everyone could see it, which is the cheaper way and the heavier one.

Six years of silence between us, and here she was breaking it a second time — because the first break had already happened, hadn't it, the day she walked into the labor camp office and went white as flour seeing me in a work detail's grey. Six years, and then three weeks of careful politeness across a dressing station, me hauling stretchers, her bandaging men, both of us performing the fiction that we were strangers who happened to share a nose.

And now she stood in the sawdust offering me the thing I could not take without dying somewhere important.

"No," I said.

"You haven't even — "

"No. It belongs to Amara."

"Amara has no claim on the manifest. She's not staff, she's not wounded, she's not registered anywhere except — " Theo stopped. Except my ledger. That's where she'd been going. Except in a book that officially does not exist.

"Then make her a claim," I said. "You're good at that. You're the most resourceful woman I have ever known, Odile Oyelaran, and I say that with love and lingering terror. Put the girl's name on that paper. Put it twice if you have to."

"And you?"

"What about me?"

"Samson." She said my name the way our mother used to, the way that meant *I see through you and I am tired of it.* "The battalion stays. You know what stays means. The thaw comes, the valley floods, and the engineers' report — I've seen it, don't look at me like that, I read everything in that office — says the low quarter goes under before any second barge could possibly — "

"I know what it says."

"Then take the berth."

"No."

She made a sound halfway between a laugh and something worse. "You always were stubborn past the point of sense."

"Runs in the family."

"It doesn't have to be this. There are forty berths and there are — "

"There are two hundred people in your book," I said quietly, "and forty seats on a barge, and you and I both did that arithmetic days ago. Don't insult me with pretending otherwise. Take the arithmetic away from my seat and put Amara in it, and then every other number is somebody else's problem for one more night."

* * *

Here is the thing I had sworn never to tell her, and which the sight of her standing there, ready to give up her own passage for a man who hadn't written to her in six years, pried out of me like a rotten tooth.

When Theo was nineteen, she stole four hundred francs from Mr. Aubertin's shop till — the pharmacy where she clerked, before the nursing, before everything — and she stole it for reasons that were half desperation and half fury, because Aubertin had shorted our mother's wages for two years and called it bookkeeping error, and Theo had found the proof and could do nothing legal with it. Four hundred francs. She gave most of it to our mother and buried the rest of the story so deep I think she half convinced herself it never happened.

But I found the gap in the till. I was seventeen and working nights hauling crates for Aubertin, and I saw the discrepancy, and I understood immediately whose hand had made it, because my sister counted things the way other people breathe.

And I went to Aubertin and I said: it was me.

He didn't believe me at first. I was the dull one, the steady one, the boy who'd return a dropped coin on a market street. So I confessed in detail. I invented details. I described the lock, the tin, the hour — I'd watched Theo well enough to reconstruct all of it — and I let him dock my wages for a year and put my name in his black book, and when the gendarmes came sniffing later over some other matter and asked Aubertin if he'd ever had trouble with his laborers, my name was the one he remembered.

That black book followed me. It followed me into conscription classifications and out of them; it was why I got routed into a labor battalion instead of a regiment, why a clerk in Lille once looked at me and said *ah, the thief* with the mild satisfaction of a man filing paper into the correct drawer. Six years of silence from Theo began the same month I took that blame, though she never knew it — she thought my silence was about an argument we'd had, some small sharp thing about her leaving home, and I let her believe it because the truth was a gift I couldn't hand her without also handing her the bill.

The bill being: your little brother spent six years carrying your sin so you could become a saint, and never once asked for thanks, and hated you a little every day for making it so easy to love him anyway.

Pride, I told you. Cheaper construction than most, holds less weight than you'd think.

* * *

"Aubertin," I said. "The till. Nineteen eleven."

Theo went very still. Stillness is her war-face; I've watched her stand that still over dying men.

"What about it?"

"You know what about it."

"That money went to Maman. For the medicine. The doctor in Roubaix said — " She stopped herself, and I watched her do the sum on her face: the year, the silence, the black book, the battalion. I watched her arrive.

"You didn't," she whispered.

"I did."

"Samson. Samson, no. Tell me you didn't take that blame."

"Would it change anything if I lied? You taught me to count, Odile. Counting doesn't lie even when people do."

Her hands came up over her mouth, and then down again, fast, the way soldiers correct themselves. And then she was angry — of course she was angry, anger is the first coat of paint over anything unbearable in my family — and her voice came out low and shaking.

"Six years. You let me think you despised me for leaving. You let me carry that for six years, that my own brother couldn't forgive me for wanting a life, and the whole time it was — it was *this*? You were punishing yourself in secret and billing me the silence?"

"I wasn't billing you anything. I was staying out of your light."

"Your *light*?" Now her voice cracked clean through. "You absolute fool. You mule. You proud, ridiculous man — do you know what I would have given? Do you know what I did give? Every ward I've ever worked, every night I've sat up with strangers' sons, half of it has been penance for a theft you didn't even — " She stopped. Breathed. My sister breathes the way she does everything, deliberately, like it costs. "We are two of the stupidest Oyelaranas who ever lived."

"That at least is settled science," I said, and she laughed, one wet broken note of it, and wiped her face with her sleeve like a girl of ten.

* * *

But pride is armor, and armor cracks in one place and still covers the rest, so when she came back at me — and she did come back, she came back within the minute, because grief makes my sister efficient — I was ready for her.

"The berth," she said. "This changes nothing about the berth. If anything — "

"It changes everything about the berth. I have spent six years paying for one crime, Theo. I will not spend my sister's life paying for another. That seat is hers. Amara's. Nine years old, no mother, no papers, no country that wants her, and the single best counter of seconds I have ever met. She gets it. You get it. You're senior staff, you get yourself across that water, and you take her with you, and you build whatever comes next on the far bank."

"And you stay here to drown on principle?"

"I stay here because the battalion stays, and because two hundred names in your ledger need someone who knows how to carry things. I'm strong, I'm expendable, and I have excellent posture. The army made me a porter, Odile. Let me port."

"It isn't a joke."

"No," I said. "It isn't. But I've noticed that when you stop joking, you stop breathing right, and I'd rather keep you breathing."

She stared at me for a long moment across the sawdust and the drowned crate and the twenty years between a girl counting stolen francs and a man nailing together rafts from wreckage. Somewhere behind her, at the far end of the yard, Odilon was humming a bargeman's hymn with the studied loudness of a man pretending not to witness history.

"If you stay," Theo said finally, "and the flood comes, and the marsh road fails — "

"Then write my name in the book. First page. Big letters. Marguerite gets second billing."

"Don't."

"Someone should get second billing. It's a good book, Odile. Whatever else happens to it, it's the truest record anyone's kept in this whole rotten valley."

She crossed the sawdust and put her arms around me, sudden and fierce, sawdust and all, and I stood there with my hammer in one hand and my sister crushing my ribs with both of hers, and I thought: six years wasted, and for what, exactly? For the privilege of being forgiven alone?

"Saturday," she said against my shoulder. "I'll put Amara's name down. And Samson — thank you. For nineteen eleven. Even though you were an idiot about it."

"Idiocy is also hereditary," I said. "Ask anyone."

She pulled back, and her eyes were red and her jaw was set, and I recognized the expression because I'd seen it on our mother and on our father and in every mirror of my life: the Oyelaran look of a person who has decided something and will inform the world presently.

She left the way she'd come, boots striking the boards hard, and I picked up my hammer and went back to the crate, and Odilon wandered over and looked at the nails and then at me.

"Well?" he said. "Did you take it?"

"Hell, no."

"Good," he said. "Berths are like coffins. Only fools volunteer early." He handed me another nail, and we worked, and the afternoon went on being Tuesday, as far as anyone knew.

It was only later, walking back toward the barracks in the blue dark with the river running high and loud under the bridge, that I heard the gossip moving through the labor lines like wind through wheat: tomorrow, they said, there was to be a staff inspection — a major from divisional command, arriving at the dressing station with lists and ledgers and the leisure to compare them. Checking manifests, they said. Checking allocations. Checking everything.

I thought of my sister's face going carefully blank, and of a certain book that officially did not exist, and I lengthened my stride.
