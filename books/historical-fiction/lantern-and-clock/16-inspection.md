## 16. Inspection

The notice arrived the way bad news always did in that town: carried by a boy too young to understand it, on paper too thin to hold its consequences.

*Staff inspection. Tuesday. Major Delorme, Fourth Section. All wards to be made ready. All records to be produced.*

All records.

I read it twice by the stove, while the rain did its patient work on the roof — a drip in the northeast corner had kept time for us since October, better than any clock, and I had grown oddly fond of it. *All records to be produced.* The major's clerk had underlined nothing, because clerks never underlined anything, but I heard the underline anyway, in the voice of a man I'd met exactly once and disliked immediately, saying: *Sister Oyelaran, we are aware you keep notes.*

I was aware that they were aware. That was the trouble with secrets in wartime; everyone who mattered pretended not to see them, until suddenly someone decided to see them, formally, with a clipboard.

The ledger sat where it always sat, wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of my instrument case, beneath the bandages I never used because they were for show. Two hundred and eleven names now. Two hundred and eleven people the official rolls had swallowed or spat out or simply declined to notice — civilians treated in this ward, deserters' wives, a Spanish laborer, three old men from the tannery, an entire family named Okonkwo whom nobody's paperwork acknowledged because paperwork, like weather, has opinions about who exists.

I had written every name myself. I knew them the way you know the rosary: by rhythm, by heart, in the dark.

And on Tuesday a major from the Fourth Section wanted to look at everything.

* * *

I will not pretend I thought about anything grand that morning. I thought about oilcloth. I thought about whether hymnals were searched. I thought, briefly and uselessly, about drowning the ledger in the cistern, and then I thought of Amara's face when she caught me doing it, because the child missed nothing and forgave less, and I put the idea down like a hot pan.

Because here is what nobody tells you about defiance: it is mostly logistics. Courage is a quarter-hour of drama wrapped in a fortnight of laundry. The question was not *will I protect these names* — that question had answered itself somewhere around the fourth funeral — the question was *where does a book weigh least?*

Not in a floorboard. Floors get pried.
Not in a mattress. Mattresses get flipped.
Not on my person. Officers respect a nurse's person right up until they don't.

I stood in the ward at dawn, before the orderlies came, holding the book and turning in a slow circle like a dog trying to find its own tail, and it was Marguerite — God help me, Marguerite — who solved it without solving it.

She was at the chapel end of the corridor, restacking the hymnals that the soldiers used as pillows, footrests, coasters, and occasionally, in moments of terror or boredom, as actual hymnals. She held one up, squinted at a water stain, and said, "Nobody has opened one of these since mobilization. I could write *God is watching* on page forty and no one would know."

I looked at the stack. Forty identical brown books. Forty places where a slim oilcloth packet could sleep between *Abide With Me* and *Battle Hymn*, flat against the boards, invisible by virtue of being boring.

"Marguerite," I said, "you are either a genius or a corrupting influence."

"I'm both. It's why they made me head sister." She didn't ask what I needed the hiding place for. That was her particular mercy: she helped without witnessing, so she could later say, honestly, that she knew nothing. We had developed this choreography over months without ever discussing it, the way two dancers stop counting steps.

We chose hymnal number seventeen from the top — third shelf, behind the lectern, in the shadow of a plaster saint missing one hand. I slid the ledger in flat, spine to spine with the psalms, and closed it, and the book looked like exactly what it appeared to be: a dull object in a room of dull objects. The omitted, tucked in with the songs of the saved. There was a sermon in that somewhere, and I resolved never to write it.

Then I went to deal with the harder problem, which was nine years old, ate like a stevedore, and could not be hidden in a hymnal.

* * *

Amara knew something was coming, because Amara always knew. Children raised on other people's emergencies develop a barometer. She had stopped playing in the corridor and taken up station in the ward kitchen, sitting on the flour barrel with her legs crossed at the ankle, watching me scrub a floor that was already clean.

"The lantern man says a big soldier is coming," she announced. "With a red hat."

"A major. Red cap."

"He's coming to count things."

"Yes."

"We're not things," she said. It wasn't a question, and I was careful not to answer it like one.

"No," I said. "We're not."

She swung her feet. "Theo. If he finds your book—"

"Theo," I corrected automatically, though she'd earned the short name a hundred times over; some habits of dignity die hard in a woman raised by a schoolmistress mother. "If who finds what?"

"If the major finds your book." Her eyes were level. Nine years old, and level. "What happens?"

I set down the brush and considered lying to her, which I had done perhaps twice in our acquaintance and badly both times. She counted backwards when she was frightened — seconds, steps, breaths, stars, whatever the world offered — and I had learned that the counting was not a game but a rope she threw herself, hand over hand, away from the edge of whatever was coming. A lie would tangle the rope. So instead I gave her the truth in work clothes.

"He would take it. And then some of the people whose names are in it would become — uncountable again. And uncountable people don't get barges."

Amara absorbed this the way she absorbed everything: silently, completely, permanently. Then she said, "You should hide it in the hymnal. Nobody opens those. Even Father Aubry doesn't, and he's paid to."

I confess I laughed, which was dangerous, because laughing at dawn before an inspection loosens something you need tight. "It's done," I said. "Now I need you to do something harder than keeping quiet."

"Harder than quiet?"

"When the major comes through, he'll want the ward to look empty of anything interesting. You are many things, little one, but invisible isn't one of them. So instead of being invisible, you're going to be *interesting* — loudly, endlessly, unbearably interesting. Do you still play the counting game with the soldiers? Backwards from a hundred?"

"With Jeannot I do it in French and with Papa Ndiaye in Wolof and with the engineer in fives."

"Then here is your task. When Major Delorme arrives, you are going to attach yourself to him like a burr and count for him. Everything. The ceiling beams, the floor tiles, his buttons if he'll allow it. Backwards, forwards, in French, in whatever tongue he brings. You will be so relentlessly, magnificently tedious that he will remember nothing else about this ward except a small girl who would not stop counting at him."

Amara considered this with the gravity of a general reviewing terrain. "He looks like a man who hates children."

"Wonderful. Men who hate children leave fastest."

"What if he hates counting?"

"Then he's in the wrong army, darling. Armies are nothing but counting. He'll love it once he remembers."

She slid off the flour barrel, dusted her skirt, and practiced: "One hundred, ninety-nine, ninety-eight—" then stopped. "Theo. If I'm loud, I can't watch the hymnal for you."

"There is no hymnal to watch. There are forty identical hymnals and a saint with one hand, and you have a job. Count."

"One hundred," she said, "ninety-nine," and went off toward the ward to rehearse her ambush on the first orderly she found, who was, poor soul, asleep.

* * *

Major Delorme arrived at two o'clock precisely, which told me everything about him before he opened his mouth: a man who believed the world kept appointments because he did. He was not large, but he was *pressed* — trousers creased to a knife's edge, boots shining with the effort of a batman's whole morning — and he wore the expression of a man who had been sent to find something and intended to find it, because careers in the Fourth Section were built on findings.

He was accompanied by Captain Roux, whom I knew slightly and trusted not at all, and followed at a distance by Amara, who had positioned herself by the door with the patience of a heron.

"Sister Oyelaran." He consulted a card. "This station houses — how many beds?"

"Sixty-two functioning, Major. Eleven more when it doesn't rain into them."

"And your records. Admissions, discharges, deaths."

"In the office. Sister Marguerite keeps the register; I keep the treatment notes." Every word true. I had learned from Marguerite that truth, cut thin enough, passes any inspection.

We walked the ward. He looked at the amputation rack and nodded approvingly, as though the machinery of maiming reflected well on our housekeeping. He looked at the morphine cabinet and I handed over the key without being asked, which surprised him pleasantly; men like Delorme expect resistance so they can enjoy overcoming it, and are unsettled by cooperation. Roux counted vials and wrote numbers. Behind us, faintly, came the sound of a small girl beginning her approach.

"Ninety-seven, ninety-six, ninety-five—" Amara had chosen the ceiling beams, pointing at each one with a wooden spoon she had liberated from the kitchen, walking backward ahead of the major's path like a herald.

Delorme glanced up. "What is that child doing?"

"That's Amara. She counts when she's nervous. She counts when she's happy. She counts when there's nothing else happening, which, Major, describes most of us here." I smiled the smile I keep for chaplains and inspectors. "Since summer. She came in with a refugee column; no papers, no family. The town knows her now."

"Mm." He made a note. I felt the note like a fishhook going in — *unregistered minor, station* — but there was nothing to be done about it except continue walking, so we continued walking.

"Eighty-one, eighty, seventy-nine—"

"You're going backward," Delorme said, despite himself.

"Backward is better," Amara said. "Forward you can guess. Backward you have to pay attention." Which was, I thought, either nonsense or philosophy, possibly both, and Delorme frowned as if he suspected it was philosophy and had not been warned.

In the office, Marguerite produced the register with the smoothness of long practice. Delorme ran his finger down columns. I watched his finger the way sailors watch weather. The register was honest — that was the terrible elegance of our arrangement; the official book lied only by omission, and omission leaves no fingerprints. Sixty-two beds, sixty-two lines. Nothing about the Okonkwos. Nothing about the tannery men, treated on pallets in the chapel because officially the chapel contained no patients, merely prayers.

"Your death rate is low," Delorme observed.

"Our river is fast," I said, "and our surgeon is good. Grief runs downstream here, Major. Other towns keep what we lose."

He grunted. Roux wrote. Somewhere behind us: "Forty-two, forty-one, forty — Theo, tell him the beams go in forties, four rows of ten, he can check—"

"*Does* anyone check?" Delorme asked me dryly.

"She'd be wounded," I said. "Counting is serious business in this house."

He moved to the supply stores. This was the danger hour — inspections grow bold after the first twenty minutes, emboldened by finding nothing, suspicious for having found nothing. In the stores stood my instrument case. In the instrument case lay bandages and a false bottom and, formerly, everything I had spent a year building. My heart performed a small, undignified maneuver, and I told it firmly that it was being melodramatic, that the case contained exactly what it appeared to contain, that the ledger was forty feet away sleeping between psalms.

Delorme opened the case himself. Roux watched the door. I watched Delorme's hands — clean hands, office hands, hands that had signed things rather than done them.

Bandages. Splints. Scissors. The tray of instruments I polished nightly because polishing was cheaper than prayer. He lifted the scissors, sighted along them like a duelist, set them back. He pressed a thumb along the case's lining, idly, the way a man pats his pockets —

— and Amara arrived.

She did not run; she had been coached, and besides, herons do not run. She simply materialized at his elbow, spoon in hand, and said, "Nineteen, eighteen, seventeen — you have nineteen buttons and I counted them twice and got nineteen both times, which is *good*, Jeannot's coat only gives fifteen twice out of five tries—"

"Child," said Delorme.

"Seventeen, sixteen. Do you want to hear it in Wolof? Papa Ndiaye says numbers sound angrier in Wolof."

"I do not require—"

"Fifteen. Fifteen in Wolof is *fudd*." She pronounced it with tremendous satisfaction, as though she had handed him a medal. "Fifteen buttons in Wolof is *fudd*. Fourteen, thirteen—"

"Captain," Delorme said to Roux, closing the instrument case with a snap I felt in my teeth, "the stores are in order. The dispensary next. Sister, your records are — adequate."

Adequate. From the Fourth Section, that was a bouquet thrown rather than tossed.

"Twelve, eleven—"

He left. That is the whole of it, the anticlimax I've since learned to distrust: he simply left, cap straight, Roux trailing, and Amara walked him as far as the gate counting his buttons backward from twelve, and he did not shoot her, arrest her, or smile, though at the very last — I saw it from the ward window, and I would swear to it under oath — he checked his own cuff to confirm the number.

* * *

That evening the rain stopped, which should have felt like mercy and instead felt like the sky drawing breath.

Marguerite retrieved hymnal seventeen and returned the ledger to me over the evening ration, wrapped in a napkin like contraband bread, and neither of us spoke of it, because our arrangement required plausible ignorance even between ourselves — a silly fiction, but fictions keep people alive the way walls do, by standing between them and things.

Amara got her supper and half of mine, by treaty. She ate, and counted her beans (thirty-one), and then grew quiet in the way that preceded her real questions.

"The major wrote your name in his notebook," she said.

"He writes everyone's name. It's how majors pray."

"He wrote *mine*."

I put down my spoon. "Yes. He did."

"Why?"

"Because you have no papers, little one, and men who make lists hate it when the world sends them somebody not on any list. It offends their religion."

Amara turned this over. "Is that bad? Being not on a list?"

I thought of the ledger upstairs, two hundred and eleven names strong, of Samson somewhere south with a labor battalion and no berth, of the bridge and its chains waiting in the fog. I thought: *it is the most dangerous thing in the world, and also the only thing that ever saved anybody.*

"It's bad," I said, "when the wrong people are counting. So we count for ourselves. That's all my book is, Amara. Us, counting for ourselves."

She nodded slowly, accepting this, filing it wherever she filed such things. Then she yawned — a vast, boneless, nine-year-old yawn — and said, "Eleven days of lamp oil left in the big jug. I checked. After that we use the little jug, which is eight days, unless we burn slower, and then it's more."

"How many days altogether, then?"

"Nineteen," she said promptly. "Twenty if we're careful."

I lit the lantern for our ritual — one flame, passed hand to hand, her small fingers around mine — and I thought: *nineteen days.* The engineers said the thaw would come early this year; the colonel said the second barge might come; the clock in the tower had begun, I'd noticed that very afternoon, to lose time, ticking a little behind itself like a man reluctant to arrive somewhere.

Nineteen days, and a child measuring our lives out in lamp oil because the town clock couldn't be bothered.

At midnight the drip in the northeast corner faltered — the freeze finally reaching the gap in the roof — and the silence woke me. I lay listening to a world that had lost its metronome, and from across the dark, Amara's voice, small and steady, counting herself down into sleep:

"...four, three, two, one. Again. Four, three, two..."

Outside, in the tower, the clock gave a shudder, a stutter, and stopped altogether.
