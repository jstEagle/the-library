## 17. The Clock in the Tower

The clock stopped on a Tuesday, or what I was prepared to call a Tuesday until someone official told me otherwise.

I heard it happen. Everyone in the low quarter did — a sound like a great throat clearing once, decisively, and then refusing to speak again. I was changing a dressing at the time, Corporal Aymes's leg, the left one, the one we were still negotiating with, and he lifted his head off the pallet and said, "Was that us?" as if a shell had landed close enough to be polite about it.

"No," I said. "That was the clock."

"Stopping?"

"Stopping."

He considered this while I finished the bandage. "Seems fair," he said at last. "I'd stop too, if I could."

There is a particular kind of silence that follows when a thing that has marked every hour of your life since you were born simply declines to continue. You do not notice the ticking until it goes. Then it is all you can hear, this absence, ringing louder than any bell ever did. The tower stands at the center of Saint-Aubin-sur-Lomme the way a spine stands in a body, and for three days afterward people kept looking up at it the way you prod a tooth that has begun to hurt — hopefully, then resentfully, then not at all, because hope is expensive and we were all on short rations of it.

The engineers, when they finally climbed the tower, reported that the mechanism had frozen. Frost in the works, they said. Cold had gotten into the gears and welded them still, and no one could say when — or whether — it would run again. The parts would have to come from Amiens, or possibly from heaven, and both deliveries were equally likely given the state of the rail lines.

"So what's the time, then?" asked Odilon the bargeman, who had come to the dressing station with a gash in his forearm from a cable and an opinion on everything else.

"It is Tuesday," said Sister Marguerite, with the confidence of a woman who has said grace at fixed hours for thirty years.

"It stopped Monday night."

"Then it is Tuesday," she repeated, "until further notice."

But that was the trouble, you see. There *was* no further notice. The church bells had gone silent weeks ago — the bronze requisitioned for shells, God's voice melted down and sent east to make widows more efficiently — and now the clock had followed them into retirement, and the town drifted loose from its moorings like a barge with its lines cut. Time became rumor. It became negotiation. It became whatever the most confident person in the room insisted upon, which in a military hospital meant it became whatever the quartermaster needed it to be.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about losing shared time: you discover how much of civilization is simply agreement. We had agreed that morning came at six, that dressings changed at eight, that morphine came at intervals measured in hours rather than in suffering. Remove the agreement and you are left with the raw material underneath — light, cold, hunger, pain — and the raw material does not keep schedule. Pain arrives when it pleases. Fever burns through the night without consulting anyone's watch. And the men in the ward, who had spent three years being moved by whistles and bugles and shouted orders, lay in their cots listening to their own hearts and discovering, several of them for the first time, that a body keeps its own poor clock and it runs on fear.

I solved it the way nurses solve everything: inventively, and with other people's property.

Lamp oil was my hour hand. The ward burned four lanterns, and each held oil enough for roughly eleven hours of light — I knew this because I had measured it, because measuring things is the only prayer I have ever been any good at. When a lantern went dry, an hour had passed, give or take. Eleven lanterns made a day, made a night, made a day again. My ledger began keeping two columns: names, and lanterns. *Delacour, fever breaking — third lamp.* *Aymes, wound closing grudgingly — seventh lamp.* The officers' clerks wrote dates. I wrote fire. History will decide which record was more honest, though I have my suspicions, having met history's clerks.

Morphine vials were my minute hand, which tells you something about the season we were in. Each vial, rationed properly across the men who needed it — and all of them needed it; this was the one arithmetic nobody disputed — lasted approximately four days. Four vials emptied meant sixteen days gone. Sixteen days since the clock stopped. Sixteen days closer to the thaw, which came on its own schedule and had never required our permission.

"You're counting in lamp oil," Samson said, finding me at the dispatch table with my ledger open and three empty vials lined up like little glass headstones.

"I'm counting in what lasts," I said. "Clocks are opinions. Oil is a fact."

He turned a vial in his brown fingers, careful, always careful, even now, especially now. "Six years you've been doing sums like that."

"Someone has to."

"Maman used to count in spoons," he said quietly, and we both stood there a moment in the country where mothers live after they are dead, and neither of us said anything else, because we had not yet learned — were perhaps never going to learn — how to visit that country together.

* * *

Amara adapted faster than any of us, children being the natural aristocracy of improvisation. Within two days she had invented a game — or rather converted her counting, her old faithful counting, the backward numbers she whispered when the shelling came close, into public currency. She went through the ward selling time.

"What's the hour?" Private Roussel asked her, playing along, because the men played along with Amara the way sailors play along with a ship's cat, out of superstition and genuine fondness.

She studied the nearest lantern with the gravity of an astronomer. "Half past oil," she pronounced. "Quarter to the next lamp."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow comes after eleven sleeps of the light." She held up small fingers. "Unless it doesn't. Then it comes later."

Roussel laughed, but I saw him glance up at the dark window, and I saw the laugh sit oddly on him afterward, like a coat cut for a bigger man. She had told the truth, that was the thing. Tomorrow *would* come whenever it pleased and not one minute sooner, and there was nothing in heaven or the Sixth Army to hurry it along.

At night we kept our ritual. Lantern-lighting, hers and mine, the small ceremony that had carried us since summer. But now the lighting of the lamps had become something larger — not a comfort merely but a calendar, a civic institution, the closest thing Saint-Aubin had left to a town crier. When Amara touched the flame to the first wick, everyone in the ward who could lift his head lifted it, and the ones who could not lift their heads turned their eyes, and for a moment the whole room agreed on something: *evening*. One word. One fact. We hoarded these agreements like sugar.

"The clock will come back," Amara said one evening, crouched over the match, shielding the flame with both hands as though it were a sparrow. "Papa-fix says frost leaves when frost wants."

"Papa-fix being the engineer?"

"Papa-fix being the engineer who let me hold his spanner." She straightened, satisfied, the lantern blooming between us. "Things that are broken get fixed. That's the order of things. That's the whole of it."

I thought of Samson's hands. I thought of six winters of silence between us, and of the letter I had not yet found the courage to answer properly. Things that are broken get fixed — unless they are people, unless they are brothers, unless they are the trust of a girl who had already lost every adult who ever promised her anything, and had decided, with the terrible economy of nine-year-olds, to believe in repair anyway because the alternative was unthinkable and she had done her share of the unthinkable.

"Yes," I lied gently, tucking the blanket under her chin. "That's the whole of it."

* * *

It was Marguerite, of course, who named the real danger.

We were scrubbing instruments — or performing the ritual approximation of scrubbing, the carbolic having gone thin as regret — when she said, without preamble, "You know they will use this."

"Use what?"

"The stopped clock." Her hands kept moving, scalpel, forceps, scalpel, her face kept its nun's calm, but her voice had that scraped-bare quality it took on when the convent-trained composure ran out and something older showed through. "No dates, Théo. No hours. Nothing that can be checked later. Do you understand what that means for your book?"

I did not answer. My hands had gone still in the water.

"When the reports are written — and they *will* be written — every omission will hide inside this fog. Men who died will be recorded as men who never arrived. Arrivals will be shuffled like cards. 'Sometime in the confusion,' they'll say. 'During the period when accurate records were impossible.' They will thank God — the actual God, mind you, whom I serve and who I strongly suspect is taking notes — they will thank Him for the weather." She set down a forceps with unnecessary precision. "Your ledger is the only place in this valley where a name is nailed to an event. And now the events themselves are becoming smoke. How will you prove when? How will you prove *anything*, when the whole town has lost the ability to say Tuesday?"

"Oil and morphine," I said. "I'm keeping my own calendar. Lamp-counts. Vial-counts."

Marguerite looked at me for a long moment, water dripping from her chapped hands, and what was in her face was not doubt exactly. It was pity wearing doubt's clothes.

"Fifty lamps from now," she said, "when some major demands to know why your figures don't match the regimental diary — and they won't match, Théo, his diary is fiction with better binding — what will you say? 'Trust me, I counted lamps'?" She shook her head slowly. "Accuracy has always been dangerous. Now it will look like madness. Or forgery. And forgery, in the wrong courtroom, is a hanging matter dressed in ink."

"They're not going to hang a nurse."

"They shot one at Lille," she said, "or so it's said, and so much is only 'so it's said' now, isn't it? That's the other gift of the stopped clock. Everything becomes rumor. Atrocity becomes rumor. Heroism becomes rumor. The dead become rumor." She dried her hands, folding the towel in thirds, in halves, in thirds again. "Keep your book. I would think less of you if you didn't. But keep it knowing what it costs, and knowing that no one — no one — will thank you for it while it matters."

She left me with the instruments and the thin carbolic and the water going cold in the basin, and outside the window the tower stood black against the darker sky, its face lit by nothing, its hands frozen at twenty past ten on a night that might have been last week.

Twenty past ten. I have wondered since whether the clock chose its moment with some final mechanical dignity — stopping at the hour of quiet wards, of rounds done, of sisters sitting down at last with cold tea — or whether it simply died mid-sentence, indifferent, like everything else that year.

* * *

On what I calculated to be the fourth day — fourth set of eleven lamps — I sat with my ledger after lights-out and tried an experiment. I attempted to reconstruct the month. Not from the official records, which had become a gallery of mirrors reflecting one another, but from memory: shellfire I could date by what it destroyed, arrivals I could date by whose blood was on which of my aprons, the courier's letter, the bridge announcement, Samson's boots coming through the door.

The reconstruction fell apart by the second week. My memories were anchored to suffering, and suffering does not come dated. Had the inspection happened before or after the clock stopped? Before, I thought. Two days before? Three? Major Quenneville's face floated up in my memory, pink and officious, and refused to attach itself to any hour whatsoever. The world had not merely lost its time; it was actively eating mine, digesting the days into an undifferentiated porridge of oil and laudanum and snow.

And I understood, sitting there in the smell of carbolic and wet wool, that this was not an accident of frost in the gears. Wars do not lose track of time by mischance. Wars *hide* in lost time the way thieves hide in crowd noise. Every day that could not be proven was a day that could not be audited. Every man whose death had no date attached had, administratively speaking, never died at all — and a man who never died cannot be mourned officially, cannot be compensated, cannot appear in any accounting that might embarrass a colonel or a ministry or a bridge-closing announced three weeks before the thaw.

The clock had not broken. The clock had been *relieved of duty*.

I am aware of how that sounds. I had four empty vials and a frozen tower as evidence, and neither would hang a dog. But certainty arrived anyway, the way it always does with me — not as thunder but as arithmetic, quiet and complete. Somewhere above me, in offices lit by better lamps than mine, men were reading reports written in a language where time itself had been decommissioned, and smiling.

I opened the ledger to a fresh page. At the top, where a date should go, I wrote:

*Night of the forty-fourth lamp. Fourth vial empty. Frost in the works of everything.*

Then, beneath it, the new arrivals. Nine names. Nine names that existed nowhere else in Christendom, pinned to lamp-oil and glass and my own stubborn subtraction, floating free of Tuesday and Thursday and every honest hour that had ever been struck by a bell.

Below me in the ward, Amara slept counting backwards, her breath keeping better time than anything in Saint-Aubin. Above me the tower kept its frozen vigil, twenty past ten forever, the whole town sentenced to a single moment that refused either to arrive or to end.

I blew out my candle, all but the stub I would need for the next round, and was pulling my boots back on for it when Odilon came through the door with snow in his beard and a strange light in his eye, saying he'd been down at the waterline and there was something I needed to see before the river took it — something washed against the pilings that he reckoned, he reckoned, had a number painted on it.
