## 19. First Frost, First Flood Warning

The frost came in the night like a debt collector — quietly, and taking more than expected.

I knew it before I opened my eyes. The water in the basin by my cot had gone stiff as a catechism, and my breath hung about my face in a small grey ghost that seemed to be trying to leave without me. Amara had burrowed against my back sometime before dawn, the way she did when the cold made her dreams argumentative, and for one full minute I permitted myself the luxury of pretending that the cold was only cold.

Then Corporal Enfield began hammering on the door, and luxury ended.

"Sister Oyelaran! Engineers are at the church. They want every senior medical officer, and they're asking for you by name."

By name. In my experience, being asked for by name was never the overture to anything pleasant. Nobody summons you by name to hand you flowers. I dressed in the dark, kissed the top of Amara's head where it emerged from the blanket like the last stubborn mushroom of autumn, and went out into a morning so sharp it felt personal.

* * *

The church had been requisitioned in September and never quite given back its dignity. The altar still stood at one end, draped now in maps instead of linen, and the engineers had strung their charts from the rafters with wire and clothes-pegs, so that the whole east end of the building looked like a laundry line hung with the valley's death warrant.

There were three of them, these engineers, standing in a row beneath their maps like men presenting a prize pig they privately suspected of disease. Two were French, mud-coloured and hollow-eyed, and the third was an English major whose moustache preceded him into rooms by several seconds and whose voice carried the particular cheerfulness of a man delivering bad news he has rehearsed.

"Sisters, Captains," he said, though none of us held rank worth the word. "Thank you for coming. I'll keep this brief."

Nobody believed him. Men who say they'll keep things brief have usually spent three days failing to keep them brief with themselves.

He tapped the largest map. The river ran through it in blue, and the low quarter of the town — the tannery streets, the wash-houses, Marguerite's ward, Odilon's mooring, every crooked lane where the omitted names lived because the omitted could not afford high ground — lay below the blue like a secret the map was ashamed of.

"The thaw," said the major, "begins early this year. Snowpack in the hills is at twice the ten-year mean. The engineers' survey confirms what the old bargemen have been saying since October." He paused, the way men pause when they hope someone else will say the terrible part for them. No one obliged. "When the thaw comes, the valley floods. Not the polite spring flooding. The other kind."

"How long?" Sister Marguerite's voice came from beside me, steady as ever, though her hands were folded so tightly her knuckles had gone white as her coif.

"Four weeks. Perhaps five if we're lucky. Perhaps three if we're not."

"And the second barge?" This from Captain Leclair, who commanded what remained of the supply line and whose optimism I had long suspected of being a war wound.

The major consulted his boots. "The second barge has been reassigned."

The words dropped into the church and lay there. Reassigned. What a gentle little verb. Somewhere behind it sat a desk, and behind the desk sat a man who had moved forty berths from our drowning town to some other purpose with the same motion of his pen he'd use to reorder bootlaces.

"You mean," I said, and heard my own voice come out flat and northern, "there is no second barge."

"I mean," the major said, with the patience of a man correcting a slow pupil, "that no second barge is presently scheduled. Circumstances may change."

Circumstances. I have come to hate that word the way sailors hate lee shore. Circumstances were what officers invoked whenever they meant *we have decided, and we would prefer you didn't ask who 'we' might be.*

"The bridge closes in eleven days," I said. "For repairs. You announced it yourself."

"The bridge closes. Yes. And after it closes, the town holds until relief comes overland or the roads dry. Standard contingency."

"Standard contingency," Marguerite repeated softly, as if tasting it. She looked at me then, just briefly, and I saw in her face the thing she had confessed to me weeks ago in the dispensary — the memory of having once stood in a room exactly like this one while men with maps decided which names would fit inside the arithmetic. She had told me she chose by order, not conscience, and that she had never forgiven herself, and that God perhaps had, but God was famously generous with other people's sins.

The major moved on to logistics. Sandbags. Elevated cots. Rations cached in the upper town. All very sensible. All entirely beside the point, because the point was standing unmentioned at the edge of every chart: two hundred souls on my ledger whom no manifest acknowledged, who would not appear in any contingency, because officially they did not exist, and you cannot evacuate a name that has never been written.

I copied the figures into my head as he spoke them — snowpack, cubic metres per second, projected crest heights — the way I copy everything, hoarding facts against the day the paper fails. And all the while I thought: *weeks, not months.* The thought had a weight to it, physical, like a stone sewn into my coat hem. I would feel it drag at me for days.

* * *

Afterward, Marguerite caught my sleeve in the churchyard. Frost furred the gravestones; the dead of this parish had better drainage than its living.

"You understood," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"That there's no barge coming. Yes."

"And that the two hundred—" She stopped, because in daylight, out loud, even among friends, the number sounded like sedition. She lowered her voice to the register nuns reserve for confidences that could get people shot. "They will simply be left. Drowned administratively."

"Drowned administratively," I agreed. "It saves ammunition. Very economical."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not joking, Marguerite. I'm breathing. Some of us require sarcasm the way the rest require prayer."

She almost smiled. Almost. Her eyes went past me to the river, grey and sluggish between its frosted banks, wearing its ice skin like a garment it hadn't finished fastening. "Theo. Whatever you're going to do — and I know you, so I know you're going to do something — do it quietly. I've seen what happens to women who write things down in wartime." A beat. "I was one of the ones who made it happen to them."

I put my hand over hers where it gripped my sleeve. Hers was freezing. Everything here was freezing. Even mercy, apparently, required mittens.

"I'm not writing anything new," I said. "I already wrote it. Six months of it. The question isn't the ledger anymore. The question is what a ledger is worth if everyone holding it drowns."

* * *

I walked back through the low quarter because I wanted to see it with engineer's eyes instead of nurse's eyes, and I recommend this exercise to no one. It is one thing to love a place. It is another to measure it.

The tannery streets sat barely a metre above the winter waterline. I counted steps down from the high road the way Amara counts backwards from a hundred, and at thirty-two steps I reached the door of the Clos family, who had taken in four refugees from the shelling and fed them from gardens the size of tablecloths. At fifty-one steps, the wash-houses where the laundresses worked waist-deep in steam eight hours a day and sang, God help us, sang. At seventy-four steps, Odilon's barge lay against its mooring, low in the water, looking patient and slightly insolent, as barges do.

At the crest projection the major had described, every one of those doors stood under two metres of moving water. Moving water does not knock. Moving water simply takes.

A woman was beating a rug on a balcony rail — Mme. Testard, whose husband was missing since August and whose son was on nobody's list but mine. She called down to me, cheerful as Sunday: "Sister! Tell me the frost means the fever will keep off the children, at least."

"It means exactly that, Madame," I lied warmly. "Cold is the best disinfectant there is."

Which is true, incidentally. That's the cruelty of good lies — they work best when stitched to truth. I walked on and let the stone in my coat hem drag.

* * *

Back at the dressing station, I did the arithmetic properly, alone, with the ledger open under the lamp where I do all my honest work. Two hundred and seven names now — I had added three during the night shift, including a boy of maybe sixteen who had crawled into the yard with his boot full of his own foot and who gave his name twice, differently, both times, because deserters learn caution young. Both versions went in the book. Accuracy, Marguerite warned me, is dangerous. But half-accuracy is worse; it gets people half-saved.

Two hundred and seven. Forty berths on a barge that had sailed without us. Eleven days until the chains went up on the bridge. Twenty-eight days, give or take the weather's mood, until the river came up the stairs of the low quarter looking for its own.

I sat with those numbers the way you sit with a dying man you cannot save — holding its hand, telling it truths it didn't want.

And then, because sitting with a problem has never once solved one, I got out the sheet of mapping paper I'd begun keeping folded inside the ledger's back board, and I looked again at the pencilled line I had drawn from the bargeman's hints. The marsh road. Overland, south of the valley rim, usable — Odilon had said, when he'd had too much wine and thought I was someone else's confessor — *usable at low water, sister, and only at low water, and only if the causeway holds, and only if you can walk in the dark.*

Low water. Before the thaw. Which meant the window existed, but it was closing at the speed of frost forming, and I had no map, no guide beyond a drunk bargeman's geography, and two hundred and seven reasons to be exactly right.

I heard Amara's feet on the stair — she has a step like a metronome, even intervals, always counting — and I closed the ledger and covered it with the hymnal, an old habit now, and pasted on the expression nurses keep for children and majors: attentive, calm, entirely unremarkable.

She came in with her mittens on indoors and snow in her hair and held up a jar. "Mme. Testard gave me pickled plums. She says the frost is a blessing."

"Mme. Testard," I said, "is a philosopher."

"She also says the river's making a noise at night. Under the ice. Like it's talking to itself." Amara set the jar on the table, studied me with those grave dark eyes that see far too much, and added: "You've got your thinking face on. The one where you're counting everybody."

I was, in fact, counting everybody. I seem to do little else these days.

"Eat your plums," I said. "Tomorrow we're going to learn a new word. It starts with *c.*"

"Causeway?" said Amara.

I am going to have to stop talking to Odilon within earshot of that child. Or possibly start thanking God that I don't.

Outside, the river spoke under its ice all night — a long, grinding, conversational groan, the sound of a thing gathering itself. Weeks, the engineers had said. Not months. I lay awake doing sums in the dark, and somewhere past midnight I heard the front door of Odilon's world open, metaphorically speaking, because at dawn there was a tap at my window and a broad silhouette breathing fog, and a voice like gravel steeped in wine:

"Sister. You'll want to talk terms before the frost breaks. Everyone wants to talk terms eventually."
