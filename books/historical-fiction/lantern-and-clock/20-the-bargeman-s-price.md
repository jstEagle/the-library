## 20. The Bargeman's Price

Odilon Ferreol kept his barge tied up under the willows a half mile downstream from the bridge, in the one stretch of bank the flood had not yet claimed and the artillery had not yet bothered to claim either. Getting to him meant walking the plank road past the tannery ruins, which meant holding your breath against what the tannery had become, which meant arriving at any negotiation already having lost an argument with your own lungs. I considered this a fair omen. Negotiations go better when both parties arrive humbled.

He was mending a net when I came down the bank, and he did not look up. This was a performance. I had watched him mend that same net for three days running, and I had begun to suspect it was the most repaired net in Europe.

"Monsieur Ferreol," I said.

"Matron." He pulled a knot tight with his teeth. "Come about the weather, have you? Everyone does lately."

"I've come about the marsh road."

The teeth stopped on the twine. Not long — a heartbeat, maybe less — but Odilon Ferreol had spent forty years reading the river, and a man who reads water for a living does not miss the way a body goes still.

"Don't know what you mean," he said. "Marsh road."

"There's no such thing officially. Nothing official has ever drowned anyone, so it stands to reason nothing official exists." I sat down on an overturned dory, because my legs were done pretending and because sitting down in the middle of a man's lie is a good way to make him want to fix it. "But I've been treating a boy from the 14th who took shrapnel at the weir, and he talks when the morphine thins. He talks about a causeway. Stone under the mud, old as the mill, wide enough for two abreast, dry at low water. He says the bargemen used it before the war, in the years the frost came early and the locks jammed. He says you know it best."

Odilon went back to his net. The net, I noticed, needed no further help.

"Boy's dreaming," he said. "Morphine makes prophets of us all."

"Perhaps. Then again, perhaps not." I let the river fill the silence for a while. It was doing its patient work below us, eating the bank a fistful at a time, and I thought: there is a creature that never hurries and always wins. "Monsieur Ferreol, I have two hundred people in this town whose names exist nowhere but in my book. When the thaw comes, the river will take the low quarter, and it will take them, and afterward some captain will write *regrettable losses* in a report and go to lunch. I would like to move them across the marsh before the water rises. I'm told you're the only man who can lead them."

"And what," he said, still to the net, "would I want with two hundred people?"

"Nothing. That's rather the point. You'd want payment."

"Ah." He set down the net at last and looked at me. He had a face like something the river had worked on for decades — all the soft stone gone, only grain and seam left. "Payment. Now we're talking like Christians. What are you offering, Matron? I don't take francs. Francs will be wallpaper by spring."

"I have a watch that belonged to my mother."

"Watch stops when the thaw stops, if it lasts that long."

"A lantern."

"I have four."

"My medical kit—" I stopped myself, because even as I said it I heard how wrong it was, and Odilon saw me hear it, and something almost kind moved through his face like a fish under ice.

"No," he said. "You don't trade away the thing that keeps them alive. First lesson of the river: never throw out the bailer to lighten the boat." He spat thoughtfully over the side. "What I want, Matron, is the book."

I felt the word land before I understood it. "The book."

"Your ledger. The famous one." He shrugged at my face, whatever it was doing. "Don't look scandalized. Half the town knows you keep one. The other half assumes and hopes. Names, dates, units, what happened to who and by whose order — every person the officers struck off their pretty lists. Two hundred souls, you say. Two hundred reasons somebody very far from this river wants that book to drown with everything else."

"Why would you want it?"

"Insurance." He picked up a coil of rope and began coiling it badly, which told me his hands wanted occupation his mind wasn't offering. "You think I run a marsh road out of charity? The day I move your two hundred across that causeway, I stop being a bargeman and start being a smuggler of military significance. To one army or both. Somebody will come asking questions with a bayonet attached. And when they do, I'll need something to put on the table that makes them go away quiet. A book full of names is worth more than gold right now, Matron. Gold can be taken. Names *accuse*."

"You'd hold my ledger hostage to buy your own silence."

"I'd hold it hostage to buy everyone's silence, including yours and mine and those two hundred people's. There's a difference, though I grant it's thin." He coiled the last of the rope and set it down with exaggerated care. "Think of it this way. Right now the book exists in one head — yours. If you die, it dies, and nobody was ever saved by a dead woman's memory. If it lives with me, and you live, then after the war it becomes evidence. Testimony. A reckoning. Somebody will want it then, and they'll pay in safety, which is the only currency I'm short of."

"And until then?"

"Until then it sits in oilcloth under the floorboards of a barge that officially doesn't exist, on a road that officially doesn't exist, being guarded by a man who officially doesn't exist." He smiled, and the smile had cost him something; I could see the seams where it had been cut. "You keep your copy of the names in your head. I keep the proof in my hull. Between us, the truth survives the winter."

* * *

I walked back along the plank road without deciding anything, which is a kind of decision, and a bad one.

Here is what I knew, walking. I knew that the ledger was the only true thing I had made in six years of war. Everything else I had done — the dressings, the morphine, the hands held — had been true in the moment and then gone, like warmth, like breath on glass. But the ledger stayed. Every name I had entered while some staff officer two miles back wrote *effective strength reduced*, every name I had coaxed out of dying men through cracked lips and fear of reprisal, every civilian name the manifests had swallowed — that was a building I had built, stone by stone, against the official architecture of forgetting. Surrendering it to Odilon felt like handing over the deed to the only house I owned.

Here is what else I knew. A ledger in a locked drawer saves no one. Two hundred people do not fit between its covers; they fit on a causeway, and the causeway fits under the water in approximately eleven days' time, according to the engineers, who had developed the habit of being right in ways I despised.

Sister Marguerite found me at the ward door, scrubbing my hands in the basin though they were clean. She had a gift for appearing exactly when a woman was about to make a fool of herself, and standing by silently while she did it anyway.

"You've seen him," she said. "The bargeman."

"How—"

"The whole town has seen him. He mends that net like a priest says mass. Everyone waits to see who kneels." She dried her own hands on her apron, unhurried. "He asked you for the book."

I turned around. "Does everyone know about the book?"

"Everyone knows about the book. Nobody knows what's in it except you. That's why it's worth anything." She looked at me with those grey eyes that had seen, I suspected, considerably more choosing than mine had. "You're going to give it to him."

"I haven't decided."

"You decided at the tannery. You're just grieving on the walk home." She said it without cruelty, which is the worst way to say a true thing. "Theo. Listen to me. I have given things away before — things that mattered, things I loved — and I will tell you the arithmetic that got me through it, since you're fond of arithmetic. A thing kept is a thing kept. A thing traded is a thing transformed. You are not losing the ledger. You are converting it."

"Into what?"

"Into two hundred walks along a causeway." She took the towel from me, folded it, hung it on its nail — Marguerite's way of ending conversations, folding the world back into order. "Besides. You told me once you measure everything by what can be carried. Ask yourself what the book weighs, and what it carries."

Then she was gone into the ward, and I stood at the basin hating her a little, the way you hate anyone who solves a problem you wanted to go on suffering over, because suffering over it felt like loyalty.

* * *

I went back at dusk, because dusk is when bargains should be made — the light too low to read faces properly, the day's regrets already decanting themselves into the river, everyone inclined to settle.

Odilon was cooking something over a spirit stove, and the smell was honest: onions, and beneath the onions, the particular poverty-smell of army beans improved by theft. He ladled a second bowl without asking. We ate sitting on the dory with the bowl warming our hands, two professionals waiting for the other to name terms first, and neither of us naming any, because we both knew the terms already.

"One condition," I said finally.

"Conditions. From the seller now."

"It stays whole. Every page. If your floorboard leaks or your oilcloth fails or you decide halfway through the winter that some captain's gold buys more silence than my names do, I will come back from whatever grave the war puts me in and haunt your hull until it rots."

"That's three conditions."

"It's one condition stated three times. I'm a nurse. Repetition is how we're trained."

He laughed — a short bark, surprised out of him, the laugh of a man who hadn't expected to find the transaction funny and resented it slightly. He finished his beans, wiped the bowl with bread, and set it down.

"Agreed," he said. "Whole. Every page. And one condition of mine, since we're trading conditions like ration cards." He looked at me, and for a moment the river-face opened and I saw the man underneath, and the man underneath was tired in a way that had nothing to do with age. "When it's over — after the thaw, after whoever wins wins — you take the book back. You see it printed or buried or nailed to a church door, whichever it deserves. I don't want to be its keeper forever, Matron. Keeping things is heavier than carrying them. I've carried boats my whole life. This would be the first thing I couldn't put down."

"All right," I said.

"All right?"

"All right. Afterward, it comes back to me, and I finish it. Whatever finishing means."

We shook on it, his hand rough as mooring line, and then he produced the oilcloth and a length of waxed cord from under the tiller bench, because he had known — of course he had known, he had probably known before I climbed the bank at the tannery — that I would come back and agree. I went to the satchel I had carried through shellfire and inspection and one burning ward, and I took out the ledger, and I held it.

It weighed almost nothing. It always had. A half pound of paper and ink, lighter than Amara asleep, lighter than a canteen, lighter than the smallest thing I had ever failed to save. And it had cost more than everything else I owned stacked together, and it was going to buy — I counted it out the way Marguerite had taught me to count — two hundred walks, one causeway, one night, one chance.

"Names float, you know," Odilon said quietly, watching me hold it. He said it oddly, as if quoting someone. "Old river saying. My grandmother had it. Paper drowns. Names float."

"Where did you hear that?"

"Your girl. Down at the water last week, lighting her lantern." He took the ledger from my hands with unexpected gentleness, wrapped it in oilcloth with the economy of a man who had wrapped many things against water, tied the cord, and stowed it somewhere beneath his feet that I deliberately did not watch. "She counts when she's frightened, that one. Counts backwards. Where'd she learn that?"

"She taught herself," I said. "She was counting when I found her. She hasn't stopped."

"Hm." Odilon looked out at the dark coming down over the water, and his voice changed — flattened, professional, the bargain sealed and the man put away. "Low water holds four nights from now, maybe five, depending on the moon and the mood of the river. One night's crossing, no more. Bring your two hundred ready to walk in the dark, quiet as thieves, and bring no lanterns until I say. And Matron—" he paused, hand on the tiller, not looking at me, "—tell no one the date. Not the sister. Not the soldiers. No one. A secret held by three people dies unless two of them are dead, and I'd rather it didn't come to that."

"I understand."

"Do you?" For the first time he looked genuinely curious. "Because I've watched you these weeks, Matron. You tell that child everything. You tell her truths other people hide behind chapel doors. It's why they trust you. It's also why, come the crossing night, you'll have the hardest job on that causeway — harder than mine. I only have to know the way. You have to keep a family in the dark."

I walked home under a sky going the colour of unpolished tin, and I did the arithmetic one more time, because arithmetic is a comfort even when it doesn't comfort. One ledger, surrendered. Two hundred lives, provisionally purchased. Four nights, possibly five. One secret that must not reach the ears of a nine-year-old girl who heard everything, forgot nothing, and had once asked me, in perfect seriousness, whether names float.

The ward was warm when I came in. Amara was asleep in her corner with the copybook open on her chest, and Sister Marguerite was trimming the lamp wicks, and everything was exactly as I had left it, which is the moment, I have learned, when you should count your spoons.

Amara's eyes opened the instant my boot creaked — they always did, a habit the war had given her along with the counting — and she looked at me in the lamplight, and then at my empty satchel, and I watched her notice the absence of the weight I had carried every day since summer.

"Where's the book?" she asked.
