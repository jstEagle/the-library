## 21. What Amara Chose

I learned arithmetic twice. The first time was at the convent school in Roubaix, before the shells came, from a nun who smelled of chalk and candle wax and who rapped my knuckles when I counted on my fingers. The second time I learned it here, in the dressing station, counting backwards from one hundred while the world did frightening things, and nobody ever rapped my knuckles for it. Theo says my counting is a gift. Sister Marguerite says it is a prayer with numbers in it. I say it is the only thing I brought with me from before, along with one shoe that no longer fits, and so I intend to keep it.

Here is what I know about counting: you can count anything. Seconds. Steps. Lanterns on water. The buttons on Samson's coat, which number eleven, one missing, and which he will not replace because he says the coat has earned its gaps.

And here is what I know about eavesdropping, which I learned this week, crouched in the linen cupboard behind the splint crates with my knees against my chest and my breath folded up small like laundry: grown-ups only say the true things when they believe no child is listening.

* * *

It began, as most of our troubles do, with Odilon the bargeman.

Odilon has hands like tree roots and a face like a door that has been shut a long time and is not entirely certain it wants to open. He comes to the station to have his shoulder seen to — he fell between barge and quay, or was pushed, depending on which story he tells and how much he has drunk — and he always brings something. Once a tin of sardines. Once an apple so old it had gone soft as a pillow. Once nothing, which he apologized for with his whole body, hat in both hands, until Marguerite laughed and told him to sit down before he wore a hole in the floor.

That evening Theo took him into the little room off the ward where they keep the forms nobody fills out anymore, because the men who would fill them out are mostly dead or gone. The room has thin walls. This is not an accident of construction; this whole town is held together with thin walls and stubbornness. I could hear every word, and I am not ashamed to say I moved closer to hear better, because the war taught me early that information is the only ration nobody cuts.

"Collateral," Odilon said. "You want me to run two hundred souls through a marsh causeway that'll hold maybe one night, low water, no lights, and if either army catches wind of it I'm shot by somebody — I don't care which uniform, they're equally punctual about shooting. You want that, you give me proof. That book of yours. Names, dates, what the officers left off. It's worth more than money, that book. Worth silence. Silence is the only currency still trading fair."

"The ledger stays with me," Theo said. Her voice does something particular when she is frightened and refusing to be — it goes very flat, like a road after a flood, smooth and hard and no good for walking. "Names aren't collateral. They're people."

"They were people," Odilon said, not cruelly, which somehow made it worse. "Now they're paper. Paper floats, Madame Oyelaran. People sink. Choose which you keep."

Then there was a long quiet, the kind that means someone is doing sums they don't want to do, and I sat in the dark of the linen cupboard among the bandages and counted backwards from one hundred and got to sixty-one before Theo said, in that flat road voice:

"I'll bring you the book the night we cross. Not before. If you take it and don't come back for us, those names die with you."

"If I don't come back," Odilon said, "we all die together, and it'll be very democratic."

He laughed at his own joke the way he does, alone, one bark, and then there were footsteps and I flattened myself down among the splints and heard the outer door, and then — this is the part I keep returning to, the way a tongue returns to a loose tooth — I heard Theo alone in the form room, crying. Not loudly. She cried the way she does everything, efficiently, in the smallest possible space, and then stopped, and washed her face in the basin, and went back out to the ward where the lanterns needed filling.

I stayed in the cupboard a long time after that, thinking about paper floating and people sinking.

* * *

The second thing I overheard was worse, and I want to be honest about the fact that I went looking for it. Eavesdropping is like stealing bread: once you've done it successfully, you find yourself hungry in a new way.

Samson sleeps in the stable loft with the rest of the labor battalion, but he comes to the kitchen most evenings, and he and Theo wash dishes together without talking, which is their whole relationship in a single domestic scene — six years of silence, and now silence shared instead of endured. That night they talked. I was supposed to be asleep on the cot behind the stove, and I was, in the way that I was also listening with my entire spine.

"The battalion's listed to stay," Samson said. "Whole company. We're 'essential labor.' Do you know what essential means in army language, Théo? It means the thing is more useful broken in place than saved."

"There are berths—"

"There's one berth. On the last barge. Vasseur's man told me this afternoon, thinking I'd be grateful for the arithmetic." A plate clinked down. "One berth, and it isn't mine, and we both know whose it should be."

"It should be yours," Theo said.

"I can't swim," Samson said, conversationally, terribly. "Never learned. And you can, and she can't, and I'm bigger than both of you, and none of it matters because there's one berth and I've already decided—"

"You don't get to decide. You've been gone six years. You don't get to come back and start deciding things."

The silence after that had six years inside it. I pulled the blanket over my head. I counted to one hundred, forwards this time, because backwards felt too much like going down stairs in the dark.

"I took the blame," Samson said finally, very quietly, "for the morphine. In Lille. When we were young and you were going to be thrown out of your training and I said it was me, and let them believe it, and left, and never wrote. I did that so you could become whatever you were going to become. So don't tell me what I don't get to decide, little sister. Deciding things for you is the only work I've ever finished."

I didn't hear her answer. Maybe there wasn't one. Some silences are answers wearing coats.

* * *

So then I knew everything, lying in the dark behind the stove with my one good shoe by the door and my bad arithmetic heart doing its sums. Here is what the sums looked like.

One berth. Three of us who are family — me, Theo, Samson — except family isn't three, family is also Marguerite who won't leave, and Odilon who wants a book, and the laundress who is pregnant, and the boy with the burned hand who follows Samson around like a small dog. But the sums that night were just three. One berth. Two hundred names on a marsh road that holds for one night. One ledger that must be traded away to buy the crossing, which means Theo gives up the thing she built out of grief, and I knew — because I had heard her crying in the form room — what that costs.

And under all of it, the sum nobody was saying out loud: someone has to stay behind, or someone has to go without, and each of the three of us was quietly arranging to be the one who loses.

Grown-ups think children don't understand sacrifice. Grown-ups think this because children cry about shoes and jam. But children understand sacrifice better than anyone, because everything in a child's life is arranged by other people's sacrifices; we live inside them the way fish live in water, and mostly we don't notice, and when we do notice, it changes us.

Theo gave up her brother for six years because he took her guilt. Samson gave up his whole life for the same reason. Marguerite gave up — I don't know all of it, but I've seen her hands shake when the wards empty, and I've heard her say *don't become me* to Theo through a door. Everyone I love is made entirely of things they've given away. And I lay there realizing that I was the only one in the equation who had never given up anything, who had arrived with one shoe and a talent for counting and been *kept*, kept like something valuable, fed and sheltered and taught letters out of a forbidden book, and that being kept is lovely but it is also, if you have any honor at all, a debt.

I am nine years old. I want to state my qualifications clearly, because grown-ups reading this later might doubt them. I can count to any number you like, forwards or backwards, in French and half in English since Theo's been teaching me. I can carry a lantern steady — steadier than some of the orderlies, Marguerite says so. I know the names in the ledger; Theo teaches me letters with them, five a week, and I have memorized the ones I couldn't yet read, the way you memorize a psalm, sound and shape together. I know where every crack in the marsh path starts, because Odilon talks when his shoulder hurts and I fetch him the liniment. I am small. I am quiet. I can fold myself into linen cupboards, which I have proved.

And I cannot swim, which sounds like an argument against my plan and is actually the argument for it, though I'll come to that.

* * *

I chose a morning for it. Mornings are best; Theo hasn't yet put on her flat road voice, and Samson's hands smell of pitch and he is gentler before noon. The three of us were in the kitchen. Theo was rewrapping bandages that didn't need rewrapping, which she does when her mind is working. Samson was fixing the stove latch, which also didn't need fixing, which he does when his mind is working. Nobody was looking at anybody. This is how families talk.

"The berth," I said. "On the barge."

Theo's hands stopped. Samson's wrench stopped. I had rehearsed this seventeen times against the wall of the privy, and in rehearsal my voice hadn't shaken, but rehearsal is a liar.

"Samson takes it," I said. "Theo stays to lead the crossing, because Odilon will only follow her and the names are hers even if she trades the book, they'll still be in her head, she knows them all, I've heard her say them in her sleep. And I stay too."

"No," Theo said. Immediately. The word came out of her the way a shell leaves a gun.

"Listen," I said, and my voice shook now, so I leaned on my counting, which is what it is for. "You always say a thing is only as heavy as what you can carry. Well. Samson can't swim, so if the causeway floods he drowns anyway, and the berth would drown with him — no. Wait. That's not why." I had rehearsed the practical reasons and found, standing there, that they weren't the truth. I started over. "Because if I go and he stays, he'll have given up his berth for a child he's known five months, and it'll be the morphine again, him carrying blame for other people forever, and you'll owe him another six years of silence. And if you go and I stay, you'll cross that marsh with two hundred strangers and spend the rest of your life wondering, and you'll become Marguerite, choosing by arithmetic until your hands shake. But if Samson goes, and you and I both stay — then nobody owes anybody. He gets out clean. That matters. Don't you see? Somebody has to get out clean, or the whole story is just people paying each other back forever."

The kitchen was very quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a mule complained about the rain, which had opinions of its own about the weather.

"And me staying," I said, softer, "isn't brave. I want that written down somewhere. It isn't brave. It's just — I've been kept. Since summer. Fed, and taught, and lit-for. Every night you light the lantern and I count and that's my whole inheritance. And I can't carry a stretcher, and I can't nurse, and I can't trade a book for a bargeman's silence because nobody buys silence from a child. The only thing I have that's worth anything is that I'm small and I don't take much room and I can wait. Waiting I can do beautifully. Ask anyone. I've been counting since June."

Theo crossed the kitchen and knelt down in front of me, which put her face level with mine, and her eyes were wet and furious at once, which is a complicated expression to receive from someone you love.

"You are not staying behind," she said. "Do you hear me? You are nine years old. You are not a soldier, you are not a debt, and you are not — Amara, look at me — you are not something I was keeping out of charity. There is no version of the arithmetic where I put you on a marsh road and board a boat without you. None. I'd rather drown."

"That's exactly what I said about you," I told her. "And about Samson. And that's the problem, Theo. All three of us would rather drown than lose the others, and there's one berth, and the water doesn't care how much we love each other. Love isn't an answer to this sum. I've done it every way. I've been doing it since the linen cupboard. There's no clean answer. There's only who can bear what."

Samson set down the wrench. He came and stood over both of us, huge in the small kitchen, smelling of pitch and cold air, and for a moment I thought he was going to argue, and I braced myself with my counting ready. Instead he crouched too, so that we made a strange triangle, and he said:

"When did you hear all this?"

"The linen cupboard. The stove cot. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry," he said. "Be exact. What did Odilon ask for?"

"The ledger. As collateral. The night of the crossing."

"What else?"

"That the causeway holds one night at low water. That either army would shoot him. That—" I stopped. That paper floats and people sink. I said it anyway, because he'd asked for exact, and exactness was the only payment I had. "That people sink."

Samson looked at Theo over my head. Something passed between them that had no words, the way it always does, six years of silence compressed into a glance — and I understood suddenly, the way you understand a trick explained, that they were both already past my plan. Not dismissing it. Using it. My offer had cracked something open, and behind the crack was a third option neither of them would have reached alone, and children are useful precisely for this: we say the unsayable, and then the grown-ups build on the ruin.

"We'll discuss it," Theo said, which in her language means *we will fight about this privately*, and she kissed the top of my head and stood, and her knees cracked, and she went out into the ward as if bandages needed her urgently.

Samson lingered. He put his hand on my hair, awkwardly, the way he does everything affectionate, like a bear trying to thread a needle.

"You argued well," he said. "For a child offering herself up."

"I wasn't offering myself up. I was offering myself *forward*. There's a difference. Up is for saints. Forward is just walking."

He laughed — one bark, alone, Odilon's laugh, and I wondered briefly if all adults kept the same one — and followed her out.

* * *

I did not win. I want to record that honestly, because whatever gets written afterward tends to make children into martyrs, and I was not a martyr; I was a girl with a plan and a wet nose who lost the argument by suppertime. Theo and Samson fought behind the form-room door in rapid low French, and Marguerite stood near me in the ward pretending to sort gauze, and after a long time the door opened and Theo came out looking like a woman who has walked through her own burning house and salvaged exactly one thing.

"Nobody is staying behind," she announced to the kitchen, to me, to the mule outside, possibly to God. "Not you, not Samson, not me. We cross together or not at all. I'll find a way to make the numbers work. Odilon wants a book — fine. He'll get a book. He never specified it had to be mine."

She said that last part looking directly at me, and I didn't understand it then, and Samson didn't either, but Sister Marguerite did — I saw her stop sorting gauze — and within the hour she and Theo were huddled over the form-room table with fresh paper and the hymnal open between them, writing fast, and Marguerite saying in her ruined voice, "Record me first, in whatever book comes next," and Theo refusing, arguing, and losing.

But that night, when the lantern was lit and I counted and Theo tucked the blanket to my chin as always, she paused at the door and looked back at me a long moment, the way you look at something you nearly lost without knowing it was losable.

"You said love isn't an answer to the sum," she said.

"It isn't."

"Mm." She turned the lantern wick low. "It isn't. But it's the reason you do the sum at all. Keep that somewhere. You'll need it in about four days."

Four days. Low water. The causeway. I lay in the dark counting down from four days, which is ninety-six hours, which is five thousand seven hundred and sixty minutes, and I had gotten as far as five thousand seven hundred and twelve when the ground shivered — distant, westward, the front clearing its throat — and the lamp flames in the ward all bent sideways at once, and Theo's boots hit the floor in the next room before I had finished the number.
