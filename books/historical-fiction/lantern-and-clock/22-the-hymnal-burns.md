## 22. The Hymnal Burns

I have a theory — untested, thank God — that grief arranges itself by weight. That when catastrophe comes, it sorts our losses for us like a customs officer: this you may keep, this you may not, and here is the receipt.

The theory did not survive the evening of the shelling. But I will tell it to you now anyway, because I believed it right up until the moment the ward caught fire, and there is something almost tender about a belief in its last minutes of life. It deserves an obituary as much as anything else that burned that night.

Here is what I believed: that if my hands were full of the right things — the ledger against my ribs, Amara's wrist in my grip, Samson somewhere within shouting distance — then everything else was ballast. Instruments, linens, the surgical kit Marguerite and I had assembled from three armies' worth of scrounging and one bishop's misplaced charity. Things could be replaced. Names could not. A man who dies without his name recorded dies twice, and I had made a small religion of preventing second deaths.

The ledger weighed perhaps four hundred grams. My kit weighed eleven kilos. I had done the arithmetic a hundred times, in the way one does arithmetic that flatters one's virtue.

Then the shells came, and I learned what four hundred grams of paper weighs when it is the only thing left of two hundred people.

* * *

It began, as these things do, with an ordinary hour. That is the cruelty of artillery — it never announces itself during anything dramatic. It waits until you are doing something humiliatingly domestic. I was mending a sock. Amara's sock, which she had worn through at the heel from pacing the ward's length while reciting her alphabet of names under her breath — *Alphonse, Béatrice, Célestin* — like a rosary with letters instead of beads. She counted everything now: seconds between lamps being trimmed, steps to the latrine, the stitches I put into her heel. Counting was how she held the world still, and God knows the world needed holding.

Marguerite was across the room doing the accounts, which meant arguing with a requisition form. Samson had gone down to the barges with Odilon's men. The ward held nineteen patients, most of them sleeping, one of them dying quietly in the corner bed with the decency to do it slowly, so we wouldn't be rude.

The clock in the tower had been dead for days, so I no longer knew what hour it was. I knew it was late because the lamp oil in our good lantern was low and the oil in the bad lantern was lower, and because Amara's counting had slowed to the rhythm of near-sleep.

The first shell fell close enough to make the lamp flame lean sideways, like a man listening.

"Another one for the engineers," Marguerite said, without looking up. She had developed the habit of narrating bombardments in the tone of a woman complaining about weather, which I understood completely and had adopted myself, because the alternative tones were screaming or prayer, and I had used up my ration of both sometime in 1915.

The second shell was not for the engineers.

I knew before the sound arrived. There is a quality to certain incoming rounds — a fatness to the whistle, a kind of satisfied descending note — that the body understands before the mind consents to. I was moving before I had decided anything, scooping Amara off her cot, and she woke the way children do in bombardments: instantly, completely, already counting.

"Four seconds," she said into my collar. "That one was four."

"That one was close," I said, "so let's find somewhere with better acoustics," which is nonsense, but nonsense delivered confidently is a sedative, and I have prescribed worse.

The third shell took the roof off the east wing.

I will not give you a symphony of it. Writers love to orchestrate shellfire — the rolling percussion, the singing steel — and all of that is true and all of it is beside the point. The point is that it is loud in a way that erases thought, and then it is quiet in a way that erases everything else, and in the space between those two silences people are either alive or they are not, and you find out which by calling names into the dust.

I called names. It is the only professional habit I refuse to break.

"Marguerite!"

"Here." A cough, then her shape resolving out of the plaster fog, dragging the requisition form, of all things, which tells you everything about Marguerite. "The east beds—"

"I know."

Amara had my sleeve in both fists and was counting under her breath, fast, a bead-chain of numbers, and I pressed her face into my shoulder so she wouldn't see the east wing, because the east wing was on fire, and the east wing was where four of my nineteen patients slept, including the man in the corner bed who had been dying with such courtesy.

We got them out. Not all of them. I will write that sentence plainly because it deserves plainness: we got fifteen out, and four we did not, and one of the four was Sergeant Delacroix of the engineers, who had spent the previous afternoon showing Amara how to fold a paper boat, and whose name was therefore in the ledger twice — once as a casualty, once in the margin where I kept the small mercies. I struck neither line. Some entries should stand even when the facts move.

* * *

The fire was the real enemy, as fire always is. Shells kill in their instant; fire takes its time, and time is the thing I had been hoarding.

The ward burned the way old timber buildings burn, which is to say enthusiastically, with a crackle almost festive, sparks rising like a celebration nobody had ordered. And in the middle of it sat everything we had: the instruments, the morphine, the bandages, the carbolic, the kit I had carried through three dressing stations and one retreat and had once traded a silver thimble to keep from a quartermaster with wandering hands. Eleven kilos of the difference between surgery and butchery, sitting in its canvas case six meters from a wall of flame.

I stood in the smoke and did the arithmetic again, and for the first time in my life the arithmetic lied to me.

Because here is what actually happened: I went in after the kit. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the whole point of the ledger and I would be a fraud to keep it only on paper. The ledger was in my apron pocket — it went everywhere with me, sewn there in a fit of paranoia after the inspection — so there was no choice to make about the ledger. The choice was the kit. Eleven kilos against the heat, against the falling beam, against the simple physics of a woman's lungs in burning air.

I got two steps with it. Then the beam came down across the doorway I'd entered by, and the smoke turned from grey to black, and somewhere outside a child's voice was counting seconds with a waver in it, and I understood — the way you understand things in fires, quickly and permanently — that I was going to die in this building for eleven kilos of steel and gauze, and that the counting voice would go on counting long past any number that meant anything.

I dropped the kit.

I want that in the record too. Not the heroism of going in — anyone can be brave for two steps; courage is cheap at retail — but the dropping. The moment when the theory of weights failed and I turned around empty-handed, choking, eyes streaming, and felt the loss of those instruments open in my chest like a wound that has not yet decided whether to bleed.

Outside, the cold hit like a slap. Amara collided with me, counting, always counting, and I held her and coughed and watched eleven kilos of mercy become light and ash above the town.

"The kit," Marguerite said. She had seen. Sisters see.

"Gone."

"And the book?"

My hand went to my ribs. Four hundred grams, warm from my own body, dry, intact. "The book is fine."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I saw her decide not to say whatever it was she was thinking — probably something about a woman who mourns stationery in a season of corpses. Instead she said, "Then we've lost nothing we can't carry," which was her way of saying it, and I loved her for the grammar of it.

But she was wrong, and I knew she was wrong, and I didn't correct her, because correcting her would have required admitting what I had actually lost in there, and it wasn't the morphine.

* * *

Here is what I discovered at dawn, standing in the wet ash of the east wall with the ledger open on my knees:

Smoke does not read. Water does not respect ink.

During the worst of it, when we'd passed buckets and beaten the embers back from the west wing with blankets — blankets we needed, off patients who needed them more — the hose water had found my kit bag where I'd finally abandoned it, and the kit bag had been sitting on top of a crate, and on the crate, forgotten in the chaos of the evacuation, had been the duplicate pages. The carbon copies. The names I'd begun copying weeks ago, after the inspection frightened me, after Marguerite warned me that accuracy was dangerous and I'd decided the answer to danger was redundancy, the way you'd answer a flood with a second boat.

Half the names. Alphonse through maybe Jules, depending on where you drew the line. The letters I had taught Amara were soaked into grey pulp, the ink lifted and bloomed into clouds that might have been anything, might have been every name at once, might have been none. The paper hadn't burned. Burning would have been clean, at least — a clean loss, a martyrdom. This was drowning. The names hadn't died; they'd dissolved, gone vague, gone illegible, gone into that terrible territory where a record exists but testifies to nothing.

I sat in the ash with the ruined pages and I did not cry, which surprised me, because I am a crier by constitution and have wept over lesser documents. What I did instead was stranger: I read them. Held each page up to the grey light and tilted it, hunting through the blooms of ink for the ghosts of letters, reconstructing — *A-l-p… yes, Alphonse Berthier, gunner, left thigh, admitted the fourth* — aloud, checking each recovered name against memory the way you count change you're sure has been shorted.

Amara crouched beside me in the ash, still in her burnt-smelling nightdress, and watched my finger travel.

"You know them," she said. It wasn't a question. "You don't need the paper."

"Everyone needs the paper eventually. Memory is a liar with good manners. It smiles and agrees and then leaves out the parts that inconvenience it."

"But you know them *now*."

I looked at her. Nine years old, ash in her hair like early frost, and she had put her finger on the thing in under a minute that I'd been circling for an hour: the loss was real but it was not yet total. The paper had drowned. I had not.

"Say them with me," she said, and patted the wet page. "I'll remember too. Two people forgetting is harder than one."

So we did that, in the ash, in the cold, while the relief column's smoke rose on the horizon and Marguerite triaged the living and Samson's silhouette appeared at the end of the street, walking back from the barges with something wrong in his stride, some news folded up inside him the way he folded everything.

*Alphonse Berthier. Béatrice Nkoulou. Célestin Marchand.* Amara repeated each name after me exactly once, the way she memorized, her lips moving, filing them into whatever tireless drawer she kept behind her eyes. By the time we reached the J's she was saying them with me, unprompted, and by the K's she was ahead of me, and I stopped reading the drowned pages altogether and just recited, and she just received, and the two of us sat in the ruin of my careful work conducting the transfer of two hundred souls from paper to breath.

Somewhere in the middle of it I understood that this was why I'd dropped the kit. Not because of the beam, not because of her counting voice — those were the mechanics. The reason underneath: the kit could save the bodies we still had, and the ledger was the bodies we'd already lost, and I could not bear to lose the same people twice in one night. Everything I had done since summer — the omissions, the risks, the hymnal, the whole crooked church of it — was built on a refusal to let the officers' arithmetic be the last word on any human being. Let the fire take the morphine. The morphine never promised anyone they'd be remembered.

Though I'll confess the morphine's absence made itself known by morning, and rather rudely.

* * *

By full light we had established ourselves in the cellar of the church — stone, low, damp, indestructible, the architectural equivalent of Marguerite. Patients on pallets along the walls. Our entire pharmacopoeia reduced to what could be carried in a bread basket: a handful of dressings, a quarter bottle of carbolic, iodine enough for perhaps thirty wounds, and no morphine at all, which meant the coming days would be conducted at full volume.

Samson found us there. He came down the cellar steps with ash on him from a different fire — the barge yard had taken a shell too — and he was carrying something wrapped in oilcloth, and his face was doing the thing his face did when he'd decided to be strong about information, which was to say it looked like a door painted on a wall.

"You're alive," he said, as if confirming a rumor.

"Barely, loudly, and against advice. You?"

He set the oilcloth bundle down. Inside was a lantern — Odilon's spare, brass, dented — and a coil of rope, and a length of candle. Salvage, offered the way men offer salvage: wordlessly, as if the objects could speak their own comfort.

"The barges?" I asked.

"One burned. Odilon's is scorched but sound. Theo—" He stopped. Looked at Amara, who was pretending magnificently not to listen while memorizing every word. "Walk with me."

Which is how I learned, in the churchyard among the wet graves, that the shell that hit the barge yard had also hit the manifest office, and that the final barge's departure had been moved up — not by weeks, by days — and that the berth list, the forty names, had been posted and reposted and argued over, and that Odilon had come to him at dawn with the marsh road on his mind and a price in his mouth.

"He wants what?" I said.

"You know what he wants. He told me to tell you himself, but I said I'd soften it, and then I realized there's no softening a man demanding your book as collateral." Samson's jaw worked. "Low water comes in a few nights. One night only, he says. And he won't take us onto that causeway unless he's holding proof that keeps both armies polite afterward. Your ledger, Theo. The real one. In his hands until we're clear."

I stood in the churchyard with ash on my hem and half my names dissolved to cloud-shapes on drowned paper, and I thought about the four hundred grams against my ribs, and the eleven kilos I'd already given to the fire, and the strange new lightness that was turning out to feel nothing like freedom.

"He can have copies," I said. "He can have my testimony sworn before witnesses. He cannot have the only complete record of two hundred people, Samson. That book is the last place most of them exist."

"I told him you'd say that."

"What did he say?"

Samson looked out past the wall, toward the river, toward the bridge with its chains waiting in some warehouse, and when he answered, his voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet before hard truths, back when we were children and he was still the brother who told me things.

"He said the fire already settled the question. He heard the ward burned. He says come at moonrise and bring whatever's left of your book — and Theo, he says if what's left isn't enough to buy silence, then the marsh road stays shut, and we all learn to swim."
