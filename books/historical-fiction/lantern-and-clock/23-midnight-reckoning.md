## 23. Midnight Reckoning

The last berth fit on one page. That was the obscenity of it — a human salvation you could fold into a pocket. Forty names for the barge, thirty-nine already written in the commandant's neat hand, and one line left blank at the bottom like an open mouth.

We sat around it the way people sit around a body.

Sister Marguerite's ward had emptied enough by midnight to give us a corner: two crates upended for stools, a candle stub burning in a condensed-milk tin, and between us the manifest itself, which Samson had flattened with his palm as though it might behave better under pressure. Amara sat cross-legged on a blanket with her knees drawn up to her chin, watching the paper the way she watched the water rise — counting under her breath, backwards from a hundred, her private liturgy.

"Start again," I said, because we had been going in circles and circles are only grief wearing sensible shoes.

"Theo goes," Samson said. "That's the whole argument. Everything else is decoration."

"And you've made this decision when, exactly? On the march north? Before or after you decided my opinion was irrelevant?"

"After. It was quicker."

He had his elbows on his knees and his hands hanging loose between them — those enormous hands, scarred at the knuckles from six years of work I hadn't witnessed. He'd grown a beard that aged him past thirty-four into some region of indefinite weathered middle age, but when he set his jaw like that he was twelve years old again, refusing to cry over a dead dog, refusing to admit the dog had even died. *She ran off,* he'd said. *Dogs run off.* He'd carried that lie so long I think it became true somewhere inside him.

"I'm senior nurse," I said. "I have a guaranteed berth. Which means the berth is mine to give away, not mine to keep — do you see? The argument works against you, brother. If anyone has standing here, it's me."

"That's lawyer talk."

"That's *arithmetic*." I heard Colonel Vasseur in my own mouth and hated it. "There are four of us who matter in this room. There is one berth. Someone must be left standing on this bank regardless, because someone has to lead two hundred omitted souls overland if the marsh road proves real, and Odilon won't move without a name he trusts. It cannot be Amara. It cannot be you — your French is worse than hers. So it's me or Marguerite, and Marguerite has a ward full of men who'd follow her into a fire. The answer was decided before we sat down. We're just performing the funeral for our other options."

Amara stopped counting. "You're talking about me like I'm not here."

"We're talking about you *because* you're here," Samson said gently. "That's the point of all of it."

"Then I should say my part." She unfolded from herself with terrible ceremony, nine years old arranging herself into somebody older. "I counted the berths twice. Thirty-nine names and one empty. And I counted us, and there are three of us, and—" her voice did something young then, cracked and recovered— "and I know how subtraction works. I'm not stupid. Somebody stays and it might as well be—"

"No," Samson and I said together, and for a moment, over her head, our eyes met across the candle, and it was the first time in six years we had agreed on anything instantly and completely.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about estrangement: it doesn't end. It just gets suspended, the way the bridge was suspended over its river, held up by structures everyone pretended were permanent. When Samson walked into my dressing station in October with a labor battalion's mud on his boots, six years of silence came with him like a third soldier needing triage. And now, at midnight, with the blank line waiting, all six years sat down at the table too.

"You took the blame," I said. It wasn't a question anymore; he'd told me weeks ago, and still I kept returning to it like a tongue to a broken tooth. "The quinine. You stood in front of the matron and said you'd sold it, knowing what it would cost, and you let me believe — Samson, you let me believe it for *six years*—"

"What was I supposed to say? 'My sister stole it, but don't worry, she stole it for good reasons'? They'd have taken your posting, your references, everything. You'd have ended up—" he waved a hand at the ward, the war, the century— "well. Somewhere worse than this. There was no version where telling the truth helped anybody."

"There was a version where it helped *me*. I spent six years being ashamed of you. Do you understand what that does to a person? I built a whole sister out of that shame. I picked fights with your memory. I rehearsed arguments with you on long marches that you never got to defend yourself in."

"Won any of them?"

It was such a Samson thing to say — wry, deflecting, aimed at making me laugh so I'd stop aiming at him — and it nearly worked. I let the silence sit until the candle guttered and steadied.

"The point," I said, "is that you have spent your whole life paying debts that weren't yours, and you are doing it *right now*, sitting there with your speech prepared about how the berth belongs to Amara, how you'll stay behind and it's fine, how you've lived a plain life and she hasn't lived at all yet. I can see the speech. It's written on your face in your bad handwriting."

"My handwriting is excellent."

"Your handwriting looks like a spider drowned in ink."

Amara made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by the occasion, which was fair. The occasion deserved strangling.

Samson leaned back against the wall. In the tin-light his face went half-dark, and I thought, not for the first time, that we had the same jaw and had used it for entirely different purposes. His to clamp shut. Mine to open.

"Theo," he said quietly. "Listen to me. I've been carrying loads since I was sixteen. Sandbags, stretchers, timber, other men's guilt. I can carry staying behind. I genuinely can. But if they put that child on a barge alone — no family, no name on any roll but yours, sent across the water to strangers — she will learn what I learned at sixteen. That love is something that happens to you *before*. That afterward you get memories and a debt. I would rather drown."

"Don't say drown."

"I'd rather *anything*," he amended, hearing it himself, softening. "But you take my meaning."

"And I take yours and raise you," I said. "If I go, the ledger goes nowhere, because I am the ledger. Every omitted name lives in my skull, and the marsh road lives in my skull, and Odilon's terms live in my skull, and none of it survives a bayonet. You want to send the only map across the water and burn the territory."

"Marguerite knows the plan."

"Marguerite knows the shape of it. Not the causeway timings. Not which culvert holds and which is a rumor with a body count."

Amara raised her hand. Actually raised it, schoolroom style, as though we were a lesson.

"Yes?" I said.

"If nobody can decide," she said, "shouldn't the person who decides be the person whose berth it isn't? Because you two each want the others to go. Neither of you wants it for yourself. So whoever chooses is choosing *for* somebody, and—" she frowned, following her own logic into the dark— "and maybe the person who's chosen *for* doesn't get to complain later. That's the rule. Like when Sister lets me pick who carries the water. Whoever picks can't also drink first."

Samson looked at me over the candle flame. Something moved through his face — surprise, and beneath it, unwilling pride.

"When did you get smart?" he asked her.

"In June," she said. "Theo was teaching me fractions."

"She taught you fractions with what?"

"Biscuits." A pause. "I mostly remember the biscuits."

And there it was, absurdly, in a freezing ward at one in the morning with a blank line on a manifest between us: laughter. Small, guilty, quickly smothered, but real. Love does that — it keeps insisting on jokes in the worst rooms. I think it may be love's most stubborn habit.

* * *

By two o'clock the candle had burned down to a thumbnail of light and we had exhausted every arrangement and found each one rotten at the core.

Send Amara and Samson both, stay myself: clean on paper, except the manifest allowed only one civilian addition, and forging a second name meant Odilon's price — the ledger — paid for nothing, since two forged berths would hang him from whichever army caught him first.

Send Amara, keep Samson's labor battalion here by military necessity anyway: then why give the single berth away from myself at all? Because the alternative was a nine-year-old crossing alone, and neither of us could say that sentence out loud without our voices failing on the word *alone*.

Give my berth to Samson: he refused before I finished offering, and I saw why when I made him say it plainly — he believed, correctly, that I was trying to buy absolution for an old theft by giving away a thing that wasn't mine to price.

Every road circled back to the same village square: one line, three names, and the arithmetic refusing to come out kind.

"I used to think," Samson said eventually, to the ceiling, "that hard choices looked dramatic. Trumpets. A moment where you *know*. This feels like arguing about whose turn it is to fetch water."

"That's exactly what it feels like," I said. "Because that's what all the real ones feel like. Nobody blows a trumpet while you're deciding. They wait until after."

Amara had gone quiet again, her counting reduced to a whisper — *ninety-one, ninety, eighty-nine* — and I realized she wasn't calming herself. She was timing us. Measuring our silences the way I measured lamp oil and morphine, because the tower clock had died weeks ago and children build clocks out of whatever's nearest.

"Theo." Her voice came small from inside the blanket. "When you wrote the names in your book. The ones the officers left off. Did you ever write your own?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I was writing the book *for* everyone else. You don't put yourself in a list you're keeping for other people."

She considered this with the ferocious seriousness she brought to everything now, this war having eaten her childhood and left her its apprentice.

"That's a bad rule," she said. "A book for everybody should have everybody in it."

Neither of us answered. The candle did instead — a final flare, then smoke, then the particular dark of hospitals at night, which is never fully dark, just dimmed and breathing.

In that dark, Samson found my hand. Not dramatically. Just laid his great scarred paw over mine on the crate between us, the way he had when we were children crossing a flooded street in Lagos, except that then he'd been the small one and I the one leading. Sixty seconds, maybe. Neither of us said anything, because saying things had always been our worst subject and holding on had always been our best.

Then Amara's breathing went long and even against my shoulder — asleep despite everything, the way only children and the very old can sleep — and Samson whispered into the black:

"You're not giving up your berth. I've seen your face. Whatever you've decided, you decided days ago, probably in the bath, probably with a smug expression."

"I don't have a smug expression."

"You have *several*. I grew up with them."

I didn't confirm or deny. What I had, actually, was Odilon's signal schedule memorized, Marguerite sworn to secrecy, and a note drafted in three drafts to each of them — letters I hoped never to send — explaining, apologizing, lying. I had woken at dawn every day that week already dressed. Love as a problem without a clean answer is still love; you simply carry it differently, the way you carry a load that doesn't balance — closer to the body, step shorter, watching the ground.

"Get some sleep," I told him. "Whatever happens, we decide nothing tonight that exhaustion gets a vote in."

"Agreed." A pause, and his voice roughened. "Theo. For what it's worth. The quinine — I'd do it again. All six years of it. I just wish you'd gotten to hate me for something I actually did."

Before I could find an answer — before I even knew whether I had one — boots sounded in the corridor. Fast ones. Then Sister Marguerite was in the doorway with a lantern held high, and the light showed her face, and her face showed me everything before she said a word.

"Theo," she said. "Odilon's boy is at the side door. The river's dropped early. He says the causeway will hold — tomorrow night, one crossing, no second chance."

Beside me, Samson sat up. Against my shoulder, Amara opened her eyes, already awake, already counting.

Tomorrow night. One crossing.

The blank line on the manifest had just gotten company.
