## 24. Vasseur Comes at Night

He came at the hour I had privately begun to call the hour of honest women — past midnight, when the lamp oil was low and everyone still awake had stopped pretending to sleep for anyone's benefit.

I heard him before I saw him, which surprised me. Colonel Vasseur had a way of arriving like weather reports: officially, in advance, with paperwork. Tonight there was no orderly, no summons, no folded slip of paper requesting the pleasure of my obedience. There were only boots on wet gravel outside the dressing station, unhurried, and then his shadow filling the doorway the way authority fills a room — completely, and without asking.

"Theo Oyelaran," he said. Not *Sister*. Not *Nurse*. He had learned my name somewhere between the offer he'd made me and the refusal I hadn't yet spoken aloud, and he used it now like a man setting a card on a table.

"Colonel." I did not get up from my stool. The man on the table behind me — a sapper with shrapnel in his thigh and morphine in his blood, breathing in the slow tidal way of the well-medicated — was more deserving of my posture than the colonel was. "If you've come about the inspection report, it was filed. If you've come to arrest me for filing it, I'll want that in writing."

Something moved across his face. In the lantern light it might have been amusement. It might have been its funeral.

"I've come," he said, "to talk."

"Officers don't talk. They issue."

"Then consider this an issue delivered verbally." He stepped inside, and I noticed — because I have spent six years noticing what men carry before they speak — that he carried nothing. No ledger, no map case, no aide. His hands were empty. His greatcoat was buttoned wrong, one button off at the throat, as though he had dressed in the dark or dressed quickly or dressed like a man who no longer believed the mirror reported to anyone who mattered.

"You'll wake my patients," I said.

"Your patients," he said, glancing at the sapper, "are the only men in this valley getting adequate rest. Come outside. Ten minutes. Then I'll leave you to your arithmetic."

It was not a request, but it was also, strangely, not a command. It was the voice of a man who had run out of the kind of power that commands and discovered he still had things to say. I told myself I went outside out of curiosity, which is true, and out of the suspicion that whatever he wanted said in private would be worse said in public, which was also true, and not at all out of the third reason, which I will get to if I am brave later.

* * *

The night was the kind they'd been giving us all month — clear enough to see your breath make its small argument for survival, cold enough to make the flooded quarter beyond the dike lie flat and gleaming under the stars like a road to nowhere that had been paved on purpose. The town clock stood above us, hands frozen at eleven minutes past some evening weeks gone. Samson had offered to climb it and set it right; I had told him not to waste the rope. Time had left us, and we had gotten along without it the way people get along without a limb they never much liked anyway.

Vasseur walked to the edge of the dike road and looked out at the water. I stood a pace behind him, which is where you stand behind colonels, and waited. Waiting is most of nursing. It is nearly all of diplomacy.

"You refused my offer," he said finally.

"I'm refusing it slowly. Refusal takes time when the thing refused is two lives."

"You didn't refuse it slowly when the terms were the ledger. You refused that in four words. I counted."

"Four words is generous of me, considering."

He almost smiled. The water out past the dike shifted and settled, the way water does when something beneath it rearranges itself and prefers not to be discussed. Somewhere across the flood, a lantern moved — Odilon's barge, or one of Amara's counting-rhythms of light, or just the moon lying to us again.

"Do you know why the bridge closes when it closes?" Vasseur asked.

"Flood season. The engineers were very thorough. Charts. Graphs. A man with a pointer."

"The engineers believe that. Their charts are accurate. Accuracy is not the same as motive." He turned his head, and for the first time since the dressing station he looked directly at me, and I saw that whatever had been eating him had been eating carefully, from the inside, leaving the uniform intact. "A causeway runs through the marshes north of the town. Old towpath, raised, stone-cored. At low water it stands a hand's width above the flood. Your bargeman knows it. He's known it since summer. So do I."

My heart performed a small, disloyal maneuver. "Then why—"

"Because a causeway that can carry two hundred omitted souls out of the valley can also carry them *in*," he said. "Deserters. Contraband. Men with rifles and grievances. Someone very senior decided the marsh road was a security risk, and the correct response to a security risk is to let the winter drown it and call the drowning an act of God. The bridge closure is engineering. The refusal to open the causeway before the thaw is policy." He said the word *policy* the way other men say the names of their dead.

I thought of my ledger. Two hundred names, give or take the ones the fire had taken from me, each one a person the official rolls had declined to notice. And I thought of the maps I had been drawing in secret from Odilon's drunken geometry, the little pencil causeway I had traced and retraced until the paper wore soft as cloth.

"It floods," I said. "Odilon says it can be crossed only at low water."

"Odilon understates for a living," Vasseur said. "Listen to me, Nurse, because I will say this once and I will deny it in the morning with enthusiasm. The causeway has a wound in it. Halfway across, where the old culvert ran, the spring melt has already undercut the stone. It looks sound. It *sounds* sound, when you walk it. But it is a held breath. When the thaw comes down from the hills it will go all at once, without ceremony, in the middle of whoever is standing on it. Your crossing must happen at low water, in one night, before the next rain, and even then—" he paused "—even then the middle thirty meters are a wager, not a road."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I paid for the survey," said Colonel Vasseur. "In March, when I still believed the war had rules and that I was one of the men enforcing them. I know exactly how much weight that causeway can carry and exactly how long it will carry it, and I have spent nine months knowing it while signing manifests that sent the knowledge nowhere." He turned back to the water. "There is your marsh road, Oyelaran. Freely given. You may draw whatever conclusions you like about the character of the man who kept it to himself until keeping it served no one but you."

I stood very still. Above us the dead clock held its dead eleven minutes past, faithful to an evening none of us had lived.

"Why?" I asked. It is the question I ask fewest, professionally — bodies do not require reasons — but tonight it came out of me before I could weigh it back down. "Why tell me now? What changed?"

And here is where I must confess what I saw, because I have promised myself this account will be accurate even where it is unflattering, especially where it is unflattering. Colonel Vasseur reached into his coat — the empty-handed man produced one object after all — and what he brought out was not papers, not orders, not the elegant little levers he had tried to trade me three weeks ago. It was a compass. Brass, old, the lid worn to bare metal where a thumb would sit. He held it out flat on his palm between us like a man offering bread.

"My name," he said quietly, "is on a list."

I did not ask what list. There is only one kind of list being made in valleys like ours, and I keep its civilian equivalent in a hymnal.

"A superior's list," he went on, as though naming it would shrink it. "Expendables. Non-essential personnel. I helped draft the criteria, you understand. Weight, usefulness, cost of removal. Very clean arithmetic. I taught the system to the man who then applied it to me." A breath. The breath fogged and hung between us like a small ghost neither of us claimed. "I am telling you this not for sympathy. Sympathy from you would be obscene. I am telling you so you understand that the compass is not a bribe. I have no rank left worth buying with."

"Everyone has rank worth buying with until the moment no one accepts the currency," I said. "You taught me that, Colonel. It was in the manifests."

This time he did smile, briefly, and it was terrible, and I looked away from it.

* * *

We stood there while the water talked to itself below the dike. I took the compass. I want it recorded that I took it — not that he forced it on me, not that I accepted it under protest. My fingers closed around the brass and it was warm from his pocket, and I hated that detail more than any of the others, and I record it anyway, because accuracy is dangerous and I have decided to go on being dangerous.

"There is a condition," Vasseur said.

"Of course there is. There is always a condition. Men like you cannot hand over a handkerchief without a lien."

"The condition is nothing." He said it flatly, and I believed him, which frightened me more than any demand would have. "Nothing for you. Nothing for the girl. Nothing for your brother, God rest his intentions. Take your two hundred across the marsh if you can. Take the whole drowned town. I ask one thing, and I ask it of you because you are the only person in this valley who will understand it as anything other than sentiment."

He paused. Out on the flood, the far lantern went dark or dipped or was swallowed.

"The ledger," he said. "Whatever happens on that causeway — and something will happen, Oyelaran, you and I both know the middle thirty meters are going to collect their fee — the names survive. Not the book. The *names*. Burn the pages if you must. Eat them. Teach them to the child until she can recite them in her sleep. I have spent this war making people disappear with ink and stamps, and it works, that is the obscenity of it, it works perfectly, nobody notices, the arithmetic balances." His voice did not break. It lowered, which is worse. "Let one book unbalance."

For a long moment I could not speak. I had rehearsed a hundred conversations with this man, most of them ending in my righteous fury, several in his arrest, one or two — on my weaker nights — in surrender. I had not rehearsed this: a corrupt man standing in the cold asking for the one thing corruption cannot purchase, which is to have meant something after.

"If you want your name remembered," I said at last, "you should know I don't write flattering entries."

"I don't want my name anywhere in it." He was already turning away, buttoning his coat against the wind, becoming once more the shape that filled doorways. "Put me in the column of causes, not the column of names. Causes don't need forgiveness. That's why men choose them."

"Colonel." He stopped, half-turned. The lantern light from the dressing station window caught the wronged button at his throat, and I found I could not let him walk into the dark carrying nothing visible. "The causeway. The middle section. How deep?"

"Deep enough," he said. "That is the whole of what I know, and it is the worst thing I know." He looked at the compass in my hand. "North by northeast from the third willow past the broken mill. Keep it boxed until you need it. Brass remembers hands, and mine have been in places yours haven't."

He walked back up the dike road toward the staff quarters, and I watched until the dark took him, and I noticed that his footsteps made no attempt at the brisk officialese of his former arrivals. They were slow. They were the footsteps of a man measuring out ground he suspected he would not be issued again.

* * *

Inside, the sapper slept his medicated tide, and the stove ticked, and everything was ordinary, which is the strangest condition a night can achieve. I sat down at my table and opened the hymnal and looked at what remained of my ledger — smoke-stained, water-warped, half its names gone into the air at the shell fire and living now only in the place paper cannot reach, which is to say in me, and increasingly, in Amara, who has taken to reciting them while she buttons her boots, alphabet by alphabet, like a psalm she believes in more than any psalm actually printed.

I set the compass beside it. Brass on stained paper. The needle trembled behind its glass and settled, pointing faithfully at a north that did not care about any of us.

There is a version of this story in which I throw the thing in the stove. I considered it honestly — I want that known. A gift from Vasseur is a debt wearing jewelry, and I have seen what his debts do to rooms. But grace does not stop being grace because it arrives late and insufficient and from a man whose hands are the wrong color for it. Marguerite would say — Marguerite *has* said, with her ruined voice and her ruined certainty — that late grace is the only kind sinners ever manage, and that refusing it punishes the wrong party.

So I took it. North by northeast from the third willow past the broken mill. One night's crossing at low water, before the next rain, with thirty meters in the middle that will collect their fee.

Which meant the next rain was now an enemy with a schedule. Which meant Odilon needed waking at dawn, and Samson's raft needed finishing, and two hundred names needed teaching to a nine-year-old before the water finished deciding.

I closed the hymnal. The stove ticked. And through the thin wall from the loft where she sleeps, I heard it — faint, steady, half-asleep: Amara, counting backwards from a hundred, the way she does when the world has grown too loud for daylight arithmetic.

She had heard the door. She had heard the boots. She was calming herself with numbers while the adults traded compasses and confessions in the cold.

I put out the lamp and sat in the dark with the brass warm in my pocket, listening to my daughter count her way down to sleep, and understood with total clarity that whatever Vasseur knew about the causeway, I would spend every hour between now and low water making sure that when the middle thirty meters came to collect, they would find my family already across.

Outside, the first drops of the next rain began to fall on the dike road — soft, patient, early.

They were ahead of schedule. So was I.
