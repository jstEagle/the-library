## 25. The Bridge Closes

The chains came up at dawn, which I thought was a coward's hour. If you must close a bridge against two hundred souls who have nowhere else to be, you ought at least do it in daylight, where God and the gulls can see your face.

They sent six engineers and a lieutenant who wouldn't look at us. The chains themselves were nothing — great rusted loops that had hung coiled on the far pylon all winter like sleeping snakes — but the sound of them going taut was something I will hear until I die. A groan, then a click, then a silence so complete that the river itself seemed embarrassed.

The town had gathered before light. Nobody announced it; news like that travels the way cold does, through walls and under doors and into the marrow. By the time the first engineer touched the chain, there were four hundred people on the embankment, and when the link locked home, they made a sound I had never heard a crowd make before. Not a scream. Screams have shape. This was the sound of a lung emptying — one long, communal exhale that seemed to take everyone's hope out with it and leave only the wet paper of them behind.

Amara had my hand in both of hers, the way she did when the shells came close, and she was counting. Not seconds this time. She was counting the links of the chain as it rose. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. As though arithmetic could hold the bridge open by sheer stubbornness, and who was I to tell her it couldn't? Half the prayers ever prayed amount to the same thing.

"Fourteen," she whispered. "That's not very many."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

* * *

I want to record what the crowd did next, because the officers' reports will not, and I have learned by now that whatever the reports omit is usually the part that matters.

They did not riot. I half expected it; hunger and fear make fine kindling, and there was enough of both on that embankment to burn the prefecture down. Instead, an old fisherman named Aubert — seventy if he was a day, hands like knotted rope — walked to the water's edge, took off his cap, and held it against his chest. And one by one, without a word passing between them, the whole crowd did the same. Four hundred people standing in the grey morning with their hats off, mourning a bridge as though it were a coffin.

The lieutenant looked at them for a long moment. Then, to his credit or his ruin, he took his own cap off too, before turning and walking quickly back across the planking.

"They know," Sister Marguerite said at my shoulder. Her voice was very level, the way it always went just before or just after something unbearable. "They understand what the chain means. Second barge after the thaw, they were told. But the thaw brings the flood, and the flood brings—"

"Yes."

"—and they've done the sum anyway. Farmers do sums faster than officers. It's all they've ever had."

Samson stood a little apart from us, as he had stood apart from everything since the night he'd refused the berth. He had his cap in his hands like everyone else, but he wasn't mourning. I knew that look. It was the look he'd worn at fourteen, standing in the doorway of the shop we'd ruined between us, deciding what portion of the blame he could carry without dropping. He was doing arithmetic of his own, my brother. Counting bodies and berths and days, and arriving at some answer he hadn't yet told me.

I didn't ask. There would be time. That's what I told myself, anyway — there will be time — and I note here, for the record I keep in my head where no major can inspect it, that *there will be time* is the great lie of every person who has ever watched a chain go up.

* * *

The barge left at noon.

She was called the *Perdrix*, which I found obscene — a partridge, a bird, a thing with wings, hauling forty lucky souls downriver while two hundred stood on the bank — but nobody asked my opinion on christening. She sat low in the water with her cargo of crates and contraband hope, and the forty went aboard in an order determined three days prior by men whose names I had copied into my ledger under the heading *Men Who Decide Without Asking*.

My berth was among them. Senior nurse, guaranteed passage, stamped twice. The document had been pressed into my hands by a captain who couldn't meet my eyes either — apparently it was contagious that week, this inability to look at women — and I had thanked him and folded it into my apron pocket, where it had sat for three days like a stone I'd swallowed.

Here is what the manifest said, and what I had done to it in the small hours of the previous night, by lamplight, while Amara slept with her mouth open against my shoulder:

Line thirty-one read, in official type, *Sr. Nurse T. Oyelaran, attached medical staff, priority embarkation.* Beneath it, in ink I aged with tea and smoke, another line now read: *Substitute: M. Douai, laundress, pregnant, certified fit to travel by Sr. Marguerite Fauvel.* Marguerite's signature was the easiest thing I have ever forged, because I had seen it four hundred times at the foot of ward charts, and because she had given me permission that morning with a single nod, the kind of nod that carries a whole conversation in it: *Do it, and don't tell me the details, and don't make me proud, I haven't the strength to be proud anymore.*

A pregnant laundress with no papers. A nurse with a stamp. Between those two facts, the clerk at the gangplank never stood a chance. He glanced at the manifest, glanced at Madame Douai's belly, glanced at me, and waved her through, because clerks are human too, whatever their handwriting suggests, and because the substitution was so obviously right that even bureaucracy stumbled into it by accident.

Madame Douai tried to give the berth back to me at the plank. She actually took my wrist — a woman who had washed this hospital's linens since before the war and had never once presumed to touch a nurse — and said, "You're the one who keeps us counted, madame. They need you here more than there."

"The baby needs the berth," I said. "And I am needed exactly here. Both things are true, and only one of them has a boat."

She cried. I did not, which surprised me, and which I attribute to the peculiar dryness that descends on a person once a decision becomes irrevocable. Grief, it turns out, requires the possibility of having chosen otherwise. Once the choice is sealed up inside you like a fly in amber, it stops aching and starts simply *being*, heavy and preserved and yours forever.

Forty boarded. Forty-one names answered the roll call, because I had entered myself twice — once in type, once in tea-stained ink — and only the inked one walked up the gangplank wearing someone else's future.

The whistle blew. The lines came off. And the *Perdrix*, that obscene little partridge, pulled away from the quay into the brown water with one berth standing empty beneath her deck awning, a rectangle of shadow where a senior nurse was officially recorded as sitting.

I watched that empty shadow until I could no longer distinguish it from any other shadow, and then I could no longer distinguish the barge at all, and then the river was just the river again, doing what rivers do, which is carry things away and pretend innocence.

"You're grinning," Samson said beside me.

"I'm not."

"You are. That specific grin. The one you had when you were eleven and you'd put a frog in Father's boot and were waiting for him to find it."

"That was you," I said. "You put the frog. I only knew about it and said nothing, which as I recall is the crime you went to prison for the shape of."

He laughed — short, unwilling, startled out of him — and for one moment we stood on the quay as we might have stood at fifteen and nineteen, before the theft and the blame and the six silent years, two siblings watching a boat and sharing the criminal knowledge that one of us had done a wrong thing that was also the only right thing available.

Then the moment closed over, as moments do, and he went back to looking at the water with that arithmetic face, and I went back to pretending I hadn't noticed.

* * *

The wail came at dusk.

I say *came* because it moved through the town like weather. It started somewhere near the fish market — Aubert's quarter, I learned later, his wife beginning it, or ending something rather — and traveled street by street, door by door, until it reached our ward and pressed against the windows like a hand.

The lamps were being lit. That was the worst of it. Our town had one ritual left — the lanterns, every evening, for the missing — and on the night the bridge closed, the ritual became a requiem without anyone deciding it should. Every lantern in every window, and behind each one a person who understood, finally and completely, that no second barge was coming, that *after the thaw* had been a lie told to keep order the way a spoonful of sugar keeps a child from noticing the medicine is poison.

Amara lit ours. She had earned it; it was her job since August, and I would sooner have taken her name off my ledger than taken the match from her hand. She carried the flame along the wick with the gravity of a bishop, then set the lantern on the sill and stood back to admire it.

"Names float," she said. It wasn't quite a question tonight. Some nights it was a question — *do names float, Theo, tell me names float* — and I answered yes, because I had decided in October that a nine-year-old facing a flooded valley was owed one beautiful lie, and I had been paying for it in small installments of guilt ever since.

But tonight she said it as a fact, settled, load-bearing, and something in my chest gave way a little. She had built her house on my lie and now she lived in it.

"They float," I said. "Every evening we send them out, and every evening they find somebody."

"Even the ones the lists lost?"

"*Especially* those." I crouched to her height. "The listed ones have clerks. The unlisted ones have us."

She considered this with the terrible seriousness of nine, then leaned her forehead against mine, which is her version of sealing a contract.

"Marguerite says you write the true book."

I felt my spine go cold. "Does she, now."

"Not the ledger. The other one." She tapped my temple, once, imperious. "The one in there. She says when people die you put them in it so fast nobody else even sees them do it."

Sister Marguerite, I decided, had some answering to do, and possibly a vow of silence to renew.

"She's mostly right," I said carefully. "It's not finished yet."

"Good," said Amara, with the flat finality of a magistrate. "Because you'll need room. You'll need lots and lots of room."

Then she yawned, which is the privilege of children, and let me put her to bed in the corner behind the store-cupboard where the shrapnel had to try harder to reach her, and within minutes she was asleep and counting in her dreams — I could see her lips moving — counting steps, perhaps, or stars, or links of a chain rising into the dark.

* * *

Vasseur came to the embankment at full dark. Stripped of nothing yet, publicly — that would come later, and I did not know it then — but standing at the rail above the chained bridge like a man attending the funeral of his own signature.

I almost walked past him. I want that noted. There was a version of this night in which I kept my eyes forward and my ledger shut and let the colonel drown in his own arithmetic without throwing him so much as a rope of acknowledgment.

Instead I stopped.

"One berth empty," he said, without preamble. He had a brandy flask in his glove and did not offer it, which was the closest thing to respect he had ever shown me. "The manifests will show you aboard. Curious clerical error."

"Clerks," I said, "are fallible."

"Mm." He looked out at the black water. "When I was twenty-two I watched a ferry go down at Brest with my entire graduating class aboard. Forty-one men. I counted them going under. Do you know what I learned?"

"That water is patient."

"That numbers are a coward's comfort. Forty-one. A clean figure. Figures don't scream." He screwed the cap onto the flask with exaggerated care. "I have spent thirty years believing that if the figure was clean enough, the screaming didn't count. Your ledger says otherwise, nurse."

"My ledger says nothing. It only remembers."

"It accuses."

"It *remembers,*" I repeated, "and you may draw your own conclusions, Colonel, since drawing conclusions appears to be your one talent."

For a moment I thought he would strike me, or laugh, or arrest me, and I found — this surprised me — that I was indifferent to all three outcomes. The bridge was chained. The barge was gone. What was one nurse's insubordination against the general collapse?

What he actually did was worse than any of it. He nodded. Slowly, gravely, the nod of a man conceding a point in an argument he had been losing privately for years.

"There is a road," he began, "through the marshes—" and then the shell fell.

Not near. Not on us. Two kilometers east, beyond the ridge, where the front had been quiet for a week — a single, distant, orange-throated thud against the clouds, followed by the rolling insult of its echo. But it changed him mid-sentence. I watched the sentence die in his mouth, watched the officer come back up over his face like a visor lowering, watched him become, in the space of one detonation's echo, a man with a war to run instead of a confession to make.

"Another time," he said.

"There won't be another time," I told him. "That's rather the whole meaning of today."

He looked at me for a long moment. Somewhere behind his eyes, I believe, a road through the marshes lay folded up like a map in a drawer, and I have wondered ever since whether the shell saved his confession or killed it, and whether it matters, and whether anything he might have told me that night would have weighed less than the compass eventually would.

He saluted. Actually saluted me, a nurse with mud to the knee and a forged manifest in her history, and walked away down the embankment with his boots ringing on the stones as though the stones had wronged him personally.

* * *

Midnight found the four of us — myself, Samson, Marguerite, and Odilon the bargeman, who had appeared at our door smelling of bilge and secrets — gathered around the last inch of lamp oil in the hospital kitchen.

Odilon laid a scrap of oilcloth on the table. On it, drawn in grease pencil, was the marsh country west of town: reed beds, drainage cuts, the drowned orchards, and threading through them a thin doubled line that might have been a path or might have been a crack in the cloth.

"Causeway," he said, tapping it. "Old towpath, pre-dates the canal company. Holds above water maybe six weeks a year, if the thaw is late and God is bored." He looked around the table. "Thaw is early. So we have less than that. Less than anyone would comfortably say aloud."

"Say it aloud anyway," said Marguerite.

"A night. Perhaps two. One crossing, one column, no second chances, and anyone who steps off the stone goes down in water cold enough to stop a heart before they can shout." He straightened. "That's the price of the road. And you already know my price for showing it."

The ledger. My ledger, two years of omitted names, the only honest document in the valley — demanded as collateral against the silence of both armies, held hostage against my own honesty.

I had known it was coming since the day he named it. I had rehearsed surrendering it. And yet my thumb had found the ledger's edge through my apron, without consulting me, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

"One crossing," Samson said quietly. He was looking not at the map but at his own hands, turned palm-up on the table as though checking them for the work they were about to be asked to do. "One night. How many can walk it?"

"With lantern discipline and your sister's talent for making frightened people do sensible things?" Odilon shrugged. "All of them. Or most. Or half. The causeway doesn't publish figures."

Outside, the town clock — stopped these ten days, its face blind, its hands frozen at some meaningless quarter-past — caught moonlight through the ward window. Time had left us, formally, and gone over to the enemy. From here on we would measure by lamp oil and morphine vials and the depth of the water in the ditches, and by how many names I could teach a nine-year-old before the ice spoke.

Amara's voice drifted from the corner where she slept, counting in her dreams. Nine. Ten. Eleven.

I put my hand flat on the table, over the grease-pencil line, and felt the whole future balance there — one night wide, water on both sides, and no berth on it anywhere for me.

"How soon," I said, "can you get me a lantern that doesn't blow out?"
