## 26. Stranded

The morning after the bridge closed, the town did not weep. It did something worse. It went quiet in the particular way of a room after a doctor has said *there is nothing more to be done* — not silence, exactly, but a lowered volume, a collective decision to stop making noise that might be mistaken for complaint.

I woke in the dressing station with Amara's cold feet against my ribs — she had migrated in the night, as she always did, from her pallet to mine, by increments, like a continent drifting — and I lay there listening to the new sound of Sainte-Marguerite-sur-Aisne, which was the sound of almost nothing. No barge engines. No chains being cranked on the bridge, because the chains had already been cranked and hung with padlocks the size of a man's fist. Just the river, running high and brown and indifferent, carrying nobody.

The last barge had gone downriver at dusk with thirty-nine souls aboard and one berth empty. Empty by my design. I had stood on the quay and watched the lantern on its stern shrink to a spark, then to a rumor, then to nothing, and beside me a woman named Odile Fournier had asked whose place that was, and I had said, *a liar's*, which was true in a way she could not have guessed. I had written forty names on the manifest. Thirty-nine were real. The fortieth was a fiction with my handwriting on it, and I have never in my life been prouder of a forgery.

Now it was morning, and morning is when despair does its accounting. Grief keeps banker's hours.

* * *

By nine o'clock the omitted had found their way to the church steps, because people who have been struck off a list will always drift toward whatever building still has a door open to them. There were perhaps two hundred of us now, counting children, counting old Père Aubry who was ninety if he was a day and insisted he could march as well as any soldier, which was a lie we agreed to let him keep. The washerwomen. The lame cobbler Verlaine. Three families from the low quarter who had watched the engineers' stakes go up along the flood line and understood without anyone saying it what the red paint on those stakes meant: *here the water will come, and here you are not included.*

They looked at me the way patients look at you when the morphine runs low — not accusing, just waiting to see what your face will do next. And I thought: they think I am the one holding something. They think the nurse has the answer in her pocket next to the scissors and the iodine.

Well. In a sense I did.

"Sit," I said, in my best ward voice, the one that brooks no argument about whether sitting is wanted. "Sit and listen, because I am only going to say this once and some of you will need to hear it twice."

They sat. Even Père Aubry.

"You have heard," I said, "that the thaw will drown the low quarter before any second barge comes. You have heard that the bridge is closed until the water drops, and that the officers' lists are full, and that your names are not on them. All of this is true." I let it sit there, ugly and bare, because I had learned long ago that soldiers and civilians alike can bear almost any truth if you hand it over whole instead of in installments. "Here is also true: there is a road through the marshes."

A murmur went through them like wind through wheat. Somewhere in it, Sister Marguerite's eyes found mine — she had stayed, having given her berth to a pregnant laundress, and she gave me now the small nod of a woman who has seen another woman decide something and approves of the deciding, if not necessarily of the sanity.

"There is a causeway," I went on, "old, built for peat-cutters, that runs from the willow stand behind the tannery to the high ground past the flooded meadows. It is under water most of the year. At low water, it holds. A bargeman has told me it will hold — once, for one night — before the thaw. When that night comes, we will cross it. All of us. Every name."

"And until then?" That was Madame Fournier again, arms crossed, the skeptic of the quay. She had earned her skepticism; I would not take it from her.

"Until then," I said, "we prepare as though our lives depend on preparation, because they do. And we do it quietly, because if the staff learns two hundred people intend to leave by an unauthorized route, they will either forbid it or confiscate it, and I am not certain which would be worse."

* * *

Organizing the omitted turned out to be the strangest nursing I had ever done. A wound tells you where to put your hands. Two hundred frightened people do not; they leak fear in every direction and you must catch it in buckets.

I divided them the only way I knew how — by what they could carry, and how far. This had become, over these months, my private measure of the world. Not rank, not wealth, not even health, quite, but load-bearing capacity: who could carry a child ten kilometers, who could carry a bundle, who could carry only themselves and needed someone else assigned to carry hope on their behalf. Old Père Aubry could carry himself, barely, but he knew every psalm by heart and I had noticed that frightened people march better to recitation, so I made him a kind of regimental chaplain of cadence. Verlaine the cobbler could not march at all, but his hands were miracles, and I set him to making sandals — rope-soled, marsh-proof, ugly as sin — out of salvaged leather and mooring line, and within four days half the town clattered like a ship's crew.

The washerwomen became my quartermasters, because any woman who has boiled other people's linens for a living cannot be impressed by catastrophe. They inventoried the town the way I inventory my kit: dried fish from the smokehouse, oil, salt, the tarpaulins off the bombed cooperage, cordage, and — my favorite find — three hundred candle stubs that the sacristan had been hoarding against a better age. We would cross, if we crossed, by lantern light, and now we had light enough.

Amara appointed herself, without election, minister of counting. She went through the work parties twice daily with her slate, tallying heads, and woe betide the man who wandered off to smoke behind the tannery without reporting. "You are *missing*," she informed a startled private of the labor battalion who had done nothing of the sort, merely lingered. "Missing persons make mistakes in arithmetic." He apologized to her with complete sincerity, and I confess I did not correct the arrangement, because a nine-year-old with a slate and a grievance achieves order that no sergeant ever has.

And every evening, when the work parties came in and the lanterns were lit — we lit them openly now, all together, a whole town of small flames, defiance disguised as devotion — I taught the marsh road.

That was the part I told almost no one the purpose of. I called it a walking exercise, a game for the children, exercise for the elders. But in the church hall, with the furniture pushed back, I had chalked the route onto the floorboards from memory: Odilon's map drawn large, corrected nightly against what I could recall of his gestures, his shrugs, the way he had tapped his pipe twice at the bend near the drowned orchard. *Here,* I would say, chalk squeaking, *the causeway narrows to a plank's width. Here the peat cutters dug too deep in '09 and the fill never held — you will feel it give, and you will keep walking, because stopping is sinking. Here the willows thicken and the ground rises and if you reach the willows you have won.* And then I would make them walk it, blindfolded at first — Marguerite's suggestion, that one; she understood better than I did what darkness does to a column of refugees — feeling their way along the pews, counting their steps, Père Aubry intoning psalms behind them while Amara called corrections in a voice of terrible authority: "*Too fast! You are too fast, monsieur, the fog does not hurry!*"

It should have been comic. Some of it was; I laughed more that week than I had all winter, mostly at things that would have horrified the medical board. But underneath the comedy ran something else, and I saw it plainly on the fourth evening, when Madame Fournier — the skeptic, the crossed arms — walked the chalk route with her eyes shut and her lips moving, counting, and when she opened them at the end she was crying without any noise at all.

"It's real," she said. "It's really under my feet somewhere."

"It's real," I said.

"I had stopped believing in anything under my feet."

* * *

There was a moment — I mark it here because moments like it deserve marking, the way Vasseur marked supply depots on his maps — when the whole enterprise nearly curdled into something uglier than despair. A rumor went round the low quarter that I was selling places on the marsh road, that the crossing list was for sale, that the nurse with the ledger had her price. It traced, I thought, to a clerk at the commissariat who had been refused a place ahead of his neighbors and had decided that if virtue was not rewarded it must not exist.

I dealt with it the only way that has ever worked against that particular poison. I stood up in the church hall, held the ledger over my head where everyone could see it, and said: "This book contains the names of everyone the officers forgot. It is not for sale. It was never for sale. If you do not believe me, read it — read your own name in it, and your mother's, and tell me what price I could ask that would be worth more than watching you walk out of here alive."

Then I passed the ledger down the rows, hand to hand, which was either great folly or great wisdom, and I still don't know which, but two hundred people read their own names in a dead officer's war of arithmetic and found themselves *counted*, and the rumor died that night of embarrassment.

Marguerite helped me gather it back up at the door. "You took a risk," she murmured.

"I've been taking risks in ink since summer," I said. "Paper can't drown me."

"No," she said, folding the covers closed with her careful ruined hands. "But paper is the only thing that drowns *them*." She meant the ones whose neat lists had struck two hundred souls off the world. She meant Vasseur's superiors, and Vasseur, and every man who had ever balanced a manifest against a conscience and found the conscience overweighted.

She had a way of saying things that kept me awake. She always had.

* * *

On the sixth night, the siege quiet broke — not into violence, but into something so close to peace that I distrusted it immediately. The town lit its lanterns as usual, and someone began singing, and instead of the psalms Père Aubry led it was an old boat-song, the one about the girl who waits at the bridge, and half the omitted knew it, and the labor battalion men knew it, and even two of the sentries on the chain-line pretended not to know it while knowing every word.

Samson appeared at my elbow with two cups of chicory that tasted of burnt acorns and God knows what, and we drank them in companionable silence, watching the lanterns double themselves in the high dark water.

"You've made an army," he said.

"I've made a queue," I said. "An army needs weapons. A queue only needs patience and good sandals."

"Verlaine's sandals," he said, flexing his feet, "are a weapon." And then, quieter, in the voice he used when he was going to say something that cost him: "Theo. The bargeman. Odilon. When does he signal?"

"When the low water comes," I said. "He'll hang a lamp on his mast, green over white. Could be eight days. Could be twenty. The thaw keeps its own counsel."

Samson nodded slowly, looking out at the black river where our reflections burned upside down. "Then we'd best keep walking your floorboards," he said. "Every night. All of us." He drained his cup. "Because I've been thinking about that causeway, sister. About one night, and two hundred people, and a plank's width of fill laid down before the war."

I felt it before he finished saying it — the cold place opening in my stomach where arithmetic lives.

"One column," Samson said. "Maybe two hundred souls, single file, in the dark, on a road that exists only between tides." He set his cup down on the step between us, precisely, like a man setting a chess piece. "We're going to need more than a game of follow-the-pews, Theo. We're going to need a miracle with a timetable — or a rope."

And as if the river had been listening, as if it resented being discussed like a solved problem, there came from downstream, faint but unmistakable across the water, the sound of ice shifting in the current — a groan, a crack, a long grinding sigh — the first cough of the thaw clearing its throat.
