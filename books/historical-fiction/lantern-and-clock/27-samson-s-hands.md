## 27. Samson's Hands

My father had a saying about hands. He said you could tell a man's whole biography from his palms — the calluses arranged like chapters, the scars like footnotes he couldn't bear to leave out. I was eleven when he said it, and I remember thinking that my own hands were a blank page, and how that felt like promise.

Six years later I took that blank page and signed my sister's name to a theft I didn't commit, which is the sort of thing a man does when he loves someone and resents them in equal measure and has not yet learned that the two can share a house without burning it down. The courts read my palms after that. Or rather, the battalion did, when they conscripted me into labor service instead of prison, and every sergeant since has read my history in the calluses: dockhand, thief, convict, mule. None of them ever got past the second chapter.

None of them ever asked what happened to make a man write his sister's name where his own should have gone.

* * *

The barge had a name once. You could still make out most of it along the bow, charred but legible — *La Concorde*, which struck me as the kind of joke God makes when He's been drinking — and she lay on her side in the shallows a half-mile downstream from the bridge, split along her keel like a loaf someone had given up on. Half the town's evacuation hopes had gone down with her when the shell fire caught the moorings two nights before the bridge closed. The other half had left on the last barge with one berth empty, because my sister has never in her life done the sensible thing when an unreasonable thing was available.

I went down to look at the wreck because there was nothing else to do. That is the honest version. The flattering version is that I went to survey salvage for the work parties Theo had begun organizing, and both versions are true, which is usually how it goes with me.

The mud took my boots to the ankle and held them like a creditor. I stood on the exposed hull, which the low water had tilted up toward the grey sky like a hand opened too late, and I put my palm flat against the plating, and I thought: here is forty tons of oak and iron that somebody built to carry people to safety, and here it is, broken, and here am I, broken, and one of us is going to have to become something else.

"You going to apologize to it all morning," said a voice behind me, "or are you going to help?"

Odilon the bargeman stood on the bank with his thumbs hooked in his belt, looking like a man who had personally witnessed every mistake the river had ever made and catalogued them by season. Beside him were three men I knew by sight and nothing else — labor battalion men, the ones the officers' manifests had struck through with the same bored pen that struck through civilians. A Congolese stevedore named Bassene whom everyone called Bas because the sergeants couldn't be troubled. A Portuguese fisherman, Inácio, missing two fingers on his left hand and none of his opinions. And a boy of maybe seventeen, French, name of Pascal, who had the particular silence of someone who had watched his family's names get written down by nobody at all.

"Apologizing seemed cheaper," I said. "She's dead."

"She's timber," Odilon said. "Dead is for things that can't float. Get your crowbar."

* * *

I should explain what I disdained, since the assignment of my conscience seems to require it.

I came up in the docks at Dakar, then Bordeaux, then the coastal runs, and I learned early that a man could climb by keeping his boots clean of other men's misfortunes. I learned to speak the way the clerks spoke. I kept my ledgers neat — and yes, the irony of that word, in this town, in this season, is not lost on me; my sister keeps a ledger of souls and I kept one of crates, and only one of us will be remembered for it. I looked at the labor battalions — men conscripted from the colonies, from the ports, from prisons like mine — as freight that complained. I looked at stevedores like Bas and saw competition. I looked at fishermen like Inácio and saw superstition in rubber boots. I did not think of myself as a cruel man. I thought of myself as a rising one, and gravity, in my experience, is just cruelty with better manners.

Then I wrote my sister's name in the wrong column, and the world wrote mine in another, and for six years the army moved me around Europe like a stone in a pocket, and everywhere I went I found that the men I'd disdained were the ones carrying everything — the timber, the wounded, the war itself — while the clean-booted men made lists.

So when I say I worked beside Bas that first morning on the wreck of *La Concorde*, understand what it cost me, which was nothing except every stupid thing I believed, and understand why it took me six years and a flooded valley to pay so little for so much.

* * *

The plan was Theo's, though she'd deny the engineering and credit Odilon. The marsh road west of town ran over a causeway that was passable only at certain lows of the river, and even then only if you could get two hundred people — the omitted, the unlisted, the struck-through — across the flooded quarter first, from the low quarter's edge to the causeway's start, across a stretch of water too deep to ford and too wide to bridge in the time the frost would allow. Unless you had craft. Unless you had a raft big enough to ferry columns of people, loaded and reloaded through a night, quiet enough not to draw artillery.

"Build me a platform," Odilon said, spitting into the water with the precision of a man quoting scripture. "Sixteen feet by ten. Buoyant enough for twenty souls and calm enough that they don't pray out loud. You've got timber." He jerked his chin at the barge. "And you've got hands."

He said *hands* looking directly at me, and I felt it land somewhere I hadn't been hit since the trial.

We started at dawn because the light was bad for artillery and good for denial. The first job was tearing planking off the barge's starboard side, above the waterline, where the wood was sound. Bas worked beside me, and I want to describe him accurately: a head taller than me, shoulders like a yoke, hands so large that a crowbar looked in them like a pencil, and a singing habit — low, continuous, in Wolof and French both, songs I didn't know, sung at a volume calculated to annoy exactly no one and to keep time better than any sergeant's count. Within an hour I was prying planks on his rhythm without having decided to. That's what labor does when it isn't being forced, I learned. It synchronizes. It's almost embarrassing how willing the body is to agree with another body.

"Your sister," Bas said around noon, passing me a plank end. "The nurse. She wrote down my brother's name."

"He's on the list? The official—"

"No." He said it flatly, the way you report weather. "Her list. Her book. She came through the ward in October and asked my name and my brother's name and where we were born, and I thought she was mad, or collecting us for something. Then the officers' manifest came out and neither of us was on it, and I understood what she was doing." He set the next plank for me to lever. "Nobody had written my brother anywhere since the ship. She wrote him down. So." He shrugged, an entire continent of philosophy in the gesture. "Now I build her raft."

I had spent six years not thinking about what Theo had done with her life, the way you don't press a bruise. Standing in the ribs of a dead barge, hearing a stranger call my sister's stubbornness a salvation — it pressed hard.

"She gets it from our mother," I said, which was true, and also an apology wearing a coat.

* * *

By the third day we had a routine and by the fifth we had a raft that was beginning to exist. Pascal turned out to have a genius for joinery — his father had been a wheelwright in the Vosges, and the boy fitted our salvaged planks with a care that shamed the rest of us into precision. Inácio supervised flotation, which meant he made us lash sealed oil drums and empty biscuit tins under the deck in an arrangement he explained twice, patiently, and then explained again whenever one of us soldiers ruined it, without once making anyone feel the fool. I noted that. I filed it away with other evidence accumulating against my old verdicts.

The work was brutal in the way that honest work is brutal: it left no room for the theater of grief. Six years I'd had a monologue running in my head — the injustice of the sentence, the size of the sacrifice I'd made for Theo, the betrayal I never let myself examine too closely, namely hers, namely whatever it was she'd done or failed to do that made my sacrifice necessary in the first place. I'd rehearsed that monologue on road gangs and dock details until it was worn smooth as a river stone. On the raft, the monologue kept trying to start and Bas's singing kept drowning it, and Pascal kept handing me things, and there was simply no room to perform.

On the sixth day, Inácio caught me staring at my hands.

"Blisters," he said, diagnostic.

"Biography," I said.

He laughed — a short, surprised bark — and showed me his nine fingers. "Mine stopped writing years ago. Two chapters missing." He flexed the healed stumps. "You know what I learned? The story doesn't need every finger. It needs the grip."

* * *

Theo came down to the wreck on the eighth day, with Amara trailing her the way the moon trails weather — you could see the change coming in the girl before it arrived. Theo stood on the bank with her arms crossed, surveying our half-built platform riding at its mooring line, and her face did the thing it always did, the thing I'd forgotten between visits and hated remembering: it refused, absolutely refused, to look proud of me until it had checked whether I could bear it.

"Well?" I said.

"It floats," she allowed.

"It's not finished."

"I know. I counted the missing planks from the bank." And then, because she is my sister and mercy is not her first language: "Bas told me about the drum lashings. Inácio's arrangement?"

"Inácio's arrangement."

"Good. Because I'm going to put children on it, Samson. Twenty at a time, in the dark, in cold water, and if one lashing fails—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to. We had both grown up on a river; we knew what water does with a mistake.

"We'll test it," I said. "Loaded. With sandbags first, then men."

"With men," she agreed, and something passed between us there on the bank — not forgiveness, we weren't there yet, maybe we'd never be there, but recognition. Two people who had each spent six years carrying the other's weight, discovering the loads had been identical the whole time.

Amara, meanwhile, had walked the length of the raft with the gravity of a harbor inspector, counting the planks aloud under her breath. I'd heard about the counting. Everyone had. When she reached sixty-two she stopped, frowned, and announced: "There's a plank missing under the third barrel. I can hear it. The sound is different."

Inácio went down and checked. She was right. The little girl had found a fault in our hull by ear that four grown men and a crowbar had missed by eye.

"Keep her," Inácio said solemnly. "Whatever she costs."

"She costs nothing," Theo said. "She pays in numbers."

* * *

Here is what I did not expect from those days on the wreck, and I record it now because a man should mark the exact hour his stupidity died: I began to look forward to the mornings. Not the work — the work was misery, my back a chorus of complaints, my blisters opening and closing like small dishonest mouths. The mornings. Bas arriving with bread he'd traded some quartermaster for cigarettes he didn't smoke. Pascal racing the light. Inácio standing in the shallows testing the current with a stick and reading the water aloud like scripture. The five of us becoming, plank by plank, something none of us had applied to join.

One evening Bas sat down next to me on the overturned hull, handed me half of an orange — an orange, in a siege winter, currency beyond gold — and said, "Your sister says you took a sentence for someone."

The river made its small noises. I turned the orange segment over in my fingers.

"I took it for her," I said. "That was the story I told. Took her sin so she could keep her nursing school, her clean hands, her future." I ate the orange. It tasted like a confession. "Took me six years and a flood to ask the obvious question, which is: what kind of love signs another person's name without asking them first?"

Bas considered this with the seriousness he gave everything, which is to say completely and without hurry.

"The kind that thinks it isn't worth asking," he said finally. "My mother carried me nine hundred kilometers once. Never asked if I wanted to go. Love like that doesn't consult. It just decides it can carry more than you can." He stood, brushing hull-grime from his trousers. "Doesn't make it right. Makes it heavy. You carry it or you put it down. Your choice. But the carrying is easier with more hands."

He walked off along the bank toward the town, singing, and I sat in the dark on a dead barge with half an orange and the beginnings of an understanding I wasn't ready to say out loud: that I had spent six years believing I had sacrificed everything for my sister, when in fact I had taken the one thing that was mine to give — my name, my clean record, my future — and spent it badly, in secret, without her consent, and then billed her for it silently ever since. Redemption, if that's the word for what the raft was doing to me, turns out not to be a feeling. It's a load-bearing structure. You find out if it holds only when people stand on it.

* * *

On the tenth day we launched her, fully loaded — twenty sandbags, then twenty volunteers, then, on the final run, twenty children from the low quarter who needed to believe the water could be crossed by something other than drowning. Pascal steered with a swept barge oar Inácio had shaped from the wreck's tiller. The raft rode low and steady, and Amara stood on the bank counting the passengers aboard and counting them again ashore, and her counts matched, every crossing, and each time they matched, Bas sang one line louder.

We named her, the way crews do, though we told no officers. Pascal wanted something grand. Inácio voted for a saint. Bas said, "Name her for the list," meaning Theo's ledger, meaning every name written down that the officials had struck out, and so we called her *the Ledger*, quietly, among ourselves — forty feet of salvaged truth floating on sealed lies and biscuit tins.

It was near dusk on that launching day, hauling the raft back to her mooring, when I saw Sister Marguerite standing at the top of the bank, apart from the others, watching the water with an expression I couldn't read — not hope, not fear, something more like accounting. She had refused something that afternoon, I'd heard; given her place away to the laundress with child, the way you'd hand off a burden you'd been waiting years to set down. Now she watched our raft come in, and her eyes went from the waterline to the far shore to the causeway country beyond, measuring, the way my sister measured, the way this whole drowned valley had learned to measure: not in meters anymore, but in names, in berths, in who could be carried and who would have to carry.

She raised one hand as I passed — not waving. Beckoning.

"Nurse Oyelaran's brother," she said. "I need to tell you something before the water rises again. And afterward, I need you to teach me how you joined those planks. There may not be much time."
