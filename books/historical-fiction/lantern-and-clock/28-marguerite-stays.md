## 28. Marguerite Stays

The list came at noon, pinned to the door of the dispensary with a rusted nail and the particular smugness of paper that believes itself final. *Evacuation Priority — Medical Personnel.* Sister Marguerite's name sat at the top of it in a staff captain's tidy hand, because seniority is the one currency that never floods, never spoils, and never has to be carried.

I read it twice. Then I read it a third time, the way you reread a letter that says someone has died, hoping the words will rearrange themselves into something survivable.

"You're to go on the next boat," I said, when she came through the door with her sleeves rolled and her hands smelling of carbolic. "First priority. They've finally noticed you exist."

"How flattering," Marguerite said, and hung her apron on its hook with the precision of a woman who had been hanging things on hooks since before I was born. "And when does this miracle depart?"

"Tomorrow, if the water holds. Odilon's taking wounded down the east channel while it's still deep enough."

"Then there's no hurry," she said, and went back to work.

I should have known. I had known her for two years by then, and in two years I had learned that Marguerite treated good news the way she treated bad news: as a symptom to be investigated before it was believed.

* * *

We worked through the afternoon in the ward that had been the schoolhouse, back when the town had children enough to fill it. Forty-one cots now, most of them occupied by men who had been subtracted from official rolls the way you subtract a wrong figure from a ledger — quietly, so the totals still balance. My own book lay in its oilcloth wrapping under the floorboard by my cot, and every day I added names to it, and every day the officers' lists got shorter, and every day the arithmetic between us grew another finger's width.

Marguerite knew about the book. She had known since autumn, since the night she found me writing by lantern light and stood over me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't read.

"You'll get yourself shot for that," she'd said.

"Probably."

"Use a fine nib," she'd said, and brought me one. "The ink dries faster."

That was the whole of our conspiracy. We never spoke of it again. There are friendships that are built out of confessions and friendships built out of silences, and ours was the second kind, which I have come to think is the more load-bearing construction.

At four o'clock she sent me to change the dressings on the far row while she took the fever cases, and when I came back she was standing at the window looking at the river, which had turned the color of old pewter under a sky the same shade. The thaw had begun in the hills. You could smell it — mud and rot and something almost like spring, if spring were a threat.

"The laundress," she said, without turning around. "The one in the last cot by the stove. Veronique."

"Veronique Aymard," I said. "Yes. Six months along, maybe seven. Her husband's on the missing lists."

"She's on nobody's manifest."

"I know."

"And you've been trying to get her on one for three weeks."

"I know that too."

She turned from the window then. Her face in the gray light looked the way church faces look — composed, and very tired underneath the composition. "They'll take forty on the east channel run. Thirty-eight wounded, a nurse or an orderly, room for one civilian if the manifest allows it. The manifest will allow whatever the staff captain writes on it, and the staff captain has written my name."

I felt it arrive before I understood it, the way you feel thunder before you bother counting the miles. "You're not going."

"I'm giving her the berth."

"Marguerite—"

"Don't." She held up one hand, and I noticed, as I had noticed a hundred times, how capable those hands were, and how thin they had become beneath their capability. "Don't argue with me yet. Let me finish, because I've been composing the argument all morning and if you interrupt it I'll lose my place and we'll both have to sit through it twice."

So I didn't argue. I pulled up the stool by the stove instead, because I am a coward who prefers to be told terrible things while sitting down.

* * *

"You asked me once," she said, "why I came to a dressing station at my age, when I could have taken a convent posting in the south where nothing worse than boredom ever arrives. Do you remember?"

"You said the south was bad for your knees."

"I lied about my knees. My knees are magnificent." She lowered herself onto the stool across from mine, and for once she let herself look her age, which was somewhere past sixty and somewhere short of eternity. "I came here because twenty-two years ago, in another war, in another town that also had a river in it, I was given a list."

I said nothing. Outside, a barge horn sounded, long and low, the river talking to itself.

"A triage list," she went on. "Not unlike yours, except that mine was written by other people and I was only asked to execute it. There were too many wounded and too little morphine and the orderlies needed to know whom to carry and whom to leave comfortable. Comfortable. That was the word they used. As if we were arranging furniture." Her hands folded themselves in her lap, one over the other, with the neatness of long discipline. "There was a man on the leave-comfortable side. A cooper, I learned afterward. He had a wife who arrived two days later and asked me, with perfect courtesy, why her husband had been moved to the corridor, and I told her it was because his wounds were grave, and every word of it was true and every word of it was a lie, and she looked at me — she was not angry, that was the unbearable part, she was only trying to understand — and I saw that she would spend the rest of her life trying to understand, and I have spent the rest of mine making sure I never have to see that look again."

"That wasn't your doing," I said. "You followed orders."

"Yes. That is precisely what it was my doing." She said it without drama, the way she recorded temperatures. "I have thought about it every day for twenty-two years, and every day for twenty-two years I have known exactly which choice I made, and it was not the choice of a bad woman. It was the choice of a tired one. A tired woman, thirty-six years old, who wanted very much to believe that the list knew something she didn't — that whoever made it had information, authority, some arithmetic she couldn't see from where she stood. And so I stepped aside inside myself. I did what was written. I was obedient, and obedience, Theo, is just cowardice wearing someone else's uniform."

The stove ticked. Down the row of cots, Veronique Aymard slept with one hand resting on the curve of her belly, the way women do when even their sleep has learned to guard something.

"So when they write my name at the top of a list," Marguerite said, "you will understand that I have seen this particular document before. I have been on the leaving side of it, and I have been the left, and I have watched what it does. And I am telling you now, while I still have the authority of being the eldest person in this room: I am not getting on that boat."

"There will be other boats."

"No, there won't. You know the channels better than I do. Another week of thaw and Odilon can't run the east passage at all, and after that it's your marsh road or nothing, and we both know what you think of the marsh road's odds." She leaned forward. "One berth. One. It goes to a woman seven months pregnant whose child will need a lifetime and who will not survive a flood and a siege to provide one. Or it goes to a nun of sixty-three who has already lived hers twice over. You do the sums, girl. You're better at sums than you pretend."

"It isn't about the sums."

"No," she agreed. "It never is. But it helps to have them correct anyway."

* * *

I want to record honestly what I did, because this book, whatever it becomes, is useless as flattery. I argued. I argued badly and then well and then badly again. I invoked her value to the ward, her experience, the fact that half the men in the fever rows trusted her face more than they trusted morphine. I pointed out that priority meant she could return north and come back with supplies, which was true, and which we both knew was the kind of truth that behaves itself beautifully at dinner parties and falls apart in rivers.

She listened to all of it with her hands folded and her eyes steady, and when I ran out she said only: "Is that everything? Because I have one more thing, and I'd rather say it to you while you're arguing than after you've surrendered."

I surrendered first. It seemed polite.

"Whatever book comes next," she said, "and there will be a book — you are constitutionally incapable of not keeping one — I want my name written first. Not for honor." She raised a hand before I could object, because I had been about to object on the grounds that her name was already in my book, entered in October, among the omitted, where it belonged. "Hear me. Not for honor. For correction. Twenty-two years ago I wrote other people's names into a column marked *comfortable*, and I have never once written my own into anything. I hid behind the list. So put me at the front of yours, in your own hand, with your own ink, as a woman who chose — so that when I stand wherever it is tired nuns stand at the end, and someone reads out what I did, it begins with a decision I made and not one that was made for me."

The stove had gone quiet. Somewhere down the corridor a man was dreaming aloud, arguing gently with someone about hay.

"Sister Marguerite Delacroix," I said. "Chose to stay."

"Add the date. Dates matter. And add what she chose it *for* — the laundress Veronique Aymard and the child coming. A choice without its reason is just behavior."

"You're dictating your own entry."

"I'm a great believer in editing," Marguerite said, and for the first time that afternoon she smiled, and the smile was young, which startled me, the way a locked room startles you when you find it full of light.

* * *

We told Veronique at supper. Marguerite insisted on doing it herself, and she did it plainly — no ceremony, no martyrdom, just the facts delivered in her flat nursing voice, as though reporting a temperature. Veronique began to cry, and then to protest, and Marguerite took both her hands and said, "You will name her Marguerite if it's a girl, and that will settle accounts entirely," and Veronique laughed through the crying, which is the only honest kind of laughter, and after that it was done.

Word travels in a small town faster than any official communication, and by evening the whole ward knew. Nobody thanked Marguerite, which was correct of them; gratitude would have made it a transaction, and what she had done refused to be transacted. But the old cooper's apprentice, who had lost two fingers and kept his humor, got up from his cot without being asked and carried her supper tray to her, and the schoolteacher pressed her hand in passing, and a boy of nineteen who hadn't spoken since he came in said, quite clearly, "Good night, Sister," and she said, "Good night, Jules," as though he had been conversing all along.

That night we sat up late in the dispensary, she and I, dividing the stores. If she stayed, she would need what remained: the lint, the iodine, the last of the quinine, the three jars of petroleum jelly that had survived the shell fire by some miracle of packing. I counted things into her arms and she counted them back to me, and it occurred to me, somewhere around the third jar, that this was the closest thing to a will either of us would ever execute, and that it was conducted entirely in numbers, and that neither of us found this strange.

"You understand," she said, as I wrapped the quinine, "that I'm not doing this to be admirable. Admiration is what people want instead of payment. I'm doing it because I have waited twenty-two years for a chance to choose correctly in public, and I am much too vain to waste it."

"Noted," I said. "Shall I put vanity in the entry?"

"Put it. Accuracy above comfort. Isn't that your creed?"

"It's my method."

"They're the same thing in you," she said, and blew out the lamp between us, and we went to our cots in the dark.

I lay awake a long while, listening to the river. It had a new sound lately — not the winter mutter but something deeper, a gathering, like a crowd before the speaker arrives. The thaw was coming down out of the hills whether our lists were finished or not.

* * *

Odilon found me at dawn. He had a way of appearing at dawn that suggested he considered sleep a personal failing in everyone, including himself, and he stood in the doorway dripping marsh water on the step with an expression I had learned to distrust, because on Odilon expressions were currency and he never spent the real ones.

"East channel runs shallow by Friday," he said. "Your sister-nurse sails tomorrow, yes? With the wounded?"

"With Veronique Aymard. Yes."

"Good. Good." He scratched his jaw, which was the sound of a boot heel on gravel. "And the rest of them? The ones in your book?" He didn't say the word *ledger* — he never did, he had decided early that ignorance was armor and wore it loudly — but his eyes flicked toward the floorboard by my cot and back, quick as a card sharp's.

"What about them?"

He glanced up and down the empty street, and then he did something he had never done before: he came inside uninvited, shut the door behind him, and lowered his voice, which for Odilon constituted liturgy.

"Low water comes Thursday," he said. "Maybe Friday, if the rain holds off in the hills, and it will hold off, because I have watched these clouds longer than you have been alive. Causeway dries enough to walk, moonless night, one tide's worth of time. Not two. One."

My heart was doing something undignified. I made it wait. "You said the marsh road was a rumor."

"I said it was a rumor to anyone who might repeat it. Are you going to repeat it?"

"No."

"Then it's a road," Odilon said, "and it holds for exactly one night, and I can guide maybe sixty souls across it if they can walk and keep silent, and the price we discussed hasn't changed." He straightened, opened the door again, and stood a moment with the gray light behind him. "Thursday. Tell no one who cannot walk. And tell your sister-nurse—" he paused, and for once the expression on his face looked like it cost him something "—tell her the river keeps stranger accounts than either army. Some of them come out even."

Then he was gone, and I stood in the doorway watching him go, and Thursday sat in my chest like a swallowed stone — four days, one tide, sixty souls, two hundred names — while behind me in the ward, unhurried, Marguerite began to sing the morning psalm in her flat nursing voice, as if the world were a place where things could still be counted on.
