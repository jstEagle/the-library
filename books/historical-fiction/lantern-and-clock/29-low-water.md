## 29. Low Water

The reed came tied to a brick, which was Odilon's idea of discretion — a man who smuggled contraband flour past two armies decided that a note might be too obvious, so he sent me a piece of marsh grass and half a house.

I fished it out of the drainage channel behind the laundry at dawn, my sleeves soaked to the elbow, and turned the brick over twice before I understood. Scratched into the clay with something sharp — a nail, a bayonet point, one of those implements Odilon kept in the same mental drawer as his conscience, i.e., rarely inspected — were three marks: a crescent, a line beneath it, and a small cross like an X that had given up.

Crescent moon. Low water. Tomorrow night.

I stood in the cold with the brick dripping in my hands and felt the strangest sensation, which was hope arriving with a deadline attached. Hope, I have learned, is not a warm thing. It is a summons. It stands at the foot of your cot at four in the morning and says *get up, there's work, you have until the water comes back*, and it does not care that you had plans to feel sorry for yourself.

* * *

I brought the brick to Samson without explanation. He read it faster than I had.

"One night," he said. He turned it over as if the answer to everything might be stamped on the bottom by some providential manufacturer. "One night for how many?"

"As many as can walk quietly."

"And the causeway holds?"

"For exactly as long as Odilon says it does. Which is a sentence I never expected to stake two hundred lives on."

Samson set the brick down on the carpenter's bench where he'd been salvaging barge planks, and he looked at it the way he used to look at arithmetic homework when we were children — with the expression of a man who suspects the numbers are lying but can't prove how. "Then we don't move two hundred," he said. "We move forty at a time. Quietly. In order. And nobody runs."

"Nobody runs," I agreed. "You've met people, haven't you? You've noticed what they do when there's water involved and a rumor of safety on the far side?"

"I've carried them out of rivers." He flexed his hands, and I looked away from the scars across the knuckles, because looking at them made my chest do something unsoldierly. "Order is possible. It just has to be built beforehand, like a raft. You don't build it while drowning."

That, I thought, was as good a philosophy of evacuation as any general staff had produced in three years, and it had cost less to develop.

* * *

The preparations took the shape of nothing, which is to say they had to look like everything else.

A town under siege learns to be unremarkable or dies of its own curiosity, and ours had gotten very good at being furniture. So: Marguerite began sorting the ward stores with unusual vigor, setting aside bandages and tinned milk in lots of ten, each lot wrapped in a blanket, each blanket folded the way you fold a thing you intend to carry at speed. Anyone watching saw a sister being tidy. What she was actually doing was packing forty field kits into laundry baskets destined, nominally, for the wash.

"The wash," she repeated when I outlined it, her mouth doing something dry at the corner. "Two years I have laundered for this army and now I launder for a rebellion. There's a sermon in that somewhere."

"There's a whole catechism. We're inventing it as we go."

"Good. The old one never fit." She stacked another bundle and paused. "Theo. If the causeway fails—"

"It won't fail."

"Everything fails. That's not despair, that's bookkeeping." She smoothed the blanket flat with both hands, an old habit, pressing the wrinkles out of things too stubborn to lie flat on their own. "If it fails, the ones who go first must be the ones who cannot swim. Children. The laundress Béatrice, who is eight months along and floats like an anchor. Then the old. Then everyone else by lot if it comes to it."

"By lot," I said. "You want me to draw lots for human beings."

"I want you to have already decided, so that on the night itself you aren't deciding. Deciding takes time. Time will be the thing we don't have." She looked at me then with her flat grey honesty, the honesty of a woman who had once chosen and knew precisely what choosing cost. "You taught me that, actually. Or you'll teach me that. I get our chronology confused these days; we keep living each other's lessons back and forth."

* * *

Amara counted.

She counted everything, because counting was her rope hand over hand up the well shaft of the world, and in the days after the brick arrived she counted with a new intensity that told me she had overheard more than any child should and metabolized it the only way her mind allowed: as arithmetic.

She counted the steps from the ward door to the kitchen (forty-two). She counted the bundles in Marguerite's baskets (thirty-eight, and when she reported the discrepancy Marguerite and I exchanged a look over her head like conspirators caught by the smallest possible customs official). She counted the lanterns we had gathered — eleven good ones, three with cracked glass, one that belonged to the church and would need to be returned or stolen outright depending on how the theology resolved.

"Why do we need eleven lanterns?" she asked me on the third evening, sitting cross-legged on my cot with the copybook in her lap, practicing her letters. She wrote *L* with a ferocity suggesting the letter had personally wronged her.

"Do you know what a causeway is?"

"A road in the water. Odilon says the marsh keeps it secret except when the water goes down to visit somewhere else." She said this with total seriousness, because in Amara's cosmology the marsh was a character with motives, and honestly I had stopped correcting her. The marsh did seem to have motives.

"That's right. And tomorrow night the water goes visiting, and we're going to walk across that road. A lot of us. At night. In the fog, probably, because the marsh makes fog the way other places make gossip."

"So the lanterns are so we can see."

"The lanterns are so we can be seen by each other. There's a difference. In fog, seeing isn't the hard part — being seen is. Everyone carries light, and no one walks ahead of their light, and if your light goes out you stand still and call your number."

"My number." She considered this. "What's my number?"

"You'll be near the front. Number nine."

"Nine." She wrote it in the copybook, beneath the *L*. Then, in the way she had of asking questions that arrived like shells: "Is Samson going to be a number too?"

I had known this question was coming for two days and it still landed badly. "Everyone is a number."

"But someone has to be last."

The copybook lay open between us, her careful letters marching down the page, and I thought about the guide rope Samson had been splicing all week, and about the way he tested knots now — pulling them until they broke, every single one, as if collecting evidence against himself. "Yes," I said. "Someone is last. That's what the rope is for. The last person holds the rope, and the rope holds everyone, and that's why we trust the last person more than anyone."

"More than you?"

"Much more than me. I'm only good at lists."

She seemed satisfied with this division of labor, which shows you how much children understand about what adults tell them versus what adults mean. She went back to her letters. I sat with the lie I hadn't quite told, warming my hands over it like a small dishonest fire.

* * *

Odilon came himself at dusk, which frightened me more than the brick had. A man who sends messages is careful. A man who comes in person has run out of messages.

He arrived through the kitchen wearing the expression of a bargeman delivering bad news about cargo — apologetic, fatalistic, already composing the version he'd tell his wife. He set a lantern on the table and stood with his cap in his hands.

"One night," he said. "Maybe less. The engineer's almanac and my knees agree on tomorrow, disagree about the night after, and I've learned to trust the knees."

"How long has the causeway held crossings before?"

"Twice in my life. Once for cattle, once for a funeral, and the funeral party still talks about it the way soldiers talk about battles, which tells you what kind of crossing it was." He laid a scrap of oilcloth on the table, and on it, drawn in tar, was the route: the drainage channel, the willow stand, the long spine of the causeway running a half-mile through reeds to the high bank at Vireloup. "You enter here, at the old sluice gate. You do not enter anywhere else, because everywhere else is either mud that eats men or water deep enough to be polite about calling itself a river. Half a mile. Forty minutes with a loaded column, if they're calm. An hour if they're frightened. Forever if they panic."

"And if the water comes back early?"

"Then the causeway becomes a rumor and anyone on it becomes a story their family won't believe." He said it without cruelty, the way you report weather. Then his voice dropped, and the bargeman fell away and left just a tired man in a doorway. "Nurse Oyelaran. The ledger you promised me. For the silence."

"It's packed."

"Not all of it."

It wasn't a question, and I didn't insult him by pretending. After the shell fire took the ward, half my recorded names had gone to smoke and standing water, and what remained was memory wearing the costume of ink. "Half of it is paper. The other half is in here." I touched my temple. "You'll get the paper. The rest stays with me until your side of the bargain holds. I'm told that's how collateral works — you hold something of mine, I hold something of yours."

For a moment I thought he'd argue. Instead he smiled, briefly, like sun through marsh fog. "My wife says the same thing about our marriage. Very well. Midnight tomorrow. Sluice gate. Bring calm people, nurse. Calm people float better than brave ones — I've seen both tested."

He took up his lantern and paused at the door. "One more thing. There's a colonel asking after you. Not officially. Asking the way a man asks when he's lost his command and kept his compass."

"Vasseur."

"He knows the causeway exists. Has known for months, I think. Why he never flooded the marsh with orders—" Odilon shrugged, and the shrug contained an entire treatise on the moral architecture of the staff. "Some men build the fire escape into the building and then burn the plans so no one can prove they hoped."

* * *

We spent the last day becoming invisible on purpose.

Samson drilled the crossing order in the wine cellar, using chalk marks on the floor for the sluice gate and the willows and the high bank, walking forty volunteers — no, forty *guests*, we'd agreed on guests, because volunteers implies choice and most of them had none — through the sequence until their feet knew it without their heads. The pregnant laundress Béatrice walked it twice, holding the chalk line, enormous and serene, complaining about her ankles with the composure of a woman who had decided that if the world intended to drown her it would have to fill out the paperwork first.

Marguerite distributed the bundles. The church lantern was returned with a donation that made theft unnecessary, though Amara was visibly disappointed; she had wanted the story of the stolen lantern.

And I sat alone in the dispensary with the ledger open on my knees, reading the names I could still read, and reciting the ones I couldn't, over and over, the way Amara had memorized them as a psalm. Two hundred souls minus smoke minus water equals whatever I could carry. I measured the book in my hands. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything. Both measurements were correct; I was learning to live inside the discrepancy.

Outside, the marsh waited with its secret road held just under the surface, keeping it the way the town had kept its missing — patiently, without malice, ready to give it all back for one night and ask no questions until morning.

Tomorrow at midnight, the water would step aside.

I closed the ledger, blew out the lamp, and went to find my brother, because someone had to be last, and I intended to spend the hours remaining arguing him out of it — or failing that, arguing him into carrying the best rope.
