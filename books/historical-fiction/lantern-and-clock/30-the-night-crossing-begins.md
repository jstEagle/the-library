## 30. The Night Crossing Begins

Odilon's signal came at half past nine, which I only knew because Amara had been counting since the church bell gave its last honest stroke and then abandoned us, as everything else had, to rumor.

"Nine hundred and forty-one," she said. "Nine hundred and forty-two."

She had been counting since dusk, not seconds exactly but breaths of time, the way she did when the world grew too large for her hands. Samson had taught her a soldiers' trick without knowing it was one — *count your steps, count your rounds, count anything that ends* — and she had made of it a small religion. Nine hundred and forty-three. Nine hundred and forty-four. The numbers fell from her like water from an eave, steady, unhurried, indifferent to the fact that we were about to walk two hundred souls across a drowned road in the dark because every official route out of the valley had been chained shut by men with better boots than sense.

"He's coming," I said. "Look."

A lantern bobbed along the mill leat, swinging in that particular impatient arc Odilon used when he wanted you to know he considered the entire enterprise foolish and himself heroic for consenting to it. He came up the bank with mud to his knees and his cap ruined and his face arranged into an expression of a man delivering bad news at a fair price.

"The causeway holds," he said. "For tonight. The river's down eleven centimeters since noon and the wind's turned north. You have until the moon clears the willows on the far bank and then perhaps two hours more. After that, Madame Oyelaran, you are no longer leading a crossing, you are conducting a drowning, and I will want it noted that I said so."

"Noted," I said. "Witnessed. Recorded."

"In what? Your famous book is soup."

"My memory is drier than you'd think."

He looked at me for a moment — this bargeman who had demanded my ledger as collateral and received instead a lesson in how much a woman can hold against her ribs without paper — and something in his weathered face shifted a degree toward respect. Or possibly indigestion. With Odilon the two were often indistinguishable.

"One night," he repeated. "One column at a time. No lanterns forward of the third marker except the guide lamp. And whoever told you the fog burns off after midnight has never stood on this river at four in the morning."

* * *

We assembled them in the yard of the tannery, which Marguerite had chosen because its walls were high and its smell discouraged conversation. Two hundred and six souls, minus the sick who could not walk and would go later if a later existed, minus Marguerite herself, who had spent three days refusing to be counted among the first and had finally agreed to go in the second column purely so that Theo Oyelaran would stop asking.

They came quietly. That was the part I had not expected. I had rehearsed speeches on my tongue for days — short ones, practical, the sort you give before hard work — and when I climbed onto the tanning vats to deliver mine, I found there was nothing to say that their presence had not already said. A schoolteacher held a boy of six by the hand. The pregnant laundress, Béatrice, whom Marguerite had saved a berth for on a barge that never came, walked with her arms cradling the weight of her own private arithmetic. Old Fournier, who had fished this river for fifty years and now intended to leave it on foot out of spite, carried his nets over one shoulder like a man going to work rather than to war.

I kept the speech anyway. It was four sentences long.

"We cross tonight because the water will not wait and neither will we. You follow the rope with your left hand and the lantern with your eyes, and you do neither thing quickly nor slowly but at the pace of the girl counting at the front. If you fall, you shout once. Once is enough; we are close enough to hear it. And if you hear someone else's once, you keep walking, because stopping to grieve on a flooded causeway is how one grief becomes two hundred."

An old woman near the back began to pray aloud, and I let her, because a prayer is also a way of counting.

Samson found me at the rope locker. He had spent the day splicing the guide line out of barge wreckage — hawser and cod-line and, I suspected, a length of someone's stolen washing line, because my brother's principles had always been flexible about rope specifically — and his hands were cracked to the wrist with salt and tar.

"You'll take point?" he asked, though he knew the answer.

"I'll take point. Amara walks second. You take the rear anchor position."

"The rear." He tested the splice, pulled it, pulled it again. "You're putting me as far from you as the line allows."

"I'm putting you where the strongest knot belongs."

That was the peace we had made, or the shape of peace, which in our family was nearly the same thing. Six years of silence over a theft I committed and he confessed to, and now we spoke fluently in assignments of danger. It was not forgiveness. But it was load-bearing.

"Theo." He lowered his voice. "The middle section. Odilon showed me where the paving drops away past the third marker. If it goes while people are on it—"

"Then you'll be at the far end anchoring and I'll be at the near end doing the same, and the rope will do the rest."

"And if the rope doesn't?"

"Then I will owe you an apology six years overdue," I said, "and I refuse to spend it tonight. Save me some trouble and don't drown."

He laughed — one syllable, surprised out of him — and for a moment he was fifteen again and I was twelve and we were stealing plums from Monsieur Aubry's tree with the flawless ethics of children who believed the world would always divide cleanly into fruit and consequence.

"Count your steps," he said.

"It's Amara's job to count."

"Then count her counting," my brother said, and shouldered the coil of line, and went to the back of the column like a man taking up a post he had trained for all his life without knowing it.

* * *

Amara wore every stitch she owned and two stitches she didn't — a sweater of Marguerite's rolled to her elbows and Samson's muffler wound round her so many times she resembled a small dignified parcel. At her waist, tied with surgical string, hung the lantern. Not the guide lamp; hers was a child's tin thing with a cracked chimney, but I had filled it myself and trimmed the wick myself, because there are superstitions worth keeping.

She was frightened. Anyone who says children have no dignity has never watched a nine-year-old decide to be brave in public. She stood at the head of the column with her chin up and her fists closed and her whole body humming like a struck wire, and when I knelt to check her lantern she whispered:

"If I lose the number, what happens?"

"Nothing happens. You start again at one. That's the whole secret of numbers, Amara — they always let you begin again."

"What if I lose the path?"

"You won't. But if you did, you'd tell me, and I'd be holding your hand, so we would be lost together, which is a different thing entirely and mostly amounts to an adventure."

She considered this. She had grown shrewd about my reassurances; she audited them the way I once audited officers' manifests. "You said the marsh road was safe."

"I said it was possible. Those are not the same word, and you know it, which is why I'm glad you're coming with me instead of staying here to correct the major's arithmetic."

The ghost of a smile. Then, because she was Amara, and because ritual is the raft fear clings to: "One thousand and six. One thousand and seven. One thousand and eight."

"Begin at one," I said. "Tonight we begin at one."

The first twelve went onto the causeway at ten o'clock precisely, which I mention with pride because I had promised Odilon punctuality and had never in my life delivered it to a man before.

* * *

I will try to set down what the crossing was like, though description fails at just the moments it matters most, the way a bridge fails in the middle of the span.

The causeway was a farmer's road that the marsh had swallowed to the ankle — old paving under black water, slick with a season's silt, running straight as a sermon between reed beds that breathed and muttered on either side. Above us the sky was scoured clean, winter-sharp, salted with stars. Below us the water lay mirror-still and carried those same stars upside down, so that we walked along a seam between two heavens and could not always say which side of it we occupied. The guide lamp burned red at the far end, a half mile off, small as a votive. Between it and us: our one lantern, Amara's tin thing, throwing its circle of gold maybe eight feet wide.

Eight feet of certainty in a half mile of night. That was the entire estate we possessed, and we moved it forward one step at a time.

"Fifty-one," said Amara. "Fifty-two. Fifty-three."

Behind her, the column took up the rhythm whether they meant to or not. Boots found the beat. Fifty-four. Fifty-five. A woman with a baby began humming — something from a village mass, tuneless and low — and the hum braided itself into the counting until it was impossible to say where the numbers ended and the prayer began. This, I thought, this is what courage actually looks like when you catch it in the wild: not the charge, not the flag, but two hundred exhausted people agreeing to move at the speed of a child's voice.

Courage as choreography. Marguerite would have appreciated the phrase. She was somewhere behind me in the second file, refusing my arm, accepting Béatrice's.

At two hundred steps the reeds closed overhead and the wind died and the sound changed — the water around our ankles went from moving to waiting, which is a distinction the feet understand before the mind does. Amara's count never wavered, but her free hand found mine and gripped it with an authority I did not argue with.

"Two hundred and thirty," she said. "Two hundred and thirty-one."

"That's the deep pool ahead," I called softly back down the column. "Knee-height, no more. Left hand on the rope, small steps, and whatever touches your leg is a fish and is more frightened of you than you of it."

"Nothing is more frightened of anything than I am of fish," said old Fournier from the fourth rank, and the nervous laugh that ran down the line was worth more than any speech I had stored in the tannery yard. Fear shared sideways is halved. Fear fed forward, down the rope into the dark ahead, is doubled. I had built the whole plan on that arithmetic, and tonight, for once, the sums behaved.

Three hundred steps. Three hundred and fifty. The far lamp grew from a votive to a coal to a small red sun, and Odilon's silhouette resolved beside it, arms crossed, radiating the satisfaction of a prophet whose doom-mongering had been temporarily vindicated.

It was at four hundred and six steps — I know because Amara said it aloud, clear as a bell across the water, and the number is carved into me now beside other numbers I carry — that the rope in my left hand went suddenly, terribly light.

Not slack. Light. The difference matters. Slack means someone has stopped. Light means the far end has stopped being attached to anything at all.

Behind me, faint but distinct, came a sound I had heard once before in a flooded dressing station in spring: the deep, patient gulp of the marsh swallowing stone.

Amara's lantern swung. Her count reached four hundred and seven.

And then the world tilted, and the seam between the two heavens buckled, and the water stopped waiting.
