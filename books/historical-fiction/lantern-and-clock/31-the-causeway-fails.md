## 31. The Causeway Fails

There is a particular sound a stone causeway makes just before it decides to stop being a causeway. I had never heard it before that night, and I have never entirely stopped hearing it since. It is not a crack, exactly. Cracks are honest. This was more like a sigh — the deep, mineral patience of something that had held back water for two hundred years finally admitting it was tired of the job.

Amara heard it too. She was walking four places behind me with her hand on the guide rope, counting steps in a voice pitched low and even, the way she counted everything now — to keep the dark arithmetic of the night from adding up wrong.

"Four hundred and six. Four hundred and seven."

"Steady," I said, though I said it to everyone and no one, the way a nurse says *this won't hurt* when she hasn't checked.

The column stretched behind us nearly to the near bank. Two hundred and eleven souls, by Odilon's count and mine, which agreed for once, which frightened me more than disagreement would have. Ahead, the lanterns made a crooked necklace across the black water, swinging at the pace of old men and pregnant women and one boy of fourteen carrying his grandmother's sewing machine on his back because he could not carry the grandmother, who had died in October and been carried far enough already.

The marsh road was not a road. It was an argument the eighteenth century had made against the marsh, built of stones laid on clay on silt on older water, and it had held ever since mostly out of habit. Odilon had told me it would hold for exactly one night's crossing at low water, and he had told me this in the tone of a man describing a horse that was sound *provided you didn't look directly at its left foreleg.*

Low water came at midnight. We had started at half past. You do the sums. I did them all night, every step, and the answer kept coming back *just barely*, which is the only arithmetic this war has ever offered me.

* * *

I should go back a little, because the beginning of the crossing deserves its own accounting, and I am a woman who keeps accounts.

We mustered in the reed yard behind Odilon's barge at dusk, in that strained hush of people trying to be quiet while doing enormous things. Samson had finished the raft three days before — a lashed platform of barge planking and empty barrels that looked like nothing so much as a drunk man's idea of a bridge — and we had hidden it in the reeds downstream of the flooded causeway's worst reach, because Odilon's route required us to cross open water twice where the old road had gone under.

Odilon stood at the head of the line with his lantern raised, looking like a prophet who had been drinking. "Rules," he said. "One hand on the rope at all times. No talking above a whisper. If you fall, you grab the rope and say nothing, and someone will pull you out, and then you will walk on as if nothing happened, because panic is heavier than any of you and it sinks fastest." He looked down the column of two hundred faces. "Also, if anyone has brandy, I will be walking near the back."

A few nervous laughs. Good. Fear is bearable in company but unbearable in silence.

Samson found me at the second rank. In the lantern light my brother looked older than thirty-four, younger than his sins, his hands wrapped in the rags he always wore for work now. He had tied one end of the spare guide rope around his own waist without asking anyone's opinion about it, which should have warned me.

"You're leading," he said.

"I know the route. I've walked it twice with Odilon."

"I'm not arguing. I'm stating facts for the record." His mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling. "For the record: my sister is about to walk two hundred people across a drowned road at midnight, and the last time she led me anywhere we were nine and twelve and it was into the magistrate's orchard."

"And we got apples," I said.

"We got caught."

"We got apples *and* caught. A complete education." I touched his wrist where the rope was knotted. "Walk behind Amara. Don't let her count faster when the water gets close. She speeds up when she's frightened and then she frightens herself with the speed."

"She gets that from you."

"She gets everything from me. That's what family is — theft with permission."

He laughed, quietly, and for a moment we were only two Oyelarans standing in the reeds, and six years of silence weighed exactly nothing, or almost nothing, or nothing enough.

Then Odilon's lantern dipped, and the column began to move, and I learned that hope sounds like two hundred people trying not to make noise.

* * *

The first mile was almost kind. The causeway rode a hand's width above the water, its old stones furred with weed, and the marsh lay flat and silver-dark on either side under a sky the color of gun metal. Somewhere far off — south, the front — artillery muttered like a card game in another room. Nobody spoke. Even the children understood, in the way children always understand the things adults never manage to explain, that tonight was made of whispers.

Amara counted. "Six hundred and twenty. Six hundred and twenty-one." Her voice floated back along the rope like a rosary. Behind her, other children had picked up the habit — I heard it, a soft secondary counting, a liturgy of numbers moving down the column — and I thought, absurdly, that if we survived, some scholar would write about the singing armies and the famous speeches and never once record that this army sang in integers.

At the seventh hundred step the causeway narrowed where a culvert had washed out years ago, and Odilon's men had laid planks. The planks flexed. Water showed black through the gaps, close enough to touch. I stood at the plank's edge and passed people across, hand to hand, the way you pass basins in a crowded ward, and I watched each face as it crossed — the laundress Adèle, seven months gone, laughing breathlessly at herself for being frightened; old Ferreol the clockmaker carrying the dead town clock's hands in his coat pocket because he refused to leave them; Marguerite, who had given up her place on the barge and who now walked with a soldier's pack on her back and a novice's calm on her face, and who squeezed my hand as she passed and said, "Still here, Théo. Still counting me among the living?"

"First page," I said. "I told you."

She moved on into the dark, and I envied her the simplicity of her fear, which had been tested long before mine.

* * *

The breach came at the place Odilon had feared and named, because bargemen name their fears the way mothers name their children: *le Pas du Diable*, the Devil's Step, a stretch where the causeway crossed a channel the river had been quietly robbing for decades. Low water was supposed to leave it shin-deep over stone. Low water lied.

I felt it before I heard it — a shiver through the soles of my boots, the sensation of standing on a drumhead someone else is striking. Then the sigh. Then Amara's count faltering — "seven hundred and... seven hundred..." — and then, off to the right, where the column's middle ranks were crossing the Devil's Step itself, a crash of stone and a scream cut short by discipline, and the black water came up through the road like something let out of a cellar.

"Rope!" Odilon's voice cracked down the line like a whip. "Everyone hold! Do not run! If you run you die and worse, you take others with you!"

The column swayed. I could feel panic traveling along that rope in both directions, a current of hands. Ahead of me a woman began to pray loudly; behind me, someone wept; and through the middle of it all, clear as a bell over water, Amara's voice resumed: "Seven hundred and one. Seven hundred and two." Steady. Slower than before, deliberately slower, and I heard in her pacing the lesson I had given her without knowing I was giving it — that fear can be walked down like a steep hill if you refuse to hurry.

But the hole was growing. Two stones had gone; then five; the water poured through the gap and undermined the edge, and I saw the lantern light catch on the faces of the people stranded at the breach — a dozen or more, roped on either side of a chasm of moving black water, the planks gone, the stones going, the causeway dying under them like a patient beyond even Marguerite's pessimism.

And then I saw my brother.

* * *

He did not hesitate, which surprised no one who knew him, least of all me. Samson went forward along the rope the way he went at everything — with his shoulders first and his doubts somewhere far behind — hand over hand, past the frozen ranks, until he stood at the lip of the breach where the guide rope ran out over open water with nothing left to anchor it.

The rope was the whole plan. Without it, nobody crossed the gap; without it, the people already across could not hold those still coming. But the mooring post on the far side had gone under with the stones, and Odilon was shouting for the raft, and the raft was ten minutes away through reeds, and ten minutes is a country where these people could not afford to live.

Samson looped the rope twice around his waist. Then he sat down in the flood, wedged his boots — my father's boots, the boots he had crossed six years of silence in — against the last sound stone, and leaned back into the current, and became a post.

"Run it across!" he called. His voice was already roughened by the water pulling at him. "I'll hold it! Go!"

They ran the rope taut, and it rose out of the water and hung over the breach, anchored at one end to a stump on the near bank and at the other end to my brother's body, and the column began to cross.

I want to be exact about this, because exactness is all I have ever had. He sat chest-deep in February water, leaning against a current strong enough to move stones, holding a rope that two hundred lives pulled upon, and he held it for — I did not count. For the first time in my life I did not count, because counting meant knowing, and knowing meant watching the number climb toward the number I could not bear.

People went over hand over hand. Adèle went, laughing and crying. Ferreol went, clutching the clock hands. Marguerite went last of the middle ranks, pausing beside him to say something I could not hear, and Samson laughed — laughed — in the water, and waved her on.

I crossed near the end. Of course I did. I had a ledger's worth of reasons to stay until the last, and also one small girl whom I had promised, silently and repeatedly since summer, that I would always be between her and whatever came next. I gripped the rope and waded the breach with the current tearing at my skirts, and when I reached the middle I was directly above him, close enough to see the white of his eyes in the dark, close enough to hear the effort in his breathing, the small animal sound of a body working at the very edge of its strength.

"Samson." My voice broke on it. Six years of unsaid things tried to get out through that single syllable.

"Don't," he said, cheerfully, horribly. "Don't you dare stand there making speeches, Théo. Cross. Count your people. That's what you do." And then, softer, with the water climbing his chest: "You took the blame for nothing, all those years ago, and let me carry it. Let me carry this instead. It's lighter."

I want to tell you I argued. I want to tell you I ordered him, reasoned with him, threatened him with the full authority of senior nursing staff. What I actually did was what nurses do: I assessed. The water was at his ribs. The rope was taut and holding. There were still forty people on the wrong side, and one raft, and one of him, and there was nothing in my kit — not morphine, not prayer, not love — that could improve his position by one inch. So I crossed, because crossing was the only medicine I had, and I hated myself with a completeness that felt almost restful.

The last of the stragglers came over. The raft arrived and took off the lame and the smallest children from the rear ranks. Odilon counted heads aloud in his bargeman's bark, and his count and mine agreed again, and agreement still frightened me.

"Samson!" I shouted back across the water. "Come! It's done — everyone's across — come!"

And he called back, and the wind took most of it, but I heard enough: "In a minute. Let them get clear of the bank. If I let go while they're pulling the rope loose—" He didn't finish. The current finished it for him, pushing him sideways, dragging at his waist where two hundred crossings' worth of strain had knotted the rope.

We worked the lashings free on our side. Minutes. Three minutes? Five? The clock in our tower had died weeks ago and time was rumor now; I measured it in the falling of the water, which was rising, in the strength of his voice, which was thinning.

"All clear!" Odilon bellowed at last, and I screamed it across the water, and my brother — my stubborn, silent, generous brother — began, slowly, with the terrible dignity of exhausted men, to work himself up the rope toward the stone.

That is when the second sigh came. Deeper than the first. The causeway, relieved of our weight and betrayed by the current gnawing its wound, shrugged off another whole section — and the stone Samson braced against rolled, tilted, went.

He held. God forgive me for the order of my thoughts, but *he held* was my first thought, relief before terror, because the rope was still taut, because his knot was good, because he had built things all winter with those hands and they were hands that did not fail. He hung out on the rope over the flood with the broken causeway grinding beneath him, and he shouted something to Odilon — I will believe until I die it was a joke — and began, hand over hand, to haul himself along the line toward us.

And that is when Amara screamed my name.

Not from beside me. From behind me — from the *wrong* side, the near bank, the side we had left — her voice thin with distance and fog and something worse than fear, coming from somewhere in the reeds where she could not possibly be, except that she was, because children wander, because columns are long and dark, because a nine-year-old who counts to steady herself had stopped counting and started running.

"Fog's coming up the channel!" Odilon roared. "Théo — the girl — she's got three with her, the Vasseur boy and the two from the laundry — they're lost in the reeds and the water's turning!"

Out on the rope, in the black flood, my brother turned his head toward the sound of a child calling my name. And I saw him make, in half a heartbeat, the same calculation I had made a hundred times over a wound — triage, the cold bright sorting of what can be saved — and I saw what he chose.

"Hold the rope, Théo," Samson Oyelaran called to me, cheerful as an orchard thief, and let go of the line, and turned into the current, swimming toward the reeds where my daughter — the one I had stolen from grief and given my name to in everything but ink — was lost in the fog.

His lantern, lashed to his belt, bobbed twice on the black water.

Then the fog closed, and took them both.
