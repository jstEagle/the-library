## 32. Clockwork of Courage

Here is what nobody tells you about fog: it is not empty. Fog is full of everything, all at once, pressed close to your face so that none of it can be identified. The river was in the fog. The marsh was in the fog. The guns were in the fog somewhere behind us, and the causeway that had been under our feet ten minutes ago was in the fog too, because the causeway had broken and now there was mostly water.

I had three people with me. That is how I counted them, always, before I counted anything else: *three people with me.* First there was Monsieur Perrin, who had one arm and a voice like a boot heel, and who had been a schoolteacher in the low quarter for thirty years, though you would never guess it from how badly he followed instructions. Then there was a woman named Colette whom I did not know before tonight, who carried somebody else's baby wrapped in a shawl, the baby being asleep in a way that seemed rude given everything. And then there was a boy of about sixteen called Filip, who had been a deckhand on Odilon's barge and who knew the marshes the way I knew my counting, which is to say he knew them until he got frightened, and then he knew nothing.

We had been in the column behind Theo. I want that understood. I was not lost because I wandered; I was lost because the world wandered first.

The lanterns had gone out ahead of us like beads pulled off a string. Theo had said, when we started: *If we are separated, do not call out. Sound travels over water and tells people where soft things are. Count instead. Count your steps, count your breaths, count anything, and the fear will have less room.* She taught me counting when I was eight and screaming, back in the summer when I had no name for anybody. She taught me letters after that, using her ledger, which is a book of names that matters more than any book in church, though I am not supposed to say so. So when the causeway groaned and tilted and the man in front of me shouted something about the rope, and the fog closed like a hand, I did not scream. I counted.

One. Two. Three. Four.

By forty-seven I was standing still in cold water up to my knees and I could see nothing but a grayness that had opinions. By sixty-two I heard Monsieur Perrin swearing in the manner of educated men, which is quieter and much more inventive than soldier swearing.

"I'm here," I said. "It's Amara."

"Child?" His hand found my shoulder and gripped it hard enough to hurt, which I decided was affection. "Where is the path? Where is anyone?"

"The path stopped being anywhere," I said. "The water took the middle of it. Theo told us if the causeway broke to go left, toward the willows, along the high ground."

"Left." He turned in place. The fog turned with him. "Which way is left, when there is no world?"

That, I thought, was a very good question from a schoolteacher, and I was glad it was mine to answer instead of his.

* * *

Theo taught me the alphabet with names. Not A is for apple. There were no apples. A was for Adjei, who came from a farm near the bridge and whose burn scars went up his arm like ivy. B was for Bintou, who sang while she washed bandages and could not carry a tune even a little. C was for Colonel Vasseur, and when I asked why she put a colonel in her special book, Theo was quiet a long time and then said, *Because a ledger does not choose. It only keeps,* and I thought that was the sort of thing grown-ups said when they didn't want to explain, but later I found out it was also true.

What I learned from this, without knowing I was learning it, was that letters are hooks. Every letter has a name hanging on it, and every name has a face, and every face has a place where it lives inside you. And places — real ones, marshy ones — work the same way backwards. You hang a memory on a landmark, and then the landmark holds still for you even when nothing else does.

Filip splashed up beside us, breathing wrong, the way you breathe after crying or running, which for him were the same activity. "The willow," he kept saying. "There was a willow. Big one, split down the middle like a struck match, on the left bank past the second marker pole. My father showed me. If we find the willow we find the dry spine of the bank, and the dry spine runs all the way to the far fields."

"Then we find the willow," said Colette. The baby shifted against her and made a sound like a small complaint filed politely. "Quietly, please. All of you. Quietly."

"How?" asked Monsieur Perrin, gesturing with his whole self, which was difficult, having only one arm to gesture with. "How does one find a tree in soup?"

Filip didn't answer. He was sixteen and male and embarrassed, and I could feel him shrinking into himself the way boys do when they've been promoted to guide and discovered the promotion is a lie. And I understood — standing there in the water, fog beading on my eyelashes — that if I let him shrink, we would stand in this soup until the water finished its arithmetic with us.

So I became, for the duration of the night, the teacher. Which was ridiculous. I was nine years old and my most recent formal education involved copying the letter H next to the name of a woman named Honorine. But Theo says a leader is just the person who starts moving first, and I have noticed she is usually right, in the annoying way she is usually right.

"Hold the lantern low," I said. "Not up. Up is for looking at faces. Low is for looking at ground and water. Theo says so."

Filip looked at me like he wanted to argue and then, mercifully, remembered the fog outranked both of us. He lowered the shuttered lamp until its little coin of light lay on the black water.

"Now tell me everything about the willow," I said. "Not just split down the middle. Everything. Names stick better when they're fat."

He blinked. Then, slowly, like someone remembering he owned a room: "It leans east. The wind off the river pushed it all its life, so it leans, and the split trunk makes a shape like..." He hunted. "...like an M lying on its side."

An M. I nearly laughed. Of course it was an M. The whole night had been arranged by somebody with a sense of humor and a copybook.

"M for Marguerite," I said. "She's a nun. She has hands like paper and she gave me a boiled sweet once and told me God counts every sparrow, which I think means sparrows are in a ledger too. If we find a willow shaped like a sideways M, we follow the M."

* * *

You cannot walk straight in fog. Everybody believes they can walk straight. Everybody is wrong. Theo proved it to me last spring on the common: she blindfolded me and told me to walk toward the church bell, and I ended up walking a great proud curve directly into a hedge. Without a landmark, the body lies. It favors one leg, or one ear, or one fear, and off you go, curving politely toward disaster.

So we needed outside things. Fixed things. Things that would hold still and tell the truth.

We moved in a line, me first because I was lightest and because the water shallowed where I chose and deepened where I mischose, and the others learned fast to step in Amara-holes rather than make their own. Behind me, Filip held the lamp low. Behind him Colette with the baby, and last Monsieur Perrin, who kept up a low continuous mutter that I eventually realized was Virgil, or possibly Caesar, some old dead man writing about rivers, which struck me as showing off under the circumstances but also steadied him, so I let him.

And I counted aloud. Not loud-loud — Theo's voice-level, the one that carries exactly as far as it should and no farther — but steady. Steps and breaths. Fifty steps, rest. Fifty steps, listen.

At the second listening, we heard water moving where it shouldn't. Fast water, a chewing sound, off to our right. The breach, eating the causeway's bones.

"That way is death," I announced, in the tone Sister Marguerite uses for announcements about sin. "We go slightly left. Left of left."

"You're very certain," murmured Colette.

"No," I said honestly. "But certainty isn't required. Theo says you don't need to be sure, you need to be *useful*, and I know which side the chewing is on."

I felt rather than saw Monsieur Perrin nod. "Verba animi pendent," he muttered. Words hang on the mind. Or possibly he said something useful about willows. With teachers you never know.

* * *

Here is the game Theo invented, back when the shells came too close and my heart tried to climb out through my teeth. She called it *the long word*. She would give me a word — LANTERN, or CLOCKWORK, or MARSHMALLOW, that one being a lie since none existed in the town, but she said the wanting of it was the point — and I had to build it letter by letter in my head, and for each letter I had to find a person from her ledger whose name started with it, and hold their face until the next letter came. It takes a long time to spell MARSHMALLOW when every letter is a whole human being. By the second W you have forgotten to be afraid, because there is no room. Fear needs empty space to live in. Theo's game fills the space.

We walked, and I spelled.

L was Lucie, who had a gap in her smile wide enough to whistle through and whistled constantly, tunelessly, gloriously. A was Amadou, who was very tall and folded himself onto bales like a carpenter's rule. N was Noor, who spoke four languages and used all four to complain about the coffee. T was Théo herself, obviously, first and last, the T that all other T's answered to. E was Émile the bargeman's boy who could imitate a heron so well the herons got confused. R was Rosalie, pregnant and furious about it, in the way of women who intend to continue being magnificent regardless. N again, Noor again, she could come twice, it was my game.

By the time I finished LANTERN, my feet told me the ground had changed. Harder. Slightly risen. The water fell away from my ankles and the mud firmed to a crust that cracked instead of swallowing.

"Stop," I said. "Feel the ground. Is it higher?"

Filip crouched, touched the earth, and I heard something come back into his voice, something young and astonished: "This is the spine. This might be the spine. The bank rises here — Papa said the bank rises where the old orchard was—"

"Then we look for trees," I said. "Row or wild, doesn't matter. An orchard leaves ghosts."

* * *

We found the first ghost twenty minutes later, if you can find a thing that is mostly absence. A row of stumps and one half-dead survivor, black arms dripping fog, standing in a ragged line that could only have been planted by a person who believed in the future. Somebody had owned this ground once. Somebody had expected apples.

And beyond it — leaning east, split down the middle, exactly as fat with detail as Filip had promised — the willow.

The sideways M. Marguerite's letter, holding up the sky.

I am not going to pretend I behaved beautifully. I sat down right there in the mud and I cried for about a minute, hard and ugly, the way you're allowed to when a tree proves that the world occasionally keeps its promises. Nobody minded. Colette rubbed my back with her free hand. The baby slept on, scandalously.

"There now," said Monsieur Perrin, and for once quoted no one. "There now."

* * *

The dry spine ran true, just as Filip's father had promised, rising gently through the fog, and we followed it with the willow behind us and my counting going in front of me like a lantern of numbers. One hundred steps. Rest. Listen — the chewing water was fading behind us, which meant the breach was staying behind us, which meant left-of-left had been correct, and I allowed myself one small private moment of being insufferable about it.

At two hundred steps we heard voices ahead, and my whole body went stiff, because in 1917 voices in fog are a coin toss between rescue and the other thing. But then the voice resolved itself, and it was calling a number — "Sixty-one! Sixty-two!" — counting, someone ahead was *counting*, and I knew before I saw her silhouette that it was one of ours, one of Theo's work-party captains, counting the survivors onto the high ground the way you'd count books back onto a shelf.

"Seventy-three," I called back, because seventy-three happened to be true, seventy-three being my running total since the willow. "Amara Oyelaran-who-isn't-really-Oyelaran-but-is," which is what I call myself in my head on important occasions. "Three with me. All walking. Baby sleeping."

The silhouette made a sound that was laughing and sobbing holding hands. Hands reached out of the fog. Colette went first, baby passed carefully from dark to lantern-light. Then Monsieur Perrin, who immediately began telling everyone about Virgil. Then Filip, who paused beside me and said, awkwardly, sincerely, "You navigated like a bargeman, small one," which from him was a medal with honors.

I came out of the fog last, into the ring of lamps on the high field, and the first face I found was Theo's, and I watched it do a thing I will remember when I am old: it was composed, entirely composed, right up until it wasn't. She crossed the ground in four strides and knelt and held my face in her wet cold hands and looked at me the way she looks at pages of the ledger, like something that must not be lost.

"You counted," she said.

"I counted," I said. "Also I spelled. Also I taught a schoolteacher, a deckhand, and a grown woman how to walk in fog, and I found Marguerite's letter growing on a tree. You need to add three names to your book. Perrin, Colette, Filip. P, C, F. Easy ones."

Her laugh broke in the middle, the good kind of break. Behind her, out on the black water, the guns had started again — the rear guard on the far bank, holding the crossing, muzzle-flashes stuttering in the fog like a clock trying to remember the hour. And through the noise I heard someone shouting from the bank, an officer's voice gone raw, ordering the last columns back, and Theo turned toward the sound and her grip on my shoulders tightened by exactly one degree, which for Theo is a scream.

Someone was missing from the far side. Someone with a compass and an arithmetic and a debt.

"Stay here," Theo said, already rising. "Count the others. Keep them calm."

I sat down in the mud, gathered the nearest frightened strangers around me the way Theo gathers me, and began to teach them the game — *give me a word, any word, a long one* — while out in the fog, where the water chewed the broken causeway and the Colonel's last march was beginning, the counting in my chest went on and on and refused, absolutely refused, to stop.
