## 33. The Colonel's Last March

They took my command at noon on a Tuesday, which I have always considered an insult to Tuesdays. A colonel should be stripped of rank at dusk, or in the grey hour before dawn — some hour with dignity to it, some hour when the fog does half the work of the sentence. Noon on a Tuesday is the hour of requisition forms and bad coffee. It is the hour of clerks.

The major who delivered the order would not meet my eye, which told me two things: that he had read what was in the file, and that he understood it was not about me. A man ruined for cause looks you in the face and dares you to dispute it. A man ruined for convenience studies his boots.

"Your papers will be processed through channels," he said.

"Of course," I said. "Through channels. Everything goes through channels now. The river goes through channels. Perhaps it will be processed out of its bed by spring."

He left quickly after that. Wise man. Wit from a disgraced officer is like fire from a wet blanket — technically possible, deeply unwelcome.

I stood in the doorway of the commandeered schoolhouse that had been my headquarters for eleven months and looked at the map table where I had spent those months doing arithmetic. That is what command is, whatever the romance-writers say. It is arithmetic. Forty berths against four hundred souls. Three days of rations against nine days of siege. One bridge against one thaw. You move the numbers around until the sum stops screaming, and then you sign it, and then you sleep, or you don't sleep, and either way the numbers do not care whether you slept.

I had been very good at arithmetic. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in my own head, which may be the only archive still taking submissions by the time anyone reads anything. I was good at it because I believed the numbers were the truth and everything else — the weeping, the lantern-lighting, the nurse Oyelaran with her forbidden little book — was sentiment clouding the ledger.

I no longer believe that. But belief arrived late, the way reinforcements always do, and late arrivals are of limited use to the dead.

* * *

Let me set down how a colonel becomes a man carrying paper through a marsh, because it happened faster than you would credit.

The list. There was a list — there is always a list; show me a war without lists and I will show you a war nobody is winning — and my name was on it. Not the dishonourable-discharge list, though that came too, in triplicate, with a stamp so faint it might have been applied apologetically. The other list. The one General Marivaux's adjutant keeps in the black oilcloth folio that travels in the second staff car, the one I had seen once over a shoulder at Châlons and pretended not to see, because at Châlons my name was not on it and pretending not to see things was a professional competency of mine.

Expendables. That is the word used. Not casualties — casualties are incurred. Expendables are allocated.

My crime, as near as I could reconstruct it from the shape of the silence around me: the ledger. Nurse Oyelaran's ledger. Two hundred names the official rolls did not carry, names whose absence made certain requisitions possible and certain deaths deniable. I had known the ledger existed since autumn. I had offered her a bargain for it — two berths, hers and one more — and she had refused me with a look I am still paying interest on. I reported the ledger's existence upward, as procedure required. And procedure, apparently, required that the man who knew about inconvenient documents become himself an inconvenient document.

I laughed when I worked that out. Alone in the schoolhouse, packing nothing, since they let me keep nothing but my compass and my coat, I laughed until it hurt, which did not take long, because everything hurts now; I am fifty-six years old and my chest has been trying to resign since Verdun and I have been refusing the resignation on the grounds that the work needs doing.

The joke, for the record: I had spent a year deciding which names could be crossed off. Now someone had crossed mine off, using the same pencil, probably, and the same calm, and the same conviction that the numbers were the truth.

There is a particular quality to being eaten by your own machine. It is not betrayal. Betrayal implies the machine ever regarded you as anything other than fuel.

* * *

The town heard the news before I finished packing, because towns hear everything before governments finish typing it. I walked down to the quay to surrender my office keys to the quartermaster and the market women looked at me the way one looks at furniture that has been sold. A child — not Amara, one of the Dufort boys — threw a clod of frozen mud at me. It missed. Children in this town throw badly, thank God; I have signed enough lists of small coffins.

Nurse Oyelaran found me at the quay. Of course she did. She has a talent for finding men at their worst moments, it being her profession to stand next to them while they bleed.

"I heard," she said.

"You heard correctly."

"They gave you nothing?"

"They gave me a receipt for the office keys. Very thorough. Somewhere a clerk is proud."

She studied me the way she studied wounds, I thought — with that terrible unhurried attention, as if the thing in front of her might yet be saved if she refused to flinch at it. I had seen that attention turned on gangrene and on shell-shocked boys and once, memorably, on a major who tried to requisition her morphine for his own headaches. She did not flinch at me either.

"The marsh road," she said, quietly. "Odilon says low water comes Friday night. One tide's worth of causeway. You knew about the causeway."

"I knew it flooded twice a day," I said. "I knew it was mined in October and half-undone by frost since. I knew the engineers rated it passable for infantry in single file at low water, in good light, in fair weather, none of which God intends to provide." I handed her my compass before I had fully decided to. It is a good compass; it was my father's; sentiment is exactly the sort of clouding I claim to despise, so draw your own conclusions. "The needle sticks a degree east. Compensate or trust your stars."

"Why give me this? You owe me nothing. By your own arithmetic you owe me less than nothing."

"By my own arithmetic," I said, "you were right and I was wrong, and I find at my age that being wrong has a cost schedule too. This is the first installment."

She put the compass in her pocket without ceremony, which I appreciated. Ceremony would have been unbearable.

"And the ledger?" she asked. Too directly, the way she asks everything. "You reported it. They'll want it burned."

"The ledger," I said, "is the only honest document within thirty kilometres, which is why every man in uniform wants it destroyed, including, until recently, me." I looked at the river, brown and swollen and patient. "Keep it dry, nurse. Paper drowns easier than people, and it is harder to bring back."

* * *

Here is the part I did not tell her, because telling her would have changed her plans and her plans were better than mine.

The rear guard. When the evacuation column moves onto the causeway Friday night, someone must hold the far bank — the mill road, the culvert, the rise above the reed beds — because Marivaux's outriders are already on the ridge and they are not there to watch the scenery. The order assigning the rear guard went out Wednesday. It specified one officer of field rank, available immediately, unattached to any unit.

Unattached. Available. Words that describe a chair, or a disgraced colonel.

I could have refused. Refusal would have meant arrest, and arrest would have meant a cell in the citadel, and the cell would flood with the rest of the low quarter come the thaw, so really refusal was just drowning with extra paperwork. But I want it understood — by me, mostly, since I am the only reader this confession is likely to get — that I did not go to the far bank because the alternatives were worse. I went because the arithmetic finally balanced honestly. For eleven months I had moved numbers around until the sum stopped screaming. Here, at last, was a sum that could not be massaged: hold the culvert for three hours, and two hundred people cross a marsh in the dark; fail, and they die in the reeds with the water rising. No berths. No manifest. No clerk. Just a causeway, a compass I no longer owned, and whatever I am worth with a rifle at my age, which is some, but not much, and will have to do.

It is the first clean equation of my career. I intend to solve it.

* * *

Thursday I spent preparing, and Thursday taught me what the year should have.

The rear guard assembled in the timber yard: fourteen men, of whom six were walking wounded detailed back from the ward, three were local men past conscription age who volunteered when they heard what the guard was for, and five were soldiers like Sergeant Brochard — solid, silent, forty years old, the kind of sergeant armies run on and never mention in dispatches because mentioning sergeants would require admitting colonels are decorative.

Brochard looked at my bare collar where the insignia had been unpicked and said only, "You know the ground, sir."

"I know the ground."

"Then you're worth two of anyone they left attached to us." He spat, with feeling, toward the citadel. "Paper ranks. My lads'll follow whoever knows where the ditch is."

I nearly told him then. About the list, about Marivaux, about what my signature had done to manifests while he was teaching eighteen-year-olds to dig. It rose in my throat like floodwater. But a rear guard does not need its commander's confessions on the eve of the work; it needs him to point at the ditch. Confession is a luxury of survivors. I filed it where I file everything, in the drawer marked Later, which I begin to suspect has no bottom.

We walked the ground twice. The culvert under the mill road, choked with ice sheets that would crack under boot but hold a rifle-rest. The reed beds, waist-deep in water going soft at the edges — spring coming up from underneath, the whole valley thawing from below like a man feverish before the sweat breaks. The rise above it all, where fourteen rifles could make a column believe there were forty.

On the second circuit, one of the volunteers — Perrin, the cooper, sixty-one years old with hands like tree roots — asked me, not unkindly: "Why you, Colonel? No offence meant. But a man with no rank, no unit — why does he take the worst post?"

Because my name was on a list, I almost said. Because I built the machine that built the list. Because every man on that causeway tomorrow night is a number I once rounded down, and this is the only arithmetic left to me.

"Because I know where the ditch is," I said.

Perrin nodded slowly, satisfied, and I learned something else Thursday: that men will accept almost any answer if you give it without flinching. Oyelaran knows that too. It may be the whole of nursing. It may be the whole of command. It may be the whole difference between us, that she uses the knowledge to pull bullets and I used it to balance books.

* * *

Friday came down grey and windless, the worst possible sky, because a windy night scatters sound and a clear one shows starlight, and grey gives us neither. Odilon's signal — a green lamp shown twice from the barge mast — came at half past ten. Low water held until roughly four. Six hours' margin, minus the crossing itself, minus everything.

The column began forming at the marsh gate at eleven. I saw them from the rise: lanterns hooded to a coin of light each, two hundred shadows in single file, and at the head of the line a lamp I recognized — Oyelaran, with the child beside her. The girl was counting. I could not hear her at that distance, but I could see her lips moving, and I understood she was counting steps aloud to keep the fear down, and I thought: there is the entire war, right there. A child counting, an army listening, a nurse leading both across a drowned road while grown men with maps and ministries decide from ridgelines whether any of them deserve to arrive.

And in my breast pocket, wrapped in oilcloth inside a hymnal I had bought from Sister Marguerite that afternoon for an outrageous price she accepted without haggling — the pages. Not the whole ledger. Oyelaran kept the book itself; I carried what Marguerite and I had copied out over two nights by stove-light while the ward slept: every omitted name, every date, every note of Theo's hand that proved what the official rolls denied. Two hundred and eleven names. Eleven died waiting; I wrote those in the margins with a cross beside each, because a list of the dead that pretends the dead aren't on it is just another manifest, and I am finished with manifests.

If I fell, the pages were to reach Odilon, and Odilon would trade them to whichever army's press paid best, and the names would become true by becoming public. That was the plan, such as it was. Plans are arithmetic too. Mine had a single term left in it: survive until the last stranger crosses.

At midnight the shooting started on the ridge, earlier than Marivaux's timetable and therefore exactly according to character — the man punctual only in his appetites — and the rear guard went to the culvert.

I will not write the battle. Battles are the one genre more dishonest than memoirs. I will write only this: the culvert held for two hours, then ninety minutes more, which is two hours and ninety minutes longer than my original estimate, and I attribute the surplus entirely to Brochard, who repositioned our wounded riflemen three times with the tenderness of a man arranging furniture, and to Perrin, who loaded rifles with his tree-root hands and sang under his breath — a cooper's song, something about hoops and staves — the whole time, softly, as if the barrels were barrels and this were an ordinary Tuesday in an ordinary yard.

Noon on a Tuesday. I keep circling back to it. Let the record show that some Tuesdays are worth more than a colonel.

* * *

The flare went up at twenty past three and showed me the causeway, and what I saw on it I will carry wherever I am going, assuming anywhere receives me.

They were crossing. All along the drowned road between the reeds, the hooded lanterns strung out like beads on a black thread, single file, patient, the child's count audible now on the wind — I could hear her, actually hear her, thin and steady as a bell buoy — and behind them, further back than we had hoped, the last stragglers: a pregnant woman supported between two others, an old priest, a boy of maybe twelve driving a handcart of somebody's salvage because even in exodus people cannot leave their things, and God forgive me, I loved them for it.

And then the middle of the line stopped, and the bead-thread broke, and a shout came up the causeway in Oyelaran's voice — one word, I couldn't take it, and didn't need to.

The causeway had breached. The water was coming through. The arithmetic had turned again, the way it always turns, and the sum was screaming.

"Brochard!" I called. "Last push — they need darkness longer! Everything you have left!"

He grinned at me, that old sergeant, teeth white in the flare-light. "Everything, sir. Been saving it."

What we did in the next minutes I did with a rifle I had taken from a dead boy in October and never cleaned properly, and I did it shouting orders in a voice I had not used since I had a rank to hang them on, and it worked, the voice worked, the men worked, the culvert held one more hour against men who wanted very much to kill us, and during that hour I felt — I commit this to paper knowing how it sounds — I felt *useful*. Not powerful. Useful. The smaller word. The better one. Power is what you exercise on manifests; usefulness is what you spend on ditches, and of the two, only one gets remembered by the people in the reeds.

The last of the strangers crossed — I saw the handcart go, saw the pregnant woman's lantern vanish into the far reeds, saw Oyelaran's lamp turn once back toward us, toward the noise of our firing, a single arc of light like a salute or a summons — and I put the hymnal higher inside my coat, against the chest that has been trying to resign for years, and said to Brochard:

"Now we go. Now we earn our own crossing."

But the water was up at the near end of the causeway already, black and moving, and the far bank was three hundred metres of reed and mud away, and behind us the ridge line flickered with answering fire, and somewhere in the fog between here and there, I could hear — faintly, terribly — a rope creaking, and a man's voice anchoring something, holding something, the way sergeants hold things.

I started running for the causeway with two hundred names against my heart, and the water rising to meet me.
