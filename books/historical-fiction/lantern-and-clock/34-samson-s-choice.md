## 34. Samson's Choice

The rope was the only honest thing on that river.

Everything else that night had been negotiation — Odilon's causeway holding by a promise rather than engineering, the fog negotiating its own terms, the water rising an inch every time I let myself believe we'd won. But the guide rope Samson had strung from the far piling to the near bank, knotted every six feet so a hand could walk it blind, that rope told the truth: hold here, move forward, do not let go.

I stood at the near end of it, lantern raised, counting people out of the dark and into the light. Amara stood beside me on the bank with her tally-slate, because I had promised her a job and because a child who counts is a child who is not drowning in fear. Behind us, the high ground filled with wet, shivering, astonishingly alive people.

"Forty-one," Amara said, and made her mark.

The fog came off the marsh in slow white tongues, and out of it came voices — some calling names, some singing snatches of hymns they didn't believe in, one man reciting what sounded like a laundry list, which I understood completely. Fear makes accountants of us all. We count what we can carry.

* * *

Samson had tied himself to the middle of the rope.

I want to be precise about this, because for years afterward people told the story wrong — that he sacrificed himself, that he leapt, that he died a hero in the manner heroes die in the pamphlets. He did not leap. He tied himself in with a bowline he must have learned from some dockhand before either of us could write our own names, and he anchored the frayed section where the causeway had given way, and when the breach widened and the current took the crossing apart under the last columns' feet, he became the bridge.

That was my brother all over. He never once in his life did the dramatic thing. He did the necessary thing, thoroughly, at his own expense, and then apologized for the mud on the floor.

He had come back into my life six years too late and exactly on time, which was the whole tragedy of him in a single sentence. The theft — my theft, the surgical kit I took from St. Aubin's stores, the career-ending offense he confessed to so that I could keep my hands and my vocation — we had finally said it aloud two nights ago in the lamplight of the ward tent, both of us crying like children, neither of us absolved, both of us absolved, which is the same thing. And then the flood had come, and there had been no more time for anything but work.

Work was the language we shared. Not letters — we were terrible at letters. Work.

* * *

From the bank I watched the last column cross.

There were eleven of them in that final group, strung along the causeway like beads on a thread that kept trying to snap. The water had come up over the stones in the hour since we began — Odilon had promised us one night of low water and the river, as rivers do, had read the fine print and found a loophole. Each figure moved bent double against the current, one hand on the rope, and each time the rope jerked — and it jerked often now, a living thing in spasm — I saw the middle section dip and rise, dip and rise, held down by my brother's weight and his will.

Odilon crouched beside me at the waterline, too old for this and doing it anyway. "The causeway's gone at the third span," he shouted over the water. "Anyone still past the third span comes across on the rope or not at all."

"How many are past it?"

"All eleven."

I looked at the rope. It ran taut into the fog, and somewhere in the middle of it hung a man I had hated for six years and loved for thirty-one, keeping two hundred strangers alive by being heavier than the river wanted him to be.

"Then we hold the rope," I said, "and we pull."

We pulled. That is the whole of my part in it. Odilon and I and three bargemen hauled on the near end while the eleven came hand over hand, and one by one they arrived on our bank — a laundress first, then two soldiers, then an old schoolteacher who kissed the mud when she reached us, then children, then more soldiers, then a man carrying a goat, which I have decided not to explain because no explanation would improve it.

"Eight," Amara called. "Nine. Ten."

The eleventh was a boy of about fourteen, half-carried by the soldier behind him, and he was ten feet from the causeway's end when the rope went slack.

Not snapped. Slack. There is a difference, and the difference was Samson letting it go loose deliberately, feeding line, because the boy had been swept off the stones and the current was dragging him downstream and a tight rope would have dashed him against the pilings. So my brother played out rope like a fisherman playing a fish, paying himself out with it, tethered to the piling, taking the boy's weight onto his own knot, and the water rose around them both and the fog closed like a door.

Amara stopped counting. Her slate trembled.

"Theo," she said, in the smallest voice I had ever heard her use, "where is Uncle Samson?"

Uncle. When had that happened? Somewhere between the hymnal and the raft, between the alphabet lessons and the night he taught her to whistle through a blade of grass, my brother had become hers without either of them filing the paperwork.

"He's holding," I said. "Keep your eyes on the water. Tell me the moment you see his lantern."

Because he carried a lantern. Of course he carried a lantern — he had wired it to the rope's midpoint with baling wire, a lamp swinging above the worst of the breach, so the crossing parties could see where death was and step around it. It burned out there in the fog like a low, drowned star.

The boy came ashore at last, hauled in by four pairs of arms, coughing the river out of himself. Eleven of eleven. Every soul.

I turned to shout it toward the middle of the rope — *all across, Samson, all across, come home* — and that was when I felt it again. The slack. Worse now. The rope hanging loose as a clothesline, and beyond the fog, no rhythm in it, no tug of a man hauling himself hand over hand toward the bank.

* * *

Here is what I know happened next, assembled afterward from Odilon, from the soldier who had carried the boy, and from the rope itself, which I unwound from the piling days later and kept until I couldn't bear to keep it anymore.

The current had taken the third-span piling. The whole anchorage shifted — Odilon heard it go, a sound like a giant clearing his throat underwater. Samson, still knotted in, went sideways into the flood with the rope's end, and the bowline held, and the river began to drown him at the length of his own tether.

He had a knife. Every labor-battalion man carried one. He could have cut himself free at any moment in those first minutes and swum for it — Odilon swore the water near the bank was survivable, barely, if you came up fast and grabbed the willows. Instead, feeling the piling fail, feeling the rope go slack in his hands, he had done the last piece of work available to him: he had taken the loose end in his teeth — his hands were needed to keep the knife-hand from freezing — and he had hauled it, inch by inch, arm over arm, back up to the second piling, the one that still stood. And there he had re-tied it. A bowline. Six feet of knots.

The rope we were pulling from the bank went suddenly taut, and Odilon laughed once, a bark of disbelief. "He's re-rigged it! The madman has re-rigged the line!"

For one full minute — I counted, because counting is what my family does instead of praying — the rope lived. It trembled and strained and I could feel him at the other end of it, the way you can feel a heartbeat through a wall, and I hauled and shouted his name into the fog until my voice tore.

Then the knots came undone.

Not cut. Undone. He had tied them wet, in the dark, with hands that had been in freezing water for the better part of two hours, and the final knot simply gave — or he let it give, having judged, correctly, that a dead man's grip on a rope was worth less than a live rope's reach to the shore, and that the eleven on our side and the hundred and ninety-odd already up the hill no longer required him to be the anchor. His work was finished. My brother had never in his life left a job half done, and he did not begin that night.

The rope came to me coiled and dripping, forty feet of it, and the lantern went out.

* * *

No. That is the version I told Amara, later, kneeling on the bank with her face pressed into my coat. The lantern did not go out. Lanterns do not go out; they sink, and the flame drowns, and there is a difference, and the difference is that I saw it.

I was at the waterline when the current brought him past. Not him — I never saw him again, not his face, not his hands, not the great stupid gentle bulk of him that had carried our mother's coffin up four flights of stairs at nineteen and never once mentioned it after. What I saw was the lantern, torn loose from the wire, riding the flood surface for perhaps ten yards in the gray wash of dawn's first suggestion.

It floated upright. That is the detail that will finish me, someday, when I am old enough to be finished by such things: it floated upright, the way he had rigged everything in his life to float upright, steady as a pulpit, burning its small yellow defiance against the brown water. And then the river folded it under — gently, almost politely, the way you close a book you have finished reading — and the light showed through the water for one instant, a blurred coin of gold sinking, and then there was only the river, going about its business.

I waded in after it.

I remember Odilon's arms around my waist, and I remember screaming words I will not record even in my own ledger, and I remember that the water was so cold it stopped being cold and became a kind of argument I was losing. Three men pulled me back. I fought them. I am a nurse; I know precisely how long a body can survive in February water, and I knew it to the second, and I spent every one of those seconds fighting men who were only trying to keep the count of the living from getting smaller.

When they finally dragged me ashore, the sun had come up enough to make the fog gray-gold, and the river ran on, flat, indifferent, glittering like something innocent.

Amara stood at the edge of the crowd with her slate hugged to her chest. She had not cried. She was looking at the water with the expression she wore when counting backwards — lips moving, eyes fixed, holding herself together with arithmetic.

I got my legs under me and walked to her, because there was no one else, because that is what family is — the ones who walk to you when walking is impossible.

"Thirty-one steps," I said. My voice came out in pieces. "From the ward tent to the lantern post. Thirty-one. You used to count them."

She nodded. Her chin crumpled. She held the slate out to me with both hands, the way you surrender a weapon.

On it, in her careful new handwriting — the letters I had taught her from the ledger's pages, from the names of the omitted — was written:

*SAMSON OYELARAN. 41 crossed. 1 stayed.*

"I wrote him first," she said. "Marguerite said whoever goes in the book first is in the book forever."

And that is when I finally sat down in the mud of the riverbank, in front of two hundred saved strangers and the corpse of my whole childhood, and made a sound I did not know I owned.

* * *

They asked me afterward — the officers, later, the relief colonels, the committees — how many were lost in the crossing. One, I told them. One man lost. They wrote it down, one, a rounding error in their arithmetic, acceptable losses, Vasseur's cold sums come home to roost on the wrong ledger.

But the truth is a number I keep separately. One man lost, yes. And one berth — the last berth on the last barge, the one he refused, the one he insisted belonged to Amara, the one I had tried to force on him and he had refused again with that maddening, immovable gentleness — answered at last. All those nights we had argued over who deserved to live, and the river settled the question in nine minutes, and the answer was: none of us. None of us deserved it. That was never the currency the ferryman took. He took only what we loved.

I stood at the water's edge until my boots froze stiff, looking for him. That is the shameful, human, unheroic fact of grief: you stand there looking for a hat, a boot, a shoulder breaking the surface, some scrap you can bury, because a grave with nothing in it is a wound that won't close. The river gave me nothing. It gave me back my own reflection, distorted, and I thought: he would say I look tired. He would put his coat over my shoulders and apologize for the mud.

Behind me, up the slope, the survivors had begun to move — Marguerite's voice organizing the injured into rows, Odilon cursing at wreckage, the goat complaining on principle. The machinery of the living, grinding back into gear because it must.

And then Amara touched my hand.

"Theo," she said. "You have to tell me the rest of the names."

I looked at her. Fog, frost, slate, child.

"The ones that burned," she said. "The pages from the fire. You said you remembered most of them. Well—" and here her chin came up, and I saw my own stubbornness wearing her small face like a borrowed coat, "—then you'll say them, and I'll write them, and nobody gets to be omitted twice."

The river ran on behind us, keeping its secrets. I took the pencil stub from my pocket. It was wet. Everything was wet. It would have to do; everything would have to do, from now on, forever, because that was the world we had chosen over the barge.

"Alphonse Devereux," I said. "Aged fifty-one. Cooper. Left-handed."

Amara wrote.

And up the hill, through the thinning fog, someone began to sing — not a hymn, not yet, just a working song, the kind Samson's battalion had sung over their rafts and ropes — and I realized my brother had gotten exactly what he always wanted, though I would have given anything to spare him the price of it: everyone he loved, safe on the high ground, building something.
