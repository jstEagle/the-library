## 35. After the Water

Dawn came up the color of dishwater, and I have never been so glad to see anything so ugly in my life.

We had come off the causeway in ones and twos and small wet huddles, the way a basket of laundry spills, and by the time the light was strong enough to count by, we had collected ourselves on the high ground above the vine terraces — perhaps two hundred souls, spread across the frost-stiff grass like the aftermath of some enormous, indifferent laundry day. Coats steaming. Boots squelching. Somewhere down the slope a man was being sick into a hedge, and somewhere else a woman was laughing in the high, cracked way that is either joy or its opposite, and no one who heard her could have said which.

I stood at the top of the terrace with my back to a dry-stone wall and took inventory of myself first, because that is the nurse's rule: you are useless to anyone until you know what you are missing. One boot. The other had gone to the river somewhere past the third marker and had not been mourned. My left sleeve was torn from wrist to elbow and I could not remember why. My hands were cut in four places and shaking in a fifth way entirely. And my ledger — I drew it out of my coat with the care one reserves for a wounded bird.

The water had found it. Of course the water had found it; the water had found everything. The cover held its shape, that grey government-issue cardboard, but when I opened it the pages came apart in my fingers like wet leaves, ink blooming outward in grey clouds until every name dissolved into every other name. Vermeulen ran into Okonkwo ran into the three Laurent sisters ran into a child whose name I had written in June with a pencil stub because I had nothing else. Two years of the omitted, blurred into weather.

I am not ashamed to say I sat down on that wall and did nothing at all for a full minute. Grief wants a chair sometimes.

It was Amara who brought me back, because Amara has never once in her life let me sit in grief longer than grief deserves.

"Theo." She was beside me, soaked to the ribs, hair plastered to her skull, holding the copybook against her chest with both arms the way she'd held it through the whole crossing, and by some miracle — by whatever arithmetic children do that adults cannot — the copybook was nearly dry. "Theo, how many did we bring?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then we should count."

* * *

Counting the living is a strange kind of prayer, and I recommend it to anyone who finds conventional prayer insufficient. You stand where they can see you — on the wall, in my case, one-booted and ridiculous — and you call out, and they answer, and each answer is a small resurrection.

"Form lines!" I shouted, and my voice came out ragged but it carried; two years of shouting over shelling teaches you register. "By families if you know your families, by wards if you know your wards, by nothing at all if you know nothing! Lines! The counting will go faster if you stand still!"

They stood still. People who have crossed a flooded causeway in the dark are remarkably compliant about standing still.

Amara appointed herself tally-keeper without consulting anyone, which surprised none of us. She sat cross-legged on the wall with the copybook open on her knees and a pencil — my pencil, the stub from June, which she had carried too — and she made her marks in careful fives, four strokes and the crossing slash, and every few minutes she called the running total aloud in her clear flat voice, the same voice she'd used on the causeway counting steps: "Thirty-one. Forty-four. Sixty-two."

Odilon the bargeman organized the far end of the line with the authority of a man who has spent his life loading cargo and considers people merely cargo that complains. Marguerite moved among the soaked ranks doing what she does — triage by touch, sorting the shivering from the genuinely dying with two fingers on a pulse. I watched her pause over an old man, watch her stay there a moment too long, watch her close his eyes herself before moving on, and I understood that our count would need a second column eventually. But not yet. Dawn counts are for the living.

"Seventy-eight," said Amara.

The names came slowly, in the order people reached me. A cooper named Bastien who had carried a stranger's mother most of the way. The laundress Marguerite had given her berth for, huge with child, insisting between contractions that she was fine, absolutely fine, she simply wished to inform everyone that she would personally kill the father upon reaching dry land, which raised a weak cheer. Three of Vasseur's rear-guard soldiers, muddy, unarmed, uncertain whether they were prisoners or refugees, deciding after some debate that they were refugees.

"Ninety-one."

I counted them and looked at each face as it passed, because looking is part of the work. And underneath the looking, unbidden, the ledger began reading itself to me. That is the only way I can describe it. The ruined book in my coat was dead, but the book behind my eyes had survived the flood entire, and it began turning its own pages.

*Adeyemi, Joseph, wheelwright, wife Celestine, daughter b. 1914.* They were in line three, all three of them. *Vermeulen, Pieter, and his sister's boys.* There — the tall boy holding the small boy's collar like a handle, the way he'd done since summer. *Okonkwo, David, stoker, left hand crushed, left hand very much present this morning and waving at me.*

Two hundred names. I had written two hundred names the officers' rolls did not hold, and here on this hillside stood — Amara's pencil scratched on — a good number of them, breathing steam into the cold air.

"Ninety-nine," said Amara. "One hundred. Theo. One hundred."

"One hundred," I agreed.

"That's a hundred marks." She held up the copybook to show me, and there it was: twenty neat bundles of five, more orderly than anything else in that valley. "Is a hundred good?"

I looked out over the terraces, at the line still forming, at the fog still sitting on the river below us like something patient, at the empty space on the wall beside me where Samson should have been leaning, arms crossed, pretending not to be proud of me.

"It's a beginning," I said, which was true, and also a lie, and also the only answer that would let us both keep working.

* * *

By midmorning the count stood at a hundred and sixty-three, the dead at eleven, and the missing at a number no one wanted to say aloud, though everyone's arithmetic kept arriving at it privately. We built fires from vine-cuttings and wrecked fence posts. Marguerite commandeered a barn. Odilon, who apparently cannot be in possession of any object without immediately negotiating its value, had already established a barter economy centered on dry socks.

And I sat by the largest fire with the drowned ledger open on my knees, and I began to recite.

I hadn't planned it. It started because Amara climbed onto the wall beside me, opened her copybook to a fresh page, and asked, in the practical tone she uses for matters of fact: "If yours is broken, should mine be the real one now?"

And I thought about Marguerite asking to be recorded first in whatever book comes next. And I thought about Vasseur going back toward the far bank with the rescued pages stuffed inside his coat, asking only that the ledger survive him. And I thought about my brother under the river, who had given his berth away twice over — once for a girl, once for a principle — and who had never once in six years asked me to write his name anywhere.

"Yes," I said. "Yours is the real one now. So we had better get it right."

I closed my eyes, and the pages turned.

"Adeyemi, Joseph. Adeyemi, Celestine. Adeyami—" I stopped, corrected myself. "Adeyemi, Beatrice, born March 1914. Write it exactly so."

Amara wrote it exactly so. Her letters were the ones I had taught her on slow evenings in the dressing station, lantern-lit, the ledger open between us like a scripture neither of us fully believed in but both of us served. Her hand was rounder than mine. It would improve. It had time to improve, which was the entire point of everything.

"Vermeulen, Pieter. Vermeulen, Tomas. Vermeulen, Henrik."

"Theo," Amara said, pencil pausing. "Some of these people are right there. By the second fire. Can't we just ask them?"

"We can and we will confirm every name against its owner before this book is done. But memory fails faster than paper, and paper has already failed us once today. So first we write what I carry, then we correct it against the living. Both books. Always both."

She considered this, nine years old, wrapped in someone's donated blanket like a small disapproving owl. "Whose names are hardest?"

An excellent question, and one I answered honestly, because she has never once accepted anything less.

"The ones I wrote fastest. The night the ward burned, I lost half my record to smoke and water, and the names I rewrote from memory that night — those I know cold. But the ones I scribbled between bandages, in June, in August, when a man gave me his name once and died before I could ask him to spell it — those are the pages where the ink was thin. Those are the ones the flood took clean." I turned a ruined page to show her: grey blooms, ghost-lines, a name like a cloud. "This one. Here. Do you see anything?"

"No."

"Neither do I. But I remember he was a bargeman's son, sixteen, and he told me his name while I held his hand closed over a wound, and he pronounced it twice because he saw I hadn't caught it. Delacroix? Delacourt? Dela—something. He was from upriver. His mother wore green on Sundays; he mentioned her twice."

Amara put down her pencil and looked at me with an expression I have learned to fear, because it is the expression of a person about to say something unbearably simple.

"So we find his mother," she said.

"What?"

"If we don't know his name, we find his mother. Mothers know." She picked the pencil back up. "You always say a name is a person agreeing to be counted. Well. He agreed. We just have to go collect it."

I have delivered babies, held dying men through their last hours, falsified military manifests, and led two hundred souls across a flooded marsh in the dark, and I confess that being out-argued by a nine-year-old beside a vineyard fire was the moment I came nearest to weeping in front of witnesses. I did not weep. I am English enough for that, wherever my family actually came from. But I took off my remaining boot, dried my foot by the fire, and continued the recitation with a clearer throat than I deserved.

* * *

We worked through the morning and into the afternoon. I recited; Amara wrote; the survivors drifted past in ones and twos, confirming, correcting, adding. *Okonkwo, David* waved at his own name being rewritten, which he declared the strangest compliment of his life. The laundress went into labor in Marguerite's barn around noon, and the sound of the newborn's cry came across the yard right as I recited the last of the June names, and nobody at the fire pretended not to notice the timing. Amara added a line at the bottom of her page — *baby girl, born on the hill, name pending* — and informed me gravely that pending was a real word and she would be using it more.

At dusk I reached the names I had been circling all day the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

"Oyelaran," I said. "Samson. Labor battalion, second company. Born—" and I stopped, because I could hear him correcting me, *you always get the month wrong, Odile, it's September, September,* six years of silence and he still sounded like my little brother in the middle of my own head. "Born September. He gave his berth away. Then he gave his life away, holding a rope, and the rope did its work, so the transaction completed. Write it exactly so."

Amara wrote it exactly so. Then she wrote something beneath it, unprompted, and turned the book so I could read: *first name in the next book.*

"That's Marguerite's line," I said.

"Marguerite can share. She told me so this afternoon. She says names aren't bread."

No. Names are not bread. Names are the opposite of bread; they are the thing you can carry infinitely and never lighten, the cargo that weighs nothing and holds everything, and I had spent two years learning that lesson in a stolen notebook while officers' lists grew shorter and shorter, pruning the world down to what could be officially alive.

I looked at Amara's page: a hundred and sixty-three living confirmed, eleven dead recorded, the rest marked *pending*, that magnificent word. I looked at the drowned ledger, finally, deliberately, closing it for the last time. And I understood that I had not lost it after all. A ledger is not paper. A ledger is a chain of custody for the truth, and the truth had simply changed hands — from my book to hers, from my memory to her alphabet, from the woman who wrote alone by lantern light to the child who counted backwards and forwards and would one day count better than I ever had.

"Theo," Amara said, as the fires came up around the terrace, dozens of small lights against the dark, the town's lantern custom rebuilt on a hillside by reflex. "What happens tomorrow?"

Tomorrow. Relief forces were rumored to the north; Odilon had opinions about the rumor's price; Marguerite had already begun organizing the barn into a proper ward; somewhere downriver officials would soon come asking questions, wanting records, wanting tidy lists that matched their tidy omissions.

"Tomorrow," I said, "we keep counting."

But even as I said it, the first thing I saw beyond the firelight was a column of dust on the north road — too disciplined for refugees, too early for relief — moving toward our hillside with the unhurried confidence of people who believe they are the ones who do the counting.

Amara saw it too. She closed the copybook and held it tighter.
