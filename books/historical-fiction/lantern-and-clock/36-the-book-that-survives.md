## 36. The Book That Survives

The relief column came up from the north on a Tuesday, though I only knew it was a Tuesday because Amara had counted the days since the causeway on her fingers and then on her toes and then, when she ran out of toes, on the knuckles of Sister Marguerite's patience.

"Twenty-six," she announced at breakfast, which was coffee without coffee in it. "Twenty-six days since the water."

Since the water. As if the flood were a guest who had finally left. I suppose there were worse ways to think of it. There were certainly worse guests.

I heard the trucks before I saw them — that particular grinding of engines that has forgotten what roads are — and stood in the doorway of what had been the dressing station and was now a roof with opinions, watching six lorries and an ambulance and a staff car crawl down the ridge road with the cautious dignity of men who had been told the war was over here but weren't sure anyone had informed the mud.

The town came out to look. That was the thing about us by then: we had become a town that looked at things instead of doing them, because most things worth doing had been done or drowned. Two hundred and some souls on the high ground, thinner than we'd been in autumn, wearing each other's coats. Odilon's barge, salvaged and beached like a whale with ambitions. The bridge, or the idea of the bridge — three piers and a memory.

The staff car stopped where the road gave out. A major got out, then a captain, then a clerk with a portable desk strapped to his back like a snail that had gone through officer training. The major had a clean face and a map case and the expression of a man who intended to solve us before lunch.

"Theo Oyelaran?" he said, consulting a paper. "Nurse? Acting matron? It says here you've been — " he turned the paper as if it might make more sense upside down — "administering."

"I've been feeding people," I said. "And counting them."

"Counting." He said the word the way a man says *water* when he's found some. "Good. Excellent. We need records. Full records. Casualty returns, civilian rolls, evacuation manifests, dispensary logs — everything. Nothing came out of this sector since October. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Miss Oyelaran? On paper, this valley is empty."

I looked past him at the line of people on the rise. At Marguerite with her arm around the pregnant laundress, whose baby was now a fact rather than a rumor. At Odilon arguing with one of his own planks. At Amara, who was pretending not to listen and hearing every word, because she had learned that from me.

"On paper," I said, "it probably is."

* * *

They set up the clerk's portable desk in the chapel, which still had a roof and therefore qualified as headquarters. The captain laid out forms. There were forms for everything, including, I believe, a form for requesting further forms. He explained the system to me twice, with the serene confidence of a man explaining a river to a fish.

Official casualty returns. Official evacuation manifests. Official civilian registers, cross-referenced against brigade rolls.

"And these are authoritative," he said, tapping the brigade rolls. "Every man who passed through this sector should appear here."

"Should," I said, and let the word sit between us like a coin on a table.

He waited for me to pick it up. I didn't.

Here is what I had learned in twenty-six days, and in the months before that: the truth does not need me to argue for it. It only needs me to produce it at the right moment, in front of the right people, and then step back. For weeks I had been waiting for this moment the way you wait for a tide — not impatiently, because impatience changes nothing about water — and now it had come, smelling of engine oil and carrying a portable desk.

But I am getting ahead of myself, and Amara would correct my count.

That first afternoon they wanted the obvious things. Dispensary figures — I had those, or the memory of them, in vial-counts and lamp-oil measures, since the town clock had died sometime in November and time had become a rumor we agreed not to spread. Rations issued. Burials. I gave them what they asked, plainly, and watched the clerk transcribe my arithmetic into his arithmetic, and noticed how the numbers shrank slightly in translation, the way names shrink when they pass through official hands. By the time my forty-one burials became his thirty-eight, I had stopped being surprised. I had simply made a note of it, in the place where I kept notes now, which was everywhere and nowhere.

The major appeared toward dusk. He had spent the day being shown the flood line by local guides who took him carefully past the places where the flood line was also a death line, and he had the particular grayness of a man doing subtraction he didn't enjoy.

"Miss Oyelaran. They tell me you got a good many people out overland. After the bridge."

"Some," I said.

"Through the marshes." He said *marshes* the way the earlier major had said *counting*. "Against standing orders, I might add. The sector was to hold for organized evacuation."

"The sector," I said, "was underwater."

"Yes." He looked at his boots, which were muddy, which meant he had seen. "Well. Orders are written by people who haven't stood in the sector." Which was either an apology or an alibi, and I have learned not to press men on which, because they usually don't know themselves.

Then he said the thing I had been waiting three weeks to hear:

"We'll want your records. All of them. Anything documenting who was here, who was evacuated, who was — " he paused, choosing — "unaccounted for. There will be an inquiry. There are questions being asked in very high places about why certain sectors were written off." His voice went flat and careful. "Certain *populations* written off. Someone kept better books than the army did, apparently. Someone always does. We'd like to find them."

I thought: *You have.*

I thought: *She is nine years old and she is washing her shirt in the river.*

I said: "Come back tomorrow."

* * *

That night I did not sleep, which was unremarkable; I hadn't reliably slept since November. But for once it wasn't grief keeping me up. It was stage fright.

Because here was the problem, and I lay in the dark turning it over like a stone with something underneath: the book that mattered was not mine anymore. That was the whole point of it. My ledger — the real ledger, the one I had kept through the autumn in defiance of every shortened list — had gone into the water with Vasseur's rear guard and come out of it ruined, its pages swollen into a single brick of pulp, most of its ink dissolved into the river along with the colonel himself. I had grieved for it properly, kneeling on the bank, and then I had done the only thing left to do with a dead book.

I had spoken it.

All those evenings on the high ground, with the survivors huddled under tarps and the frost making its opinions known, I had sat with Amara by the lantern and recited names while she wrote them in a copybook she had made from ration-paper sewn with thread pulled from the hem of her one good dress. She had insisted on sewing it herself. I had offered needle and thread and she had accepted the thread but not the hands; the girl understood, even at nine, especially at nine, that a thing you make yourself belongs to you in a way nothing given ever can.

Page by page, name by name, spelling corrected and misspelled and corrected again. She had known some of the names already, because I had taught her her letters from the ledger itself, back when the ledger was alive — had taught her to read by the dead, which sounds like a curse and was in practice a psalm. She could recite the first thirty names faster than I could. She had added her own annotations, in the merciless pencil of a child: *good whistler* beside one soldier, *gave me orange peel* beside another, *told bad lie about chickens* beside Odilon's cousin.

So the question that kept me awake was not *would the officials accept the record.* It was *whose record was it?*

It was hers. That was the answer, and it terrified me, and it was the only answer I could live with.

In the morning I found her at the water trough and asked, formally, because the moment deserved form: "Amara. The major wants the book. The one with the names. My names — the ones you copied."

She wrung out her shirt and considered me with the level gaze she reserved for matters of consequence, which included chickens, rivers, and justice.

"It's my book," she said. "You said so. You said whoever writes the names keeps the names."

"I did say that."

"So they can look at it." She hung the shirt on the fence with two clothespins, taking care, aligning the seams. "But I show it to them. Not you. Because I know all the names. If they ask questions, I can answer." She turned and looked at me fully. "You cry at some of them."

"I do not cry at — "

"*Émile Fournier,*" she said. "Page four. You do the swallowing thing."

There was no answer to that, so I gave none.

* * *

The chapel smelled of candle wax and wet plaster. The major sat behind the portable desk with the captain on his right and the clerk on his left, and beyond them, because word travels fast in a town of two hundred souls, perhaps half the survivors had gathered in the doorway and the broken pews — Odilon with his cap in his hands, Marguerite straight-backed in her graying habit, the laundress with her great belly and her small smile. They had not been told to come. They had simply come, the way people come to a verdict, because that is what this was, whatever the paperwork called it.

Amara walked up the aisle with the copybook held against her chest with both arms, the way children carry things that are heavier than paper. She wore her hair braided because I had braided it, badly, that morning, and she had declined to fix it on the grounds that it was evidence I tried.

She set the book on the desk. The major reached for it. She moved it two inches away from his hand — not far, just far enough, a negotiation conducted entirely in inches — and said:

"You have to wash your hands first. Some of the pages have blood on them already and it shouldn't get confused with new blood."

Somewhere in the pews, Odilon made a sound he converted into a cough at the last moment.

The major looked at the captain. The captain looked at the ceiling. Then the major produced a handkerchief, spat on it, scrubbed his fingers with the gravity of a surgeon, and nodded.

She opened the book herself. And I will tell you what I saw, because I was standing at the rail where witnesses stand, and I have decided that witnessing is a trade like any other and I intend to remain in practice.

I saw a child's hand, careful and uneven, growing steadier page by page, because that is what practice looks like and the officials should have to see it. I saw two hundred and seventeen names. Not my two hundred and seven — she had added ten of her own, civilians and stragglers whom I had missed or never known, gathered during our evenings on the high ground from other mouths while I slept or failed to sleep. She had conducted her own inquiry. Nobody had authorized it. Nobody had needed to.

I heard her read the first names aloud, because the major asked her to, wanting samples to verify, and her voice in the chapel was thin and clear and did not shake until page four, and then I did the swallowing thing, and she glanced at me and kept going, which was her forgiveness, which was better than comfort.

And I watched the clerk try to do what clerks do. He began cross-referencing against the brigade rolls, running his finger down columns, and I saw him arrive at the gap — the void where two hundred lives should have been and weren't — and I watched his finger stop on the empty space the way a man's foot stops on a missing stair.

"Sir," he said. "These names. They're not in any roll I have. There's no — none of these people officially existed in this sector. No admissions, no evacuations, no — " he floundered — "no entries."

"No," I said, from the rail. "There aren't."

Everyone looked at me. Even Amara paused over her page.

"That was rather the point of them not being entered. Things that are entered can be struck. These were kept separately precisely so that no one could strike them." I came down the aisle slowly, because I wanted to say the next part walking. "Your rolls were shortened, Major. Deliberately, at staff level, beginning in October. Civilians struck from manifests. Wounded reclassified. An entire low quarter of this town removed from the evacuation plan because removing them made the arithmetic easier for someone. Colonel Vasseur knew. Others above him knew better. If your inquiry wants to find out why certain populations were written off, the answer is that writing them off was the plan, and the book on that table is the correction."

Silence. The chapel kind of silence, which is different from other silences; it has weight and attention.

The major looked at the copybook for a long moment. Then he did something I did not expect and have never stopped being glad of: he looked past the book, at the child holding it.

"How do I know," he said to Amara, gently, "that these are true?"

And my girl — because she was mine by then, whatever the papers would eventually say, and I was hers, and that transaction had been settled somewhere on the causeway in November and neither of us had ever invoiced the other — my girl put her finger on a name and said:

"This one is Joris Vanneck. He had a limp on the left side and he carried me on his shoulders during the fire at the mill and he told me his secret was that he couldn't swim, which was funny because of the flood, except it wasn't funny." Her finger moved down. "This one sold eggs. This one played the accordion badly on purpose to make his brother laugh. This one — " and here her voice went quiet and precise — "is my mama. She isn't crossed off. She's just on the first page, because she was first."

Nobody in the chapel breathed. The clerk had stopped writing.

"That's how you know," Amara said. "Because nobody makes up a limp on the left side."

* * *

It took them two days, in the end, to do what a child had done in a winter: copy the names into their forms, verify what could be verified against the living — and the living were generous with verification, lining up at the chapel door to put their marks beside the names of their dead, Odilon identifying eleven, Marguerite nineteen, the whole town turning out like a jury that had already deliberated and was merely waiting to be sworn.

On the third morning the major signed the final register and did an odd thing. He closed it, and then he opened it again, and read the first page once more — the page with Amara's mother, and after her, in Amara's careful descending order, Marguerite, Samson, and the rest, the honored dead given the head of the table at last.

"These will go to the inquiry," he said. "Names restored to the rolls. Compensation claims, pension eligibility, next-of-kin notification — all of it proceeds from this." He hesitated, and then, quietly, off the record: "There are officers who will be very unhappy to see this book. I want you to know I'll be naming that unhappiness myself, before they can name it for me." He looked at me. "You understand that someone will try to say the record is unreliable. A child's copybook."

"Major," I said, "everything reliable in this world was first a child's copybook. Yours included. Check inside the front cover of your own school primer someday, if you kept it. You'll find the same hand."

He almost smiled. "And the original ledger? Your ledger? The inquiry will want it."

"The original," I said, "is in the river. Where the river can keep it. What survived is on the table." I nodded at the copybook, at Amara, who was showing the clerk, of all people, how she had sewn the binding. "Which is how it works. The first book teaches the second book. The second book outlives the first. That's not a failure of record-keeping, Major. That's the entire method of the human race."

He wrote that down too, or started to, and then looked at my face and didn't.

* * *

That evening the lanterns came out again. It had become our custom — every evening since the crossing, the town setting lights in windows and along the high-ground path, one lantern for each name that had gone into the water and not come back. Amara lit ours and set it on the sill and stood with her hands folded, doing her count, though lately the count had changed. She no longer counted seconds backward to calm herself. She counted forward now. Days since. Names saved. Knuckles of the living.

"Twenty-nine days," she said, coming to sit beside me on the step. "Since the water. Since Uncle Samson's lantern."

We did not speak of him much, she and I, because we spoke of him constantly, in the language of people who share a silence: the way I now salted food for two out of habit and caught myself; the way she still, sometimes, assigned him a step-number in her counting before remembering and letting the number stand anyway, unfinished, like a door left open.

"The major took the book today," she said.

"He borrowed the book. It comes back tomorrow. They're copying it."

"I know." She swung her feet. Her boots nearly fit her now; she was growing the way children grow in wartime, all at once and sideways. "Theo. When they give it back — can we start another one?"

"What kind of another one?"

"A new one. For the living part." She frowned, working it out. "The old book is for people who are finished. But there's people who aren't finished. Marguerite's baby isn't born yet and she doesn't even have a name picked. And Odilon says his barge will float by spring and he needs somebody to write down the repairs or he'll forget and it'll sink, he *said* so. And the letters." She turned to me, suddenly urgent. "Theo, the mail truck came today and there were *letters*, real ones, from across the water, from people who went on the barge, and nobody has written them down anywhere, they're just — loose. Letters shouldn't be loose. Letters get lost."

I looked at her — at the lantern-light on her face, at the fierce bookkeeping of her — and felt something in my chest turn over, the way a key turns, the way a clock turns, the way everything turns eventually if you wait.

"And who," I said, "do you propose should keep this new book?"

"Well." She examined her fingernails with tremendous sophistication. "I know how to sew one. But my spelling is still bad in the long words. So I thought maybe you could do the long words and I could do the rest, and we could both sign it. Both keepers. Like partners."

"Partners," I said.

"Like the barge and the rope," she said. "One pulls and one holds."

Inside the house, on the table, the mail sack sat open with its loose letters spilling out — letters from across the water, from strangers who had become ours on a night causeway, from a rebuilt ward and a floating barge and a life going on loudly in a dozen hands. Tomorrow the inquiry would return the old book, and the day after, the mail truck would come again, and the world, astonishingly, was still arriving.

Amara held out her hand.

"Shake on it," she said. "Before you get sensible."

So I shook on it.
