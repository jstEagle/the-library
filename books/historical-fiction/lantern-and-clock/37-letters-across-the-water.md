## 37. Letters Across the Water

The mail came to the convalescent home on Thursdays, which meant that by Wednesday evening I was already a person I did not entirely respect — someone who checked the road twice from the window, who invented errands that required passing the gate, who had opinions about the postman's horse. Six years of war had taught me to wait for ambulances with a steady heart, but a plain envelope addressed in a stranger's hand could still undo me completely.

Amara found this hilarious.

"You've sorted the same linens three times," she said, not looking up from her copybook. She kept the copybook on the table now at all hours, the way other girls kept dolls. "You hate linens."

"I am a professional."

"You sighed at the pillowcase."

I sat down and let her win, because she usually did. Since the northbound train, since the small room over the convalescent home where we had hung the lantern on its nail as though it were a family portrait — which, I suppose, it was — Thursday had become our second Sabbath. The first belonged to Samson, kept in silence and in the winding of his stopped watch, which I carried and which Amara was not yet allowed to touch. The second belonged to whoever had crossed the water and lived to hold a pencil.

The letters came slowly, carried in packets through military post, through Red Cross channels, sometimes through routes so roundabout that I suspected certain letters of having seen more of the war than most soldiers. They arrived for me because Odilon had spread my name along the river like seed. *Write to the nurse,* he told people. *She keeps the book.* The book itself was ruined — swollen, illegible, its pages fused into a single grey brick that I could not bring myself to bury and could not bear to open — but Amara's copy had survived, and copies of the copy were already circulating among the saved, passed hand to hand like scripture, which in a sense they were.

Here is what came across the water that spring. I have kept every letter. They are filed in an ammunition crate, which strikes me as the correct place for them.

* * *

*Dear Nurse Oyelaran,*

*You will not remember me. This is the beginning of every letter you will receive from any of us, so let me spare you the repetition: none of us believes we are remembered, and all of us are wrong.*

*I was third column, the night of the crossing. A cooper from V—, forty-one years old, with a bad knee and no one waiting. You do not know my name because you did not write it down; you knew it only for the length of one lantern's light, and you said it back to me when my knee went out on the causeway, and you said it the way you say a thing you intend to keep.*

*I am writing to tell you what became of the knee. It healed crooked. I limp. I have taken an apprentice who is sixteen and impatient and thinks I walk slowly on purpose, and every time he complains I tell him about a woman who walked four miles of flooded causeway in the dark carrying a lantern and a child's counting, and I tell him his complaint is noted in a ledger somewhere, and he goes back to work.*

*My wife died before the war. I thought that was the end of being known. It was not. Thank you.*

*— F. Aubertin, cooper*

* * *

Sister Marguerite's letters came monthly, in a hand that had steadied since the winter, though it would never again be the hand of a woman who trusted institutions. She had taken charge of a ward in a requisitioned schoolhouse two provinces north, and she wrote about it the way converts write about their faith — with the fervor of someone who had been given a second chance and intended to spend it down to the last coin.

*Theo,*

*They gave me thirty-two beds and a coal shortage and a doctor who is nineteen. I told the matron I would need authority over triage or nothing at all, and she looked at me the way they look at women who use the word* authority*, and then gave it to me, because the alternative was doing it herself.*

*I want to tell you what I have learned, since you were too kind to say it to my face on the bank: that choosing is not the sin. Refusing to choose — handing the choice upward so that your hands stay clean while someone else's hands get dirty — that was my sin, and I committed it for eleven years under the respectable name of obedience. You chose. You chose wrongly by every rule I was ever taught, and two hundred people are alive, and I have stopped praying for forgiveness for the eleven years and started praying for the strength to keep choosing.*

*Record my name first in whatever comes next. You asked me once if I meant it. I mean it more each month.*

*There is a girl here, fourteen, lost her whole street at Second Marne. She follows me on rounds and pretends she doesn't. Yesterday I caught her straightening my dressings when she thought I wasn't looking. Her hands are better than mine ever were. I am going to make a nurse of her and tell her, someday, about a woman who measured the world by what can be carried, and teach her to measure it the same way.*

*Yours in the same book,*
*M.*

* * *

Odilon wrote rarely and at length, in the manner of men who distrust paper and compensate with volume. His spelling was a lawless country. He described the rebuilding of his barge in terms that suggested he was raising a difficult child.

*Nurse,*

*You will be pleased to hear the* Perdrix *floats again, though she floats the way an old dog swims, with complaints. I have planks from three wrecks in her hull and I can tell you which plank came from which wreck, which is a kind of genealogy, my father would say. He would also say I paid too much for the caulking, and he would be right.*

*The army wanted her for transport. I told them she was unseaworthy. She is entirely seaworthy. But there are cargoes and cargoes, and I find I have developed preferences. I move flour, and lamp oil, and occasionally persons whose paperwork is thin and whose need is thick, and the officers' reports omit these voyages, which suits everyone, you will notice, except the reports.*

*This is the trick I learned from you, though I doubt you meant to teach it: what the lists leave out is where the real cargo rides.*

*The causeway held, by the way. Engineers walked it in April and pronounced it sound until June. I have marked the low-water days on my calendar for the rest of my life. Some men keep saints' days. I keep yours.*

*— O.*

P.S. *The compass you sent back with the boy — I know whose it was. I have mounted it at the helm. It does not point quite true anymore. Neither do I. We agree.*

* * *

The strangest letters were from strangers — people I had never touched, who had heard the story secondhand and written anyway, as though the tale of the omitted names had become a kind of shrine at which anyone might leave an offering.

One came from a schoolteacher in the south who had copied Amara's book into her own hand and begun reading the names aloud to her pupils each Friday, "so that they understand that history is made of persons." One came from a widow whose son appeared in the ledger — a name I had recorded at two in the morning in a flooded dressing station, a boy I had treated for shrapnel and never learned the color of his eyes — and she wrote to thank me for the spelling of his name, which the army had gotten wrong on the death notification, and which she had corrected on his stone with her own chisel. "You gave him back his accent," she wrote. "No one else did."

I read that one to Amara twice. The second time my voice failed on the word *chisel*, and she took the page from me and finished the paragraph herself, evenly, the way she counted steps — and I understood that she was learning to carry things too, and that some of what she was learning, she was learning from watching what I could not lift.

* * *

Not all the letters were easy. There was one from a man of the fourth column, a mason, who wrote to say that he had reached the far bank and turned around, and gone back onto the causeway toward the breach, and been driven off by the water, and that he had not slept properly since. "I owe the man on the rope," he wrote. "Tell me what to do with a debt like that." I sat with that letter for a long evening. In the end I wrote back the only true thing I had: that Samson had not cut himself free until the last stranger crossed, and that the debt was therefore already discharged by design, and that the only interest it collected now was lived, not paid — every day he woke, every board he set, every child he taught to lay stone level. "Build something," I wrote. "Build it straight. Send me no more letters about it, but send word when the something stands."

He wrote once more, a year later. Three lines. *The schoolhouse in V— stands. Straight. Thank you.* I put it beside Marguerite's and felt the crate grow heavier in the way that matters.

And there was a letter I did not show Amara, from an adjutant of the late Colonel Vasseur's regiment, enclosing a note in the colonel's own hand that had been found among his effects after the rear-guard action — a note addressed to no one, or possibly to me, or possibly to himself. It said, in full:

*The arithmetic was wrong. Not the numbers. The premise. I counted berths. Berths were never the unit. Tell the keeper of the book that the pages reached the far bank, and that I ask no further entry, but that if entries must be made, mine should read: corrected too late, corrected.*

I have not decided whether to enter it. Amara says a book that only holds the clean hands is a lie with good posture. She is nine going on merciless. I think the colonel goes in.

* * *

In May came a fat envelope stamped with the seal of a children's home outside Tours, containing a letter from a girl named Sylvie, aged ten, whom I had never met, who had crossed on the second night of the evacuation in the care of a laundress named Beatrice — six months pregnant at the causeway, Marguerite's berth surrendered to her, the baby born in Tours in July and named, Beatrice wrote separately, Marguerite, "because names should go where they're needed."

Sylvie's letter said:

*Dear the lady with the lantern,*

*Beatrice says you counted us across. I can count to a thousand now. Marguerite can't count yet but she can yell, which is better. Beatrice says when I am big enough I can come see the bridge. Is the clock fixed? Somebody said the clock got fixed. I hope the lantern lady is well.*

*Your friend,*
*Sylvie (age 10)*

*P.S. What happened to the ninth hundred step?*

I wrote back that the ninth hundredth step had gone into the river with everything else, and that this was why one always counts past the end of a thing — because endings are unreliable, but counting continues. I do not know if this is philosophy. It may simply be a description of Thursdays.

* * *

It was near Midsummer, on a Thursday, that the thickest packet yet arrived — seven letters bundled together, forwarded through three depots, tied with the waxed twine of field kitchens. Amara brought it in from the gate herself, running, and set it on the table between the lantern and the copybook as though laying a relic on an altar, and then pretended to do her sums while watching me open it with the naked greed she had inherited honestly from me.

Six of the letters were from the causeway columns. I will not transcribe them all here; the crate holds them. A farrier who had carried a stranger's mother the last quarter mile. Twin sisters who had crossed holding one rope between them and now, restored to their village, had opened a laundry together and named it for nothing, "because," they wrote, "we owed it to nobody, which is the best kind of name."

The seventh letter was heavier stock, official-looking, and for one absurd moment my heart performed the old maneuver — the flinch of a woman who has spent a war expecting forms to arrive bearing bad arithmetic. I opened it with Amara's counting audible under the table, three seconds to a breath, her private metronome.

It was from the Ministry. It concerned records.

*Madame Oyelaran,*

*This office is charged with reconciling evacuation manifests for the river sector, winter 1917–18. The official rolls for the period are materially incomplete. We have received representations — numerous, detailed, and mutually corroborating — to the effect that a private record exists documenting persons omitted from those rolls, particularly civilians evacuated via an unregistered route during the thaw of last March.*

*We request that you make this record available to this office at your earliest convenience.*

I set the page down on the table, next to the lantern, next to the copybook in Amara's careful ascending hand — her name first, then mine, then Marguerite's, then Samson's, then two hundred more, copied twice, recited nightly like a psalm, carried now in a skull as well as a crate so that no water, no fire, no ministry could subtract them.

"They want the book," Amara said.

"They want the book."

"Which book?"

I looked at her — at the child who had counted a column across a flooded causeway, who had memorized the alphabet of the omitted, who had been writing for a year in a hand that officials would one day mistake for a clerk's — and I felt the old vertigo, and under it, new and solid, the floor of it: whatever we gave them, the book had already done its work. It had crossed the water. It was walking around in coopers' knees and schoolhouses and a baby named Marguerite. Ministries could have the words. The keeping was ours.

"The real one," I said, and took down the ammunition crate, and began, with my brother's daughter counting softly beside me, to choose which pages a government would be permitted to believe.
