## 38. What Theo Carried

The relief officers arrived on a Tuesday, though I only knew it was Tuesday because Amara had counted the days since the causeway crossing and refused to round down. She was precise about grief in a way that would have made a Jesuit proud. Four hundred and six days since the bridge closed. Three weeks since the water let us go. Tuesday.

They came with clipboards and clean boots and the particular smell of men who had not been shelled recently — soap and cigarette paper and institutional confidence. A captain with a red mustache informed me that all civilians remaining in the valley would be processed for evacuation north within forty-eight hours, and that personal effects were limited to what one could carry.

"How much is that?" Amara asked him.

He blinked at her. Adults rarely expected the child to be the one asking the clarifying questions, which had always struck me as a failure of imagination on the part of adults generally.

"However much you can carry," he said.

"That's not an amount," she said. "That's a tautology."

I did not teach her that word. Sister Marguerite did, God rest her, before God got around to resting her — she'd earned it, holding the far ward door through two hours of shell fire and then dying eleven months later of a fever that should have killed no one, having already decided which deaths she was willing to owe. She'd asked me to write her name first in whatever book came next. I had. It's on page one, above the fold, where names of consequence belong.

But that was earlier. This is now, and now is a room with a floor, which after that winter still feels like luxury — and a canvas kit bag open on the floor, and me standing over it with the peculiar paralysis of a woman asked to summarize herself in objects.

* * *

You would think, having survived a flood by carrying things — litters, lanterns, children, the whole wet catalogue of a town's forgotten names — that I would know exactly what weighs and what doesn't. But knowing what carries well is different from knowing what's worth carrying, and I stood there like a woman packing for a holiday instead of a life.

The compass first. Vasseur's compass, brass-cased, its needle still swinging with the lazy confidence of an instrument that never doubted itself even when its owner finally did. He'd pressed it into my hands on that last night, this man who had spent a war doing arithmetic with human lives, and asked nothing except that the ledger survive him. The ledger hadn't, not intact — the river took half of it and blurred the rest into a language of grey ghosts — but the names had survived somewhere better than paper. They'd survived in a nine-year-old's memory, recited like psalms, copied out in pencil by lamplight until her hand knew them better than her own name.

I turned the compass over. There was a dent in the casing I hadn't noticed before, or had noticed and filed away without deciding what it meant. A dent from what? Shrapnel? A fall? The colonel had carried it through three campaigns and it had outlived him by exactly as long as it took to pass from his hand to mine. Objects don't grieve, but they do accumulate. They sit there being dented at you.

"You're holding it wrong," Amara said from the doorway. "A compass goes where you can reach it. You taught me that."

"I taught you that about the lantern."

"It applies." She came in and put the compass in my coat's inner pocket, the one over the heart, which she considered the correct pocket for everything important and which I had stopped arguing about sometime around the causeway. "What else?"

The watch. My father's watch, which had stopped at 4:17 on some unrecorded day in the autumn of 1917 when the tower clock died and time in the valley became a matter of rumor and lamp oil. I'd kept it in my apron pocket through the worst months, checking it out of pure habit, finding it dead each time, and finding — this was the strange part — that the deadness itself was a comfort. A watch that lies to you is worse than a watch that says nothing. Mine said nothing. It was honest in its silence, and in a winter when every official report lied, I found I valued that.

Samson had worn a watch too. Samson had worn it into the water. When they pulled the wreckage off the causeway three days later, the watch was gone, the wrist it had lived on gone with it, and so my stopped watch became the family's entire horological estate: one broken timepiece representing two men, one lost to the river and one to a fever, neither of whom had gotten to see how anything turned out.

I put it in the bag, then took it out, then put it back. Packing is mostly this. Deciding, un-deciding, and lying to yourself about which decision was first.

* * *

The lantern caused trouble.

Not real trouble. The trouble of an object that has become argumentative with history. It was a cheap thing, tin and glass, bought from a peddler in August of 1917 for what was then an outrageous price and later seemed like the best money I ever spent. Amara and I had lit it every evening of the occupation — our ritual, our small defiance, two flames' worth of insistence that somebody was home. It went across the causeway hanging from my hand on the night of the crossing. It had lit the first column and the last. Its glass was cracked on one side where Samson's elbow had knocked it against a piling, and I had never cleaned the crack properly because the crack was, in a way I couldn't explain to anyone without sounding unhinged, his.

"Take it," Amara said.

"It's heavy."

"It isn't."

Everything weighs almost nothing, it turned out. That was the discovery of that afternoon, standing in my last room in the valley town whose name I will spell correctly in every book I ever write and pronounce wrong on purpose in every conversation, because grief deserves a private joke. I laid things out on the floor and weighed them with my hands like a woman pricing at market, and the arithmetic came back absurd. Compass: nothing. Watch: nothing. Lantern: a little more than nothing. Copybook: less than nothing, a few ounces of paper that outweighed the bridge itself.

The copybook. Amara's hand-copied ledger, the second book, the one that mattered. Two hundred and seventeen names in pencil, then ink when Odilon's cousin produced a bottle of it from God knows where, written in a child's careful hand that grew more confident page by page until by the final pages she was writing like someone who expected to be read. Which she now had been — by the relief commission, by a major who compared it to the official rolls and went pale, by clerks who entered every name into the record while refusing to meet my eye. The omitted were omitted no longer. It had taken a flood, a drowned brother, a disgraced colonel, and nine years of a girl's handwriting, but the record was the record now. Bureaucracy abhors a vacuum, especially one filled against it.

I wrapped the copybook in oilcloth and put it in the center of the bag, cushioned by everything else, which is to say by almost nothing.

* * *

There were other things, of course. Things that didn't make the cut and things that did and things I argued about with myself at length, and since no one else was in the room and Amara had gone to help Odilon load his rebuilt barge — the man could not stop rebuilding things; I believe he'd rebuild the war if given timber and a week — I'll confess the arguments here.

I kept Marguerite's scissors. Small, surgical, older than me, the only instrument she'd asked to have buried with her and which I had not honored, because I needed them, because need outranks sentiment in a dressing station, and because I suspected Marguerite would have agreed. She'd spent her whole career choosing which mercy lived and which died by order rather than conscience, and had spent her last year trying to pay that debt forward one laundress at a time. She'd have said: use the scissors, you fool, sentiment is for people with surplus. Then she'd have used them herself to cut a bandage, because her hands never stopped even when her sermons did.

I left behind the morphine vials. Empty ones — I'd kept them through the occupation as currency and units of time, measuring the days in grains and milligrams, and there had been a week in November when the calendar was literally a row of vials on a windowsill. But they were empty now and the war's economy had moved on, and there is something obscene about carrying drug paraphernalia into a peace you hope to live in. I gave the whole row to the last nurse on duty, a Belgian girl named Colette who reminded me uncomfortably of myself at twenty-three, minus the self-doubt, and told her to measure her days in something kinder.

I very nearly left the letters. Chapter upon chapter of letters had reached me over the past month — from soldiers crossed safely north, from families reunited in refugee towns, from strangers who'd been on the causeway that night and remembered my brother anchoring the rope. Each letter was a small weight of paper, and paper adds up. But I sat on the floor and read three of them through again, the ones I always reread — the corporal who wrote "your brother held the line so we could hold our children, tell your family"; the laundress Marguerite saved, whose baby was named Marguerite and who enclosed a lock of hair I will keep until my hands are gone; Odilon's note consisting entirely of "barge sound, come north when ready, P.S. your girl counts too loud, tell her the fish complain" — and I understood that these weren't weight. These were ballast. Ballast keeps you upright. You carry it precisely so you don't blow away.

All the letters went in. The bag still wasn't full. That offended me, in some irrational place — a life reduced to a half-empty kit bag seemed like poor craftsmanship, either mine or God's — and so I did something I'd been avoiding. I went to the corner where Samson's boots stood.

* * *

His labor battalion had issued him boots two sizes too large, the army's idea of provisioning, and he'd stuffed them with straw and complained about them with a fluency that suggested six years of silence between us had been, on his side at least, merely a pause for breath. He wore them into the water. They came back. The river returned the boots and kept the man, which felt like the river's idea of an apology, and I had kept the boots by the door all this time without once deciding to, the way you keep a habit you're ashamed of.

Boots weigh more than nothing. That's their nature. Leather and nails and mud from a road that's underwater now.

Amara found me holding them. She was quiet in the doorway the way she is quiet when she's deciding whether to count or speak, and then she spoke, which meant she'd counted first.

"He'd say take them," she said.

"He'd say burn them. He'd make a joke about my feet."

"He'd say take them and *then* make the joke." She came and sat on the floor beside the bag, cross-legged, businesslike, nine years old and chairman of the board. "Theo. What are you actually afraid of? That they're heavy?"

"They're heavy."

"They're boots. You carried a man heavier than these boots for two miles along a dike with your shoulder dislocated. I was there. I counted your steps."

Two hundred and eighty-one steps. She'd told me that number every week since. She keeps it the way I keep names.

"I'm afraid," I said, and stopped, because the true sentence underneath was ugly and I'd been walking around it for weeks. "I'm afraid that if I pack them, I've finished grieving him, and if I leave them, I haven't started. And I don't know which of those is worse, and the boots have somehow become the scale."

Amara considered this with the gravity she brought to everything, including fish complaints. Then she picked up the right boot, looked inside it, and reached in and pulled out something I had never seen: a flat piece of tin, bent double, which she straightened with her thumbs. It was a watch case. Dented, water-bloomed, its face gone, its hands gone, its guts long since claimed by the river — but stamped inside the lid, in engraving that the water had failed to eat, one word: *Samson.*

Our mother had it engraved when he was fourteen. I'd forgotten. She'd forgotten to tell me, or I'd forgotten to listen; the Oyelarans were fluent in forgetting.

"The river sent it back too," Amara said. "Odilon found it in the nets in March. I've been keeping it until you were ready. You weren't ready for a long time."

I want to tell you I handled that moment with dignity. What I actually did was sit down on the floor of my last room in that town and hold a dead watch case and make a sound I'd heard other people make across a hundred dressing stations and never once expected to hear from myself, and Amara put her arms around my shoulders and counted quietly against my hair until it passed, which is what she does, which is what I taught her, which is the whole of my legacy: a child who knows that grief has a length and that you can survive any length if you number it.

Afterward, we packed together. The boots went in — both of them, straw and all. The watch case went into the inner pocket, over the heart, next to the compass, because Amara ruled that important things shared. The bag closed with room to spare.

And that was the whole revelation, the one I'd circled for days: it weighed almost nothing. Compass, watches, lantern, copybook, scissors, letters, boots, a lock of baby hair — the entire estate of Theo Oyelaran, forty-one years old, and it lifted with two fingers. Everything I had that mattered, everything the war had failed to take or had taken and returned changed, fit in a bag a child could carry, and frequently did.

The heavy things had never been things. The heavy things were two hundred and seventeen names and four hundred and six days and one brother, and none of those could be packed, which meant none of them could be left behind, which meant I had been carrying them all along and would carry them north and forever, and my back was equal to it. Grief, it turns out, is the lightest cargo in the world. It just refuses to be set down.

* * *

We left at dawn, because Amara insisted on seeing the bridge in morning light one more time and I had learned which battles were hers to win. The bridge stands rebuilt — new timber, old stones, the same river running under it with the same indifference that has always been the river's chief virtue and chief crime. Someone had hung a lantern on the near piling. Not ours. One of many now; the town had adopted the custom, and there were lanterns on sills and doorposts all down the high street, little tin declarations that somebody was home, somebody remembered.

At the station, the northbound train breathed steam like an animal asked to work too early. Odilon was there with his cap in his hands, pretending he'd come to inspect the locomotive. Colette was there. Half the omitted were there, the ones who'd stayed to rebuild, and they lined up along the platform without being asked, and I understood that we were not seeing each other off. We were mustering. Some formations don't dissolve just because the war ends; they just change what they're for.

Amara climbed aboard first, bag over her shoulder — my bag, technically, though ownership had been a fluid concept between us since the causeway. She took the window seat and opened something on her knees. A notebook. New, black covers, unmarked pages, purchased from the station kiosk with coins she had earned counting inventory for Odilon, honestly come by, which is more than most ledgers in this valley could claim.

She smoothed the first page flat. She licked the pencil, which is a habit I have failed and do not wish to cure.

"What are you writing?" I asked, stowing the bag overhead.

"Our names," she said. "First ours. Then everyone else. That's how a book starts."

The whistle blew. Somewhere behind us, faint over the water, the tower clock struck seven — repaired in June, wound nightly, accurate to within a minute, keeping time at last for a town that had learned to live without it. The wheels began to turn. Amara bent over the fresh page, tongue between her teeth, and wrote the first two names in a hand steady as a mason's line, and I watched the valley slide backward past the window — the bridge, the lanterns, the river going about its business — while beside me a child opened a blank book and began, in pencil, to make the world count.
