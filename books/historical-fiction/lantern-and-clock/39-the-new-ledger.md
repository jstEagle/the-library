## 39. The New Ledger

The train station at Vireloup had been built by men who believed in permanence, which is the surest way to invite impermanence. Half its glass roof was gone, letting in a cold spring light that fell in long pale bars across the platform like the fingers of someone checking whether you were still breathing. The other half remained, and beneath it stood a queue of people who had learned to wait without complaint because complaint had stopped being useful sometime around the second winter.

Amara held my hand. She was ten now — or eleven; we had settled on celebrating her birthday twice a year to make up for lost arithmetic, so she was either ten going on eleven or eleven going on twelve, depending on which of us you asked. She held my hand not because she needed to but because she had decided that we were the sort of people who did that in public, and I had agreed to be that sort of person, and it was one of the few contracts of my life I had never once regretted signing.

Two bags between us. That was all. A compass in my coat pocket that had belonged to a colonel who paid his debts too late but paid them; a watch stopped at twenty past four on an afternoon neither of us could name anymore; a lantern with a cracked chimney that Amara refused to let me mend, on the grounds that cracks were part of a thing's history; and a copybook, swollen with water damage and dried again into something like leather, full of names in a child's careful hand.

The relief officers had asked for records. They always do. There is a form for everything except the things that matter, and the forms are printed in triplicate by men who have never watched a river take back a valley.

"They'll want to see the book," Amara said. It wasn't a question. She had heard the officers say it too.

"They can see it," I said. "They just can't keep it."

"And if they insist?"

"Then they can copy it out themselves, every name, in their own hand, and when they're done they'll understand what it weighs." I looked down at her. "That's the trick with ledgers, you know. Anyone can read one. Very few people are willing to carry one."

She considered this with the gravity she gave to everything, her small face tilted up toward the broken glass, and then she said, "Marguerite says you were born stubborn."

"Marguerite says many true things about me. It's her worst habit."

* * *

We found our carriage. Third class, because the army's gratitude came in third class, and because after the marsh road, after the causeway, after everything, third class felt like an extravagance — seats, walls, a destination. There were six others in the compartment: a widow with a hatbox she guarded like ordnance; two soldiers asleep before the whistle finished blowing; a clerk type whose hands kept moving as though filing papers even in sleep; a laundress named Colette who had been pregnant in October and was now merely exhausted, her son asleep against her chest in a sling made from an army blanket; and an old man who stared out the window at the flooded fields as though auditing them.

The town slid past. Or what remained of the town, which is a different thing than the town itself. The bridge showed first — Odilon's rebuilt span, wider than the old one, the engineers proud of it in the way engineers are proud of anything that survives them. Someone had hung lanterns along its railing already, though dusk was hours off. Amara pressed her face to the glass.

"Odilon said he'd hang them every evening," she said.

"He keeps his bargains."

"That one wasn't a bargain. He did it for free."

I had no answer for that. Some kindnesses resist entry into any ledger, being the only currency that loses nothing by circulation.

Then the low quarter, where the water had finally gone down enough to show what it had done. Streets became canals became streets again, but changed, the way a face changes after fever. People moved through the wreckage already, salvaging, stacking, beginning — because that is what people do, they begin, they begin again, they begin badly and anyway. The tower clock stood above it all, its face dark, its hands frozen where Samson's rope had failed and hadn't failed, where the water had won and lost, where I had counted the living aloud until my voice broke and Amara took over the counting in a clear child's voice that shamed us all into steadiness.

Twenty past four. We never did learn whether it had stopped at some meaningful moment or simply died of neglect like everything else that winter. I had stopped winding it months ago. Some clocks you keep running. Others you keep exactly as they are, because their whole job now is to remember.

* * *

The train pulled north through country I didn't recognize and country I did, alternating, as memory does. Fields drowned and fields dry. Church spires standing in water like men wading out to meet something. And everywhere the poplars, those patient witnesses, lifting their new buds toward a sky that couldn't decide whether to rain or forgive.

Colette's son woke and howled, and she fed him without embarrassment, and the old man adjusted his stare to the middle distance, and Amara watched the infant with an expression I recognized because I had seen it in mirrors: hunger and fear and hope, all wearing the same borrowed coat.

"You'll want one someday," Colette teased her.

"I want one now," Amara said. "But Theo says babies don't do well on marsh causeways."

"It's true," I said. "I have it on excellent authority from experience nobody should have."

The soldiers slept on. One of them murmured a name in his sleep — a woman's name, or a mother's, or a river's; from this distance they sound alike — and turned his face to the window where the light caught it and made him briefly, terribly young.

I thought about the ledger. I thought about how it had begun, that first flooded week in the dressing station, when the officers' lists grew shorter as the wounded kept arriving, as though the war could be managed by managing the paperwork. I had started writing names in the back of a ration book because grief needed somewhere to go and my hands knew how to hold a pen. I had not known I was founding anything. You never do. Institutions are just habits that outlive their despair.

And I thought about the cost, which accounting requires. The ledger itself was ruined — river-soaked, ink-bled, half its pages fused into a brick of pulp that I carried for weeks before admitting it would never speak again. But paper was never really the point. Marguerite had understood that before any of us. *Record my name first,* she'd said, *in whatever book comes next,* and then she had given her place on the barge to a pregnant laundress, choosing differently the second time, closing her own circle with her own hands.

Whatever book comes next. Well. Here it came.

Amara had dug the copybook out of her satchel and was smoothing its warped pages on her knees. Then she reached deeper into the bag and produced — I confess I did not expect this — a notebook entirely new. Blue covers, unlined paper, bought that morning from the station kiosk with money she had earned mending sacks at the relief depot, money she had hidden from me with a conspirator's skill that boded either wonderfully or terribly for her future.

"What's that?" I asked, though of course I knew. You always know. You spend years waiting for a moment and then it arrives looking ordinary, sitting on a child's knees on a third-class train, and you understand that all your important moments will be dressed this way.

"A new book," she said. "For the new ledger."

"Amara—"

"The officers have their copy," she said, with the patience of someone explaining tides to a rock. "But their copy is theirs. This one is ours. Marguerite said." She opened it. The first page was blank and white as low water under moonlight. "You always say a book starts with who you owe."

"I don't think I've ever said that in my life."

"You've said it every day of your life. You just say it with your hands instead of your mouth."

There is no reply to that which preserves dignity, so I made none. I watched her dip the little pen she'd also bought — the whole enterprise had apparently been budgeted — and I watched her think. Her lips moved slightly, shaping the letters before committing them, a habit from our alphabet lessons in the lantern light, when every name in the ruined ledger had become a spelling exercise and a psalm at once, and she had memorized them the way other children memorize prayers, which is to say completely, and forever.

She wrote. Slowly, tongue between her teeth:

**Théodora Oyelaran.**

**Amara Oyelaran.**

I had not given her my surname. She had taken it, the way she took everything that season — quietly, deliberately, as her right. I looked out the window very hard at some poplars until my eyes agreed to behave.

Then, beneath us, in the same careful hand:

**Sister Marguerite Delacroix.**

"She's alive," Amara said, as if reminding herself. "She gets to be in the book while she's alive. That's better."

"It is better. Make sure you tell her that. She'll argue."

"Good. I like arguing with her. She argues properly."

And then Amara stopped. Her pen hovered over the place where the fourth name would go, and the train rocked us gently, and outside the light had gone long and gold, and I understood that she had arrived at the page's center of gravity and knew it.

She wrote:

**Samson Oyelaran.**

Her pen rested there a moment. Then she added, beneath it, in smaller letters, the way one annotates scripture: *He carried the rope.*

"Yes," I said, though she hadn't asked anything. "He did."

"He's in the water part of the story now."

"That's one way to put it."

"Odilon says the river keeps nothing. He says it just moves things somewhere else and forgets it did." She looked up at me, and her eyes were dry and enormous and older than ten or eleven, whichever she was. "Do you think that's true?"

I considered lying to her. I had lied to her once, in this whole long catastrophe — the night of the lanterns, when she asked if names float, and I told her they float higher than anything, because love required it and I was too weak to be honest and too honest to be weak anywhere else. That lie sat in me still, a small smooth stone I turned over on bad nights. I had sworn that one lie would be the last currency I ever stole from her.

"No," I said. "I think the river remembers everything. I think that's what rivers are *for*. But I think remembering isn't the same as keeping. The river remembers him the way we do — by carrying him somewhere."

"Where?"

"North," I said, and nodded at the window, at the country unrolling ahead of us, at whatever waited there that we could not yet spell. "Same direction as the train."

* * *

Dusk came on. The soldiers woke and ate bread and shared it without being asked, which is the whole of civilization in one gesture. Colette's son slept the imperial sleep of the well-fed. The old man dozed at last, his audit suspended. And in the blue-gold failing light Amara worked her way down the second page, then the third, writing the names she had memorized — the omitted, the saved, the crossed-over, the carried — reciting each one aloud under her breath like a rosary with a spelling requirement, and I listened, and corrected her only twice, and both times she said "*I knew that*" in a tone that meant she hadn't.

At the end of the third page she paused again, pen lifted.

"One more," she said.

"There's no one left."

"There's always room for one more. You taught me that." And she wrote, in letters larger than the rest, as though to make certain no future reader could miss it:

**Colonel Aurélien Vasseur.** *He gave us the compass.*

I stared at it. "Amara. He was — what he was—"

"He was what he was," she agreed, with terrifying reasonableness. "And he gave us the compass, and he carried the pages, and he fell facing the wrong way for a man like him. Books aren't for judging, Theo. You said. Books are for keeping."

I had said that. God help me, I had said that, probably during the flood, probably to justify myself to myself, and here it came back to me dressed as a child holding a pen.

"The compass is in my pocket," I said at last.

"I know," said Amara. "It's going in the book too. Someday. When we find glue."

* * *

Night filled the windows. The lamp above our seats came alight, and Amara lit — I am reporting this exactly — the cracked lantern she had smuggled aboard, setting it on the seat between us like a small stubborn planet, and the conductor, passing through, looked at it, looked at her, and continued on, because some regulations are not worth dying for and some conductors are wise.

She fell asleep mid-name, around the letter R, her head against my arm, the notebook open on her lap, the pen still in her fingers, leaving a faint ink comet across the page where her hand had drifted down. I eased the pen away. I read the names she'd written, all of them, twice, three times, the way you reread a letter from someone you'd given up expecting.

Then I noticed, at the bottom of the fourth page, below her sleeping hand's reach, in that same careful script — she must have written it earlier, before boarding, planning this the way she planned everything, secretly and thoroughly:

*This book belongs to everyone in it.*

Below that, a blank line. Below that another. Below that, pages and pages and pages, white as the flooded valley under starlight, empty and waiting, the way the future is empty and waiting, which is to say: not really, not if you look closely, because the future is just the past that hasn't been written down yet, and we had a great deal of writing to do.

Somewhere ahead of us, past the dark fields and the dark water, a city waited with forms in triplicate, and questions, and a world that would want to know how two hundred omitted souls had crossed a drowning valley, and who was responsible, and by what authority.

Amara slept. The lantern burned. The train ran north.

I picked up the pen.
