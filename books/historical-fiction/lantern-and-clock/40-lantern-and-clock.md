## 40. Lantern and Clock

The clock takes eleven turns of the key, never ten, never twelve. I have counted every evening for nine years, and if you think that is a strange thing to count, then you did not know me as a child, and you do not know what counting was for.

I climb the tower stairs at dusk because that is when Theo taught me to light the lantern, and because dusk is honest. Morning lies and says it will last forever. Dusk only says what is true: that the day ended, that some people did not see it end, and that light is a thing you must make on purpose.

The stairs are ninety-one steps. I used to know them by the sound of my shoes; now I know them by the sound of my knees, which is a different psalm entirely, less joyful, more insistent. At the top, the town spreads out along the river — the rebuilt bridge with its pale new stone that does not yet match the old stone in the pilings, the market square where Odilon sells fish he swears he caught himself, the low quarter where the water no longer reaches but everyone still builds their doorsteps two bricks higher than the plans call for. Habit is a kind of architecture too.

First the lantern. It sits in its iron bracket on the bridge's northern tower, the one that survived, and I light it with a taper I keep in my coat pocket next to a compass I no longer trust to point anywhere but north and a stopped watch I refuse to have repaired. The watchmaker in the county town has offered three times. Three times I have said no. Some things are meant to stay at a particular minute, the way some names are meant to stay in a particular mouth.

The lantern catches, steadies, burns. It has burned every evening since the bridge reopened — through the year of the drought, through the influenza winters, through the night a drunk barge hand tried to warm his hands on it and lost his eyebrows for his trouble. The town council voted funds for it the way councils vote for things: reluctantly, and then claimed credit generously. There is a plaque now. It says ERECTED IN MEMORY OF THE PASSED, which is wrong in a way I have decided not to correct, because the lantern was never for the passed. It was for the omitted. For the ones whose names lived first in a nurse's ledger written in pencil, in a flooded dressing station, by a woman who measured the world by what could be carried.

I was nine years old when I learned that names can float. I asked, once, on a night full of lanterns on the water, and Theo lied to me — the only lie she ever told me, though I did not learn it was a lie until years later, when she finally gave me back the truth like something heavy she had been carrying in her teeth. She said the names float up to heaven. What she knew even then was that names float down rivers, and drown, and wash ashore, and are read aloud by whoever finds them, which is heaven of a sort, but a heaven that requires labor. Someone has to do the reading.

That someone, it turns out, is me.

* * *

After the lantern, the clock.

Eleven turns of the key. The mechanism is French, or claims to be, and was drowned for a season in 1917 along with most of the valley, and came out of the water sulky and unreliable, like an aristocrat made to dig his own garden. A horologist from three towns over spent a year on it. He said afterward that flood water gets into everything, into the gears, into the pivots, into the places oil should be, and that a clock that has been under water never quite forgets it. It runs slow in damp weather. It hesitates at the hour sometimes, gathering itself, the way people hesitate before saying a name they are afraid of mispronouncing.

I wind it and set the hands by the lantern below, because the two are married now whether the horologist likes it or not. Light and time. Clock and lamp. When Theo was dying — slowly, in her own time, in her own bed, complaining about the quality of the nursing with a straight face while I changed her dressings — she told me the title of the book she never wrote. *Lantern and Clock*, she said. Everything you need to survive a war: something to measure the dark with, and something to measure the waiting. She said it as a joke. Most of the true things she said were jokes.

Then I go out onto the tower gallery, and I read.

* * *

The reading is the part the town does not understand, or understands only in the way towns understand anything — as decoration, as ritual, as the sort of thing Amara does, the way old Monsieur Fouchard argues with pigeons and Madame Bérenger wears black even to weddings. They hear my voice carry across the water on calm evenings, name after name, and they take off their hats if they are the sort who take off hats, and they go home to supper.

They do not know that I am not performing. I am paying.

It began the winter after the flood, when Theo's ruined ledger — smoke-stained, water-warped, half its pages fused into a brick of pulp — finally surrendered the names we could not recover any other way. We sat by her stove, she and I, and she recited from memory and I wrote, and when her memory failed she would close her eyes and say, *he was tall, he whistled through the gap in his teeth, he called me Sister though I was no such thing*, and between the two of us we would worry the name loose like a splinter. Two hundred and fourteen souls, all told. It took us four winters. Her voice went before her mind did. On the last pages the names are hers alone, dictated, and mine in ink, and there is one entry — Samson Oyelaran — that I wrote without being asked, because he was in the ledger of her heart long before he was in any book, and because a berth is not the only way to cross water.

Now I read them aloud from the tower, a few each evening, cycling through, so that no name goes longer than a season unheard. Tonight begins with Achille Dantin, who drove a milk cart and died holding a rope that was not attached to anything, and ends — tonight, this rotation — with Marguerite Soyer, who refused a berth and gave it to a pregnant laundress, and asked that her name go first in whatever book came next.

Theo put her first. I keep her first. Some orders you obey forever.

Marguerite's ward in the south sends letters twice a year, always on the same thin paper, always beginning *Chère petite comptable* — dear little accountant — which was her joke, from the days when I counted seconds to keep the world from flying apart and she counted patients the way governments count votes. In her latest letter she reports that the ward has a new stove, that the laundress's daughter is training as a midwife, and that she herself is failing in the manner of lamps rather than clocks: dimming evenly, without drama. She asks about the lantern. She always asks about the lantern. I tell her it burns. It is the truest sentence I own.

Odilon rebuilt his barge, mostly, and named it after his mother, which disappointed everyone who hoped he'd name it something dramatic. He carries goods upriver now instead of people, and pretends he never did anything else, and every spring when the thaw comes he moors below the bridge for one night and drinks one glass with me in silence, which is his entire vocabulary of grief, and I have learned to speak it fluently.

And the strangers — the ones who crossed the causeway in the fog, guided by a nine-year-old girl counting steps and reciting an alphabet stitched out of dead men's names — some of them write. A carpenter in Lyon. A widow who was pregnant that night and whose son is now taller than the bridge towers, or claims to be; mothers exaggerate. Each letter says some version of the same thing: *I do not know why I survived.* And each time I want to write back: *You survived because a woman falsified a manifest, and a man anchored a rope with his body, and a colonel found his conscience late and paid full price, and a child counted. Nobody survives by accident. Survival is a work of many hands.*

I do not write that. I write: *You survived because you were counted. Come see the lantern sometime.*

Some of them come.

* * *

Here is what I have learned in the years since, the knowledge I was given young and have spent my whole life translating:

Memory does not keep itself. That is the whole secret, and it is disappointing how few people know it. Memory is a clock that must be wound and a lantern that must be filled and lit, every single evening, or it goes out, and once it goes out people say things like *erected in memory of the passed* and think they have done something.

Theo understood this. Not at first — at first she kept her ledger out of anger, that fierce private arithmetic of hers, adding up the souls the officers' lists left off, daring the world to be accurate. But somewhere between the flooded dressing station and the causeway, between losing the paper and saving the people, her ledger stopped being a weapon and became an inheritance. She handed it to me the way you hand someone a lantern: here, hold this, walk ahead, I will follow as far as I can.

She followed as far as she could. Nine years past the flood, six past her bed, and on the last evening I wound her small bedside clock and lit her lamp and read her the day's names, and she listened with her eyes closed, and at the end she said, clear as a bell: *Eleven turns. Don't let anyone tell you ten.*

Those were nearly her last words. Her actual last words were a complaint about the soup, which I record here for accuracy, because accuracy is dangerous and therefore worth being.

Samson I barely got to know again before the river took him back. Six years of silence over a theft that turned out to have been his all along — or hers, depending on which confession you believe, because near the end they both claimed it, sitting across from each other at her table like two lawyers fighting over a client neither would bill. He took the blame to save her career; she took the blame back to save him the weight of it; the truth, as usual, weighed nothing at all, which was the point of the story, though I only understood that later, packing her things after she died, finding how little a whole life weighs. A compass. A stopped watch. A copybook in a child's uncertain hand.

My hand. My copybook. The second ledger, the one I made while the first one drowned.

I have it still. It sits on the shelf in the clock tower room, between the winding key and a tin of lamp oil, and twice a year I take it down and turn its soft pages and look at the letter A I learned first — *A for Achille*, Theo said, *Achille Dantin, milk cart, gap-toothed whistle* — and I am nine years old again, memorizing the dead like a psalm, not yet knowing that the psalm would become my profession, my prayer, my inheritance, my love.

* * *

Tonight the fog is coming up the valley early, the way it does in November, and I finish the names quickly — Achille, the widow Perrin, the three Vasseur cousins who never learned their uncle died on the far bank carrying papers for a woman he'd tried to ruin — and I bank the lantern against the damp, and I start down the ninety-one steps with the key cold in my pocket.

Halfway down, I stop, because the clock behind me hesitates at the hour, gathering itself, and then strikes eight, and the sound rolls out over the water and the bridge and the low quarter with its two extra bricks, and somewhere below a dog answers, and somewhere above the lantern burns steady inside its glass.

I stand in the stairwell with my hand on the wet stone and I think: they built a plaque, and they got it wrong, and someday I will be gone and someone else will have to decide whether to fix it. Perhaps they will leave it wrong. Perhaps wrong plaques are how towns grieve — clumsily, generously, claiming credit. But perhaps someone will come up these stairs, some child sent on an errand, some stranger off one of Odilon's barges, and find the copybook on the shelf, and read the names, and understand that the lantern was never for the passed and the clock was never for the hour. That they are for the counting. That someone loved these people enough to number them, and that the numbering goes on.

That is the monument. Not stone. Not bronze. A woman's pencil, a child's ink, eleven turns of a key, a flame against the fog, every evening, without fail, for as long as there is anyone left to read aloud:

*Achille Dantin. Marguerite Soyer. Samson Oyelaran. Thérèse "Theo" Oyelaran.*

*Amara, who counted, who was counted, who counts still.*

The fog closes over the river. The lantern holds. Tomorrow at dusk I will climb the ninety-one steps again, and the clock will need its eleven turns, and the names will be waiting, patient as water, glad — I have always believed they are glad — to be spoken one more time into the listening dark.
