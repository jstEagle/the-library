## 1. The Weight of Water

There were three ways to measure a life in Port Serrano, and Mirabel Marchetti had spent nineteen years learning all of them.

The divers measured in fathoms — how far down a man could go on one breath, how long he could hold the ache in his chest before his hands found a shell. The company measured in tons and tides: pearl tonnage shipped east, tide tables consulted like scripture. And Mirabel, who belonged to both worlds and neither, measured everything in the only units that mattered to someone whose whole life could fit into a seabag.

Her mother's coral beads: four ounces. The diving knife her father had given her on her tenth birthday, hilt wrapped in new leather because her hands were smaller than his: half a pound. Her books — the arithmetic primer, the dog-eared dictionary she'd bought off a sailor for two hours' mending, the slim book of poems she told no one about — eleven pounds together, if you didn't count what was inside them, which weighed nothing and everything. The ledger she was legally required to keep for Halcyon Trading Company, being the property of Halcyon Trading Company along with the rest of her: three pounds, eight ounces, and it would stay behind without argument.

She did this arithmetic the way other people said prayers. Not because she intended to go anywhere — where would she go? — but because there was comfort in knowing your own weight. A diver's daughter learned early that you had to know exactly what you carried before you ever left solid ground.

* * *

The counting house smelled of ink, mildew, and Mr. Prewitt's pipe, in roughly that order of permanence. Mirabel sat at the second desk from the window — hers by seven years of custom — balancing the October column while rain ticked against shutters that hadn't been properly oiled since the last cyclone, which was to say since the cyclone before last.

"Marchetti." Prewitt didn't look up from his own ledger. "Your column's drifting again."

"It isn't drifting, Mr. Prewitt. It's reconciling."

"There's no such word."

"There is. Page forty-one of the primer."

"I wrote page forty-one of the primer."

"You wrote it wrong," Mirabel said pleasantly, and dipped her pen.

This was their liturgy. Every morning, the same exchange, every morning Prewitt pretending offense and Mirabel pretending not to notice that he always left the window desk to her, that he always "forgot" his spectacles on days her father came up from the water coughing, so that she might leave an hour early without it going into the record. Prewitt was a dried prune of a man who had once, when Mirabel was fourteen and weeping over a sum she couldn't make come right, quietly rewritten the entire shipping schedule so her error vanished. He would deny this under oath. He had, in fact, denied it under oath, during the inquiry of 1896, and winked at her from the witness box while doing it.

The rain thickened. Outside, through the warped glass, she could see the harbor — gray water, the lighthouse standing on its spit of rock like a patient grandmother, and the boats pulled up high on the sand where the divers had lashed them against the season's first real weather. Her father's boat among them, the *Salacia*, hull patched in six places with six different colors of pitch, floating or beached according to the year's luck and nobody's planning.

Her father was out there now. She could see him, small in the rain, talking with old Serafim about something, gesturing with both hands the way he did when his lungs let him forget them. He'd been down twice yesterday. Twice, in late October, with the water already cold as a grudge.

She put her father's name in the ledger the way the company required — *M. Marchetti, diver, leasehold bed 14, output recorded* — and felt the familiar small wrongness of it. On paper her father was a line item. In the world he was a man who had once held his breath for four minutes and eleven seconds to win a bet with a customs officer, and who sang badly and constantly, and whose cough had lately taken on a wet, deep sound at the end, like something turning over in the hold of a ship.

Four minutes and eleven seconds. She wondered what it cost him now just to climb the stairs to their rooms above the chandlery.

"Marchetti," Prewitt said again, softer this time, and nodded at her column. "It's come right."

It had. It always did. That was the trouble with sums, Mirabel thought, blotting the page. They came right no matter what was happening in the world that made them.

* * *

The letter arrived at half past three, and Mirabel knew it was trouble before she touched it, because trouble at the Halcyon Company always traveled first class.

It came by company courier — not the post, but a boy in a clean shirt, which meant someone specific had sent it and wanted it known they'd sent it. It was addressed to Mr. Corvane himself, the manager, and it was sealed with wax the color of dried blood. Corvane was away at the Company House across the bay until Friday. Prewitt took the letter, weighed it in his hand the way he weighed everything, and set it in the locked drawer of his desk.

Then he looked at the drawer. Then he looked at the stove. Then he looked at the window, where the rain was coming down like the sky had a personal grievance, and Mirabel watched a thought move across her superior's face as legibly as an entry in a ledger.

"Marchetti," he said. "Go home early. Your father—"

"My father is fine, Mr. Prewitt."

"Your father," Prewitt said, with the delicacy of a man handling a live shell, "was heard coughing on the pier this morning by three separate people, all of whom mentioned it to me before nine o'clock, which suggests they mentioned it to everyone else in Port Serrano too. Go home. I'll close up."

He never closed up. Closing up was Prewitt's religion, performed alone, at precisely five o'clock, with the blinds drawn and the door locked and woe betide anyone who knocked after.

Mirabel gathered her shawl at a speed that was dignified, or nearly. At the door she paused — and here is where honest accounting requires us to record what she did next, though she would spend some weeks refusing to name it even to herself. She had left her good pen. That was the reason she gave. She went back in for her pen.

The pen was on her desk. Prewitt was at the stove, feeding the letter into it, unburned.

For a moment neither of them moved. Rain hammered the shutters. The fire made a low orange sound.

Prewitt did not startle. That was the remarkable thing. He looked at her the way he looked at a column that wouldn't reconcile — tired, resigned, faintly fond — and then he turned back to the stove and fed the letter in anyway, and stood watching it curl.

"Clumsy of me," he said, "leaving correspondence where anyone might read it. Terribly clumsy. You'd want to watch the door on your way out, Marchetti. The wind takes it."

The paper blackened. Through the char, for half a heartbeat, she saw writing — fine copperplate, a heading, figures — and then the middle of the page glowed and folded inward like a closing eye, and it was gone.

Mirabel found her pen. It was, as established, on her desk, exactly where she'd left it. She picked it up with fingers that were entirely steady, which surprised her, and walked to the door, which the wind did indeed take, pulling it wide so the rain gusted in and Prewitt swore and hurried to shove it shut, and in those three seconds of commotion Mirabel Marchetti bent down near the stove — where a scrap of burning paper had lifted on the updraft and fluttered against the iron fender, singed at one corner, its face intact — and closed her hand around it the way she used to catch falling shells as a girl, without looking, without hesitating, because on the water you caught first and examined after.

"The wind," she agreed, and went out into the rain.

* * *

She did not open her fist until she reached the fish market awning two streets down, and then she did not open it to look but to hide, tucking the scrap into the lining of her shawl seam with the quick blind competence of a woman who had been sewing things into hems since she was tall enough to reach the needle. Then she stood under the awning among the smell of brine and scale and let herself shake, just a little, just once, before walking home.

At home there was soup to make and a father to pretend not to listen to. She did both.

Elio Marchetti ate his soup propped in the good chair with a blanket over his knees, which was new this autumn and which they were both pretending was about drafts. He talked between spoonfuls — about Serafim's grandson, who had dove his first ten feet that morning; about the price of lamp oil, criminal, an outrage; about whether the monsoon would come early and whether that was good or bad depending on which debts came due first.

"And how was the counting house?" he asked, in the tone of a man asking about weather in a country he'd sworn never to visit.

"Balancing," Mirabel said. "Everything balances eventually."

"That's what I tell your Uncle Tomás and he says that's exactly the problem with arithmetic." Her father smiled, and then coughed, and the cough went on longer than soup deserved, deep and rolling, and Mirabel kept her eyes on the pot and stirred what did not need stirring and thought: *four ounces, half a pound, eleven pounds, three pounds eight ounces.* Weighing things. Measuring her escape route she did not intend to take, because measuring it was the only way to keep from screaming.

Later, when her father slept sitting up in the good chair because lying flat made the cough worse, Mirabel took the scrap from her shawl seam and unfolded it by candlelight.

It was perhaps a sixth of the original page. The edges were browned and brittle. But the copperplate was fine and the clerk who wrote it had pressed hard, and by angling it toward the flame she could read most of it, though the sentences began and ended in amputation:

*...the Marchetti family, holding lease-bed 14 and appurtenant shore rights, present no obstacle to consolidation provided the matter is handled...*

And below that, in different ink, a figure: *£40*, and beside it initials she did not recognize, and beneath everything the ghost of the Halcyon seal, cracked and red, like a wound that had decided to stay.

Mirabel read it four times. *Present no obstacle.* Her family. Bed fourteen — the bed her grandfather had worked, her father had worked, the bed the divers joked was less a lease than a marriage, sixty years of shells and lungs poured into it. *Provided the matter is handled.*

Handled how? Handled by whom? Forty pounds — forty pounds was what a diver made in a good year, when the beds were generous and his lungs held. Was that a price? A payment? Payment to whom, for what?

She sat very still and listened to her father breathe, that wet turn at the bottom of each breath, and understood — in the wordless way you understand a storm swell before you can see it, in the way her body knew things before her mind consented to — that whatever the sentence meant when whole, none of its possible meanings were innocent. Companies did not burn letters that contained nothing.

She should ask her father. The thought came and she set it aside carefully, the way you set aside a knife with a loose handle. Her father would laugh. Her father trusted the company the way he trusted the sea — not because it was kind, but because he knew its rules and had survived them, and a man who has survived a thing his whole life cannot easily be told the rules have changed. He would say, *Mirabeletta, you read too many ledgers, numbers make you superstitious.* And she would have no proof, only a scrap and a feeling and the memory of Prewitt's tired, fond face above the stove.

*Watch the door on your way out,* he'd said. *The wind takes it.*

He had burned the letter. And he had let her see it burn, and let her keep the wind-blown piece, or at least had not chased her for it. Two facts. Both true. Requiring reconciliation.

Mirabel got the little brass scale from the mantel — a relic of her mother's, kept for weighing pearls — and placed the scrap on one pan. It weighed almost nothing. Less than an ounce. Lighter than the coral beads, lighter than the knife, lighter than any single thing she had ever counted toward the weight of her life.

And yet when she sewed it into the shawl seam that night, working by feel in the dark so the candle wouldn't wake her father, her hands were as careful as if she were carrying water.

Outside, the rain kept on. Out past the harbor bar, unseen in the dark, the first long swells of the monsoon were already assembling themselves over the horizon, patient as arithmetic, on their way to Port Serrano with the new century riding in their pockets.
