## 2. Indentured

The trouble with being the only person in Port Serrano who could read a ledger sideways was that nobody ever believed what you found in one.

Mirabel had learned this at fifteen, when she'd discovered that Mr. Corvane's predecessor — a soft, sweating man named Pell who had since fled to the capital with a company launch and somebody else's wife — had been skimming freight charges for three years. She had brought her findings to her father, who had patted her hand and told her she was clever but oughtn't worry her head about such things. She had brought them to Tomás, who had said, "Of course he was stealing, little sister. Where do you think his hat came from?" And she had brought them, in a moment of youthful idealism she still winced over, to the assistant harbor master, who had thanked her gravely, done nothing whatsoever, and thereafter tipped his hat to her with an expression she now recognized as fear.

So on the morning after the fire in the clerk's brazier, Mirabel did not tell anyone anything. She sat at her desk in the counting house with her hair pinned back so tightly it gave her a mild headache, and she did what she always did with unbearable things: she filed them.

The scrap of paper lay folded inside her left boot, which was not where a sensible person kept evidence but was exactly where a bookkeeper kept evidence, because nobody in the history of commerce had ever searched a woman's boots. The Halcyon Trading Company would search a crate, a cabin, a rival's warehouse, even — once, memorably — a coffin that had been behaving suspiciously, according to the harbor gossip. But boots were beneath their dignity, in every sense.

She worked through the morning's entries with her usual neatness: forty crates of dried fish out on the *Merrow*, twelve bales of cotton in from the *Adelaida*, the usual dribble of pearl consignments marked with the divers' names in her own careful hand. Old Sefa's lot. The Verrano brothers' lot. Her father's lot, small this season, smaller than last.

And then, in the quiet hour after lunch when the clerks went to the back steps to smoke and complain about their feet, she opened the lease register.

* * *

Every diver in the lagoon held his or her bed under a lease from the Halcyon Company — a word that sounded grand and legal and was, in practice, four sheets of foolscap with wax seals, renewed each year at a fee most divers paid in pearls and a few paid in stubbornness. Mirabel's father held Bed Fourteen, which his own father had worked before him, back when the Marchettis had still been prosperous enough to have illusions. The register recorded every bed, every renewal, every fee, going back forty years.

It was, Mirabel thought, not for the first time, a strange kind of ownership. The divers knew every stone and channel of the lagoon the way they knew their own children's faces. The company knew the lagoon as a column of figures. And yet the column of figures owned the faces.

She began, methodically, to count.

Lease renewals for the current year: thirty-one recorded. Thirty-one divers, thirty-one beds. That much matched her own consignment ledgers; she checked twice, because checking twice was both her habit and her armor.

Then she turned to the margin notes, and here her stomach began its slow, cold descent.

Corvane's hand was thin and slanted, like a man writing while leaning away from something. In the margins beside eleven of the thirty-one beds — including, she noted with a sensation like swallowing river ice, Bed Fourteen — he had made small annotations. Most were initials she didn't recognize. Two were dates, next year's dates. One, beside old Sefa's name, said simply: *arrears trivial — other grounds available.*

Other grounds.

She wrote the phrase down on her blotter without meaning to, then looked at it for a long moment, then erased it, then wrote it again because erasing it felt like lying, and Mirabel Marchetti lied about many things — her age to customs officers, her opinions to clerks, her appetite to Tomás when he brought too much food — but never, ever to a ledger.

*Arrears trivial — other grounds available.*

Eleven beds. Eleven families. If you counted dependents, and Mirabel always counted dependents, it came to somewhere north of sixty souls who drew their bread from those beds, plus the dockworkers who loaded the catch, plus the net-menders, plus the boy who sold fried dough to the divers at dawn and had never once been seen selling it to anyone else.

She spent the afternoon pulling threads. A survey order, dated last month, authorizing "reassessment of productive waters." A requisition for printed notices, quantity fifty, purpose unstated — fifty, when there were only thirty-one leases, which meant either terrible arithmetic or preparations for people who didn't hold leases yet. And a single entry, buried in the disbursements column, of a payment to a legal firm in the capital whose name she copied into her memory with the reverence other people reserved for curses: *Whitcombe, Brae & Fenn, re: title consolidation, per instructions.*

Title consolidation. As if the lagoon were a chest of drawers that needed tidying.

By the time the light through the counting house window went amber, Mirabel had assembled, in the privacy of her own skull, a complete architecture of disaster. The company intended to void the divers' leases. It had manufactured or would manufacture grounds — arrears, breaches, technicalities — and it would do it with paperwork, which was the company's preferred instrument for killing things, being cheaper than guns and leaving less to explain. The burned letter hadn't been an aberration. It had been a page torn from a blueprint.

She looked down at her hands, ink-stained and steady, and realized she had been holding her breath long enough that her collar felt tight.

"Marchetti." It was Pell's replacement's replacement, a junior clerk named Odo with a face like a worried egg. "Mr. Corvane wants the lease register back before close of day."

"It's on my desk," Mirabel said pleasantly, "and has been all afternoon."

Odo looked at the register, which was indeed on her desk, closed and squared to the desk's edge as precisely as if it had never been touched. He looked at Mirabel. Something behind his eyes performed a brief calculation and decided, wisely, against having any thoughts at all.

"Right," he said, and took it, and left.

Mirabel exhaled, retrieved the scrap of paper from her boot, read it again by the last of the light — *...the Marchetti family holding to be included in the first consolidation...* — and put it back. Then she put on her shawl and went home to lie to her father.

* * *

Her father received the news the way he received all news now: seated, wheezing slightly, with the patient irritation of a man being asked to move furniture.

"You found some words on some paper," he said. He was mending a diving weight's rope, his thick fingers still deft though the cough stole the middle out of his breath. The lamp between them turned his gray hairs gold, which she suspected was the only gold the family would see this year. "Words written by men in offices. Mirabel. Men in offices write words the way gulls make noise. You don't build your life around gull noise."

"Papa, it names us. By name. Our family's holding is in the first group to be—"

"We have held Bed Fourteen since before I had lungs," he said, and laughed, and the laugh turned into the cough, and Mirabel sat very still and hated herself for noticing, as she always noticed, how long it lasted. When he surfaced he wiped his mouth and continued as though nothing had happened, which was itself a kind of lie, and she forgave it because she was fluent in that language too. "Paper doesn't take a bed. Lungs take a bed. Hands take a bed. The company can write whatever it likes about Fourteen; it still needs someone who knows where the good shelf drops off, and there are perhaps six people alive who know, and two of them are at this table."

"The company doesn't need to know where the shelf is, Papa. It just needs to sell the knowledge to whoever buys the lease next."

He looked at her then, really looked, and for a moment she thought she'd reached him. His eyes were the same dark water hers saw in the mirror every morning.

"You are your mother's girl," he said finally, which was his highest compliment and also, frequently, his way of ending an argument. "She saw storms in teacups too." He tied off the rope with a sailor's knot, unnecessarily perfect. "Eat your soup."

There was no soup yet. There wouldn't be soup for an hour. But Mirabel understood the sentence for what it was — *I love you, and I am frightened, and I will not look at the thing you've shown me* — and so she said, "Yes, Papa," and lit the stove, and let the evening fill up with small safe noises.

* * *

She found the dockworkers at the Net & Anchor, which called itself a tavern the way a rowboat might call itself a navy. Tomás had the corner table he regarded as deeded property, and around him the usual congregation: Bruni the loader with forearms like mooring posts, quiet Inés who could splice cable in the dark, and young Ferro, who was nineteen and certain about everything, which Mirabel supposed was the traditional burden of nineteen-year-olds. She had carried the certainty herself once. It had weighed more than the ledgers.

She waited until Ferro's third anecdote wound down, and then she told them. Not everything — she kept Whitcombe, Brae & Fenn and the fifty printed notices and the scrap in her boot — but the shape of it. Leases. Consolidation. Dispossession dressed in paperwork.

She had expected anger. She had expected Tomás's crooked grin and some remark about burning things, because Tomás's solutions existed on a spectrum that ran from *fire* to *more fire*.

What she got instead was silence, and then Bruni saying, gently, the way you'd speak to someone standing too near a drop, "Girl, you want to be careful who you say this to."

"I'm saying it to you."

"And we're flattered." Bruni turned his tankard in a slow circle on the table, watching it as if it might bolt. "But think what you're carrying. You're telling me the company means to take eleven beds. You know what happens if that reaches the wrong ears before it's true? Or after? Divers stop paying fees, waiting for the sky to fall. Fees stop, arrears start, and arrears—" he spread his enormous hands, "—well. Even you said it. Arrears are grounds."

"She didn't say arrears are grounds," Ferro protested. "Corvane wrote—"

"I know what she says Corvane wrote," Bruni said. "On a letter half-burned that nobody's seen. Forgive me, Mirabel. But a fragment's a fragment. Half a knife cuts no rope."

"Half a knife," Mirabel said, "tells you someone nearby owns a whole one."

Inés, who averaged perhaps nine words a week, looked up from her mug. "Bruni's right," she said, and the table went still, because Inés endorsing caution was like the tide endorsing retreat — notable, and usually temporary. "Not wrong, either. Both true. Keep your papers, girl. Keep them dry and keep them dark. But don't wave them. A warning shouted early gets the shouters drowned."

Tomás said nothing at all, which frightened her more than argument would have. He was watching her with an expression she'd seen him wear exactly twice before: once before a run through a customs blockade, and once at her mother's funeral, when he'd been the only person brave enough to be angry at God in church.

"Well?" she said.

"You did the right thing, telling us," he said at last. "Now here's the hard part." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the scar along his jaw, souvenir of a French trawler and a misunderstanding about cargo. "You're going to have to sit in that counting house every morning and smile at those men and keep their books, knowing what you know. No hints. No glances. No heroic speeches. Can you do that?"

"I've been doing it for years."

"No." His voice was soft. "You've been doing their arithmetic for years. Now you'll be doing their arithmetic *against* them, and pretending you aren't. It's a different animal, little sister. It bites."

Around them the tavern roared on — dice, an accordion, someone's cousin singing badly about a girl from up the coast — and Mirabel sat in the warm ordinary noise of her people and felt the strangest sensation: that she had never been further from any of them. They loved her. They would hide her, feed her, lie for her, bury her troubles in the harbor mud if it came to that. But not one of them believed her. Not really. To believe her fully, they would have to believe the world worked the way her ledgers showed it working, and that way lay sleepless nights, and they had enough of those already.

"I can do it," she said.

"Good." Tomás raised his glass. "To boring books and quiet seasons."

They drank to it, all five of them, solemn as a christening, and Mirabel smiled and joined the toast and felt the folded paper shift faintly inside her boot like a small live thing.

* * *

She walked home late along the seawall, alone, which was her favorite company. The lagoon lay flat and silver under a fat moon, and out on the water she could see the riding lights of boats whose owners slept trusting in tomorrow's water. Bed Fourteen's buoy marker bobbed black against the shine, a mile out, patient as a promise.

*The Marchetti family holding to be included in the first consolidation.*

Her father was wrong, probably. Gulls and teacups and storms. Probably.

And yet.

She stopped at the bend in the seawall where, twenty years ago, a lighthouse keeper's son had dared her to swim out past the marker and she had, and had nearly drowned, and had been hauled back by that same boy, who had cried harder than she had and made her swear never to tell anyone, a secret she had honored for two decades out of pure affection for his dignity. Elias. He spent his nights up in the lighthouse these days, keeping the lamp, watching the water the way she watched ledgers — professionally, obsessively, on the lookout for whatever shouldn't be there.

If anyone else in this town was going to notice something wrong with the lagoon before it happened, it would be him.

She stood a moment longer, weighing it — the loneliness on one side, simple and known, and on the other the risk of saying a dangerous thing aloud to one more person who might not believe her.

Out past the buoy, far across the silver water, a boat moved without lights.

Mirabel watched it slide along the edge of the pearl beds, low and slow and deliberate, and decided that some knowledge was too heavy to carry by oneself.
