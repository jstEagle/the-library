## 3. The Lighthouse Keeper's Son

Elias Crane had learned to read the water before he could read letters, and he trusted it more. Letters lied — he had watched his father compose a dozen courteous ones to the Halcyon Company requesting lamp oil that never arrived, each signed *yours in faithful service*, each answered with silence or with an invoice for oil already delivered twice. But the water told the truth to anyone patient enough to sit with it. The water said the lagoon was two feet lower than it should be for this tide and no wind to account for it. The water said something large was moving out past the reef channel, where nothing large had any business being at night.

He set down his father's telescope, picked it up again, and made himself count to one hundred before looking. This was his father's method: *the eye is a gossip, Elias, but the patient eye is a witness.* His father was three months gone now, not dead but folded into the small room behind the lamp room with his bad heart, sleeping through most of the daylight hours like a cat that had worked nights all its life and saw no reason to stop merely because it could no longer climb stairs.

The light above Elias turned its slow wheel — flash, shadow, flash — and in one of those shadows the shape out on the water resolved itself into a longboat, oars muffled, riding low. Not smugglers. He knew every smuggler who used the lagoon's dark side, because they paid their lamp-oil tax in coffee and once in a coat so fine Elias still felt guilty about how well it fit. Smugglers did not carry lanterns shuttered with tin hoods, and they did not carry surveying instruments, and they certainly did not spend four hours creeping along the reef line in a pattern that could only be called a grid if you were feeling generous and a search if you were not.

Elias counted to another hundred. The boat completed its pass along the eastern shallows — the shallows, he noted, where the Marchettis' pearl beds lay, where old Matteo Marchetti had seeded shell when Elias was in short trousers, where Mirabel herself had learned to hold her breath until the whole dock would hold theirs with her.

There it was again: her name arriving unbidden, the way it always did, settling into his thoughts the way silt settles into everything in this town, quietly and permanently.

He went up the spiral stairs two at a time, which his knees approved of and his father's opinions about noise did not, and found the old man awake after all, propped on his cot with a logbook open on his chest, pretending he had been reading it rather than sleeping on it.

"Boat in the eastern shallows," Elias said. "Surveyors' instruments. No running lights. They've been at it since the moon set."

His father absorbed this the way he absorbed weather, which was to say without any visible alarm and with a great deal of invisible arithmetic. "Company boat?"

"Halcyon paint scraped off the bow, but the oar-locks are company issue. I'd know them anywhere. Father replaced half of them himself before they stopped sending spares."

"And what," his father said, "do you propose to do about it?"

It was a fair question, delivered mildly, and it deserved a fair answer, and Elias discovered he did not have one. What *did* one do about a boat? Report it to whom? The harbor master dined with the Halcyon agents every Thursday; the whole port knew it by the smell of roast duck drifting from his windows on Friday mornings. Report it to the company itself? That was like reporting a leak to the sea.

"I'm going to row out," Elias heard himself say, "and ask them what they're charting."

"You are going to do no such thing tonight." His father raised a hand before Elias could object. "Tonight you are going to trim the wick, wind the mechanism, and watch. Tomorrow, in daylight, when fools can be identified and drowned more conveniently, you may row out and ask whatever you like. Vigilance is a virtue, son. Impatience is just vigilance with poor manners."

Elias trimmed the wick. He wound the mechanism. He watched. And all the while the light swept its wheel over the water — flash, shadow, flash — and he thought about Mirabel Marchetti at nine years old, standing on the end of his father's jetty with her arms crossed, informing him that she had decided he was her brother now, since God had clearly failed to provide one, and would he please stop staring at her because brothers didn't stare. He had been staring at her ever since, in one form or another, for eleven years, and had gotten considerably worse at explaining why.

* * *

In the morning the tide brought him answers whether he wanted them or not.

The surveyors' camp had been struck before dawn, but they had left things behind, the way companies always did — a stake driven into the sand of the eastern shoal, ribboned in red; another; a third, close against the boundary stone that old Marchetti had planted twenty years ago with his own hands and that the divers' cooperative had maintained ever since with a stubbornness the monsoon itself had learned to respect. The stakes marched right up to that stone and stopped, politely, the way a knife stops against a whetstone. Waiting.

Elias stood in the shallows in his trousers, which were going to be ruined, and looked at the stakes, and then looked at the boundary stone, and understood something with the clarity of cold water: whoever drove those stakes had done it at night, in secret, and had stopped exactly at the line not out of respect but out of calculation. They were measuring what they intended to take. You did not measure a thing unless you meant to move it.

He pulled the nearest stake out of the sand, considered it, and put it back — wrong way up, ribbon buried. A small vandalism. A signature. Let them wonder who.

Then he rowed for the divers' beach.

The Marchetti beds sat below the point where the lagoon went from green to that deep bottle-blue the divers called *the purse*, because that was where the pearls were kept and also where your money went if you dove careless. Half the fleet was beached there for the morning, hulls tipped like tired animals, nets drying, and the sound of the place was the sound Elias loved best in the world: mending, arguing, and somebody's grandmother singing something inappropriate about a fishmonger.

Tomás Reyes spotted him first, as Tomás spotted everything first; it was his gift and his occupation. The smuggler straightened up from a coil of rope with the unhurried grace of a man who had never once hurried except when it saved his life, and smiled his crooked smile.

"Lighthouse boy. You've wet trousers and a face like a summons. Come to convert us?"

"Come to warn you." Elias kept his voice level, though his heart had begun doing the thing it did whenever he stood this close to the center of other people's lives. "There was a boat in the shallows last night. Surveying instruments, muffled oars, no lights. They ran a grid off your eastern beds for four hours. This morning there are stakes."

That stopped the beach. It stopped the way weather stops conversation — everyone simply turning toward it. Old Sefa lowered her netting needle. Two of the Ferran cousins came down from where they'd been re-tarring a hull. And Mirabel, who had been sitting cross-legged on an upturned dory with a ledger across her knees — because of course she had a ledger; the girl balanced accounts the way other people breathed — Mirabel looked up, and her eyes went from Elias's face to his soaked trouser cuffs and back, and she read him the way she read columns of figures, instantly and completely.

"How many stakes?" she asked.

"Six that I found. Probably more. They stop exactly at your father's boundary stone."

Mirabel closed the ledger. The soft clap of it sounded, in the sudden quiet, louder than the surf.

"They can't," said young Nico Ferran, who was nineteen and certain of everything. "The leases are registered. Grandfather signed them before I was born."

"Registered where?" asked Tomás softly, and Nico's certainty died on the vine, because everyone knew where documents lived in this port: in the Halcyon counting house, under the Halcyon roof, in Halcyon ink, kept by Halcyon clerks. Elias watched Mirabel go very still at that, watched some thought move behind her eyes like a fish under ice, and filed it away, because watching Mirabel think was a habit he had never managed to break and did not particularly want to.

Matteo Marchetti came up from the waterline with his mask pushed back and his chest heaving, and he was coughing before he reached them, that deep wet rattle that had been getting worse since the spring, and Elias saw Mirabel's hands tighten on her ledger until the cover bowed. Nobody mentioned the cough anymore. That was the arrangement. The whole beach had agreed, silently, to pretend the sound was temporary.

"Papa," Mirabel began, "there were surveyors—"

"Surveyors." Matteo waved this off as he might wave off smoke. "Halcyon measures everything. They measured my father's coffin before he'd finished dying of their contract prices. Paper men with paper fears." He looked at Elias, and there was kindness in it, which somehow made it harder to bear. "Boy, you keep good light and better watch. But you bring ghosts onto a working beach, and my divers need their sleep, not their fright."

"They weren't ghosts," Elias said. "They had instruments."

"Instruments." Matteo shrugged, a diver's shrug, all shoulders, the gesture of a man for whom the surface world had never held much weight. "So do the tide tables. Am I to fear the almanac?"

There was laughter at that, relieved laughter, the kind people make when they want very much to believe a thing is small. Only Mirabel didn't laugh. She was looking at Elias with an expression he couldn't name — gratitude, maybe, or grief — and when the beach returned to its mending she caught his sleeve and drew him aside, behind the stacked dories, where the smell was tar and salt and, faintly, the jasmine oil she wore when she could afford it, which was rarely, which is why he noticed.

"You believe me," he said. It came out as a question, which annoyed him.

"I believe six stakes don't drive themselves." She glanced back toward the water, where her father had already gone under, toward the purse, holding his breath the way he'd taught her to. "And I believe my father believes whatever lets him finish the season. There's a difference between not seeing a thing and refusing to, and he's been refusing so long he's forgotten which one he's doing." Her jaw set. "Show me the stakes."

* * *

They walked the eastern shoal together at low tide, which took an hour they did not have and both pretended not to notice spending. Elias showed her the boundary stone and the stakes pressed up against it like strangers crowding a door, and Mirabel crouched over each one, reading the ground — heel marks, keel scar, the little crater where an instrument's tripod had stood — narrating it half aloud in the clipped rhythm she used for reconciling accounts.

"Four passes east-west, spacing even. Then north-south, tighter. They weren't sounding for depth, they were mapping boundaries. Depth they'd have taken farther out." She stood, wiped her hands on her skirt, and looked at the red ribbon he'd left buried. "You turned this one."

"It seemed rude to leave a gift."

"It was clever." She said it plainly, and something in his chest performed a small, undignified maneuver. Eleven years, and she could still do that with one word. "Clever and stupid. If they come back and find their marker moved, they'll know someone saw them."

"Good," said Elias. "Let them."

Mirabel turned to look at him fully then, and he wished she wouldn't, because when she looked at him fully he lost access to roughly half his vocabulary. "Let them know what, Elias? That the lighthouse keeper's son is watching? They'll add you to a list. There's always a list. My father says the company keeps one, and he laughs when he says it, but he checks the harbor master's face every morning anyway."

"Then let me be on it." He heard his own voice go quiet and stubborn, the way it did. "Mirabel, I've watched this lagoon my whole life from the best vantage point on the coast. I've watched your family work those beds since before I could swim. If the company means to take them — and they mean to, you know they mean to — then somebody has to have seen them first. That's the whole job of a lighthouse, isn't it? To see the thing before it hits you."

She was silent a moment. Out on the lagoon the light of late afternoon lay flat and gold, and somewhere beneath it her father was rising toward the air with his lungs full of the sea that was killing him slowly, for beds the men with tripods had spent last night measuring.

"Elias." She said his name carefully, as if setting it down on a high shelf. "Thank you. And be careful. Those are not — those aren't the same thing, and I need you to remember they're not."

"They're the same thing," he said, "if you're careful about who you're careful for."

She stared at him. Then she laughed — one short, surprised breath of it, quickly suppressed, the laugh she kept for when he accidentally said something true — and cuffed his shoulder, and walked back along the tideline ahead of him, and he followed, carrying the moment with him like a coal in cupped hands, certain he would burn his fingers and not caring.

* * *

The company's answer came within the week, because companies answer defiance faster than they answer letters.

Elias found it on Thursday morning, painted on the lighthouse's lower gallery while he slept: a neat white band of lime wash around the ironwork, and beneath it, in a clerk's precise hand, the word **OBSTRUCTION**. No signature. No explanation. Just the word, applied evenly, professionally, the way you mark a rock that ships should avoid.

His father, woken by the commotion, stood at the base of the tower in his nightshirt and studied it for a long time. Elias braced for anger and got instead the terrible mildness of the old man's worst days.

"Well," his father said at last. "At least they spelled it correctly." He leaned on his cane and looked up at the lamp room, at the light that had been the Crane family's whole stubborn career for forty years, flashing over the reefs, over the beds, over every secret the lagoon kept. "They've called us an obstruction, son. In my experience, institutions only write down what they intend to remove."

Elias looked at the word drying in the morning sun, and at the eastern shoal beyond it, where the red-ribboned stakes waited at the boundary stone like a held breath. Somewhere across the lagoon, Mirabel was opening her ledgers for the day, and he would have wagered the lamp itself that she was looking for something in those columns — some entry, some figure, some small telltale discrepancy — the way he had gone looking for a boat in the dark.

They were watching, both of them, from their separate towers.

And out past the reef, right on schedule, a company launch was putting into the harbor with its papers squared and its intentions dry, carrying a man named Corvane, whose name Elias did not yet know — but whose arrival the harbor master was already, at that very hour, rehearsing his welcome for.
