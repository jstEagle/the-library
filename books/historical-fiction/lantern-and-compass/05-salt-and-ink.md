## 5. Salt and Ink

The copying began on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were the days Mr. Corvane took his long lunch at the Harbor House and the counting room emptied of everyone but me and a ceiling fan that turned with the resigned slowness of a thing that had given up.

I had thought about it all through Sunday mass, all through Monday's columns of figures, all through a sleepless night in which my father coughed in the next room like the tide working against a seawall. If I was going to do this — and I had decided, somewhere between the third hour of lying awake and the fourth, that I was — then I would do it the only way I knew how. Carefully. In order. With good penmanship.

There is a strange comfort in method. When your world is tilting, you can at least keep your inkpot level.

The diving journal had been my father's since before I was born, a fat ledger-bound book he'd bought secondhand from a chandler who'd mistaken him for an ambitious man. He used it to record depths, bottom-times, the weather, the quality of his catches. Half of it was empty. The paper was thick and salt-cured and smelled faintly of him — brine and tobacco and the camphor he rubbed into his chest of an evening. Nobody at the Halcyon Company would ever open it. It was, in their arithmetic, a poor man's notebook, and poor men's notebooks were beneath notice.

That was precisely its virtue.

* * *

I worked in the early mornings before the counting room opened, when my father still slept and the light came gray and forgiving through our one good window. I could not simply take the lease papers from the office — they were counted, logged, locked behind Corvane's door each night. But Corvane believed, as most men in love with ledgers believe, that what is written once need never be written again. He let me make fair copies of everything. It was my whole function: to turn his scrawled figures into clean entries fit for the colonial office in the capital.

So every Tuesday and Friday, when the fan sighed above me, I copied a little more than I was asked to.

A lease abstract here. A diver roster there. The schedule of bed-assignments for the coming season, with three families' names struck through in red — the Okonkwos, the Ferrands, and ours — and a notation in Corvane's cramped hand: *lapsed, reversion pending*. Lapsed, as if the beds had forgotten to pay their own rent. Reversion pending, as if the lagoon were a purse someone had set down and walked away from.

And always, folded flat inside my sleeve, the scorched scrap of the letter. I had pressed it between two pages of my father's journal like a flower, and now I made its twin: same words, same damning line — *the Marchetti family interest being material to the consolidation* — written out in my best round hand, witnessed by nobody, dated by nothing. A ghost with good handwriting.

The hiding required more artistry than the copying. I couldn't paste pages into the journal; the binding might gape, the thickness betray itself to anyone who handled it. Instead I sewed. My mother had left behind a workbasket and I had left behind, until now, any reason to use it. I learned to fold a page into eighths, stitch it flat against the inside of the journal's spine-gutter with waxed thread, and cover the seam with a strip of matching endpaper. By lantern light, pricking my thumb more often than the cloth, I built a book inside a book. My father's depths and bottom-times on the surface; the company's sins stitched into its bones.

"You're sewing," my father observed one morning, not as a question. He sat propped in his chair with his blanket around his shoulders like a man pretending to be older than he was, which lately he was.

"Mending," I said.

"Whose?"

"Mine."

He looked at me a long moment. His eyes were still sharp even when the rest of him had gone soft at the edges; consumption had not yet reached them. Then he nodded slowly, the way he nodded at weather he couldn't change, and asked no more. That was the bargain we had always kept, he and I. He did not ask where I went; I did not ask what the water took out of him. We loved each other in the space between those silences, and mostly the space was wide enough.

Mostly.

* * *

Tomás found out on Thursday, because Tomás found out everything on Thursdays — market day being the confessional of the harbor, where every secret changed hands at least twice along with the fish.

He appeared at our door at dusk carrying a papaya he hadn't paid for and wearing the particular grin he wore when he knew something I didn't want him to know. He was twenty-two to my nineteen, broad as a bollard and nearly as fixed in place, and he had been calling himself my brother so long and so loudly that the harbor had adopted the fiction wholesale. He'd fished me out of the lagoon when I was seven and sinking like a coin, and he had never once let me forget that I owed him a life, though he collected the debt only in laughter.

"Peaches," he said, which was not my name but had become inevitable after an incident involving a fruit crate and my hair, "you are doing something interesting."

"I am mending."

"You have thread marks on your fingers and ink under your nails and you've started walking past the Harbor House twice a day like a girl casing a jewel shop." He bit the papaya. "Also, Little Nia saw candlelight in your window at four in the morning and says you were talking to yourself."

"I was proofreading."

"At four in the morning." He leaned in the doorway, filling it, entirely too pleased with himself. "Mirabel. Sister-of-my-heart. Pestilence-upon-my-naps. Tell me you're not forging anything without me."

"Tomás—"

"Because if you're forging something without me, that's practically apostasy. We made vows over that drowned biscuit in 'ninety-three. Shared contraband, shared fate."

"That biscuit was yours and you shared it under duress."

"And I've repented ever since." His grin faded by degrees, the way sun goes off water, until what was left underneath was the other Tomás, the one the harbor rarely saw — the one who'd pulled me out of the lagoon and gone pale afterward and pretended he hadn't. "Truly, peaches. What is it?"

And there it was: the whole weight of the thing, sitting in my chest, asking to be put down. I had been carrying it alone since the burning barrel. I had told myself that telling no one was prudence, but prudence and loneliness wear very similar coats, and only one of them keeps you warm.

I looked past him at the street, empty, gold-lit, smelling of tar and frying onions. Then I stepped aside and let him in.

* * *

I showed him the journal first, and let him find the hidden leaves himself, because I wanted to see his face when he did. It took him eleven minutes, during which he read the diving records aloud in a mocking falsetto, complained about my father's spelling of *Nassau*, and then went suddenly quiet at page ninety-one, where the endpaper strip met the gutter just slightly wrong.

His finger traced the seam. He looked up at me. "You stitched this."

"With waxed thread. It comes apart with a razor and goes back together with a needle."

"How many?"

"Nine so far. Lease abstracts, the roster, the red-lined schedule, and the copy of the letter fragment."

"The Marchetti letter." He said it flatly, without question, and I realized he'd heard more in the market whisperings than he'd ever let on. He set the journal down on our table with the care of a man handling something that might go off. Then he sat down across from it, heavily, and rubbed both hands over his face. "Mira. Do you know what happens to you if Corvane finds this?"

"He extends my indenture. Blacklists Papa. Puts us off the beds entirely."

"No." He dropped his hands. "What happens is worse and simpler. Men who forge land deals will forge anything else. You'd go out on a night dive and not come back, and the report would say *unfortunate accident*, and everyone would nod, because the sea takes people, doesn't it? That's the beauty of drowning a person. The sea signs the confession for you."

I had known this. Somewhere beneath my careful Tuesdays and my neat stitches, I had known it. But hearing it said aloud, in Tomás's voice, made it solid in a way my own thoughts never managed. I sat down across from him and we regarded the little book between us like a third guest.

"So," I said, when the silence had gone on long enough. "Now you know. And knowing it, you'll want me to stop."

"Now I know it," Tomás said, "and I want you to do it properly."

I confess I stared at him.

He leaned back and crossed his arms, settling in, the storyteller's posture. "My cousin runs coastal freight out of Port Anselm. Magistrate Duarte sits for the colonial circuit there — a real one, not Corvane's drinking friend here. A man who has spent his career looking for exactly this kind of paper, because companies like Halcyon have done this on three coasts already and the Crown is embarrassed by it. Embarrassment, Mira, is the one thing money can't fully patch." He tapped the journal. "These pages are worth nothing in a drawer. They're worth everything in front of Duarte. But they have to travel four hundred miles of coast, past Halcyon's agents at every customs desk, and they can't travel in any way that points back to you."

"And you're offering—"

"A boat is leaving after the spring tides. Small cargo, honest manifest, dishonest everything else. Your father's journal can ride in the false bottom of my chart locker, where it'll be safer than it is in your own house." He held up a finger before I could speak. "Before you argue: yes, it's dangerous for me too. Yes, I've considered that. No, I'm not doing it for you specifically, I'm doing it because Corvane's people boarded my cousin's pinnace last month and 'requisitioned' half her hold without paying, and there are men on this coast who have wanted Halcyon taken down a peg for years and never had the paper to do it. You've gone and made the paper, peaches. You're the most dangerous woman in this port and you did it with a needle."

Despite everything — despite the fear sitting in my stomach like swallowed ballast — I laughed. It came out of me unexpectedly, a real laugh, and Tomás looked absurdly pleased, as if he'd planned it.

"There she is," he said. "I thought the ink had eaten you alive."

"It may yet."

"Well." He reached across and turned the journal so its spine faced me, my own crooked stitches catching the lamplight. "Eat it first, then. Eat it well. If we're doing this, we do it right: full copies of everything, dates verified against the ledgers, your father's rosters cross-checked with the divers themselves where you can do it quietly. Duarte will want corroboration, not just accusation. Corvane's friends will call it forgery — a girl's spite-work. It has to be iron."

"You've thought about this."

"I've thought about Halcyon my whole life, Mira. You're new to hating them; I was born to it." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. "One condition."

"Which is?"

"When the monsoon ends and the papers are away — when the ship comes that carries whatever comes next — you're on it. You and your father. Whatever this costs, you don't stay to pay it. Promise me."

And there it was again, the future arriving early to collect, dressed this time in my brother's voice instead of Corvane's. Everyone wanted my promise. Corvane wanted my silence; Tomás wanted my absence; my father wanted, God knew, only my safety, purchased at whatever price to his pride. Everyone had drawn up the terms of my life except me.

So I gave him the answer I seemed to be giving everyone lately.

"I promise," I said, "that when the time comes, I'll choose."

Tomás studied me for a long moment, and I watched him decide whether to press — whether to demand the specific word *leave* instead of the general word *choose*. He was a smuggler. He understood cargo, risk, the mathematics of holds. But he was also my brother, made not born, and brothers-made understand that some promises cannot be forced without breaking the very thing they're meant to protect.

"All right," he said finally, standing, taking the papaya core with him like evidence. "Choose. But choose fast. Spring tides wait for no one, and neither—" he paused at the door, and the humor drained out of his face entirely, leaving something older and graver underneath, "—neither does the monsoon. And Mira — tell Elias. Whatever you two are or aren't, he watches that water every night. He should know what he's watching for."

Then he was gone into the blue evening, and I sat alone with my father's journal and the sound of his cough through the thin wall, and I picked up my needle, and I began page ten.

Outside, far out past the harbor mouth, the sea lay flat and shining and patient, the way it always did before it decided otherwise.
