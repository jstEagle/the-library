## 6. The Monsoon Arrives

The first sign was the gulls. They left the lagoon all at one afternoon, a white ribbon of them pouring over the headland and away east like a thought too big to finish, and old Manolo looked up from his nets and said, "There it is, then," in the voice of a man greeting a relative he did not like but could not refuse at the door.

By nightfall the wind had come around to the southwest and begun to taste of iron.

Elias felt it before he heard it — the lighthouse hummed under his boots, a note low enough to be felt in the teeth. His father used to say the tower sang when the weather meant business, and for eleven years since his father's death Elias had climbed down into the lamp room whenever that note started, put his palm flat against the stone, and listened the way other men checked a child's forehead for fever.

He was doing exactly that when Mirabel came up the stairs two at a time with her hair already whipping loose from its pins.

"You're going to want more oil than usual," she said. "Tomás says this one's got a long back on it."

"Tomás says every storm has a long back."

"Tomás is right about most things and wrong loudly about the rest." She leaned against the doorframe, catching her breath, and Elias noticed — as he tried very hard not to notice, and failed, as he failed every time — that she still stood the way she stood over a ledger: weight even, eyes moving, tallying. Even now she was counting the storm through the window the way she counted everything, in columns and consequences.

"They've stopped loading," she said. "The *Halcyon Star* turned about mid-channel. Corvane's ships won't move again until February."

"I know."

"You know what that means?"

"It means," Elias said, "that nobody can be dispossessed by sea for four months, and the divers can breathe, and I can stop rehearsing arguments with harbor officials who are bought anyway." He paused. "And it means you'll have time to help me with the gallery rail, which has been trying to kill me since August."

Mirabel smiled — the quick, crooked one, the one she gave rarely and never to company men. "You didn't call me here for the rail."

"I called you here because there's a leak in the store room roof that I can't reach alone and you weigh less than I do."

"Elias."

"And," he admitted, "because I wanted someone to hear the first storm of the season with."

* * *

The monsoon did not arrive so much as take possession. For three days the wind came in long shoving gusts, then settled into a roar that made conversation on the gallery possible only if you shouted, and after that the rain came — not falling but arriving, sideways, in sheets, as though the sea had grown tired of waiting below and come up to collect the land personally.

They boarded the lower windows together, Elias holding the planks while Mirabel drove the nails with a neat, economical violence that surprised no one who had seen her close a deal. The store room leak turned out to be a cracked slate reachable only by lying flat on the wet roof and reaching past the gutter, which they accomplished in driving rain, both of them soaked through and laughing so hard by the end that Elias nearly slid off twice.

"There is something wrong with us," Mirabel gasped, flat on her back on the slates, rain hammering her face, holding the new slate in place while the masonry set.

"Statistically certain," Elias agreed. "Nearly everyone in this town is wrong in some way. We've simply chosen our wrongnesses well."

Inside afterward, wrapped in blankets that smelled of lamp oil and salt, they drank his father's terrible coffee — Elias had never found the courage to change the blend, and Mirabel had once described it, with love, as *seawater that had been told about coffee secondhand* — and watched the world outside the lamplit glass become nothing but gray motion.

It was, Elias thought, the happiest he had been in years. Which frightened him more than the storm did.

* * *

On the fifth day the wind eased just enough for the lagoon to show itself, and what it showed was strange and beautiful: the water gone glass-flat between squalls, the pearl beds visible in long dark ribbons beneath, and the divers' boats drawn up high on the sand in a huddle like sheep. The divers themselves gathered under the drying sheds, playing cards, arguing, mending gear that did not need mending, and — Elias learned when he carried down a crate of spare lamp glass — conducting a kind of parliament.

"The leases are suspended," Serafina the elder was saying as he came in. "Corvane calls it 'administrative pause.' You cannot pause a family's living like a clock."

"With respect," said her nephew, "a paused clock still exists. Let us use the pause."

Elias set the crate down. "The light will hold through anything this season throws," he said, because it was expected of him, and then, because he could not help himself: "Use the pause for what?"

Silence, the particular silence of people deciding how much a lighthouse keeper's son should know. It was Mirabel who broke it — she'd been sitting near the stove with her father's journal open on her knee, though Elias had noticed she wasn't reading it.

"For getting ready," she said lightly. "For spring. For whatever spring brings."

Everyone nodded as though this were an ordinary sentence. Elias, who had known Mirabel Marchetti since she was nine years old and had once helped her bury a stolen compass in a biscuit tin behind his father's coal shed — *for later*, she'd said, with the gravity of a general — understood perfectly that he had just been told nothing, on purpose, by everyone, together.

He did not ask. He walked home through the squall with the question sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone.

That evening he found her in the lamp room, polishing the Fresnel lenses with the cloth he kept for that purpose, unasked, the way she had done since she was tall enough to reach them.

"You're planning something," he said.

Mirabel kept polishing. "I keep books, Elias. Planning is just bookkeeping done in advance."

"That's not a denial."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

He wanted to press. He wanted, badly, to say *tell me, let me carry some of it, whatever it weighs*. But he had learned — slowly, over years, mostly by watching her go stiff and polite whenever anyone demanded her confidence — that Mirabel's secrets were not locked boxes but held breath. You did not grab a diver's arm while she surfaced. You stayed at the top of the water and you waited, and you made sure she knew where the boat was.

So instead he said, "Hand me the rouge cloth. The third panel has a spot."

They polished glass side by side while the wind leaned on the tower and the great lens turned its slow patient circles overhead, and after a while Mirabel's shoulders came down from around her ears, and she began, without preamble, to tell him about the storm of 1889 — not the official version but her father's version, in which the lighthouse keeper of that year had been drunk and the light had gone dark for forty minutes and her father, then a young diver, had steered three boats in by feel and memory and been sick with terror afterward where no one could see.

"He never told anyone that last part," Mirabel said. "Told me when I was twelve, to explain why he checks your light every night from the water. He counts your flashes. Did you know? Every night, out of habit, twenty years on."

Elias had not known. Something moved in his chest and would not settle. "Twenty years," he managed. "Your father has been auditing my work ethic since before I was born."

"He'd say he was quality control."

"He'd be right." Elias looked at the turning lens rather than at her, because some things were easier said to glass. "Tell him the flashes have never missed. Not once. Even the night my father died — I was fourteen, I didn't know how the mechanism—" He stopped. Started again. "Even that night. The light went round. Someone had to make sure the boats came home."

Mirabel set down the cloth. In the revolving glow her face came bright and fell dark and came bright again, like something seen from a ship.

"I know," she said quietly. "That's the year I decided."

"Decided what?"

But she only shook her head and reached past him to check the clockwork, and Elias understood that he had been given the edge of an answer, and that the rest would come or it would not, on its own schedule, like weather.

* * *

It came nine days into the monsoon, at two in the morning, in the middle of the worst blow of the season.

The generator coughed — once, twice — and caught, and Elias was already out of bed and on the stairs when the second sound came: knocking at the base of the tower, urgent, fists on wood. He opened the door to a wall of rain and Mirabel inside it, hooded, wild-eyed, holding a lantern that the wind kept trying to take from her.

"The lamp—" she shouted over the roar.

"Is fine. It coughed and caught. Come in before the sea takes you."

She came in, dripping, and stood in the entryway making a puddle of heroic proportions, and Elias saw that under the fear in her face there was embarrassment, and under the embarrassment something else entirely.

"I saw it go dark from the house," she said. "One flicker. And I—"

"Put on boots, took the lantern, and ran a quarter mile through a cyclone."

"Yes."

"To check on a light you could see had relit."

"...Yes."

Elias did not laugh, though it cost him. Instead he took the lantern from her hands, set it down, and pulled the dry blanket off the hook by the door — the one he had hung there eleven days ago, without admitting to himself whom it was for — and put it around her shoulders.

"Sit by the stove," he said. "I'll go up and check the mechanism properly. Then we'll drink the terrible coffee, and then, Mirabel, you are going to tell me what woke you before the flicker did. Because you were already awake. Nobody looks that afraid of weather."

She sat. He climbed the stairs, checked the gears with hands that were steadier than he'd expected, and came back down to find her staring into the stove with the blanket up to her chin, looking about fifteen years old.

"Tomás sails when the monsoon breaks," she said, without looking up. "East. With papers. Evidence. What Corvane is doing to the beds, to the divers, to the leases. What he tried to do to me."

Elias lowered himself onto the bench across from her. The wind screamed against the shutters like something impatient.

"The surveyors," he said. "At night. That's what you're building the case from."

"Partly. There's more. There's—" Her jaw worked. "There are things I can't say yet, Elias, and I need you to not ask, because every person who knows is a person Corvane can lean on, and I couldn't bear—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "I couldn't bear it being you."

The words landed somewhere below his ribs and stayed there.

"Then I won't ask," he said. "But hear my terms. One: the light stays lit, every night, no matter what, so that whatever happens, the boats can find their way home. That part is mine and I don't share it. Two:" — he leaned forward, elbows on knees, and made himself say it plainly, because his father had raised him plain — "you come here on the bad nights. Whatever hour. Not to check on the lamp. To check on the fact that you shouldn't be carrying this alone anymore, even if you carry it quietly."

Mirabel looked at him across the small stove-lit room while the century outside rattled its shutters, and Elias watched her do the thing she always did — the tallying, the weighing, the column of costs — and then he watched her stop doing it. Watched her simply sit, for once, inside a number she hadn't calculated.

"You've loved me since the biscuit tin," she said at last. It was not a question.

"You buried that compass facing north-northwest," Elias said. "Wrong by six degrees. I've loved you since I corrected it and you threw a shell at my head."

Her mouth trembled — laughter and something older fighting for the same ground — and then she crossed the small space between the benches and put her forehead against his, rain-cold, and stayed there, and the storm went on shouting at the walls of the little tower as though it objected to being ignored.

"I don't know how this ends," she whispered.

"Nobody ever does," said Elias. "That's what the light is for. So it doesn't matter quite so much."

* * *

The monsoon settled into its long middle age after that — weeks of gray, broken by furious blue hours when the whole town spilled outdoors blinking like creatures after a flood. Elias and Mirabel developed a routine so quiet neither of them named it: she came most evenings with bread or ledgers or both; he taught her the clockwork until she could strip and reset the rotation train blindfolded ("In case," he said, and she said, "*In case,*" in a voice that suggested she was filing the capability under plans he wasn't supposed to know about). They argued amiably about whether the terrible coffee was improving (it was not; Mirabel had begun smuggling better beans into the tin in increments, and Elias pretended not to notice, and this pretense was the tenderest thing either of them did).

On the first clear night of February, they stood on the gallery together after the lamp was lit, watching the lagoon shine black and silver below. Out at the anchorage, far off, a single shape rode at anchor that had not been there at dusk — a company vessel, riding lights burning, come sneaking in ahead of the season's end.

"She's early," Mirabel said.

"Ships are."

"She's a mail packet, Elias. Company mail comes on the *Halcyon Star*." Her hand found his on the rail and gripped it. "Mail packets carry passengers' berths. And telegrams."

Below them the light swept round, and round again, patient as twenty years, and Elias watched the stranger at anchor and felt the stolen season close gently behind them like a door.

The monsoon was breaking early this year.
