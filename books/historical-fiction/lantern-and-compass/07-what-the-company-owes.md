## 7. What the Company Owes

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Mirabel would later decide was fitting. Tuesdays were reconciliation days at Halcyon House — the day the ledgers were balanced against the day books, the day small discrepancies were hunted down and corrected. Of course Corvane would choose a Tuesday. He thought of her as a discrepancy.

The message was delivered by a boy of perhaps twelve who stood in the doorway of their rooms above the chandlery, holding out a cream-colored card as though it might bite him. It might have been the rain running off his cap in sheets, or the way the whole street smelled of drowned charcoal, but Mirabel took the card and felt the weight of it immediately — good paper, heavy stock, the company's crest embossed rather than printed. Mr. Corvane did not send notes. He sent small monuments.

*Miss Marchetti will call upon the manager at four o'clock. The matter is not optional.*

"The matter is not optional," she read aloud, and her father looked up from his chair by the window, where he sat wrapped in two blankets despite the wet heat, doing the thing he did now instead of diving: watching the lagoon and pretending he wasn't measuring it.

"Corvane," he said. Not a question. A man who had spent thirty years underwater learned to recognize the shape of a shark by its shadow.

"Mr. Corvane," she confirmed, tucking the card into her apron pocket. "He wants to discuss my indenture."

Her father was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere down the lane, Tomás's dog was barking at the sea, which the dog did every monsoon, on principle, as though the ocean could be scolded into better behavior.

"Elias will walk you there," her father said finally.

"Elias doesn't need to—"

"Elias will walk you there." His voice had the flat calm he used for weather that was about to turn. "And you'll stand where he can see the door."

* * *

She made Elias wait across the street from Halcyon House, under the awning of the coffee house, which closed during the monsoon and whose owner was content to let a lighthouse keeper's son shelter in exchange for someone to complain to later. She could feel him watching through the glass as she crossed the muddy street — she knew the particular weight of Elias's attention the way sailors knew the pull of a current, without looking, without needing to.

Inside, the counting room was nearly empty. Monsoon meant few ships, few cargoes, and clerks released early to their lodgings. Only Corvane remained, seated behind his desk like a man posing for a portrait titled *Reasonable Authority*, and beside the desk — this detail she catalogued instantly, because she was her father's daughter and a bookkeeper besides — stood the company solicitor, a thin man named Prewitt who had the face of a man perpetually smelling something he was too polite to mention.

"Miss Marchetti." Corvane gestured to the chair before his desk. "Sit, please. Tea?"

"I know where the tea is kept," she said. "I've restocked it eleven times."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth. Corvane appreciated precision; it was one of the things that made him dangerous. He appreciated it the way a fisherman appreciated an honest net — as a tool that could be relied upon to hold.

"Prewitt," he said, "the file."

Prewitt opened a leather folder and read aloud, in the dry voice of a man reading a laundry list: "Mirabel Marchetti, entered indenture at fourteen years of age, debt of passage and training assessed at forty-one pounds, twelve shillings. Served five years, three months. Outstanding balance—" he paused delicately, "—nine pounds, six shillings, calculated to date."

"Nineteen months," Mirabel said. "Nineteen months and I'm free."

"Nineteen months," Corvane agreed pleasantly, "at current performance." He slid a sheet of paper across the desk. "However. There are provisions in the standard contract regarding *unsatisfactory conduct*. Irregularities in accounts. Breaches of confidentiality." He let the last words sit in the air like flies. "Should such conduct be established, the term of indenture may be extended at the company's discretion. Two years. Three. At the company's discretion."

Mirabel looked at the paper. It was an affidavit. She had written dozens of them herself, in this very building, for divers attesting they had received their fair share, for boat captains attesting the company's cargo weights were true. She knew exactly what an affidavit was worth, because she had watched them manufactured.

"You want me to sign that I never saw the letter."

"I want you to sign that no letter exists containing irregular references to lagoon leases," Corvane said mildly. "Which happens to be true, since the letter in question was destroyed. One cannot attest to the contents of ash, Miss Marchetti. Even you can't balance that account."

"And if I don't sign?"

Corvane rose, walked to the window, hands clasped behind his back. Below, the harbor lay gray and churning, the ships straining at their moorings like dogs that smelled lightning coming.

"Then I regret to inform you that certain irregularities have come to light in your handling of the salvage ledgers. Nothing criminal — I would never suggest that a Marchetti was dishonest; your father's reputation is iron. But *irregular*. Enough to extend your indenture. Enough, also—" he turned, and his eyes were not unkind, which was somehow worse, "—to place your father's name on the diver blacklist. Every bed, every berth, every buying station from here to the capital. He would never work the water again. And Miss Marchetti, between us — the man needs to be working less, not never. You've seen his cough."

The rain filled the silence. Mirabel kept her hands folded in her lap and thought about all the things she could say and all the things they would cost. This was the discipline the ledgers had taught her: every word drawn against a balance. She had learned arithmetic from men like this. It seemed only fair to use it back.

"You've made a careful offer," she said at last. "You always do. But you should check your figures, Mr. Corvane. You're assuming the letter was the only copy."

Something moved behind his face — quick, then mastered. Prewitt's pen stopped scratching.

"That sounds very nearly like a threat," Corvane said softly.

"It sounds very nearly like bookkeeping," said Mirabel, and stood, and left the affidavit on the desk, unsigned, squared neatly against the desk's edge the way she squared everything.

Elias fell into step beside her halfway across the street, saying nothing, offering his umbrella, matching her pace. It was only two blocks later, when her hands started shaking so badly she couldn't manage the gate latch, that he took her elbow and steered her toward the lighthouse instead of home.

"Cocoa," he said, in the tone of a man announcing a strategic necessity. "My father keeps the good stuff for storms."

* * *

They sat in the lamp room while the rain hammered the glass, and Elias's father — a vast, kind, mostly silent man whom everyone called Keeper, including, Mirabel suspected, his own wife, wherever she'd gone — brought up three cups and left without commentary, which was the Keeper's particular genius.

"He can't do it," Elias said. "Extend your indenture. There'd have to be a hearing. Evidence."

"There'd have to be evidence he *manufactures*," Mirabel said. "Elias, I keep those ledgers. I could make a saint look like a thief, or a thief look like a saint, and nobody would ever catch it but another bookkeeper. That's what he taught me. That's what he *paid me* to learn." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I'm fluent in the language they use to hurt people."

"You're fluent in the language they use to *count*," Elias said. "It's not the same thing." He turned his cup slowly. Steam rose off the cocoa in the cold lamproom air. "Tomás says the divers are already asking questions. Half of them saw the surveyors' boats that night. They know something's coming even if they don't know what. When it comes — when Corvane moves — those people are going to need somebody who speaks ledger."

"Then Corvane will bury that somebody."

"Maybe," Elias admitted. He had his mother's stubborn jaw and his father's horizon-watching habit, and at the moment both were aimed at her with unbearable steadiness. "But you should know that when my father talks about you — and he doesn't talk much, so when he does it counts — he says you're the kind of person a lighthouse is for. Somebody out in the dark, still doing the work, whether anyone sees or not."

Mirabel looked into her cocoa. "That's the nicest thing anyone's ever said about me and I understood none of the comfort of it."

"That's because being a lighthouse is a terrible job," Elias said. "Ask anyone."

* * *

Her father was waiting when she got home, and she knew before she got her boots off that he had heard. News traveled through this town faster than sickness and stuck faster than salt. Old Chandra from the drying racks had probably told the chandler's wife, who told everyone within earshot of the church bell, which was everyone.

"So," her father said. "He offered you freedom for a signature."

"He offered me slavery for a signature. Freedom was just the bait."

"And you refused."

It was not a question, and she didn't treat it as one. She hung her wet coat and began ladling rice into bowls, because her hands wanted employment and her mouth wanted a delay.

"Mirabel." Her father's voice stopped her the way a hand on the arm would have. "Sit down."

She sat.

Sergio Marchetti had been a big man once — broad through the shoulders, hands like spliced rope — and even diminished, coughing, wrapped in his blanket, he occupied more room than his body explained. He studied her for a long moment with eyes that had spent thirty years reading water and now read his daughter just as easily.

"When your mother died," he said, "the company held my debt. Forty pounds. I dove seven years to clear it, and every year the interest ate what the pearls earned, and every year I dove deeper to make up the difference. That is how the company owns people, Mirabel. Not with chains. With arithmetic." He coughed, wet and long, and waved off her reaching hand. "I did not fight them because fighting them meant losing, and losing meant you went hungry. Do you understand? Everything I did — every dive, every year of silence — I did *for you*."

"I know, Papà."

"Then hear me." He leaned forward, and his voice, though thinned, had the old bedrock in it. "You will not fight them. You will sign whatever he puts in front of you, you will serve your nineteen months, you will keep your head below the waterline until the debt is paid. Promise me."

"And then what?" The words came out sharper than she intended. "Then the leases are voided and the divers lose the beds and we sit in a clean counting house somewhere, free, watching them tow away the boats of every person who carried me through every winter of my life? Auntie Rupa fed me when you were at the bottom of the lagoon. Tomás taught me to swim. The Oyelas family lent us boat money when your gear was stolen. What do I owe *them*, Papà? Because I've been keeping accounts since I was nine years old, and I am telling you — that debt is on the books, and it has interest, and it is not going away."

For a moment something flared in her father's face — pride, she thought, quickly drowned, deliberately drowned, the way a man drowns a fire he cannot afford to feed.

"You are twenty years old," he said quietly. "You think debts are paid in justice. They are paid in years. I have fewer of them than you do, and I am telling you how it is spent best." He reached out and took her hand, and his grip, once able to crack oysters barehanded, was light as paper. "Promise me, *figlia mia*. Sign the paper. Be safe."

Mirabel looked at his hand around hers, at the blanket, the untouched rice, the window where the lagoon he loved was busy drowning the town that had bled him. And she lied to her dying father for the first time in her life.

"I promise I won't do anything foolish," she said, which was not quite the same thing, and they both pretended it was.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat at the little table by the guttering candle with her father's diving journal open before her — the log of thirty years of bottom time, depths and durations and pearl counts recorded in a hand that had grown shakier page by page, year by year, the handwriting itself a chart of the disease eating him.

Between the pages lay the documents. The lease papers with their suspicious annotations. The roster of licensed divers. The scrap of the burned letter with *the Marchetti family* legible in Corvane's own crisp hand.

She had promised not to fight. She turned the promise over in her mind the way she turned figures, testing its load-bearing walls. She had promised not to fight. Nobody had said anything about *keeping records*.

Mirabel dipped her pen.

Under the entry for 14 June 1893 — forty fathoms, two hours, nine shells — she began, in her smallest hand, in the cramped cipher-shorthand she'd invented at fifteen to practice sums in secret, to write down everything. Dates. Amounts. Names. Who signed what, who witnessed it, where each original document slept. A ledger inside a ledger. An account of what the company owed — not in pounds and shillings, but in leases and lungs and years.

Somewhere outside, the storm found its full voice, and the candle guttered, and she wrote.

Near dawn she heard her father cough twice in his sleep, deep and hollow, like a bell rung underwater —

— and when she pressed the journal flat beneath her mattress and blew out the light, she was already thinking about boats.
