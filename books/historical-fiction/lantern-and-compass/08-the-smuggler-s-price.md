## 8. The Smuggler's Price

Tomás Ferreira had three rules for moving cargo that did not officially exist, and he recited them now, in his head, while Mirabel laid the pages out on his cabin table like a fortune-teller dealing a very bad hand.

Rule one: never carry what you cannot eat, burn, or throw overboard. Rule two: never let the cargo know it is cargo. Rule three: never, under any circumstances, fall in love with anyone involved in the transaction.

He was zero for three.

"Say it again," he said, because the lamp was guttering and he wanted to blame the light. "Slowly. As if I were stupid."

Mirabel looked up at him with that expression she'd had since she was nine years old and had caught him stealing oranges from the company store — half fondness, half arithmetic. "You are not stupid, Tomás. You are merely a man who has built his whole life out of not being responsible for anything."

"I am a *boatman*," Tomás said, with dignity. "The sea is responsible for me. It's an arrangement we have."

She smoothed the papers flat on the table. His cabin — if you could call four planks and a hammock a cabin — smelled of tar and old rain, and in the lamplight her hands moved like they did when she balanced the Halcyon ledgers: precise, unhurried, as if order were something you could talk into existing.

"This is the letter fragment," she said. "The one naming my family in the pearl-bed scheme. This is the lease schedule — see here, every bed marked in red comes up for 'review' at the end of the monsoon. Review means voided. And this is the diver roster, cross-referenced against who owes what. Corvane isn't buying the beds, Tomás. He's manufacturing debts so he can seize them."

Tomás picked up the letter scrap between two fingers, the way he'd pick up something that might bite. The paper was scorched at one edge. Someone in the company offices had tried to feed this to a stove, and Mirabel had fished it out like a cat pulling a fish from a market barrel, and now here they all were, ruined.

"You know what this is?" he said. "This is not contraband. Contraband is simple. Contraband wants nothing from me except silence and speed. This—" he tapped the roster "—this wants me to be *brave*. I've met brave. Brave drowns."

"Brave also eats," said Mirabel. "Eventually."

"That's the rumor."

* * *

He should have said no. He wanted that on the record, somewhere, in some great ledger of the sky where all of Tomás Ferreira's sensible decisions were recorded — a thin book, admittedly, but lovingly maintained.

He should have said no, and instead he found himself the next morning standing on the dock at low tide with Elias Quill, watching the harbor through a spyglass he had definitely not acquired from a navy man who owed him money.

"There," Elias said. He didn't take the spyglass; he'd grown up reading water and knew it better than glass could show. "Company launch. Third time this week it's circled the anchorage. They're counting boats."

"They're always counting boats. Counting boats is free. I count boats."

"They're counting *our* boats." Elias had his mother's patience and his father's jaw, and just now the jaw was set in a way Tomás distrusted deeply, because it meant the boy had decided something. "When the monsoon lifts, Corvane moves. Mirabel's papers say it. You've seen them."

"I've seen them," Tomás admitted. "I've also seen a man lose a hand to a winch because he wouldn't wait one more minute for the tide, so forgive me if my first instinct is *wait for the tide*."

"And your second instinct?"

Tomás lowered the spyglass. Out on the lagoon the water lay flat and pewter-colored, pre-storm calm, and the divers' boats sat moored in their crooked little fleet like toys left out by a careless giant. Old Marchetti's boat among them, patched twice where it should be replaced once. Somewhere out there the old man was coughing into the wind and calling it salt.

"My second instinct," Tomás said heavily, "is apparently to become a person who does things for other people, which as I understand it is a disease with no cure."

Elias almost smiled. "So you'll take the papers."

"I'll take the papers." Tomás held up a finger before relief could settle too comfortably on the younger man's face. "*But.* Not for free, and not for money. Money I can refuse. What I want—" and here he made himself say it, because a price named plainly hurts less than one discovered later, or so his mother had claimed, and his mother had been wrong about nearly everything but he kept her sayings anyway, like ballast, "—is the Marchettis. Both of them. On this boat, when the monsoon ends, gone east before Corvane can spit. Mirabel signs nothing more for the company. Her father dives nowhere near those beds. We go to the capital, we put these papers in front of whatever magistrate will look at them, and then we keep going until this port is a story we tell badly at dinner parties. That is my price, Elias Quill, and it is not negotiable, and I hate it, and I'm making it anyway."

Elias was quiet a moment. Beyond him the lighthouse stood white against the graying sky, and Tomás watched the boy glance at it the way a man glances at a woman he hasn't yet found the courage to be obvious about.

"The lighthouse can't sail," Elias said quietly. "You're asking her to choose."

"I'm asking her to *live*. There's a difference, though granted it's subtle and most people miss it."

"She won't run. You know that better than I do."

"Of course she won't run," Tomás snapped. "I raised half of her. She got her stubbornness from me and her conscience from everyone else, which is the worst possible division of assets. But I'm stating the price anyway. That's how prices work. You state what you wish things cost and then reality laughs at you."

* * *

He told her that evening, aboard the *Salvadora*, over the fish stew he pretended was his own recipe and had actually learned from a one-eyed cook in a port whose name he changed every time he told the story.

"When the monsoon breaks," he said, ladling with a generosity he reserved for serious conversations, "I sail east. Papers go to the magistrate. And you and your father come with me."

Mirabel's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. Then completed the journey, because she was Mirabel, and even world-altering proposals did not excuse letting good stew cool.

"With you," she repeated.

"On this boat. To the capital. Away." He counted the words off on his fingers. "It's three ideas but one sentence, which I consider efficient."

"Tomás—"

"No. Hear the whole offer before you start dismantling it, you always start dismantling, you're like a customs officer." He set down the ladle. The cabin lamp swung gently with the swell, and shadows moved across her face, and he hated how easily he could read it, after all these years: not surprise. Grief arriving early, getting ahead of itself. "Here is what happens if you stay. I've done the sums, since nobody else in this family respects arithmetic. Corvane knows you saw the letter. He'll squeeze. First the contract, then the rations, then your father's boat rights, and then — Mira, listen to me — then he doesn't need tricks anymore, because a man with a cough like your father's can be killed by an ordinary winter. No plot required. Just time and weather and the company looking away. That's the ledger if you stay. That's the whole column."

She was silent. Through the hull came the small patient sounds of the harbor at night — halyards ticking against masts, water worrying the pilings.

"And if I go?" she said finally. Her voice was very level, which was worse than shouting. "If Papa and I sit safely in the capital while you take the papers — Tomás, if you're caught with those pages, it isn't a fine. It's a rope, or whatever the colonial courts use when they're feeling modern and efficient. And if you *aren't* caught, and the magistrate takes months, and meanwhile Corvane voids the leases anyway because nobody's here to stand in front of the divers and say *no, this is ours, I have the record of it* — then what? I save my own skin and watch the beds go from a distance? I was raised on those beds. Half my blood is lagoon water and the other half is ink, and you're asking me to ship both halves east."

"I'm asking you to *survive*," Tomás said. "It's a modest request. People do it every day. There are entire villages of survivors, extremely dull places."

"And who keeps the books here?"

"The books don't need—"

"Somebody always keeps the books," she said. "That's the lesson of my whole life. Somebody writes down who owes what. Corvane writes it one way. If everyone who knows the truth leaves, then his version is the only version left standing, and I might as well have burned that letter myself."

Tomás stood up abruptly, which in a cabin the size of his meant he was now essentially standing in the stew. He braced a hand against the bulkhead and looked at her — this girl he'd taught to swim by throwing her off the dock, this woman who now out-argued him in his own home waters — and felt the particular ache of loving someone whose courage you admired precisely because you'd never managed any yourself.

"Then make me a different offer," he said. "Go on. Name it."

"You carry the papers. That part stands — I'm not proud enough to risk those pages on my own seamanship, which is none. You take them east, through the tail of the monsoon, and God and the Virgin and whichever saints owe you favors keep you from company patrol boats."

"And?"

"And I can't promise you the rest." Her hands were flat on the table now, steady, as if the truth needed ballast. "I can't promise you we flee, Tomás. I can't promise it because I don't know yet what I'll owe when the storm ends. Ask me then. Ask me when the summons — if there's a summons — ask me when I've seen whether the papers land. I'll promise you this much and no more: I will not lie to you between now and then. Not one word. That's everything I have."

"That's nothing," Tomás said. "That's a promise shaped like a hole."

"It's the only honest shape there is."

They looked at each other across the table, across twenty years of shared bread and stolen oranges and her holding his hair back the one time he'd drunk the bad rum, and Tomás thought: *rule two, never let the cargo know it is cargo*, and understood with perfect clarity that he had failed it years ago. These people had never been passengers to him. They were the ship.

"All right," he said, and heard his own voice go rough. "All right. Papers east. Question open. But hear *my* terms, Mira, and mark them well." He leaned close, jabbing a finger at the table as if driving nails. "If I come back with a magistrate behind me and find you still standing on this dock like a lantern someone forgot to snuff, I will be unbearable. I will bring it up at every meal for the rest of our lives. Every anniversary of this night. 'Remember when Mirabel Marchetti chose ledgers over living?' There will be songs."

"There won't be songs."

"There will be *songs*, and I will compose them myself, and I rhyme terribly on purpose when I'm angry."

Despite herself — despite everything — her mouth twitched. "That," she said, "is genuinely the worst threat ever spoken in this harbor."

"I mean every word."

"I know you do." She reached across and squeezed his wrist, brief and hard, the way she'd held onto him in rough water once, long ago, trusting him entirely and apologizing for it at the same time. "Thank you. For the price. Even though I can't pay it."

"Yeah, well." He turned away busily to stir a stew that needed no stirring. "Everyone owes something. It's practically your religion."

* * *

Three days later, provisioned and oath-sworn, Tomás went down to the harbor office to pay his anchorage fee — a formality he usually delegated to forgetting — and found the queue doubled, and the mood wrong. Dockmen muttered. A clerk he knew by his gambling debts jerked his chin toward the posted notice board, where a new sheet hung, crisp as a slap.

*By order of the Harbor Master,* it read. *All outbound berths for the season following the monsoon are hereby allocated under Company supervision. Vessels without assigned berth clearance will not be cleared to sail.*

Every berth. The whole departing year, locked behind one office door — and Tomás Ferreira, reading it twice with the letter fragment burning in his memory and Mirabel's promise-shaped hole sitting in his chest, realized that the company wasn't only trying to keep people in.

Someone was keeping track of exactly who could get out.
