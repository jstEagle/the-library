## 10. The Diver's Lungs

My father fell in the market on a Tuesday, between the fishmonger's stall and the spice seller's awning, and he would be mortified to know how many people saw it do it.

That was the thing about Papa. He had spent thirty years of his life half-naked in a lagoon, holding his breath against the pressure of deep water, surfacing with pearls and oysters while the sun tried to boil the sea — and through all of it he had maintained the unshakeable conviction that he was a private man. He did not fall. He did not cough in front of company. He did not, under any circumstances, require help.

So when his knees went out from under him in front of forty witnesses and a crate of mackerel, he did what any dignified man would do: he attempted to make it look intentional. He reached out one hand as though reaching for something on a low shelf, lowered himself with terrible grace into the dust of the market square, and sat there, breathing in whistles, pretending to inspect the fish.

"Papa," I said, arriving too late to catch him, because I had been haggling over rice two stalls down and my mother's daughter does not abandon a negotiation mid-sentence even for catastrophe.

"I am looking at this mackerel," he said, from the ground.

"You are lying in it."

"The dust is good for the joints."

The fishmonger, a wide woman named Serafina who had known my father since before I was born, leaned over her stall and looked at him the way you look at a boat taking on water. "Marchetti," she said, "you're gray."

"It is early."

"It is noon."

I got my arm under his and we walked home the long way, past the harbor, so that no one would see us take the shortcut behind the chandlery where the footing was bad and he might stumble again. He weighed nothing. That was the part that frightened me most, I think, though I didn't know yet that it should frighten me — I thought only that Papa had always been built like a bollard, knotted and salt-hard, and now when I took his weight he felt like driftwood. Like something the sea had already finished with and given back.

He talked the whole way home to keep me from talking. He told me about the price of mackerel. He told me a story about a Portuguese captain he'd once guided through the channel in fog, a story I had heard eleven times and could have recited myself, and I let him tell it because his voice was the only instrument either of us had for measuring how bad things were, and by its thinness I could read the depth.

At the door of our rooms above the sailmaker's loft, he stopped and gripped the frame and coughed — the diving cough, the one he'd had for two years, dry at first and then wet, the one we had all learned to call old man's weather and treat with rum and honey and the fiction that divers simply wore out, the way ropes did, without anything so dramatic as a cause.

This time the cough went on long enough that a neighbor opened her shutters.

"Wind," said Papa, to the shutter, to the street, to me. "Dust from the market."

"There was no dust in the market," I said. "Serafina sweeps twice a day."

"Then it is the fish."

"Papa."

"Mira." He turned and looked at me, and there was something in his face I had never seen there before, or rather something I had seen only once, years ago, at my mother's grave: the expression of a man who has run out of explanations and knows it. "Don't," he said. "Not yet. Let me have my supper first. Let me be well until after supper."

So we had supper. He ate half of what I put in front of him and told the story of the Portuguese captain a twelfth time, and I laughed in the right places, and afterward he slept sitting up in his chair because lying flat made the cough worse, which I pretended not to notice, which he pretended not to notice me noticing. The lamp burned low. Out in the lagoon the water lay flat and pewter-colored under a rising moon, and somewhere out there the night boats were working the beds, and every one of those divers would surface tonight and cough in the dark, and every one of them would call it nothing.

I sat across from my sleeping father and wrote in the margin of his diving journal — my ledger habit dying hard even in the margins of someone else's book — *Tuesday. He fell. He is gray. He ate half.*

And then, because I could not stop being what the Halcyon Company had made me, I added: *Half of very little.*

* * *

Dr. Anselm kept his surgery above the apothecary, up a staircase that smelled of camphor and disappointment. He was a small, tidy, exhausted man who had come out from the capital twenty years earlier meaning to stay eighteen months, and he examined my father the next morning with the resigned tenderness of someone who has delivered this same news many times and hates it more each time.

He listened to Papa's chest for a long while. Then he listened some more. Then he asked Papa to breathe in, breathe out, say ninety-nine — which Papa did in his own language, sounding like a man reciting a curse — and Dr. Anselm straightened up and looked at me over my father's shoulder with a face like a closed ledger.

"Well," said Papa cheerfully. "Out with it. Is it the fish?"

"It is consumption," said Dr. Anselm.

The word went into the room and sat down among us like a paying passenger. Nobody moved to give it a chair.

Papa laughed. Actually laughed, a short bark that turned halfway through into coughing, and Dr. Anselm put a hand on his back and waited him out with professional patience while I stood by the window and felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts.

"Divers don't get consumption," Papa said, when he could. "Divers get the cough. The cough is nothing. The cough is the water leaving the body. It is the sea paying rent."

"The sea has been paying rent in your lungs for thirty years," said Anselm. "The rent is overdue. Mr. Marchetti, the disease may have started as the damage every diver carries — the broken alveoli, the scar tissue, all of it. But what is in your chest now is consumption, full stop. It is in both lungs. It is advanced."

"How advanced?"

Anselm hesitated, which was answer enough, but he was an honest man and gave the rest of it anyway. "You will have good weeks. You may even have a good month or two. But another season here — another monsoon in these rooms, another winter of damp, and God forbid another dive—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Another dive would kill you outright. I want that understood. No more water. None. Not wading."

Papa looked at him with genuine offense. "I have been in the water since before your grandfather owned a stethoscope."

"And it has sent you your bill." Anselm took off his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose, and for a moment he was not a doctor but just a tired man far from home. "There is a sanatorium in the hills past the capital. Dry air, high altitude. It is not luxurious. But men go there to die of old age instead of this. It requires—" he glanced at me, and I understood the glance; doctors learn quickly what families can bear to hear in front of the patient— "it requires travel, and funds, and time. Time most of all. He cannot earn his passage by diving, and he must not delay until he is too weak to travel at all."

"How much time?" I asked.

"If he rests? If he goes soon?" Anselm put his spectacles back on and became clinical again, because precision was his way of being kind. "Months. A year, perhaps, if the hills are kind to him. If he stays here and keeps working the beds — I will not insult either of you by pretending that is a real option, but if it were, then less. Considerably less."

We walked home slowly, Papa leaning on my arm and pretending not to, and he was silent for three entire streets, which for my father was the equivalent of a week of silence for anyone else.

"Your mother's brother," he said at last, "had the same thing."

"I didn't know that."

"He hid it well. He died in spring. Everyone said it was sudden." Papa snorted softly. "It was not sudden. He simply counted better than the rest of us, and never let his face show the sum."

"Papa—"

"Mira." He stopped walking. He turned to look at me, and his eyes were clear and brown and entirely, terribly calm. "Before you begin. Before you start making lists. I want you to hear me: I am seventy pounds of wet rope held together by stubbornness, and I have heard what the man said. I am not stupid. But I am also not going to spend whatever time remains being ferried around like cargo and pitied in hill stations. I am going to die a diver, in sight of the lagoon, or I am going to be extremely annoyed in the afterlife."

"That is the stupidest thing you have ever said, and you once told a customs officer that contraband was a regional style of hat."

He smiled at that — a real smile, brief and crooked, my mother's smile worn loose on my father's face — and then it folded away and he looked out at the water, flat and bright and indifferent in the morning light.

"You were four years old," he said, "when I brought you down for the first time. Just below the surface, in the shallows, your hand in mine. You weren't afraid. You looked at everything. You looked at me like I was showing you a cathedral." His grip tightened on my arm, or perhaps I tightened on his; by then I honestly couldn't have said which of us was holding which up. "Everything I have done since your mother died, I did so that you would never have to hold your breath for anyone. And instead I signed you to Halcyon, and you have been underwater for nine years, holding your breath in ledgers instead of lagoons, and I have told myself it was different. It is not different."

"It is different."

"It is not." He started walking again, slower, and I matched him. "So. You will make your lists. You will find a way to turn my dying into arithmetic, because it is the only language you trust, and I forgive you for it because you are your mother's daughter and she did the same. But hear this one number clearly, Mira, and keep it separate from all the others: whatever you decide to do — whatever scheme you and that smuggler boy and the lighthouse family are cooking, and yes, I am old, not blind — you will not spend yourself buying me months I do not want and cannot use. Do you understand me?"

I understood him perfectly. I intended, with filial devotion, to disregard every word.

"Yes, Papa," I said.

* * *

I told Elias that evening, at the lighthouse, because I needed to tell someone and because Tomás was at sea and because Elias had this way of receiving bad news — steadily, without flinching, the way the tower itself received storms — that made telling him feel less like breaking and more like setting something heavy down.

He was up in the lamp room cleaning the Fresnel lenses when I came in, and he heard my footsteps on the stairs and called down, "If you've come to tell me the harbor master filed another complaint about the light, I've already drafted a reply and it contains the phrase 'insufficient imagination,' and you cannot stop me."

"My father has consumption."

The sound of the ladder. His feet on the stairs, quick, and then he was there in the lamplight with lens cloth still in one hand and his face gone carefully still, the stillness of a man bracing a door against wind.

"Mira."

"Both lungs. Advanced. The doctor says he can't survive another season here. There's a place in the hills, if he goes, if he goes soon, if—" My voice did something unauthorized on the last word, cracked sideways, and Elias crossed the space between us and simply held on, and I let the crack run its course against his shoulder with the lamp turning slow and steady above us like a patient heart.

When I had finished — not the crying, but the report; the crying was briefer than you'd think, because grief that has been rehearsed for two years under the alias of *the cough* performs poorly at its own premiere — Elias sat me down in the keeper's chair and put tea in my hands and asked exactly the right questions in exactly the wrong order, which is to say he asked practical ones first because practical questions are how he says *I love you* without alarming either of us.

"The berth," he said finally, quietly, into the pause. "The last ship of the year. One berth left that the company hasn't bought up."

"I know what you're going to say."

"Do you? Because I'm not sure I know what I'm going to say." He ran a hand through his hair, dislodging soot. "A month ago it was an abstraction. A number on a manifest. Now it's—" he gestured vaguely in the direction of town, of the sailmaker's loft, of the chair where my father slept upright— "it has a face. It has your father's face."

"It has three faces," I said, and hated myself even as I said it, because I knew what I meant and he knew what I meant: Papa, who could not survive another season. Tomás, who was carrying our evidence east through the tail of the monsoon with company agents hunting him, and who would need a way out when he came back, because Corvane would feed him to the harbor sharks without a trial. Me, who had an indenture that Corvane could extend with a signature and a grudge.

And Elias, who was bound to the lighthouse the way Papa was bound to the lagoon — not by paper, but by something older and less negotiable — and who had therefore removed himself from the arithmetic before anyone else had even begun it. I had caught him doing that. Subtracting himself quietly, the way careful people settle debts they don't owe.

"Don't," I said. "Whatever you're doing, in your head. Don't."

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're standing in a doorway counting exits. I can see it. You've already decided you're not in the equation."

Elias looked at me for a long moment. The lamp turned. Down below us the lagoon held the last of the daylight, and the first stars came out over the water, and somewhere out there a night boat's lantern swung slow along the beds where men were diving, right now, holding their breath.

"Fine," he said. "Then here's what I'm actually thinking, since you insist. One berth. Three people who need it, and only one of them dies without it. Your father needs it the way a drowning man needs it. Tomás needs it the way a hunted man needs it. You need it the way—" he stopped, and chose his words with visible care— "the way a person needs a future, which is real, Mira, but it is not the same as drowning."

"And so the answer is obvious."

"And so the answer is obvious," he agreed gently. "And you've known it since the doctor's office. So why do you look like a woman reading a column of figures that won't add?"

Because. Because Papa had forbidden me to spend myself on him and would refuse the berth out of pride if I presented it openly. Because Tomás had demanded the family flee together and would call it betrayal to be sent away alone. Because the berth was reserved, Elias suspected, for whoever Corvane chose, and taking it meant playing the company's game by the company's rules. Because every road out of this town ran through a man who wanted something from me, and I did not yet know which price I could afford to pay.

But what I said was: "Because I'm my father's daughter, and my mother's, and we count better than our faces show. And because tomorrow morning I have to walk into the Harbor House and sit at my desk across from Mr. Corvane and keep his books with these hands, knowing what his ledgers are doing to everyone I love, and now knowing that they are doing it faster than I can." I looked down at my tea. "Corvane summoned me, you know. This morning, before the doctor. A note on my desk. 'Miss Marchetti, my office, ten o'clock, regarding your contract.'"

Elias went very still. "Regarding your contract," he repeated. "That's the second time in a month he's used those words."

"The second time in a month," I agreed, "that he has smiled at me across a desk like a man holding the other end of a rope. And Elias — this morning I would have walked in there afraid. This evening I'm walking in there tomorrow having buried fear somewhere between the surgery and the lamp room, because I found out today that my father is dying, and a woman who knows exactly how much time she has left stops being frightened of men with stationery."

The lamp completed another slow revolution, sweeping its beam out across the black water, touching the far heads of the channel, coming round again.

"Ten o'clock," said Elias quietly.

"Ten o'clock," I said, and drank my tea, and watched the light go round, and began — the way I begin everything, the only way I know how, the way that has saved us and damned us and will, one way or another, save us or damn us again — to count.
