## 11. Corvane's Offer

Mr. Corvane sent for me on a Tuesday, which I found telling. Tuesdays were payroll days at the Halcyon Company, the one day of the week when every diver, rigger, and dockhand in the port had reason to hate the counting house, and therefore the one day when nobody looked twice at who went in or out of Corvane's office. If a girl was going to be summoned to the manager's office and seen doing it, Tuesday was the day you'd choose. He was a careful man. I had counted his carefulness for three years across a hundred ledger lines, and I knew its weight the way I knew the weight of coins by sound.

The clerk who fetched me — young Pell, whose collar never sat straight and whose ears turned red whenever I corrected his figures — would not meet my eyes on the walk up from the ledgers' room. That too was information. A man embarrassed for you walks beside you and stares at his shoes. A man frightened of you does the same. But a man frightened *for* you looks everywhere else, and Pell looked at the ceiling, the harbor, the gulls, as though one of them might intervene on his behalf.

"Mr. Corvane says to sit," Pell said at the door, and then, very quietly, so quietly I nearly missed it: "He's in a good mood. That's worse."

I could have kissed him for the warning. Instead I straightened my collar, tucked my father's diving journal more securely under my arm — where it lived now, always, the false pages flush against the true ones like a lie pressed to a heart — and went in.

* * *

Corvane's office smelled of lamp oil and dried cuttlefish ink, and beneath both, faintly, of the sea, which no amount of shuttered windows could keep out in this town. The town was built on the sea's sufferance and everyone knew it, even the company men who pretended otherwise with their wool suits and their imported paper. Corvane stood at his window with his back to me, watching the lagoon, and I took the opportunity to study him the way I studied anything I meant to survive: thoroughly and without sentiment.

He was not a large man. He was not a small man. He was one of those men who seemed sized exactly to whatever room he stood in, which I had come to understand was a kind of discipline rather than an accident. His hands were folded behind his back, and they were the only part of him that ever moved quickly — quick, precise hands, hands that had signed leases and suspended them, hands that had written my name into a contract when I was fourteen years old because my father's lungs were already going soft and the company needed a bookkeeper who worked for less than an honest wage.

"Miss Marchetti," he said, without turning. "Do you know what that is?" He nodded toward the harbor, where the *Vespera Kestrel* rode at anchor, the last ship out before the monsoon closed the roads entirely. Her masts made thin black marks against the gray sky, like tally strokes on a slate.

"A ship, Mr. Corvane."

He laughed. He had a good laugh; that was the unfairness of him. It was warm and unhurried and it made you want to laugh along, the way a fire makes you want to put your hands out. I had watched him deploy it in negotiations with divers who couldn't read their own contracts, and I had watched it work.

"A ship," he agreed. "One ship. And do you know how many berths she has left after the company's charter?"

"One," I said.

He turned then, and his face was doing something I had never seen it do. It was being kind. It fit him poorly, like his Sunday coat fit Pell — the right shape in theory, wrong in all its seams.

"One berth," Corvane said, "on the last ship of the year, out of a harbor I control, in a season that will drown anyone who tries to swim. Sit down, Miss Marchetti. Please." He indicated the chair across from his desk, and I noticed — carefully, without letting my eyes linger — that there were two documents upon it. One was thick. One was a single page.

I sat. I did not tuck the journal further under my arm, though everything in me wanted to. I set it instead on my knee, casual as a sewing basket, and folded my hands over it.

"You've been with us six years," Corvane said, settling into his own chair. "Six years, and in that time I have never once had cause to correct your figures. Do you know how rare that is? Pell miscounts monthly. The man before you stole from me for two years before I caught it, and I only caught him because he was stupid, and stupidity is the one sin this company punishes faster than theft." He laced his fingers together on the desk. "You are the best bookkeeper I have employed in nineteen years in this trade. Possibly the best I will ever employ, if we're being honest, and I'd like to be honest today. I have the feeling honesty is going to matter between us."

"That would make a pleasant change," I said, and immediately wished the words unsaid, but his smile only deepened, and I understood my error. He had *wanted* a little defiance. It proved to him I was frightened, and frightened people are easier to buy.

"I'm going to speak plainly," he said, "because you strike me as a woman who respects plain speech, and because the hour is late — in every sense. There is a letter." He let that sit. "A letter concerning certain leasehold arrangements in this lagoon, drafted through the head office, addressed in part to parties in this district. Some months ago a portion of that letter was destroyed in this building. Improperly destroyed, I might add — burning correspondence is not our practice, and its destruction has created a certain ambiguity in the record. Ambiguity is expensive, Miss Marchetti. Ambiguity keeps magistrates awake at night and investors awake longer."

I kept my face still. Six years of listening to men explain arithmetic they thought I couldn't follow had taught me stillness the way the sea teaches the barnacle.

"It has come to my attention," Corvane continued, "that a fragment of that correspondence may have been — salvaged. Seen. Perhaps even copied." His eyes went, briefly, unbearably, to the journal on my knee, and then returned to my face, and I knew the look to my journal was deliberate, a courtesy of menace, a way of saying *I know, and I'll be gentle about knowing.* "Now. I don't much care whether it's true. What I care about is that nobody can prove otherwise. And the cheapest way to make a thing unprovable is for a credible witness to swear, in writing, before a notary, that she never saw any such thing."

The single page on his desk. Of course.

"And the berth?" I asked. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect.

"The berth," Corvane said, "is yours."

* * *

Let me tell you what temptation actually looks like, because the songs get it wrong. Temptation is not a devil with a forked tongue offering you gold. Temptation is a tired woman sitting in a lamplit office with her dying father's journal under her hands, being offered, in a single envelope, everything she has been measuring her life against for months: escape, safety, and her father's wages continued — which is to say, his medicine, his rice, his one more winter. Temptation wears the mask of mercy because mercy is the only face that gets through a locked door.

Corvane laid it out with the clean economy of a man reading a balance sheet, and I will grant him this: it balanced. Sign the affidavit — the single page, one paragraph, my signature and the notary's stamp — and the ambiguity dies. No inquiry. No magistrate. No scandal. In exchange: the last berth on the *Vespera Kestrel*, passage east, a letter of recommendation to any trading house in the capital — "and I have influence in all of them" — and Salvo Marchetti's contract honored in full, his wages uninterrupted, his lease on his own bed protected, for so long as he was able to work. Which, we both understood, would not be long. But long enough to die somewhere other than a debtor's row.

"You would be doing him a kindness," Corvane said, and here his voice dropped into something almost tender, and I hated him most for that, because I believed he felt it. "The man pulled pearls out of that water for thirty years. He gave this company its best seasons. He shouldn't spend his last ones worrying about his daughter. Let him worry about his lungs instead — a proper paternal complaint." The good laugh again, brief, self-deprecating. "Sign the paper, take the berth, and go home tonight and sleep, Miss Marchetti. I don't think you've slept in weeks. I recognize the look. I invented the look."

And God help me, I considered it. Not the affidavit — I knew to the marrow of me that I could not sign that page, that the moment my pen touched it every diver in the lagoon became a squatter on their own inheritance, and Tomás's boatload of evidence became so much ballast, and Elias's intercepted telegrams became nothing, and the whole hard season of copying and hiding and lying awake became a story with no ending. I could not sign. That was bedrock.

But the berth. For one long minute — I know it was a minute, because I counted my breaths, an old habit from the ledgers, sixty to the minute, steady as a clock — I sat there and let myself see it. My father on the deck of the *Kestrel*, salt wind on his ruined chest, sleeping in a real berth instead of propped upright in ours. Myself in the capital, employed, free, my indenture bought out and burned, starting a life that belonged to me. And behind it all the quiet, poisonous whisper that has undone better people than me: *you've done enough. You salvaged the scrap. You copied the pages. You sent Tomás east. Someone else's turn now. Take your father and go.*

That whisper is the company's finest product. They manufacture it cheaper than rope.

"May I see the affidavit?" I asked.

Something flickered in Corvane's eyes — satisfaction, I supposed at first. Later, walking home, I decided it had been surprise. Perhaps he had expected more fight. Perhaps the fight was yet to come and he was simply waiting for it, the way patient men wait out weather.

He slid the single page across the desk. I read it the way I read everything now, twice, hunting the trap in the grammar. It was short and it was vile and it was beautifully drafted. *I, Mirabel Marchetti, being of sound mind, do solemnly depose that I have never seen nor possessed any portion of correspondence concerning leasehold rearrangements in the Halcyon Lagoon district...* Sound mind. Never seen nor possessed. Note that it did not say the letter did not exist. It said *I* had never seen it. A lawyer's affidavit, engineered so that if the letter surfaced anyway — if Tomás's packet reached the capital, if a magistrate's hand opened my father's diving journal — the affidavit would remain technically true, and I would remain technically perjured only in the eyes of God, whom the company did not consider a creditor.

They had taught me bookkeeping, these men, and I had learned the deepest lesson of bookkeeping: every figure can be true and the sum still be a lie.

"Well?" Corvane said.

"There's a word missing," I said.

He raised an eyebrow.

"*Knowingly,*" I said. "It should read 'never knowingly seen nor possessed.' As drafted, a person could see a letter in passing and honestly sign. You want certainty, Mr. Corvane, and this page doesn't purchase it. If I'm to swear away my own testimony, the document ought to be airtight — surely you agree. Surely the company agrees. The company loves an airtight clause."

For a moment the room went very quiet. Outside, a gull screamed at the harbor, and beneath my hands the diving journal felt suddenly hot, as though the hidden pages inside it had caught some of the fire that had eaten the original letter.

Then Corvane smiled, slow and genuine, and I understood I had made my first real mistake of the meeting: I had shown him my quality. He had offered the berth to a frightened girl; the girl had answered with drafting notes. Frightened girls run. Bookkeepers negotiate.

"You want time to consider," he translated.

"I want the document correct," I said. "Consideration follows correctness."

"Take until Friday," he said. "Noon. The notary will be here, and the *Kestrel* sails on the evening tide regardless. Miss Marchetti—" and now he leaned forward, and the kindness came back, the terrible well-meaning kindness of a man who has spent nineteen years confusing ownership with stewardship, "—I am not your enemy. I am the arithmetic. Every choice you've described to yourself as a war between right and wrong is, from where I sit, a column of numbers, and the numbers say: one girl saves one father, or one girl loses everything defending a lagoon full of people who cannot save themselves and will forget her within a season. I've read the divers' rosters. I know what they owe the company store. Kindness, Miss Marchetti, is knowing which debts can be paid and which cannot, and forgiving yourself accordingly."

"I'll take it under advisement," I said, which was the closest I have ever come to laughing in that office.

He walked me to the door himself — himself, not Pell, another courtesy of menace — and as I stepped past him he said, softly, "Your father dived my beds for thirty years. Whatever you decide, remember I knew him before you did."

* * *

Pell was waiting in the corridor with his eyes fixed on the ceiling again. We walked down the stairs together in silence, and only at the bottom, where the ledgers' room breathed its familiar smell of paper and salt-damp, did he speak.

"He offered you the berth," Pell said. Not a question. News traveled through that building faster than pay.

"He offered me the berth."

"And?"

"And I told him his affidavit was badly drafted," I said, "and he gave me until Friday noon."

Pell stared at me. Then, for the first time in three years of shared employment, he grinned — a crooked, terrified, delighted grin — and said, "You asked a man like Corvane for revisions."

"I asked him for time wearing the clothes of revisions," I said. "There's a difference, and I'd thank you to note it in the margin."

He laughed and fled, and I stood alone among the shelves with my father's journal under my arm and did the arithmetic Corvane had promised me, honestly, the way I do all arithmetic.

Two days. One berth. One affidavit that would murder a lagoon with my signature at the foot of it. And a father at home who did not know that his daughter was currently worth, to the most dangerous man in the port, precisely one ticket east.

I had until Friday noon to find out what Tomás's evidence was worth in the capital, whether the monsoon would hold off two more days, and how to tell Elias that the company had just priced my conscience at exactly the cost of my father's lungs.

I picked up my pen, opened the payroll ledger to Tuesday's column, and began, very steadily, to work — because the first rule of buying time is that no one watches closely a girl who is visibly busy. But under the figures, in the private shorthand only I could read, I was already drafting my answer to Corvane. Not the affidavit. Something else. Something with numbers in it that he would not enjoy reading at all.
