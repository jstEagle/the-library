## 12. The Journal Goes East

Tomás Reyes had stolen many things in his life: crates of rum, a ship's bell once, a magistrate's hat (worn briefly, at a party, to general applause), and — on one memorable Tuesday — an entire rowboat that turned out to belong to his own cousin. But he had never stolen anything that mattered the way this mattered, and he did not like how it felt. It sat too heavily in the oilskin bundle beneath his coat, against his ribs, like a second heart that beat with somebody else's blood.

Mirabel had pressed the bundle into his hands at the lighthouse two nights ago, and she had not let go of it right away. Her fingers were small and ink-stained and they gripped the oilskin as though it might grow wings.

"You'll keep it dry," she'd said. Not a question. A commandment.

"I'll keep it dry," he'd agreed.

"And you won't read—"

"Chiquita." He'd put his free hand over hers. "I can't read half of your numbers anyway. You write arithmetic like a madwoman writing prayers."

She'd laughed then — a wet, surprised laugh, the kind that escapes people who have been holding themselves together too long — and she'd let go, and Tomás had understood that he was now carrying the weight of every diver in the lagoon, plus one dying bookkeeper, plus one stubborn girl who refused to run, plus one lighthouse keeper's son who watched Mirabel the way sailors watch the horizon.

No pressure, then.

* * *

The *Paloma* was not much of a ship. She was forty feet of salt-rotted ambition with a patched mainsail and an engine that started on the third prayer and ran on spite. Tomás loved her the way one loves a bad dog: completely, and while apologizing to everyone else.

"Storm season," said Old Wen, his crew of one, spitting into the water as they loaded the last crate. "You know what storm season means."

"It means empty waters and rich freight rates."

"It means drowning."

"That too." Tomás cinched the crate down. "But it also means Corvane's inspectors are all huddled in the harbor office drinking bad brandy and congratulating themselves. Nobody expects a fool to sail east in the tail of the monsoon."

"Nobody expects a fool to do anything," Wen said. "That is why fools survive."

Tomás grinned and clapped him on the shoulder, and if his hand shook slightly, Wen was polite enough not to mention it. That was the arrangement between them: Wen noticed everything and said nothing, and Tomás pretended not to notice Wen noticing. Twenty years of friendship distilled to its purest form.

They slipped out of the harbor before dawn, running dark, no lantern, the lighthouse behind them sweeping its long arm across the water once, twice — Elias keeping his promise. One pass for good luck. Two for goodbye.

Tomás watched until the light blurred into the rain, and then he went below and unwrapped the oilskin just enough to confirm what he already knew: the diving journal was there, its pages stuffed with lease papers, the salvaged letter fragment in its careful glassine sleeve, the roster of divers' names in Mirabel's upright hand. He touched the whole thing once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child, and wrapped it back up.

Then he did something he had never done on any smuggling run in his life.

He checked it again ten minutes later.

"This will be a pleasant voyage," Wen observed from the helm, "if you check it every ten minutes for six days."

"I'm a careful man."

"You're a terrified man. It's different."

Tomás settled back against the crate, listening to the rain drum the deck above, and admitted to the darkness that Wen was probably right. He had carried contraband before, but contraband only wanted money from you. This wanted everything. If he lost this bundle, Mirabel lost her fight. If he lost this bundle, old Sefa lost her beds, and the Ferreira boys lost theirs, and his own cousin lost the rowboat business he was trying to buy back from the company's lease men. If he lost this bundle, a man named Marchetti would spend his last season coughing blood in a company dormitory instead of in his daughter's care.

And Mirabel herself — Mirabel would still stay. That was the terrible thing. He knew her well enough to know it. Lose everything and she would still stand up in front of the magistrate with nothing but her memory and her ledgers and let the company grind her small. She had chosen to be the last person off the sinking deck, or not off it at all, and Tomás, who had built his whole life around leaving places before they finished sinking, could not decide whether he admired her or wanted to shake her.

Both, probably. Both at once. That was family.

* * *

The first three days were almost kind.

The monsoon's tail dragged across the coast in long gray bands, and the *Paloma* pushed through them with her usual dignity-free determination, shipping green water over the bow and shaking it off like a wet hen. Tomás navigated by dead reckoning and memory, hugging the coast where he could, and when the squalls thinned on the fourth afternoon the sea went strangely gentle, all silver and swells, as though the ocean felt guilty about the weather and was making amends.

He cooked rice with dried chili and told Wen about the time he'd smuggled a piano.

"A piano," Wen repeated.

"For a wedding. The bride's family couldn't get one through customs, so I dressed it in fishing net and called it salvage. Played a chord on it during the inspection to prove it was broken." He smiled out at the water. "Corvane's predecessor nearly bought it himself. Man had no ear."

"You have no ear either."

"Which is why the fraud worked. We recognized each other."

Wen made the sound he made instead of laughing, which was a sort of hydraulic wheeze, and for a few hours the *Paloma* was simply a boat going somewhere, and the bundle under Tomás's coat was simply paper, and paper could not drown, and nobody he loved was within a hundred miles of being harmed.

He should have known better than to enjoy it.

* * *

The smoke came at dusk on the fifth day.

It rose off the water ahead of them, thin and black against the last copper light, and Tomás felt his stomach drop before his head caught up. Steam launches didn't burn coal that dirty unless they belonged to the Halcyon Company; he'd watched those funnels from enough darkened harbors to know their color the way other men knew the faces of their enemies.

"Wen."

"I see it."

"They shouldn't be out here. Storm season. I counted on them being huddled in port, that was the whole—"

"You counted on company men behaving sensibly," Wen said, already turning the wheel. "I mentioned the flaw in this plan. Several times."

The launch came on fast, cutting a white seam through the darkening sea, and Tomás did the arithmetic he'd done a thousand times in a thousand harbors: speed, distance, light remaining, options. The options were short. The *Paloma* could not outrun a steam launch on her best day, and this was not her best day; her best day had been sometime around 1887.

"Customs inspection," he muttered. "Be a customs launch."

The launch got close enough for him to see the men at her rail, and they were not wearing customs uniforms. They were wearing the gray coats of Halcyon security, and one of them raised a speaking trumpet.

"Sloop! Heave to! Inspection under harbor authority!"

"Harbor authority," Tomás breathed. "We're fifty miles from the harbor."

"They've extended the concept," Wen said dryly.

For one absurd moment Tomás considered the speaking trumpet himself — *Sorry, wrong sloop, we're carrying a piano* — but the bundle against his ribs argued otherwise, beating its borrowed heart against his own. He thought of Mirabel's ink-stained fingers on the oilskin. He thought of her laugh, wet and surprised, and how rarely he'd heard it.

"Heave to," the trumpet said again, closer now. "Or we board by force."

Tomás looked at the sky. The last light was dying in the west, and eastward — eastward there was a bank of weather sitting on the horizon like a closed fist, the kind of wall the monsoon threw up when it wasn't quite done with you. No sensible sailor would steer for it.

"Tomás," Wen said warningly, because Wen had known him twenty years and could read a stupid idea forming on his face like large print.

"They want us stopped," Tomás said. "So let's not stop."

* * *

He put the *Paloma*'s bow at the black wall of cloud the way a man puts his shoulder into a door he knows is locked.

The wind found them first, then the rain, sudden and sideways and hard as gravel, and the world shrank to the length of the deck. Behind them the launch's searchlight swung wildly, stabbing through the murk, catching sheets of rain and turning them to burning silver. Tomás steered by feel and blasphemy, calling depths to Wen, who worked the sails and answered in a language that was mostly coughing.

The bundle rode against his ribs. Every wave that slammed the hull sent his mind spiraling: *the oilskin, the oilskin, is it dry, is it safe,* and every time his hand found the shape of it under his coat he steadied again, a compass needle finding north.

The launch fell behind in the worst of it — he heard her engine laboring, saw her light swinging further and further aft — but the sea was collecting its toll regardless. A wave took the jib sheet clean off its cleat. Something below decks began to make a new and interesting noise. The bilge, which had been politely damp for years, announced itself as a rising opinion.

"We sink," Wen shouted, "and your precious papers drown with us!"

"Then we don't sink!" Tomás shouted back. "New policy!"

Somehow — he never afterward remembered exactly how, only that it involved Wen singing something obscene about a fisherman's wife and Tomás laughing so hard he nearly lost the wheel — the *Paloma* came through the worst band of weather and out the far side, into a night of ragged stars and exhausted swells. The launch was gone. Either sunk, turned back, or sulking, and Tomás found he didn't care which.

He cared very much about the bilge.

They pumped by turns for two hours, Tomás working the handle until his arms burned, and when the water finally gave ground he slumped against the mast, soaked to the soul, and did the thing he had been refusing to do since the harbor.

He opened the bundle.

Just the edges. Just to look. The oilskin had held; the journal inside was dry, the letter fragment safe in its sleeve, Mirabel's neat columns staring up at him like a hundred small witnesses. Names he knew. Beds his neighbors had dived for thirty years. And tucked between pages, in her handwriting, a note he had not seen her add:

*Tomás — if anything happens to you, ask for Magistrate Oyelaran directly. Do not trust the port office. Do not trust anyone who offers you a drink first. Come home after.*

*Come home after.*

He sat in the dripping dark with those three words longer than any grown smuggler should sit with anything, and then he wiped his face — the rain, he told himself, it was the rain — and wrapped the journal up and lashed it inside the cabin, high on the shelf, wrapped twice more in waxed canvas.

Wen appeared at the hatch with two cups of something hot and terrible.

"You cried," Wen observed.

"The sea sprayed upward."

"Ah. Yes. Upward spray. Famous phenomenon."

"Shut up and drink your tea."

They drank. The stars came and went behind torn clouds. Somewhere east, past another day of this, lay the capital, and a magistrate, and whatever chance a stack of paper had against a company with steam and lawyers and men in gray coats.

"You know," Wen said at last, contemplatively, "they will try again. Men like that do not stop at one launch."

"I know."

"And the harbor master is bought, you said. So when you come home—"

"When we come home," Tomás said, "there may not be a home to come to. Not the way we left it." He turned his cup slowly. "But there'll be a hearing. There'll be a record. There'll be Mirabel standing up in some room full of company men, reading their own sins back to them in ledger-hand, and God help me, Wen, I want to be in the room when she does it."

Wen nodded, which in his language was a speech.

They sailed east through the rest of the night, and in the morning the wind shifted fair, and Tomás allowed himself, cautiously, one hour of hope. It lasted fifty-eight minutes.

Because at midday, rounding the last headland before the capital's roads, they sighted the harbor — and Tomás, raising his battered spyglass, saw that every berth along the quay flew the gray pennant of the Halcyon Company, and that a clerk stood at the foot of the landing stairs checking papers, and that the clerk was looking at every incoming vessel with the particular patience of a man who has been paid to wait for one boat in specific.

He lowered the glass slowly.

"Wen," he said. "How do you feel about arriving somewhere that isn't here?"
