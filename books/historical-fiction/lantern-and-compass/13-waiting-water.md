## 13. Waiting Water

The monsoon did not end so much as sulk. For three days the rain fell in a fine, malicious drizzle that soaked a person more thoroughly than any proper downpour, because a proper downpour at least had the decency to let you know you were beaten.

Mirabel felt the same could be said of Mr. Corvane.

She knew the company was tightening its grip before anyone told her, because she saw it in the ledgers first — she always did. On Tuesday morning, seated at her desk in the counting house with her ink-stained cuffs and her aching wrist, she entered the week's ration requisitions and found them halved. Rice, oil, lamp fuel, the dried fish that kept the diving families through the storm months when no boats went out: all of it struck through and reduced by a neat, unhurried hand. Not Corvane's hand. He never dirtied a page himself if he could persuade someone else to. It was young Prewitt, the junior clerk, whose loops were still anxious and uncertain, who had copied out the new figures under instruction.

"Mr. Corvane's compliments," Prewitt said, not meeting her eyes, "and he asks you to initial the revised schedule."

Mirabel looked at the numbers for a long moment. Half rations for two hundred souls, effective immediately, retroactive to Monday. She thought of Mrs. Okonkwo down on the strand with seven mouths and a husband whose lungs were full of the lagoon. She thought of the way rice stretched, and the way it stopped stretching.

"And the reason given?" she asked pleasantly, because pleasantness was a knife you could carry openly.

"'Operational economies during seasonal disruption,'" Prewitt recited, and had the grace to look ashamed of it.

"How lovely," Mirabel said. "Someone in the head office has a gift for poetry."

She initialed the schedule. What else was there to do? That was the question that had been sitting on her chest since Tomás's boat had gone east over the horizon eleven days ago — what else was there to do, and how long must she do nothing, and how did a person stand upright under the weight of a secret while everyone around her grew thinner?

* * *

The leases came next.

On Thursday a notice was nailed to the company wall by the harbor steps, where every diver passing to the sheds would see it. It announced that, pending review of "irregularities in title documentation," all pearl-bed leases in the lagoon were suspended until further notice. Suspended — as though they were ornaments hanging from a hook, and not the floor beneath two hundred years of lives. Divers could continue working the beds, the notice allowed, magnanimously, but their catch must be sold exclusively to Halcyon at revised rates, and no family might mortgage, transfer, or borrow against its lease while the review proceeded.

Which meant no credit. Which meant, with half rations already biting, that the divers were now tied to the company twice over — once by their hands and once by their breath.

Mirabel read the notice three times while the crowd gathered behind her. She heard old Sefa Okonkwo mutter something in the diving tongue, the low syllables like stones dropped in water.

"It's the letter," said Nani Reyes, too loudly, the way she said everything. The fishwife elbowed forward, salt-crusted and furious. "The one the girl saw. They're frightened of it, that's why they're doing this."

A dozen faces turned to Mirabel. She felt the attention settle on her like rain.

"I can't discuss company correspondence," she said, which was true, and safe, and tasted like a lie.

"You *saw* it," Nani pressed. "Everyone says so. You burned your fingers pulling it from the fire. Well? Is it true what they say it contains?"

What it contained, in the fragment Mirabel had salvaged and copied and sewn into her father's diving journal and sent away across the sea with a smuggler who might by now be at the bottom of it: a scheme to void the divers' leases by manufacturing debts against them, dispossess forty families, and consolidate the pearl beds under Halcyon's sole ownership before the colonial administration woke up and noticed there was a lagoon here at all. She knew it the way you know a bone is broken — completely, privately, and without being able to show anyone the X-ray.

"The notice speaks for itself," she said. "Leases suspended pending review. I would advise everyone to keep exact accounts of everything the company takes from now until the review concludes. Every measure of rice. Every pearl weighed. Everything in writing, witnessed, dated."

"Accounts," Sefa said heavily. "You give us *accounts*, girl, when our children are hungry?"

It was unfair and entirely fair, and Mirabel took it the way she had learned to take most things, which was standing still with her chin level while something in her quietly bled.

"Paper is the only weapon we are permitted," she said. "So yes. Accounts."

* * *

That evening she walked home along the seawall in the drizzle and found the town waiting for her, or that was how it felt. Word traveled fast in a port of four streets. At the chandlery, Widow Marchmont — no relation, though the town insisted otherwise — leaned out her door and asked, in the tone of a woman asking about the weather, whether Mirabel thought things would come right. At the net-menders' shed, six men paused in their work to watch her pass, and one of them, old Bravo, actually lifted his cap, which he had never once done in fifteen years, and which frightened her more than anything Corvane had done yet.

They thought she had a plan.

That was Tomás's doing, partly. He had spent years being the town's answer to impossible questions — how to get medicine past the customs house, how to move a coffin that needed moving, how to make a debt disappear — and now that he was gone, the questions had nowhere to go but to her. And partly it was the ledgers'. In a town where most people signed with an X, the girl who could read a company's columns backwards was the nearest thing to a lighthouse, and God help her, the actual lighthouse belonged to Elias, and even Elias didn't know what she carried.

That was the loneliness of it, she thought, climbing the hill to their cottage in the dark. Not the hunger coming, not even Corvane. It was standing in front of people who trusted her, being asked for hope, and knowing that hope existed — it was *in transit*, somewhere east of Ceylon, sewn into a journal, carried by a man with a crooked smile — and not being able to say so. To give them hope without proof would be cruelty if the proof never arrived. To give them despair would be to hand Corvane the town on a plate. So she smiled, and initialed his schedules, and counted the days.

Eleven days. Twelve, by the time she reached the door.

Her father was awake, which surprised her; mostly he slept now between coughing fits, folded into his chair by the window like a sail taken in. The doctor's words sat in the house with them — consumption, another season here would kill him, sea air or no air at all — though neither of them spoke them aloud anymore. He had a ledger open on his knees. Her father, the pearl diver who had fought the company for thirty years to keep one bed and one boat, was teaching himself to read figures, laboriously, with a pencil stub and his tongue between his teeth.

"Papa, you should be sleeping."

"I sleep like the dead already." He tapped the page. "Explain this to me. This mark here, this little cross."

"That means the entry is carried forward. You'll find its twin on the next page."

"Twin." He nodded slowly, pleased. "Everything has a twin, then. A thing and its shadow." He looked up at her, and his eyes were still sharp even when the rest of him had gone soft at the edges. "You have a shadow these days, my daughter. I see it following you. When do you mean to introduce us?"

Mirabel busied herself with the lamp.

"I don't know what you—"

"Mira." He almost never shortened it. "I am dying slowly, not stupidly. There is a difference, though the company does not recognize either condition. Something left this town on Tomás's boat, and you have been walking like a woman carrying water since — carefully, and pretending the pitcher is empty." He closed his own ledger, gently, the way he closed everything, as if the world were owed gentleness. "I will not ask. But I will tell you this, and then you may go to bed. Whatever it is — whatever you are waiting for — the people cannot wait as long as you can. Hunger has no patience and no memory. Feed them first, fight second. Even the just cause starves like the unjust one."

He coughed then, long and hard, and she held the cup for him and hated the sound, and when it passed he patted her hand as though he had delivered a blessing instead of a problem.

Because he was right, and she knew it, and she had no idea how to feed anyone.

* * *

She tried arithmetic instead, which was her form of prayer.

Saturday night, after the lamps in the counting house were out, she stayed late with her own private ledger — the one she kept locked in the drawer with her mother's ring, where she recorded truths the company's books were not permitted to hold. She listed the diving families. Forty-one households. She estimated stores: what remained in the company warehouse, what the divers had laid in before the season closed, what the gardens on the ridge produced in a wet year, which this was not. She assigned each household its ration, its garden, its fish, and then she subtracted, the way she subtracted everything, calmly and mercilessly, and arrived at a number.

Nineteen days. Nineteen days before the first family went hungry in earnest, and after that the curve steepened, because hunger borrowed and hunger begged and hunger sold its pearls early at ruinous prices, which was precisely what Corvane wanted, because a starving man's lease could be bought for a bowl of rice. She could see the whole machine of it, elegant and vile, every gear cut by her own profession. The letter had named it. *Consolidation through indebtedness.* She had once believed such sentences were the invention of novelists.

She put down her pen and pressed her palms against her eyes.

There was rice in the warehouse. There was always rice in the warehouse — Corvane was cutting rations not because rice was short but because hunger was useful. If she falsified a requisition order — she knew the forms, she knew the seals, she had written enough of them in Corvane's name to write one in anyone's — she could release two weeks of grain to the strand families tonight. It would take Corvane perhaps three days to notice. It would cost her her position, probably her freedom, certainly the last shreds of access she needed if the magistrate's summons ever came and the testimony had to be built column by column.

And if she was caught before the evidence landed, everything Tomás risked his life to carry would arrive at a magistrate's desk attached to nothing but a fugitive bookkeeper and a rumor.

Or she could do nothing for nineteen more days and trust the sea.

She sat in the dark counting house for a long time, listening to the rain argue with the roof, feeling the whole town's hunger queue up behind her ribs. *The burden of being the one who knows.* Everyone thought knowledge was power. Nobody warned you that knowledge was mostly weight — that the ones who see the flood coming must stand on the seawall and smile at people who ask about the weather.

* * *

Sunday, the town found a way to feed itself without her plan, which humbled her thoroughly.

It started with Nani Reyes, who appeared at the strand market — suspended leases notwithstanding, no notice had mentioned markets — with two crates of mackerel and a hand-lettered sign reading FAIR PRICES FOR FRIENDS, RUINOUS PRICES FOR HALCYON CLERKS. By noon half the fishing widows had followed suit, trading outside the company's channels with the cheerful lawlessness of people who had survived cyclones. Old Bravo organized a work crew to mend the ridge terraces in exchange for shares of the yam harvest. Mrs. Okonkwo established, with military precision, a communal pot at the end of the strand that somehow made three fish and a sack of split peas feed thirty children, and dared anyone to comment.

"They don't need me," Mirabel reported that evening, sitting on the seawall beside Elias, watching the lighthouse beam swing its slow arm over the water. She meant it as comfort and heard, too late, how much disappointment was in it.

"They need you exactly as much as before," Elias said. "Only now they've remembered they also have themselves. That's not subtraction, Mira. That's what you call leverage." He knocked his shoulder against hers. "Besides. Someone has to keep the accounts of the communal pot. Mrs. Okonkwo counts peas like a pirate counts gold."

Despite everything, Mirabel laughed — a real one, startled out of her. It was Elias's particular gift, that: he treated her dread as a fact worth respecting and a burden worth sharing, and never as a reason to pity her, and somehow the combination let her breathe.

"I'm sorry I can't tell you," she said quietly.

"I know what you can tell me and I've stopped keeping score." He picked a loose thread of rope off the seawall and wound it around his finger, unwound it. "But I'll say one thing, and then I'll drop it forever. Whatever's coming — whatever you're waiting for — don't wait alone past the point where waiting becomes the same as drowning. There's a difference between carrying a secret and being sunk by it, and the difference is usually one other pair of hands."

The beam swept overhead, and in its light she studied his face — patient, weathered, certain of her — and wished with her whole chest that the pair of hands could be his. But the telegram wires ran both ways, and Corvane read what passed along them, and every confidence was a door left open.

"Nine days," was all she said. "Ask me again in nine days."

Elias nodded slowly, and wound the thread once more around his finger, and did not drop it forever but tucked it away, the way he tucked everything concerning her, for later.

* * *

Monday morning there was a new notice on the company wall, and this one had been written in haste — Prewitt's anxious loops again, but rushed, the ink blotted, posted before dawn. Effective immediately, the harbor master's office would require company-issued permits for all vessels leaving port, including fishing boats, including rowboats, *including ships' tenders*. All outbound traffic subject to inspection.

Mirabel stood in front of it while the drizzle spotted the paper, and the cold that went through her had nothing to do with the weather.

Corvane wasn't squeezing the town anymore. He was sealing the jar. No boats out meant no evidence traveling east, no appeals, no smugglers' runs, no escape — a port turned into a locked room with two hundred people in it, waiting for the review to conclude however Corvane concluded it.

Unless. Unless he wasn't sealing the jar against the town at all.

Unless somewhere out on the grey monsoon water, a small fast boat with a crook-smiling man aboard was already cutting east toward the capital, and somewhere in the capital a magistrate had opened a diving journal and begun to read, and Mr. Corvane — who received telegrams the way other men received weather reports — knew it.

Mirabel initialed the notice. She entered it in the ledger. Then she walked, at a perfectly ordinary pace, to the harbor office, where the wire clerk was eating his lunch, and asked, in the pleasant voice she kept sharpened for exactly such occasions, whether she might see the day's telegram log — purely for the record of shipping expenses, since company telegrams were billable to the account.

The clerk hesitated. The log lay open on his desk.

And there, third from the top, dated yesterday, addressed to Halcyon head office over Corvane's signature, was a line of capitals that Mirabel read in the four seconds it took the clerk to remember his instructions:

*MAGISTRATE IN RECEIPT OF DOCUMENTS STOP ACCELERATE CONSOLIDATION BEFORE RULING STOP ALL MEANS AUTHORIZED.*

She thanked the clerk. She walked back along the quay through the drizzle, past the mended nets and the communal pot and the children who waved at her, the girl with the ledgers, and her mind was already running the figures, and the figures said: nineteen days of food, a ruling that might take months, and a man with permission to use *all means* — and Tomás, somewhere out on that iron-colored sea, sailing straight into a harbor that had just been ordered shut.
