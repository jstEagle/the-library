## 14. What Elias Saw

The lighthouse taught Elias patience the way the sea teaches a stone: slowly, and by wearing away everything that wasn't essential. He had spent eleven years climbing the same hundred and forty-two steps — he had counted them once, out of boredom, and then counted them again to make sure the first count hadn't been boredom lying to him — and in all that time he had learned that most of what happened in the world happened too fast, and the lighthouse's whole business was to be the one thing that didn't.

Which is why it offended him so deeply when things started happening at night.

He was on the gallery deck just past midnight, third watch, with a thermos of his mother's ginger tea going lukewarm beside him and the lamp turning its great patient circles overhead. The monsoon had pulled back to its winter quarters but left the harbor choppy, the water slapping at the pilings like an impatient creditor. Below him, the town slept. The Halcyon Company offices were dark. Everything was exactly where it should have been.

Except for the light moving along the quay.

It wasn't the harbor master's patrol lantern — that swung low and yellow and lazy, because old Mr. Prynne had never in his life hurried for anything, including the fire that time the fish sheds went up. This light moved quick and clipped, bobbing from the company office door down toward the telegraph shed at the harbor's edge, and behind it came two men walking with the particular posture of people doing something they would rather not be seen doing.

Elias knew that posture. He'd grown up next to a smuggler's son.

Tomás Ferro walked like an honest man pretending to be dishonest so no one would suspect how honest he actually was — all swagger on top, careful feet underneath. These two walked the other way round. All care, no swagger.

Elias set down his tea. He watched the light disappear into the telegraph shed, and he did what he always did when the world started moving too fast: he slowed himself down, step by step by step, and went to see.

* * *

The telegraph shed sat on stilts over the harbor's edge, built in the optimistic early days of the company when someone had believed Port Serrano would become a metropolis instead of what it was, which was a port with ambitions of being a large village. The operator, Mr. Quill, kept hours that could generously be called irregular and honestly be called fictional; half the messages that came through the wire waited until morning for anyone to read them.

But the wire itself didn't sleep. It ticked and chattered all night in the empty shed, patient as insects, and if you stood under the floorboards where the cables ran through, and if you had spent your childhood learning Morse off your father's signal lamp because there was nothing else to do on a rock in the sea, you could hear the rhythm of what was being sent before it ever reached a human eye.

Elias crouched beneath the shed in the smell of tar and salt rot, rain dripping off the boards above onto the back of his neck, and listened.

The sending hand was fast. Not Quill-fast — Quill typed like a man afraid the machine might bite — but practiced, commercial, the kind of speed that belonged to a clerk who had done this ten thousand times. Elias worked through the clicks, lips moving, translating the way other men hummed tunes.

...CORVANE TO HALCYON HOUSE... EVIDENCE REPORTED TAKEN EAST... MAGISTRATE IN RECEIPT...

His stomach dropped somewhere around his boots.

Magistrate. Evidence. Tomás. Tomás had made it. Through the tail of the monsoon, past the company cutters, all the way east with Mirabel's copied pages sewn inside a diving journal, and the summons — if it was a summons — was in a magistrate's hands.

For about four seconds, Elias felt something that was almost joy.

Then the key kept talking.

...RECOMMEND IMMEDIATE CONSOLIDATION OF LEASES PRIOR TO ANY RULING... SUSPEND ALL DIVER CONTRACTS EFFECTIVE FIRST OF MONTH... SURVEY PARTY TO MARK BEDS FOR TRANSFER... LOCAL AUTHORITY COMPENSATED AND COOPERATIVE...

Local authority compensated. That was Prynne. That was the harbor master with his lazy yellow lantern, bought and paid for, which explained the berth business, which explained everything, and Elias found he wasn't surprised so much as tired in a new place, a place under the tiredness he already carried.

And then the last line, and this one he translated twice because the first time he thought his cold fingers had miscounted:

...IF GIRL REMAINS ON SITE SHE BECOMES MATERIAL WITNESS... ADVISE HANDLING...

Advise handling. Two words. He turned them over and they had no good side. You handled cargo. You handled trouble. Nobody wrote *advise handling* about a person they intended to leave alone.

Above him the key stopped. Boots crossed the boards. The door banged, the clip-bobbing light retreated up the quay toward the company offices, and Elias stayed crouched in the dark under the shed with the rain coming down his collar, listening to the harbor slap its impatient hands against the pilings, and understood that the clock they had all been living on — the slow, patient, magistrate-will-come-when-it-comes clock — had just been thrown into the sea.

Corvane wasn't waiting for a ruling. Corvane was going to finish the job first and let the ruling arrive afterward, like a guest showing up after dinner to find the plates licked clean.

* * *

He climbed back up the hundred and forty-two steps without feeling any of them, which was how he knew he was rattled. His father used to say you could measure a man's trouble by whether he noticed the stairs.

In the lamp room, he stood for a while watching the beam sweep out across the black lagoon, around and around, touching the pearl beds every eleven seconds whether anyone thanked it or not. His father had kept this light for thirty years and died keeping it, more or less, though officially he'd died of his heart in a chair, which was also a kind of keeping. Elias had taken the post at nineteen because somebody had to and because it put him, by his own private arithmetic, exactly between the sea and Mirabel Marchetti, and he had never once been sorry about the arrangement even when the arrangement meant nights like this, standing in the dark holding knowledge that burned.

There was a proper channel for what he'd heard. There was always a proper channel. He could report the intercepted transmission to — to whom? Prynne, who was bought? Corvane, who had sent it? He laughed out loud, alone, and the sound bounced oddly off the glass.

No. There was only one person this news belonged to, and she was asleep above her father's sickroom with a ledger for a pillow, or not asleep at all, which was likelier. Mirabel Marchetti had not properly slept since October. She slept the way a cat sleeps in a house full of dogs: technically, and with one ear working.

He couldn't go tonight. That was the discipline the stairs had taught him — you didn't run down a mountain in the dark shouting bad news, because bad news shouted in the dark arrived broken, and broken bad news did twice the damage. He would wait for morning. He would carry it carefully. He would stand in her doorway with the dawn behind him and say, *I have something, and it's bad, but we're going to hold it together while I tell you.*

That was the plan. It was a good plan. It was, in fact, the exact right plan, and he was still admiring it when the knock came at the lighthouse door at four in the morning, soft and double-tapped, in a rhythm he'd known since he was eight years old.

Mirabel.

* * *

She stood on the bottom step with an oilskin over her shoulders and her hair pinned up wrong, the way it got when she'd dressed in a hurry in the dark, and behind her the town was still black except for the company offices, which were lit on both floors.

"You're awake," Elias said, which was the stupidest sentence available to him, and he chose it anyway because the alternatives were worse.

"I'm always awake." She looked past him up the stairwell, then back, and he saw that her eyes had the flat shine of someone running on fear and arithmetic in equal parts. "The offices are lit. Both floors. At four in the morning. Corvane hasn't lit both floors since the cyclone, and that was because the roof was off."

"You've been watching the offices."

"I've been watching everything." She said it simply, without apology. "Someone has to. Your lighthouse watches the sea. Somebody has to watch the land."

He stepped aside and she came in, shaking the rain off the oilskin, and stood in the entryway where generations of keepers had scratched their initials into the doorframe — his father's E.T. among them, and, lower down, smaller, a clumsy M.M. carved the summer they were both eleven, which she pretended not to look at and he pretended not to notice her looking at.

"Tea?" he offered. "Fair warning, it's my mother's recipe, which means it's less tea than a threat."

"Elias." She turned and looked at him, and whatever she saw in his face stopped her, because her chin came up the way it did when she was bracing. "You know something. You have the face. You've had the face since I got here."

"I was coming to you at dawn," he said. "I had a plan. It was a very orderly plan."

"The last orderly thing that helped anybody was the tide table." She folded her arms. "Tell me."

So he told her. He gave it to her straight, because she had earned straightness the way other people earned wages — daily, and in full. The telegraph shed. The message east. The magistrate had the evidence — that part he watched land on her like sunlight, brief and warm, before the rest of it came down like the rest of the storm. The leases suspended effective the first of the month. The survey party to mark the beds for transfer. Prynne bought and confirmed. And the last line, which he made himself repeat word for word, because she would want it word for word: *if girl remains on site, advise handling.*

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened, and the lamp turned overhead with its small steady sounds, and Elias realized he was gripping the banister hard enough to whiten his knuckles and let go.

"First of the month," she said finally. "That's nine days."

"Eight. If the message goes out tomorrow morning, the suspension papers will be printed and ready before—"

"No, I mean—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "The magistrate is east. East is three weeks by the fastest boat in fair weather, and it is not fair weather. Even if he rules the day the journal reaches him, even if he wires back the same hour, the ruling arrives *after* Corvane has marked the beds, voided the contracts, and moved divers off water their grandmothers dived. Possession first, justice second. That's not an accident, Elias. That's the design. That's how the company does everything — it builds the fact first and lets the law catch up to it, and by then the fact has roots."

"I know." He did know. Hearing her say it made it worse, the way a diagnosis is worse spoken aloud than suspected. "So we don't let him build it. Nine days is enough to—"

"To what?" She dropped her hands and looked at him, and underneath the fear there was something else starting to move, the thing he loved best in her, which was the part of Mirabel that treated catastrophe as a bookkeeping problem with entries that could be contested. "What can we do in eight days with a bought harbor master, a suspended lease, and a town that mostly wants to believe the company will behave if we're polite?"

"Well," Elias said, "politeness is off the table, then."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. Small, exhausted, real. "That's the spirit."

"I mean it." He came down a step so they were nearly level, and took her hands, because some things needed saying with hands attached. "Listen to me. Corvane thinks he's fast because he moves at night with the wire and the money. But I grew up on this rock, and I'll tell you what's faster than money here. Gossip. My mother. The fishermen's wives' morning net-mending circle, which has ended governments, or would have, if any government had ever been worth the effort. And divers who are angry. You have never seen anything faster than an angry diver, Mirabel, except possibly an angry diver's wife."

"That's not a plan. That's a riot."

"It's a foundation. Plans come later. Right now the plan is: nobody in this town hears the news from Corvane first. Because the man's whole method is darkness — night surveys, sealed letters, bribes paid quiet. Every single thing he's done has depended on nobody knowing. So we make everybody know. We wake this town up and we point it at the water and we do not let him mark one bed, sign one paper, or move one diver without a hundred witnesses standing on the shore counting out loud."

She was quiet again, but differently now. Her thumbs had started moving against his palms, back and forth, the way they did when her mind was writing columns. He could almost hear the abacus.

"Suspended contracts aren't enforceable if the suspension is challenged," she said slowly. "There's a clause — Article Eleven, I've read it so many times I could recite it drunk — disputes over lease status stay execution pending review. If even one diver formally contests the suspension, in writing, before the survey party completes its marking, the whole consolidation stalls. Legally. On paper. Corvane knows it, which is why he needs to move before anyone files."

"So someone files."

"Everyone files." She said it and then heard herself say it, and her eyes went wide and bright and slightly terrifying. "Every diver on the roster. Forty-one families, forty-one challenges, filed the same morning, witnessed, dated, copies kept. Let Corvane's clerks spend their nine days drowning in paper. I'll write the forms myself. I know the format better than his own office does — I learned it from the inside." She laughed, a short wild note that echoed up the stairwell. "They taught me the weapon, Elias. They handed me the weapon and asked me to dust it."

"There she is," Elias said.

"There she is what?"

"The woman who balances ledgers for breakfast." He tucked a strand of wrongly-pinned hair behind her ear. "I've been telling you for years you were wasted on them."

"You've been telling me I was wasted, full stop. You weren't fussy about the employer."

"I stand by the general principle."

But the humor thinned out between them, because it had to, because there was still the last line sitting in the room like wet clothes neither of them wanted to pick up. *If girl remains on site, advise handling.*

"He means you," Elias said quietly. "Mirabel. Whatever 'handling' means — and I've sailed with Tomás, I've heard what that word means in company mouths — it means you. Eight days isn't just their deadline. It's yours. When this is over, win or lose, you cannot still be standing on site when the investors answer that wire."

"And go where?" she said, not bitter, just tired, and honest. "On what berth?"

He didn't have an answer for that. He thought about the harbor, the berths, the one unclaimed slot on the last ship of the year that he'd found stitched into Prynne's books, and about how finite a thing a berth was — one plank of wood, one seat on the edge of the world — and how it was going to have to be divided among people he loved like a loaf among the starving, and he hated the arithmetic of it with his whole heart. But he held her hands and said the truest thing he had:

"We'll make one. However many we need. I don't know how yet, but I have eight days, a lighthouse, and a talent for staying awake, and I am not going to be the reason you end up handled."

She leaned her forehead against his chest, just for a moment, just long enough. Above them the lamp turned. Around them the rain came down on the rock, on the town, on the dark lagoon where the pearl beds lay sleeping under their eleven seconds of light apiece, unaware that in eight days men with stakes and string would come to claim them unless a bookkeeper's daughter and a keeper's son could raise a town faster than money could buy one.

"Eight days," Mirabel said into his coat.

"Eight days," Elias agreed. "Starting with waking up your brother. Where's Tomás?"

Her head came up sharply. "He's due back on the morning boat. Why?"

Elias looked out the lamp room glass toward the eastern horizon, gray-black and empty as far as God's own eye could see, and felt the first cold thread of a new worry wind itself around all the others.

"Because if Corvane wired investors tonight," he said slowly, "he may have wired the coast stations too. And the morning boat comes in through the company cutters' patrol line."

Mirabel had gone very still.

"*Advise handling*," she whispered. "He didn't only mean me."
