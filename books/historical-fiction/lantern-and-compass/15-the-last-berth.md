## 15. The Last Berth

There was a particular kind of arithmetic I did better than anyone else in Port Sable, and it had nothing to do with numbers.

Anyone could subtract. What I did was carry the remainders — the sums that didn't come out clean, the debts with no column to put them in. I had spent eleven years at the Halcyon Company's counting desk learning that every ledger is a story wearing a very dull coat, and that if you wanted to know the truth of a place you read not the entries but the margins: what was smudged, what was written twice, what had been scratched out so hard the paper tore.

Lately all my arithmetic had come out wrong.

I sat in the back room of our house above the chandlery with a sheet of paper before me and four names on it, and I could not make them fit the space available. The space available was one berth on the *Meridian*, the last ship of the year, sailing east on the second of January whether the century was ready or not.

*Papa.* Who coughed now in a way that sounded like surf dragging stones, who had begun to nap in the afternoons and pretend he hadn't. The doctor had used the word *consumption* the way a priest uses a name at a funeral, gently, once, and then never again.

*Tomás.* Who was somewhere east of the storm line with our whole case sewn into his coat lining, carrying a magistrate's hopes in one pocket and his own in the other, due back when the weather permitted and not a day sooner. Tomás, who had said, the night before he sailed, "When I come back, we go together. All of us. Swear it," and I had sworn it because at the time I believed oaths were things you could keep by wanting them hard enough.

*Elias.* Who could no more leave the lighthouse than the lighthouse could leave the reef. His father's lamp was lit by hand every dusk, and Elias's hands were the only ones on this coast that knew the trick of the old burner, and besides — he would have laughed at the idea of a berth. Elias belonged to the water in the way Papa did, except that where Papa took from it, Elias kept watch over it. You might as well have offered the horizon a ticket.

And *me*. Whose name Corvane had spoken three days ago across his polished desk like an item being appraised for auction. *One berth, Miss Marchetti. Reserved. For whoever I choose to fill it.* He had let the silence do most of the talking, as he always did. He had almost smiled when he slid the affidavit across the desk again, the same one, third time now, denying the letter existed. *Sign it, take the berth, and your father keeps his wages through winter. Refuse, and I will extend your indenture to 1904 and blacklist Marchetti from every company wharf from here to Aden.*

I had told him I would consider it. I had even made my face do something grateful and frightened, which was not entirely acting, because I was grateful and frightened, only not about the things he thought.

Then I had gone home and done what I do. I made lists.

* * *

The trouble with being the one who knows things — and I had been that one since the day I pulled a burning letter out of a clerk's brazier — is that knowing is a solvent. It dissolves the comfortable lies people live inside, and it does not spare the knower. I knew how much Papa weighed against the berth. I knew what Tomás would say. I knew what Elias would say, which was worse, because Elias would say *take it yourself* and mean it, and mean also that he'd say goodbye without making it heavy, because that was how Elias loved things: lightly, so they could fly.

I spread the paper out under the lamp and wrote it plainly, the way I'd write any account.

**Berth: one (1).**
**Claimants: four (4).**

Underneath, the honest columns.

Papa: dying, slowly but on a schedule now, and the schedule said another wet season in Port Sable would finish him. The dry air inland might save him; the sea air would not; the ship went east past a dozen ports where a consumptive pearl diver could sit in sunlight and be an old man instead of a dead one soon.

Tomás: healthy, resourceful, capable of stealing any ship he liked if it came to that, and — this was the part I kept circling — owed. Owed everything. He had taken a scared girl's salvaged scrap of paper and turned it into a plan, and then he had taken the plan and put his own body between it and the Company. If the *Meridian* sailed without him and the magistrate's men came for the witnesses, Tomás would be caught at sea or caught ashore with no way out. But Tomás, I argued with myself, Tomás has escaped things his whole life. It's practically his trade.

Elias: immovable, incorruptible, and secretly of the opinion — he'd never say it — that a man who abandons a lighthouse in storm season deserves whatever the dark does to him.

Me: the one Corvane actually wanted gone. Which meant, if I was honest, that the berth was bait, and bait is for catching, not for riding.

I looked at the page for a long time. The lamp guttered. Outside, the tail of the monsoon rattled the shutters like a creditor at a door.

Every honest column came out the same way. Every sum landed on Papa.

And yet I did not write it down. Because writing it down would make it a decision, and decisions made alone are the loneliest documents there are. I had watched Corvane make them for years — tidy entries in a fine hand, each one deciding some diver's family off the beds, made alone in a carpeted room — and I had told myself the difference between us was that I would never decide for other people in secret.

I sat with that difference for most of one night, and near dawn I discovered it had gotten smaller.

* * *

"You're doing your ledger face," Papa said the next morning. He was at the table with tea he wasn't drinking, and his hands around the cup were the hands that had taught me to hold a pencil, back when they could still pull fifty pounds of shell up a ladder line without shaking.

"I don't have a ledger face."

"You have four faces. The one for Corvane, the one for the dock boys, the one for that lighthouse boy — don't look surprised, Mirabel, I raised you, I've seen you lie about where you were since you were nine — and the ledger face. The ledger face is the one where you've already counted something and you don't like the total."

I put my own untouched tea down. This was the moment. I felt it open under me like a hatch. Tell him. Say: *there's a berth, Papa, one berth, and I've worked the sums and the sums are you.*

What came out instead was: "The cooperative's accounts need recopying. Some pages got damp."

He looked at me a long moment. His eyes were still sharp; it was only everything south of his lungs that was failing. Then he nodded, slow, and let it go, and I understood with a cold sliding feeling that he was letting it go on purpose, that he too was keeping a ledger, and that perhaps his margin notes were about me.

"Recopy them carefully," he said. "Damp pages smear. And Mirabel —" he lifted the cup at last, drank, grimaced at it — "whatever you're counting, count it twice."

That night I couldn't sleep. I lay listening to him cough in the next room, each fit longer than the last, and I did what I always do when the human arithmetic fails: I went to the actual ledgers.

The harbor master's book was easy enough to consult — I had been balancing the chandlery's accounts against it for years, and nobody guards a manifest from the girl who tallies their rope and lamp oil. The *Meridian*: berths twelve, eleven sold, one reserved. Entry in a clerk's hand, but the instruction beneath it in another — Corvane's own initials, and the notation I had been taught to write myself a hundred times: *held per H.C. instructions, release to bearer of letter.*

Letter. Not name. Whoever Corvane handed the chit to, that was the passenger. It could be me, walking out with signed paper. It could be anyone he chose to disappear. It was, in short, not a berth at all. It was a leash with a boat attached.

I closed the book very quietly, as though the harbor master's office were asleep, and stood in the doorway looking out at the harbor, where the *Meridian* rode at anchor with her running lights doubled for the season, patient as a trap.

And here is the thing I want set down honestly, because this account is the only ledger I'll ever keep that matters: I did not decide right then. I decided in pieces, the way drowning happens, and by the time I recognized it as a decision it had already been made.

* * *

I bought the berth in my father's name on a Tuesday, in the rain, wearing my plainest shawl and the face I wear for Corvane.

It was absurdly simple. That was the part that made my hands shake afterward. Three years of secret letters and forged pages and smuggled journals, and in the end the transaction took four minutes at a shipping office run by a man whose spectacles fogged whenever he bent over his own counter. I paid the deposit in coin I had been saving since I was fifteen — my escape fund, I had called it, back when escape was a girlhood fancy and not an actuarial science. The clerk wrote *M. Marchetti, Sr.* in the manifest, misreading my handwriting, and I did not correct him, because *Sr.* was a small mercy: it put a generation between the Company's files and the ticket.

"One passenger, east passage, second of January," he recited. "You'll want to settle the balance by the thirtieth."

"The bearer will settle it," I said, and gave him the phrase from the harbor master's book, *per H.C. instructions,* just loosely enough that he assumed someone else's authority covered it, and he nodded the way clerks nod, which is gratefully, at anything that means less paperwork.

Then I walked home through the rain and I felt — I had expected to feel relief, or dread, or the clean click of a solved equation — I felt none of those. I felt like a woman who had quietly moved money out of someone else's account. Which, in a sense, and from a certain direction, is exactly what I had done. The berth was Tomás's by oath. It was Elias's by love. It might even, if Corvane's mercy ran further than I believed, which it did not, be mine.

Instead I had spent it, alone, in the rain, without asking a soul, on the only answer every column agreed on.

Papa would live. Papa would hate it. And someday, when he was an old man in a dry country breathing easily, he would learn what I had done, and either forgive me or not, and I would deserve whichever he chose.

I stopped under the chandlery awning and pressed my forehead against the wet wood and laughed until it turned into something else, because I had finally learned to write in Corvane's hand: decisions in secret, entries unexplained, the reasoning left for the auditors. I had told myself for months that the difference between us was procedure. It turned out the difference was only what the decisions were *for*.

"Miss Marchetti?" One of the divers' wives, ducking under the awning with fish wrapped in paper, peering at me through the rain. "You all right, child? You look like you've seen a ghost."

"Worse," I said, and found I could smile. "I've seen a balance sheet."

She clucked and moved on, satisfied, because in Port Sable that explanation covered nearly anything.

* * *

That evening Elias found me on the lighthouse stairs, because Elias always found me, the way iron finds north. We sat on the hundredth step with the lamp turning above us and the sea making its endless argument below, and I held the secret in my mouth like a stone.

"Corvane came by the point today," he said. "Two men with him, measuring the tideline. I doused the light for ten minutes to spoil their survey." He said it mildly, the way other men report weather. "His fellow will complain to the harbor master."

"Let him. The harbor master's ledger says the Company holds every berth on the last ship out, and magistrates find that interesting these days." I paused. "Or they will."

Elias looked at me. He has a way of looking that makes concealment feel like a waste of good material. "Mirabel. What have you done?"

"Nothing. Yet. Something. Both."

"Ah." He settled back against the wall, patient. With Elias, patience is not passivity; it's a kind of faith. "Whenever you're ready. The lamp turns itself for another quarter hour."

So I told him — not everything, not the ticket, not the name on the manifest. I told him the shape of it. *One berth. Four of us. I've done the sums.* And I watched him arrive, without being told, at exactly the conclusion I had reached, because he is Elias, and he said:

"It has to be your father."

"Yes."

"And you're going to arrange it without telling anyone, including him, because you've decided that's kinder."

"How —" I stopped. "Yes."

He was quiet while the lamp swept its shadow over us, once, twice. Then he said, "You know Tomás will call it betrayal. He'll say you became the Company."

"I know."

"And he'll be wrong, but not entirely wrong, and it will hurt him more than the truth would have."

"I *know*, Elias."

"Good." He took my hand. His palm was rough with lamp-work and salt. "Then do it anyway. Your father gets on that ship alive, and everything else can be survived. But Mirabel—" he turned so I had to look at him, "—when it comes apart, and it will come apart, don't you dare stand in the wreckage alone. You didn't get us into this sea by yourself, and you won't answer for it by yourself. That's not how we do our accounting."

I said nothing, because I was already keeping the other secret, the one I hadn't confessed: the ticket bought, the name written, the balance due on the thirtieth.

Above us the great lens turned, and turned, and turned, throwing its beam out over water that did not care what any of us decided, and below us the town lights came on one by one like a ledger filling up, and I sat in the warmth of the man who loved me and lied to him by omission, and understood at last why Corvane slept so badly, if indeed he slept at all.

Three days later, a fishing skiff ran the last of the monsoon into the harbor, flying no colors, and a familiar figure stood in her bow with his coat soaked through and his grin barely intact — and I knew, before he touched the dock, before he saw my face, that the summons in his breast pocket was going to change every sum I had made.

Tomás was home. And I had three days of secret left to spend.
