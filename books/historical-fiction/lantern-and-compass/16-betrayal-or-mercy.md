## 16. Betrayal or Mercy

The sea had tried to kill Tomás Ferro four times between here and the capital, and he had decided somewhere around the third attempt — a rogue wave off the Sentinel Shoals that picked up his little boat like a mother cat picking up an unruly kitten and then thought better of it — that he would never again complain about the monsoon. The monsoon kept ships in port. The monsoon meant no company agents boarding him at musket-point in the gray half-light of dawn, patting down his coat while he smiled and thought very loudly about fish.

He had the papers.

They were sewn into the lining of that same coat now, right over his heart, which was either poetry or stupidity depending on how the next week went. A magistrate's summons, signed and sealed, commanding one Mr. Ambrose Corvane, manager of the Halcyon Trading Company, Port Lazzara, to appear before the colonial court to answer charges of fraud, conspiracy, and unlawful dispossession. Tomás had read it so many times he could recite it in his sleep, and often did, which was why the other fishermen on the northbound run had given him the stern of the boat to himself.

*Answer charges.* He loved those two words. He wanted to have them embroidered on a cushion.

The harbor came up out of the haze the way it always did — first the lighthouse, patched and bandaged like a soldier back from the front; then the masts, thin as reeds; then the smell, tar and salt and drying nets, the smell Tomás's whole body understood as *home* even when his mind was busy pretending he'd rather be anywhere else. And then, because it was Port Lazzara and the town had a sense of humor about its own misfortunes, it began to rain.

He stood at the rail of the coastal steamer with his hands jammed in his pockets and let himself feel, for exactly one full minute, magnificent. He had done it. Through the tail of the monsoon, past the company cutter that had come alongside in the dark with lanterns swinging, past the customs man in the capital who had looked at Tomás's coat for three seconds too long and then been distracted by an argument about duty on dried squid — through all of it, the papers had come home. Mirabel's careful copies, her father's journal, the letter fragment with *the Marchetti family* written in Corvane's clerkly hand. And now a summons with a wax seal on it, heavy as an anchor, legal as sunrise.

Corvane would be served within days. Within weeks, the leases would stand. Within months, Tomás told himself, he'd drink rum on Elias's lighthouse gallery without calculating, out of old habit, how fast he could be over the rail and into the water.

He was still savoring this when the steamer tied up, and he saw Mirabel waiting on the dock, and he knew — before she said a word, before she even finished raising her hand — that something was wrong.

That is the trouble with found family. You learn each other's faces too well. A stranger would have seen only a small, composed young woman in a shawl, rain beginning to bead on her dark hair. Tomás saw the set of her shoulders, which was the set she got when she'd done arithmetic she didn't like, and the way her eyes went to his chest — to the coat — before they went to his face.

"Tomás." She said it carefully, the way you set down something breakable.

"Mira." He came down the gangway slowly. "You look terrible. Hasn't anyone been feeding you?"

"You look worse."

"That's the sea's opinion, not mine." He stopped in front of her. Rain drummed on the crates. Around them the dock went about its business — stevedores, gulls, one dog conducting a vigorous personal investigation of a barrel of salt herring. "I need to see Elias. Tonight. There are things we have to—"

"I know what you're carrying," Mirabel said. "I can see the shape of it in your pocket, and you've been holding your arm stiff since you got off the boat, which you only do when the coat matters. I keep the books, Tomás. I notice things."

"Good," he said. "Then you'll want to hear this part standing up."

He told her about the magistrate. The long gray office in the capital, the man behind the desk who had read her copied pages twice, made no expression whatsoever, and then sent for the summons paper as if he were ordering lunch. He told her about the near-boarding, the cutter's lanterns, the wave off Sentinel Shoals. He told it well, because he'd rehearsed it, and because he wanted to give her the gift of it properly: *you did this, all of you, and it worked.*

Mirabel laughed once — a real laugh, brief and startled out of her — and pressed both hands over her mouth, and for a moment she was twelve years old again, stealing guavas off the company barge with him while he taught her which watchman could be trusted to be asleep.

Then she said, "There's something you should know before we go to the others."

And there it was again, the arithmetic shoulders.

* * *

She told him on the walk up from the docks, under one umbrella that neither of them offered to share properly, so that they were both half-soaked and increasingly formal, like diplomats negotiating over a border.

"I took the berth," she said. "On the last ship. In Papa's name."

Tomás stopped walking. The umbrella tilted; rain ran down his collar. "You what?"

"There's one berth left. Corvane controls every other one through the harbor master — Elias found the payments in the ledgers. One berth, unclaimed. It's ours if we claim it fast enough. Papa can't survive another season here. The doctor said—" Her voice caught and smoothed itself out, that bookkeeper's discipline of hers, numbers balancing even when nothing else did. "So I secured it. For him."

"When?"

"Three days ago."

Three days ago Tomás had been forty leagues north, sleeping in a net hammock in a fisherman's loft and dreaming, actually dreaming, of coming home with the summons and watching her face when she saw it. "Three days," he repeated. "And you didn't write."

"A letter takes two days to get where you were, and might be read getting there."

"You didn't send word by any of six boats that leave this harbor every morning carrying people who owe me favors."

"Every one of whom reports to somebody," Mirabel said. "It wasn't safe."

"It wasn't *mine*," Tomás said, and heard his own voice come out flat and strange, and watched her flinch.

They walked the rest of the way to the divers' quarter in silence. That was the thing about anger between people who love each other: it doesn't always shout right away. Sometimes it walks beside you quietly, getting heavier, like ballast.

* * *

The Ferro family rule — established by Tomás's grandmother, enforced by Tomás's mother, and observed by Tomás mostly in the breach — was that no important conversation happened until everyone had eaten. So they ate. Mirabel's father, Rafael, propped in his chair by the window with a blanket over his knees, insisted on hearing the story of the wave off Sentinel Shoals twice and improving it the second time ("Four times its size, this wave, and our Tomás apologizing to it"). The old man laughed until the cough took him, and Mirabel was there before Tomás could move, cup and cloth already in hand, and the cough passed, and dinner went on, and the whole time Tomás sat with the summons against his heart and the anger sitting across from him wearing Mirabel's face.

Elias arrived after supper, wet from the lighthouse walk, bringing bread and the particular wary cheerfulness of a man who has walked into a room and correctly guessed that the temperature in it is not natural.

"So," Elias said, setting the loaf down. "Either someone died or someone caught a fish bigger than Tomás says he did."

"The summons," Tomás said, and took it out at last, unwrapped it from its oilcloth, and laid it on the table where the lamplight could reach the seal.

The room went still. Rafael Marchetti put down his cup. Mirabel leaned forward, and Tomás watched her read her own fate in another person's formal language — *appear before the court to answer* — and something in her face unlocked, just slightly, like a door opening onto weather.

"It's real," she whispered.

"It's real. Served on Corvane within the week. The magistrate wouldn't set a date further out — he said, and I quote, 'before the company finds something else to lose.'" Tomás allowed himself the grin he'd been saving. "Your pages, Mira. Your columns. The man read them and didn't blink, but he also didn't stop reading. I watched him cancel his evening over them."

Rafael reached out and touched the paper with one finger, gently, the way he must once have touched pearls. "All this from my diving journal," he said. "Forty years of water depths, and it ends up sinking a company."

Then Elias, being Elias — careful, observant, constitutionally incapable of letting an awkward thing sit unexamined — looked from Tomás to Mirabel and back and said, "All right. What's the other thing?"

* * *

It came out badly. Tomás would admit later that he'd intended it to come out well — calm, reasonable, brotherly — but intentions at that hour of a long night have the durability of wet paper.

"You took the berth," he said, "in secret. From me. From Elias. From your own father, from the sound of it." Rafael's head came up sharply at that, and Tomás registered it and barreled on anyway. "You sat at this table and let us plan around you, knowing you'd already decided the ending."

"I decided the only ending that keeps Papa alive," Mirabel said.

"That's not the point."

"Then say the point plainly, Tomás, because you know I'm slow with anything that isn't in columns."

The point. He had carried the point forty leagues through the tail of a monsoon and now it wouldn't come out in order. "The point," he said, "is that you did what Corvane does."

The words landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Elias said, "Tomás," in a low warning voice, and Rafael said nothing at all, which was worse, and Mirabel went very white and then very still.

"Do you want to try that sentence again?" she asked. Her voice was quiet. Tomás knew her quiet voices. This was the dangerous one.

"No," he said. "Because it's true and I've been chewing on it since the dock. Corvane decides what happens to people and tells them afterward, dressed up as mercy. *Sign the affidavit and your father keeps working. Take the berth and go quietly.* Every kindness he's ever offered came with the decision already made, in a room none of us were invited into. And you—" His hand shook; he flattened it on the table. "You booked Papa on that ship without asking him. Without telling me, the man who offered to carry you all east on his own boat, who has been planning the route in his head since chapter—since *autumn*. You decided whose life gets saved and whose gets consulted. That's a ledger, Mira. That's a column with names in it and you filling in the figures. That's the company."

The lamp hissed. Outside, rain worked steadily at the roof.

"You're right about one thing," Mirabel said at last, and her voice was still quiet, which meant it cost her something enormous. "I decided alone. I told myself it was for safety, and some of that was even true. But some of it was that I couldn't bear to watch you argue me out of it. Or watch Papa refuse, and have it be refused to his face, in time to do something else. So yes. I chose the version where nobody gets to say no to me." She swallowed. "That was wrong, and I'll answer for it."

"Good," Tomás started—

"But don't you dare," she went on, and now the quiet cracked and something hotter came through, "stand in this kitchen and tell me I'm *him*. I have spent nine years keeping his books with my eyes open so that someday I could do exactly what I did. I found the letter. I copied the pages. I burned my nights doing it. And when the berth appeared — one berth, Tomás, one — I looked at my father, who taught me to swim before he taught me to count, and I put his name in it, and I would do it again tonight. If that makes me the company, then the company should hire me, because apparently I'm the only one in this town willing to make an ugly decision with my own name attached to it instead of hiding behind a harbor master."

"Papa hasn't agreed," Elias said carefully. "Has he."

"He doesn't know yet," Mirabel said. "That was going to be tonight. Before you two turned it into a tribunal."

"It's not a tribunal," Tomás said, running both hands through his hair. "It's—Mira, I rowed through a storm for you. I slept in a customs shed. I have been composing speeches since the capital and not one of them ended with you calling me Corvane's apprentice."

"You called me Corvane first."

"I called what you *did* Corvane. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Rafael asked.

Everyone turned. The old man had been silent so long they'd half forgotten him, sunk in his blanket by the window, but his eyes were clear and following the argument like a man reading a page. He pushed himself upright, waved off Mirabel's helping hand with the mild tyranny of the very ill, and looked at his daughter.

"You bought me a berth," he said. "In my name. Without asking."

"Yes, Papa."

"And Tomás is angry because you didn't ask *him*."

"I'm angry because she didn't ask anyone," Tomás said. "Sir, you of all people—"

"Sit down, boy. I'm not finished deciding whose side I'm on." Rafael's voice was thin but it still had the old weight in it, the diver's voice, used to carrying up from deep water. He looked around the table, at the summons lying in the lamplight with its fat official seal, at Elias standing by the door with the untouched bread, at Mirabel with her chin up and her hands folded like a woman giving evidence.

"Here is what I know," he said. "My daughter has been carrying this family's arithmetic since she was ten years old, and she has never once dropped it, and I have never once thanked her properly, because thanking her would mean admitting I noticed. She bought me a ticket out of this drowning town because I am dying, and she couldn't bear to watch me argue, because she knows — she *knows*, the little accountant — that I would have argued. I would have said, spend it on yourself, you are young, you are the future, take the berth, Mira. And she'd have stood there with her jaw set and given the berth away to one of you two idiots, and the ship would sail empty, and Corvane would win, and I'd die in February anyway."

Silence. Rain.

"So she stole the decision," Rafael went on. "From all of us. Including me. Especially me." He held up a hand before Mirabel could speak. "That was a hard thing and a wrong thing and I'm angrier about it than I can conveniently express while sitting down. But she stole it *for* us, which is not what Corvane does, and if any of you can't tell the difference, you haven't been paying attention for nine years, and I'll thank you to start."

Tomás looked at Mirabel. Mirabel looked at the table. Elias, wisely, studied the bread.

"I'm still angry," Tomás said finally, because somebody had to be honest in this family and it was clearly going to be him. "Not at the berth. At the secrecy. We're supposed to be — we're not blood, any of us, we built this, and the whole point of building it is that nobody decides alone anymore. Not the company. Not us. Nobody."

"I know," Mirabel said. And then, because she was Mirabel: "I've entered it in the ledger. Under debts. Mine."

"Put it under losses," Rafael said. "Debts get paid. Losses get carried forward." He paused, and something moved across his ruined face that was almost a smile. "Speaking of which. Nobody has actually asked me what I intend to do about this berth."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when Rafael had been eased into bed and Elias had gone to relieve his mother at the lighthouse and the lamp had burned down to a thumb's width of oil — Tomás stood in the doorway of the little house and looked at Mirabel, who sat at the table with the summons in front of her, copying out something in her small precise hand.

"For the record," he said, "I didn't mean it. What I said on the dock road. Mostly."

"You meant some of it."

"I meant all of the anger and maybe a tenth of the comparison. The rest was fear talking with my voice. I spent two weeks thinking the hardest thing would be getting the papers past Corvane's agents." He came and sat down across from her. "Turns out the hard part is coming home and finding out you've been fighting the war without me."

"You weren't supposed to fight it," Mirabel said. "You were supposed to survive it. That was always the deal, Tomás. You get out. That's why it had to be you."

"I know what the deal was." He tapped the summons. "Deals change. Ask your magistrate." Then, because the silence had gone soft and needed breaking: "Does Elias know about the berth yet? Properly?"

"He knows there's a berth. He doesn't know whose name is in it."

"And Papa's about to find out." Tomás leaned back and studied the ceiling, listening to the rain ease off toward morning. "You realize what happens next. He refuses it. He's sitting up in that bed right now composing the speech. *I will not leave while my daughter stands trial against a company in the town it owns.* Something noble and infuriating and completely impossible to argue with."

"I know," Mirabel said quietly.

"And then we're back where we started. One berth, three candidates, five days until the ship sails, and Corvane served before the week is out." Tomás grinned, tired and crooked and a little bit terrified. "Happy New Year, Mira."

She didn't answer. She was looking at the summons, at the date written at the bottom of it in the magistrate's iron hand — the date Corvane must appear, which was, Tomás realized as he followed her finger, the same date the ship was scheduled to sail.

The hearing and the last berth out, on the same morning. Someone would have to choose, and this time, Tomás swore, nobody was choosing alone.
