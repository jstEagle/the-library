## 17. The Century Turns

The last day of the old century arrived wearing its best clothes, as if the harbor itself had decided to make an impression on the new one. The monsoon had loosened its grip overnight into a fine silver drizzle, and the light came down soft and forgiving over the rooftops, gilding the puddles in the lanes, turning the fishing boats at their moorings into a fleet of small bright promises.

Mirabel did not trust it.

She had learned long ago that beautiful days were the ones to watch. Beautiful days were when Mr. Corvane smiled. Beautiful days were when the company sent its clerks out with clean shirts and cleaner lies. So she stood at the window of their rooms above the chandlery, watching the *Halcyon Star* sit fat and patient at the deep-water berth, her funnels breathing slow grey smoke, and she held the ticket in her hand until the paper grew damp from her palm.

One berth. First class, no less — Corvane's own generosity, purchased in her father's name, which was its own kind of insult. The company had printed his name in block letters: **MARCHETTI, SANTINO**. Destination: the capital. Departure: six o'clock this evening, on the turn of the tide.

Beside it on the table lay the other paper, the one Tomás had carried through the tail of the monsoon and back: the magistrate's summons, bearing a wax seal the color of dried blood, commanding one Mr. Edmund Corvane, resident manager of the Halcyon Trading Company, to appear before the colonial court at the Harbor House tomorrow morning, the first day of the new century, to answer charges of fraud, conspiracy, and unlawful dispossession.

Two papers on one table. One bought with money. One bought with Tomás's blood, salt, and nine days of terror on open water.

Mirabel had spent three weeks arranging the first so that her father would never have to see the second. That was the whole design of it, elegant as any ledger entry: Papa would board the ship tonight, angry, betrayed, cursing her name in two languages perhaps, but he would be *alive* by Thursday. He would breathe capital air that didn't taste of salt and coal dust. He would outlive her anger at him, and someday, when the hearing was done and whatever happened happened, she would write him a long letter explaining everything, and he would forgive her, because he was her father, and fathers forgave daughters who saved their lives. That was arithmetic she could stand behind.

The flaw in the design, she now understood, was that her father had kept ledgers longer than she had.

She heard him on the stairs — that terrible new sound of his climbing, pause-rest-pause, each step negotiated like a trade agreement — and she slid the summons beneath the diving journal out of pure reflex, though she knew, even as she did it, that it was already too late for hiding things.

He came in drying his hands, thin as a bundle of lashed reeds, his good coat hanging off him like something left behind by a larger man. The consumption had carved him through the wet season, but his eyes were as clear as they'd ever been, and they went — of course they went — straight to the table.

"Six o'clock," he said pleasantly. "You've been guarding that ticket like it owes you money."

"Papa—"

"Sit down, Mirabel." He lowered himself into his chair by the window, the one he'd repaired four times because he refused to buy a new one while the company still counted him as property. "And bring me the other paper too. The one under my journal. You've moved it three times since Tuesday. You think I don't notice? I noticed when your mother hid the brandy from me in 1888, and I found it in the flour bin, and I drank less, not more."

There was no argument to make against a man who had audited his own household. Mirabel drew out the summons and laid it beside the ticket, and then she sat across from him with her hands folded like a schoolgirl awaiting sentence, which, she supposed, in a way she was.

Her father read the document twice. His finger traced the magistrate's seal. For a long moment there was no sound in the room but the drizzle against the glass and the distant, idiotic cheerfulness of the harbor bells ringing in practice for midnight.

"So," he said. "Tomás made it."

"Tomás made it."

"And you meant to put me on that ship tonight without telling me any of this."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, and to her astonishment he looked — not angry. Something older than angry. He looked at her the way he used to look at difficult columns that balanced anyway.

"Your mother did the same thing to me once," he said. "Hid a debt so I wouldn't worry. A small one. Eighteen rupees. I found it and I didn't speak to her for a week, and do you know why? Not for the money." He tapped the summons. "Because she decided what I could bear. That is not love, Mirabel. That is bookkeeping. It looks tidy from the outside, but someone has been erased from the column."

"I know," Mirabel said, and her voice cracked in the middle of it. "I know exactly what it is. I learned it from the best company in the port." She stood up and went to the window because she couldn't say the rest facing him. "Papa, you cannot survive another winter here. Dr. Ferreira said —"

"Dr. Ferreira says many things. He also drinks his own tonics, so we take his pronouncements in moderation."

"*Papa.*" She turned around. "The ticket is in your name. Six o'clock. Please. Please just take it and go, and let me do this one thing right. Tomorrow I testify. Whatever Corvane does after, whatever he takes out of me for it — you'll be gone. Safe. And I can fight better knowing it. That's not erasure, that's *strategy*, and you taught me strategy."

Her father was quiet for a while. Then he did the thing he always did when he was about to win an argument: he reached for the teapot and poured two cups with great care, as if the fate of empires depended on the temperature of the water, and pushed one across the table toward her.

"Sit," he said. "Now. Let us look at the accounts."

"Papa—"

"Accounts, Mirabel. You want to fight me with feelings; I will fight you with figures, and you know whose ground that is." He laid the ticket flat and smoothed it with two fingers. "Item one. One berth, first class, value approximately everything we own, purchased by Edmund Corvane. Question: why does a man fighting for his life before a magistrate give his enemy's daughter a free ticket?" He didn't wait for her answer. "Answer: because he wants the Marchettis gone. Both of us, or failing that, the embarrassing sick one. A quiet departure looks like guilt. It makes your testimony tomorrow look like the tantrum of a girl who fled. He is not offering mercy, tesoro. He is offering stagecraft."

"He isn't offering anything," Mirabel said. "*I* secured it. In your name. Weeks ago, before the summons, before — I did it so there would be a door open. Whatever else it started as, it's yours now. It's the only door."

"Item two." Her father's voice stayed gentle, maddeningly gentle. "Assets remaining in the Marchetti name: one daughter, age nineteen, fluent in double-entry, feared by clerks, loved by divers. One summons, duly served. One community of some three hundred souls who will stand in that courtroom tomorrow and watch the witness stand to see whether the woman who kept their rosters stands alone." He looked up at her. "If you send me away tonight, how does that column read?"

"It reads *alive*," Mirabel said fiercely. "It reads alive, which is the only column that matters when you're dead, Papa. I have watched you cough blood into a handkerchief and fold it away like laundry. I have measured my whole life in portable things because of you — because everything we own could fit in two cases in case the company called the debt. Do not ask me to stand in front of three hundred people tomorrow knowing that every word costs me my father."

The room went very still. Outside, the rain had stopped altogether, and the evening light was coming up gold along the water.

Her father set down his cup. When he spoke, his voice had changed — softer, and lower, the voice he used for the rare true things.

"When I was a young man," he said, "I dove the beds off the point, before the bad air took my chest. And there is a thing about diving that nobody tells you on shore. When the bottom silt rises and you cannot see — the temptation is to bolt for the surface. Every animal bone in you screams for the surface. But the men who bolted, they tore their lungs, or they hit their heads on the hull, or they came up so fast the air in their blood turned to froth. The men who survived were the ones who went *slow*. Who trusted the rope in their hand, and the partner above, and their own count of the seconds. Slow is how you live. Panic is how you drown." He folded his hands over the summons. "I am dying slowly, Mirabel. It is the one skill I have left. Let me use it properly."

"That is a horrible thing to say and I refuse to find it wise."

"You'll find it wise tomorrow." Almost, he smiled. "You asked me once why I never tried to buy out my indenture early, when the company offered, years ago. You were eleven. You said, 'Papa, why are we owned?' Do you remember what I told you?"

Mirabel remembered. She had carried the sentence all her life like a coin sewn into a hem. *"We are owned in the books,"* he'd said, *"but the sea doesn't read."*

"The sea doesn't read," he said now. "But men do, tesoro. Men read ledgers, and men read courtrooms, and tomorrow men will read *you*. And if your last living Marchetti relative is safely on a ship, paid for by Halcyon money, then everything you say in that room becomes a story about a girl with a grievance. But if I am sitting in the third row, coughing into my handkerchief where the magistrate can see exactly what the company's pearl beds cost the men who work them—" he lifted his chin, and for a moment she saw the diver he had been, the man who had walked on the bottom of the world, "—then I testify too. Without saying a word. My lungs are evidence, Mirabel. They are the only part of me the company cannot cross-examine."

"You'll kill yourself."

"I will die either way, and probably here, which is where your mother is buried and where you learned to walk and where every bed lease in that lagoon has my mark on it alongside forty other families." He reached across the table and took her hand, and his grip, though thin, was still a working grip, callused at the pads from thirty years of rope and pen. "You wanted to choose for me because choosing is heavy. I understand. I carried you twenty years so you would not have to carry me. But tesoro — listen to your old father, who has done only one calculation correctly his entire life." 

He turned the ticket over and, taking the pencil from behind his ear, wrote on the back of it in his careful bookkeeper's hand:

**Refused. See attached testimony.**

Then he slid it back across the table to her.

"There is one berth," he said. "It was always going to be an unbearable sum, whoever took it. You thought to spare me the bill. But the bill is mine, and I pay my debts. Now—" and here his voice gentled all the way down, became simply her father again, tired and fond, "—the only question left in this account is yours. The ship leaves at six. The hearing is at nine. Corvane expects you to spend the night choosing between them, wringing your hands, and he has prepared arguments for both. So deny him the whole night, tesoro. What do you *want*?"

Mirabel looked at the ticket. MARCHETTI, SANTINO, in block letters, voided now by five words in pencil. She looked at her father, who had just handed her back the weight she had stolen from him, and discovered that the weight, returned, weighed almost nothing at all — because it was hers, and chosen things are always lighter than imposed ones.

She thought of Elias up at the lighthouse, blacking the lantern for the New Year, keeping his vigil over the water the way he had kept it his whole life. She thought of Tomás somewhere below the hill, whistling outside the divers' shanties, spreading the news that the summons had teeth. She thought of three hundred people, and a courtroom, and her own voice, which had spent years reading numbers aloud in rooms where no one listened.

Tomorrow it would read a letter into the record. The whole of it. Every clause.

"I want to stay," she said, and heard her own voice come out steady, and was surprised, and then wasn't. "I want to testify, and win, and keep the books for the cooperative afterward, and grow old arguing with you about rounding errors. I want the century to arrive and find us all still here."

Her father closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does when a long column finally balances.

"Then that is what the record shows," he said. "Go and fetch your good dress. And Mirabel —" he caught her at the door, "—tell that lighthouse boy to burn the lamp bright tonight. Half this port will be awake to see the year 1900 come in, and sailors are superstitious about new centuries. As well they should be." His eyes glittered, and suddenly he looked fifteen years younger and entirely capable of mischief. "New centuries begin however the first hours go. Make sure ours goes loudly."

She was halfway down the stairs, laughing and crying in the same breath, when the harbor bells began to ring — not the practice peal now but the real thing, six o'clock exactly, the tide turning, and through the window she saw the *Halcyon Star*'s deck lights blaze on, one berth empty, her father's refusal riding in her pocket like a lit coal.

Somewhere across the water, in a house with more windows than conscience, Mr. Corvane was surely watching that ship prepare to sail one passenger short, and recalculating.

Let him recalculate, Mirabel thought, stepping out into the blue first hour of the evening. Tomorrow she would read him his own columns, and the century could start with the sound of them.

Behind her, up the hill, the great lens of the lighthouse swept the darkening water — once, twice — and then flared so bright it hurt, burning like a promise kept.
