## 18. Hearing at the Harbor House

The Harbor House had been built by men who believed that architecture could make them important. It had a dome, four columns, and a floor of imported teak that creaked in exactly the way no local wood creaked — a floor that had traveled farther than most of the people who walked on it. For nineteen years I had passed it on my way to the company counting house and thought of it as the place where other people's decisions were made.

On the morning of the hearing, I stood at the foot of its steps with a crate of ledgers under one arm and discovered that the columns looked smaller from below. Everything did, when you were carrying your own weight instead of someone else's.

"You're doing the thing," Tomás said beside me. He had insisted on walking me there, over my objections, over his mother's objections, and over what he claimed were the objections of several saints whose names he invented freely.

"What thing?"

"The face. The ledger face. You get it when the columns won't add." He squinted at the doors. "Breathe, Miri. They're just men in coats. I've smuggled past worse."

"You've been caught by worse."

"Twice," he agreed cheerfully. "But never twice by the same man, which is the relevant statistic."

Inside, the chamber smelled of beeswax and old varnish and the particular staleness of rooms where powerful men nap after lunch. The magistrate, a Mr. Okafor, was a compact, precise man who had arrived from the capital two days earlier and had already offended the harbor master by asking to see the harbor master's books. I liked him immediately, in the way one likes anyone holding a weapon you need.

The ledgers went onto the witness table in their crate, wrapped in oilcloth against the humidity. My father sat in the front row, wrapped in a blanket despite the heat, because Dr. Ferrand had pronounced him well enough to attend but not well enough to pretend he wasn't dying, which my father considered an insult to his character. Beside him sat Elias, out of his lighthouse for the first time in weeks, wearing a shirt that was visibly Tomás's and visibly unhappy about it. The divers filled three rows behind them — Roshan and his daughters, old Mateo who had dived until his ears gave out, the Widow Chen who ran the net menders' stall and had closed it for the morning, which in her economy was roughly equivalent to declaring a national holiday.

Corvane sat across the aisle with two lawyers from the capital and the expression of a man watching weather approach his picnic.

I had rehearsed this. In the lighthouse, in the counting house after hours, in the bath, I had read those ledgers aloud until Elias threatened to leave home if he heard the phrase "carried forward" one more time. But rehearsal is a country you visit; the morning itself is where you live.

"All rise," someone said, and then we were underway.

* * *

The first hour belonged to procedure, which is to say to Latin and patience. The magistrate's clerk read the summons. Corvane's senior lawyer, a smooth man named Pellering whose voice sounded like money being counted slowly, rose to observe that the charges rested on "the fevered interpretation of commercial documents by persons of limited education."

"Then it should be easy to refute," Okafor said mildly, without looking up. "Call your first witness."

Pellering called Mirabel Marchetti, which surprised no one except perhaps himself; I suspect he intended to dismantle me before lunch.

I took the oath with my hand on a book they offered, which turned out to be a shipping almanac, which felt appropriate. Sworn on the tides, more or less.

"Miss Marchetti. You are indentured to the Halcyon Trading Company."

"I am."

"As a bookkeeper."

"For eleven years."

"And yet you come here to testify *against* your employer. Isn't that so? A girl raised on the company's coin, educated — such as it is — at the company's expense, now turning on the hand that fed her." He let that hang like smoke. "Why should this court trust the arithmetic of a woman so obviously aggrieved?"

The room had gone very quiet. Somewhere behind me, Tomás exhaled through his teeth.

I had prepared answers about duty and truth and justice. What came out instead was the truth, which is always better organized.

"Because, sir, I am good at arithmetic. Better than anyone in this room, including your client's own chief clerk, who carries his sevens wrong and has done for six years, which I know because I correct them." A ripple through the divers' rows; the Widow Chen made a sound like a kettle beginning to think about boiling. "You ask why you should trust me. You shouldn't trust me. You should trust the ledgers. They don't belong to me. They belong to the Halcyon Company. Every figure I'm going to show you today was written by Halcyon pens, in Halcyon ink, initialed by Halcyon clerks. I'm not here to tell you what happened." I opened the crate. "I'm here to read it to you."

Okafor set down his pen and folded his hands, which I would learn was his version of sitting forward.

"Proceed," he said.

* * *

I began where I had begun everything: with column forty-one.

"This is the general lease ledger, maintained continuously since 1874. Each diver family holds a bed lease on the lagoon's eastern shallows, renewable annually upon payment recorded here. Note the payments — punctual, thirty-one years of them, initialed twice yearly by the company agent. The beds are lawfully held. Does the court accept that as the baseline?"

"Objection," Pellering said, "the witness is testifying to conclusions—"

"The witness is testifying to entries," Okafor said. "Overruled. Continue, Miss Marchetti, and go slowly. I intend to follow every number."

And so I went slowly. I showed him entry 2213, dated last March: *Survey costs, eastern shallows, per Mr. C.'s instruction — charged to maintenance.* And beneath it, in the same hand, the same month, a second entry charging survey costs again — this time to the land acquisition fund. "The company surveyed the divers' leased beds twice and paid for it twice, once disguised as roof repairs. One does not commission two surveys of one's own roof."

I showed him the disbursement schedule from October, when the divers' rations were cut — reductions recorded as *provisional adjustments pending tenancy review.* "Tenancy review of what, sir? The leases were renewed in June. There is nothing to review unless the review's purpose is to manufacture something to find."

Pellering objected four times in the next twenty minutes. Okafor overruled him four times, each with the tone of a man declining a second dessert.

Then I brought out the letter.

It was only a scrap — the corner I had salvaged from the clerk's brazier, singed at the edge, but the writing intact: *...the Marchetti name may be usefully attached to the instrument, the family being contract-bound and therefore unable to contest...* and beneath it, the fatal fragment, *...upon execution, all eastern bed leases revert to company title.*

"Whose hand is this?" Okafor asked.

"It belongs to Mr. Corvane. I have reconciled his correspondence against his dictation log for three years. He writes his f's with a crossbar. No one else in the office does."

Corvane's lawyers produced a flurry of paper arguing the fragment proved nothing, was inadmissible, was possibly a forgery, was certainly regrettable. While they argued, Okafor picked up the scrap himself and turned it toward the light, and I watched his eyes move along the line about the Marchetti name and stop there.

"Miss Marchetti," he said quietly. "This is your family's name."

"Yes, sir."

"Contract-bound and therefore unable to contest. Do you understand what that sentence means?"

"I have understood it since the night I pulled it out of a fire, sir. It means the company intended to use my father's indenture as a leash to make our family sign away every diver's livelihood in the lagoon — including his own — and call it consent. It means they chose us because they thought a bookkeeper's daughter would do what she was told."

The silence afterward had a texture. I did not look at my father, because if I had, I would have stopped being useful, and I had eleven years of useful in me and one morning to spend it.

* * *

We broke for midday meal. The divers ate on the steps like it was a festival; someone had brought fried dough, and Tomás was selling rumors at two for one. Elias found me in the corridor, where I was standing with my back against cool stone trying to remember how knees worked.

"You were magnificent," he said.

"I was reading out loud."

"You were reading out loud like a broadside fired at close range." He touched my elbow, briefly, the way we touched each other in public — a whole letter compressed into two seconds. "Your father's telling anyone who'll listen about the sevens. He's claiming he taught you that."

"He taught me none of it. He taught me to hold my breath for two minutes and to be suspicious of anyone who owns more boats than children."

"That explains your entire personality," Tomás said, arriving with fried dough for three. "Eat. Magistrates can smell low blood sugar. It's why they're so cruel before lunch."

Through the open doors I could see Corvane in the anteroom, speaking low and fast to Pellering, one hand flat on the wall as though steadying himself against the ship's roll. He caught me looking. He did not look away, and neither did I, and it occurred to me that we were finally having an honest conversation, eleven years late.

* * *

Afternoon belonged to Corvane himself.

Pellering put him on the stand to repair the damage, which was like sending a man to bail the ocean. Corvane testified in his dry, reasonable voice about prudent management, seasonal adjustments, routine surveys. He was good. He had been good at this his whole life; I had watched him make suppliers feel honored to be underpaid.

Okafor listened for an hour. Then he removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, and said, "Mr. Corvane, explain entry 2298."

"There are many entries, Your Honor. I keep a large office."

"Entry 2298. Dated the twelfth of November. *Berth reservation, last sailing, one passage, fee waived, per arrangement.* Fee waived. The company does not waive fees, Mr. Corvane. Your own ledgers record a dispute over a waived fee of three pence in 1891 that consumed nine pages of correspondence. Who was the berth reserved for?"

"A shipping irregularity," Corvane said. "An administrative courtesy."

"To whom?"

"A matter between the company and—" He stopped. I watched him stop, watched the calculation happen behind his eyes, watched him choose. "Between the company and the party concerned."

Okafor turned to me. "Miss Marchetti, you audited these entries?"

"Yes, sir. The berth was reserved in a blank name — the only blank passenger entry in the harbor master's whole registry, which the harbor master's own clerk confirmed in his deposition. Every other line has a name. That one has Corvane's initials and a dash." I laid the harbor manifest beside the ledger. "And sir, note the date of the reservation. The twelfth of November. Two days before the rations were cut. He bought the empty berth before he began starving the divers toward it."

The chamber made a sound — not applause, nothing so legible, but a long collective intake of breath, thirty people understanding the same arithmetic at the same moment. The berth had never been mercy. It had been inventory.

"Mr. Corvane," Okafor said, in the mild tone of a man opening a door onto a very long drop, "reserved for whom?"

For the first time in nineteen years of watching him, I saw Mr. Corvane hesitate before speaking. The pause lasted two seconds. In bookkeeping, two seconds is a chasm.

"I decline to answer on advice of counsel."

"Mm," said Okafor, and wrote something down that I would have given a year of my life to read.

* * *

The end, when it came, came the way accounts close — not with thunder but with a final line drawn under a sum already totaled.

Pellering, sensing the tide, made a strategic retreat into technicality: even granting everything, he argued, the leases were company paper governed by company terms; whatever irregularities existed were matters for commercial arbitration, not this court.

Okafor let him finish, which was the cruelest thing I saw all day.

"Mr. Pellering, I have spent two days listening to your client's own records. His ledgers renew the divers' leases in June. His ledgers pay double for surveys of those same beds in March. His ledgers cut the lessees' provisions in November while reserving an anonymous berth on the only ship leaving before the new year. His own correspondence — in his own hand, f's crossed or otherwise — proposes attaching an indentured family's name to an instrument of dispossession precisely *because* they could not refuse." He stacked his pages and squared their edges. "You invite me to read all this as commerce. Very well. As commerce, it is fraud dressed in double-entry, and I have jurisdiction over fraud anywhere in this territory. As law, the leases stand. They stand, sir, because thirty-one years of payments say they stand, and those payments are the only honest numbers your client ever kept."

He turned to the room.

"The leases of the eastern pearl beds are confirmed to the diver families in perpetuity, renewal formalities suspended pending cooperative administration. Mr. Corvane is recalled to the capital to answer charges before the colonial board, effective immediately; his authority in this port is suspended as of noon today. Bailiffs will secure the company offices and all books therein — Miss Marchetti, you will prepare an index of those books for the court, at day wages."

"At day wages," Tomás whispered behind me, scandalized with admiration. "She's billing the empire."

The chamber erupted — not the divers first, strangely, but the Widow Chen, who stood up and shouted something triumphant in her own language and then translated it badly on purpose so everyone could join in. Roshan was embracing his daughters. Old Mateo was crying without any attempt to hide it, which undid me far worse than anything Okafor had said.

My father did not stand. He was too proud to be helped up and too ill to manage it alone, so he stayed seated in the flood of celebration and reached out and took my wrist as I passed — his grip still stronger than the doctors said it had any right to be — and pressed something into my palm.

His father's compass. Brass, salt-eaten, the needle wobbling on its pin for forty years and finding north anyway.

"You'll want it," he said, "for the index," as though that explained anything, and it did, it explained everything, and I had to turn away quickly and pretend to check the oilcloth.

* * *

By evening the Harbor House had emptied and the news had spread down to the water, where someone had already started singing, badly, and been joined by everyone. I sat on the steps with the crate, lighter now by one letter, which Okafor had sealed as evidence and I had watched leave my hands with less grief than I expected. Evidence was safer than secret. Secret was a weight; evidence was a lever.

Elias found me there and sat down without asking, which was our oldest arrangement.

"So," he said. "You won."

"We haven't won. A magistrate ruled. Rulings are paper. Paper needs holding." I leaned my head on his shoulder. "But it was a good day."

"It was a very good day. Your sevens are famous now. Children will study them."

"Don't," I laughed, "I'll get insufferable," and he said, "too late," and we sat while the singing swelled below.

It was Tomás who noticed, because noticing was his trade. He came up the steps two at a time, and his face had gone from festival to something older and colder, the face he wore in the boat at night.

"Miri. The counting house." He was breathing hard. "There's light in the counting house. After dark. And four men on the dock who aren't dockmen — I know every back on that waterfront, and those backs are wrong."

I looked at Elias, and Elias was already on his feet, already looking south toward the lighthouse, where the lamp would be needing lighting, alone, in a tower full of oil and paint and dry timber.

The needle in my father's compass trembled in my pocket, and pointed, the way it always had, toward the water.
