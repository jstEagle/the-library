## 20. What Cannot Be Carried

The rain ended on a Tuesday, the way the rain always did — not all at once but in stages, like a guest who kept remembering one more thing to say. By Wednesday noon the clouds had thinned to rags. By Thursday the lagoon lay flat and shining as a coin left out on a table, and the whole town came blinking into the streets like creatures surprised by daylight after a long sleep underground.

Mirabel watched it from the window of her father's room, where she had watched it for eleven days.

Eleven days since the hearing. Eleven days since she had stood in the Harbor House with the company's own ledgers open on the table before her and read Corvane's arithmetic aloud until his columns collapsed beneath him like a house of cards in a draught. Eleven days since the magistrate had said the words — *the leases stand* — in a voice as dry as any ledger line, and half the harbor had begun to weep while the other half began to sing.

And eleven days since the fire at the lighthouse, since the arrests, since four divers had spent three nights in the company lockhouse before the magistrate's men walked them out into the light. Since Tomás had taken a boot heel above the eye that was only now fading from plum to yellow. Since her father had sat through all of it propped up on pillows, coughing into his handkerchief, refusing with a wave of that same hand every suggestion that he be carried anywhere at all.

He was asleep now, in the afternoon heat. His breathing had the ragged rhythm she knew better than her own heartbeat — two short pulls, one long, a pause that never stopped frightening her no matter how many hundred times she'd heard it. The doctor came twice a week and said things like *comfortable* and *managed*, which were the words doctors used when they had run out of honest ones.

On the table beside the window lay her indenture contract.

It had come back to her that morning, delivered by a clerk so young his collar was still newer than his fear. He had knocked as though the door might bite, handed over the folded document with both hands the way you'd return a borrowed knife, and said, in a voice barely larger than the rain's last drips: *With the company's compliments, miss. It's been voided. Legally.* And then he had fled down the stairs as if pursued.

She had unfolded it on this very table and read it through once more, top to bottom, though she knew it by heart — had known it, in the way of things learned too young and too hard, since she was nine years old. *Mirabel Marchetti, daughter of Emilio Marchetti, bound to the Halcyon Trading Company for a term of twelve years, in consideration of debts accrued...*

Debts accrued. Her whole life pressed flat between those four words, dried like a flower in a book.

The paper was fine stock, heavier than it looked. Company paper. She remembered signing it — or rather, she remembered her father's hand closed around hers, guiding the pen, because she had been too small to make the signature legible on her own. Nine years old, and her name already spoken for. She had thought at the time that the ink smelled like almonds. She had thought, because no one told her otherwise, that this was simply what happened to daughters of pearl divers who could not pay the diving master.

Outside, someone was laughing. Actual laughter, ringing off the wet stones of the street, and underneath it the creak of oars and the shout of boats going out again — the first boats since the storm season closed. The lagoon opening its hand.

Her father stirred. "If you're going to hover," he said, without opening his eyes, "hover more quietly."

"I'm not hovering. I'm standing near the window thinking."

"You stand near the window thinking the way a gull stands near your lunch." He opened his eyes then, found her, and smiled the crooked smile that the cough had worn thin but hadn't managed to break. "Come sit. All this pacing is bad for my nerves."

"You're asleep."

"I was. Now I'm an old man awake in the afternoon with nothing to do but watch my daughter wear a hole in the floorboards. Sit."

She sat. The chair by his bed had molded itself to her shape over these weeks; she suspected she would carry its ache in her back for years, and found she didn't much mind. On the sill between them sat his diving journal, fat and salt-swollen, the pages she had hidden inside it long since given over to the magistrate but the journal itself returned — evidence examined and released, like everything else the company had ever handled without understanding what it held.

"That clerk came back," her father said. "Little one. Looked like a rabbit that had wandered into a chapel."

"He brought me something." She reached over and laid her hand flat on the contract without looking at it. Under her palm the paper felt cool, almost alive, like the skin of the lagoon at dawn.

Her father followed the gesture. He was quiet a moment. Then: "So it's finished, then. Properly."

"Properly voided. There's even a stamp." She lifted the corner to show him the red seal, absurdly festive, like a wax flower on a cake. "They stamped it. As though it were a passport instead of a prison sentence."

"They do love their stamps." He coughed then, briefly, and she was beside him before she'd decided to move, holding the cup, steadying the pillow, doing all the small offices of love that had become as automatic as breathing. When it passed he waved her back to the chair with the same hand that had forbidden her, months ago, to fight. That hand had been wrong about the fighting. She tried to remember that hands could be wrong about things and still be beloved.

"Papa," she said. "I'm going to burn it."

His eyebrows rose — one of them, the one that still moved independently of the other. "Burn it."

"Burn it. Tonight, I think. Or now. I haven't decided whether ceremony requires waiting for dark."

He considered this the way he considered everything now: slowly, with pauses, as though each thought had to be dived for and brought up from depth. "You know," he said at last, "when you were small, you kept everything. Pebbles. Buttons. A dead crab you loved so much we had a funeral. Your mother said, *that girl will never let go of anything.*" He looked at the window, at the light. "She'd have liked watching you now."

Mirabel found, to her surprise, that her eyes were stinging. She had not wept at the hearing — her voice had needed to stay level for the ledgers — and had not wept when the divers walked free, and had not wept when Elias had appeared at her door the night of the fire smelling of smoke and triumph, his hands bandaged, saying only *it held, Miri, the old girl held* about a lighthouse as if speaking of a grandmother. Grief and relief, it turned out, kept their own calendar. They arrived when the rooms got quiet.

"There's a basin," her father said gently, and pointed, and she laughed and cried at the same time, which was undignified, and he pretended not to notice, which was kind.

* * *

Word traveled fast in a town the size of theirs, faster than it had any right to. By evening, word had traveled that Mirabel Marchetti meant to burn her contract, and by the time she carried the thing down to the beach behind the counting houses, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, she had acquired an audience.

Tomás was there, of course, perched on an overturned skiff with his bruised eye going gold at the edges, looking for all the world like a man attending a coronation. Elias came down from the lighthouse with the smell of fresh paint on him, walking beside old Serafina the pearl diver, who had declared months ago that Mirabel was the granddaughter her bones had been owed all along. Behind them straggled perhaps thirty others — divers mostly, and dockworkers, and the widow Paqui who ran the chandlery and had never in living memory been seen outdoors after supper.

"This," said Tomás, "is the best idea anyone has ever had in the history of this town, and I include the invention of rum."

"It's a piece of paper," Mirabel said, feeling suddenly foolish.

"It is a piece of paper," Tomás agreed, sliding off the skiff with the grace of a man who had never once done anything gracefully on land, "that owned you. Twelve years of you, signed away when you were small enough to believe ink was just ink. We are going to burn it on a beach, publicly, and there will be singing, and I have already arranged the singing." He produced, from somewhere, a bottle of the rough plum brandy he traded in and did not drink. "For toasts. Somebody else drink it, I've a reputation."

Serafina took the bottle with the dignity of a woman accepting tribute. "Fire," she said. "Good. Fire remembers."

Nobody asked what she meant. Serafina's pronouncements were accepted in that community the way weather was accepted: without argument, and usually proven right.

Elias came to stand beside her at the tideline, where she had cleared a small hollow among the stones and stacked driftwood in a structure that was less a pyre than a suggestion of one. He looked tired in the way she had come to think of as honest-tired — worked-through, salt-scoured, nothing hidden in it. The lighthouse stood behind him up the point, patched and painted, her lantern and his, though the lantern part was mostly his and she'd stopped pretending the distinction mattered.

"I brought this," he said, and held out a stub of candle. "For lighting it. Seemed right. Fire from the lighthouse."

"It's not from the lighthouse. It's from your pocket."

"Everything in my pocket is from the lighthouse. My pockets live there."

She laughed, and kissed him briefly — they had gotten plain about it lately, kisses exchanged like tools passed between workmates, efficient and warm and nobody making speeches — and knelt to set the contract on the driftwood.

Unwrapped, under the last grey daylight, the document looked smaller than it had that morning. Smaller than it had looked nine years ago, certainly, when it had been large enough to swallow her whole future. She read the first line once more. *In consideration of debts accrued.*

Then she touched the candle to the corner, and the flame took it.

Paper burns differently than wood — quickly, brightly, almost eagerly, the way certain kinds of grief go up all at once once you finally let them. The corner blackened, curled, glowed orange along its edge. The words *Mirabel Marchetti* darkened and swelled and vanished. Someone behind her — the widow Paqui, by the timbre — began to hum, and then others joined in, an old shanty about the sea giving back what it takes, sung slightly off-key and entirely without shame.

"Should you want to say anything?" Elias asked quietly.

Mirabel watched her name go. "No," she said. "I've said plenty into the record already. Besides—" the flame climbed the second page, and the seal burst in a small pop of resin and light, and thirty people cheered as though a ship had come safely home— "besides, I'm keeping the ashes. Some things you don't throw away. You just change what they're for."

Tomás, overhearing, pointed at her with the brandy bottle. "See, that," he announced to the crowd, "is the bookkeeper's heart. Even her bonfire has an entry in double columns."

* * *

Afterward — after the singing, after Serafina had poured a libation of plum brandy directly onto the sand "for the lagoon's books," after the crowd had drifted home in twos and threes with the loose, unhurried contentment of people who had witnessed something complete — Mirabel scraped the cooled ash into a small tin that had once held throat lozenges, and carried it upstairs, and sat with her father while the town settled toward night.

The monsoon was well and truly over. Through the open shutter she could hear the harbor working the dusk: block and tackle, a bell buoy, someone's dog announcing the tide. In a few weeks the pearling season would open under new management — their management, the cooperative's, though the word *cooperative* still fit some mouths awkwardly, like shoes not yet broken in. There was so much to do. Rosters to make, prices to negotiate, a counting house with no roof yet and no name yet either, though Tomás had proposed six, all terrible.

But tonight there was only the room, and the lamp, and her father awake and propped comfortably against his pillows, and the ledger open on her knees.

It was the old company ledger — the first one, the one she had balanced at fifteen with a pencil stub and a headache, the one Corvane had let her keep when everything else went to the magistrate because it was, he'd said with the smile she had come to loathe, *obsolete*. She had been writing in it since the hearing. Not accounts. Something else. She wasn't sure yet what to call it.

"What are you putting in it tonight?" her father asked. He liked to ask. It had become their evening office, this accounting.

She dipped the pen. "Debts," she said. "Paid."

"Whose?"

"Ours. Everyone's. Listen." She found the page. Outside, the bell buoy swung and rang, patient as ever, keeping its own faithful count of the water. "To Papa Marchetti: for the year of storms endured in a bed by the window without once complaining loudly enough to be heard in the street, paid in full — though he'll dispute the figure. To Tomás: for carrying the journal east through the tail of the monsoon with company agents shooting at his wake, paid in full, plus interest, the interest being that we forgive him the next six boat names he proposes." Her father laughed, which cost him, and she paused until the coughing settled, and went on. "To Elias Thackeray: for vigilance, for paint, for standing between this family and every storm that asked, paid in full and gladly and forever, terms to be renegotiated upward annually."

"And to yourself?" her father said. "What does the bookkeeper owe the bookkeeper?"

She looked at the page a long time. The lamp fluttered in the sea-draught and steadied.

"To Mirabel Marchetti," she wrote as she spoke it, slowly, feeling out each word like a diver feeling along a reef in poor light, "for staying when leaving was offered, and for learning at last the difference between a debt and a gift: paid. Paid in staying."

She set down the pen. Her father reached over and rested his hand on the page — not reading it, he couldn't read her writing upside down and half by lamplight, just resting his hand on it the way you'd rest a hand on a shoulder.

"Your mother kept a book too," he said. "Recipes. Nothing in it but amounts and instructions, no names, no stories. I used to tell her she should write down who taught her what. She said the food would remember." He smiled at the ceiling. "I thought it was nonsense. Now I keep a journal full of depths and dates and I catch myself writing down who was on the boat, what the sky did, what somebody said. The numbers were never the account, Miri. They were only ever the excuse."

Below them, in the dark, the bell buoy rang. Somewhere out on the water a boat lamp moved, a single small light finding its way home by it.

"The counting house needs a name by Friday," Mirabel said. "Tomás has suggestions."

"God help us all. What's the worst one?"

"*The Pearl Exchange & Rum Parlor.*"

Her father considered this with great gravity. "That's the worst one?"

"The others involve puns."

"Then we must find a better name by Thursday," he said, and pulled the blanket up, and closed his eyes with the deep satisfaction of a man who had, at last, put his affairs in order — or nearly, for as she trimmed the lamp and gathered the ledger, he added, half-asleep already: "Ask the Serafina. Old divers know what a building wants to be called. And Miri — when the spring comes, and the young ones start coming round asking you to teach them the numbers..."

"Yes, Papa?"

"Teach them the stories too. The numbers are only the excuse."

She stood a long moment in the doorway with the lamp, watching him breathe his ragged, stubborn rhythm, and thought about spring — about what might be built in it, and who might come asking, and what, exactly, she would say on the first morning when a new face looked across a new counter at her and asked where the lessons should begin.
