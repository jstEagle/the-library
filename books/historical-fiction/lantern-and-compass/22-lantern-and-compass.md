## 22. Lantern and Compass

The girl came down the ladder from the counting house loft with a smudge of ink on her nose and the particular expression of someone who has just discovered that arithmetic can lie.

"The columns don't balance," she announced.

"Then either the business is failing," Mirabel said, without looking up from the net she was mending, "or you've made an error, or — and this is the rarest possibility — someone has stolen from us. Which would be exciting, but it's Tuesday, and theft generally waits until Friday."

Nena Marchetti, aged eleven, sat down on the step beside her grandmother with the dignity of a magistrate. "It's not an error. I checked twice."

"Then show me."

Nena spread the ledger on the salt-bleached boards between them. The counting house had been built four years ago on the foundations of the old drying shed, and its floor still remembered the smell of fish, which everyone agreed kept the accountants humble. Through the open shutters came the sound of the lagoon at morning work: hulls scraping, the dive bell's dull bronze tongue, someone's mother calling someone home for rice.

Mirabel ran her finger down the column. Nena watched, chin lifted, absolutely certain.

"There," Mirabel said, tapping a figure. "You carried the three from the tens place into the hundreds twice."

Nena stared at it. Her shoulders fell half an inch. Then, being her father's daughter and her great-grandfather's granddaughter both, she rallied. "The ledgers should be designed so that's harder to do."

"They should. When you run this place, design them so." Mirabel closed the book and handed it back. "But today, the error is yours, and tomorrow you'll never make it again, because it stung. That's how the numbers teach you. That's how everything teaches you."

Nena took the ledger, then hesitated, in the way she had of hesitating when she was actually gathering courage rather than losing it. She got that from Elias. Everyone said so. *She gets her stubbornness from you*, Elias would say, and Mirabel would say *her stubbornness is mine, her patience is yours, and her talent for getting mud in her hair belongs to no one but herself.*

"Grandmother. Can we go to the water? Before accounts?"

"It's always before accounts with you."

"That's why I'm asking now."

Mirabel laughed and folded the net. "Go get your suit. And take the bucket — if we're skipping work, we're pretending to gather shellfish."

* * *

The lagoon in spring was a sheet of hammered glass, green-gold where the light came through the shallows. Mirabel walked slowly these days; her knees had opinions about stairs and sand alike. But the water forgave everything. It always had.

Nena dove off the low rock like something thrown, all elbows and delight, and came up streaming. "I held my breath to forty!"

"You counted fast."

"I did not!"

"Your grandfather counted slow at your age. It's a family trait. We are a household of optimistic counters." Mirabel waded in to her waist and stood, letting the water do its old work on her joints, watching the girl's dark head bob out toward the beds. The pearl beds — *their* beds now, leased by the cooperative, worked by men and women who owned their own masks and their own mornings. Some days Mirabel still caught herself waiting for the company boat, the one with the painted eye at the bow, before remembering there would never be another. Twenty-two years, and the flinch had faded but not vanished, like a scar that aches before rain.

"Nena!" she called. "Show me your float!"

Nena flopped onto her back and floated, arms wide, face to the sky, drifting in the slow current with her eyes shut. Mirabel looked at her and saw, layered one atop another like tide lines on a piling: her father at that age, so the story went, already diving deeper than the grown men; herself at eleven, balancing other people's debts in a room that smelled of lamp oil and fear; and beneath all of it, faint as a watermark, the woman she might have become if the last ship of 1899 had sailed with her aboard. She could not see that woman clearly. That was the mercy of it. She had stopped trying to imagine her around the time the cooperative put her name on the door of the counting house — *M. THACKERAY, KEEPER OF ACCOUNTS* — and found that the name fit her hands.

Nena's father — Tomás's boy, Rafa, who had his father's grin and none of his debts — was out with the far boats today. Tomás himself was in the capital, ostensibly retired, actually running what he insisted on calling a shipping consultancy and what the harbor master called *that smuggler's retirement plan*. He wrote twice a season, always the same closing line: *Still owe you a berth. Still keeping it warm.* He'd owed her that berth for twenty-two years and would owe it forever, and they had long ago agreed this was better than repayment.

"Grandmother," Nena said, paddling back, breathless. "Tell me the story."

"Which story?"

"You know which one."

"The one about the oyster who wouldn't open?"

"*That* one." But she grinned, because she knew she was being managed, and permitted it, because she wanted the other story more than she wanted to win. She climbed onto the flat rock and wrapped her arms around her knees, and Mirabel hoisted herself up beside her, groaning theatrically, and settled in the sun.

* * *

"You know most of it," Mirabel said. "A greedy man named Corvane wanted to take the beds from the divers. A girl who kept his books found out. There was a letter—"

"Framed on the wall of the counting house."

"Cheating," Mirabel said mildly. "Yes. Framed on the wall. And a lighthouse keeper's son who watched the water when no one else did. And a smuggler who carried the truth through a storm. And a ship with one berth left."

"And the girl gave the berth away."

"She tried to give it to her father. Her father refused it, because he was a stubborn man who would rather die in his own town than live safe somewhere he hadn't earned. Then she meant to give it to herself, and couldn't. She stayed instead, and read the ledgers aloud in front of the whole harbor, and the magistrate heard every column, and the scheme died in its own arithmetic."

"And the berth just — sat empty?"

"For one night. Then the new year came, and the ships changed, and by spring there were berths enough for anyone who wanted one. That's the part people forget." Mirabel looked out at the beds, at the little sail of the far boats tilting against the sky. "Everyone thought the berth was the last chance in the world. It was only the last chance that week. Chances are like that. They feel like doors closing when mostly they're tides turning."

Nena considered this with the ferocious seriousness she brought to everything, including shellfish. "But you didn't know that. When you stayed. You gambled."

"Yes."

"And Papa says Grandpa Tomás gambles too, but he cheats."

"Papa says correctly."

"But it wasn't really a gamble, though." Nena picked at the barnacles on the rock, lining them up by size without noticing she was doing it — the bookkeeper's tic, third generation now. "Because you'd already done the hard thing. The letter, and the copying, and staying to testify. The berth was just the ending. You'd already decided who you were before the ship came."

Mirabel was quiet for a moment. The sun moved a finger's width across the water.

"Yes," she said at last. "That's exactly it, and I've been trying to say it for twenty years, and you've just said it at eleven. Your great-grandfather would have liked you enormously."

"Tell me about him. The real version. Not the coughing sad version."

Mirabel smiled. "The real version. All right." She shifted, settling her back against the warm stone. "He once dove on a bet to fetch a ring out of thirty feet of water, and when he came up he'd forgotten whose ring it was, and he refused to return it until someone proved ownership. He held court about it for a week. Called himself a judge of the deep. Your great-great-aunt had to describe the engraving before he'd hand it over, and even then he made her pay him a fish."

"What kind of fish?"

"A small one. He was a judge, not a bandit."

Nena laughed, the bright unguarded laugh she would lose for a few years in adolescence and find again, Mirabel hoped, in her thirties, the way everyone eventually did.

"He taught me to dive here," Mirabel said, nodding at the water. "Right off this rock, more or less. He said the lagoon would tell me its temper if I listened with my ears under the surface. And he taught me numbers, after — when his lungs were bad and he couldn't dive, we'd sit with the fishing tallies and he'd make games of them. He said numbers were honest the way water was honest. They hold whatever shape you pour them into, but they never pretend to be more than they are." She paused. "I think he was describing himself. He wasn't clever the way some men are clever. He was *true*. Everything else grew out of that."

"Is that why the letter's on the wall? Because it isn't true?"

Mirabel turned to look at her. Eleven years old, and cutting straight down to the keel.

"Why do you think it's there?" Mirabel asked, because she had learned — from ledgers, from diving, from her father — that the question behind the question was usually where the value lay.

Nena frowned at the middle distance. "Elias says it's so we don't forget. Aunt Odile says it's so visitors ask about it and you get to tell the story again. Papa says it's to annoy dead rich men." She glanced sideways. "I think it's a warning. Not about companies. About... deciding things about people in rooms where those people aren't. The letter decided your whole family's life and nobody asked any of you."

There was a silence, filled only by the small patient sounds of the lagoon.

"Tomás said almost the same thing to me once," Mirabel said quietly. "On a dock, angry, and he was right to be. Deciding other people's fates in secret — that was the company's whole method. Columns full of lives, entered without consent." She reached over and tucked a strand of wet hair behind Nena's ear. "That's why the cooperative's books are open books. Anyone may come read them. Any diver, any day. If we ever decide something about the beds, the decision happens in the Harbor House with the doors open. It's slower. It's much slower. Sometimes it takes weeks and everyone shouts."

"Do you shout?"

"I have been known to contribute."

Nena grinned, then sobered, working at something. Mirabel waited. She had learned that too — from the water, from the ledgers, from Elias, who could stand silent at a rail for an hour and then say the one true thing.

"When I'm big," Nena said, "I want to keep the books. Like you. But also dive. Can you do both?"

"I did. Badly balanced some years, well balanced others. The trick isn't doing both perfectly. The trick is choosing it — every year, choosing it again, instead of just falling into it. Falling into a life and choosing one look identical from outside. From inside, they're different worlds." Mirabel looked at her own hands, the ink stains long since faded, the diver's callus still there on the base of her thumb like a keepsake. "Nobody handed me my place, Nena. Not the company — they offered me a berth to abandon it. Not luck alone, though luck helped, luck always helps. I built it. Out of a stolen letter and a borrowed journal and a smuggler's promise and a lighthouse that burned down and got built back up. That's what inheritance actually is, in the end. Not what you're given. What somebody built, and what you decide to keep building."

Nena absorbed this, then said, with devastating practicality: "So when you die, I get the counting house."

"I am *not dying*, I have columns due Thursday—"

"*Eventually.*"

"Eventually, the counting house is yours if you choose it, yes. Along with the frame on the wall, and the debt of one warm berth, and the story, which you will tell badly at first and then better." Mirabel stood, joints registering formal complaints, and offered her hand. "Come on. Shellfish. If we return empty-bucketed, your grandfather will know we were swimming and gloat."

* * *

They walked home along the tideline in the long gold light, Nena ranging ahead and doubling back the way children do, as if the path were too good to pass in one direction only. The counting house windows glowed; someone had lit the lamps early. Above the door, the name board. On the inner wall, in a plain black frame, the letter itself — Halcyon Trading Company letterhead, the wax seal cracked like old ice, the words *the Marchetti family* sitting in their cold official sentence. Visitors asked about it. Children dared each other to touch it. Once a year, on the last night of December, Mirabel took it down and let it rest in a drawer overnight, and hung it back up on New Year's Day. She had never explained this to anyone. Some rituals were only for the person performing them.

Her father was on the porch of the little house above the beach, wrapped in two blankets though the evening was mild, watching the water with the unhurried attention of a man who had spent eighty-one years learning its moods and intended to spend several more. His lungs were quiet this spring. The doctors used the word *stable*, which he pronounced as though tasting it and finding it adequate. He had buried his wife, survived consumption, outlived an empire of pearl, and judged a ring dispute with total integrity; he regarded further drama as beneath him.

"Shellfish?" he asked as they came up the path.

"Two crabs and a moral victory," Nena reported, holding up the bucket.

"An acceptable haul." He peered into it, then at his granddaughter, then — longer — at Mirabel, with an expression she knew. It was the one he wore when the tide had brought in something he'd been expecting.

"Elias is up at the light," he said. "Said to tell you the new glass goes in tonight, and he'd like a witness who can count."

"Can't he count?"

"He counts fine," her father said gravely. "He likes *your* counting."

Nena made a noise of profound pre-adolescent disgust, kissed her great-grandfather on the top of his blanket-wrapped head, and clattered inside with the crabs.

Mirabel lingered on the path. The first star was out over the point, and the lighthouse above it stood white and mended against the dusk, its gallery gleaming where Elias had scraped and painted and scraped again, twenty-two years of maintenance on a tower the company had set afire and the town had refused to surrender. Tonight the new lantern glass would go in — the fourth pane in her memory — and the light would sweep the water all night the way it had swept it the winter a girl with stolen pages had needed, more than anything, to know that someone was watching.

"Go on," her father said, waving his blanket at her. "Witness the glass. The ledgers will keep."

"The ledgers always keep. That's their whole virtue."

"Mm." He settled back, eyes on the darkening lagoon, and added, softly, in the tone of a man giving away the last thing he owns because he knows it isn't really his to keep: "The berth you made yourself. How does it ride?"

Mirabel looked out at the water, at the beds below it, at the lit window of the counting house where her granddaughter was already, no doubt, rechecking a column just to watch it come true.

"Like it was built here," she said. "It rides like it was built here."

And she climbed the hill toward the light.
