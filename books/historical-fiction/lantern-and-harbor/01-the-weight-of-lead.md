## 1. The Weight of Lead

The lead knew her hands before the lamplight did.

Lior Ishikawa had come to that conclusion somewhere in her nineteenth winter, three years after the last one she'd spent watching her mother's fingers do this same work — the same quick arithmetic of thumb and forefinger, plucking letters from the California job case as if from a dictionary only printers could read. Lead held warmth differently than skin did. It gave it back slowly, grudgingly, the way stone gave back the sun hours after sunset. By the time a compositor had been at her tray for an hour, the metal was hers, and she was its, and neither would admit how much they needed the other.

She sat now in the mouth of the shop, the long composing stick balanced in her left hand, setting the evening's work: *Proclamation 447, Concerning the Registration of Foreign-Born Persons in the District of Vessany*, by order of the Provisional Directorate of Coastal Affairs. Fourteen hundred copies by morning. The words went into her fingers and out through them without passing anywhere near her heart, which was, she had learned, the only way to set government type and keep your supper down.

*All persons of non-native origin resident within said district shall present themselves to the Office of Census and Loyalty no later than the feast of Saint Anselm...*

Her thumb found the capital A with the ease of long marriage. Twenty-point Caslon, the government's preferred face — heavy, self-satisfied letterforms that looked like men who expected to be obeyed. Her father called it "the magistrate's voice." He said every typeface had a voice, and that a printer who couldn't hear the difference between a font that whispered and one that barked had no business standing at a stone.

Outside, the harbor was doing what the harbor always did at this hour: settling. Ropes creaking against mooring posts. The last lighters groaning toward the breakwater with their loads of grain and contraband and ordinary hope. Somewhere down the waterfront a drunk was singing a song about a girl from Maren with only half the words right. Vessany's nights had a sound like no other city Lior knew — the sound of a place holding its breath without knowing it.

The shop itself breathed around her. She could hear it if she listened: the tick of the iron frame of the Albion press cooling as the coal stove burned low, the whisper of paper stacked and re-stacked against damp, the small metallic conversations of ten thousand sorts settled in their compartments like a city asleep. The Ishikawa Printing House had stood on Tanner's Cut for sixty years, first as a bookbinder's, then as her grandfather's gamble, then as her father's life's slow argument with the world. It smelled of ink and oil and the particular sweetness of old paste, and Lior had long ago stopped being able to tell whether that smell meant home or merely meant everything.

From the back room came the sound of her father working.

Elias Ishikawa cut typefaces the way other men prayed — privately, at strange hours, with an intensity he never explained. Through the open door she could see him bent over his graver at the cutting bench, a jeweler's lamp throwing his shadow huge up the plaster wall, the steel punch taking shape under his hands stroke by patient stroke. He was cutting a new face tonight. He'd been cutting it for eleven days, and he hadn't told her what it was for, which was unusual; her father narrated his craft the way some men talked in their sleep, helplessly and constantly. This silence was new. She didn't like it.

*How is it possible,* she thought, watching him, *that a man can hold a chisel steady enough to cut a serif a tenth of a millimeter wide, and yet cannot hold a conversation about my mother for longer than four words.*

Three winters since Amara Ishikawa died. Three winters, and the shop still kept her habits — the good shears still hung on the left hook where she'd insisted they go, though Elias was right-handed and cursed them daily; the second teacup still lived rim-down on the shelf above the sink; the ledger from her last full year still lay open on the office desk, because closing it would mean admitting there would be no more entries in her hand. Grief in a print shop does not announce itself. It accumulates like dust in the typecases, settling into the compartments you use least, waiting.

Lior blew a strand of hair off her forehead and mis-set a word.

*Registration.* She picked it out letter by letter — she always picked it out whole, refusing to slide the line into the hellbox and pretend it hadn't existed — and reset it, and her anger cooled into the dull shape it took most evenings now. It wasn't even anger, really. It was something more tired than that. Setting proclamations she despised for a regime she served with her hands but not her heart, in a city where everyone she knew did exactly the same, and called it living.

Because that was the thing about Vessany that the capitals never understood, and that Lior understood perfectly: nothing happened here. Oh, ships happened, and tides, and smuggling, and the occasional dockside riot over fish prices that the garrison dispersed with water hoses and laughter. But history — real history, the kind that turned over governments and redrew maps — happened in capitals. Happened in plazas with names like Unity Square, among students and lawyers and men with excellent coats. Revolutions were made of marble and rhetoric. Harbors were made of fish scales and rope burns and the smell of tar, and harbors waited. Harbors always waited, and then, when whatever was going to happen had happened, the news arrived by coach like everyone else, three days late and already stale.

That was what she'd have said, anyway, if anyone had asked. Nobody asked. In Vessany, opinions about the Directorate were like the tide tables — consulted quietly, never posted publicly, and wrong within a fortnight regardless.

She reached the end of the paragraph and slid the composed line onto the galley with the others, wedging it true. Eleven lines done of forty. She allowed herself a stretch, spine cracking, and looked out the front window into the street.

And saw the notice.

It had not been there when she'd come in at dusk. She was certain — certain the way a printer is certain, the way someone is certain who knows every square centimeter of her own doorstep, every flaw in the glass, every rust stain on the iron boot-scraper shaped like a leaping fish. The notice was nailed to the doorframe at eye height with a bright new nail, and beneath it the gaslight from the corner lamp fell just so, illuminating it like a museum piece.

She was outside before she remembered the cold, still in her apron flecked with ink.

Cream stock, official watermark, the Directorate's seal stamped in violet ink that had smudged slightly at one edge — careless work, some junior clerk's stamp, applied in haste or contempt. The heading read:

*NOTICE OF INSPECTION.*

*By authority granted under Article Twelve of the Press Integrity Act, premises registered to Ishikawa, E., printer, are subject to examination by an officer of the Ministry of Information, Censorship Division, at a date to be determined. All plates, matrices, type, and printed matter shall be made available. Failure to comply constitutes an offense under—*

She stopped reading. Not because the rest was unfamiliar — she'd set notices like it herself, twice, for other shops along the Cut — but because the rest didn't matter. What mattered was the date-to-be-determined, which meant any day. What mattered was the name of the division, which everyone in the trade knew was a euphemism the way *harbor pilot* was sometimes a euphemism for informer. And what mattered, most particularly, was that in the eleven years Lior had worked in this shop, through two changes of government and one purge and the registration act of her mother's last autumn, no such notice had ever been nailed to this door.

The Cut had been quiet. The Cut stayed quiet by unspoken agreement — the shops along it printed wedding invitations and bills of lading and the occasional seditious joke set in six-point type and sold for a copper to men too drunk to remember where they'd bought it. Quiet shops did not get inspected. Quiet shops were part of the machinery of things going on.

She heard the door behind her, felt the cold draft of the shop exhaling past her into the street. Her father came out bareheaded, wiping his hands on the rag he always carried, folded into quarters, worn soft as cloth gets when a man worries it for thirty years.

Elias Ishikawa was not a tall man, and the harbor wind always seemed surprised by him, pushing at his clothes as if expecting someone larger. His hair had gone the color of pewter early, and his hands — his hands were the hands the whole Cut knew, wide-palmed, scarred at the heel of the palm where steel punches had slipped over the decades, precise as instruments even at rest. He stood beside his daughter and read the notice without touching it, the way you read a diagnosis.

"Hm," he said.

That was all. *Hm.* Thirty-nine years of living reduced, in crisis, to a single syllable, and Lior loved him so much in that moment and wanted to shake him so badly that she did neither, and said instead:

"When?"

"Date's undetermined." He tilted his head, reading it again from a different angle, as though the paper might confess something to a man who knew how type was spaced. "Fresh nail. Fresh paper. Nailed well, though — look at the angle. Whoever put it up has done it before."

"That's what you take from this? The carpentry?"

"That's what I take from this first." He pulled the rag from his pocket, unfolded it, refolded it along different creases. "The rest comes later."

"Father." She kept her voice level the way she kept her composing stick level — by attention, by effort, by refusing to let the tremor show. "Shops on Anchor Row got these notices in the spring. The Kellermans'. The widow Brast's bindery. You know what happened."

"I know what happened."

"The Kellermans lost their foreign accounts. Widow Brast lost everything, because they found a plate in her storeroom she swore she'd never seen, and swearing she'd never seen it was worse than having cut it herself." Lior turned to face him fully. In the gaslight her father's face was unreadable, which was itself a kind of answer; she had inherited her ability to compose a blank expression from him, along with her hands and her stubbornness and her habit of sleeping badly. "We are not the Kellermans. We're quieter than the Kellermans. So why us?"

For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he reached out and touched the notice — one finger, flat against the cream paper, the gesture of a man checking whether a wound is real.

"Because the times changed," he said. "Or because somebody wrote our name somewhere. Or because a clerk in the Censorship Division had a quota and drew names from a hat. There doesn't have to be a reason, Lior. That's what an inspection is. It isn't an accusation. It's a net dragged behind the ship to see what the sea gives up." He turned from the door. "Come inside. It's cold, and you've fourteen hundred copies to finish, and Proclamation 447 will not register the district by itself."

"That's it? You're not worried?"

"I'm going to check the storeroom," he said, which was not an answer, and which was the worst possible answer, and they both knew it, and he walked back into the shop with his shoulders squared in the way he squared them when he intended to be lied about.

* * *

Inside, the warmth closed over her again and she resented it — resented how easily the shop wrapped her back up, the stove's breath, the familiar dark of the press, the lead giving back its heat into her palms as she took up the stick once more. Routine was a mercy. Routine was also a trap, and she could feel both truths at once tonight, sitting down to the stone while her father disappeared into the storeroom where the old plates slept in their paper wrappings, labeled in her mother's handwriting, some of them older than Lior herself.

She set type and listened.

*All persons of non-native origin...* — the small sounds of her father moving among the racks. — *shall present themselves...* — a drawer opening on runners that needed waxing. — *to the Office of Census and Loyalty...* — a long silence, longer than inventory required. — *no later than the feast of Saint Anselm.*

She stopped. Looked through the doorway.

Her father stood at the far wall of the storeroom with his lamp raised, and he was looking at something on a high shelf — looking at it the way men look at graves, she thought, or at old letters they should have burned — and then he took down nothing at all, straightened, adjusted the lamp, and turned back to the inventory ledger with his face arranged into ordinary business, and began counting bolts of paper aloud in a voice pitched carefully to carry.

"...four reams of the double-crown wove, sound, five reams of the folio, one spoiled at the water damage..."

Lior set the next line. Her hands did not shake, because she had trained them not to, and she thought: *revolutions happen in capitals. Nothing happens here. Nothing has ever happened here.*

And then she thought of the fresh nail, angled by a practiced hand, and of her father counting paper to a room where no one listened, and of the shelf he had looked at without touching, and for the first time in her nineteen years the harbor at night did not sound to her like a city settling.

It sounded like a city waiting.

* * *

She finished at half past two. Fourteen hundred proclamations, dried, stacked, tied in bundles of fifty for the ministry courier who would collect them at eight and pay them in scrip worth less each month. She washed the stone, killed the lamps one by one until only the jeweler's light remained burning over her father's bench, where he had returned to the unfinished punch as though the storeroom had never opened its mouth.

Up close she could see the face he was cutting, upside down and mirrored in the steel, and it was beautiful — a display face, ornamental, nothing like the government's heavy Caslon. Slender stems, fine hairlines, terminals flaring like the wake of a boat. She had never seen it before, and yet something about it tugged at her memory the way a name does when it belongs to a stranger.

"It's lovely," she said. "Whose commission?"

Her graver paused mid-stroke. Just for a heartbeat. Then it moved again, shaving a curl of steel thin as onion skin.

"A friend's," Elias said. "Old commission. I'm finishing what I owed."

"You don't owe unfinished commissions after twenty years, Father."

"This one I do." He set the punch down at last and looked up at her, and in the small fierce circle of the jeweler's lamp his eyes were very tired and very awake. "Go to bed, Lior. Lock the door behind you. And tomorrow—" he hesitated, and she watched him choose, visibly watched the sentence change direction in his mouth like a ship coming about, "—tomorrow we'll clean the shop. Properly. Top to bottom. Every drawer, every shelf, every wrapping."

"Why?"

"Because," said her father, picking the graver back up, "a net drags behind every ship, my love. The trick is deciding what the sea finds when it does."

She stood a moment longer. The lamp hissed. Outside, very faint, the tide was turning against the pilings, and somewhere below Tanner's Cut, down where the streets gave out into shingle and rope-walks, a dog began to bark and would not stop.

She went up the narrow stair to the room above the shop where she had slept her whole life, and locked the door as he asked, though the lock had never once in her memory been turned, and lay in the dark listening to the harbor breathe.

She was nearly asleep when she heard it: the front step creaking under a weight that was not her father's — lighter, quicker, twice — and then the softest knock at the street door. Two taps. Hesitant. Like a question asked by someone who hoped the answer would be no.

Downstairs, the jeweler's lamp clicked off.

In the dark, Lior Ishikawa held her breath, and the harbor held its, and nobody in Vessany answered the door.
