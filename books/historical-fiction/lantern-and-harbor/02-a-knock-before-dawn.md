## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The knock came at the hour when even the harbor stops believing in itself.

Lior heard it through the floorboards, three soft raps and then a fourth, weaker, like an apology. She had been awake, though she would not afterward be able to say why — perhaps the tide turning against its usual rhythm, or the gulls quarreling over something dead on the pier, or simply the particular quality of silence that precedes a change in a household's fortune. She lay in her narrow bed beneath the sloped ceiling of the room above the shop and listened to the sound repeat itself: knock, knock, knock, knock-that-trailed-off.

Below her, in the print shop that was also their kitchen and her father's entire kingdom, Elias Ishikawa did not stir. Her father slept the way he set type — deliberately, with his back to the world, his breathing measured as if even unconsciousness were a craft to be practiced properly. He had gone to bed at nine with a candle stub and a page of proof corrections for the Ministry's grain requisition notices, and he would rise at four to begin again, and between those hours nothing short of fire moved him.

The knock came a fifth time.

Lior pulled her coat over her nightdress and went down the stairs she knew by feel, thirteen steps, each one creaking its particular confession. The shop smelled of lead and lamp oil and the ghost of last night's ink, which never fully left the air no matter how Elias scrubbed the stones. Through the window over the composing stone she could see the street below their door, wet-slicked with the mist that came off the water every night without fail, lamplit at the corner where old Bette still kept her watch-lamp burning for husbands who would never come home from the sea.

She put her hand on the latch and stood a moment in the particular paralysis of a person raised to be invisible.

*Do not open doors after curfew.* This was written into the same proclamations she set each week in neat columns of government type. *Citizens discovering disturbances are directed to report them to the district officer and refrain from personal involvement.* She had composed that sentence herself, letter by letter, the *r* dropping into place with a small click, the *f* nested against it, and she had thought, as she always thought when she set the regime's language into metal: *how easily words become cages when someone else holds the tray.*

Another knock. Weaker still. Almost done.

And beneath it — a sound she had not heard before, or had not let herself hear. A thin, furious mewling, the sound of something very small demanding the world attend to it.

Lior opened the door.

The fog came in first, cold and tasting of brine and tar. Then the light from Bette's corner lamp fell across their doorstep and showed her what sat there: one of the flat wooden crates the fishing boats used for herring, its slats dark with old scales, its lid propped at an angle as though whoever placed it had been interrupted. Inside, on a nest of sacking that smelled of fish and salt and something sweeter underneath, a bundle.

The bundle was screaming.

Not crying — Lior would learn the difference later, would learn the whole vocabulary of an infant's grievances — but screaming, red-faced and furious, arms punching at the air as though the child had been trying for some time to fight her way out of the situation and had been defeated only by her own size. A scrap of wool was pinned around her with what looked like a fishhook worked free of its shank and bent flat. And tucked beneath her chin, folded twice, was a single printed page.

Lior gathered the crate inside with both hands — it weighed less than a basket of laundry, how could something so loud weigh so little — and kicked the door shut behind her, and stood in the dark of the shop with her heart going like the press at full run.

"Hush," she said, uselessly. "Hush, you'll wake—"

The word *father* died in her throat, because she heard him on the stairs.

Elias descended the way he ascended, deliberately, candle in hand, and stopped on the third step from the bottom, and looked at the scene before him with the expression of a man reading a page that has been set entirely wrong. His eyes went from the crate to the bundle to the printed paper, now half-unfurled from the baby's flailing, and Lior watched — she would remember watching — the color leave her father's face the way ink leaves a plate when the solvent goes on. Not all at once. In stages. Like a thing being carefully, thoroughly erased.

"Light the lamp," he said. His voice was level. That was the frightening part; she understood later that it was the levelness of a man standing on ice and listening for the crack. "Lock the door first."

She locked the door. She lit the lamp on the composing stone. The baby screamed on, indifferent to all of it, and Elias came down the last three stairs and knelt beside the crate, and for a long moment he did not touch anything but the paper.

He unfolded it with two fingers, the way he handled proofs fresh from the press, and read it upside down before he turned it around, because twenty years of this trade will teach a man to read any text in any orientation, and Lior saw his thumb tremble — actually tremble, this man whose hands were steady enough to cut a hairline serif by lamplight — before he mastered them.

The page was a leaflet. Or had been meant to be; it was creased and damp-spotted and one corner had been torn away, but the impression was crisp enough, printed from a good plate by someone who had known what they were doing. The header read, in a bold condensed face: **THE HARBOR DOES NOT BELONG TO THE MINISTRY.** Below it, columns of argument Lior's eyes skimmed too fast to hold — calls to the dockworkers, to the pilots, to the fishermen, language about tides and solidarity and the armistice that was already being whispered about in taverns — and then, at the foot, a printer's device she did not recognize: a lantern hanging above a wave, rendered in fine engraved line.

Except her father recognized it. She knew it from the way he held the page at arm's length, the way a man holds a snake, or a love letter he has no right to.

"Elias?" she said. She almost never called him by name. "Papa. Whose is this?"

"Where did you find it?"

"It was wrapped around—" She gestured at the crate. "Under her chin. Like a collar. There was more under the sacking."

There was. When Elias lifted the fold of burlap with the same two careful fingers, they found a second paper, smaller, folded to the size of a coin, and inside it, in pencil, in handwriting that shook or hurried or both, five words:

*Forget the face. Please.*

Beneath the papers, the child. Six months old, perhaps; fat fists, black hair plastered to a skull still soft at the crown, eyes screwed shut against the outrage of existing. When Lior reached in and lifted her — she did not decide to do it; her hands decided, and she ratified the decision afterward — the screaming stopped as abruptly as a press released. The baby considered Lior with an expression of profound, offended scrutiny, hiccuped twice, and grabbed a fistful of her nightdress with the grip of a drowning sailor.

"Well," said Elias.

"Well," said Lior.

The lamp guttered. Outside, somewhere down the lane, boots went past — patrol boots, the heavy-soled kind, two men walking with the unhurried confidence of people whose word was backed by the state — and the three of them, the setter of type, the novelist's daughter who was only a compositor's daughter really, and the abandoned child of God knew whom, stood absolutely silent in a print shop full of evidence until the boots faded toward the breakwater.

Elias took the leaflet to the stove and fed it into the coals. Lior watched the header burn — *THE HARBOR DOES NOT BELONG* — the letters browning, curling, going black at their edges before the flames took the sense out of them entirely. He burned the note too, after a moment's hesitation she pretended not to see, and stirred the ash with the poker until there was nothing left but grey.

"Papa. You know that mark. The lantern and the wave."

"I knew a man once," her father said, "who used it." He did not look at her. He looked at the stove, at the ash, at his own hands, which had gone still in the way they went still when he was cutting type, total attention, total withdrawal. "Go back to bed, Lior."

"And do what with—"

"She'll sleep in the crate tonight. It's warm by the stove. In the morning we will decide what sensible people decide."

"There's nothing sensible in this house," Lior said, and surprised herself with it, and surprised him too; his head came up sharply, and in the lamplight she saw something move across his face that was not anger but its cousin, the recognition of a thing said truly. The baby, swaddled now in Lior's coat, had closed her eyes. Her breath came in small tidal snuffles against Lior's collarbone.

"Morning," Elias agreed quietly, and climbed back up the stairs, and his candle went out behind the door of his room, and did not relight, which meant he did not sleep either.

*

Morning arrived grey and guilty, the way mornings did in Vessany in the season of the mist.

By law, foundlings were the province of the District Officer of Welfare, which meant, in practice, the province of whoever in the Ministry building could be bothered to walk down Harbor Lane before noon. Elias was explicit about it over breakfast — bread, dripping, tea brewed thrice — while the child slept in the drawer of the type cabinet lined with sacking, an arrangement he had objected to and then improved by planing a splinter off the lip of the drawer without admitting he was doing it.

"We take her to the Officer at nine," he said. "We say we found her, we know nothing, we saw no one. The streets are full of refugees from the eastern counties since the spring purges. She is one of hundreds. No one will look hard at one more."

"You burned the leaflet," Lior said.

"I burn everything the censor objects to. That is my trade."

"That's your survival. Your trade is letters." She heard her own voice and did not recognize its edge. "You knew that mark, Papa. You held it like it bit you. Who printed it? Who left her?"

Her father buttered his bread with terrible precision. "A man I testified against," he said at last, "twenty-three years ago. Or rather, a man I testified *about*, saying I did not know him, which was true in the way the Ministry likes truth: technically, at the hour of questioning, I claimed never to have met him, and the claim was believed because I made it convincing." He set down the knife. "He is dead now, I am told. Or dispersed. In Vessany the two are synonyms."

The clock in the corner ticked. The child in the drawer sighed in her sleep, a small wet sound, and both of them glanced at the cabinet and then away from each other, embarrassed by the glance.

At twenty past nine, the knock came again — official this time, three raps with authority behind them, and a voice calling, "Ishikawa. Open, please."

Officer Brant of the District Welfare Office was a large, damp man who sweated even in the mist and who carried his authority the way a porter carries furniture, painfully and with visible relief whenever he could set it down. He had inspected the shop twice a year for a decade and had never once found anything worth reporting, largely because Elias kept nothing worth finding, and Brant's ambition extended exactly as far as his pension.

"Foundling," Brant said, wiping his boots with unnecessary thoroughness. "Reported by Bette Marn, the lamplighter, says she heard an infant in the night, says she saw you take a crate in." He produced a ledger and licked his pencil. "Description of circumstances?"

Lior heard herself speak before her father could.

"There were no circumstances," she said. "She was asleep when we found her. We heard nothing in the night." *The first lie,* said some quiet clerk in the back of her mind, making a record of it, setting it in type: *the first lie Lior Ishikawa tells an official.* It slid out of her mouth with horrifying ease, smooth as a well-cut sort dropping into its place. "My father believes her people may have come in on the night boats from the east. The refugee landings. There was nothing with her — no note, no token."

Brant wrote: *nothing with her.* His pencil squeaked. Behind him, in the drawer, the child stirred and made a pre-scream grumbling noise, and the whole shop seemed to contract around the sound like a held breath.

Elias crossed the floor, opened the type cabinet drawer with the blandness of a man retrieving a quoin, and lifted the baby out, and Brant looked at the infant and then at Elias and then at his ledger, and Lior watched the arithmetic happen behind his damp face: *foundlings are forms, forms are work, work is trouble, trouble is for younger men.*

"The eastern boats," Brant said. "Likely, likely. They've been running them in past the buoy line all month. Half these children come from nowhere." He wrote another line, and another, and Lior understood that the regime's greatest mercy was its indifference — that the machinery which could crush a man for a lantern-and-wave device stamped in a corner of paper could not be bothered to follow one drawer-borne infant through the correct channels. "Foundling ward takes her today. You'll sign here. And here. Ishikawa, your signature — you're registered as a licensed compositor, Grade Two, correct?"

"Grade Two," Elias agreed, and signed, and his hand did not tremble at all, and Lior hated him a little for how well he lied, and loved him a great deal for doing it badly enough that she could see the seams.

Brant tucked his ledger away, paused at the door, and added, almost as an afterthought, the thing that turned the morning from a survived danger into a suspended one:

"Oh — and you'll have seen the notice. New inspection schedule. Censor's office is sending someone down from the capital this month. Not old routine, this one. They say he actually *reads* what you print." Brant chuckled at the absurdity. "Imagine. Well. Good day to you both."

The door closed. The footsteps faded. Father and daughter stood in the middle of the shop, between the composing stone and the press, with the foundling-signature drying on the welfare form and the child — unreported to any ward, unclaimed by any state — blinking up at them from Elias's arms.

"He thinks we gave her up," Lior said.

"Brant thinks whatever requires the least writing." Elias handed the baby over with the deliberation of a man surrendering a live coal. "The form is filed. As far as the Ministry is concerned, the Ishikawa foundling left this house at nine-forty this morning." He looked at her a long moment, and she looked back, and between them passed the whole unsaid architecture of what they had just built: a child who officially did not exist here, a leaflet officially burned, a name officially never known.

"What do we feed her?" Lior asked.

Elias sighed the sigh of a man who has just seen the size of a job ahead of him — a whole new font, unmixed, no matrix, every letter of it dangerous. "Milk," he said. "And God help us, we'll need to find some. Go ask Ferro at the salt warehouse. He asks no questions and answers none either, which makes him the only honest man on this street."

Lior wrapped the child against the mist and stepped out onto Harbor Lane. At the corner, Bette's watch-lamp still burned pale in the daylight, and Bette herself gave Lior a long, unreadable look from her doorway — the look of a woman deciding whether she had witnessed charity or conspiracy, and choosing, for the moment, to witness nothing.

In her arms, the baby yawned, enormous, unbothered, having slept through the forging of her own false history.

"Nell," Lior said to her, quietly, out loud, testing the word like a new sort in the hand. It was against every rule her father had laid down and she knew it — *do not name it, do not hold it close, names are hooks and hooks catch* — but the syllable fit the child somehow, round and stubborn as her fists. "Nell."

Down the lane, the mist was lifting off the water, and somewhere beyond the breakwater, out past the buoy line where the night boats ran, the sea kept all its secrets, including the face she had been instructed to forget — and had already, helplessly, begun to memorize.
