## 3. What the Censor Reads

The typecase had a false back, and Elias Ishikawa had built it so well that he sometimes forgot it was there.

He stood in the workroom after midnight with the lamp turned low, one hand flat against the cedar panel behind the drawers of twelve-point Caslon, feeling for the seam only his thumbnail could find. The child slept upstairs in the drawer they had lined with his dead wife's shawls. Lior had gone to bed an hour ago, or had gone upstairs at least; he heard her now and then through the floorboards, turning, turning, the way a body turns when it is pretending to sleep. She had lied to the harbor official that morning. He had heard it through the window — her voice level, almost bored, telling the man in the grey coat that no, nothing had been left at their door, that shops like theirs received deliveries of paper and lead and nothing else, and the official writing it down without even asking what she meant, because who suspects a tired girl in an ink-stained apron?

She had lied well. That was the part that frightened him.

Elias found the seam. The panel swung inward on hinges he had oiled every month for twenty years, and there they were: three copper plates wrapped in oilcloth, each one engraved edge to edge with Tomas Verel's words in Elias's own hand. He did not take them out. He never took them out anymore. He only looked, the way a man looks at a scar in the bath, checking that it still hurts the way he remembers.

It did.

* * *

Twenty years ago the harbor had smelled the same — tar, brine, the sweet rot under the fish market — but Vessany had been younger then, or Elias had, which amounts to the same thing when you are twenty-four and your hands are steadier than your convictions. He had been the finest engraver on the coast. Everyone said so. His mother said it first, then his masters, then the guild examiner who ran his thumb over a proof sheet and murmured *look how the burin sings*, and finally a broad-shouldered man with ink permanently blackening the crescents under his nails, who appeared at the shop one rain-washed evening and said, "I'm told you can cut letters small enough to hide in a margin."

"I can," Elias said, because it was true.

"Good," said Tomas Verel. "Because I need some hidden."

They worked at night, those first months, in the back room of a chandler's storeroom where the tallow candles guttered and the rats ran bold along the keel-timbers stacked against the wall. Tomas would bring pages of prose — dense, furious, luminous prose, the kind that made Elias read a paragraph twice before he could bear to destroy the rhythm by cutting around it — and Elias would engrave the plates that let ten copies become ten thousand. He learned the whole liturgy of the underground press: how to warm copper against a stove pipe so the wax took evenly, how to lay acid so the fine hairlines of a lowercase *e* didn't drown, how to print with muffled rollers and wrap the wet sheets around legitimate stock so a customs search would find nothing but almanacs. He learned that Tomas laughed easily and slept badly. He learned that the essays carried a name — *The Lantern* — because Tomas believed a port city could not be darkened, only blinded, and blindness was temporary.

"Regimes don't fear guns," Tomas told him once, watching Elias clear acid from a line of type with the tip of a brush held like a surgeon's instrument. "Guns are honest. They announce themselves. What they fear is *repetition*. One leaflet is a madman's grievance. Ten thousand are a fact."

Elias had wanted, more than anything, to be part of a fact.

He thought about that sentence now, standing over the open panel with the cold breathing out of the wall at him. Twenty years was long enough that memory stopped being a film and became sediment: layers of it, compressed, some strata poisoned. He remembered the work with love. That was the obscenity of it. He could still feel the joy of those nights, the purity of being needed by something larger than himself, and laid directly over that stratum, pressed down until the two images fused, was the smell of the Ministry of Information's waiting room — floor wax and lilies, absurdly, lilies, as though grief were a flower one could arrange.

* * *

They had come for Tomas in the spring of the last purge. Not for Elias — not yet. The regime worked outward from the center like a stain through cloth, and for eleven days after the arrest Elias printed nothing, engraved nothing, burned what could be burned and buried what couldn't in the yard beneath the lemon tree that never fruited. On the twelfth day two men from the Bureau came to the shop. They were polite in the way of men who have never needed to raise their voices at property. The taller one set a single sheet on Elias's composing stone.

A page of *The Lantern*. Set in Elias's letterforms. Anyone in the trade would have seen it in a heartbeat — the distinctive hooked descender on the *g*, the way the crossbar of the capital T sat a half-hair high, proud, unmistakable. It was a signature as legible as any name.

"You are the best engraver in Vessany," the tall man said. It was not a compliment. It was arithmetic.

Elias looked at the page for a long time. He was aware of his own heartbeat, of the dust motes climbing through the light from the window, of the terrible clarity that arrives in some men at the moment of their cowardice — the moment when all possible futures become visible at once, laid out like proofs drying on a rack, and you reach for the one that lets you keep breathing.

"They'll want the name of the engraver eventually," the man said. "Names are currency here. You may spend yours, or you may spend his."

There was more, afterward. There was always more — the forms, the oath, the quiet arrangement whereby a promising young craftsman's shop license would remain unexamined, his imports of copper unwarranted, his future secure, in exchange for a single testimony given calmly, on record, before a tribunal that had already decided everything except the shape of its paperwork.

Elias gave it. He stood in the hearing room with its lilies on the table — someone had placed lilies there, someone with a sense of ceremony he would never forgive — and he said, in a voice he barely recognized as his own, that he had never known Tomas Verel. Never met him. Never engraved for him. That the similarity of letterforms was coincidence, a common style of the coastal schools. He watched the words leave his mouth like sheets leaving the press and could not recall a single one of them.

Tomas Verel was executed that autumn. They did not use the word *executed*. The official record said he died of a fever in custody, and in a sense this was true: the fever of the state, caught in a cell, treated with nothing.

The silence Elias bought with his lie was expensive and it kept its terms. No one came again. The regime, having extracted its confession, lost interest in him the way a leech loses interest once fed. And Elias went on engraving — government proclamations, customs seals, the portraits of ministers whose policies had killed his friend — and he told himself, in the first years, that he would make it right, that he would find Tomas's people, that the plates in the wall would someday be used for their purpose. Then Lior was born, and her mother was alive, and there were meals to earn and a child to keep unremarkable, and *someday* became a room in the house of a man's mind where he no longer went, though he paid its taxes monthly in the coin of shame.

Then her mother died. Three winters ago. The purge after that one was smaller, quieter, hardly worth the name; a hemorrhage, the physicians called it, though Elias had always privately believed that a woman can bleed out from more causes than the body lists. She had known about the plates. She had never once asked him why they stayed in the wall instead of the sea. In nineteen years of marriage she had asked him exactly one question about them, on the night after the funeral of a mutual friend taken in the second purge, and the question was: "Do they still print clearly?"

He had tested one that very week. They printed perfectly. He told her so, and she nodded, and turned back to darning, and that was the entire conversation, and he had loved her more than he knew how to say for not making him say anything else.

* * *

Now there was a child asleep above his head who weighed less than one of his copper plates.

He climbed the stairs quietly, out of habit, though the fourth step announced him anyway, groaning its wooden note, and crossed to the drawer where Nell — Lior had named her already, of course she had, against every advice he'd given and against his own better judgment, which was dissolving hourly — lay sleeping with one fist curled near her mouth. Beside the drawer, smoothed flat on the dresser where Lior had left it, was the wrapping she'd come in: a single printed page, torn with evident haste from a larger sheet, the paper cheap and the impression uneven, pulled in some cellar on a press run too fast.

Elias had known the plate on sight. Of course he had. A man does not forget his own handwriting.

It was the third plate. His third, cut in the year before the arrest, the one with the hooked descenders and the proud crossbars, the one whose margins he had filled — at Tomas's delighted insistence — with the smallest text he ever cut, a running commentary invisible unless you knew where to look. He had not needed to look now. Twenty years had passed and his eye went straight to the lower left corner the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

Someone was printing from his plates. Someone had access to Tomas Verel's press, or its heirs, and that someone trusted the Ishikawa door enough — or despaired enough — to leave a granddaughter on its step with four words in a careful hand: *forget the face.*

He understood the instruction completely. It was not cruelty. It was mercy of the coldest, most practical kind: whoever wrote it was saying *do not look for us, do not claim connection, do not let this child carry our history into your household, because history is the thing that kills.* Forget the face and the child lives. Remember it and you have joined us, and we are dying.

Elias stood over the drawer a long time, listening to the small wet sounds of an infant dreaming.

The trouble was that forgetting was a craft he had practiced for twenty years and mastered at nothing. Guilt behaved exactly like ink. This was not metaphor; it was his professional observation, the one area of expertise the Bureau had accidentally granted him. Ink never fully leaves the fibers it has bitten into. You can wash a sheet, bleach it, expose it to sun until the paper yellows around a ghost of the mark, but hold it at an angle to the light and there it is — the image latent, waiting for someone who knows how to look. Every censor knows this. Every censor builds a career on the certainty that nothing truly prints clean, that somewhere under the whitewash the old words wait at an angle to the light.

His testimony waited at an angle to the light. It waited whenever he cut a government portrait and felt his burin move in the old sure strokes. It waited whenever Lior asked, casually, terribly, what her father had done before proclamations, and he said *engraving, mostly engraving*, which was true the way a mask is a face.

And now it waited in a fish crate that still smelled of mackerel, wrapped in a page pulled from a plate he had sworn before a tribunal he had never touched.

He bent close to the child. She had Tomas's jaw — he saw it suddenly, the heavy hinge of it, the same jaw that had tilted back laughing in a candle-lit storeroom while rats ran the keel-timbers. Or he imagined he saw it, which after twenty years amounted to the same thing. Perhaps the daughter. Mara, the daughter's name had been; Tomas spoke of her the way other men spoke of scripture. If Mara lived, if Mara had carried the press forward into this new purge, then the child was Verel blood, and the debt Elias owed that blood had a face now, and hands, and a cry that could split the night.

"Forget the face," he whispered to her. Her fingers closed around his own, grip astonishing, absolute, the grip of someone who has decided to hold on before understanding what holding costs.

"No," he told her. "I've tried that already. I'm not any good at it."

* * *

He went back down and closed the panel and stood a moment with his palm flat against the cedar, feeling the plates' faint cool presence the way you feel weather through a wall.

Tomorrow, or soon, the ministry would hear rumors — a baby appearing in a printer's household travels through a port city faster than any leaflet. Inspections would follow. And inspections meant censors, and censors meant men who read paper the way Elias read proofs: at an angle, hunting the ghost image, the latent mark, the thing that did not quite wash out. He had spent twenty years ensuring his own sheet showed nothing. Now there was a child in his house with another man's jaw and a wrapper printed in his own dead friend's letterforms, and every angle of the light had changed.

He banked the lamp, sat at his stone, and did what he always did when his hands needed to think: he set type. Slowly, silently, letters that spelled nothing, an alphabet walking itself in circles while the master slept.

Above his head, through the floorboards, he heard Lior's bed stop creaking. Heard her rise. Heard her bare feet cross to the drawer and stop there, and stay, a long time, in the particular silence of a person making a decision.

He did not go up. But he found, to his surprise, that he was no longer afraid of what she would choose — only of how quickly the world would come to test it.
