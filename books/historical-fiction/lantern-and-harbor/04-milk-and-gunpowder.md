## 4. Milk and Gunpowder

The baby woke before the lamps did, and after three nights of it, Lior understood that infants keep their own hours the way tides do — indifferent to the schedules of printers, censors, and governments alike.

She had named her on the second night. It happened almost against her will, the way a sneeze happens, or a confession after too much wine. Elias had said, for the fourth or fifth time, *we cannot grow attached, Lior; we must think of her as cargo, as a problem to be moved along*, and Lior, rocking the small warm weight against her shoulder in the blue hour before dawn, had heard herself say: "Her name is Nell."

Elias set down his coffee cup with the deliberate care of a man trying not to shatter something. "Names are hooks," he said. "You hook a thing to yourself, you feel it when it's torn away."

"Then I'll feel it," Lior said. "But she isn't a parcel. She has a face. The note said forget the face — well, I've looked at it. It's too late for forgetting."

Her father looked at her for a long moment across the kitchen table, lamplight pooling in the hollows of his eyes, and whatever he saw there made him turn away and busy himself with the stove, feeding it kindling as though the fire were a conversation he could win.

So Nell she was. The name settled over the household like weather.

* * *

The problem of milk solved itself, or rather, the harbor solved it, because Vessany was a city where everything could be obtained by someone who asked sideways enough. On the fourth morning there came three knocks at the back door — two quick, one slow — and Lior opened it to find Dario Ferro standing in the alley mist with a covered pail in one hand and an expression of profound suspicion on his weathered face.

He was perhaps thirty, built like the rope he handled, all knots and utility, with salt-cracked hands and eyes the gray-green of the harbor in February. He kept a boat called the *Petrel* two slips down from the fish market and was known — though never officially, since official knowledge would have ruined him — as the man who could move anything across the water: contraband tobacco, unlicensed Bibles, letters between separated families, once, famously, an entire piano.

"I hear," he said without preamble, "that old Ishikawa's house has sprung a leak. Small one. About this big." He held his hands apart at the length of a loaf of bread.

Lior's stomach performed a slow revolution. "I don't know what you mean."

"Course you don't." He handed her the pail. Goat's milk, still warm, a cloth tied over the top. "That's from Marta Ovid's goat, which does not exist, and which you are not receiving, because there is no baby here. I'll come again tomorrow. Same nonexistent arrangement."

"Why?" It came out blunter than she intended. "Why help us?"

Dario looked past her into the dim interior of the shop, at the great black bulk of the press, and his face did something complicated. "My mother had a printing shop in Terrano," he said. "Before the Ministry decided what words were allowed to weigh. They took everything except her hands, and her hands forgot how to do anything else, so she forgot other things instead, toward the end." He shrugged, as if he'd said too much already, which he had. "Goats need milking anyway. Waste not."

He turned to go, then paused. "One more thing, printer's daughter. The harbor closes in six weeks. Maybe less, if the armistice men in the capital find their pens quicker than expected. After that, nothing leaves Vessany without ministry paper, and ministry paper doesn't grow on trees — it grows on presses like your father's, which is exactly why they'll be watching them. Six weeks. Whatever needs doing, do it inside that window."

"Doing? We're not doing anything."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Nobody ever is. That's why nobody gets caught."

* * *

The days found their shape around Nell the way a river finds its course around stones. Lior rose before light to feed her — goat's milk warmed with sugar, per Marta Ovid's instructions relayed through Dario, tested against the wrist — then set her in the old laundry basket beside the composing stone while Lior worked, so that the morning's government proclamations were set to the rhythm of small sounds: the snuffle, the sigh, the occasional outraged cry that meant a pin had come loose or the world had been insufficiently interesting.

It should have made the work harder. It made it bearable instead. There is only so long a person can set type reading *BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL MINISTRY OF HARBOR AFFAIRS, ALL CITIZENS SHALL...* before the letters begin to swim and curdle, before each word feels like swallowing a small stone of obedience. Nell was an antidote. When Lior set the word SHALL she could look over and see a fist waving from the basket, indignant at its own powerlessness, and think: *not you. Whatever else happens, not you.*

Elias changed too, though he'd deny it under oath. He grumbled about the noise, the smell, the disruption of a workshop forty years in the ordering. But Lior caught him more than once at odd hours, standing over the basket in the gray light with an expression she hadn't seen on him since her mother died — that look of a man auditing his life and finding discrepancies. And one evening she came downstairs to find him carving, from a scrap of boxwood left over from a decorative border, a small toy boat. He hid it behind his back with the guilt of a boy caught poaching and said, "It's for ballast," which fooled no one, least of all him.

The danger of it all grew quietly, the way damp grows inside a wall. Lior understood this intellectually. Emotionally, she kept discovering it anew, in ambushes:

The census clerk who came door to door midweek, tallying households for the grain ration rolls, and who paused at the sound of crying upstairs and asked, "Children in the home?" — and Lior heard herself answer, "Cats. In heat. The whole alley's wailing," and the clerk, a bored woman with chilblains, wrote *no dependents* and moved on. Lior closed the door and discovered her heart was somewhere near her throat and refused to descend.

The afternoon a Ministry truck idled outside the shop for two full hours, and Lior paced with Nell in the back room, whispering nonsense syllables — *hush now, hush, little anchor, little cargo* — while the diesel stink crept under the door, until the truck finally coughed and left, having been, as far as anyone could tell, parked there for no reason at all, which was worse than a reason.

And the night Dario came for his pail and stayed leaning against the doorframe, watching Lior attempt to soothe Nell through a bout of colic, walking the length of the shop with the baby screaming against her shoulder, and finally said, "Here, give her over. My sister had twins; I have hands."

"You have smuggler's hands."

"Smuggler's hands are the steady ones. Nothing shakes them anymore." He took Nell with absurd gentleness and began a slow walk, swaying, murmuring something that turned out to be a sea shanty stripped of every word a censor would object to, which left approximately nine words, repeated in a low drone. And Nell — traitor, opportunist, pragmatist — went silent mid-shriek and stared up at him with her enormous dark eyes, and fell asleep against his collarbone in four minutes flat.

Lior sat down on the composing stone's stool and felt something crack open in her chest, warm and terrifying. Dario stood in the middle of her father's shop, in the light of the work lamp, holding a nameless illegal infant, humming a censored song, and he looked — right. He looked like he belonged to the room, to the basket, to the whole improbable household they were building out of secrets.

"You should put her down," Lior said, when she trusted her voice.

"In a minute." He looked at the sleeping child, then at Lior, and his expression lost its practiced ease. "Six weeks, Lior. I meant what I said. And I'll tell you something else I probably shouldn't. There are people — my people, harbor people — making lists. Who goes out on the last boats. It's arithmetic. Tonnage and heads. Every crate of this, every body of that." He shifted Nell carefully. "A baby weighs almost nothing. Costs almost nothing. If it comes to it, there's room in a hold for her, even when there's room for nobody else. You understand me?"

She understood him perfectly, and hated the understanding. He was offering, gently, in the language of freight, to save the child and let the rest of them drown in place. "And if it comes to it," she said, "you'll take her and I won't know where, and I won't be able to follow."

"That's how saving works," Dario said, not unkindly. "It doesn't usually let you watch."

After he left, Lior stood a long time at the front window, looking down the crooked throat of Lantern Street toward the harbor, where the masts rocked against the last gray light like a forest of thin needles stitching ship to sky. Six weeks. Somewhere across the water, men in good coats were deciding where the new borders would run and who would be forgiven and who would be processed, and their decisions would arrive in Vessany as paper — proclamations, lists, amnesty terms printed on cheap stock — and every sheet of it would pass through shops like hers.

Through her hands.

Nell slept in the basket by the press, one fist curled near her cheek, and the lamp hissed, and Lior thought about ink. About how it got under the skin and stayed, how her father's knuckles carried shadows in the creases that twenty years of washing had never fully lifted. Some things soak in and become part of the grain. She was beginning to suspect that a person could love like that too — invisibly, permanently, against all advice.

* * *

On the ninth day, the danger stopped being abstract.

It came in the form of Marta Ovid's actual granddaughter, a girl of eleven named Sela, who pounded on the back door at dusk, breathless, and delivered her message in the flat voice children use when repeating words they don't fully understand but sense are heavy: "Grandmother says don't send milk tomorrow. Or the next day. She says the Ferro boat was boarded at the customs pontoon and they went through everything with knives, even the flour, and there was a new officer asking about the print shops. Grandmother says—" Sela screwed up her face, retrieving the exact words, because Grandmother had insisted on exact words. "She says: *the inspector is coming back around, and goats get quiet.*"

Lior thanked her, gave her a copper, watched her run off down the alley, and stood in the doorway until the cold had climbed through her shawl and into the marrow of her.

Inside, Elias was already lighting the lamp over the composing stone, preparing the morning's work. Nell gurgled in her basket, utterly unaware that the world had teeth. And Lior opened her mouth to tell her father everything — the boarded boat, the new officer, the countdown underneath their lives — when her eye caught the corner of the typecase where, beneath a loose board, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the page that had come with the baby. The forbidden page. Her father's early engraving, proof of an old sin, folded like a knife.

*The inspector is coming back around,* Grandmother had said. Not *an* inspector. *The* inspector.

"Dad," Lior said quietly, and her father turned, and seeing her face, set down his composing stick. "We need to talk about twenty years ago. All of it. Before someone else talks first."

Outside, down on the water, a customs bell rang the hour — six weeks minus five days — and the fog came up Lantern Street like a tide with orders, putting out the lamps one by one.
