## 5. The Inspector's Eye

Inspector Ambrose Corvane believed that a man could be known by his letters, and he had built a career — an entire quiet, unlovely life — on the corollary: that a city could be read the same way.

Vessany, in his long acquaintance with her, was a document set in haste. Her alleys were kerning errors; her harbor was a marginal gloss, scrawled by smugglers and tide-traders in a hand the ministries pretended not to see. The new regime had tried to reset the whole page twice in Corvane's memory, first with the purges and then with the proclamations, and both times the type had fought back. Cities, like fonts, remembered their cutters. You could melt down every punch and matrix, and still some handbill would surface years later with the ghost of an old serif in its *R*, and Corvane would know it the way a mother knows footsteps on stairs.

He knew this made him strange. His colleagues at the Ministry of Information collected informants; Corvane collected specimens. In the drawer of his desk, beneath the official seals and the lists of the disloyal, lay a portfolio of typographic ephemera gathered across two decades — tavern bills, playbills, funeral invitations, a wine label from a house that no longer existed, printed in Vessany or brought to Vessany by ships whose captains never asked why a gray civil servant wanted to buy paper trash. Other men kept photographs of wives. He kept a broadside from the year of the Bread Riots because of the exquisite hang of its italic ampersand, and he had never told anyone so, not even under questioning, though there had been questions once, early, about whether a man so devoted to printing might not be devoted to printers. He had laughed then, genuinely, and shown them his ledgers, and they had let him alone. A censor who loves type is the safest kind. He loves the letters more than what they say.

This was almost true. It was true enough to survive scrutiny, which in Vessany was the only truth that mattered.

The inspection notice had been nailed to the Ishikawa print shop door for nine days before Corvane came to honor it. Nine days was deliberate. The Ministry's schedule was a fiction maintained for the benefit of anyone who might wish to destroy evidence, and evidence-destroyers needed time the way seedlings needed rain. What was hidden would be hidden well by now, pressed into walls or sunk in cisterns or folded into the bindings of innocent books, and what remained would be exactly the sort of remainder a careful man would keep. Corvane did not inspect shops to find contraband. Contraband was for constables. He inspected shops to find the men behind them, and a man stripped of his secrets showed more of himself than one still dressed.

He went on foot, as was his habit, down from the Ministry quarter through the fish market, where the morning's catch lay silvering on crushed ice and the gulls conducted their eternal litigation overhead. He wore his good coat, the charcoal one with the ministry buttons polished but not too polished — a man whose buttons gleamed was a man trying to be seen, and a man whose buttons were dull had stopped caring, and either extremity invited contempt. Somewhere between, in the honest middle, was authority. He carried nothing but a leather folio and his walking stick, unadorned, and the small brass loupe he kept in his waistcoat pocket the way other men kept watches.

The Ishikawa shop sat where the Salt Quarter began to remember it had once been marsh, a narrow-fronted building leaning companionably against its neighbors, its upper windows reflecting the harbor in warped panes. The sign above the door said ISHIKAWA & DAUGHTER, LETTERPRESS AND ENGRAVING, in lettering Corvane had always found quietly superior — cut, he judged, twenty years ago or more, before the standardization edicts, when a sign-cutter could still permit himself an opinion. The notice he'd sent was nailed beside it, and someone had driven the nails in very neatly, which he noted with approval. Even compliance could have pride of craft.

He knocked with the head of his stick. Three measured raps.

After a moment, the door opened on a chain, then fully, and a young woman stood in the gap with composing stick still in her hand as though he had interrupted her mid-sentence.

She was perhaps five-and-twenty, dark-haired, ink-stained at the cuticle in the way of people who work with their hands and have stopped apologizing for it, and she looked at him with an expression Corvane had learned to read across a thousand doorways: the flicker behind the eyes where fear goes to put on its coat. She was good at this, better than most. But she was good the way a well-set page is good — you could admire the evenness of the line and still see, if you looked, the places where the justification had been forced.

"Inspector Corvane," he said, and offered his papers. "Ministry of Information. I believe we sent word."

"You sent word." Her voice was level. "My father's expecting you. Come in, Inspector. Mind the ink."

*Mind the ink.* No one had ever said that to him before, and something in him — some unfed thing that had waited decades in the drawer with the wine label and the riot broadside — leaned forward like a dog hearing its name.

* * *

The interior of the shop struck him the way cathedrals struck other men.

It was dim, lamplit even at midmorning, the windows having been long ago half-shuttered against salt glare, and the air was the air Corvane loved best in the world: oil and solvent and hot metal, the smell of lead freshly cast, underlain by the papery sweetness of size and the faint mineral breath of damp stone. Two presses stood against the far wall, iron-framed, oiled, older models kept in condition that bordered on devotion. Typecases lined the left wall in ranks, their hundred small drawers labeled in a hand that had matured across the labels' faces — the earliest ones rounded and uncertain, the recent ones spare and exact. Twenty years of one family's life, filed alphabetically.

And everywhere, everywhere, paper. Reams stacked in the manner of the trade, proofs pinned along a string, a galley resting on the stone bench with its forme locked up and waiting. Corvane crossed to it without meaning to, the way a man crosses a room toward a window.

"May I?"

"It's your proclamation," the daughter said. "You may as well see how it looks."

It was the monthly loyalty decree, the one every licensed shop in Vessany set and reset and set again, and it was, Corvane saw at once, beautifully done. The measure was clean, the spacing generous without being lax, the widow on the last line corrected by a judicious loosening of the third paragraph rather than the lazy stretch of wordspacing lesser compositors used. He bent close. The type itself was the ministry's mandated face, the dull standardized roman decreed eight years ago, identical in every legal shop in the province — and yet it looked different here. Better. The way a competent singer can make a common song sound written for her voice.

"You've adjusted the leading," he said.

"A point and a half," she said. "The mandate doesn't forbid it. It forbids changing the face. It says nothing about the air around it."

"The air around it," Corvane repeated softly. He straightened. "Yes. That is precisely what the draftsmen at the Ministry have never understood. They legislate the letter and ignore everything that lets it breathe."

Something passed over the young woman's face — surprise, quickly mastered, and below the surprise something wry, as if she had expected to dislike him and was annoyed to be delayed in it. "You set type yourself, Inspector?"

"Once. Badly." He said it lightly, and it was light; there was no confession in it. "Apprenticed two years in my uncle's jobbing house in Carrow, before the civil service found me. I could never cast off copy without cheating. My master said I lacked the patience to serve other men's words." He turned a proof toward the lamp. "I've regretted it my whole life, in the way one regrets all honorable failures."

Behind him he heard the father come down the stairs.

Elias Ishikawa moved the way old craftsmen move, weight low, hands slightly open at his sides as though perpetually about to pick something up. He was past sixty, gray at the temples, with the deep quiet of a man who had spent his life being the last person consulted and the first person obeyed. He bowed to the inspector's papers with correct formality and not a hair's breadth more.

"Inspector. The kettle's on, if you take tea."

"I take tea," Corvane said, "the way the Ministry takes loyalty. Regularly and without much discrimination." He smiled to show it was a joke, and Ishikawa returned a smile that showed it had been received and filed.

* * *

They gave him tea in the little room behind the presses, where the household lived folded into the trade: a table scrubbed pale, two chairs, a shelf of dishes, and through the inner wall, when the wind shifted, the breathing of the sea in the foundations. Corvane had inspected one hundred and forty shops in his career and drunk tea in perhaps sixty of them, and he could rank them all in his sleep by what their hospitality concealed. The Ishikawas offered him the good cups. That interested him. Fear hid things; courtesy decorated them. This household was decorating.

He worked methodically, folio open, making his notes in the small upright hand the Ministry required, asking after licenses and output registers and the disposition of waste metal, and the answers came smooth as planed wood. Elias Ishikawa was neither nervous nor expansive. He answered what was asked and offered nothing, and his daughter — Lior, the register said, Lior Ishikawa, compositor, licensed — poured tea with the unhurried economy of someone feeding a stove, and watched her father watch the inspector, and Corvane noted that too: a family that communicated in glances was a family with something to communicate.

Then, because he had earned it with forty minutes of tedium, he asked to see the typecases.

"Standard inventory," Ishikawa said. "You'll find the faces all current."

"I don't doubt it." Corvane drew the brass loupe from his waistcoat pocket. "Indulge me, Master Ishikawa. It's the only part of the inspection I enjoy, and I am old enough to insist on my enjoyments."

The daughter glanced at her father — a fraction of a second, a hinge turning — and Corvane saw it and pretended, professionally, not to.

The cases were indeed current. Roman, italic, the mandated titling face, all within tolerance, all bearing the foundry marks of the state works. Corvane ran his fingers along the type beds with the tenderness of a physician, lifted a few sorts to the lamp, murmured his approvals. And then, at the fourth case from the stair — the case set lowest, nearest the floor, in the shadow of the workbench — he stopped.

The label read MISCELLANEOUS ORNAMENTS, and inside were ornaments: fleurons, rules, a border of oak leaves worn nearly blind with use. And among them, mixed carelessly, artlessly, a handful of text sorts in a face that was none of the mandated faces at all.

Corvane held one to the loupe.

The world narrowed to a circle of brass and glass, and inside it bloomed a lowercase *e* with a slanted bar and a spur like a bird's foot, and Corvane felt the floor of his certainties shift a half-inch, the way a ship's deck shifts when the tide turns beneath it.

He knew that *e*. He knew the whole face, though he had seen it perhaps four times in his life and never in any catalogue. It was a private cutting, a display face from the old days, pre-standardization, with an italic that leaned like a woman listening at a door. Twenty years ago it had run through the underground press like a signature through a forged will — on the pamphlets, on the seditious sheets, on the famous broadside that had ended Tomas Verel's career and very nearly his life. Corvane had been a junior examiner then, cataloguing seized materials, and he had spent three weeks with that face, learning its habits the way one learns a stranger's habits across a prison visiting-room: the distinctive *g* with its flattened bowl, the *y* whose tail curled back on itself like a hand withdrawing from a handshake. The examiners had called it Verel's face, though Verel hadn't cut it. Someone else had. That question — *who cut it* — had been the only interesting one in the entire case file, and it had never been answered, and Corvane had carried the unanswered thing in his professional heart for twenty years the way a collector carries a gap in a series.

And here was a battered sort of it, buried in an ornament case in a licensed shop, disguised among fleurons by a craftsman clever enough to hide a tree in a forest.

Or — he made himself consider it — careless enough to keep a relic. Old types circulated. Foundries closed; their stock scattered into jobbing shops like coins into beggars' cups. A dozen shops in Vessany might hold a stray sort of Verel's face and never know what they held. Innocence was possible. Innocence was statistically probable.

But the Ishikawas were not a statistical family. The Ishikawas were the family of the engraver who had signed the plates for Verel's early legitimate work — that was in the record too, in the trial transcripts Corvane had read more times than any transcript deserved — and had testified, tearfully enough to be believed, that he had never known what the plates were for.

Corvane set the sort back in its compartment with two fingers, gently, aligning it as he had found it. His pulse, he noticed with detached interest, had quickened like a young man's.

"That's an odd face," he said mildly, to the room at large. "Old, isn't it? Pre-standardization. I haven't seen its like in years."

Silence, one heartbeat long. Then Ishikawa, from across the room, in the tone of a man discussing weather: "Scrap stock. Comes in with the wastepaper sometimes. We melt the lead for slugs; the good sorts go in the ornament case till sorting day."

"Sensible." Corvane selected a fleuron at random, held it up, admired it. "A pity to melt any of it. This border work — is it yours, Master Ishikawa?"

"My father's."

"Ah." He let the syllable sit. "There was fine engraving in this city once. Before the edicts simplified us all." He closed the case drawer with the soft click of a book shutting. "Well. Everything appears in order."

He finished the inspection properly, initialed the register, drank the last of his tea. At the door, pulling on his gloves, he paused.

"Your proclamation," he said to the daughter. "The one on the galley. The best-set government work I've seen in this province, and I've seen all of it. Whoever taught you to think about the air around letters understood something most compositors die without learning." He inclined his head. "Keep the shop exactly as it is, Master Ishikawa. Order loves a well-kept page."

"Inspector," said Ishikawa.

"Miss Ishikawa." Corvane settled his hat. "Thank you for the warning about the ink. In twenty years, you're the first person to offer it."

He felt her eyes on his back all the way down the street, steady as a leveling rod, and he smiled privately into his collar. Charmed — yes, he admitted it; the girl was quick and grave and unafraid in a city where everyone was afraid, and some dusty chamber of him had enjoyed the hour enormously. That changed nothing. Charm was a property of the observed, not the observer.

Unconvinced — that also changed nothing and explained everything. A stray sort proves nothing; a hidden seam proves nothing; twenty years of coincidence prove nothing, legally, and Corvane was a creature of the legal. But he had not risen by believing in coincidence, and he did not begin today.

At the corner he stopped, took out his notebook, and wrote, in the small upright hand: *Ishikawa. Ornament case, 4th from stair. Face matches Verel specimen 12–19. Scrap-stock explanation plausible. Return unannounced.*

He looked up once at the shop's upper windows, where the harbor light lay warped in old glass, and wondered — idly, professionally, the way he wondered about all beautiful unfinished questions — who had cut Verel's face, and whether the answer had been living all these years above a print shop in the Salt Quarter, setting the Ministry's proclamations a point and a half looser than the law required, letting the letters breathe.

Three streets away, at the mouth of Fish-Gut Alley, a boy was already running toward the Ministry annex with a rumor: that the leaflets appearing in night markets all over Vessany — the ones everyone denied printing and everyone had read — had been set in a face that belonged to a dead man.

Corvane had not yet heard it. But he would hear it by evening, and by midnight he would be sitting under his lamp with the seized-specimen portfolio open before him, comparing a twenty-year-old *g* against a fresh-printed one, and finding, with something like grief and something like joy, that he could not tell them apart.
