## 6. Letters to No One

The plate lived in the false bottom of the coal scuttle for eleven days, and for eleven days it burned through the floorboards of my sleep. I would wake with the shape of it against my palm — the raised lines of a harbor scene, boats rendered in strokes fine as eyelashes, and beneath them the words Tomas Verel had commissioned from my father when I was six years old: *A port is a promise made to strangers.* Twenty years later, the regime had shot him for those nine words, and here they sat in our kitchen, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling faintly of copper and old ink, waiting.

Father would not touch it. I watched him not touch it the way you watch a man not look at a bottle — the elaborate care of his glances sliding past the scuttle, the way he took his tea standing, as if sitting near it might count as complicity. He had testified once already that he never knew Tomas Verel. He intended, I understood, to go on testifying with his whole body, every day, until the war or the armistice or the censor's office forgot us both.

I was not so disciplined.

On the twelfth night I took the plate out while he slept. The house held its breath around me. Our shop clock — a government-calibrated thing that struck the hours as if filing a report — said three, which meant the patrol on Printer's Row would have passed ten minutes ago and would not circle back until four. I knew this because I had been counting patrols since I was twelve years old, the way other girls counted suitors. Invisibility is its own education.

The press stood in the back room like a sleeping animal, iron flanks still warm from the day's proclamations. That morning I had set two hundred copies of an edict raising the flour tax *for the duration*, the letters neat and obedient under my hands, each word a small betrayal I committed without thinking, the way you breathe. Now I cleaned the rollers myself, working tallow into the leather until it moved soft as a tongue, and I locked the plate into the chase, and I pulled the first proof.

It came off the bed imperfectly — one boat's sail too heavy with ink, the paper dented where my fingers had trembled against the tympan. But the words came up dark and certain. *A port is a promise made to strangers.*

I stood in the lamplight holding my father's twenty-year-old sin in my two hands, and I felt nothing at all like fear.

* * *

You should understand what printing does to a person who has done it her whole life. Setting type is obedience made physical. Every letter must be picked up individually, placed face-out in the composing stick, packed tight enough that a thousand impressions will not shake it loose. There is no room in it for opinion. The type says what it says because someone above you decided what it would say, and your craft lies only in making the saying beautiful — even spacing, even margins, even color of ink across the page. My mother used to call a well-set page *quiet*. She meant it as praise. I had spent twenty-two years learning to be quiet in exactly that way: legible, orderly, impossible to argue with, impossible to hear.

But pulling a proof of words the ministry had forbidden — that was a different act altogether. It was the same press. It was the same motion of arm and lever, the same kiss of platen to paper. And yet the page came up loud. Loud as shouting. I printed twelve copies that night before terror caught up with me, and then I printed thirteen more out of spite for the terror, and then I washed the plate and hid it again and lay awake until dawn listening to Father's snoring and thinking: *this is what I am, then. This is what was always under the quiet.*

* * *

Dario Ferro found out because Dario Ferro finds everything out; it is his profession, or close enough to one. He brought milk twice a week now, official business for Nell's stomach, unofficial business for his own curiosity, and on the third Thursday of the leaflets' existence he set the jug down on our counter and produced from inside his coat a copy of my own work, folded to a crease gone soft from handling.

"You've got a kerning problem," he said.

I kept my face still. "I don't know what you're holding."

"Course you don't." He unfolded it on the counter between us, smoothing the page flat with two fingers, and pointed. "*Promise made to strangers* — see how the *m* crowds the *a*? And there." His nail moved down the line. "The *r* and *s* are kissing. It reads rushed. Like whoever set it was frightened."

"Maybe she was."

"Maybe she should eat something before her next shift." He looked up at me then, and his eyes were doing the thing I both resented and waited for — that quick brown assessment that treated me as a puzzle whose solution he intended to enjoy. "Twelve points of space between those letters, minimum. You're a professional. Act like one."

"The professional," I said carefully, "would ask how a milk smuggler learned typography."

"The professional would ask why the daughter of the best engraver on Printer's Row sets leaflets at night with a roller that's wearing unevenly on the left side, which means she's pulling them on the small proofing press in the back room instead of the big one, which means she's hiding it from—" He stopped himself at the edge of Father's name, courteous as a man stepping back from a ledge. "From somebody."

The shop ticked around us. Out front, gulls argued over something dead on the quay; the harbor smelled of tar and low tide and the particular metallic boredom of a city under martial law. I realized I was smiling. I realized I had been smiling for some seconds.

"If I told you," I said, "that you'd imagined that kerning error—"

"I'd know you were lying, because nobody who pulls proofs that clean makes mistakes on purpose." He tapped the page. "Except maybe now. Maybe a deliberate flaw. Something identifiable. So people know which press is telling the truth."

That stopped me. It had not occurred to me that the leaflets might want a signature, that truth might need a maker's mark like any other ware. I thought of the little boat in the plate's corner, Tomas's harbor, and something turned over in my chest.

"Get out of my shop, Ferro," I said.

"Gladly. Milk's paid for." At the door he paused, silhouetted against the notice nailed there — *BY ORDER OF THE MINISTRY OF PUBLIC CLARITY* — and added, without turning around, "There are walls on Salt Lane that could use some reading material. The ones facing the fish market. High traffic, low light, and the constables there can't read anyway, which helps everyone."

Then he was gone, whistling something the ministry would have called seditious if it could have caught it.

* * *

We fell into it the way rivers fall toward the sea — inevitably, and with a great deal of noise eventually.

He came on milk nights and stayed after, ostensibly to discuss distribution, actually to sit on a crate beside my proofing press while I worked, arguing. This became the texture of those weeks: lamplight, the smell of tallow and wet paper, Nell asleep in the drawer we'd lined with blankets — she slept in a typecase drawer, which Dario declared the most Vessany thing he'd ever seen — and Dario's voice running underneath everything like current under ice.

He was wrong about kerning, it turned out, or at least imprecise. He had learned his letters aboard a smuggler's brig from a Lithuanian mate who set the ship's false papers, and his whole typographic philosophy was built on deception: *make it look official, make it look boring, make it look like nothing worth reading twice.* Mine was built on beauty. We fought about it constantly and with enormous pleasure.

"You're wasting ink," he said one night, watching me pull a proof. "Nobody needs that kind of evenness on a wall broadside. They read it at a run."

"People deserve beautiful things," I said. "Especially the ones being lied to. Beauty is proof there's something besides survival. That's the entire argument, Dario. The regime prints ugly. Look at their edicts — cramped leading, cheap stock, ink you could strip a table with. Ugliness is policy. So we print gorgeously, and anyone who sees it knows, without being told, that another world is possible because here it is, right there, on the wall, in two colors."

"Two colors?" He leaned over the press. "You're doing two pulls?"

"Black for the text. Red for the boat." I said it lightly, but my heart was going hard, because the red boat was the thing I had added — my own addition, my own small blasphemy carved into the margin of a dead man's plate. In Tomas's original the harbor held five gray vessels. In mine, one of them sailed red, bright as a wound, bright as a lantern. Anyone who saw it saw a decoration. Anyone who saw two of them on two different walls saw a pattern, and patterns are how the sleeping wake up.

Dario looked at the red boat for a long moment. When he spoke again his voice had lost its argumentative lilt.

"Ferro," he said. "My route runs salt, tobacco, and occasionally people. Leaflets are heavier than all three, morally speaking. You understand that? Salt doesn't get a family shot against a warehouse wall."

"I understand it."

"And you're going to keep doing it whether I help or not."

"Yes."

He sighed the sigh of a man accepting a burden he had walked toward willingly, and picked up the fresh bundle of fifty, counting them with a smuggler's hands — fast, accurate, tender. "Salt Lane tonight. The fish market wall, and the stairwell behind the chandlery. Paste's in the jug under the milk, don't tell your father." He paused. "Actually, definitely don't tell your father."

* * *

We went out at half past two, and Vessany at half past two was a city holding its breath underwater. Fog off the harbor had swallowed the streetlamps to dim coins; our footsteps came back to us off shuttered fronts as though someone else walked a few paces behind, copying us. Dario carried the paste and the papers inside his coat, against his chest, and he joked about it — *if they shoot me, aim for the broadsides, they'll stop the bullet first* — but his jokes had gone quiet and quick, and I understood we had crossed out of the country of banter into the country where things were real.

The fish market wall rose out of the fog like the side of a ship. Dario brushed paste in long strokes while I held the lantern low, and when he pressed the first leaflet flat and smoothed it with the heel of his hand — *smoothing it the way you'd smooth a sheet on a child's bed,* I thought, absurdly, thinking of Nell — the red boat came up out of the darkness and hung there, luminous, ridiculous, brave.

"A port is a promise made to strangers," Dario read aloud, barely whispering. Then: "Huh."

"What?"

"It's better than I remembered." He stepped back. Paste dripped off the brush onto the cobbles and neither of us moved to wipe it. "The words. I've seen this one before, you know. Months ago. Before your version. Somebody else was printing it — cruder work, single pull, no red boat. I figured it was old stock from the war's first year." He turned to look at me, and the fog had gotten into his hair, beading it silver. "It wasn't old stock, was it. It was your father testing whether the plate still pulled clean."

I hadn't known that. I stood there recalculating my father — the man who wouldn't touch the scuttle, who testified with his whole body — and finding that somewhere in some earlier midnight he had stood exactly here, or somewhere like here, with paste on his hands. Testing. The way I was testing. Whether we were our fears or something older than them.

"He'll stop if he finds out it's me," I said.

"He won't find out." Dario took the brush back. "Men like your father are very good at not finding out. It's practically a trade." He said it gently, but it landed where it landed.

We did the stairwell last. By then the fog was thinning toward that gray hour that isn't night or morning, and the town began making its small pre-dawn sounds — a cart, a dog, water moving under the quay. On the way back Dario walked closer than the street strictly required, shoulders almost touching, and at the mouth of Printer's Row he caught my sleeve.

"Lior." First time he'd used my name without the *Ishikawa* attached, and it sounded different unarmored — softer, and heavier. "One more distribution note. There's a woman asking after these. Down by the cooperage. Dark hair, scar through one eyebrow, asks sailors if they've seen the red boat. Not angry-asking. Hopeful-asking."

The fog seemed to move between us.

"Anyone could hope," I said. "Hope isn't rare these days."

"No," he agreed. "But she described the boat before she'd seen the leaflet. Said her father used to draw it that way, red, in the margins of everything." He let go of my sleeve. "Thought you'd want to know before the constables want to know. Goodnight, Lior Ishikawa."

I went up the outside stairs to the room above the shop and did not sleep. Below me the presses breathed their iron breathing; in the scuttle the plate lay hidden; in the city, on walls and in stairwells, fifty red boats waited for daylight and readers. Somewhere a woman with a scar through her eyebrow was asking after them, and I lay in the dark trying to decide whether that was the most wonderful thing I had ever heard or the end of everything, and finding — with a thrill like the platen coming down — that I could not tell the difference.
