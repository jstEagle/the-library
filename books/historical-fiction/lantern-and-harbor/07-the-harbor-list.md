## 7. The Harbor List

The letter came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to the letter itself but everything to Elias Ishikawa, because Tuesdays were the days he sharpened his gravers, and a man ought to be holding steady tools when the world hands him something that cuts.

It was delivered not by post but by a clerk from the Harbor Commission — a boy of perhaps nineteen with a satchel and a soldier's cap two sizes too large, who stood in the doorway of the print shop as though standing in doorways were a profession he had not yet learned to love. Behind him the morning fog was lifting off Cannery Row in rags, and the gulls were working the fish waste heaps with their usual industry, screaming their small opinions at the sky.

"Elias Ishikawa." The boy read it as a statement, not a question, because there was no one else it could be. A man does not run a print shop for thirty years and remain anonymous to the harbor's paperwork.

"That's me."

"Registered allocation." The boy held out the envelope — heavy stock, cream-colored, the regime's seal pressed into wax the color of old blood. "You'll want to read it privately, sir. That's the recommendation on the slip."

*Privately.* The word did its work before the envelope even changed hands. In Vessany that autumn, *privately* had become a word with teeth. It was used about taxes, about loyalty interviews, about the lists that everyone knew existed and no one had seen. Elias took the envelope and felt its weight — one sheet, maybe two — and thought absurdly of lead type, how a single letter weighs almost nothing, and how a sentence of them can sink a man.

"Thank you," he said, because in Vessany you thanked the weather, the soldiers, and the fog, in that order of sincerity.

The boy touched the brim of his oversized cap and moved on down the row, and Elias watched him go, watched the fog close behind him like water over a dropped coin, and then he stood in his own doorway for a long moment with the letter held away from his body the way one holds a dead bird — carefully, and without quite believing it is real.

Behind him, inside the shop, Lior was singing.

She sang only when she thought she was alone, and she was almost always wrong about being alone; the shop's acoustics betrayed her daily. She was setting type by sound as much as sight — he could hear the thin click of brass against brass, the practiced shuffle of the case drawers, her voice running through some dockside ballad about a lighthouse keeper's daughter with the words worn smooth where she'd forgotten them. Three winters since her mother died, and Lior had begun filling silences the way her mother used to, unconsciously, generously, as if silence were simply a room left unheated.

Elias turned the envelope over in his hands. Through the paper he could feel the embossing of the seal, a raised circle like a blind man's idea of the sun.

He did not open it in the doorway. He did not open it upstairs, either, where Nell was sleeping in the drawer-cradle they had lined with felt offcuts, her breath making the small sound of a bellows in a very distant room. He carried it instead to the back of the shop, to the press room, where the smell of ink and oil and solvent was thick enough to be a kind of company, and where the great iron proofing press sat under its canvas shroud like furniture in a house where no one has died yet but everyone expects to.

He broke the wax with his thumbnail.

---

*By order of the Harbor Commission of Vessany, acting under the Emergency Port Act and in coordination with the Ministry of Information—*

The language was the regime's finest product: dense, polished, load-bearing. Elias had spent his life reading type, and he could read past words the way a jeweler reads past gold to the flaw underneath. The flaw here was in the fourth line.

*...is hereby allocated one (1) berth aboard the vessel* Meridian, *designated Final Evacuation Priority One, sailing upon conclusion of armistice proceedings or such date as the Commission shall fix, whichever shall first occur.*

One berth.

He read it four times. The number did not grow. He held the page up to the window, as if the light might reveal a second berth hidden in the margins the way a watermark hides in paper, waiting for someone to tilt the world correctly. There was nothing. There was his name, his trade — *Master Engraver, plate work, classification Essential Cultural Trade* — and there was the number, sitting in its parentheses like a stone in a shoe.

One.

The irony arrived late, as irony often does in good men, and it arrived wearing its best clothes: the regime, which had spent twenty years trying to erase what he could do, now classified that same skill as essential. His hands, which had cut the plates for Tomas Verel's forbidden pamphlets and then sworn before a tribunal that they had never done any such thing — those hands were worth exactly one seat off a sinking harbor. Loyalty plus trade equaled escape. The arithmetic of the state was very clean. It was everything else that was smeared.

Because there was Lior. And there was Nell.

Elias set the letter down on the stone slab where he mixed ink and put both hands flat on either side of it, the way a man steadies himself against a table when the ground has quietly tilted. Through the wall he could hear Lior still singing, half a verse, then the clatter of the composing stick, then her voice again — and threaded beneath all of it, faint as a pulse under a wrist, the small wet breathing of the child upstairs who was not his granddaughter and whom he loved with a ferocity that frightened him more than any tribunal ever had.

Twenty years ago he had made a choice at a tribunal table, and he had told himself every story a man tells himself: that Tomas Verel would have been taken anyway; that silence kept the shop alive; that a living printer with a guilty conscience prints more good in the world than a dead martyr with clean hands. Some of those stories were even true. But guilt is ink, he had learned. It is not on your hands. It is in them. It gets into the whorls and stays there, and no amount of turpentine or confession or time lifts it entirely, so that everything you touch afterward carries a faint gray shadow of it, visible only to yourself, in certain lights.

And now the state had priced his survival at one seat, and the seat was warm already with the shape of everyone he could not fit into it.

* * *

He hid the letter the way he had once hidden plates: badly at first, then better.

His first instinct was the flour tin, and he rejected it within the hour because Lior baked when she was worried and he knew her worry by its schedule. His second was beneath the floorboard under the proofing press, but the board had been lifted twice this year during inspections and Inspector Corvane's eyes had touched it both times with the particular tenderness a man reserves for places other men think are secrets.

In the end he chose the place that had never once been searched, because it had never once occurred to anyone that it contained anything: the specimen books. Forty years of type specimens, bound in calf and buckram, arranged on the shelf above his workbench in chronological order — a typographer's autobiography told in fonts. He slit the spine of the 1808 volume, the year of Lior's birth, and slid the folded letter between cover and binding, and pressed the book shut, and set it back in its place between 1807 and 1809, between the years his daughter came into the world and learned to walk, and there the letter sat, wedged inside her beginning, which struck him later — much later, in the dark, staring at the ceiling — as either poetry or cowardice, and he could not determine which, and suspected the answer was that they had become the same thing.

Lying was a skill, and Elias discovered he was worse at it than he had been at forty. At forty he had lied to a tribunal with his heart rate steady enough to bore a physician. Now he flinched at the word *letter*. When Lior asked, on Wednesday, whether the post had come, he said "nothing for us" and heard his own voice perform the sentence like an actor in a bad production — one beat too long before the denial, one shade too casual after it.

If she noticed, she said nothing. That was the worst of it. His daughter had inherited her mother's habit of watching people with perfect patience and saying nothing until the silence itself became a question, and Elias, dodging that question nightly across the dinner table while Nell banged her spoon and demanded — in the imperial grammar of infants — more bread, began to understand that secrecy does not protect the ones we love from harm. It merely protects them from *us*, temporarily, at the cost of becoming strangers to them in our own kitchen.

He caught himself rehearsing speeches. Standing at the case, distributing type, he would compose the opening lines — *Lior, there is something you must know* — and hear immediately how they would land, how her face would do the thing it did when she was hurt and refusing to show it, the slight lift of the chin, the eyes going flat and bright as coins. He composed a dozen openings and burned them all. There was no honest way to say: *The government has offered me a lifeboat, and I have accepted it in my heart before even asking whether you would want it, and I have not told you, because telling you makes it real, and real things can be argued with.*

And beneath all of it, deeper than the letter, deeper than the berth, lay the thing he had not yet confessed and knew — with the terrible clarity that comes at three in the morning — that he eventually must: that the child on the third floor, the child with Verel's jaw and her grandfather's stubborn cowlick, existed because of him. Because twenty years ago a dissident printer needed plates cut by the finest hand in Vessany, and Elias had cut them, and taken the money, and then sold Tomas Verel's name to save his own skin. Tomas had died in the Marsh Prison before the purge ended. His daughter Mara had vanished into the provinces. And now a baby had appeared on the doorstep wrapped in a printed page torn from one of Elias's own engravings, with a note that said *forget the face*, and Elias understood — had understood from the first moment, with the certainty of a man recognizing his own handwriting — that the world had mailed him a bill twenty years overdue, and interest had compounded.

The harbor was closing. Six weeks, Dario Ferro said, maybe less; the armistice talks in the capital would conclude, the port would seal, and the *Meridian* would take aboard its final manifest of tradesmen and loyalists and sail for free waters, and everyone remaining would inherit whatever peace the generals decided to call peace.

One berth. Four souls. He counted them at night like a miser counting coins that belonged to someone else: himself, whose skill the letter valued; Lior, whose skill the letter didn't know existed; Nell, whose value no list could hold; and Mara somewhere out in the marshes, hunted, if the whispers Dario carried were true, for the crime of leading rebels through country the army could not map.

Four souls. One seat. And a father's love, which had always seemed to Elias the cleanest thing in his life, discovering overnight that it could curdle — that love hoarded becomes secrecy, and secrecy becomes a kind of theft, and theft from one's own child is still theft.

* * *

On Friday he went down to the harbor to look at the ship.

He told himself it was errands — he needed lamp black, and Ferro's cousin sold it cheap off a lighter near the breakwater — but the lie was transparent even to himself, and he walked the length of the quay in the cold afternoon light with his collar up, looking at the *Meridian* where she rode at anchor inside the boom chain.

She was a coastal steamer converted in haste: gun mounts on her aft deck, cargo nets draped over her rails, and painted along her hull, fresh enough to still shine, the regime's registry number in white letters taller than a man. Berth allocations had been posted — the Commission believed in publicizing generosity — and a knot of people stood before the harbor office board reading the lists the way crowds in other cities read casualty notices. Elias drifted close enough to see without seeming to look.

The lists were organized by trade. *Physicians: six berths. Master shipwrights: four. Precision trades — engravers, instrument makers, die cutters: one each.* One each. Somewhere in the city, he thought, an instrument maker was reading the same number and doing the same arithmetic he had done in the press room, and somewhere a die cutter, and somewhere a physician with five children. The Commission had not allocated berths. It had allocated dilemmas, several hundred of them, distributed evenly across the trades, like a printer setting the same page of grief in a hundred editions.

A woman near him began to cry — quietly, the professional crying of someone who has children waiting at home and cannot afford to fall apart on a public quay. No one looked at her. Everyone understood her completely. That was Vessany in the last weeks: a whole harbor full of people understanding one another perfectly and pretending, for safety, not to.

Elias bought his lamp black from Ferro's cousin, who counted his change slowly and said, without looking up, "Heard you got your letter, engraver."

News traveled faster than ships in this town. "You hear a lot," Elias said.

"I hear the *Meridian* sails the night before the signing. Not after. Before." The cousin bit a coin, frowned at it, pocketed it. "They don't want witnesses with sea legs when the terms go public. You want my advice—" he glanced up then, and his eyes were older than his trade "—don't be on the pier when she casts off. Piers are bad places for people who've changed their minds."

Elias carried the lamp black home along the waterfront, past the boom chain with its links thick as his torso, past the customs shed where a soldier was nailing up another notice, and the whole way he felt the letter's weight in the specimen book behind him in the shop, three streets and a lifetime away, wedged inside the year of his daughter's birth.

He tried once more, on the stairs, to compose the confession. *Lior, I have been offered a boat.* He climbed past the second floor where Nell slept, and paused at the landing because the child had started talking in her sleep — not words yet, just vowels, bright little sounds like bubbles rising, and he stood in the dark stairwell listening to an orphan dream, and the speech dissolved in his throat for the hundredth time.

In the shop below, lamplight was burning. Lior was still at the case — she worked late now, taking government proclamations she set with flawless surface compliance and, he sometimes suspected, private contempt — and through the open door he watched her hands move among the type, quick and sure, and thought: *she is better at this than I ever was.* Not the setting of type. The other thing. The thing he had failed at twenty years ago and was failing at again: the courage to let a truth stand in the light and take its impression.

He went up to bed without speaking. The house settled around him, beam by beam, the old wood ticking as it cooled, and he lay in the dark doing the arithmetic again, because he could not stop doing it, because it never came out different:

One berth. Four names. And a letter hidden in a book on a shelf, growing heavier every night it stayed unconfessed, until he was sure that soon — a week, a fortnight, whenever his nerve finally matched his guilt — he would have to take it out, unfold it, and watch his daughter's face learn what her father had decided about her future without asking her.

He did not know, as he finally drifted toward an uneasy sleep, that the decision was about to be taken out of his hands entirely — that downstairs, in the dark shop, Lior had found the specimen book shelved slightly out of sequence, spines misaligned by the width of one folded page.

That she was already reaching for it.
