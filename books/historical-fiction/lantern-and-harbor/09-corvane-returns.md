## 9. Corvane Returns

The leaflet arrived at the Ministry of Information on a Tuesday, folded inside a consignment of salt-tax receipts from the eastern districts, as if the paper itself were ashamed of what it carried. Corvane found it because he found everything, or so his subordinates believed; in truth he found it because he had trained himself to be bored properly — to read even the dull documents with the fullness of attention that dullness deserved. Boredom, practiced well, was a kind of vigilance.

He was sorting receipts by watermark when his thumb caught the wrongness of one sheet. Too light. Too smooth. The ministry's stock was rag paper, coarse as sacking, made to remind every citizen that government documents were durable things. This page slid between his fingers like a fish.

He unfolded it under the green-shaded lamp.

*THE HARBOR IS NOT BEING SOLD. IT IS BEING GIVEN.*

The block beneath the line was a coast road rendered in cross-hatch, a lantern hanging from a crook, and below it a second line: *Read the terms they will not print.*

Corvane sat very still for a long moment. Around him the ministry breathed its institutional breath — the tick of the regulator clock, the scrape of clerks' chairs, someone two rooms away laughing at something he would never be told. He held the leaflet the way a physician holds an X-ray of a friend.

Because he knew the plate.

Not the image — the image was nothing, a lantern on a hook, ten thousand apprentices could cut a lantern on a hook. He knew the *hand*. The cross-hatching tightened toward the light source in gradations of four, never three, never five, each stroke terminating with a slight thickening where the burin lifted, a habit of pressure like a signature. He had seen it twenty years ago in a pamphlet called *The Marsh Gospel*, six plates long, beautiful enough to be dangerous twice over — dangerous for what it said and dangerous because it taught the eye to expect truth to look like craftsmanship. The ministry had burned every copy they seized. Corvane had kept one, hidden inside a folio of typographic specimens labeled *Obsolete*, and he had never examined his own conscience about why.

Elias Ishikawa had cut those plates. Corvane had proved it once, to his own satisfaction, in a room with bad light and worse coffee, and then Elias Ishikawa had stood before a tribunal with the face of a man describing weather and sworn he had never known Tomas Verel at all. And Corvane, twenty-seven years old and already fluent in the language of looking away, had confirmed the testimony with a technical analysis so deliberately shallow it amounted to perjury by adjective: *the work is competent, consistent with several practitioners of the period.*

Now the hand had come back from the dead, printing lanterns on fish-paper, and it was saying things about the armistice terms.

He should have taken it downstairs. He should have logged it, escalated it, set the machinery moving — the raids, the interrogations, the small bureaucratic avalanches that buried printers alive. Instead he locked the leaflet in his writing desk, beneath the specimens, beside the old marsh gospel, and sat with his hands flat on the blotter like a man steadying a table that wanted to tip.

There was a version of this where he told himself he was being strategic. Building a case quietly. Avoiding the panic of a premature raid that would scatter the offenders into the marshes. He let himself think that version, because a man needed a ladder even to climb down into a thing.

But underneath the strategy was simpler and more terrible: he wanted to know who was holding the burin now. He wanted, with an appetite that appalled him, to see the work again.

* * *

He dressed down for the errand. Not shabby — Corvane could no more dress shabby than he could mispronounce a ligature — but civilian: gray coat, no ministry pin, hat brim low enough to be forgettable. He took the ferry across rather than the bridge because the bridge had eyes he knew, and walked up from the water through streets that smelled of pitch and frying onions and the particular mineral cold coming off the harbor in late autumn.

Six weeks, they were saying in the taverns. Six weeks until the armistice concluded and the port sealed. Men said it the way they spoke of weather that threatened their crops — not arguing with it, only measuring it.

The print shop stood where it had always stood, wedged between a ropewalk and a chandler's, its upper story leaning toward the light like a listener. The censor's inspection notice was still nailed by the door, weathered soft, its wax seal gone the color of an old bruise. Corvane paused before it. *By order of the Office of Textual Integrity, premises subject to review.* His own office. His own order, countersigned years ago in a stack of forty such notices he had signed in eleven minutes on an afternoon he could not otherwise remember at all.

Through the window, oilcloth-filtered, he could see the glow of a lamp and the silhouette of a press, and a smaller silhouette bent over the composing stone — a woman, working late, her hands moving with the economy of someone who had been setting type since she was tall enough to reach the cases.

He knocked.

The lamp inside went dark first. Then footsteps, unhurried, and the door opened on a chain — Lior Ishikawa, one eye visible, a candle stub behind her throwing her face half out of the world.

"The shop's closed."

"The shop," Corvane said, "is exactly what I've come about." He removed his hat, which in Vessany was halfway to an apology. "I hope the hour doesn't offend. I'm a man with an announcement and no patience. My sister is to be married in the spring, and I want invitations worthy of the occasion, and everyone tells me—" he gestured at the leaning building, the darkened window, the whole stubborn edifice of it, "—there is no better hand in the harbor district."

The eye regarded him. He watched it do arithmetic — hour, stranger, closed sign, chain — and he admired the speed of it while pretending not to.

"Invitations," she said.

"Two hundred. Cream stock. I'm told I'm particular." He smiled the smile he used on witnesses: warm, faintly self-deprecating, a door left open. "My name is Corvane."

Something passed behind her eye — not recognition, he thought. *Weight.* The name landing somewhere and being put away carefully rather than dropped. Then the chain scraped and the door opened fully onto candlelight and ink-smell, and she stepped back to admit him.

"Then you'd better come in, Mr. Corvane, and tell me about your sister."

* * *

The shop at night was a different country from the shop by day. By day, he remembered, it had been all business — the proclamations stacked, the apprentice (gone now, he noted; no apprentice) sweeping lead dust. At night it was intimate the way workshops are when the work sleeps: the press a black animal in the corner, the typecases glowing faintly amber, the smell of oil and paper and the cold-metal sweetness of freshly cast sort. Corvane stood just inside the door and let it all enter him, and had to manage his own breathing, because there was a pleasure in this room that he had never been permitted anywhere else, and the pleasure was sharpened tonight by everything it might be hiding.

Lior relit the lamp and turned it low. She had, he noticed, thrown a shawl over her shoulders with the effect of armor rather than comfort, and she placed herself behind the composing stone so that it stood between them like a counter in a shop of ideas.

"Your sister," she prompted.

"Marguerite." He supplied the name without hesitation; he had invented her on the walk over, along with her fiancé, a customs officer named Pell — plausible people, useful people, people whose wedding would require two hundred invitations and no other questions. "She wants ivy on the border. I told her ivy is common. She told me I have no romance in me. We are both correct."

That earned him almost a smile. Almost. It reached her mouth but stopped at the eyes, which stayed on his hands — ah, he thought, *she is watching my hands* — and so he made his own hands idle and harmless, turning his hat brim, resting one on the edge of the stone.

"Show me what you'd propose," she said.

"I couldn't presume to design it. That's why I'm here." He let his gaze travel, casual as smoke, across the wall of typecases. Each drawer faced inward, brass label-holders catching the lamplight. "Though I confess I judge a printer by her letters before I judge her letters by her printer. May I?"

She hesitated one beat too long, then nodded, and he crossed to the cases as if browsing a library. Roman faces, a didone, a battered old textura missing its ampersands — he read the labels without opening them, and felt the shape of the collection the way a blind man reads a face. Something was missing. He could not say what, only that the wall had the symmetry of an argument with a hole in it, a gap where a case had been moved or never replaced, the dust outline faint on the shelf.

"And these?" he asked lightly. "All yours?"

"All ours." Her voice was level. "Father's, mostly. I inherited his alphabet and his headaches."

"Elias Ishikawa." Corvane turned, letting genuine warmth into his face because it required no acting. "I've admired his work for years. There was a map of the northern channels, must be fifteen years ago now — the soundings done in such fine stipple the water seemed to breathe. I've wondered who cut it."

"He doesn't take commissions anymore. His eyes." A lie delivered smoothly, or perhaps not a lie; Corvane filed it either way. "But I can show you proofs of the invitation border, if you like. We keep a book."

"We keep a book" — the plural slipping out like a coin from a torn pocket. So the father still touched the work. Still cut. Or she did, and had learned his cuts so thoroughly that the distinction no longer mattered, which was, Corvane reflected, its own kind of inheritance and its own kind of danger.

She fetched the proof book and laid it open, and he bent over it with honest appetite. Good work. Better than good. Her spacing was modern where her father's was classical; she kerned bravely, trusting white space the way some women trusted deep water, and where the old man's serifs ended in certainty hers ended in a question. He lost himself for a full minute in a specimen of swash capitals and came up for air to find her watching him with an expression he recognized because he wore it in mirrors: the look of someone assessing how much another person loves the thing they both love, and recalculating everything accordingly.

"You actually care about this," she said. Not quite a question.

"Typography?" He straightened. "Mademoiselle, I care about typography the way sailors care about weather. Professionally. Obsessively. It has decided the course of my life and I expect it will decide my death, though hopefully not for some decades." He turned a page gently, by its corner. "Most people think letters are furniture. They are not furniture. They are the only clothing truth is allowed to wear in public, and most truths go out dressed shamefully."

Silence. The lamp fluttered. Somewhere above them the house ticked and settled, and outside, far off, a ship's bell counted a fog-bound hour.

"That's a strange thing for a man to say," Lior said slowly, "who only wants wedding invitations."

"It's a strange city," Corvane said pleasantly. "Full of strange men."

* * *

They negotiated — two hundred invitations, cream stock, ivy reluctantly conceded ("my sister will win; she always wins; it saves time to surrender early"), delivery before the thaw. He paid a deposit in good silver and watched her write the receipt in a hand so disciplined it told him nothing, which itself told him something. All the while the cat-and-mouse went on beneath the transaction like current under ice. He asked whether the shop did other private work — cards, broadsheets, commemorative sheets — and she answered each question precisely, neither expanding nor retreating, giving him the exact surface of a truthful woman and not one hair more. Twice he offered openings a guilty person would fill. Twice she declined to fall in.

And once — only once — the mouse crossed the cat's path in the wrong direction. He asked, idly, lifting the proof book as though admiring its binding, whether she had ever printed from engraved plates, copperwork, the old pictorial style. And her hands, reaching for the receipt, stopped. Not dramatically. A hesitation the length of a heartbeat, the length of a burin lifting off copper, and then motion resumed as though the pause had never existed.

"Plates are old-fashioned," she said. "Lead and ink are cheaper."

"So is forgetting," Corvane said, smiling, "but it costs more in the end."

He let the words sit in the room between them like a coin spinning down, and he watched it land, and he saw that she understood it however she chose to understand it — as flirtation, as philosophy, as warning — and that she would turn it over privately later, which was all he wanted. He was not here to catch her tonight. He was here to be *known* to have come, so that whatever was printed next in this room would be printed knowing his eye existed. Fear, properly applied, was the finest preservative of order ever devised. Also the least reliable. He knew that too.

At the door he put on his hat and paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the shop — the sleeping press, the shawled girl, the wall of cases with its eloquent absence.

"My compliments to your father," he said. "Tell him the stippled maps are still remembered. Some of us keep everything, mademoiselle. It's a curse of the profession." He tapped his temple. "The eye files what the heart would discard."

"Good night, Mr. Corvane," said Lior Ishikawa, closing the door with the gentle firmness of a tide going out.

* * *

He walked back down to the water the long way, past the ropewalk, past the chandler's barrels breathing tar, and stood awhile at the pier's end where the harbor lay black and patient under a thinning fog. Lanterns swung on moored hulls. In five weeks, six, the last ship would load and the port would seal, and whatever the armistice papers said would become the only truth permitted to wear clothing here.

He thought about the leaflet locked in his desk. *Read the terms they will not print.* He had, of course, already read them — months ago, in draft, in a committee room smelling of cold tea, where a deputy minister had explained with a small dry smile that amnesty clauses were instruments of transition, not permanence, and that certain lists would need to be *reconsidered* after signature. Corvane had signed the minutes. That was what his hands did; they signed. He had gone home and washed them longer than necessary and told no one, including himself, what he had felt.

Now a lantern on a fish-paper leaflet was telling the whole harbor what he knew, in cross-hatchings that lifted off the copper with a slight thickening, four grades to the light, always four.

He could end it tomorrow. One report, one raid, and the shop between the ropewalk and the chandler's would be emptied and sealed, and the girl with the careful hands would vanish into the machinery he served. He turned the idea over the way he'd turned pages of her proof book, admiring the construction of it, the efficiency.

It refused to close.

Behind him the city murmured in its sleep. Out on the water a single lantern moved — a rowboat, unhurried, making for the shadowed side of a freighter — and Corvane watched it go without deciding anything at all, which was, he understood, already a decision.

When he reached the ministry he did not go upstairs. He unlocked his writing desk instead, took out the leaflet, and laid it beside the old marsh gospel, and set the two papers side by side under the green lamp — twenty years between them, the same hand.

On the margin of the new one, in pencil, in a clerk's flawless hand, he wrote a single word:

*Soon.*
