## 10. The Room Above the Shop

The room above the shop had been her mother's. No one said so anymore, but the room insisted: in the faded ring on the washstand where the blue enamel basin used to sit, in the hook by the window that still held the shape of a dressing gown long since burned for warmth during the hard winter, in the way the floorboard near the hearth gave a soft sigh underfoot — the same sigh, Lior imagined, that her mother's heels had drawn from it a thousand times.

It was where she took Dario Ferro, on the evenings when the harbor fog came up thick enough to swallow the customs lanterns, and the shop below slept with its lead and its secrets, and the two of them could be, for three or four hours, simply young.

She told herself it was practical at first. He brought milk — real milk, not the chalky ration powder — and someone had to inventory what he carried, didn't they? Someone had to count the tins, check the seals, argue about the price. That arguing was done best sitting close on the narrow settee, their shoulders touching through wool and salt-stiff canvas, was a fact neither of them remarked upon. Some facts, like some cargo, were better left undeclared.

Tonight he arrived late, smelling of pitch and cold spray, his knuckles split from rope-work. She had a fire going — small, guarded, the chimney flue choked with wet moss so the smoke would drift out thin as breath — and a pot of barley tea, and Nell asleep in the drawer-bed they'd lined with Elias's old vest. The baby slept the way gulls rode wind: absolutely committed, utterly without effort, as if sleep were simply the element she'd been born into.

"She's grown," Dario said, kneeling by the drawer before he'd even shed his coat. He looked at Nell the way he looked at tide tables — with respect for something that would not negotiate.

"Children do that," Lior said. "I'm told it's customary."

He laughed, low, because of the child, and the laugh moved through the small room like warm water. He shed his coat finally and sat, and she poured the tea, and for a while they watched the fire eat its slow meal of driftwood, which spat colors — green, once, startlingly blue — from the salts locked in it.

"I brought something else," he said.

"You always bring something else."

"This one's not for sale." From inside his shirt he drew a flat packet wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it with the ceremony of a man defusing something. Inside lay a book. Small, hand-sewn, the cover made of a chart of the Vessany outer banks cut down and pasted over board. "Poems," he said. "Printed inland, two years ago, before the presses there went quiet. A man on my last run traded it to me for passage. I've been carrying it around like a fool, waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For an occasion." He put it in her hands without looking at her, which was how Dario did all his most vulnerable things — watching the horizon instead, as though feeling might come at him broadside otherwise.

She turned the pages. The type was crooked in places, lovingly crooked, the kind of crookedness that meant someone had stood at a case setting line after line by candlelight because he could not *not*. She felt her throat tighten and was annoyed at it.

"It's badly kerned," she said.

"There she is," Dario said to the fire. "There's my romantic poetess."

But when she glanced up he wasn't laughing. The firelight had gotten into his eyes and turned them the color of tar with lamplight behind it, and he was looking at her with an expression she'd seen on sailors' faces only when land appeared after long crossings — that stunned, disbelieving hunger, quickly mastered.

* * *

They had met properly eight months ago, over a dispute about herring barrels, though he claimed afterward he'd noticed her two years before that, buying rope at the chandlery on Quay Street, and had rehearsed a greeting for the entire walk home and then not used it. He ran cargo the way his father had run it and his grandmother before him: light-footed, gray-hulled, half a step ahead of the revenue cutters, moving whatever paid — brandy in bad years, medicine in worse ones, and lately, though he'd only begun admitting this in the last month, people.

The night he first told her, rain had come in sideways off the sea and the whole city ticked and dripped. They'd been in this same room, and she'd asked why his new crewman — the silent one, Sef, who ate standing by the door — never showed his face at the customs house for the manifest signing.

"Because he doesn't have a face for manifests," Dario said. "None of us would, if the papers were honest."

She waited. With Dario, waiting worked better than asking; questions made him charming, but silence made him true.

"They're paying in trade now," he said finally, turning his cup. "Not coin. Names. There's a chain — marsh families, then the coastal runners, then us. We take them out on the dark tides, twelve at a time in a hold built for salt fish. South along the coast, then a fishing boat flagged for a country whose name I can't say in this room." He looked up. "You should know, because you're keeping things in this house now that have weight. If it ever comes to it — if anyone ever asks you what Ferro does at night — you can decide how much of me to spend."

That had been the moment, though she hadn't known it then. Not the first kiss, which came later and tasted of tea and rain. The moment was this: a man handing her his life folded small, saying *spend it as you need.* No one had ever trusted her with anything heavier than type.

Now, weeks later, with the poem-book warm in her lap and the fire burning down to amber coals, he grew quiet in a different key. She knew his silences the way she knew typefaces — could read the family, the weight, the intent. This one was boldface. This one had been composed carefully and set aside and picked up again many times.

"Say it," she said.

He turned his cup once more, a full rotation, like a man steering through something. "There's a berth opening," he said. "Early. Before the harbor closes."

Her hands went still on the book. Outside, somewhere below the fog, a buoy bell clanked twice and gave up.

"My run south — next dark tide, six days from now — I've got room. Real room, not hold-room. A cabin. The *Mirenne*'s master owes me past forgiving, and she puts in at Tallow Reach, and from Tallow there are papers to be had for children that are—" he chose the word with visible care— "unregistered. Unclaimed. A child travels easiest. Nobody interrogates an infant. Nobody checks an infant's face against a list." His eyes went, despite himself, to the drawer-bed. "I could take her out, Lior. Six days. She'd be across the water before the armistice is signed, before Corvane's reports move another inch toward this street, before any of it lands on this house. She'd grow up in a country where her grandfather's name is a story instead of a sentence."

The fire popped. In the drawer, Nell sighed and turned her fist against her cheek and did not wake.

"And you?" Lior heard herself ask. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, level and strange, a compositor's voice, reading type upside down and backward. "Would you take her?"

"I'd deliver her. To a marsh family on the first leg, then the *Mirenne*, then—" He stopped. "Yes. I'd take her myself if I could, but my route runs through waters that'll be patrolled to death by then. It has to be the *Mirenne* and it has to be soon, and it has to be her alone."

*Alone.* The word entered the room and sat down among them like a third guest.

Lior rose. She crossed to the drawer and looked down at the sleeping child — the improbable eyelashes, the down-fine hair that caught firelight and held it, the small mouth working at some dream, perhaps of milk, perhaps of nothing, because Nell had been born after the world stopped owing anyone dreams and so made her own. Six months Lior had known her. Six months of 2 a.m. feedings and the particular weight of a sleeping infant against a shoulder, heavier than lead type by every measure that mattered, and lighter than anything she had ever carried.

If she said yes — and the yes was right there, reasonable, armored in love, every argument polished — then Nell would live. That was the whole of it. Nell would live, in a country with a kind flag, raised by strangers whose faces Lior would never know, and the note pinned to the fish crate that morning would come true at last: *forget the face.* Everyone would forget the face. Nell most of all would forget the face — Lior's face, Elias's face, the lamplit shop, the smell of ink and oil, the sound of the press like a great patient heart. Nell would grow up unhaunted, and unremembering, and safe.

And Lior would stand in the shop below and pull proofs of government lies for the rest of her life, telling herself it had been love, that it had been the brave thing, that safety purchased with forgetting was still safety.

"Lior." Dario's voice, gentle. "You're doing the thing where your jaw goes like a vice-grip on a bolt."

"I'm thinking."

"You're typesetting. I can see it. You're arranging the words before they're said." He stood and came to her, not touching, but close, close enough that she could feel the warmth off his coat and the sea underneath it. "I know what I'm asking. I've run it through my head forty times. Every way I slice it, taking her out is the only arithmetic that keeps her breathing. But—" and here his voice roughened, and he looked away at the fire like a man looking at the horizon— "but I've watched what it costs to send children ahead alone. I ferried a girl out of Marsh End in the spring, eleven years old, alone, her parents staying for the network. She waved at me from the *Mirenne*'s rail like I was the adventure and everything behind her was nothing. And her mother, when I gave the report — Lior, her mother thanked me and then walked into the water up to her knees and just stood there. Facing south. For an hour. In March."

The coals shifted, collapsing softly into red.

"So I won't pretend it's simple mercy," Dario said. "I'm offering you a wound. A clean one, maybe. But a wound."

Lior bent and lifted Nell from the drawer. The baby came up against her chest with that boneless trust, head lolling into the hollow of her neck, one hand curling into Lior's collar — and Lior stood in her mother's room, above her father's shop, in a city being quietly sold, and understood that the choice had already been made. It had been made in the winter, on the doorstep, when she'd looked down into the crate and the infant had looked up and neither of them had flinched. It had been made every time she'd called the child by a name she'd invented against all advice. Love, she thought, was not a feeling you had. It was a debt you volunteered for, and debts of this kind did not permit partial payment.

"No," she said.

Dario closed his eyes briefly, and she saw — she would remember always that she saw — relief move through him like a tide, quick and deep, before grief followed it in.

"Listen to me," Lior said, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her. "If we send her alone, she survives, but everything else dies. She becomes a face forgotten, exactly as the note ordered. And I will not run a household on forgetting. My father tried it — twenty years of forgetting, and look what washed back up on our doorstep. The past doesn't drown, Dario. It just waits for low tide." She pressed her lips to Nell's hair. "Either she goes with someone who loves her, or she stays with someone who loves her. Alone is not one of the options. I don't care how safe the cabin is. Alone isn't safe. Alone is just loss with better weather."

Dario was quiet a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, the way men nod at sea when a decision is made and the weather must simply be sailed.

"Then we find another way," he said. "The final ship, the allocation lists — I have friends in the harbor office, drunk friends, generous friends. There may be more room than the regime admits. There's always more room than the regime admits; it's practically the national motto."

"And if there isn't?"

"Then when the last ship loads, we'll stand on the pier and do the terrible arithmetic together." He touched Nell's back with two fingers, feather-light, a man checking whether something precious was real. "I warn you, Ishikawa — I'm very good at arithmetic."

"You're very good at smuggling," Lior said. "Arithmetic you fudge. I've seen your accounts."

He laughed again, and this time the laugh had sorrow in it and also something else, something that sounded almost like hope, and he pulled her in with the baby between them, so that the three of them stood pressed together in the small room above the shop while outside the fog thickened over the harbor, over the masts and the moored guns and the negotiation tables where men were even now pricing the coastline, deciding who would be remembered and who would be erased.

Later, much later, when Dario had gone down the stairs on cat feet and the fire was ash and Nell slept on, Lior sat by the dead hearth with the poem-book open on her knees and did not read it. She was listening. Below her, faint through the floorboards, she heard her father's chair shift in the front room — heard it shift, and settle, and go still — and realized with a cold sliding feeling, like a keel leaving shallow water, that Elias had been awake all along.

And that in the silence that followed, she could hear him writing.
