## 11. Confession at Low Tide

The tide went out an hour before dawn, and Elias Ishikawa went out with it, the way he had taken to doing all that winter — walking the exposed flats below the harbor wall where the mud gave up its dead things: broken oars, bottles sealed with wax, the ribcage of a skiff that had gone down before Lior was born. He walked there because no one else did. Because the mud remembered footprints and forgot them again with the tide, and a man who was composing a betrayal in his chest needed ground like that under his feet.

The berth letter rode against his breastbone, folded to the size of a prayer.

*One seat. Engraver's trade. Final sailing before armistice.* The ministry seal on it had been blue as a bruise, and when he'd first broken it open — alone, up in the type room while Lior slept — he had read it four times and each time the words rearranged themselves into a different cruelty. One seat. Not one for him and one for the child. Not one for him and one for his daughter. One.

He had told himself, in the weeks since, that he was waiting for the right moment to tell her. That was a lie he set in lead and printed daily, and the impressions grew sharper with every run. The truth was simpler and more shameful: he was waiting for courage, and courage kept not coming, and meanwhile Nell had learned to pull herself upright against the leg of the composing stone, and Lior had begun humming to her in the evenings, a low tuneless sound that filled the shop the way his wife's voice once had, and every day he took the letter out and looked at it, and every day he put it back, and said nothing.

Tonight the tide would be lowest near dusk. Tonight, he decided on the flats, with the gulls screaming down at him like creditors, he would tell her everything. Not just the berth. All of it. The testimony. Tomas Verel. The whole architecture of his survival, which she had lived inside all her life without knowing it was built on another man's bones.

Because the raid on the Salt Row shops had changed something. He had heard the hammers through the walls two nights ago — soldiers prying up floorboards three doors from his own door — and understood that time was no longer a thing he could spend slowly. If the shop fell, Lior would learn the truth anyway, from an interrogator, in a room with no windows. Better she learn it from him here, where at least the sea could hear, and the sea forgave nothing but also held nothing against anyone.

He climbed the harbor stairs at dusk. The lamps were being lit along the quay by a man on a ladder, one flame at a time, and Elias thought: *that is what I am about to do, one small fire after another, until the street is bright enough to see what we have become.*

* * *

Lior was at the stone when he came in, setting type by lamplight, and Nell was asleep in the drawer lined with felt that had been Lior's own cradle twenty-two years ago. The girl — his daughter, though some days she seemed a force of nature merely lodging in his house — did not look up. Her fingers moved among the letters with the blind fluency of long habit, and he stood a moment in the doorway watching her hands, because he knew he was about to hurt them, and wanted one last sight of them at peace.

"Elias," she said, using his name instead of *Father*, which she did when she was tired or suspicious. "You've been at the flats. You smell like low tide."

"Low tide is honest," he said. "It shows you everything the water's been hiding."

She did look up then. She had her mother's face — Anneli's narrow jaw, Anneli's eyes the color of wet slate — and it was Anneli looking at him now across twenty years, asking without asking. He hung his coat on the peg. He sat down across the worktable from her. He took out the letter and laid it between them like a card turned face up.

She read it fast; she read everything fast. He watched her eyes reach the number *one*, watched her go back and confirm it, watched her face do the arithmetic and arrive, before anything else, at the question of the child.

"Nell isn't listed," Lior said quietly. "Trade exemptions don't cover infants."

"No."

"Then this seat was never for her." Her voice stayed level. That was the thing about Lior that frightened him most, sometimes: she did not break where other people broke. She went very still and very precise, like a press taking weight. "And it isn't for me, because I'm only a printer's daughter, not an engraver. So it's yours."

"It's mine," he said. "Yes."

"And you've known for how long?"

"Six weeks."

Something crossed her face then — not anger yet, its scout, riding ahead of the army. Six weeks. Six weeks of suppers, of her carrying Nell up and down the stairs, of her minding the censor's inspections, of her trusting him with her whole back turned. He watched her count the days the way she counted em quads, exactly, without mercy.

"Say the rest," she said. "There's a rest. I can see it behind your teeth. Say all of it, Father, or I will spend the rest of my life imagining worse."

So he told her.

* * *

He began, as he always began these confessions — the ones he rehearsed nightly into the dark — with the plates. Twenty-three years ago, a younger Elias Ishikawa, newly widowed of his first apprenticeship and hungry for coin, had engraved copperplates for a client whose name he knew within a month was not the name on the invoices. Tomas Verel. A printer with soft hands and a fanatic's calm, who made arguments in ink that the government of Vessany had forbidden and half the harbor secretly believed. Elias cut the faces of martyrs into copper. Elias cut the maps of the arsenal. Elias cut, one terrible week, the plate of a caricature so precise in its mockery that he laughed aloud at his own workbench, alone, at midnight, and the laughter scared him more than any knock ever had.

"I wasn't brave," he told Lior, and the lamplight made her face unreadable, which he deserved. "I want that understood before anything else. I was never brave. I was paid well and I liked the work and I told myself the plates were only plates. And then Verel was arrested."

The raid on Verel's shop had emptied the harbor quarter of air for a week. Elias had spent that week engraving a commission for a chandler — fish labels, harmless things — with his door barred and his heart going like a runaway press. And on the ninth day, a man from the Ministry of Information had come anyway, courteous as Corvane was courteous, and sat down, and explained the arithmetic of Elias's life with the patience of a man reading a ledger aloud.

They knew who had cut the plates. The line quality was distinctive; experts had compared the engravings to Elias's signed commercial work and matched the burin hand like a signature. They could prove it whenever they wished. What they offered instead was silence, purchased cheaply: Elias would testify — briefly, quietly, in a closed session — that he had never known Verel at all, that the plates must have been copied from his style by persons unknown. Deny the man. Deny knowing him, deny the work, deny the friendship that had in fact existed, the long evenings arguing politics over Verel's bad wine while their children — 

No. Their children came later. But the denial itself was the wound, and he made himself say it plainly to Lior now.

"I testified that I had never known him," Elias said. "I swore it with my hand on the book. And they let me keep my shop, and my name off the lists, and my life. And they took him to the marsh prisons, and I sent money through Ferro's father for two years, and then the routes closed, and then I stopped hearing anything at all, and then your mother died in the purge that followed, and I told myself grief had burned the guilt down to ash. It hadn't. Ash keeps the shape of what burned. You can still see the letters in it, if you look."

Lior had not moved through any of it. Her hands lay flat on the stone, among the loose type, and her knuckles were white as the letters themselves.

"The child," she said. "You think she's his."

"The page she was wrapped in. I know my own line, Lior. That plate was mine — the portrait of the marsh martyr, cut in my twenty-ninth year. Verel kept it after I stopped working for him; I saw it in a leaflet years later and thought someone had stolen it. Now I understand he kept it because he loved it, the way printers love. And if the plate passed to his daughter, and his daughter wrapped her child in a sheet pulled from it—" His voice failed on the arithmetic. "The note said *forget the face*. That's what people say when they're hiding from the Ministry. That's what Mara Verel would have been taught to say since birth."

"Mara." Lior said the name like she was testing whether it fit in her mouth.

"She's alive. There were reports — rebels moving through the eastern marshes, led by a woman the soldiers call the Lantern. I didn't connect it. God forgive me, I chose not to connect it."

"And so the baby is her granddaughter," Lior said, "and you knew, and you said nothing, and you took a letter for one seat and hid it in your coat for six weeks." At last her voice cracked, but not the way he feared — it cracked downward, into something quiet and terrible. "Where were you going to send money from, Father? From inland? To whom? Me? Would you have written to me — *dear daughter, I denied a man to his ruin, I abandoned his orphan grandchild to whatever the harbor does to orphans, but here is fifty crowns, buy yourself good bread*?"

"I was going to give you the shop," he said, and heard even as he said it how small it sounded, how it was the sound of a man offering furniture in place of a soul. "The shop is worth—"

"The shop." She stood up. The motion dislodged a few pieces of type, which rang on the stone like tiny bells, absurdly musical. "Do you know what I thought, all those nights you were upstairs with your letter pressed to your chest like a second heart? I thought you were afraid. I thought, *he's old, he's frightened, the raids have rattled him.* I was making excuses for you, Elias, I was building you a better father than you are, and the whole time you were already packed."

"That's not—"

"Whose idea was the testimony?" she asked suddenly. "Did they threaten you? Or did they only offer, and you reached?"

He was silent a moment too long, and she read the silence the way she read proof sheets, instantly, catching every error.

"They offered," he said. "I reached. Your mother was pregnant with you. I want you to know that — not as excuse, there is no excuse, but as fact. I reached because you were the size of a plum and I could feel the world's teeth already."

"Don't." The word came out flat and final as a forme locked shut. "Don't put me under your hand when you swear it. Mother went into the purge year pregnant too, and she didn't testify against anyone, and she died anyway. Do you remember how she died, Elias? Because I remember the story you told me — fever, you said, a fever in the autumn. I was eleven before Ferro's mother got drunk at the regatta and told me soldiers came to the house looking for *Verel's engraver* and your wife stood in the door and told them there was no such man in it, and they pushed past her, and she caught something in that cell they held her in, something the fever story was wearing as a coat."

He had not known that Lior knew. Twenty years he had carried the true version folded smaller than the berth letter, and all that time his daughter had been carrying it too, folded differently, and neither of them had ever once unfolded theirs in the other's presence. Grief in that house had been conducted like smuggling — quietly, at night, in small unmarked packages.

"She died protecting the lie that kept us alive," Elias said hoarsely. "That's the truth of it. She knew what I'd done and she stood in that doorway anyway, and I have spent twenty years unable to decide whether that makes her braver than me or twice as damned."

"She'd have taken the child to the boat herself," Lior said. "If it were her, and the letter said one seat — do you actually believe, in whatever is left of you that still knows her, that Anneli Ishikawa would have boarded that ship?"

The question went into him like a burin. Because the answer was no. The answer was so clearly no that it rearranged the entire confession, showed him his six weeks of careful self-justification as what it was: not caution but cowardice wearing caution's apron. Anneli would have put the baby aboard and stood on the pier with her slate eyes daring the gangplank to argue. Anneli had stood in doorways all her life.

Nell stirred in her drawer at the change in the room's weather, whimpered once, and settled again when Lior's hand found her head — automatically, without looking, the way a compositor finds a letter by touch. Watching that, Elias felt the last of his plan come apart in his hands like wet paper.

"There's a woman in the market," Lior said, quieter now, dangerously quieter. "I've seen her twice. She has Verel's jaw. I nearly spoke to her. Do you understand what that means? I chose not to. I've been becoming you, Father, one small silence at a time, and I hated myself for it, and now I find I learned it at home."

"Lior—"

"No. You've confessed. It's my turn to say the true thing." She lifted Nell out of the drawer and held her against her shoulder, and the child slept on with the absolute trust of the very young, and Lior looked at Elias over the small head with eyes that were not angry anymore. Anger would have been easier to bear. What was in her face was assessment — the cold, clear look of a printer deciding whether a plate was worn beyond repair. "You will not take that seat. You'll give it to me, and I'll decide who boards, and you will stand on the pier and watch me do it, and that is the penance, do you hear me? Living through the watching. That's what you get."

"And what do you get?" he asked, because some stubborn engraver-part of him still needed to cut the whole design before the plate was done.

Lior rocked the baby once, twice. Outside, far down the harbor, a ship's bell rang the hour, and beneath it, faintly, he could hear the tide beginning its long return over the flats, erasing his footprints, forgiving nothing.

"What do I get?" she repeated. "I get to find out whether I'm my mother's daughter after all."

She turned toward the stairs with Nell, and had one foot on the bottom step when the knocking came — three slow blows, iron on the street door, heavy enough to shiver the type in the case — and through the dark of the shop, in a voice trained to courtesy the way blades are trained to edges, Inspector Corvane called out that he apologized for the hour, but he had a question about a wedding invitation.
