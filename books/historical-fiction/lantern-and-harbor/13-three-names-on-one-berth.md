## 13. Three Names on One Berth

The letter sat on the typecase like a stone no one would admit to having placed.

Lior had found it that morning, folded into the drawer where her father kept his finest chisels — the ones he used only for faces, for the delicate work of noses and jawlines cut into copper. It was addressed to *E. Ishikawa, Master Engraver*, in the sea-green ink of the Harbor Authority, and it weighed almost nothing at all. She had held it up to the lamp and read it three times before the words stopped sliding off the page.

*One berth. The vessel* Meridian *departs on the eve of the armistice. The bearer of this document and one dependent under the age of two years will be granted passage. No exceptions will be made.*

One berth. One dependent under two.

She counted the days against the wall calendar where she chalked the tides. Eleven. Eleven days until the harbor sealed itself like a wound, until the armistice was signed in some marble room far from the smell of fish and tar, until the borders closed around Vessany the way a hand closes around a moth — gently, if you were lucky, but finally either way.

And she counted the people.

Her father, downstairs already, sharpening a burin he hadn't touched in weeks because sharpening was what his hands did when his mind was walking somewhere he wouldn't let it speak about aloud. Mara, sleeping in the back room on the pallet beneath the window, her body still carrying the marsh-fever, her face in sleep looking so much like Nell's grown thirty years forward that Lior sometimes had to leave the room. Nell herself, six months old now, cutting her first tooth with the stoic fury of a dockworker, currently banging a wooden composing stick against the floorboards upstairs like a tiny printer protesting her wages.

And Lior. Who could have the berth too, if she abandoned the press — the letter said nothing about trades, but the allocation list pinned to the customs house door said everything: *printers and engravers of licensed works, priority standing.* Her name was not on any list. Her father's was. If she went as his dependent, they would take her; the regime loved an obedient household traveling together, easy to account for. But going meant leaving the press, and the press had become, in the last week, something more than iron and screws. It had become a voice. Hers.

Four candidates. One berth. She did the arithmetic standing at the case, her fingers resting on the cold lead sorts without seeing them, and the arithmetic came out brutal every time, the way simple sums so often do.

* * *

They did not discuss it at breakfast. They discussed it at everything except breakfast.

Mara raised it over the milk Dario had smuggled in that week — real milk, thin and blue-ish but milk — saying lightly, to the window rather than to them, "When I'm well enough to walk to the end of the street, I'll be well enough to walk out of the harbor district, and then there are only three names to argue over." And Elias said, "You'll walk to the ship," in a voice that ended the sentence like a gate, and Mara smiled the small stubborn smile Lior was beginning to know, and Nell threw her spoon, and the moment passed the way dangerous moments do in small houses — by being buried alive under the ordinary.

But that night, after Mara slept and Nell slept, Elias lit the second lamp and set it between them at the workbench, which was how he had always begun serious conversations, as if the light were a third party whose presence demanded honesty.

"You found the letter," he said.

"I found the letter."

"Then you know what I've decided."

Lior set down the sort she was holding. "You haven't decided anything. You've decided to *not say*, which is different, and worse, and you taught me the difference when I was nine and lied about breaking the galleys."

Something moved through her father's face — grief and pride fighting for the same doorway. He turned the letter over with one finger, though he knew its contents by heart; she could see he'd read it so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

"It should be Nell," he said quietly. "That's my decision. That's what I've been not saying."

"The child can't travel alone—"

"She won't be alone. I'll take her." He said it fast, the way a man pulls off a bandage. "I'm old, Lior. My hands are still good, but the eyes are going — you've watched me squint at the proof pages for a year and pretended not to notice, the way I pretended not to notice your mother's cough. A berth is wasted on a man who's finished most of his sentences. But that girl—" His voice caught on the word *girl*, and he steadied it. "That girl hasn't started a single one. And she is Tomas Verel's granddaughter, and this regime has a long memory for granddaughters. If anyone in this house has earned the last seat out of Vessany, it's her."

"And you'd go with her. As her dependent."

"As her grandfather, whatever their paper calls it." He looked at her then, fully, and his eyes were wet and would not admit it. "And you'd stay. Run the shop. Keep your head down until the armistice settles, print your proclamations, live. That's the whole plan. It isn't clever. Plans don't need to be clever. They only need to keep the right people breathing."

Lior sat with it. She wanted, violently, to say yes — to be given permission to survive, to be handed absolution the way you hand someone a receipt. For one whole breath she nearly took it.

Then she heard herself say: "And Mara?"

The lamplight hissed.

"Mara," Elias said carefully, "has her own routes. Dario says—"

"Dario says the marshes are crawling with patrols since the rebel column came through. Dario says they're shooting curfew-breakers on sight and filing them as armed combatants because dead rebels look better in reports. You know all of this. You've been in the room when he says it." She stood, and her chair scraped back, loud in the small shop. "You've done the sum, Papa. You've added Nell and subtracted everyone else, and you didn't even show your work."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. None of this is fair. That's what I've been trying to tell you for eleven days' worth of not-saying."

* * *

Mara came to her the next evening while Elias was out — ostensibly buying oil, actually, Lior suspected, standing in line at the customs house to look at the allocation list again, reading names the way other men read scripture.

Mara held Nell against her shoulder, rocking in the slow half-circle her body had learned in six months of hiding, and her voice was low, marsh-soft, the voice of a woman who had learned that loudness kills.

"I heard you two," she said. "Walls here are paper. I was a smuggler's daughter before I was anything else; I hear everything."

"We weren't quiet."

"No one is, in the end." Mara smiled faintly. "I want you to know what I've decided, so you can fight me about it properly instead of behind my back."

Lior braced.

"I take the berth alone," Mara said. "I leave Nell with you."

The room tilted. "That's insane."

"It's arithmetic," Mara said, and the word landed like a blow, because it was Lior's own word, her own private sum, spoken back to her by a stranger who wasn't one. "Think it through. I'm hunted — Corvane's men have my description in three districts now. A woman with an infant moves slowly, gets rooms refused, gets remembered. A woman alone can move. I can reach the inland columns, rejoin the network, and when this war ends however it ends — and it will end badly for them, Lior, you've seen the leaflets you've been printing at three in the morning thinking I don't hear the press — I come back for her." She shifted Nell to her other shoulder. "Whereas if I take her into the marshes and we're caught, we're one corpse and one smaller corpse, and the plate dies with us, and everything Tomas built dies with it."

"And if you don't come back?"

"Then she grows up with you." Mara said it simply, terribly, the way people say the truest things. "In a print shop that smells of ink and lamp oil, with a woman who named her when naming her was an act of defiance. I've watched you for weeks. There are worse fates than being raised by you. There aren't many better ones anywhere."

"You don't know me."

"I know you burn your practice sheets so no one reads your handwriting. I know you hum when you set type and stop humming when you set lies. I know you check on my daughter twice a night and pretend it's once." Mara's eyes were steady and full of something Lior couldn't bear to name. "I know enough."

Nell stirred, sighed, settled. Outside, a patrol boat's lantern swept the water beyond the shutters, drawing its slow bright line across the ceiling, across all three of them, and vanished.

"I won't agree," Lior said hoarsely.

"You don't have to agree. I'm telling you what I'm going to argue at the dock, when the time comes, so nobody wastes our last ten minutes shouting." Mara kissed the top of Nell's head. "Love measured in logistics. That's all any of us have left. So measure it well."

* * *

Dario came at midnight, which meant the conversation Lior had been dreading arrived wearing salt spray and a split knuckle.

He sat on the loading step outside the pressroom, and she brought him bread and the last of the milk, and he ate and drank and said nothing for a while, which was unlike him, and therefore told her everything before he opened his mouth.

"I can move two," he said finally. "Maybe. The *Cormorant*'s owner owes me and the patrol rotation has a gap on Thursday nights, but only two, and only small craft from the breakwater, and only if nobody official looks too hard." He turned the cup in his hands. "It's not the berth. It's not legal passage. But it's two."

"Two," Lior repeated.

"Elias and the baby. Or Mara and the baby. Or—" He stopped. Looked at her. The harbor light caught the side of his face, the crooked nose, the ink stain on his wrist from helping her distribute leaflets that she'd never managed to scrub off him and secretly never tried to.

"Or me," she finished.

"Or you." He said it flat, refusing to dress it up. "If you left, Ferro could run this place as a dead drop and the press goes quiet and nobody hangs for it. That's the honest version. Every night you stay and pull that lever, the honest version gets more expensive." He set down the cup. "I'm not asking you to go. I'm telling you the price list, because somebody in this cursed city should be doing sums out loud."

"Dario." She reached over and took his split hand, careful of the knuckle. "Thursday. Two seats. Put Elias and Nell on the boat."

"Lior—"

"Don't argue yet. Hear the whole sum first." Her voice was shaking and she let it shake; there was no one left in this house to perform steadiness for. "Papa takes Nell inland from wherever you land. He engraves plates for whoever's printing truth up-country, because his hands are good even if his eyes squint, and because a man needs a reason that isn't just escape. Nell grows up free of this harbor. Mara stays hidden here with me — because she's too hunted to move and we both know it, and because in twelve days the armistice either changes everything or proves it changes nothing, and either way she's safer inside walls with a press than outside them. And I stay."

"Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is that I abandon none of you and save none of you and keep the only thing I have, which is the press, and the voice it gives me, and—" Her breath broke. "And the chance that when this ends, there's a harbor left for all of you to come back to. That's my arithmetic. Check my work."

Dario was quiet a long time. Somewhere across the water, the *Meridian* rode at anchor, lamps burning, already loading cargo for a departure that would carry one seat that mattered and hundreds that didn't.

"Your work checks," he said at last, roughly. "It's still a terrible sum."

"All the best ones are."

He pulled her hand up and pressed his lips to her ink-stained knuckles, and neither of them said the word *love*, because they had both grown up in Vessany, where the important things were always set in the smallest type.

* * *

They were still arguing it four days later — gently now, wearied into courtesy, four people circling one seat like moths negotiating a single flame — when the knock came at the street door.

Three knocks. Measured. Unhurried. The knock of a man who had never once needed to force a door because doors opened for him.

Through the gap in the shutters Lior saw the sea-green coat, the neat figure, the pale patient eyes taking in the shop front, the allocation letter pasted beside the door, the whole small theater of their lives.

Inspector Corvane stood in the street holding a leather folio under one arm, and he was smiling.

Lior's hand found the seam in the typecase wall where the plates slept, and behind her, in the back room, she heard Mara go very still, and Nell, sensing weather the way infants do, began — softly, terribly, precisely on cue — to cry.
