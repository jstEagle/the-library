## 15. What Forgetting Costs

The note had said *forget the face*, and for eleven months Lior had done the opposite of everything it asked.

Now, in the blue hour before dawn, she stood in the yard behind the shop with a wash bucket, a bottle of lamp oil, and every piece of evidence that could hang them all: Tomas Verel's recovered plates wrapped in oilcloth, the bundle of Mara's letters hidden inside a false bottom of the flour bin, the leaflets she had printed in the small hours with her sleeves rolled and her heart hammering like a compositor's mallet. She had stacked them neatly on the flagstones, because even in destruction she was her father's daughter and could not abide disorder.

The armistice would be signed in two days. Everyone knew it now; the town criers had stopped pretending otherwise, and the harbor had gone strange with waiting, masts swaying like men who couldn't decide whether to kneel or run. When the ink dried on that treaty, Vessany would belong to the Provisional Directorate in name and to the men who had bought it in fact, and anyone whose name appeared in Corvane's careful files would have a short amnesty and a long grave.

*Forget the face.* She struck a match. The wind off the water bullied the flame sideways but didn't kill it, and she held it to the corner of the topmost leaflet until the paper caught, curling orange, browning, blackening, letting go of its own words one line at a time.

*The Directorate, having no intention of honoring the amnesty clauses appended under duress...*

She had copied those words from a document that had come through the shop three days earlier — or rather, a document that had been *meant* to come through the shop, except the courier's satchel had been misdelivered in the confusion of the evacuation lists, and Lior had found the sealed packet wedged behind the counter where a harried soldier had dropped it. She should have sent it on. She had opened it instead, standing exactly where she stood now in thought if not in body, and read the secret annex to the treaty: the clause that promised amnesty to all printers, smugglers, and sympathizers of the rebellion, and the private memorandum beneath it, initialled in a hand she recognized from years of setting government type, instructing that *the names collected under the press inspections be processed according to standing order nine* after the signing.

Standing order nine. She had set that order herself once, years ago, letter by brass letter. It meant detention without record. It meant faces forgotten on purpose, by men whose job was forgetting.

She had known then what she would do, and had spent three days pretending not to know, and now stood burning five copies of the truth in the dark because she had thought, briefly, that being sensible might feel like safety.

It felt like swallowing lead type.

The fire ate the leaflets quickly. Paper is honest that way; it burns as though it meant to all along. The plates were harder. Oilcloth, then flame, then the first plate — the lighthouse scene, Tomas Verel's design, her father's engraving from twenty years ago, the image that had opened every forbidden pamphlet of the old movement like a signature — going dull in the heat, the fine lines filling with soot. She watched the lighthouse blur. Somewhere in the smoke a gull cried out over the rooftops, absurd and lonely, and Lior Ishikawa, who had spent her whole life being careful, who had told her first lie to an official at seventeen and gotten better at it every year since, put out the fire with the wash bucket.

Steam hissed. Soot ran off the plate in gray rivers. The lighthouse re-emerged, unrepentant, every hairline of its beam intact.

She sat down on the wet flagstones and laughed until the laugh turned into something else, and when it was done she gathered up the scorched leaflets and the warm plates and carried them back inside like a woman carrying coals into a house already on fire.

* * *

Her father found her at the composing stone an hour later, her hands moving through the case with the blind certainty of sleepwalking.

"You're setting it," Elias said. Not a question. He stood in the doorway in his nightshirt, gray-haired, hollow-eyed, and behind him the typecase wall hid the rest of Tomas's plates in their cavity between beams, the seam chalked in her memory with a word she still couldn't decipher: *Soon.*

"Five hundred," Lior said. "Maybe more if the paper holds."

"They'll shoot you." He said it the way he might have said *it's raining*, flatly, because they had passed the point where the facts needed emphasis. "After the signing. Standing order nine, if they're feeling gentle."

"I know."

"Lior." He came forward and put his hands over hers on the composing stick, and his palms were shaking, and she understood suddenly that he was not trying to stop her. He was holding on. "Your mother—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended, and she didn't take it back. Her mother, dead three winters now, taken in the last purge by men who had never learned her face and never bothered to. "You always start there. As if grief were an argument."

"It isn't an argument. It's the only thing I know about what they do." He released her hands slowly, finger by finger, the way you let go of something you fear will sink. "I testified against my friend to keep you alive. I have regretted it every day since, and I would do it again tomorrow, and I have never once figured out what that makes me. If you print this, I'd like to know what it makes *you* before they tell you."

"A printer," Lior said. "Actually one, finally. Not a girl who sets other people's lies in someone else's type."

Elias looked at the forme locked on the stone — the secret terms, the annex, the initials, all of it justified tight and true, margins clean as a hymnal — and something moved through his face that she had seen only twice before: once at her mother's wedding portrait, once at her mother's funeral. He touched the edge of the forme like a man touching a rail over a long drop.

"The chase needs another quoins," he said. "You've left the lower right slack. It'll slur on the pull." And he went to the cabinet for the iron wedges, and that was all the blessing there was, and it was enough.

* * *

They printed through the morning with the shutters barred and the shop sign turned to *Closed for Repairs*, which was, Lior thought, the truest notice the door had ever borne.

It was slow work, deliberate work, the kind of work that forgives nothing. Her father pulled the press — he insisted, though his shoulder complained — and she fed the sheets, dampened paper, stacked and turned and stacked again, and the smell of ink and size filled the room like a tide coming in. Five hundred copies. Each pass of the press took eleven seconds and each sheet weighed almost nothing and together, in the bale, they would weigh more than any of them could carry, which was, she supposed, the entire principle of the thing.

Somewhere around the two-hundredth sheet she stopped counting and started listening to the press, the way it breathed in and out, the wooden groan of the platen, the wet kiss of type meeting paper. Her mother had died hating this shop a little, Lior remembered; had called it *the family's third child, the greedy one*. And here was Lior married to it now in every way that mattered, feeding it the most dangerous meal it had ever eaten, and loving it — God help her — loving the rhythm, the discipline, the absolute demand that your hands be honest even when your mouth had learned to lie.

Toward noon a knock came, four beats, pause, two beats. Dario.

He slipped in through the alley door smelling of tar and cold harbor water, and he looked at the drying racks hung with five hundred identical pages and went very still, the way he did aboard a boat when the weather changed.

"That's the annex," he said.

"That's the annex."

"How many?"

"Five hundred. Give or take the spoilage."

Dario crossed the room and picked up a sheet and read it through, though he must have known what it said; half the underground had heard rumors. His lips moved slightly as he read, which he would have denied, and when he finished he set it down with exaggerated care, as though it were already contraband — which, as of this moment, it was.

"The armistice is a lie," he said quietly.

"The armistice is a lie in beautiful type. Look at the kerning on 'amnesty.' I outdid myself."

He laughed, one short bark, and then didn't laugh at all. "Lior. After the signing, they seal the port. Anyone with a name in Corvane's files — that's you, that's Elias, that's me twice over — standing order nine doesn't need ships. It needs a list and a quiet street." He took her hands, and his were rough and warm and steady, and she hated how much steadiness helped. "My boat goes out tomorrow midnight with the last loading. There may be room. There may not. But if we spend tonight distributing these instead of packing—"

"We *will* distribute them tonight. That's not the question."

"Then what is?"

She looked at the racks. Five hundred faces' worth of paper, if paper had faces; five hundred chances for a stevedore, a fishwife, a clerk, a soldier to read, before dawn, what was being signed over their heads in a borrowed villa across the bay. Against that: her father's single berth on the final ship. Nell asleep upstairs in the crate that had long ago become a cradle, breathing in the small fast rhythm that Lior knew better than any typeface. Dario's shoulder wound, bandaged and angry. Herself, with no berth at all, never having had one.

"The question," she said, "is whether paper can outrun orders. And I've decided not to wait for the answer, because I already know it doesn't matter. Even slow truth gets there. Even drowned truth washes up."

Dario looked at her for a long moment — the look she had first seen across a joke about kerning months ago, wonder wearing a smuggler's squint — and then he nodded once, and began folding papers into his coat the way only a man who has hidden things from soldiers his whole life can fold.

* * *

They went out at dusk in threes: Dario's crew taking bundles to the taverns and the ropewalk, Elias carrying a bale of "scrap paper" to the chandler's who owed him, Lior walking the harbor steps herself with two hundred sheets under her shawl, heart going like the press, eleven seconds to the pull.

She left them where people gather and don't speak: on the fish-market scales, weighted with a mussel shell; tucked under mooring lines; slid into the letter-racks of the harbor office itself, that temple of official typography, because the irony tasted like brandy. A woman selling chestnuts took one, frowned, began to read, and looked up at Lior with an expression that crossed a market crowd like wind over grain — not understanding yet, but awake.

By full dark, the city was murmuring. She heard her own sentences coming back to her out of doorways, in strangers' mouths, misquoted already, growing beyond her. That was the strange grief and glory of printing, she thought, the thing her father had tried to warn her about and failed to make terrible: once you set words loose, they forget *you* instantly. The type remembers everything; the printer is forgotten. Perhaps that was what the note had really meant, all those months ago. Forget the face. Become the press, not the page.

At the top of the harbor steps she paused, breath ragged, shawl empty, and looked down at the black water where the last ship rode at anchor, riding lights doubled in the swell. Two days until the signing. One night until the loading. Somewhere across the roofs, Inspector Corvane would soon be holding one of her five hundred children up to his beloved lamplight, reading the annex in her justification, admiring the kerning on *amnesty*, and knowing — the way he had known at the typecase, chalk in hand — exactly whose hands had made it.

Behind her, running footsteps on cobbles, uneven, favoring one side. Dario's voice, torn low and urgent:

"Lior — the route's blown. They took Pieter. We have till midnight — maybe less — and I need you to get everyone you love into a boat."
