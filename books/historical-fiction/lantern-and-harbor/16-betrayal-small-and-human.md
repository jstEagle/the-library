## 16. Betrayal, Small and Human

Dario Ferro had learned to read the sea the way other men read ledgers, and on the morning the route broke, the water told him before any man did.

He was mending nets on the *Salt Lark* when he noticed the gulls working the east channel — the wrong channel, the deep one, where nothing schooled this time of year but navy hulls. The birds wheeled in a tight, nervous spiral, and beneath their cries came a sound he felt through the soles of his boots before he heard it: the slow, patient thud of a government steam launch turning against the tide.

His crewmate Piet had been taken three nights ago. Dario had told himself it meant nothing, that Piet knew nothing worth knowing, that a man could be held and questioned and released with nothing worse than bruises and a fine for unlicensed cargo. It was a comfortable story. Dario had been telling himself comfortable stories since he was fourteen years old, which was the age at which he had first smuggled something across the harbor mouth and discovered that fear, handled properly, tasted almost like joy.

The steam launch was not running down smugglers tonight. It was running a pattern. Two passes along the pilings of the old salt wharf, one wide loop past the oyster beds, then a stop — deliberate, hovering — above the sunken skiff where, until last spring, Dario had cached fresh water for his passengers.

He set down the net. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He had always imagined that the moment the route broke, terror would arrive like weather, all at once. Instead it came the way rot came into a hull: quietly, from inside, already long at work.

Piet had broken. That was the shape of it now, plain as chart lines. Not out of cruelty — Piet didn't have the imagination for cruelty — but out of the smallness that lived in every man who had ever been cold and hungry and threatened in a room without windows. They would have shown him his mother's address. They would have described, in courteous detail, what would happen to his sister's children if his memory failed him. And Piet, who could gut a fish and sing after it and weep over his dead father's pipe, would have looked up with wet eyes and said everything. Names. Tides. Times. The little church with the loose slate where families waited. The cove behind the breakwater where Dario ferried them to the tramp steamers that asked no questions.

Everything except the shop. Piet had never known the shop. That was the one mercy in the ledger, and Dario counted it twice.

* * *

He rowed to the cove at dusk, because the passengers were already there — seven of them, a family of marsh fishers and an old schoolteacher and a boy with a broken hand, waiting under the tarpaulin among the salt-crusted barrels, believing in him the way only desperate people believe, wholly and without receipts.

The cove smelled of kelp and tar. In the last light the water lay like poured pewter, and the child in the fisher family was asleep against her mother's collarbone, her thumb hooked in her mouth, and Dario stood in the shallows holding his boat's painter and understood that his life had become a thing measured in other people's breathing.

He did not have the boat tonight to take them out. The *Salt Lark* was watched; he'd seen the navy pinnace loitering off the mole with its lights doused. Tomorrow, maybe. Tomorrow the tide ran high at midnight and there was a gap in the patrol schedule he'd bought from a coaling clerk whose price was flattery and gin. But tomorrow was a word men like Dario used the way gamblers used chips, sliding it forward and hoping no one asked what backed it.

And tomorrow, too, they would come for him. Piet's knowledge had a half-life now. Every hour the ministry let it cool, they were deciding whether to harvest the ferryman himself or simply net the whole route at once — the passengers, the contacts, the safe houses, and threading through all of it like a silver wire through dark water, the print shop on Lantern Row where a girl with ink-stained fingers had argued with him about kerning while her father's press slept upstairs like a heart in a chest.

*Forget the face.* That was the instruction that had come with the child, years of distance ago, back when Nell had been a bundle in a fish crate and the world had still pretended to be orderly. Dario had never managed it. He suspected no one in that house had. You cannot forget a face you have come to love; you can only decide what you will pay to keep seeing it.

He pushed off from the shallows and rowed back toward the harbor lights, and as he rowed he made his arithmetic, and the arithmetic was brutal in the way that honest arithmetic always is.

One night. One boat. Two errands that could not both wait.

* * *

The harbor at night was a city of small betrayals of light. Lanterns swung from the moored fishing fleet, orange and unsteady. The customs house burned its official lamps, white and certain. Between them moved the dark boats, the ones with no papers and no names, and Dario had spent twenty years learning to be one of those lights that showed itself to no one.

He came at the shop from the back, through the alley where rain had carved a gutter down the middle of the cobbles, and he knocked the signal — two, pause, three — and waited in the smell of wet stone and lamp oil until the door opened on its chain and Lior's eye appeared, one eye, wary and bright.

"It's midnight," she whispered.

"I know what time it is." He slipped inside. The pressroom was warm; the banked stove breathed like a sleeping animal, and somewhere above, Elias's floorboard creaked once and went quiet — the old man listening, deciding it was Dario, allowing sleep again. Nell's basket sat by the typecase, and the baby stirred, sighed, did not wake. In the lamplight Lior's hands were smudged to the knuckles, and Dario felt the familiar useless ache, the one that arrived whenever he looked at her too long: the wish to be a different kind of man, one with a trade that left his hands clean and his name on a door.

"Piet talked," he said, because there was no gentle version and she deserved better than gentleness that wasted time.

Her face did something complicated and settled into resolve, which was, he thought, the most Lior expression in the world. "How much?"

"Everything but you. The cove, the church, the tides, the clerk. They'll sweep the route within a day, two at the outside. When they do, they'll pull threads, and some of those threads run through this house."

"Then we move the presses—"

"Listen to me." He took her hands, ink and all. They were smaller than he remembered each time, and steadier. "There are seven people in the cove right now. A family. An old teacher. A boy with a broken hand who is sixteen and has done nothing in his life but get caught in the wrong crowd. If I go for them tonight, I can't also be here when the patrols start working the streets inland. If I stay to watch your door, they sit in that cove until the nets close, and then they are names on an amnesty list that doesn't exist."

"You're telling me you have to choose."

"I'm telling you I've already chosen wrong twice in my head and I can't make it settle either way." His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated it, hated the small human sound of it. "I'm not brave, Lior. I'm practical. Practical men don't get to want everything, and I want everything. I want those seven alive. I want you alive. I want the old man and the baby and the plates in the wall and—" He stopped.

"The plates?" she said softly. "So you know about the plates."

"I know everything about this house," he said. "That's the problem. Knowing things is how men like me die."

She was quiet a moment. The stove ticked. Above them, deliberately loud, a floorboard crossed to the stair, and Elias came down with a shawl over his nightshirt, gray and upright, looking between them the way he looked at a misregistered proof — seeing at once where the layers failed to meet.

"How many in the cove?" Elias asked.

"Seven."

"And how long since the crewman talked?"

"Three days. Maybe less, now that they've finished confirming."

Elias nodded slowly, and Dario watched the old man perform the same arithmetic and arrive, visibly, at the same sum. "Then you row," Elias said. "You take your boat and you take your seven, and you don't look back at this harbor. We are printers, Ferro. We have survived by being boring. We can be boring a few more days."

"You can't," Dario said. "Boring ended for you the day the crate came to the door."

No one answered that. The stove ticked. Nell turned over in her sleep with a small sound like a wave folding, and all three of them looked at her, and Dario saw in the other two faces the exact reflection of his own heart: the unbearable weight of a person who could not yet argue for herself.

"Go," Lior said finally. Her jaw was set. "Row your seven. Then come back for us tomorrow night, whatever's left of the route. Dario." She gripped his sleeve. "Come back."

"I'm a ferryman," he said. "It's the one thing I know how to do."

* * *

The tide was high at half past one and lying about itself, the way spring tides do, showing more water than it meant to give back. He took the small skiff, not the *Salt Lark* — the skiff drew nothing, could slide over the oyster beds where a launch couldn't follow — and he loaded it at the cove in absolute silence, passing children over the gunwale like packages of something holy, the schoolteacher climbing aboard with his books wrapped in oilcloth because even now, even fleeing, the man would not leave his books, and Dario thought: *this is what they never understand, the ministers and the inspectors. You cannot frighten people out of what they love. You can only find out what it is.*

Seven souls plus himself. The skiff sat low. The marsh family sang no songs; the boy with the broken hand held his arm against his chest and watched the dark water with the ancient patience of someone who had already lost more than fear could touch.

They were a mile out, past the breakwater, in the long swell that meant open water and the steamer waiting beyond it like a promise, when the searchlight found them.

It came off the navy pinnace without warning — a hard white column that turned the world to bone, and in it Dario saw his own shadow thrown huge against the sailcloth, and the faces of his seven, bleached and blinking, and the child awake now and not crying, which was somehow worse.

"Skiff! Heave to!" The voice carried flat across the water, amplified by a brass horn, absurdly official against the sound of the sea.

Dario heaved to the oars and did nothing of the kind.

He put the skiff around and pulled. Not for the steamer — too far, the pinnace was faster, he'd known the mathematics before he shipped the oars — but for the shallows north of the beds, where the launch's draft died and its light went blind over the flats. It was the only move. It was a bad move. Both things were true, and he pulled anyway, the oars bending, his shoulders burning, the passengers' weight dragging the stern like regret.

The first shot went wide by ten yards. He heard it crack across the water and felt the passengers flinch as one body. The second came closer, closer in the ugly intimate way of aimed fire, and the third did not miss.

It took him in the left shoulder, high, near the joint, and the world went white and then very simple. There was no pain at first, only the fact of the oar coming free and his own blood arriving on the windward breeze with a smell like hot iron, like the forge where he'd apprenticed at fifteen, like every beginning and ending of his life compressed into a copper coin on the tongue.

"Dario—" the schoolteacher began.

"Sit down," Dario said, and was proud, distantly, of how level it came out. He got the oar shipped one-handed, clamped his right palm over the wound, and steered with his knee against the tiller line, running the flats by memory in the dark while the searchlight swept and slid off the shallows behind them, and the pinnace's bow-wave turned, hesitated, turned away — because the navy did not ground its hulls for smugglers, not even ones who bled well.

They made the steamer at four in the morning, in gray pre-dawn light with gulls screaming overhead, and the mate of the tramp took the seven aboard with quick silent hands, and Dario sat in the skiff tied alongside with his shoulder soaked and stiffening, refusing the ladder, doing sums.

Seven saved. Route burned. Shop warned — yes, warned, but warned was a word for people who could act on it, and printers could not outrun anything; they could only hide what they loved and stand in front of it.

The mate leaned down. "Ferro. You look like drowned rope. Come up, we'll sew you."

"Not yet." Dario fumbled under the thwart for the bailer, for the rag, for anything, and found instead the flask he kept for emergencies, and laughed, briefly and painfully, because this was certainly one. He drank, poured half of it into the wound, and did not scream, mostly. "How long to high water tonight?"

"Tonight? Near eleven. Ferro, whatever you're planning—"

"Nothing," Dario said. "I'm planning nothing. I'm planning the only thing." He looked back toward the harbor, where the customs lamps still burned their confident white, and beyond them, invisible at this distance but perfectly visible to him anyway, the warm window of a print shop on Lantern Row, where a woman was probably awake, probably setting type, probably arguing in her head with the ghost of some typesetter dead a hundred years about the spacing of a word nobody would ever read.

He had told her he was practical. Standing — sitting — bleeding in a borrowed skiff with the route dead behind him and gunfire ahead of him, Dario admitted at last what he had been hiding from himself since the night a fish crate appeared on a printer's doorstep: that he had never once, in all his years of ferries and fares and careful margins, been practical about the things that mattered.

He rowed back in with the sun coming up, one-armed, slow, leaving a thin dark thread on the bright water behind him, and by the time the harbor mouth opened before him he had stopped pretending the choice was hard. Only the cost was hard. The cost was always hard.

The city was waking. Bells somewhere. Soldiers at the customs house in greater numbers than yesterday, he noted, counting them the way he counted everything now, in units of danger. Eleven hours to high water. One shoulder that might or might not keep working. One shop full of people the world had ordered him to forget.

He pulled for his mooring, and the wound beat in him like a second heart, and he thought: *tonight, then. Tonight I tell them all of it — the raid coming, the berths, the arithmetic — and God forgive me, I'll tell them there may be room for only one.*

The lanterns along Lantern Row were going out one by one as morning took the street. All except one.
