## 18. One Berth

The harbor at midnight did not look like a place of escape. It looked like a wound that had finally been allowed to bleed.

Lamps swung on the mastheads of a dozen ships, and beneath them the crowd moved in slow eddies — families pressed together like type locked into a chase, porters hauling trunks, soldiers with fixed bayonets standing at intervals along the quay as if they were punctuation marks inserted into a sentence no one dared finish. The tide was high, black and glossy, carrying on its surface a litter of discarded papers: passports forged and abandoned, letters never sent, proclamations from a government that had already stopped believing in itself. The armistice would be signed at dawn on the other side of the seawall, in a room none of these people would ever enter, and everything they had been — every name, every trade, every loyalty — was being weighed tonight against a single measure: one berth, one body, one seat on the *Meridian*, whose gangplank was down and whose captain had already begun to look at the sky.

Lior came down Harbor Street with Nell against her chest, wrapped in the fisherman's shawl that still smelled of tar and salt, and Mara walked beside her with her hood up and her hands trembling in a way Lior had never seen them tremble, not even when she had told the story of the marshes, of carrying this child through reed-water for three nights while patrol boats swept their lamps across the surface.

"The pier," Mara kept saying, as though to fix it in her own memory. "Which pier?"

"The third. The *Meridian*. Elias is already there."

They had argued until the argument wore smooth as sea glass, and what remained was not agreement exactly but something quieter — the shape of a decision that all of them had touched and none of them could lift alone. One berth. Four names. And in the end it had come apart into arithmetic so simple it felt obscene: Mara could pass through checkpoints only tonight, only now, only in the chaos of the final loading, because by morning her description would be nailed to every customs house from Vessany to the marshes. Nell could not go alone; Dario had offered, and Lior had refused once already, and would refuse again. Elias had a trade the inland resistance needed more than the coast did — plates, presses, hands that knew both — and a network waiting beyond the hills that would shelter him precisely because he was an engraver and not merely a fugitive. And Lior —

Lior had a press. That was the whole of it. She had a press and ten thousand sheets of paper hidden under the floorboards of the drying room, and a document in her memory that the men signing papers at dawn intended to make worthless before the ink on their signatures dried.

"You could still change your mind," Mara said, for the fourth time, or the fifth.

"No."

"You could print there. They have presses everywhere."

"Not this paper," Lior said. "Not these people. You can't scatter truth from a harbor you've already left." She heard how it sounded — like a line from a pamphlet, like something she might have set in lead — and hated it a little, and believed it completely.

The baby woke as they reached the mouth of the pier, woken perhaps by the wind coming off the water, and began to cry in her small furious way, and Lior felt heads turn, and Mara pulled the shawl back from her face and took her daughter out of Lior's arms with the practiced motion of a woman who had done it a hundred times before the world broke, and the crying stopped almost at once, and that was worse than anything. That quiet was worse.

"She doesn't know me," Mara said. Her voice didn't break. It just went flat, like a sheet fed crooked into a press. "She knows you."

"She'll know you," Lior said. "Faces are patient. It's what I learned from a note pinned to a fish crate."

Mara laughed — one syllable, wet — and then they were walking down the planks toward the lamplight and the gangway and the man with the manifest, and Lior stopped counting things.

* * *

Elias Ishikawa had stood on many piers in his life. He had stood on them as a boy watching his father's crates of type go aboard ships bound for capitals he would never see; he had stood on them as a young man delivering engraved plates wrapped in oilcloth to men whose names he wrote down nowhere; he had stood on this very pier three winters ago with an urn of ashes in his arms because the purge had taken his wife and given him back only what the authorities considered sufficient. He had thought, standing there then, that he understood what it cost to remain behind.

He had been wrong. Remaining behind was nothing. Remaining behind while your daughter handed your life's work to someone else — that was the price.

"Take the case yourself," Lior was saying to Dario Ferro, who stood at the foot of the gangway with the traveling typecase on his shoulder and a bruise the size of a plum spreading out from his bandaged shoulder, because of course he had rowed through gunfire two nights ago, because of course he had taken a bullet doing the thing any sensible man would have split in two, and had come out the far side grinning and saying only, "The shoulder was empty anyway. Nothing in it I use." Elias had decided, somewhere in those hours, that he could forgive the smuggler almost anything, including loving his daughter, if only because a man who rowed into a crossfire for strangers would row anywhere for family.

"Inland by the mill road," Dario was saying. "There's a cellar under the tannery at Brevoort that hasn't been found in two wars. Your press goes ahead of you by cart. You follow as a wool merchant. Wool merchants are boring. Be boring, old man."

"I have spent my entire life being boring," Elias said. "It has kept me alive longer than my honesty has."

And there it was — the sentence he had been circling for twenty years, said aloud at last on a pier at midnight, and nobody flinched. His daughter looked at him and he saw that she had heard it too, heard the confession inside the joke, and that she had decided, in her merciless way, to let him carry it rather than take it from him. That was mercy of a kind he hadn't earned. He would spend whatever years remained trying to deserve it.

The letter from the Harbor Commission sat folded in his breast pocket, the single berth, the engraver's exemption, the stamp of a regime that had valued his hands right up until the hour it planned to silence everyone else's. He had carried it for six weeks like a coal sewn into his coat. Now he took it out, and looked at it one last time — the fine engraved border, the official seal, the beautiful, damning typography of the state — and held it out over the black water.

"Wait," Lior said.

He paused.

"Give it here." She took the letter from his hand, unfolded it, and turned it over, studying the reverse as though the answer were printed there. Then she tore it — slowly, deliberately, along the fold, and then again crosswise, and dropped the pieces one by one into the harbor, where they floated for a moment like pale moths and then darkened and sank. "No one uses that berth," she said. "That's the point. If it goes unused, they'll fill it with some customs officer's cousin. But if we tear it up, then no one travels under your name, which means no one is looking for your name, which means—" she gestured at the typecase on Dario's shoulder, at the road inland, at the whole invisible architecture of the underground press they had spent three nights planning— "you're free."

It was elegant. It was the kind of solution he might himself have cut into steel. And it terrified him, because elegance in his experience always preceded catastrophe.

"Lior." He said her name the way he used to say it when she was small and reaching for the composing stick. *Careful. Careful.* "If this goes wrong—"

"If this goes wrong," she said, "then I'll have gone wrong doing the first thing in my life I've done entirely on purpose. Papa. Go be a wool merchant."

He embraced her then, and felt her hold on harder than she meant to, and let her, and did not say any of the thousand things pressing against the back of his teeth — about her mother, about Tomas Verel, about the night twenty years ago when he had traded a man's life for his own silence and had told himself ever since that survival was neutral, survival was simply what happened. None of it needed saying anymore. She had read all his plates. She had printed his confessions without knowing it, years before he made them.

He took the typecase from Dario, settled its weight on his own shoulder — heavier than grief, lighter than guilt, familiar either way — and climbed the plank of the cart that waited at the landward end of the pier. From its bench he turned once. His daughter stood at the rail with the wind pulling her hair sideways and a child's shawl still draped over one arm, forgotten there, and behind her the lamps of the *Meridian* burned in a long reflection across the water like a line of setting type waiting to be justified.

*Be boring,* he told himself, as the wheels began to turn. *Be boring, and live, and build her something worth printing.*

* * *

Dario Ferro had ferried forty-one souls out of Vessany harbor in eleven months, and he had a private superstition that he remembered none of their faces, because remembering faces was how routes died and passengers drowned. Faces were cargo. You loaded them, you delivered them, you forgot them.

He was looking at Lior Whitlock — Ishikawa, he corrected himself, though she'd never once corrected him — and understanding that the superstition had been a lie the entire time. He remembered every face. He simply hadn't cared about any of them enough for the memory to cost anything.

"You could still come," he said, low, beside the rail, while Mara showed the manifest man a dead woman's papers and the baby slept through her first act of fraud. "I can put you off at Brevoort same as him. Two days' sail. No one checks a crew manifest twice."

"And leave the shop? Leave the press?" 

"Yes," he said plainly. "Leave all of it. Paper burns. Plates melt. I've watched a lot of beautiful things burn in this harbor, Lior, and I've never once regretted not being made of paper."

She smiled at that — the crooked smile, the one that started a half-second late on the left side — and put her hand flat against his chest, over the bandage, where his heart was doing something unprofessional. "You're made of rope and stubbornness," she said. "You'd float."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me."

"Dario." Her voice dropped, and the smile went out of it, and he saw the thing underneath, the thing she had been carrying upright all night like a full pail. "When it's done — when the armistice is signed and they come for the printers, because they will come for the printers — don't come back for me. Don't row into the guns again. Take the route north and keep it running. Promise me the route."

"I promise you the route," he said, because he was a liar, but he made himself say it, and he watched her decide to believe him, and he understood that this too was part of her choice — she was distributing her mercy tonight the way he distributed cargo, evenly, to everyone, holding none back for herself.

He kissed her then, briefly, hard, in front of God and the manifest man and the whole indifferent harbor, and tasted salt that wasn't only the sea. Then he stepped back onto the planks, because the gangway was rising, and because staying one second longer would have made him into a different man — a man who stayed — and the route needed the man who went.

"Hey," he called up to her, over the widening gap of black water. "Your kerning's terrible."

"So's your steering," she called back. "One of us should improve."

The gap widened. He stood on the pier with his useless shoulder and his working heart and watched the lanterns swing outward, and he did not forget her face, and for the first time in eleven months he hoped to God the memory would survive him.

* * *

The gangplank rose like a slow eyelid closing.

Lior stood at the rail of the *Meridian* — she had boarded to see them settled, and no one had stopped her, because tonight no one stopped anyone, the rules having dissolved into the general emergency of departure — and looked down at the deck where Mara sat against the mast with Nell in her arms, and watched the moment happen that she had rehearsed and dreaded in equal measure: the baby woke, blinked at the stranger holding her, opened her mouth to cry—

—and stopped. Studied the face. Reached up with one small hand and touched Mara's cheek, exploratory, serious, the way she touched everything new: the typecase, the press handle, the edge of the forbidden plate. As though filing it. As though adding it to the collection of faces she had been assembling all her short life, refusing every instruction the world had given about forgetting.

"There," Lior whispered, to no one, to the wind. "Told you."

Mara looked up and found her through the rigging and the lamp smoke, and neither of them waved, because waving was for departures that might be undone, and this one would not be. Instead Mara lifted her free hand and pressed it flat over her heart, and Lior pressed her own hand flat in answer, and that was the whole farewell, complete, sufficient, a sentence fully justified.

Then Elias was calling up from the departing cart at the shore end of the pier — something about the tannery cellar, something practical, his voice pitched carefully ordinary — and Dario was shouting something about kerning, and the ship's bell rang twice, and the *Meridian*'s canvas filled with a sound like a great page turning, and Lior understood that the time for standing at rails was finished.

She went down the gangway while it was still lowering — against the flow of the last latecomers pushing up, against the sailors shouting, against the whole current of the night, which felt correct, which felt like exactly the direction she had been walking her entire life without admitting it. Her boots hit the planks of the pier. Behind her the gap between ship and dock widened by inches, then feet. Ahead of her the harbor road climbed toward the upper city, toward Harbor Street, toward the shop with its dark windows and its floorboards full of truth, toward the press that had been waiting all night like a held breath.

She did not look back at the ship. She knew what looking back cost; she had watched her father pay it for twenty years. Instead she looked forward, up the hill, at the one light still burning in the upper city — the shop lantern, which she herself had hung in the window at dusk, facing the sea, out of a habit she had not yet examined and did not intend to stop.

The gangplank rose behind her. Somewhere out on the water a child cried once, briefly, and was quieted by a voice Lior did not know and was glad not to.

She put her hand in her pocket and found the scrap of paper she had carried there for months — the original note, *forget the face*, softened now by handling, its ink going gray, its command wearing thin as an old coin. She had meant to burn it. Last night she had stood at the stove with it in her fingers and found that she could not, and had understood instead what it wanted to become: not an instruction but a headline. Ten thousand copies. The true terms. The dawn.

Lior Ishikawa turned her collar up against the wind coming off the water, and began to climb the hill toward the light, and behind her the harbor sealed itself shut, and ahead of her, above the door of the shop, her lantern burned steadily seaward like a single unerasable character set in the face of the night.

She had three hours until the signatures dried.

She intended to use every minute of them.
