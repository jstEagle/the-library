## 22. Lantern and Harbor

The winters had softened in Vessany — or perhaps I had. The wind still came off the water with its old salt edge, the gulls still quarreled over the fish stalls at first light, but the city that had taught me to keep my eyes down now let me lift them without flinching. Ten years is a long time for a harbor to hold its breath. Ten years is long enough to learn how to breathe again.

I was on the roof of the shop that October morning, replacing slates the autumn storms had loosened, when I saw him coming up Rope Walk. I knew his walk before I knew his face: the rolling gait of a man who had spent half a life compensating for decks that moved under him. But the face, when he looked up and found me looking down — that was what stopped my hands.

Gray. All of it. The beard he had worn black as tar when we were young and foolish was silver at the edges, iron through the middle, as if the sea had finally gotten around to salting even Dario Ferro.

"You'll fall," he called up, "and then who will yell at me about kerning?"

"Everyone," I said. "It's become a civic duty."

He laughed, and the sound of it undid something in my chest that had been folded small for years — the careful crease of a woman who had learned not to expect anyone's return.

* * *

He came, he said, to stay two weeks. That was eleven months ago.

In the beginning he kept his boat, the *Mireille*, moored at the north quay out of habit, the way a man keeps a knife under his pillow after a war. He would walk down each morning just to look at her, touch her rail, come back shaking his head like a husband visiting a grave. But the trips grew shorter, then sporadic, then one day I noticed he'd taken to sleeping past the tide instead of rising with it, and I understood the boat had lost its argument.

"I'm too old to run cargo," he told me over supper, in the kitchen that smelled of onions and lamp oil and always, faintly, of ink. "And too honest to run anything else."

"You've never been honest a day in your life."

"That's why I said it. It felt novel."

Nell, who was then nine and sharp as a burin, snorted into her soup. Elias — my father, gray himself now, though he wore it like accumulated light rather than loss — raised one eyebrow above his spectacles and went back to his letters. He had been teaching Nell her alphabet on scraps of misprinted paper, tracing the shapes with her small finger the way he had once traced them with mine, thirty years and one revolution ago.

Dario stayed because there was nothing left to run from, and someone had to be. The armistice had held; the republic that replaced the regime had proven clumsy but alive, which was more than anyone had dared hope; and the men who had once put prices on printers' heads were, most of them, dead or selling real estate inland. The harbor filled again with ships whose names meant trade rather than escape. And every evening, from the bracket above our door, our lantern burned seaward — and every evening, Dario was the one who climbed the step to light it, before he knew exactly why we did it.

"Why *do* you?" he asked me once, in November, when the rain came sideways and the flame gutted in its glass. "Sailors don't need it. The harbor lights have been government-run for years."

"It's not for sailors."

"No," he said, after a while. "I didn't think it was."

He never asked again. That was one of the thousand things I loved about him — his instinct, honed by all those years of smuggling people out under gun barrels, for knowing which doors must stay closed and which must stay open, and never confusing the two.

* * *

My father came home in spring.

I want to write that I saw him from the roof, the way I'd seen Dario — but the truth is humbler and stranger. I was at the case, setting type for a wedding order (the young of Vessany had developed a passion for being married with good typography, as if beauty in small things could inoculate against large uglinesses), when the door opened and did not close again, and I knew by the silence of it — no bell jingle, no hesitation — who stood there.

He had left six years earlier, gray already at the temples, to carry the underground press inland with the last cells of the resistance. We had corresponded in ciphered margins, in letters about weather that were never about weather. Then the letters thinned, then stopped, and for two years I printed his name nowhere and thought it everywhere.

Now he stood in the doorway of the shop he had built, older than the mountains behind the town, thinner, his engraver's hand curled at his side with the palsy the cold inland winters had given him. His spectacles were the same. His eyes were the same.

"Lior," he said. As if testing whether the word still worked.

"Papa."

I crossed the shop floor — ten steps I could have walked blindfolded, had walked blindfolded during raids and blackouts and one memorable fire — and he caught my arms before I reached him, holding me off at the length of his reach, reading me the way he read proofs, tilting his head for the light.

"You've been printing," he said.

"Every day."

"Good." And then his face broke — the great dam of it, all those years of endurance cracking at once — and he pulled me in, and I felt how much smaller he had become, this man who had once seemed to fill the whole architecture of my world, and I held him anyway, harder, because small things are easier to hold onto.

Nell came down the stairs with her hair unbrushed and her shoes on the wrong feet and stopped dead at the sight of us. She had been three when he left. She studied him with the frank, appraising stare she gave everything — type specimens, strangers, the tide line — and then said:

"You're the grandfather. From the letters about weather."

"Yes," Elias said gravely, crouching to her height with his bad knee popping. "I'm afraid I am."

"Did it ever snow where you were?"

"Every year. Terrible snow."

"Good," said Nell, satisfied. "You can tell me lies about weather too. Dario does."

That night we lit two lanterns — the one above the door, and the big harbor lamp on the roof that we had mounted the year the amnesty came, visible from the mole, from the breakwater, from the last stretch of dark water before the open sea. Dario lit both. My father watched from the window, his hand pressed flat against the glass like a man feeling for warmth on the far side of a wall.

"What does it mean?" he asked me quietly, when the others had gone up. "The lantern facing out."

I thought of a fish crate on a doorstep, twenty-odd years ago. A scrap of paper wrapped around a shivering child, printed from a plate my father had cut in another life. *Forget the face*, the note had said. Three words that had failed, utterly and gloriously, to be obeyed.

"It means," I said, "that if anyone out there is looking for us — Mara, or Tomas's ghosts, or anyone the war took and scattered — they should know the door is open. That some faces get remembered whether the world likes it or not."

Elias was silent a long time. Out on the water, the lamp threw its small defiant circle across the swells.

"Tomas would have liked that," he said at last. "He used to say a press was just a lantern that printed."

"He never said that."

"No," my father admitted. "But he would have, if he'd thought of it. I've had years to improve his sayings."

* * *

Nell set her first line of type the morning after her tenth birthday.

She had been begging for a year — properly begging, with arguments prepared, the way children of printers learn to argue — and I had resisted, because the type is heavy for small hands and the composing stick bites fingers that slip, and because I remembered being seven years old and watching my own mother's hands move through the case like birds through reeds, and I wanted Nell's first time to mean something, the way mine had meant something, though I couldn't have said what.

But that morning she appeared at my elbow in her good dress, hair brushed to violence, and announced that she was ready, and Dario — who was mending the treadle on the Heidelberg and pretending not to listen, which fooled no one — said, "Well. The proof of the pudding," and my father cleared his throat in the way that in our family constitutes a formal motion, and I gave in.

"Choose your letters carefully," I told her, putting the stick in her hands. It nearly dropped; she caught it, jaw set, furious with gravity. "Type reads backward. Everything you set will be mirrored until the press turns it right side round. So you're writing the world backwards in order to make it legible. Remember that."

"Why?"

"Because that's the whole art," I said. "And possibly the whole life."

She bent over the case, tongue between her teeth, hunting the compartments. Her hands — Elias's hands, Mara's stubbornness, some third thing entirely her own — moved slowly, deliberately, choosing each letter like a stone for a wall.

She set, with laborious care, five words. When she locked them in the chase, inked the forme, and pulled the lever of the little hand press with both arms and her whole weight, the sheet that emerged was crooked — the line wandered uphill, the spacing gaped like a missing tooth, and the *e* was upside down — and it said:

**NELL ISHIKAWA FERRO PRINTS**

We had never settled her surname. There had been years when giving her any name at all felt dangerous, years when Ishikawa was armor and Ferro was risk, and the question had simply sunk below the surface of our days and lived there, unasked. Now she had answered it herself, in lead, backwards, uphill, with an upside-down *e*.

Dario turned away quickly and became very interested in the treadle. My father removed his spectacles and polished them longer than spectacles require. I picked Nell up and she wriggled free, because she was ten and had important work, but not before I saw her face — flushed, fierce, lit from inside like paper held against a flame.

"It's crooked," she said.

"It's perfect," I said. "Crooked means human. Straight means machine. Print the crooked ones first; you can learn the straight ones later."

She pinned the sheet above her bed that night, and it was still there years afterward, browned and foxed with age, and I never once asked her to take it down. Some errors are the truest record a house keeps.

* * *

The letters from Mara came twice a year, in spring and autumn, carried by whatever ship was sailing from the northern coast where she and her rebels-turned-councilmen had made their uneasy peace with governance. Nell knew her — knew the tall woman with the sea-gray eyes who visited every second summer and held her a moment too long before letting go, and called her *little bird* in a voice that made even Dario look at the floor. We had decided, all of us together one long night when Nell was four, that truth given early and in pieces weighs less than truth given late and whole. Nell knew she had two mothers, one of houses and one of blood, and that the arrangement, like most arrangements worth having, was imperfect and permanent.

This autumn's letter contained the usual news — marsh drainage, school taxes, the endless parliament of the north — and then, at the bottom, in Mara's cramped field-hand script, a single line that made me sit down:

*They are asking, in the schools now, who printed the armistice papers. They want to teach it to the children.*

I read it aloud at supper. Nobody spoke for a moment. Outside, the harbor slapped at its pilings, indifferent and eternal.

"They've got it wrong," Dario said finally. "Half the pamphlets say it was a syndicate. Half say it was a man from the capital."

"They can say what they like," my father said. "History is set loose type. Everyone picks up the letters they need." He looked at me across the table, and his eyes — Tomas Verel's plates, my mother's patience, the whole long lineage of people who chose ink over safety — rested on me with a gentleness that had taken him sixty years to learn. "But you know what happened. You know who stood on the steps."

"I know who stood on the steps," I agreed. My hands, under the table, were not quite steady. Even now, the memory of that dawn could find the exact seam in me and pull it — the soldiers on the mole, the rifles that wavered, the paper leaving my hands like white birds, ten thousand truths falling on the crowd that had come to watch a surrender and stayed, instead, to witness something no treaty had words for.

"Then maybe," said Nell, who missed nothing, "you should tell them. Before they print it crooked."

"Crooked is human," I reminded her.

"Not all crooked," she said, with terrible ten-year-old logic. "Some crooked is just wrong."

From the mouths of apprentices. I laughed until I had to wipe my eyes, and Dario refilled the glasses, and my father began, as he always did after strong feeling, to talk about typefaces, and the evening closed over the moment like water over a dropped coin — gone, but gleaming somewhere down in the dark where I could go back and find it.

* * *

Here is what I do now, every night, and have done for years, and will do until my hands refuse.

At dusk, when the fishing boats come in and the streetlamps stutter awake along the quay, I take the lantern down from its hook above the door of Ishikawa & Daughters — the sign was Dario's joke, then Nell's demand, then simply true — and I carry it up the outside stair to the roof, where the big lamp stands in its iron cradle facing the sea. I trim the wick. I fill the reservoir. I strike the match and cup the flame against the wind, the way Dario taught me the first winter, the way smugglers do, the way anyone does who has ever carried something fragile through weather.

Then I set it burning, and I stand a moment in the dark, and I look out at the black water.

There is no list of names attached to that light. No ledger, no registry, no inscription. It burns for everyone the wars asked us to forget — for Tomas Verel, dead before his granddaughter could speak; for the passengers Dario ferried through gunfire whose names even he never knew; for my mother, three winters dead before the story truly began, whose hands I still see moving through the type case like birds through reeds; for the ones who drowned, and the ones who vanished inland, and the ones who simply could not come back. For every face the world handed me with a note saying *forget*. The light says no. The light says: I remember you, whoever you were, wherever the sea took you, and if you are out there on the water tonight, this flame is aimed at you.

Some nights Nell comes up with me. She is taller every season, all elbows and opinions, and she has learned to trim a wick without wasting oil, and she stands beside me in the wind and looks out at the dark with her gray eyes — Mara's eyes, the sea's eyes — and asks, sometimes, about the years before her, the knock before dawn, the crate, the note. And I tell her. All of it, piece by piece, the way we agreed: truth early, truth in pieces, so it weighs less. She listens the way she sets type — slowly, deliberately, choosing each fact like a stone for a wall.

Tonight she beat me to the roof. I came up the stairs with the lantern and found her already there, framed against the last band of sunset, holding the matches, and beside her on the parapet lay a sheet of paper, fresh from the press, ink still smelling of the plate.

I set the lantern down and read it.

It was a prospectus — her composition, her design, unmistakably hers, the spacing confident now, the lines true. **ISHIKAWA & DAUGHTERS ANNOUNCES AN APPRENTICESHIP**, it began, in a display face my father had cut decades ago and she had unearthed from the back of the case, **OPEN TO ALL PERSONS REGARDLESS OF ORIGIN, PAPERS, OR NAME. APPLICANTS MUST BE ABLE TO READ, OR WISH TO.**

Below, in smaller text, she had set an address: ours. And below that, a single line, centered, in letters chosen one at a time by a girl who had learned to write the world backwards in order to make it legible:

**THE LANTERN IS LIT EVERY NIGHT. FOLLOW IT HOME.**

"Well?" she said, striking the match. The flame caught; the wind tried; she cupped it, steady-handed, and fed it into the wick, and the light leapt up between us and ran out across the darkening water, further than any lamp had a right to reach.

Somewhere out past the mole, past the breakwater, past the last buoy, a sail was standing in toward the harbor — too late for the fishing fleet, too small for cargo, running a light I could not yet make out. Nell saw it at the same moment I did. We stood together at the parapet, grandmother's daughter, smuggler's child, printer's apprentice, two generations of faces the world had tried to make us forget, watching the shape grow against the last of the dusk.

"Do you think they can see us?" she asked.

The lantern burned between us, aimed at the sea.

"They can see us," I said.
