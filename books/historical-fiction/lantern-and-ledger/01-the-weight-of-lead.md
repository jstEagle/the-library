## 1. The Weight of Lead

The type cases in the Almeida shop were older than the city's walls and twice as stubborn, and Wendell Almeida had learned to read them the way priests read scripture—by touch, in the dark, without thinking. Her fingers moved through the compartments the way water moves through a channel it has cut for itself: lower left for lowercase, upper right for caps, the thin wooden divider her father had nailed in place thirty years ago when a journeyman kept confusing the *f* with the long *s*. She could set a line of body text faster than any compositor on Ropemaker's Row, and she could do it while listening to everything else—the bell over the door, the tread of boots outside, the particular quality of silence that meant someone was reading the placard in their window too carefully.

That morning the silence had a name, and the name was Censor.

She knew it before he reached the door. Two soldiers from the garrison had been walking their circuit along Quay Street since dawn, and censors never traveled without soldiers, the way ticks never traveled without dogs. Wendell set down her composing stick, wiped the ink from her fingers onto the rag that lived folded in her apron pocket, and did what she always did: she made herself small.

It was a skill she'd been perfecting since childhood, though no one had ever praised it, because praise would have required noticing her, and being noticed was the one failure she could not afford. Smallness was not weakness. Smallness was craft. She had studied it the way other girls studied lace-making or pianoforte—patiently, obsessively, learning every variation. There was the smallness of lowered eyes and mended hem, which satisfied soldiers. There was the smallness of ink-stained hands and ink-stained jaw, which satisfied censors, because a printer who looked like a printer was a printer doing nothing remarkable, and nothing remarkable was the safest thing in Vessany to be. There was even a smallness of voice, pitched just below attention, useful with tax collectors and landlords and anyone else who dealt in lists.

Her father called it hiding in plain lead. He said it half-admiringly, half-sadly, the way he said most things now, his voice gone soft at the edges like paper left too long in sea air.

"Wendell." His hand found her shoulder in the dimness behind the press. "The gentleman at the door."

"I see him."

"Do you?" A pause. His eyes, milky as skimmed milk where the cataracts had taken hold, turned toward the light anyway, out of habit. Old habits died harder than men did, in Vessany. "I can't see him at all. Describe him."

"Gray coat, city colors. Not garrison. Bureau of Public Morals and Information." She watched through the gap between the press frame and the shelf of quires. The man on the doorstep was smoothing his coat front with both hands, an officious little gesture, the gesture of a man who enjoyed his own authority the way some men enjoyed snuff. "Young. Nervous. The kind who reads your ledgers looking for something to find, because finding nothing would make him look foolish to whoever sent him."

"And we have nothing for him to find?"

The question hung there a moment longer than it should have. In the corner, beneath the stack of blank folio paper, sat three reams of stock her father had bought from a dealer whose name they did not put in writing, and the price they'd paid suggested the stock had not come through any customs house Wendell knew of. It was nothing. Everyone in the paper trade bought gray stock; half the pamphleteers in Vessany printed on it. It proved nothing except that the Almeidas paid less than they should, which made them honest like everyone else.

"We have nothing," she said. "We print bills of lading, funeral notices, and the almanac. We are the dullest shop in the district."

"The almanac." Her father smiled faintly. "Four thousand years of astronomy and weather lore, and not one word in it a man can be hanged for."

"That's the ambition, Papa."

He laughed, quiet and dry, and went forward to meet the censor, because it was proper that the master of the shop receive inspectors and the daughter remain among the type cases, invisible, industrious, unremarkable. She heard the doorbell, heard her father's welcome—warm but not obsequious, the tone of a man with nothing to hide and too much dignity to pretend otherwise—and then the two voices settled into the low murmur of inspection.

Wendell picked up her graver.

* * *

There were perhaps six people in Vessany who could cut a new typeface by hand, and Wendell Almeida believed—with the private, unfashionable vanity she permitted herself exactly once a day, usually while sharpening her tools—that she might be the best of them. It was not a skill you advertised. Advertising it would mean commissions, commissions would mean clients, clients would mean names in ledgers, and names in ledgers were how people in this city ended up face-down in the harbor with their pockets turned out.

So her work went out unsigned. The elegant italic she'd cut two winters ago appeared in wedding invitations across the merchant quarter and no one knew whose punch had struck it. The sturdy text face she'd finished last spring—she thought of it privately as the *harbor* face, broad-shouldered, salt-hardened—now carried the shipping notices of four different printing houses, none of which suspected the hands that had made their letters. She sold her punches through a broker who took a quarter and asked no questions, and in return she asked no questions either, and between them they maintained the beautiful fiction that good letters simply happened, like weather.

Her father had taught her the cutting when she was nine, back when his eyes were still sharp enough to catch her errors and cruel enough to say so. *You're not carving a letter,* he'd told her, guiding her wrist over the steel blank. *You're carving the space around a letter. The letter is what remains when you've removed everything that isn't it.* She had thought it a strange thing to say about metal. She understood it now. Every character she cut was an act of removal, of subtraction, of deciding what did not belong. The counter of an *o* was a held breath. The serif was a small courtesy extended to the reader's eye.

And lately—this was the thought she did not say aloud, not even to herself in full sentences—lately the whole city felt like a page her father had described. Everyone removing. Everyone subtracting. Deciding what could not be said and cutting it away until what remained was so thin, so courteous, so empty of anything that wasn't it, that the words hardly weighed anything at all. Bills of lading. Funeral notices. The almanac.

She worked the graver while the censor's voice drifted through the shop. He was asking about circulation. He always asked about circulation, as if the number of copies told you whether a printer was loyal, as if truth were measured in tonnage.

"—the almanac, principally. Twelve hundred copies, distributed through Ferro & Sons at the Corn Exchange." That was Tomas's father's firm, still trading under the old name even though old Ferro was dead and the sons were one son. "Bills of lading as commissioned. Funeral notices as commissioned."

"You do no original publication? No commentary?"

"My daughter cuts type, sir, and I am going blind. Between us we manage to print what other people write. I assure you, no one pays us for our opinions, because we haven't any worth the paper."

A pause. Then, mildly, with the little laugh of a man establishing rapport before the real business: "Everyone has opinions, Master Almeida. The law concerns itself only with those who express them."

Wendell's graver slipped. A hairline scratch ran across the blank steel, ruining the punch she'd been three days on. She stared at it, breathing slowly, and told herself the anger was unreasonable. The man hadn't threatened anyone. He'd said it almost kindly. That was the worst of it—that the machine no longer needed cruelty, only clerks, only mild voices, only men who smoothed their coats and spoke of the law the way one speaks of rainfall, inevitable and impersonal and nobody's fault.

She set the ruined punch aside and began another.

Through the doorway she could see them now without turning her head fully, using the reflection in the tin sheet her father had hung by the stove years ago so he could watch customers while facing his case—a trick he'd taught her without ever explaining why a printer might need to watch customers sideways. The censor was a narrow man with a clerk's stoop and a red ribbon in his buttonhole, the bureau's color. He had a notebook out. Of course he had a notebook out. Notebooks were the bureau's true product; the printing houses merely supplied the raw material.

"He keeps a list," her father had told her once, years ago, of the censor who'd preceded this one. "Every shop, every press, every apprentice. They think they are cataloguing us. One day, Wendell, someone will open those notebooks and discover they have written our history for us—every man they watched, every word they feared. Fear is a very accurate index."

She had been sixteen then and had thought it just another of her father's aphorisms, the kind he dispensed with his tea. Now, watching the red ribbon bob as the young censor bent to inspect the press frame, she understood that the aphorisms had all along been instructions.

*Be dull. Be small. Be the space around the letter.*

* * *

The censor stayed forty minutes. He examined the press—a Stanhope, secondhand, its iron frame pitted with rust along the base but sound where it mattered. He examined the type cases, running one finger along the dividers with the delicacy of a man testing wine. He examined the ledger, in which Wendell's careful entries recorded every job in her roundest, most obedient hand: *Mr. Calloway, 200 bills of lading, paid. Widow Serrano, funeral notice for her mother, unpaid, extend credit.* He paused longest at the almanac proofs pinned above the slop stone, and Wendell watched in the tin and saw him read the month of March—tides, saints' days, expected frosts, a note on the migration of the herring—with the concentration of a scholar decoding scripture.

Then he straightened, closed his notebook, and delivered the ritual.

"The Bureau thanks Master Almeida for his cooperation. You will be advised of any additions to the schedule of restricted terms." From his coat he drew a printed card, already prepared, already bearing the shop's name in a clerk's hand—which meant they had known they would visit today, which meant the visits were scheduled across the whole district like doses of medicine—and laid it on the counter. "Compliance is assumed. Inspection confirms it. Good day."

Her father saw him out. Wendell remained at her case, setting a line of nothing in particular, her back to the room, her hands steady through sheer discipline. The bell rang. The boots receded up Quay Street. The soldiers' footsteps faded toward the Customs House, and the ordinary noises of the port came flooding back into the silence—gulls, rigging, a cart driver swearing at his mule, the distant hammering from the shipwrights' yard.

Only then did she cross to the counter and pick up the card.

It was a short list. It was always a short list; the bureau understood that long lists taught printers what to avoid and short lists taught them what to fear. Six terms, each one a door slammed shut. *Committee. Representation. Rights of—* the phrase broke off mid-word, forbidden even in its forbidden form, which struck Wendell as either bureaucratic laziness or a kind of genius. And at the bottom, newly added, in fresher ink than the rest:

*Corvel.*

She stood very still.

Minister Corvel. The name had been in every paper three weeks ago—*Minister Corvel resigns his office for reasons of health*—and then in none of them at all, which was its own kind of announcement. Men did not simply stop existing in Vessany unless someone very powerful needed them to, and the speed of the forgetting had been remarkable even by the standards of this city, where memory was a perishable commodity like fish. Resigned for reasons of health. Gone from his offices within the week. Gone from the society pages, gone from the parliamentary record, gone from the mouths of men who had dined at his table. Wendell had noticed the erasure the way she noticed everything—sideways, silently, filing it in the part of her mind where she kept things that were dangerous to know.

Now the bureau had printed his name on a card and sent it to every print shop on Quay Street, and the message required no translation. *This face is forgotten. Assist us.*

"Papa," she said quietly. "They've added Corvel to the list."

Her father had come back from the door and was standing with his head tilted, listening to the street the way blind men listen, his whole face turned toward the sound of the city. When she spoke, he didn't startle. He seemed, if anything, to have been waiting for it.

"Is he dead, do you think?"

"They said his health failed."

"Yes." He smiled without any humor in it at all. "Mine is failing too, by their measure. I see nothing, I print nothing, I know nothing. Very healthy." He held out his hand, and she placed the card in it, and he read it with his fingertips as though the words might be embossed—they were not—and gave it back. "Burn that. No—no, keep it. Keep it in the drawer with the others. We should have a complete collection by now. An archive of everything we are not allowed to remember."

"Papa."

"I'm joking, Wendell." He wasn't. Or rather, he was joking the way tired men joke, which is to say he was telling the truth in a costume. He crossed to the window and stood looking out—he could still make out light and shadow, shapes of motion, the world reduced to what it had been before he'd learned to read—and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

"There's a ship in from Marivaux this afternoon," she said at last, because someone had to be practical. "Ferro wants his notices reset for tomorrow. And the widow Serrano still hasn't paid for her mother's notice, and won't, and I'll set it anyway."

"Of course you will." He turned from the window. Even clouded, his eyes found her with unsettling accuracy, as if blindness had taught him to aim by some other sense entirely. "You know what your grandmother used to say? That the Almeidas were in the business of other people's words. That we were the riverbed and everyone else's thoughts were the water passing over us. She meant it as a warning, I think. Rivers don't get to choose what flows through them." He tapped the counter once, softly, with one knuckle. "But I sometimes think she had it backward. The riverbed chooses the course. Without us, the water goes nowhere."

Wendell looked down at her hands. Ink in the knuckle creases, a graver callus thick as a coin on her right middle finger, the fine white scars of a dozen slips across her thumb. Hands that had touched more words than she could count and had signed none of them.

"Then let's hope," she said, "that nothing interesting flows through us."

Her father laughed and went back to his case, feeling for the types by touch alone, and the shop settled into its afternoon rhythm—the creak of the press, the tick of type into stick, the gulls outside crying over the harbor like creditors. Wendell set Ferro's shipping notices line by line, and her hands did their flawless work, and she was small, and safe, and invisible, and she believed it completely, the way one believes in fair weather.

Three days later, in the rain, a stranger would leave a bundle on their doorstep, and the water would begin to rise.

But that evening there was only the almanac to proof, and the lamp turned low, and her father humming an old song about the river at Beloz as he locked the cases for the night, and Wendell Almeida blew out the lantern believing—as she would believe, right up until the moment she couldn't—that the smallest life in Vessany was also the safest one, and that a woman who made herself into the space around the letter could go on living there forever, untouched, unwritten, free.
