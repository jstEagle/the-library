## 2. A Bundle at the Door

The rain came down on Vessany the way it always did in late autumn—not falling so much as settling, a grey wool wrapped around the rooftops, dripping from every eave and awning until the gutters ran brown with ink-wash and coal dust. From the window of the print shop, where I sat sorting type by candlelight because my father's eyes could no longer be trusted with the small sorts, the harbor was invisible. The ships, the cranes, the customs house with its new flag—all of it swallowed. Only the sound remained: rain on slate, rain on the canvas awning over our door, rain filling the world to the brim.

I have often thought since that the weather knew what was coming. Rain is good for erasing things. It washes footprints, blurs faces, turns the whole city into one long smear of grey. Whoever chose that night to leave a child on our doorstep understood the weather the way I understood lead.

It was past ten o'clock. My father had gone up to bed an hour before, moving slowly on the stairs, one hand on the wall, because his knees had begun to complain the way old presses complain—predictably, painfully, at every joint. I stayed below out of habit more than necessity. There were wedding announcements for the Marchetti family to finish setting, and I liked the shop at night. Liked the smell of oil and paper and the faint metallic breath of the type cases, liked the tick of the candle as it burned down. In the dark, the letters seemed to hum in their compartments, thousands of them, patient as seeds waiting for someone to decide what they would grow into.

The knock—or rather, not a knock. A sound like something soft striking the door. Then footsteps, quick ones, splashing away through the flooded lane.

I set down my composing stick and listened. Vessany after dark was not a place where sounds went unexamined. Soldiers patrolled the quay in pairs; the censor's men made their rounds of the printers' row with lanterns and lists. A stranger running from our door was either drunk or afraid, and neither kind of stranger brought good news.

I lifted the bar and opened the door a hand's width.

The bundle lay against the threshold, half sheltered by the overhanging sign that read ALMEIDA & SON — PRINTERS, though there was no son, only me, and the paint had faded until even that modest lie was dissolving. The bundle was wrapped in a man's coat, good wool once, now soaked dark as wet earth. It moved. That was the first thing I understood—not saw, *felt*, the way you feel a wrongness in a room. It moved, and then it made a sound: a thin, furious mewling, the complaint of an infant who has been cold for some time and intends to say so.

A note was pinned to the coat with a common brass pin. The packet beneath it was heavier—oilcloth, tied with cord, sealed with wax that had gone soft in the damp but held.

I should have called for my father then. Instead I crouched in the doorway with the rain blowing in past me, and I read the note. Three words, written in a quick, slanted hand on paper too fine for the message:

*Forget this face.*

* * *

"Turn it over to the authorities." My father stood at the bottom of the stairs in his nightshirt, hair wild, squinting at the child in my arms as if she—or he—were a misprint that had somehow gotten onto the press bed. "First thing tomorrow. The garrison, or the parish wardens, whichever opens first. Wendell, we are not equipped for this."

"We're not equipped for much," I said. "We took the Corvel funeral broadside with two days' notice."

"That was paper." He came closer, holding his candle high, and the light wavered across the small face—red with crying, chapped at the cheeks, perhaps three months old. A boy. "That is a life, Wendell. A life left in the rain at the door of a printing shop in a city where men disappear for less than this. Whoever left him wanted him found by us specifically, or wanted him found by anyone at all, and I don't know which possibility frightens me more."

I didn't answer right away, because the boy had stopped crying. He was looking up at me with the grave, unfocused stare infants have, as if I were a great blurred distance he was trying to bring into view. His fist had closed around my thumb with a grip that seemed, given his size, absurdly determined.

"The authorities," I said finally. "Which authorities? The garrison answers to the Intendant, who answers to the palace. The wardens answer to the city council, which hasn't agreed on anything since spring. And if the republicans get their committee into the customs house next month—" I shrugged. "There isn't one authority anymore, Father. There are several, and they don't like each other, and a baby handed to any of them becomes a question someone else gets to answer."

"Then we ask questions ourselves. We find who left him. Someone will come looking."

"Someone left a note instead of a name." I nodded toward it, still lying where I'd dropped it on the counter. "'Forget this face.' That's not a plea, Father. That's an instruction. Whoever wrote it knows exactly how memorable this child might be—to whom, I don't know. But nobody writes *forget* about a child unless remembering would be dangerous."

My father lowered himself onto the stool by the press, the one worn smooth by forty years of his weight, and looked at the oilcloth packet where I'd set it beside the ledger. His eyes, clouded as they were, went to the seal.

"You opened it?"

"No. It's not mine."

"It's on your workbench."

"It's on *your* workbench," I said. "And it was on your doorstep, which makes it, by the laws of this city, no one's business but ours. Which is precisely why I haven't touched it. Sealed packets left in the night have a way of being evidence, and evidence has a way of being fatal."

He was quiet a while. The candle fluttered when the door let in a draft; I had shut it, finally, and barred it, and the boy had begun to doze against my shoulder with the boneless trust of the very young. He smelled of rain and milk and something faintly herbal—somebody had rubbed his gums with clove oil recently. Somebody had been taking care of him, hours ago. Somebody with fine paper and a fast hand and shoes that splashed away through the flood.

"Wendell." My father's voice had changed, gone quieter, the voice he used for corrections rather than arguments. "You're holding that child the way your mother held bread dough. Like something you've already decided to keep."

"I'm holding him because he'll cry if I put him down, and if he cries, the neighbors hear, and if the neighbors hear, we have a story to explain."

"And what story will we explain when the censor comes? Because he comes, Wendell. You know he does. Twice since summer. What do we say then? That we found an infant among the type cases like a stray apostrophe?"

"We say nothing, because we won't have him by then. Tomorrow we take him somewhere sensible."

"Where?" he asked, and the word hung there between us, and neither of us filled it.

Because there was nowhere sensible. I turned it over in my mind while the boy breathed against my collar—the orphanage on Saint Ferreol's Lane, run by the diocese, which reported births to the parish register, which the Intendant's clerks read. The foundling wheel at the hospital, where two of every five children died of the fever before winter was out—I had printed the hospital's own annual report, I knew the numbers better than anyone outside its walls. Handing him to a neighbor meant handing him to gossip, and gossip in Vessany traveled faster than any press I'd ever worked.

And underneath all of it, the other thought, the one I did not say aloud: *forget this face.* If I gave him away, I would be obeying an instruction written by a stranger in the rain. And I had spent twenty-four years perfecting the art of being overlooked, unnoticed, unremarkable—and some stubborn, buried part of me rose up at the idea that a scrap of fine paper could tell me what to forget.

"He can sleep in the type loft tonight," I heard myself say. "There's a blanket with the rag stock. Tomorrow we decide."

My father looked at me for a long moment. Then at the packet. Then he sighed the sigh of a man watching a forme lock up crooked and knowing the correction will cost him the morning.

"The loft," he agreed. "One night. And Wendell—" he raised the candle, and in its light his failing eyes caught the flame like water catches it, "—whatever is in that packet, we do not open it. Whatever it says, whoever sent it, the moment we read it, we stop being bystanders. Do you understand me? Reading makes us responsible. That is the whole trouble with literacy."

"Yes, Father."

"I mean it."

"I know you mean it. Go back to bed."

He went, slowly, pausing on the third stair to look back at the two of us—the printer's daughter and the foundling—in the light of the guttering candle. He looked, for just a moment, very old and very frightened, and I loved him for going upstairs anyway, because going upstairs was his way of trusting me, and trust was about to become the rarest currency in Vessany.

* * *

The type loft was a low space above the composing room, reached by a ladder, where we stored paper stock and the great cases of display type too heavy to keep below. I spread a blanket over the flat top of a case of forty-eight-point roman—the biggest letters we owned, bought secondhand from a bankrupt theater printer—and laid the boy on it. He slept the way children sleep who have cried themselves empty, utterly, his mouth slack, one arm flung above his head.

I told myself I would go down and bank the stove and sleep. I told myself all the sensible things.

Instead I sat on the ladder with the oilcloth packet in my lap and the boy's breath rising and falling behind me, and I looked at the seal.

Wax, deep red, cracked by the damp along one edge. And pressed into it, half legible, a device: a pillar and an open book. I knew that device. Every printer in Vessany knew it, because it appeared on the front matter of every official document the Ministry of Records ever issued, and we had set copies of those documents for clients for years. It was the mark of the Minister's own office.

Minister Corvel. Dead six weeks, if the rumors were true—found floating near the grain quays with his rings still on his fingers, which everyone agreed proved it was not robbery, and nothing else about it was agreed at all. The palace called it an accident. The republicans called it murder and marched about it. The newspapers—we printed two of them—had been instructed in writing to refer to the matter as *closed*, and both editors had paid us promptly to make sure the instruction was followed.

I sat in the dark loft with the minister's seal under my thumb and the rain hammering the roof, and I felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does when a ship's rope slips its mooring.

Someone had left the dead minister's papers on the doorstep of a tiny print shop at the wrong end of Printer's Row. Not to the great houses. Not to the republican committee, whose agents would have paid in gold. To us—to a shop that survived on wedding announcements and customs forms, run by a blind man and his careful daughter.

*Forget this face,* the note said. But notes pin two things together, and only one of them was the child.

Behind me, the boy stirred and murmured in his sleep—a small sound, nothing like a word yet, nothing like anything except life insisting on itself. And I understood, sitting there in the dark with history warm and sealed in my hands, that whatever I decided about the packet, I had already decided about him. Somewhere between the doorstep and the loft, between the crying and the sleeping, the stranger's instruction had failed. I had looked at his face. I had memorized it without meaning to: the chapped cheek, the furious cry, the grip on my thumb.

You cannot forget a face you have already begun to protect.

I put the packet on the shelf beside the paper stock, unopened, exactly where my father could see it in the morning and disapprove of. Then I lay down on the boards beside the type case, close enough that the boy's breath moved the hair at my temple, and I watched the rain-shadow shift on the rafters until the candle died.

Tomorrow we would decide. Tomorrow we would be sensible.

But in the last moment before sleep took me, I heard again the footsteps splashing away through the flooded lane, quick and desperate, and I thought: whoever you were, you chose us. And God forgive me, some cold clear part of my heart already wanted to know why.
