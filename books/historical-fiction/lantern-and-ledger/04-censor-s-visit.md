## 4. Censor's Visit

The knock came at half past nine, which meant it was official. Customers knocked like apology—soft, hesitant, apologetic of the interruption. The Bureau knocked like it owned the door, because as far as the Bureau was concerned, it did.

Wendell had the baby against her shoulder before her father finished crossing the shop floor. She'd learned his rhythms in three weeks: the way he stirred before dawn and slept deepest at midday, the small fist he kept pressed to her collarbone even in sleep, the terrible, perfect silence that always seemed to arrive at the worst moment. He was silent now, thank God or whatever governed such luck, mouth open against her neck, breath damp and even.

"Wendell." Her father's voice was low, pitched for her alone. "The loft."

"I know."

"The papers—"

"Are already where they should be." They weren't, quite—the packet sat inside the hollowed case of fourteen-point Egyptian, under a false bottom she'd cut herself two nights ago, telling herself it was for mislabeled sorts and not admitting what she was really preparing for. But it would take him thirty seconds to finish the job, and she needed twenty less than that.

She went up the ladder one-handed.

* * *

The type loft was not a room so much as an afterthought—a platform above the composing stone, reached by a ladder built for someone with more patience and fewer bones. Type cabinets lined its walls in ranks, each drawer labeled in her father's careful hand from thirty years ago, when his eyes could still find the labels without lamplight. Dust hung up here like weather. Light came through a single window facing the alley, glazed with grime enough to turn noon into dusk.

Wendell knelt in the narrow aisle between cabinets, the baby pressed to her chest, and did not breathe while the knock came again.

Below, through the floorboards, she heard her father open the door.

"Master Almeida." A voice she didn't know—young, dry, with the particular flatness of a man who had rehearsed sounding unimpressed. "Censor Second-Class Bréhal. Bureau of Public Morals and Information. We are conducting routine inspections of licensed print establishments."

"Routine," her father said warmly. "Of course. Come in, come in. Mind the ink on the third stone; it never dries the way the manufacturers promise."

Wendell eased back against the cabinet and let her body go quiet the way she'd taught herself to go quiet on the streets when soldiers passed—invisible, unremarkable, a woman made of furniture. She had spent her whole life being overlooked and had thought of it as safety. Now she understood it was also armor, and that armor only worked if you never moved inside it.

The baby shifted. She cupped the back of his head with her palm, felt the downy softness there, the astonishing smallness of his skull beneath her hand. *Forget this face*, the note had said. As if forgetting were a thing you could decide, like choosing a font size.

Below, boots on the shop floor. The creak of weight settling against the composing stone.

"You operate alone," Bréhal said. It wasn't a question, but her father answered it like one.

"Myself, my daughter, and a journeyman, Tomas Ferro, who is presently at the paper merchant's. Four hands between three people, Censor, which is why our output is modest and our debts are not."

A dry sound that might have been a laugh. Papers rustled. "Your license permits general commercial work. Jobbing print. Bills of lading. Playbills." A pause. "No political matter."

"No political matter," her father agreed.

"Yet your name appears on a distribution list."

Wendell's heart stopped against her ribs. Below her feet, she could feel her father's stillness come up through the boards like cold through stone.

"Distribution list," he repeated, in a tone of mild, aging confusion. It was masterful. She had watched him talk to tax assessors and guild inspectors for years and had always known, underneath the performance, that he was afraid of them. There was no fear in his voice now. Only the weary courtesy of a man who has been interrupted during work.

"A list of shops receiving certain pamphlets. Seditious material circulated last month. *An Address to the Working Printers of Vessany.*"

"Ah." Her father exhaled, and Wendell could hear the shrug in it. "That. Yes. A boy left copies in my doorway, Censor, along with half the street, I expect. I set them under the press stone for kindling. You're welcome to look. I believe some are still there, stuck together with ink, good for nothing but burning—which, if the pamphlet's authors had any sense of irony, they might appreciate."

Silence below. Then the sound of the press stone's cover scraping back, pages being peeled apart.

"You did not report receipt."

"I did not read it either, Censor. My eyes—" and here his voice did something she had never heard it do before: it used his failing sight as a weapon. "I have been a compositor for forty-one years, and for the past three I can no longer read my own type without a glass and strong light. Small text is lost to me. Large bills of sale, broadsides, headlines—I manage. Pamphlets set in nine-point? They might as well be written in water." Another rustle of pages. "If the Bureau wishes to inspect what I cannot see, I welcome it. I will even provide the lamp oil."

Wendell pressed her forehead to the cabinet wood. She had spent years grieving her father's eyes, mourning each dimming season as a loss. She had never once considered that blindness could be a performance of loyalty, an alibi built out of infirmity. He had turned his affliction into a shield and stood behind it holding the door open for inspection.

*This is what it costs*, something in her observed, cool and distant. *He has made himself smaller too.*

The baby yawned against her collarbone. She curled around him.

Below, footsteps crossed toward the type racks. Toward the wall. Toward the ladder.

"We'll have a look at your cases," Bréhal said.

"Please do." Her father's voice followed him like a courteous shadow. "The upper cases are my display faces—no sense hiding anything there, it's all advertisements. The lower drawers hold the jobbing sorts. The case nearest the ladder is fourteen-point Egyptian; I use it for auction bills. Heavy stuff. Mind your back lifting the galleys."

It was, Wendell realized, the most precise map of the shop he had ever given anyone—and every coordinate of it was true except the one that mattered. The false bottom in the Egyptian case sat four inches beneath the real bottom, and unless a man lifted the entire galley frame out and looked up into the case from below, he would find nothing but lead.

Boots on the first rung of the ladder.

Wendell flattened herself behind the corner cabinet, into the dead space where two cases met at right angles, and pulled her skirts around her like a tent. The baby's eyes opened—dark, alert, absurdly calm—and looked directly up at her.

*Don't*, she begged him silently. *Not now. Not yet. You have your whole life to notice things.*

Bréhal's head rose through the hatch. She saw him from the side and above: a narrow face, a clerk's complexion, hair oiled flat, a leather folio under one arm. His eyes went across the rows of cabinets with the efficient sweep of a man who had inspected a hundred shops and found the same dust in all of them.

He stepped fully onto the loft. Three paces. Four. He stopped at the display cases, drew out a drawer of pearl-type, held a line up to the grimy window light, put it back. He walked the aisle—her aisle—close enough that she could have hooked a foot around his ankle, close enough that she could smell the camphor on his coat, and stopped at the far end where the Egyptian case sat.

Her lungs would not work. The baby, God bless him or curse him or both, lay absolutely still against her chest, watching the dust motes with the solemn attention of a scholar.

Bréhal lifted the galley—not out, just tilted, checking the weight, checking the label against his folio. "Egyptian, fourteen point," he murmured, writing something. Then he set it back exactly where it had been, squared it to the rail with two fingers, and turned around.

On his way back to the hatch he paused at the window. Wiped a circle in the grime with his thumb. Looked down at the street, where the morning market was assembling itself in defiance of the price of bread.

"Quiet shop," he said, to the window or to her father below. "Quiet street. That won't last."

"They rarely do," her father called up. "Will there be anything else, Censor?"

Bréhal descended. Through the floorboards, Wendell tracked him by sound—footsteps to the center table, the scratch of a nib, the snap of the folio.

"One administrative matter." Paper sliding across wood. "A revised schedule of proscribed terms, effective immediately. All licensed houses must post it where compositors may consult it. Words removed from lawful use in printed matter." A beat. "You'll find the list has grown."

"How much has it grown?" her father asked.

"It is now four pages." The dry voice might have been embarrassed; it might have been proud. In Vessany that winter, the difference had become impossible to distinguish. "Sign the acknowledgment."

Wendell heard the pen scratch. Heard the door open. And then Bréhal paused on the threshold, and said, in a tone stripped of all its clerical boredom:

"Master Almeida. A word of professional advice, since we are both tradesmen. When the city turns—and it will turn, it is only a question of which direction—shops like yours become very important very quickly. Everyone will want you. Both sides. Remember that ink does not ask permission. It simply records. The man who controls what it records controls everything after." A pause. "Choose your records carefully."

The door closed.

* * *

She stayed in the loft until her father called her name—twice, softly, the second time with the particular weariness of a man who knows his daughter is listening to every word and waiting for permission.

When she came down the ladder, the baby on her hip, he was standing at the center table reading the proscribed list with his face an inch from the paper, which was the only way he could read anything now. He did not look up.

"Four pages," he said.

"Is it alphabetical?"

"Nearly." He traced a line with one cracked fingernail. "*Citizen.* They've banned the word *citizen*. And *famine*—you may not print the word *famine*, though the grain barges sit empty at the quay and children beg at the fish market. You may starve, Wendell, but you may not describe it." He straightened, pressing his knuckles into the small of his back. "Where were you?"

"In the loft. Behind the corner cases."

"He passed within an arm's reach of you."

"Yes."

Her father finally looked at her—at her, then at the baby, whose fist had found the collar of her dress and gripped it with the unreasonable strength of infants. Something moved across his face that was not anger, though it wore anger's clothes. Grief, maybe. Or arithmetic: the sum of what he could already see coming.

"That man wrote down the serial number of our press," he said quietly. "Did you see? While he pretended to check the Egyptian case, he copied the maker's plate. Every registered press in Vessany is now inventoried, and ours has been noted twice in one month. First the pamphlet list, now this visit. We are in the ledger, Wendell. Twice."

"Then we're already suspects. It hardly matters how many times they write us down."

"It matters enormously." He lowered himself onto his stool, suddenly old, his hands flat on the table beside the banned words. "There is a difference between being suspected and being watched. Suspects are forgotten. Watched men are visited again, and each visit costs something—a favor, a signature, a compromise. They will come back, and next time they will not send a clerk who checks labels."

The baby, sensing the gravity in the room or simply bored by it, began to fuss. Without thinking, without deciding, Wendell began the slow walk-and-sway that calmed him, tracing a circuit around the composing stone, past the press, past the case of Egyptian type where a murdered minister's testament lay hidden four inches beneath the surface, waiting like a coal.

"Father," she said, when she had completed one full circuit and found her voice steady. "What did he mean? At the door. About ink recording."

Her father was quiet for a long moment. He picked up the four-page list of forbidden words and held it at reading distance, though she noticed his eyes had gone somewhere beyond the paper, to the window, to the market street, to the gray light over the harbor where three warships sat at anchor because the government no longer trusted its own city.

"He meant," her father said slowly, "that whoever prints first, prints forever. That is the oldest truth of our trade, and that clerk knows it better than any man in the Bureau. Why else ban words? A word is only dangerous because of what happens when it is written down—because ink makes a thing *real*, and reality, once printed, cannot be recalled." He set the list down. "They are not afraid of words, Wendell. They are afraid of the record. And they have just handed us, in their own hand, a list of everything they cannot afford to have recorded."

Wendell stopped walking. The baby had gone still against her, watching her face with those grave dark eyes—the eyes, she thought not for the first time, of the man whose blood ran in him, whoever that man had been, whatever minister's bed or secret marriage or desperate arrangement had produced him. *Forget this face.* She carried it everywhere now. Forgetting had turned out to be impossible.

"So we post their list," she said. Her own voice surprised her—level, almost pleasant, discussing trade. "We nail it to the wall where Tomas can consult it. And every word on it—"

"—becomes a word we know matters," her father finished. He looked at the list, then at his daughter, and for a moment she saw him clearly as he must have been at thirty: a man who had chosen a craft rather than a fortune and never regretted it until perhaps this exact morning.

"I am too old for what you are thinking," he said. "And you are too young for it. And that child is too small for any of it. I want that understood between us before I say the rest."

"Understood."

He sighed, and rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands, and when he took them away his gaze had settled on the press itself—the great iron frame of it, patient as a cathedral.

"There is a word on that list I have been thinking about since the clerk left. Not *citizen*. Not *famine*." He tapped the page once. "*Testimony.* They've banned the word *testimony*, Wendell. Think about what a government must be, to outlaw the act of bearing witness."

He rose, stiffly, and took his hat from its peg.

"Watch the shop. I am going to call on Father Anselme, who owes me a debt from the flood year, and whom I have not needed until today." At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around. "And Wendell—if a journeyman named Ferro returns while I'm out, be careful what you say to him. I counted the impressions on our press pad this morning. We ran forty sheets of auction bills Tuesday. The pad shows sixty."

* * *

She stood a long time in the empty shop after the door shut, the list of banned words on the table, the baby heavy on her hip, the testament sealed in its lead-lined dark.

Sixty impressions. Twenty sheets of something else, run off in the night or the grey hour before opening, when only one other person in the world had keys to the shop.

Outside, the market bells began to ring the hour, and somewhere down the paper district a whistle sounded—the short, sharp pattern the porters used for trouble.
