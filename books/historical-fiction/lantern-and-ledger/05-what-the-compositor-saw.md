## 5. What the Compositor Saw

Tomas Ferro had been a compositor for nineteen years, and in nineteen years he had learned that a press room kept no secrets from the man who set its type. Paper told on itself. Ink told on itself. And people—people were only machines with looser tolerances, prone to slippage at the seams, and Tomas had spent his life reading the seams.

He had read the seams of Almeida & Daughter within three days of taking the journeyman's bench, six years ago, when he was still a thin-ribbed young man with ink under every fingernail and a reputation for leaving shops before they could leave him first. He had seen that old Almeida measured his galleys twice because his eyes could no longer be trusted once, and pretended otherwise with the ferocity of a man defending his own grave. He had seen that Wendell cut type like a surgeon and spoke like a locked drawer, and that the customers who came to the shop sorted themselves into two kinds: those who noticed her and those who did not, and that she worked very hard to belong to the second kind.

He had also seen, over six years, everything change in the space of one rain-soaked night in the month of storms.

It began, as all revelations in the print trade began, with paper.

Tomas ordered the stock. That was his work as much as composing: knowing how many reams of foolscap remained in the loft, which supplier would extend credit through another week, whose rag paper carried less watermark than promised. So when a full stone of fine linen paper appeared in the storeroom—paper too good for jobbing work, too good for the almanacs and circulars that kept the shop's ledger breathing—he knew it had not come through him. And paper that entered a shop without passing through Tomas Ferro was paper someone wanted uncounted.

He said nothing. Saying nothing was a specialty of his. He had grown up in a house where saying the wrong thing meant his father's belt and saying nothing meant dinner, and he had refined silence into a craft to sit alongside composition. He could lock up a forme without a sound. He could walk a floor of sleeping men without a creak. He could stand at his stone with his hands moving in their patient dance—em, en, quad, slug—and let an entire conversation happen behind him without turning his head.

So he stood at his stone and let the shop tell him things.

The old man went down to the storeroom more often than storerooms required. Wendell climbed to the type loft four, five, six times a day, and she came down each time with her bodice straightened and a look on her face that Tomas recognized because he had worn it himself, once, in another city, in another life: the look of a person carrying something alive and warm and utterly ruinous.

And there were sounds. Small ones. The shop had its own music—the groan of the press screw, the shing of the platen, old Almeida's cough, the rain against shutters—and beneath that music, faint as a fly's footstep, Tomas heard what he heard. A breath too soft to be an adult's. A cry muffled so quickly it might have been imagination, if a man hadn't spent nineteen years listening for the one wrong sound in a quiet room.

Then, one grey morning in the third week, he saw the face itself.

Wendell had rigged the type loft cleverly—he granted her that—with a curtain of hanging proofs and a crate turned on end, and she thought herself invisible, the way all careful people thought themselves invisible, not understanding that invisibility was simply visibility deferred. She had brought the child down during the midday lull while her father dozed in the chair by the stove, and she stood at the window with the baby held up before the glass, showing him the street. Showing him carts and rain and the fish-seller's striped awning. Showing him the world, quietly, the way you'd show a prince his kingdom.

Tomas saw them both reflected in the window glass, and neither of them saw him, because he was standing exactly where he always stood, among the cases, hands moving, em, en, quad, a man made of furniture.

The child laughed. A small wet sound like a coin dropped in water.

Wendell's reflection froze. Her eyes found Tomas's reflection in the glass, and for a long moment the two of them looked at each other through the window and the street and the falling rain, and Tomas understood that whatever happened next would be decided in the next ten seconds.

He looked back down at his case and kept setting.

* * *

She waited until the old man left for the paper merchant's—a lie about a delivery that fooled nobody, since everyone in the trade knew the paper merchant closed his doors at midday—and then she came to stand at the end of his stone with her arms folded and her chin up, which was, he had to admit, braver than most. Most people in her position would have begun with denial. She began with arithmetic.

"You've known," she said. "How long?"

"Three weeks. Nine days. This morning." He slid a line of type into the stick without looking up. "I count things. It's the trade."

"Then you'll want paying."

The words came out flat and hard as a piece of cast lead, and Tomas heard what they cost her to say. He set his stick down and finally looked at her properly. She was twenty-four years old and had the kind of face that got described in official documents rather than poems: narrow, watchful, ordinary until it wasn't. Right now it wasn't.

"I don't want paying," he said.

"Everyone wants paying."

"True. But not everyone wants coin." He wiped his hands on his apron, slowly, giving the moment the weight it deserved. Nineteen years in the trade had taught him that timing mattered as much in speech as in composition; a word set too early or too late spoiled the whole line. "Let me tell you what I see, and then we'll know what I want."

She didn't move. He took that as permission.

"I see a shop that takes in no deliveries but received a stone of linen paper fit for a lord's missive. I see my employer's daughter climbing to the type loft forty times a day like a woman visiting a sick relative. I hear, some nights when I'm locking up late, a sound about the size of a hunger." He held up his ink-stained thumb and forefinger, a few inches apart. "A sound this big. And I see a sealed packet that came into this shop the same night as the sound, wrapped in oilcloth, that has not gone into any forme, but that you've read—I can tell you've read it, because you've started walking differently. People who've read dangerous papers walk like the floor is thinner."

Her face did not change, which told him he was right about all of it.

"So," he said. "You offer me payment. Here is mine: I want to know what we're printing next."

"We're not printing anything. There is no *we*."

"There's always a *we*," Tomas said. "The moment two people know a thing, there's a *we*. The only question is whether it's a conspiracy or just two fools standing near each other when the wall comes down." He picked his stick back up, returned a letter to its case, the little click of lead on wood filling the pause. "Your father wants to hand it all to the authorities. You want to keep it. I watched you argue through the storeroom door last night—you're better at whispering than he is, but he's better at being frightened, and frightened old men are loud in their own way."

"Tomas—"

"Minister Corvel is dead," he said quietly. "Murdered, they say, though murdered men don't usually leave their houses feet-first with the coroner paid before the body's cold. And a minister who is dead and was important had papers. Everyone in this trade knows it the way sailors know weather. And now there are papers in this shop, and a child in the loft, and I am the third pair of hands under this roof, and you cannot make me blind."

The stove ticked. Outside, a cart rattled past, and somewhere down the street a woman was arguing about the price of eels with the total conviction of the righteous.

"What do you want, then?" Wendell asked. Her voice had gone low, and he heard under it the thing she was actually asking: *what will it take to buy you, and is the price one I can pay?*

"I want in," Tomas said. "Or out. One or the other, and you don't get to choose which. If I'm in, then I'm in—my hands, my stone, my silence, all of it, and you tell me what's true and we decide together what's wise. If I'm out, I walk tonight and you find another journeyman, and you spend the rest of your life wondering which morning the knock comes and whether it was me who sent it."

"That's not a choice. That's a hostage negotiation."

"Yes," Tomas agreed. "I learned negotiation in rooms where the other party owned the building. It colors a man's style."

For a moment he thought she would laugh, and then he thought she would strike him, and she did neither. She came around the stone instead, close enough that he could see the sleeplessness printed under her eyes in fine grey lines, and studied him the way she studied a piece of type before it went in the stick: checking it for flaws, for cracks, for the hairline defect that would spoil the impression.

"Why?" she asked. "Truly. Not the negotiation. Why would you tie yourself to this? You don't know me. You don't know that child. You could walk out of here tonight and be in Tarrow by the packet boat and none of this would ever touch you."

It was a fair question, and Tomas Ferro had spent six years not answering fair questions, so it cost him something to answer this one.

"Because I was a clerk once," he said. "Before the trade. In a customs house in Marrow, when I was younger than you. There was a ledger there with false entries—grain sold twice, grain that never existed, men starved in the districts while ships recorded full cargoes sailing past them. I found it. I was proud of finding it. I thought finding it was the same as ending it." He looked at his hands, at the ink in the creases that never fully washed away anymore. "I reported it to my superior, who thanked me, praised my diligence, and buried the ledger in a cabinet, and I let it be buried, because I was nineteen and wanted my wages. Three years later the famine reached Marrow and I stood in a bread line behind a woman who'd eaten the wallpaper off her kitchen wall, and I understood that I had helped bury those people. Not with a shovel. With a filing cabinet."

The stove ticked. The eel argument outside resolved itself in favor of the woman, loudly.

"I have been a man who files things," Tomas said, "for a long time. Good work. Clean work. I compose other people's words beautifully and I never once ask what they mean. And then three weeks ago a stranger's child came to live above your head, and I heard you singing to it when you thought the shop was empty—you sing worse than anyone I've ever heard, by the way, it's genuinely remarkable—and I thought: here it is again. Another ledger. Another cabinet. Another chance to be the man who files things." He met her eyes. "I'm thirty-nine years old. I don't want to file this one."

Silence stretched between them, and Tomas let it, because he had said everything and a compositor knows better than to crowd the page.

"You could destroy us," Wendell said finally. "One sentence to the wrong person and my father dies. The child dies. Probably me too."

"Yes."

"And you say you want in."

"I say I want in."

"And if the committee comes, or the censor comes, or the widow Corvel comes with knives instead of coins—if it goes badly—will you hold?"

Tomas considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty more than comfort.

"I don't know," he said. "I've never been tested. That's most of why I'm standing here offering. A man reaches my age untested and starts to suspect his whole life was rehearsal." He shrugged. "But I'll tell you this: I set type six days a week for men I despise, obituaries for magistrates who bought their offices, advertisements for wines watered at the dock. My hands are honest and everything they've ever made has been lies. Let me make one true thing before I'm done. That's my price. Not coin. A true thing, set in lead, that I helped choose."

Wendell looked at him for a long time. Then she went to the press, laid her palm flat on the great oaken beam as if taking an oath upon it, and said:

"The packet is Corvel's testament. His own hand. Names, dates, payments—royalists and republicans both, half the men now shouting loudest for the people's liberty took the palace money to stay quiet, and half the king's ministers arranged the riots they blamed on radicals. He wrote it before they killed him and he hid it with his son, and his son brought it here, and the son is upstairs asleep in a laundry basket lined with felt." She exhaled, shaky, as though the words had physical weight and she'd been carrying them alone until now. "That's the true thing. Whether we ever print it, I don't know yet. But now you know it, and God forgive me, so does someone besides me."

Tomas nodded slowly, and inside his chest something shifted that he had no name for—a settling, like a forme being locked, all the loose furniture drawn tight.

"There's a third thing," Wendell added, and now her voice changed, flattened, the negotiator returning. "If you're in, you're in completely, and that means I need to know I can trust you at two in the morning, when my father's asleep, when there's a knock, when the choice is fast. So I'm going to ask you once, and I want you to remember I asked: is there anyone paying you? Anyone who sent you to this shop, anyone who's spoken to you in the last three weeks, anyone at all?"

"No," Tomas said, and it was true, and it would remain true for eleven more days, which he did not know and could not have guessed, and so the word came out of him clean and whole, the way a well-cast letter leaves the mould: perfect, and unaware of what it would someday have to print.

"Then you're in," Wendell said. She put out her hand, and they shook over the composing stone, ink to ink, and it was done.

She went back upstairs. He heard her footsteps on the ladder, then the low murmur of her voice speaking nonsense syllables to a child who answered in the only language it knew, delight.

Tomas stood alone in the empty shop with his hand still faintly warm and his heart beating strangely, and he understood that he had just done the bravest and possibly the stupidest thing of his life, and that either way, there was no longer any version of Tomas Ferro who filed things.

He returned to his case. He drew out letters one by one—em, en, quad, slug—and set, in reversed and shining lead, the opening words of a jobbing advertisement for boot polish, which was the sort of thing honest hands had always been used for.

Outside, the rain cleared. Somewhere across the city, in a tall house on the Corvel square, a woman in mourning black was asking servants questions about a delivery, and a boy was running toward the print district with a message, and the afternoon was already arranging itself into lines no one had approved.

Tomas set the last letter, tightened the screw, and felt—for the first time in nineteen years—the particular weight of a page that mattered.

It would cost him, that page. But the forme was locked, and the press was ready, and there is a point in composition when the type is set and no hand can unmake it without breaking something.
