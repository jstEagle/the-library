## 6. The Widow Corvel

The bell above the shop door had a cracked voice. Wendell had meant to fix it for three years, and now, as it rang out on a gray Tuesday morning thick with river fog, she cursed herself for the delay.

She was at the stone sink in the back room, washing milk off the little clay cup she'd been using as a pap spoon, when the bell sounded. Her hands went still in the water. Customers came to the Almeida shop at two hours: mid-morning, when clerks ran errands for their masters, and late afternoon, when gentlemen drifted in from the coffeehouses wanting visiting cards and funeral broadsides. This was neither hour. This was the dead stretch when the street emptied and even the gulls stopped arguing over the fish market refuse.

From the loft above came the small, wet sound of the boy waking—half sigh, half grunt—and Wendell's heart slammed against her ribs like a fist on a locked door.

*Hush,* she willed upward, uselessly, silently. *Hush, little one. Be type. Be furniture. Be nothing at all.*

She dried her hands on her apron, wiped them twice more though they were already dry, and went through into the front of the shop.

The woman standing between the cases of type was not what Wendell expected, which was itself a surprise; Wendell had expected someone recognizable from the news-sheets, a figure of mourning silk and veils. Instead there stood a plain woman of perhaps forty, dressed in the brown wool of the respectable middling sort, a gray shawl crossed over her chest and pinned with jet. Her face was long and pale, her hair drawn back under a bonnet that had seen better seasons. She might have been a ship chandler's wife, a schoolmistress, a widow of modest means come to order memorial cards.

That last thought arrived and Wendell felt it land somewhere in her stomach like a dropped quoit.

"You are Almeida's?" the woman said. Her voice was low and even, an instrument kept carefully tuned.

"The shop is Almeida's," Wendell said. "I'm his daughter. My father"—she gestured vaguely toward the stairs, toward the fiction of the upstairs rooms—"is resting. His eyes. The work strains them. But I can take an order."

"I don't want to place an order." The woman turned her head slowly, taking in the shop: the press with its great iron arm, the composing stones, the banks of type cases rising to the ceiling like a city of small dark drawers, the smell of ink and oil and the tallow candles they were careful never to burn near paper. "I want to inquire after a delivery."

Wendell's mouth was already dry, but it went drier. "A delivery," she repeated. It came out not as a question but as an echo, and echoes, she knew, were suspicious things. Honest people answered. Only the guilty repeated.

"Some weeks ago." The woman's eyes came back around and settled on Wendell with the patience of a cat at a mousehole. "In the rain, I believe. A night delivery."

"We have deliveries every day," Wendell said. "Paper comes Tuesdays. Ink came—" She stopped. Reorganized. "If you'll tell me what it is you're missing, madam, I can say whether we've seen it."

*You can say whether we've seen it.* As if *saying* were a neutral act. As if the truth were a drawer she could open or close at will, and this woman could not hear the rattle of things shifting inside her voice.

The woman unclasped a small reticule at her wrist. From it she drew—not papers, thank God, not anything with weight—but a single coin, and laid it on the counter with a click that seemed louder than the bell had been. It was gold. Even in the flat morning light, even for a woman dressed in brown wool, it sat on the scarred counter like a small sun.

"That is for the answer," the woman said. "Whatever the answer is. You see, I am not your enemy, Miss Almeida. I am only a woman who has lost something."

"And what would that be?"

"A package." A pause, thin as onionskin. "And a person."

In the loft overhead, directly above their heads, the boy made a sound. Not a cry, not yet—a gurgle, the busy experimental noise he made when he found his own feet or his own fists interesting. Wendell heard it the way a mother hears it, which horrified her even as the word *mother* crossed her mind, because she was not, because she had known him five weeks, because—

The woman looked up at the ceiling.

Wendell did the only thing her body thought of. She coughed. A tremendous, theatrical, fatherly cough, the kind her father made of a morning, deep and rattling, and she beat her own sternum as if dislodging phlegm. "Forgive me," she said, wheezing. "The dust. Type dust. It gets into everything—the lungs especially. My father swears by a vinegar steam but I tell him it's the dust or the grave, no help for it in this trade."

The woman lowered her gaze from the ceiling. Her expression had not changed. That was the terrible thing about her, Wendell thought later; the not-changing. Most liars watched for reactions. This woman simply absorbed the room.

"Type dust," the woman said.

"It gets everywhere." Wendell was aware that she was smiling. It felt wrong on her face, stretched and glassy, the smile of a person being robbed who insists on being polite about it. "You said a package. We're a print shop, madam. Packages come to us to be printed, not to be—kept. If you've lost documents perhaps the harbor office—"

"I haven't lost documents." The woman drew the shawl closer. Beneath its gray fold Wendell saw, for just a moment, the glint of a finer fabric underneath. Silk. Brown wool over silk. "What I lost cannot be replaced by any harbor office. I only want to know where it went. And I am prepared to be grateful."

The coin sat between them. Wendell looked at it and thought: *that is blood money, or bribe money, or grief money, and I do not know which and I cannot ask.* She thought: *royalists pay in gold. Republicans pay in promises. Which are you?*

She thought: *forget this face.*

The note was still hidden inside the false bottom of the ledger cabinet, folded to the size of a communion wafer, and its four words passed through her mind now with the force of a commandment she had already broken.

"We haven't had any delivery of that kind," Wendell said.

There. It was done. A lie, spoken aloud, in her father's shop, standing under the portrait of her mother, in sight of the press that her grandfather had carried across the mountains in three pieces on a mule. She had lied to censors before, in the small ways the trade required—an edition misdated, an imprint softened—but never like this, never with a person hidden overhead, never feeling the lie leave her mouth like something alive that would now live independently of her and grow.

The woman studied her. For a long moment there was no sound in the shop but the tick of the cooling press and, far off, the foghorn on the mole, moaning its one flat note.

"No," the woman said at last. "I don't believe you have."

It was gently done. That was the worst of it. No fury, no accusation. Just the quiet arithmetic of a person marking a sum in a ledger and moving on to the next line.

The woman picked up her coin. She held it a moment between finger and thumb, and then, instead of returning it to the reticule, she set it back down on the counter and slid it across the wood toward Wendell.

"For your trouble," she said. "Shops have troubles. Fires, floods, thieves. The quarter is not safe lately—I'm sure you know. The paper district especially. So many accidents waiting to happen in places like this. Dry goods, lamp oil, old paper." Her eyes moved once, unhurried, along the shelves where the printed sheets stood in stacks, awaiting collation. "It would be a kindness, Miss Almeida, if whatever you have not received were to find its way to someone who has lost it. Before the trouble finds you instead."

Wendell's hands, on the counter's edge, were white at the knuckles. She watched them as though they belonged to another woman and marveled that they did not shake.

"I don't know what you think we have," Wendell said. Her voice came out lower than she intended, and harder. Something under the fear had begun, unwelcome and warm, to burn. "But whoever you are, you can't come in here and frighten my father's household with talk of fires."

"Can't I?" For the first time, something moved behind the woman's eyes—not warmth, exactly, but a flicker, like light through a shuttered window. Grief, maybe. Or its impersonation. "My husband was a man who believed that words were safe. That if a thing was written honestly, honestly enough, it would protect itself. He wrote very honestly, Miss Almeida. And now look at me. Coming to printers' shops in the fog, leaving gold on counters, threatening honest tradespeople." She smiled, and it was the first expression Wendell had seen on her, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "So much for the protection of words."

*Her husband.*

The words went through Wendell like a nail through paper.

Corvel. The minister. The man whose handwriting she had studied by candlelight until three in the morning, whose testament lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards of the back room, whose name had not appeared in any official notice but whose death every kitchen and wharf in Vessany had already agreed upon without asking permission of the government. If this woman was his widow—if—

Then the boy overhead was not merely a bundle abandoned in the rain. He was a target. And so was she.

"Your husband," Wendell managed. "I'm sorry for your loss."

"Are you?" The widow drew her shawl straight with both hands, a small precise gesture, a woman putting her armor back in order. "So is everyone. It's remarkable how sorry everyone has become. The men who signed his death warrant are sorry. The men who applauded it are sorry. Even the newspapers, which printed lies about him for eleven years, are sorry now, in their careful way, printing nothing at all, which is their kind of sorrow." She took up the reticule. "Grief has made me blunt. Forgive me. I will ask only one thing more, and then I will trouble you never again."

Wendell said nothing. Above them, God be thanked, the boy had gone quiet, lulled back down by luck or by his own strange temperament—that preternatural calm he had, sleeping through hammer of press and roll of thunder alike, as if he already understood that noise was the enemy of his kind.

"If a child should cross your path," the widow said, "in your trade, in this quarter, among all the bundles and parcels that pass through it—you will understand that such a child has no name worth printing. No inheritance worth claiming. Whoever keeps such a child keeps nothing but danger, and does the child no favor by keeping it. There are people—serious people, on more than one side—who would prefer such children had never been born. I am not one of them. I want you to remember that. When others come—and they will come, Miss Almeida, they will come—I am the one who wants the child to go on breathing."

The foghorn moaned. Somewhere down the street a cart rattled over cobbles, and a man's voice swore at the mule in the cheerful obscenity of the fish market, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, oblivious, while Wendell stood gripping the counter like a woman gripping a rail in a storm.

"Why tell me this," Wendell said quietly, "if there's no child here?"

The widow paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and looked back. In the gray light her face seemed suddenly older, the lines around her mouth carved deeper, and Wendell saw—or imagined she saw, and would spend years deciding which—the briefest crack in all that composure. Something raw and young looking out through the widow's eyes like a prisoner at a high window.

"Because you lie worse than anyone I have ever met," the woman said, "and yet you did not sell me to the censor's office when I offered you the chance. That is a kind of character. Character is rare in this city just now." She pulled her bonnet forward against the damp. "Good day, Miss Almeida. Mind your lamps."

The bell gave its cracked cry. The door closed. Through the fogged glass Wendell watched the brown figure cross the street, unhurried, and turn toward the harbor, and vanish into the murk the way certain people can vanish, as if the fog had been waiting for them.

She stood a long time without moving.

Then she took the gold coin off the counter, holding it at arm's length like something venomous, went to the back room, pried up the floorboard, and dropped it in with the testament. It landed on the oilcloth with a soft, obscene clink. Payment for the answer, the widow had said. Whatever the answer is.

Wendell knelt there a moment, staring down into the dark gap between the joists, at the papers that named killers in two factions and the coin that bought her silence and the note folded small as a wafer—*forget this face*—and she understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water closing over her head, that she had been wrong about the shape of her life. She had thought invisibility was a wall. It was not a wall. It was a window, and from the other side, now, someone was looking in.

She pressed the board back down and sat on the floor with her back against the type case, breathing the smell of lead and ink, and tried to think what her father would advise, and realized with a lurch that she could not tell him. Not this. Not yet. Because her father would look at her with those failing eyes and say the thing he had said five weeks ago at the door:

*We give it up, Wendell. We give it all up, before it costs us everything.*

And for the first time she would have to answer him aloud: *No.*

Above her, faint through the floorboards, the boy woke properly at last and began to cry—the full-throated, unreasonable cry of a healthy infant who wants picking up, indifferent to ministers and widows and factions and the fall of regimes. Wendell put her head back against the little drawers of letters and laughed, once, a strange cracked sound halfway to sobbing, and hauled herself up the ladder to fetch him.

She was settling him on her hip, his wet face buried against her shoulder, when she saw it through the loft's small window: across the street, half-hidden in the doorway of the cooper's yard, a man in a porter's cap who had been there yesterday too. He was not looking at the shop.

He was looking up at the loft window. At her.

He touched his cap politely, almost kindly, and melted away into the fog.

The coin downstairs had not been the payment, Wendell understood then, her arms tightening around the child until he squawked. It had been the receipt.

* * *

That evening her father noticed her jumping at every knock—at the paper merchant's boy, at the chimney sweep's tally stick, at nothing.

"You're worn thin," he said, peering at her across the supper table with his good eye. "Sit. Eat. Whatever's troubling the shop, it will keep till morning."

"There's no trouble," Wendell said, and heard how badly she said it, and saw him hear it.

Her father said nothing more. But after supper she found him in the front room, standing at the counter where the widow had stood, turning over in his fingers the ring of wax left by the coin, and looking out through the fogged glass at the dark street, at the empty doorway of the cooper's yard beyond, with an expression she had never seen on his face before.

It was the expression of a man counting the exits in a room he has loved all his life, and finding them few.

"Wendell," he said quietly, without turning around. "Who came to the shop today?"

And before she could compose even one of her bad lies, the knock came at the door—three slow raps, deliberate, official—and a lantern swung in the fog outside, and a voice with the flattened vowels of the Censorship Bureau called out that they would inspect the premises in the morning.
