## 7. Setting the Forbidden Type

Tomas Ferro had been a printer's man for nineteen years, and in all that time he had never once been afraid of type.

Lead was honest. Lead sat in the stick the way you placed it, no more, no less. A compositor could trust lead the way he could not trust masters, or governments, or women who carried sealed packets through rain. You set the word, the word stayed set. There was a comfort in that, an architecture. The world outside the shop might be dissolving into rumor and smoke, but inside, on the stone, everything held.

He thought about this now, standing at the composing stone at half past midnight with a candle guttering beside him and Wendell Almeida watching him from the shadows of the type loft like a cat deciding whether the dog below was friend or meal.

"You're certain," she said. Not a question. She'd asked it four times already, and each time he'd answered, and each time she'd asked again, as if repetition could grind the risk finer until it sifted away to nothing.

"I'm certain," he said again. "But I want it said plain, so there's no mistaking after. If we do this, my name goes on it."

"It can't go on it. That's the whole—"

"Not printed." He tapped the stone with one knuckle. "I know better than that. But known. Between us. You and me and your father, if he ever learns of it, which God forbid. If the excerpts run, I set them, and you know it, and someday—if the world turns kind—you say to someone, *Tomas Ferro set that type.* That's all. No medal. Just the record. Someone ought to know what I did with my hands before they hang me for it."

Wendell came down the ladder slowly, one rung at a time, and he understood she wasn't hesitating out of caution. She was deciding how much of herself to hand over.

"Credit and risk," she said. "Shared down the middle. If they come for the shop, they come for both of us."

"Down the middle." He put out his hand and she took it. Her grip was harder than he'd expected, callused along the pads of her fingers where the type cutter's knife rode. Nineteen years he'd worked beside her and never once considered what those hands did while he was locking up formes and pulling the press. Now he thought about it constantly. It was becoming a problem.

* * *

They worked by two candles shielded behind pasteboard screens, because a lit window in the paper district after curfew was a confession, and because Father Almeida slept badly these days and would come shuffling down in his nightshirt if he saw light pooling under the composing room door, and neither of them had yet found words to explain to the old man why his daughter was setting treason between the hours of midnight and matins.

The testament lay open on the stone, wrapped in its oilcloth. They had agreed—Wendell had decided, and Tomas had agreed to the decision—that they would not print it whole. Not yet. Whole, it was a manifesto, and manifestos got shops burned. Excerpts, though. A paragraph here, a passage there, slipped into the ordinary run of jobbing work: a bill of lading, a wedding notice, the weekly price list for salt fish. Words that walked out of the shop dressed as nothing, carrying knives.

"Start here," Wendell said, and read aloud: "*The men who ordered the grain seized at Porto Lune were not republicans, whatever colors they fly now. I dined with them. I have names.*"

Tomas picked up his composing stick.

It was strange work, stranger than any he'd done. Every character had to be chosen twice—once for sense, once for safety—and set not in the shop's usual faces but in a bastard mix, a line of this body, a line of that, so that no censor comparing impressions could match the excerpt to any single job the Almeida press had ever produced. Slower than sin. His fingers knew the cases blind, but tonight he made himself go slow anyway, deliberate, a man disarming something.

*The men who ordered the grain seized.*

He set it letter by letter. The lowercase e's were running low in the third box; he'd have to redistribute soon, which meant lighting the lamp at the case properly, which meant risk, which meant he'd stopped caring somewhere around midnight and would need to start caring again by morning.

Beside him, Wendell worked too—not type, but the cutting she did better than anyone in Vessany. She was recutting a damaged e, her graver whispering through the lead, curls of metal falling away like pale thread. In the candlelight her face had that fixed quality he'd first noticed weeks ago, the one she wore when the baby slept against her shoulder in the loft above: attention total and unguarded, the look of someone who had found the thing she was for.

"The boy," he said quietly, to say something. "He sleeps through the press now."

"He sleeps through anything." A curl of lead dropped. "Tomas."

"Mm."

"Why did you stay? All these weeks. You guessed about him—I know you guessed, you've been watching me count steps on that ladder since September. You could have sold us a dozen times."

He kept setting. *I dined with them.* The n's were awkward; his hand wanted to hurry and he wouldn't let it. "Because I watched you lie to the widow Corvel," he said, "and you did it so badly that I understood you'd die before you gave the child up, and there was no profit in selling out a woman who'd die anyway. It felt like kicking a lantern into the sea. Wasteful."

"That's a terrible reason."

"It's the only honest one I have. The others are worse." He slid the line into the galley and reached for the next. "And because I was in the customs house the day Corvel's body came through. I saw the crowd take off their hats. All of them, even the soldiers. Whatever else is true, people loved him, and somebody wants them to stop loving him, and I've spent nineteen years putting words on paper and I've never once put down the ones that mattered." He heard his own voice go flat and hard and didn't try to soften it. "I'm tired of printing salt fish prices, Wendell. I'd rather hang for one page than retire on ten thousand bills of lading."

She said nothing for a long moment. Then she blew a fleck of lead off her cut and went back to work, and the silence between them changed shape entirely—it stopped being the silence of employer and journeyman and became the silence of conspirators, which is tighter woven and warmer and far more dangerous.

* * *

By three o'clock they had three paragraphs locked in the chase, and Tomas knelt to wash his hands in the basin, and that was when they heard the knock.

Not at the street door. At the alley door, low and deliberate, three raps and a pause and one more—the rhythm of a man who knows someone is awake inside.

Wendell snuffed the nearer candle so fast that wax spattered Tomas's wrist. In the dark he heard her move, quick and soundless, up toward the loft, and then her voice, pitched low through the floorboards: "Stay. Don't cry out." To the baby, then. Good. The baby was the one thing in this shop that could not be reasoned with or hidden behind a forme.

Tomas picked up the iron hand-press bar—heavier than any weapon he'd ever held—and crossed to the alley door. "Who knocks?"

"A friend of salt fish prices," came the answer, muffled, wry. "Open, Ferro. I can smell your candle."

He knew the voice. Everyone in the market district knew the voice of Madame Serrado, who sold fish by day and, rumor insisted, moved other cargo by night—letters, people, occasionally a coffin that weighed less than it should. He unbolted the door a hand's width. Rain came in with her, and the smell of wet wool and brine, and beneath it the faint sweetness of lamp oil, which he chose not to think about.

"You're printing," she said, not asking. Her eyes had already gone past him to the stone, to the oilcloth, to the chase sitting on the galley like a loaded thing.

"We're doing jobbing work."

"At midnight. Behind screened candles. With your mistress hiding in the loft like the Holy Family." She smiled without warmth. "I brought bread and a warning. Which do you want first?"

"Bread," said Wendell from the ladder, descending, because apparently she had decided Madame Serrado was survivable. Tomas admired that and resented it in equal measure.

The woman set a wrapped loaf on the stone directly atop the testament's oilcloth, as if to say she knew exactly what lay beneath and was not impressed. "The warning, then. The censorship bureau has a new list going round tomorrow. Banned words. Not banned books—banned *words*. Grain. Names of ministers. The phrase 'public trust.' They're going door to door with it, and printers who can't account for their stock get their formes confiscated and their licenses reviewed. Some get worse." She looked at the chase. "Whatever that says, it had better be able to pass as something innocent, because tomorrow it will be evidence."

"It already is," Wendell said. "We're printing excerpts into ordinary work. Salt fish lists. Wedding notices."

Serrado's eyebrows rose, and for the first time she looked genuinely interested. "Into the *notices?*"

"Who reads a wedding notice closely?" Tomas said. "A name, a date, a church. And underneath, in mixed type nobody can trace, forty characters' worth of Corvel. By the time anyone notices, five hundred copies are wrapping mackerel in three parishes."

Madame Serrado laughed—a short, real laugh, quickly strangled. Then she grew serious in a way that made the room smaller. "Then listen, and I'll say this once. When you're ready to print more than crumbs—if you ever decide the whole testament should see light—I can put those pages in more hands in one market morning than your press could reach in a year. Bread sellers carry everywhere. Nobody searches a woman selling bread." She straightened her collar, business concluded. "But understand what you're starting. Words like these don't inform people. They *arm* them. Once they're out, they'll be used by men whose purposes aren't yours. Corvel's testimony will become a weapon, and weapons don't ask their bearers what they intended."

"We know what we're holding," Wendell said.

"No one ever does." Serrado let herself out into the rain. Three seconds later the alley was empty, as if she'd dissolved into it.

* * *

They finished at dawn, because now there was urgency in it. Tomas distributed the case, redistributed the e's, and set the final lines while the sky behind the shutters went from black to the gray of wet ash. Wendell cut the last altered character—a false date, backdated, so the notices would seem to have been printed days ago—and they stood together over the stone and looked at what they'd made.

Three paragraphs of a dead man's testimony, disguised as commerce. Forty pounds of type. It looked like nothing. It looked exactly like every innocent thing the shop had ever produced, and that, Tomas thought, was the entire genius of it. The most dangerous object in Vessany this morning was indistinguishable from a fish list.

"Lock it up," Wendell said. "We pull proofs today during normal hours, normal ink, in the middle of the regular run. Tomorrow the notices go out with the deliveries."

He began planing the forme, checking height with his fingertips the way his old master had taught him, back when printing was just printing. Halfway through, his hands stopped, and he heard himself say the thing he'd been not saying all night.

"When this runs—if it's traced—we won't hang for excerpts, you know. They'll assume there's more. They'll tear this place apart looking for the whole testament, and everything here burns, including—" He gestured vaguely upward, toward the loft, and couldn't finish.

"I know." Wendell's voice was very steady. Steadier than his. "That's why we print fast and scatter the copies wide. A fire spread thin stops being a fire."

"That's not how fires work, Wendell."

"It's how this one will."

He looked at her—at the lead dust on her dark sleeves, the small burn scar on her wrist from the candle, the absolute lack of doubt in her face—and felt something shift in his chest that frightened him more than censors or committees or burning shops. It was the feeling of having handed someone the other end of his rope, and finding that the knot held.

"All right," he said. "Locking up."

He slid the quoins tight. The forme sat finished on the stone: three paragraphs of forbidden truth wearing the clothes of a salt fish price list, ready to walk out into a city that was already choosing its story.

And that was when they heard Father Almeida's step on the stair—slow, deliberate, unmistakably awake—and his voice through the door, quiet and terrible:

"Wendell. Bring the oilcloth packet to the kitchen. We need to talk about what my shop has been printing in the night."
