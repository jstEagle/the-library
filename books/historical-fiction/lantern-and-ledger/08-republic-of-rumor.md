## 8. Republic of Rumor

The price of bread went up on a Tuesday, and by Thursday the city had begun to lie about it.

Wendell heard the first lie at the market, standing in a queue that stretched past the fishmongers' stalls and into the Rue des Salines, where the women of the district had arranged themselves with the grim patience of an army that had fought this war before. A man two places ahead of her was saying that the bakers were hoarding flour, that they had cellars full of it, that the guild was in league with the intendant's office. The woman beside him said no, it was the harbor — grain ships turned away by the customs men, bribed by merchants who wanted scarcity because scarcity paid better than abundance ever had.

They were both lying. Or neither was. That was the trouble; Wendell could no longer tell, and she suspected no one else could either. In the print shop she saw the broadsheets that circulated through Vessany like coins worn smooth by handling — *the palace hoards*, *the assembly plots*, *foreign gold buys the mob* — each one set in type so similar to her own work that she sometimes caught herself reading a line and thinking, idly, *I would have kerned that tighter*. Six presses in the paper district were turning out these stories, some for pay from royalist coffers, some from republican committees, most for nothing but the pleasure of being first. None of them agreed on anything except that someone, somewhere, was betraying the city.

She carried her sack of flour home along the quay, one hand on the baby bundled against her chest under her coat. He had learned to sleep through noise, which was its own kind of commentary on the times. The harbor bells were ringing out of sequence — three strikes, silence, three strikes — the signal fishermen used when the customs launches were searching hulls. Nobody on the quay remarked on it. That was how you could measure a city's fever: not by what it shouted, but by what it had stopped bothering to mention.

At the shop, her father was at the stone with his good eye an inch from a galley of wedding invitations, moving type with tweezers because his fingers could no longer feel the difference between an em and a three-quarter em, and his pride would not let him use a setting gauge where a customer might see.

"Flour?" he asked, without looking up.

"Half ration. The line went around the salt quarter."

"Mm." He lifted a letter with the tweezers, held it trembling over the galley, set it down wrong, picked it up again. "Your mother stood in lines like that once. Before you were born. The harvest failure year." He got the letter placed. "There was a rumor then, too. That the granaries were full and the magistrates were letting people starve to keep prices up. Whether it was true—" he shrugged, a small careful motion that did not disturb his work "—it didn't matter that year. It mattered the year after, when everyone had already decided."

"Decided what?"

"What had happened." He took off his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "Stories are like type, Wendell. Loose in the case, they're just so much metal. Set and printed, they're the only version of events anyone can hold in their hands. Afterward, nobody remembers the loose letters."

He said it as craft wisdom, the kind of thing he'd said a hundred times over the composing stone. But lately everything he said landed differently, because locked in the proof cabinet upstairs, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a false bottom, lay forty pages of Minister Corvel's testament — the loose letters, as it were, waiting for a hand to set them. And every day the city grew hungrier for a story, and every day more stories arrived to feed it, and none of them were his.

* * *

The bread riot came to their own street on Friday.

It began, as such things did, small: a baker on the Rue des Salines sold out by mid-morning and shuttered his shop, and the women who had waited two hours in the cold began to knock, and then to pound, and then someone put a fist through the shutters, and the sound traveled down the street like fire down a fuse. Wendell heard it from the shop doorway, where she stood with Tomas wiping ink from their hands after the morning job — funeral notices, ironically enough, twelve dozen of them, for a merchant whose name nobody in the crowd would have recognized.

By the time they reached the corner, the baker's door was off its hinges and the crowd was not a crowd of rioters, whatever the evening broadsheets would say. It was mostly women. Wendell recognized half of them: Madame Okonkwo from the candle stall, who had once traded tallow ends for printing scrap; the Perrin sisters, who mended sails; old Ysande, who had been selling herbs at the market since before Wendell was born and who now stood in the wrecked doorway of the bakery holding a sack of rolls above her head and distributing them to the smallest children first, with the fierce orderliness of a quartermaster.

"Take only what your house needs," Ysande was calling. "Only what your house needs, and leave the rest for the street." No one was smashing. Someone had dragged the baker himself behind his counter, unharmed, protesting; someone else was already sweeping broken glass, because even insurrection has to be walked on barefoot come Sunday.

It lasted less than an hour. The watch arrived at the end of it, when there was nothing left to suppress but crumbs and swept glass, and arrested four people anyway, because a report had to be filled out and reports required names. They took the youngest Perrin sister, who had done nothing worse than carry a sack, and Ysande herself, who refused to give anything but her name and her trade, loudly, three times, as though entering it into the record were a kind of victory.

Wendell watched from the shadow of the print shop's awning, the baby pressed against her ribs, feeling his heartbeat quicken against her own. She should go inside. She should go inside and lock the door and be small, the way she had been small all her life — the cutter of type no censor looked at twice, the daughter no soldier remembered. For years invisibility had felt like a talent, almost a virtue. Her father praised her for it. *You have your mother's gift, Wendell. You can stand in a room and be part of the furniture.*

But Ysande was being walked past her now between two watchmen, her herb basket overturned, dried thyme scattering across the cobbles, and Ysande looked directly at her — knew her, named her: "Wendell Almeida. Tell the salines women where I've gone." Not a plea. An instruction. An assignment, given to the one person on the street with a press.

Wendell stood very still until the watchmen turned the corner. Then she gathered up the fallen basket, set it on the bakery step, and went inside, and her hands were shaking, and inside the shaking resolved itself into something with a different shape entirely.

* * *

Tomas found her in the composing room after close, standing before the type cases with a candle, not setting anything, just looking at the compartments of lead the way a miser looks at coins or a general at a map.

"You'll wear out the floorboards," he said from the doorway.

"The watch took Ysande. Old Ysande, from the herb stall. She's sixty if she's a day and she gave bread to children."

"I heard. Half the street heard." He came in, pulled the second stool to the stone, sat. Tomas always sat at the stone as though it were a table laid for him; it was one of the things about him that irritated her and that she had, without noticing when, stopped wanting to change. "They'll release the old woman. The girl too, likely. A night in the cells to teach them the price of sacks."

"And if they don't?"

"Then they don't." He shrugged. "Wendell. What is it you're doing, standing at the case with a lit candle and no forme?"

She hadn't known herself until the words arrived. "I was looking for the size of it. What we could say, if we said anything. How many pages a testimony runs, set double-columned, at long primer." She touched the case edge. "Corvel's pages. Forty sheets in his hand. Names, dates, payments — signatures, Tomas. He signed things. Men in the ministry, men on the reformist committee, captains of the guard. If it were printed, the rumors would have something underneath them for the first time. All those stories fighting in the street — his pages are the ground they're standing on."

Tomas was quiet a moment. The candle guttered and steadied. Down the street, faintly, the salines women were still singing outside the watchhouse — a low, patient sound, not a protest so much as a vigil kept aloud.

"You know what happens to shops that print things like that," he said finally.

"I know what's happening to shops that print other things." She turned to face him. "Marchand's was burned Monday. The *Evening Courier*'s press broke a window last month and no one was charged. The city is choosing its story right now, Tomas, this week, while the bread lasts. Whoever prints first—"

"—writes history," he finished. "Yes. Your father says that. Everyone who owns a press says that; it flatters us." But he was leaning toward the case now despite himself, reading the labels on the compartments — long primer, pica, the small sharp diamond of nonpareil — the way another man might read a horizon. "And whoever prints second is a copyist, and whoever prints last is dead."

"Or forgotten."

"Often the same thing." He tapped a compartment of quads with one finger, thinking. Then: "Say it were possible. Say we set it. Who signs the imprint? Whose name goes at the foot of the page?"

"No one's. Anonymous."

"There's no anonymous in this city anymore. Everything printed is owned within a day — claimed by whichever faction it embarrasses least. Anonymous only means they hunt slower." He looked at her, and his face, usually so easy, had gone still and serious, and she understood that he had been having this argument with himself for longer than she'd known. "You're not asking me to help set type. You're asking me to help decide what the city will believe happened."

"I'm asking whether the truth gets a place in the queue," Wendell said. "Because right now it doesn't. Right now it's sitting in a cabinet getting older while six liars run ahead of it."

Above them, the floor creaked — her father, climbing to bed, pausing on the stairs as he always did to check the stove dampers. They both went silent until his door closed. The conspiracy of it startled her: they had become conspirators in posture before either had said yes.

Tomas rose, took the candle from her side of the stone, and set it between them, equidistant.

"Not yet," he said. "Not this week — the watch is in the streets and every press in the district will be searched before Sunday, I'd stake my indentures on it. But soon. We plan it properly: the forme locked overnight, the press worked at dawn, the sheets gone before the shutters open." He held up a finger before she could speak. "And I want something in return."

"Payment? We barely clear costs—"

"Not money." He hesitated, and for a moment he looked, oddly, younger — uncertain in a way she had never seen him. "When it's done. When it's printed and it's out in the world doing whatever it does. I want to know that I chose it. That I wasn't just — swept along. That it was mine too." He turned the candle a quarter-turn, absently squaring it with the edge of the stone. "Let me be one of its authors, Wendell. Even unnamed. Especially unnamed."

It was such a strange request that she nearly laughed — asking for liability as if it were a gift. But she thought of Ysande's voice on the street, *tell the salines women where I've gone*, handing her a task like a torch, and she thought perhaps it was not strange at all. Perhaps that was what everyone in Vessany was starving for now, more than bread: to be an author rather than an audience. To have the story happen *because* of them.

"It's yours," she said. "The risk and the credit. Both invisible. Agreed."

Tomas nodded once, and put out the candle between finger and thumb, and in the sudden dark the type cases seemed to fill the whole room, forty pounds of potential in every drawer, the whole unwritten city racked and sorted at their backs.

* * *

She went out at dusk the next day to the watchhouse, carrying the herb basket refilled with the only currency she had — bread from her own half-ration, two days' worth — and found the salines women camped outside it in the drizzle, a quiet row of stools and umbrellas that the watchmen had evidently decided was more trouble to disperse than to ignore. Ysande had been released at dawn, Madame Okonkwo told her. The Perrin girl was still inside. The singing resumed.

On the way home she took the long route, along the canal, because the baby slept better with motion, and because — she admitted it now, walking under the dripping elms — she wanted to see the paper district. Four of the rival presses were running with shutters open, lamplight spilling gold onto wet cobbles, the clatter of hand presses audible over the water: everyone rushing into print, everyone racing to be first with the next version of the week. Smoke still hung faintly over Marchand's burned shell, and already some enterprising soul had chalked on the scorched wall: *WHO BURNED HIM? ASK THE MINISTRY. ASK THE COMMITTEE.* Two answers, two factions, one wall. The city was writing on itself everywhere now.

She stood a while at the canal rail with her sleeping cargo, watching the lit windows, and let herself think the thing she had been circling for days.

The testament in the cabinet was not going to keep. Stories did not age well in hiding; they soured, or worse, they became irrelevant, overtaken — and an overtaken truth protected no one. Every day she waited, Corvel's pages mattered less and the rumor mill mattered more, and the boy asleep against her chest drifted further from being his father's heir and closer to being simply a foundling the Almeidas had inexplicably taken in. History was being typeset right now, all over the district, in six competing hands, and the truest hand of all was locked in a cabinet because she was afraid.

Ysande had handed her a task in front of the whole street. Maybe it hadn't been about the basket at all.

When she reached the shop, the ground-floor lamp was burning late, and her father's silhouette bent over the stone, and with it — she stopped on the step — a second silhouette, unfamiliar, narrow-shouldered, hat still on.

Through the window glass she could see why the man had come. He was laying papers on the composing stone, official papers, stamped along the edges, and her father was standing very straight in the way he stood only for the censor's bureau, and on the topmost page, large and fresh, Wendell could read the bureau's seal and, beneath it, a new directive — posted publicly tomorrow, delivered privately tonight, to every licensed press in Vessany:

*By order of the Censor General: all matter touching the late Minister Corvel, his estate, his writings, or his associates, is henceforth subject to prior restraint. Possession of same to be declared within eight days.*

The stranger left the shop. Her father stood alone among his type for a long moment before he turned down the lamp.

Eight days. Wendell counted backward from the doorframe, the baby waking against her chest, beginning to fuss. Eight days to decide whether loose letters become history — or evidence.
