## 10. Betrayal Priced in Bread

The card came folded inside a loaf.

Tomas found it on the fourth of Brumaire, a Thursday, when he went to the bakery on Rue Callais because it was his turn and because Wendell had been up all night with the boy's coughing and someone had to bring bread that wasn't gray. The baker, old Sarembre, wrapped the loaf in paper without meeting his eyes—normal enough; nobody met anybody's eyes anymore—and Tomas carried it home through streets where the gutters ran with something that wasn't quite rainwater and the walls wore fresh bills like scabs. THE PEOPLE DEMAND. THE MINISTRY CONCEALS. WHO PRINTS THE TRUTH?

He had set some of those words himself. Not those exact ones. Others like them. Enough like them that he could feel the letters under his fingers even now, walking, the ghost-memory of lead against his thumb pads, that beautiful blind language he'd read by touch since he was nine years old.

At the shop he put the loaf on the composing stone between the job case and the ink slab, and it was only when he cut it—Wendell was upstairs, Father Almeida at the press checking friskets with his ruined eyes an inch from the wood—that the knife met something that did not tear the way paper tears.

A card. Heavy stock. Cream-colored, the kind of cream that cost money. On one side, embossed, a crest: two towers and a falling crown. On the other, in a hand so fine it might have been engraved:

*M. le Chevalier d'Aubrac requests the pleasure of M. Ferro's company. Thursday, eight o'clock. The Sign of the Two Anchors. A matter concerning the late minister's effects.*

*Effects.* That was one word for a dead man's testament. Another word for a living child.

Tomas stood very still. The shop smelled of ink and oil and cold metal and, faintly, upstairs, of milk and wet wool—the smell of the baby, which had gotten into everything the way smells do, into the type cases, into his own coat collar, until he could no longer tell whether it clung to him or came out of him.

He looked at the door. Looked at the stairs. Heard Wendell's voice above, low, singing something tuneless to make the coughing stop.

Then he folded the card along its crease and put it in his breast pocket, and cut himself a slice of bread, and ate it standing up, and did not taste it.

* * *

He should have burned it. He knew that the way he knew the width of an em quad, without thinking, without deciding. He should have burned it and told no one and let the whole thing pass over the shop like weather.

Instead he spent the afternoon setting a funeral bill—a child's funeral, God help the city, there were so many now they'd begun to use the same forme twice—and every third line his hand drifted to his breast pocket, where the card lay with its corners pressing through the cloth like small patient teeth.

Here is what Tomas Ferro knew about himself, and had known for twenty years, and carried the way other men carried a limp: he was a man who could be bought. Not cheaply. Not easily. But bought. He'd learned it young, apprenticed to a master who paid wages in accusations, and Tomas had learned to survive by making himself useful to whoever was standing, and when the master fell, Tomas had been standing elsewhere. He'd told himself since that this was prudence. Craftsmen survived; idealists hanged. There were no monuments to compositors, and that suited him fine.

Except then Corvel's clerk—that was who Tomas had been, three nights a week, for money he'd told himself was honest copying work—except then Corvel died with his throat opened ear to ear in a canal boat, and the clerk found himself holding the only complete copy of a testament that would hang half the grandees of Vessany, royalist and republican both, and holding also a squalling bundle that had no business being anywhere but at the bottom of the harbor, and instead of drowning either one, Tomas had walked them through the rain to the Almeida shop and left them at the door like a debt he couldn't pay.

Forget this face, he'd written. As if ink could command anything. As if he hadn't seen the face every time he closed his eyes since.

And now: *the Sign of the Two Anchors, eight o'clock.*

He worked until the light failed. Father Almeida asked him twice if he was well—he'd dropped a line of brevier, which he never did, and the old man had heard the scatter of type across stone and turned his clouded eyes toward the sound like a man hearing glass break in a dark house. Wendell said nothing, but she watched him the way she watched everything, sideways, as if she were proofing him for errors.

At half past seven he took his coat off the peg.

She looked up from the stone where she was distributing type back into the case, her fingers moving over the compartments without looking, the way she did everything, invisibly, perfectly. "Where are you going?"

"Out."

"Out where?"

"The Two Anchors," he said, and was surprised at himself, and more surprised still when she went white—not angry-white, afraid-white—and said, quietly, "Don't."

"Why not?"

"Because I know what the Two Anchors is."

"So do I," he said. "That's rather the point."

They looked at each other across the composing stone, across the scattered type, across the three months of shared silence and shared crime that had made them conspirators without ever once making them friends. She had flour on her cheek or powder—lead dust, more likely, the poison they all breathed and pretended not to notice. Her hands were stained black to the second knuckle.

"If you're going to sell us," she said, "tell me first. I can be gone with him in ten minutes. I've practiced."

The words went into him like a bodkin. *Us.* She said it without drama, without appeal, just laying the fact on the stone between them like a forme locked up ready for the press.

"I haven't decided anything," he said.

"That's a decision," she said. "That's exactly the decision they're counting on." And she went back to her distribution, fingers finding the boxes blind, not looking at him again.

* * *

The Sign of the Two Anchors sat where the harbor district gave way to the silk quarter, a liminal tavern for liminal men: customs officers with royalist sympathies, royalist agents with republican manners, and wine good enough to make everyone forget which flag they'd spat on that morning. Tomas had drunk there before, years ago, in another life when his crimes were ordinary.

The chevalier d'Aubrac was easy to find. He sat alone at the corner table with a bottle of Graves already breathing and the unshakable comfort of a man who has never once been thrown out of anywhere. Younger than Tomas expected. Soft-handed. He wore his sword the way other men wore a watch—as ornament, as inheritance, as reminder.

"Monsieur Ferro." He didn't stand. "Thank you for coming. Sit. You'll take wine? It's honest, which in this city is nearly treasonous."

Tomas sat. Did not drink. "You have me confused with someone worth buying."

"On the contrary." D'Aubrac poured two glasses anyway, pushed one across. "I'm told you are the finest compositor in the Paper District. I'm told your master's shop does work for three committees of the new assembly while flying, if you'll pardon the expression, entirely beneath suspicion. I'm told—" he smiled, briefly, without warmth— "that a certain document which certain parties would give their estates to possess passed through hands such as yours. Hands that touch everything and are touched by nothing. Invisible hands, Monsieur Ferro. My favorite kind."

"I set funeral bills," Tomas said. "Baptism notices. Occasionally an almanac."

"And occasionally," d'Aubrac said pleasantly, "a testament."

The fire crackled. Somewhere behind Tomas a dice game turned sour and recovered. He became aware of his own heartbeat, slow and enormous, the way it got in his ears when he stood too close to the running press.

"Let me describe your situation," the chevalier continued, turning his glass by the stem, watching the wine climb the sides. "You are a journeyman in a failing shop run by a blind man and a girl. The city is coming apart. Within the month—within the fortnight—there will be soldiers in every printing house, whichever side wins, and every sheet ever pulled in this shop will be read backward and forward by men looking for reasons. Reasons to hang. Reasons to burn. Now." He leaned forward a degree, no more. "Or: you deliver to me the minister's papers—all of them, every page, including any copies—and in exchange you receive four hundred livres and my written assurance, countersigned, that neither the bureau nor any committee of inquiry shall trouble the establishment of Almeida & Son. Immunity, Monsieur Ferro. For the old man. For the girl. For yourself."

"For the child," Tomas said, before he could stop himself.

D'Aubrac's face did something very small. "Ah," he said. "So there is a child."

The room tilted. Tomas understood, with the terrible clarity of a man who has locked the wrong line of type, that he had just confirmed everything, and that the confirmation had cost nothing to buy except one question asked gently at the right moment.

"There is no child," he said.

"No," d'Aubrac agreed mildly. "Of course not. Still—my assurance covers all persons residing at the premises. Children, apprentices, cats. I am generous by nature." He produced a second card from inside his coat and laid it beside the first, face down, and slid both across the table with two fingers. On the back of the new one, in the same engraved hand, an address in the Faubourg and a number.

"Four hundred livres," d'Aubrac said, "and everyone in your shop grows old. Or refuse, and I want you to consider carefully what happens next, because it will happen whether or not you say yes to me. The republicans want those papers too, Monsieur Ferro, and their methods are less courteous than mine. They will come asking. And when they ask you, you will have to decide in a cellar somewhere, with tools nearby, and they will not offer immunity." He finished his wine and rose, unhurried, settling his coat. "Thursday next. The same hour. Bring the pages or bring your refusal—I am a patient man, but I am not an infinitely patient one."

At the door he paused. "One more thing. The testament, as we call it—it names men on our side as well as yours. Corvel was an equal-opportunity accuser. Think about that. Think about who benefits from its publication, and who from its loss, and ask yourself whose interest you truly serve by keeping it." A thin smile. "Good evening, Monsieur Ferro."

* * *

Tomas walked home the long way, through streets gone quiet in that particular way cities go quiet—not sleeping but listening. Lamps out early. Shutters drawn. Once, from an alley mouth, he heard the sound of many feet moving fast and purposeless, and pressed himself into a doorway until they passed.

The cards rode against his chest like coals. Twice he took them out intending to drop them down a drain, and twice he put them back, and he understood perfectly well why, and the understanding made him sick.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not stop doing, walking, the abacus clicking in the dark behind his eyes: four hundred livres was eleven months of the shop's best income. The immunity was real—d'Aubrac's countersignature would hold against anyone but a mob, and mobs could be waited out. The old man would live. The girl would live. The boy—

The boy would grow up in a city ruled by whoever won, and if the royalists won and the testament vanished, the boy grew up as nothing: no name, no claim, no target. A printer's devil with no past. Wasn't that safer? Wasn't that kinder? Forget this face—he had written it himself; why not finish the job?

And underneath the arithmetic, quieter, the other ledger. The one where Corvel's voice lived, dry and precise on those pages Tomas had read by lamplight three months ago and had never afterward stopped reading: *Let it be recorded that on the fourteenth of September I delivered to His Majesty's council a warning which they chose to burn... Let it be recorded that Messieurs the following consented...* Names. Dates. Sums. A dead man's hand reaching up out of the canal to point, and Tomas held the wrist, and was considering, on a dark street in Brumaire, letting go.

*Invisibility,* he thought, *is just complicity with better posture.*

He reached the shop near midnight. The lamp in the front window still burned—the night lantern, the one Father Almeida insisted on, though it violated three curfew ordinances, on the grounds that a print shop without light was a body without breath. Through the glass Tomas saw the press looming in the gloom like a beast asleep, and on the composing stone a forme locked up and waiting, and he thought: I could burn the shop tonight. End it clean. Papers, press, cards, all of it ash, and no one hangs, and no one is free either.

Inside, he climbed the stairs past the loft where the boy slept—he paused at the sound of small even breathing, the only honest sound left in the city—and found Wendell awake at the worktable, mending a cracked chase with wire, her sleeves rolled, her jaw set. She didn't look up.

"Well?" she said.

"They offered four hundred livres and immunity for the whole household. In exchange for the papers."

Her hands stilled on the wire. "And you came back to tell me. That's something." Now she looked up, and her eyes in the lamplight were the color of wet slate. "What did you tell them?"

"Nothing."

"Nothing is a thing, Tomas."

"I know it." He took the two cards from his pocket. He meant—to this day he would swear he meant—to put them on the table, to show her everything, to let her decide, because she was braver than he was and always had been and perhaps courage could be borrowed the way money could.

Instead his hand stopped halfway, and closed, and returned them to his breast pocket.

"I need till Thursday," he said.

Wendell looked at him for a long moment. Then she went back to mending the chase, wire whispering through brass, and said, in a voice flat as a platen coming down:

"Thursday, then. But Tomas—whatever you're carrying in that pocket, decide which side of the press it belongs on. Because Thursday morning I start setting the testament. With you or without you."

He climbed to his cot above the press room and lay in the dark doing the abacus, and the last thing he felt before sleep took him, finally, mercifully, was the edge of the cards against his heart, and how much lighter his coat would hang without them—and how he was going to wear them anyway, one more day, one more after that, the way a man wears a debt he means to honor right up until he doesn't.
