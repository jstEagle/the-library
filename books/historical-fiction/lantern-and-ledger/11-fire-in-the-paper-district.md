## 11. Fire in the Paper District

The smell reached us before the bells did—that sweet, greasy reek of burning paper and hot lead that every printer knows the way a sailor knows a coming storm. I was at the stone, inking a job lot of handbills for the chandler on Ropewalk Street, when the light through the shop's front window turned the color of an ember.

"Father," I said. "Come look."

He came to the glass with his bad eye squinted shut and his good one watering, and together we watched the sky over the Paper District go orange from rim to rim. The district sat east of us, down toward the harbor—a crooked warren of presses, rag warehouses, ink shops, and the homes of men who made their living setting other people's words. From our door we could see three separate columns of smoke climbing into the dusk, leaning westward on the wind like drunkards all walking the same direction home.

"That's Quill & Anchor burning," my father said quietly. He knew the silhouettes of his rivals' chimneys the way I knew the notches of my type cases. "And that one—Vasseur's. Old Vasseur."

The church bells of Saint-Aubin began then, all of them at once, which meant fire, and after them came the deeper note of the garrison bell, which meant soldiers.

I did not think about Vasseur or the Quill & Anchor men. I thought about the sealed packet hidden beneath the floor plank under my workbench, and about the boy asleep upstairs in the drawer we had lined with felt, and I felt the heat of those fires on my face as if they had been lit for me.

* * *

We learned the shape of it by morning, in fragments brought by customers who came not to order printing but simply to talk, because a print shop in Vessany has always been a place where news pools like water in a cellar.

The censorship bureau had escalated. That was the phrase everyone used—*escalated*—as though it were a fever, as though it were weather no one could be blamed for. Two weeks ago the censors had come with lists of banned words and phrases; now they came with torches, or rather, they sent men who were not censors at all but plainclothes toughs with river mud on their boots, and by the time the watch arrived there was nothing left to censor but ash.

Three shops burned that first night. Two more the next. The official notice pasted to the customs house said only that *premises found in violation of public order would be subject to seizure*, and everyone in the city understood that *public order* meant whatever the man holding the torch said it meant.

"It isn't random," Tomas said. He had come in early, earlier than usual for him, and he stood at the window turning his cap in his hands. He had been doing that lately—turning things over in his hands, watching doors. "They hit Vasseur first because Vasseur ran the reformists' broadsides last spring. Then the Quill & Anchor, because old Marchetti refused a commission from the Bureau outright. They're working down a list, Wendell. A ledger."

He said the word *ledger* and looked at me, just briefly, and I understood that he was thinking of the packet too, whether he would admit it or not.

"Then someone should warn the rest of the list," I said.

"The rest of the list is being warned." He nodded toward the street, where two boys were running past shouting something about fires on Cordwainer Row. "That's what the city does now. Word runs ahead of the fire. But word doesn't put out anything." He set his cap down on the counter. "Your father knows yet?"

"He knows."

"And?"

I looked up at the loft stairs. My father was up there already, and had been since dawn, doing something I had told myself not to wonder about.

* * *

What he was doing, I discovered at midday, was building a fortress out of furniture.

He had cleared the long composing table in the back room—the one where we assembled full formes for bookwork—and laid across it everything the packet had ever touched: the oilcloth it was wrapped in, the spare quire of paper we'd bought in case we ever grew brave enough, the iron weight shaped like a sleeping dog that held down proofs. And beside these, opened up like dissected animals, lay six of the great double-deep type cases, the ones we used for folio Bibles and never otherwise, their compartments emptied of type, their bottoms pried loose.

"Father," I said. "What are you—"

"Hollow cases." He did not look up. His hands, which had shaken for two years whenever he tried to read a proof, were steady as stones when they worked at wood. "A case this depth, with a false bottom seated flush, holds forty sheets folded in eighths. Lined with felt, it holds them dry. Sitting in the rack among the others, it holds nothing anyone would look twice at."

I stood in the doorway with the baby on my hip—he was heavy now, eight months and thick as a loaf—and watched my father saw a strip of pine to width, sand it, seat it, test it with his thumb. The testament pages lay on the table in their oilcloth, and I noticed he had handled them with the cotton rags we kept for cleaning ink off the rollers, so that not even his fingerprints touched them.

"When did you decide this?" I asked.

He paused. In the lamplight from the back window his face looked older than it had at Christmas, and thinner, as if the weeks had been planing shavings off him.

"When I heard the bells," he said. "Wendell, come here. Help me measure the felt."

So I helped. We worked through the afternoon while the boy played on a blanket among the sort trays, mouthing a wooden quoins and gurgling at the sound of the saw, and we built false bottoms into six cases until my fingers smelled of glue and pine, and neither of us spoke the thing we were both thinking, which was: *if they come, will it be enough?*

It would not be enough. We both knew it. A torch does not open drawers. It burns the whole library and asks questions of the ashes. But there is a kind of arithmetic that fear forces on you, and its sums are always small: not *how do we survive* but *what do we save*, and then *what do we save first*, and then, terribly, *who*. The papers or the boy? I caught myself making that sum once, standing with the felt scissors in my hand, and I put the scissors down and picked up my son—my son, though no priest had ever said the word and no law would ever recognize it—and held him until the thought went away.

It did not go far.

* * *

That evening the market women brought us news instead of fish.

Madame Oury came in first, as she always did, her basket smelling of brine, and she talked loudly about the price of codfish while her eyes went around the shop counting heads, counting exits, noting the new bars we had fitted to the back window the week before. Behind her came her sister and the Delacôte girl, and between them they delivered the day's intelligence in the coded shorthand of women who have survived every regime by hearing everything and repeating almost none of it.

The Bureau had posted new men at the district gates. Printers' journeymen were being questioned at the harbor—asked whose work they'd set lately, what commissions they remembered, whether their masters kept strange hours. A reward notice had gone up, unsigned, offering fifty crowns for information leading to the recovery of *certain documents removed from the estate of the late Minister Corvel.*

The late Minister Corvel. There it was, printed at last, in official ink: *late*. For five weeks the government had said nothing about Corvel at all—not dead, not fled, not disgraced—as if silence could unmake a man. Now they named him in a reward notice, and naming him was itself a confession: the papers existed. Someone upstairs was frightened of paper.

"Fifty crowns," Madame Oury said, loud enough for the street. "For fish prices, you'd want ten times that. Fifty crowns wouldn't keep a cat in herring." Her eyes met mine above the counter and held, flat and unreadable, and I understood that she knew exactly what fifty crowns was the price of, and exactly whose shop she was standing in, and that her sister and the Delacôte girl knew too, and that all four of us would carry this knowledge home in our baskets alongside the cod and tell no one.

"Terrible weather for October," she said.

"Dreadful," I agreed. "Everything's gone up in smoke."

She left a whole dried cod on my counter without my having ordered it, and would take no payment.

After they were gone I stood a long time looking at the fish. Solidarity, I thought, can be a heavier thing than betrayal. Betrayal you can carry alone. This—this knowing, passed silently from woman to woman like a coal wrapped in cloth—you have to carry together, and together means responsible, and responsible means if any one of us breaks, we all burn.

I hung the cod from the beam by the stove, and I swear the shop felt more defended with it there than it had with the new window bars.

* * *

We moved the pages at midnight.

My father wanted it done in darkness, by feel, the way he had composited type before I was born, back when shops saved candle-ends. So we snuffed the lamps and worked in the black heart of the back room, he and I and Tomas, who had stayed without being asked, and who moved around us as quietly as a man walking on eggs.

First the oilcloth came off. Then my father counted the pages aloud in a whisper—thirty-two, plus the cover sheet in that proud, slanted hand I knew better now than my own father's—and wrapped each bundle of eight in felt, and slid each bundle into its hollow case, and I seated the false bottoms and trimmed the seams with a razor until my thumbnail could not find the join. Four cases held the testament. The fifth held the boy's birth linens and the note that had come with him, *forget this face*, folded small. The sixth held, at my father's instruction, thirty blank sheets of the good rag paper, "so the weight tells true if a case is lifted," he said. "A liar's case feels empty. An honest box lies best when it weighs honestly."

Tomas carried the finished cases up to the type rack in the loft and shelved them among the others, third row, between the Greek and the long primer, and he did it with the ease of a man who had spent eleven years racking cases, so that even I lost track of which ones they were.

When he came down he stood wiping his hands on his apron, and in the dark I heard him say, not quite to either of us:

"You know they don't search, anymore. The ones who burned Vasseur's. They don't search and they don't arrest. They just burn."

"We know," my father said.

"I say it because—" His voice caught on something. "Because I've been thinking about what I'd carry out. If there were minutes. What a man saves first. I've been thinking about it a great deal lately."

Nobody answered him. The stove ticked. Upstairs the boy slept, or seemed to.

Then my father struck a match and relit the lamp, and the flame stood up straight in the still air, and he looked at both of us across it the way he used to look at a fresh proof, hunting errors.

"They will come here eventually," he said, in the mild tone he used for stating prices. "This is the oldest press on the street. If they are working down a list, we are near the bottom of it, and the bottom of a list is only the end of waiting. So we prepare as craftsmen prepare: we do good work now, and we keep our tools in order, and when the day comes we meet it with the shop in order." He turned the lamp key down until the flame was a bead. "And nobody speaks of what is in the racks. Not to me, not to each other, not in your sleep. A fortress is made of walls and silence, and of the two, silence is the load-bearing one."

* * *

I could not sleep. It is always thus after moving the papers; my body wants to count them again, to lift each case and weigh it, to be sure. Around two in the morning I went up to the loft with the lamp hooded, telling myself I would check the third row and nothing more.

The boy woke as I passed his drawer, of course. He always knows. He put up his arms and made his small imperious noise, half grunt, half command, and I lifted him out and sat with him in the rocking chair by the type cabinets, and he gripped my collarbone with one fierce hand and stared at the shadowed ceiling as if reading it.

From the loft window, between the chimney pots, I could see the Paper District. One of the burned shops was still smoldering after three days—a dull red pulse in the black, breathing slow. Beyond it the city lay quiet, and I tried to see it as it must look from whatever room the arsonists planned in: a map with pins in it, a list of addresses, our name surely written somewhere in somebody's fair hand, *Almeida & Son, printers*, waiting its turn like a line of type awaiting imposition.

*Almeida & Son.* My father had never changed the sign. There had never been a son.

The boy's hand opened and closed on my shirt. Below us, six hollow cases sat in the rack holding a murdered minister's testimony against their false hearts, and above us the sky held its smoke, and somewhere in the city men were sharpening the argument that would finally settle everything, the one they intended to have printed afterward.

I kissed the top of his head, where the hair came in fine and dark—Corvel's coloring, Tomas's guilt, my whole heart's ruin—and I whispered to him the only vow the hour allowed:

"Not this shop. Not this face. Not while I hold the brush and the ink."

Down in the street, a lantern stopped opposite our windows. Held there. Moved on.

I sat very still in the dark, counting footsteps, and they did not come back—but in the morning, wedged under the front door, we found a printed card, cheap stock, badly imposed, the work of some underground press: *THEY BURN THE PRESSES. THE WORD SURVIVES. ASK YOUR PRINTER WHAT HE DARES.*

Someone was calling us out. Someone knew.
