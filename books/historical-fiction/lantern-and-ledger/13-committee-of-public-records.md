## 13. Committee of Public Records

The summons came the way frost comes—not announced, simply there one morning, whitening the door.

Tomas Ferro found it wedged into the iron latch of the shop's rear entrance, folded twice and sealed with no seal at all, just a thumbprint of red wax pressed flat by someone who wanted him to know that ceremony was a thing they could take or leave. He stood in the alley with his collar up against the river damp and read it three times before the words stopped rearranging themselves.

*The Committee of Public Records requests the attendance of Tomas Ferro, journeyman compositor, at the former Custom House, on the ninth hour. Attendance is not optional.*

*Not optional.* He almost admired it. In the old days the censors had wrapped their threats in courtesy—*his Excellency requests*, *the Bureau would be grateful*—as if politeness were a form of armor. The new men didn't bother. The new men had learned that fear worked better naked.

He tucked the paper into his apron and went back inside, past the racks of drying broadsheets, past old Almeida bent over the stone with his loupe screwed into his eye like something grown there, past the type loft where—

He did not look up at the loft. That had become a discipline: not looking. Eleven weeks of not looking, of keeping his eyes on his composing stick while an infant's cry came muffled through the floorboards above, of pretending the extra bowl of bread mash on the shelf was for no one, of pretending he hadn't been the one to carry the bundle up those stairs in the first place.

Wendell was at her cutting table, graver in hand, working the counter of some capital letter he couldn't identify from across the room. She looked up when he came in—or looked toward him; she'd gotten good lately at looking *through* people—and he considered telling her. He considered it for exactly as long as it took to see her face, which had the particular set of someone carrying something breakable across ice.

"Summons?" she asked. Of course she'd seen it in his walk. She'd known him six years; she could read his shoulders the way he read galley proofs.

"Committee of Public Records," he said. "They want me at nine."

Her graver stopped moving. "Ferro is your name on it?"

"Every letter."

"They've never named anyone before." She said it quietly, and he heard what lived under it: *they've never needed to.* The committee had so far contented itself with lists of banned words and burned shops. Naming a man meant they'd begun to think in specifics, and specifics were where violence lived.

"I'll be back by ten," he said.

"If you're not—"

"You'll burn everything worth burning and tell them I'm a drunk who owes you money." He managed half a smile. "Which has the advantage of being half true."

She didn't laugh. She watched him tie his apron strings with fingers that weren't quite steady, and then she said the thing he least wanted to hear: "Tomas. Whatever they offer you—they already know what your answer will be before you walk in. The offer is just the shape of the trap."

He knew that too. It was why his hands were unsteady. A trap whose shape you know is still a trap.

* * *

The former Custom House had been built to impress merchants, back when impressing merchants mattered more than frightening them. Its marble stair still wore its balustrade of carved cornucopias spilling stone fruit, though someone had chiseled the royal cipher off the keystone, leaving a raw scar in the granite like a missing tooth.

Inside, the smell had changed. Tomas had delivered print jobs here under the old regime—customs manifests, tariff schedules, the dull arithmetic of trade—and the building had smelled then of ink and tobacco and the sea-salt sweat of clerks. Now it smelled of lamp oil, wet plaster, and something faintly organic he chose not to name. The great hall where ships' papers had once been stamped was partitioned into offices by standing screens made of salvaged doors, and behind each screen sat a desk, and behind each desk sat a citizen in a plain coat, writing.

That was the thing about the revolution's servants, he thought, climbing the stairs two at a time because a boy with a musket had told him to hurry. They wrote constantly. The old regime had ruled by spectacle—the scaffold on feast days, the governor's coach glittering through the streets. The new one ruled by paper. Somewhere in this building was a file with his name on it, and the file was growing even now, line by line, in a hand steadier than his own.

A woman with cropped hair and ink-stained cuffs received him on the second landing, checked a ledger, and led him down a corridor to a corner office that still had its original windows—tall, arched, looking out over the harbor where the fishing boats came in gray and small in the morning light.

Two men waited inside. One was young, barely older than Wendell, with a scholar's stoop and spectacles mended with wire. He sat behind the desk and did not rise. The other stood at the window with his hands clasped behind him, watching the boats, and he was older—fifty perhaps—with a face like a folded map, all creases and no marked roads. He wore the plain coat of a man who wanted very much for you to believe he owned nothing finer.

"Tomas Ferro." The young one read it off the ledger without looking up. "Compositor. Six years at Almeida's. Apprentice before that at Corvel's old press house on Tanner's Row—until it closed." He glanced up then, and his eyes were quick and colorless. "You knew Minister Corvel."

"I set type for a gazette he patronized. For four months. Years ago."

"You'd know his hand, then."

Here it was, then, arriving sooner than expected. Tomas felt his heartbeat settle into the slow deliberate rhythm he'd learned at his first press, feeding sheets by hand: when the machine is dangerous, be the machine. Calm. Regular. Unremarkable.

"A hand's a hand," he said. "I know good kerning when I see it."

The young man smiled thinly, as if Tomas had confirmed something. The older one turned from the window.

"My colleague is Quenard," he said. His voice was mild, unhurried, the voice of a man reading aloud to children. "I am called Belhomme. I chair the subcommittee concerned with printing and public instruction." He gestured to a chair. "Sit, comrade Ferro. You are not accused of anything."

*Yet,* said the room, the way rooms do.

Tomas sat. The chair was hard oak, positioned so that the window light fell full on his face—he noted this professionally, the way he noted everything now, cataloguing details the way another man might count his change. They would see every twitch. So he gave them none.

Belhomme remained standing, which let him loom gently, which was apparently his gift. When he spoke again it was about paper.

"Do you know how much paper this city consumes in a week, comrade? Since the barricades began? More than in the whole year before. Proclamations, decrees, lists of prices, lists of the dead, denunciations—" he pronounced the word without distaste, merely as inventory "—songs, rumors, lies. Everyone in Vessany has become an author. Everyone wants the world remade in their own hand. And most of what they write is garbage. Bad composition, worse spelling, no argument at all. Rumor is cheap, comrade Ferro. It costs nothing to make and nothing to believe, and so it is worth nothing."

He crossed to the desk and drew a document from a drawer—a printed sheet, but a fine one, laid paper, wide margins.

"The Republic does not intend to win the streets and lose the story. Streets can be retaken. Stories cannot. Whoever prints the account of these years—the testimony, the trials, the fall of the old men—that account will outlive everyone in this room. We intend to print it well."

He slid the document across the desk. Tomas did not pick it up, so Quenard picked it up for him and turned it to face him.

It was a contract. He read it the way he read everything, fast and completely, and it took longer than most because it was thorough. The Committee of Public Records hereby retained the establishment of Almeida & Son—*and Son*; someone had done their research badly, or deliberately—to produce the official history of the revolution in Vessany: proceedings, proclamations, the collected testimonies of public officials past and present. All materials to be supplied by the committee. All materials to be printed as supplied, without alteration, addition, or omission. Compensation generous—generous enough that the figure stopped him like a hand on his chest. Wages for life, effectively. Protection. Priority supply of paper at official rates, in a city where paper was becoming dearer than bread.

And in the fourth clause, in smaller type, as if it hoped not to be noticed:

*The contractor shall surrender to the Committee any documents, manuscripts, or materials in his possession or knowledge pertaining to the affairs of the late Minister Corvel, for review and authorized publication.*

There it was. Not the history contract at all—that was the velvet. This was the hand inside it.

"We understand the shop has fallen on hard times," Belhomme said, still in that reading-aloud voice. "The old man's eyes. Orders drying up since the censorship bureau squeezed the loyal printers out of business—though between us, comrade, we were never sorry to see some of them go. Your trade is dying. In six months, half the presses in Vessany will be cold iron. We're offering yours a body temperature." He smiled at his own joke, mildly, and let the smile fade. "We're offering you the chance to be the hand that writes the Republic. Not a scribe in a garret somewhere, not a rumor-monger with a smuggled press. The official historian's craftsman. Your name in the colophon. Men have killed for less than what we're offering you this morning."

"And if I refuse?" Tomas asked, because the silence demanded filling and because refusing to ask was itself an answer.

Quenard answered, not Belhomme, and his quickness suddenly made sense—he was the knife kept for questions the velvet preferred not to touch.

"Refuse?" He sounded genuinely puzzled, a scholar confronted with bad logic. "No one refuses a contract, comrade. One either signs it, or one is found to be... unsuitable for civic trust. Unsuitability is recorded." He tapped the ledger on his desk. "Everything is recorded now. That is rather the point of our committee. A printer who declines to serve public truth—well. What else might he decline? What else might he be *printing* instead?" He spread his thin hands. "You see the difficulty. We don't threaten you. We simply observe that refusal raises questions, and questions in this city have a way of answering themselves. Mobs are efficient readers, comrade Ferro. They skip to the last page."

Through the tall windows the fishing boats came in, low in the water, and Tomas thought absurdly of the child upstairs in the type loft, who would be awake now, who would be gumming a piece of wooden furniture or pulling himself upright on the rack of long prims, and he thought: *I carried you here in the rain. I have spent eleven weeks pretending I didn't. And now the whole machinery of the state is asking me, politely, whether I exist.*

Belhomme watched him with the patience of a fisherman who had already cast.

"You knew Corvel's hand," the old man repeated, softly. "So does half the city. His testament exists—we know this as surely as we know the tide tables. Royalists claim it. Republicans deny it. Every day it doesn't appear, it grows larger in the dark, and things that grow in the dark come out with teeth." He leaned forward slightly, folding his creased map of a face around something that was almost kindness. "Help us bring it into the light, properly edited, properly framed. The truth, comrade, but the truth wearing suitable clothes. Nudity frightens the people. History must be dressed before it can be presented to company."

"And if the testament names men on both sides?" Tomas said, and heard the words leave him before caution could catch them.

Neither man moved. But the room changed temperature. Quenard's pen stopped. Somewhere below, a door slammed, and the sound traveled up through the marble like a shot.

"Then," Belhomme said at last, gently, "it will require *very* careful editing. Won't it?"

He rose, signaling the audience was over, and extended his hand. Tomas took it. The old man's grip was dry and warm and entirely without strength, which somehow frightened him more than a crushing one would have.

"Think about it until tomorrow," Belhomme said. "Bring your answer and the signed contract to this office. Or don't bring anything, and understand what your empty hands will mean." He released Tomas's hand and turned back to the window, to the boats, dismissing him with nothing more than his turned shoulder. "One more thing, comrade. The widow Corvel—you've heard of her? A woman asking after deliveries, after papers, after children. We believe she is not a widow at all. We believe she is dangerous. If she contacts your shop again..." He paused, considering the harbor. "Inform us. Loyalty is also a kind of authorship. You are a man who works with records, Ferro. Consider what record you wish to be in."

* * *

He walked back through the market district without deciding to, his feet choosing the long route, past stalls where women sold eels and secondhand lace and news that changed price hourly. The bread line outside the bakers' arcade stretched around two corners, and it was silent in a way crowds shouldn't be—no grumbling, no jokes, just the patient shuffling of hungry people who had begun to understand their hunger was a weapon someone else intended to use.

At the corner of Salt Lane, a woman was selling songsheets, printed on butcher's paper, crooked margins, verses about the minister's murder set to a tune everyone knew. *Who killed Corvel? Who killed Corvel? Ask the men in fine linen, ask the men in silk.* Three sous. People bought them and folded them away like contraband, because they were.

Rumor, Belhomme had said, is worth nothing.

But the woman at the corner had sold forty songsheets while Tomas stood watching, and every one of those sheets was a question the committee couldn't answer, and questions, unlike answers, multiplied.

He understood then, fully, what he had walked into. The committee didn't need him because they lacked presses—Vessany had presses enough, and would have more when the fires finished clearing the field. They needed him because of the one press that had *not* burned, the one compositor who had not fled, the one shop in the paper district still standing and still quiet. Quiet shops worried them. Quiet was where testaments hid. The contract wasn't an offer. It was a net drawn slowly around the only building in Vessany where the truth might still be sleeping.

And clause four was the drawstring.

By the time he reached the shop his mind had done what his mind always did under pressure—it had gone cold and organized, sorting the situation like a case of type, every fact in its compartment. Tomorrow he must return to the Custom House. He could sign, and become the Republic's pen, and wait for the moment the net closed anyway, because men like Belhomme did not pay for life and leave witnesses. Or he could refuse, and be unsuitable, and watch the mob arrive at the shop within a month, armed with questions that answered themselves. Or—

Or there was a third column in the ledger, one he hadn't dared total until now.

He put his key in the shop door and found it already unlocked, and for one cold instant he saw the raid, the soldiers among the cases—but no. Inside, Wendell stood at the stone with a proof in her hands, and her face was wrong, white around the mouth, and old Almeida was nowhere in sight.

"Tomas." Her voice was flat and careful. "Someone has been resetting my galleys. Passages from the packet. Names moved. Blame shifted." She looked up, and her eyes were the eyes of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want to finish. "Only three people alive know those pages are in this shop. My father cannot see to compose a line anymore. I was at the market all morning."

The proof trembled very slightly in her hands.

"So," she said, "you'd better tell me what the committee offered you today. And you'd better tell me before I decide what I'm going to believe."

Behind him, the shop door stood open to the street, and somewhere down Salt Lane the songsheet woman was singing still—*ask the men in fine linen*—and Tomas Ferro understood that whatever he said in the next thirty seconds would be the truest sentence of his life or the last lie of it.
