## 14. Altered Proofs

The mistake—their mistake, the committee's mistake, whoever's mistake it had been—was a comma.

Wendell found it at half past four in the morning, when the shop was cold enough that her breath showed in the lamplight and the only sound was the tick of the stove giving up its heat. She had come down for the proof copy of the third gathering, intending to read it through one last time before the formes were locked. She had read Corvel's testament so many times now that she could have recited it standing at a gallows, blindfolded, with soldiers shouting. She knew its rhythms the way she knew the stairs of her own house. Which was why the comma stopped her like a hand on her chest.

*And I say plainly that the order came not from me but was carried to me,* the original read. *Carried.*

In the proof, the sentence said: *And I say plainly that the order came not from me, but was carried to me.*

A comma. A small silver sliver of lead, no bigger than a fingernail paring, set where no comma belonged. It changed nothing that a magistrate would notice. It changed everything that a reader would feel. *Not from me but carried to me*—that was a man insisting on his innocence even as he named his murderers. *Not from me, but carried to me*—that was a man shrugging, a man admitting he might have refused and didn't, a man laying a hairline crack in his own testimony into which any prosecuting counsel could drive a wedge. The comma made him complicit. The comma made the ministers who received similar orders merely men of their time. The comma was a knife ground down to the size of a punctuation mark, and someone had set it with loving care.

She sat very still for a long moment, the way she did when she heard soldiers' boots on the cobbles outside, listening to her own blood.

Then she took the proof to the composing stone.

* * *

The formes were still open—unlocked, the quoins slack, which itself was strange, because Tomas locked his formes the way other men bolted doors at night. She lifted the first line of the altered passage out of the galley and held it to the lamp. Even before she read it backward, she knew. Every compositor's hand is a signature. Her father set type like he spoke, deliberate and slightly old-fashioned, his word-spacing generous, his em-quads always true. Wendell herself set tight and quick, her lines dense as rope. And Tomas—

Tomas set lean. He starved his spaces. He used thin spaces where other men used thick, packed his letters close as teeth, and he had a particular way of turning an asterisk at the head of a section break, tilted just off plumb, that she had teased him about for six years. Six years of watching his hands move over the case, quick and sure, quicker than hers though she'd never say it.

The type in the galley was Tomas's work. Not only the comma. Once she looked, she saw the whole passage had been reset—not corrected, *reset*, pulled apart and recomposed, and the new lines were his lines. The tilde-slight asterisk. The lean spacing. Three paragraphs rewritten around the fault line of that comma, and in each of them, subtly, the weight shifted. Where Corvel wrote *the royalist councilors pressed the order upon us with threats of dismissal*, the new type read *the councilors pressed the order upon us with threats of dismissal*. One word gone. *Royalist.* Gone as if it had never been cast. And further down, where the minister named General Maraval—hero of the republic's street fighting, darling of the committee—a line had been reworked so that Maraval's name stood adjacent to the order rather than at its origin, so that a hasty reader would take him for a messenger rather than a maker. She counted four such alterations. Four. Each one small enough to deny. Together they bent the testament like a bar of lead across a knee, not broken, just—bent. Just enough.

The proofs were dated for tomorrow. The forme was scheduled to go to press at nine.

Wendell stood in the composing room with the galley in her hands and felt something in her chest come loose and swing free, the way a sign swings loose in a storm, banging against the wall of everything she had believed for six years.

Tomas had known about the child for months. Tomas had set the forbidden pages by lamplight with her, had inked his fingers black alongside hers, had sworn—with his hand flat on the stone, which for a printer is a Bible—that what went into this shop as truth would come out as truth or they would all burn it first. And now there was a comma. There was a missing word. There was General Maraval, moved two inches to the left.

She thought: they got to him. Of course they got to him. Everyone could be gotten to; she had learned that from the censor's gentle voice, from the widow's coin, from the historian's silk. Bread cost what bread cost. Tomas sent money to a mother in the southern parishes—she knew that much—and mothers in the southern parishes ate or did not eat depending on what their sons earned. She thought all of this in the space of ten heartbeats, and then she thought, worse than all of it: *he never told me.* He let her set type beside him. He let her believe.

She put the galley down. She banked the lamp lower, so the light wouldn't show in the windows. And she sat down on the composing stool to wait, because Tomas always came back to the shop before dawn now—he slept badly, he'd admitted once, since the fires started, and the shop was the only place he slept well—and she wanted to look at his face when he came in. She wanted to see whether guilt walked in ahead of him or whether it had learned to wait outside like a hired man.

* * *

He came at ten past five, stamping rain off his boots, shaking out his coat, and when he saw the lamp burning low in the composing room he smiled—the tired, real smile he'd worn more often these past weeks, the smile of a man who had become her co-conspirator and found he liked having a purpose.

"You're up early," he said. "Or late. I can never—" He stopped.

She watched his face do three things in the space of a breath: register the open forme, register her sitting between him and the door, and understand. He was not a good liar. He had never been a good liar; that was why she had trusted him, in the end, after the business of his demands and her fury, because a man who couldn't lie about small things couldn't be bought for large ones. She had believed that. She had built her whole arithmetic of trust on it.

"Wendell." He set his coat down slowly, the way you set things down when a dog is growling. "Before you say anything—"

"Read me the sentence," she said.

"What?"

"The third paragraph of the third gathering. Read it to me."

He didn't move. In the low lamplight his face was all shadows and wet highlights, rain still beaded in his hair. "You've already read it," he said quietly.

"I want to hear how it sounds in your mouth."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, he crossed the room and stood at the stone, an arm's length from her, and looked down at the type as if it were a body he'd found. "It was the committee's own compositor who drew up the corrections," he said. "I want you to know that. They have a man—a little fellow, spectacles, works out of the Records Office—and he marked the passages in red and gave me the revisions and I was to set them as written."

"And you set them."

"I set them."

"With your hands. On our stone."

"My hands are the only ones in Vessany fast enough to reset three paragraphs in one night without leaving a galley trail a censor would catch." His voice cracked on the last words, and she realized he had rehearsed none of this; he had walked in carrying it and it was falling out of him however it fell. "Yes. My hands."

"Whose orders?" She kept her voice level, and it frightened her how easily level came, how much it sounded like her father. "The censor's? The historian's? Whose?"

"A courier came Tuesday. Committee of Public Records, seal and all. The contract we signed—" He stopped himself, but too late; the word *we* hung there, and she seized it.

"*We* signed. You signed for the shop. You signed in Father's name, with my knowledge?" She stood up. The stool scraped back and the sound seemed enormous in the sleeping shop. "When?"

"After the summons. After they—" He passed a hand over his face. "After they made it clear that refusal meant suspicion, and suspicion meant the press, Wendell. The press. Everything. Your father. The—" He glanced upward, toward the loft where the boy slept above the type cases, and could not finish, and she hated him a little for using the child even wordlessly, and understood him completely for doing it, and hated herself for understanding.

"So you bent it," she said. "Four places. I found four. How many are there really?"

"Four."

"How do I know that?"

"Because I'll show you every mark that little spectacled vulture made, and you can compare them against your copy, and you'll find four and only four." He said it fast and fierce, and then quieter: "I would not lie about the number. I've lied about enough. Not the number."

She turned to the galley again because she could not keep looking at his face and think clearly. Her fingers hovered over the lines without touching them—you never touched composed type carelessly; a careless finger could pie a paragraph into ruin—and the craft of it steadied her, the way craft always did. Four alterations. The comma. The missing *royalist*. Maraval displaced. And one more she hadn't fully parsed: a clause softened, *with threats of dismissal* becoming *in difficult circumstances*, which was so blandly bureaucratic it was almost genius, almost invisible, the kind of change that no reader would ever protest because no reader would ever notice.

"It reads beautifully," she said, and her voice finally shook. "That's the worst of it. Whoever drafted these knows the trade. It doesn't shout. It murmurs. Anyone who compares it to the original will see it, and everyone else will absorb it, and in twenty years the printed copies will be the only copies left and Maraval will be a messenger and the councilors will be *councilors*, blameless and gray, and Corvel will have testified against himself by comma." She looked up. "Do you understand what we're holding, Tomas? This is the last true account of a murdered man. It isn't paper. It's the only version of him that gets to survive."

"You think I don't understand that?" For the first time his voice rose, and he strangled it down to a whisper, glancing at the ceiling, at the loft, at the small snoring weight above them. "You think I set those lines without wanting to put my fist through the stone? They came here, Wendell. Not a letter. Here. Two men in committee coats stood in this room where your father taught me to mix ink and they talked about contracts and cooperation and then one of them, so gently, so reasonably, mentioned that shops that burned in the Paper District had all been *difficult* first. And then they asked after the child."

The room went very still.

"They asked—" Her mouth was dry. "They asked after him. By what? What did they call him?"

"The heir," Tomas said. "They called him the heir. They know he exists, Wendell. They may not know he's here, but they suspect, and every day we keep him and keep the original, we're gambling with—" He gestured at the ceiling, helpless. "With the only innocent person in this entire affair."

Wendell wrapped her arms around herself. The stove had gone fully cold now and the composing room's chill was the chill of cellars and churches, air that had stopped belonging to the living. She thought of the boy asleep upstairs, how he slept with his fists curled under his chin, how her father had taught him to pick out the letter W from the jobbing case—*for Wendell*—and how the boy said it, *Wenduh*, with total satisfaction, as if he'd invented it.

"Tomas," she said. "Six years. You ate at our table."

"Don't." The word came out broken. "Don't do that. Don't make this a ledger between us, because if you open that ledger you'll find my page already full. You know what I did before this shop. You know what I cost you before you ever trusted me with the child's existence. I was a man who sold silence for wages once already—I told you that myself, I confessed it with my own mouth—and they knew, Wendell. That's why they came to me and not to you. They read the ledger too."

The lamp guttered, caught, steadied. Rain worked at the shutters. Somewhere far off toward the river a patrol drummed its slow midnight count, four beats and a rest, the city's pulse taken and found thready.

"Show me the marks," she said at last.

He exhaled, and some of the rigidity went out of him, and she understood that he had come back to the shop at five in the morning half expecting to find the formes pied, the galleys scattered, his betrayal already answered in kind. Instead she was asking him to show his work, which was, in the language of their trade, a kind of terrible mercy: it meant she was still treating him as a craftsman. It meant the conversation wasn't over.

He brought the courier's sheet out from behind the third drawer of the stone cabinet—of course he'd hidden it there; it was the first place anyone would look, which was either stupidity or despair—and spread it under the lamp beside the galley. Red ink, precise and small, a schoolmaster's hand. Beside each marked passage, the revision in full. And at the bottom, in the same red hand, a line that was not about type at all:

*Amendments to be incorporated without alteration of surrounding text. Compositor will confirm completion by noon Thursday. Gratitude of the Committee, and its attention.*

"Attention," Wendell said. "Like a creditor."

"Like a hunter," Tomas said.

She read the red sheet twice through, committing it to memory—she had a compositor's memory; whole columns lived in her head—and then she did something that surprised both of them. She picked up the composing stick from the rack, slid a leading into place, and began, methodically, to unlock the altered lines, lifting out the poisoned type a slug at a time and returning each piece to its proper compartment in the case. The comma first. She held it up between finger and thumb, this tiny machination, this grain of statecraft, and dropped it into the comma box with a click.

"We have until nine," she said. "The forme goes to press at nine, and it goes correct, or it doesn't go at all."

"If we reset it—if they compare tomorrow's sheets to the proofs they approved—they'll know within the hour. They'll know it was deliberate. They'll know it was defiance." He was pale. "That's not a risk, Wendell. That's a declaration."

"Yes," she said. "It is."

"And your father? And the boy?" His voice dropped to almost nothing. "If they come for the shop—"

"Then they come for the shop." She kept lifting type as she said it, piece by piece, the rhythm of it like a heartbeat, like a drum. "Tomas, listen to me. I spent my whole life learning how to be invisible. Small shop, small girl, head down, cut the type, sweep the floor, let the soldiers pass. I thought invisibility was safety. Then a stranger left a child at our door and I learned the truth, which is that invisible people aren't safe—they're just useful. We're the dark a thing like this happens in." She nodded at the red sheet, at the galley, at the whole bent machine of it. "I'm done being the dark. If they want the testament bent, they'll have to stand here and bend it themselves, in the light, with their own hands, and let the whole street watch them do it."

Tomas looked at her for a long moment. Whatever moved behind his face, he didn't try to hide it this time—shame and fear and something older and harder, the thing that had made him carry a baby through the rain to a print shop door rather than drown it in the harbor.

"Give me the stick," he said hoarsely. "Your spacing's too tight anyway. If we're going to commit treason by breakfast, we'll do it with proper word-spacing."

They worked side by side and did not touch, and did not speak beyond the necessary words—*em quad, thin, turn the line*—and the type went back into the forme true to Corvel's hand, true to the dead man's voice, every comma in its rightful place. It took until eight. At ten past eight, as Wendell tightened the final quoin, they heard boots in the street outside. Not one pair. Many, unhurried, spreading along the front of the shop like water finding the low places.

Through the window, in the gray light just before the presses were to begin, Wendell saw the committee's little compositor—the schoolmaster with the red ink—standing across the street with a folded proof sheet in his hand, comparing it, even now, even from memory, against whatever the morning brought.

And beside him, waiting with the patience of a man whose errand was not typography, stood two soldiers with a wagon.

Tomas looked at her across the stone.

"It's early," he said. "They weren't due until nine."
