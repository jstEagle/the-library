## 16. Night Run

The plates went into the bread first.

That was Mother Ivetta's idea, delivered in a whisper through the shutter of her stall that afternoon while the militia drilled in Cathedral Square and the smell of smoke from the burned print shops still hung over the Paper District like a bad memory. *Bake them into the loaves,* she said. *No soldier in Vessany is going to cut open a loaf of bread to look for treason. They're hungry, not thorough.*

So we spent the day baking.

Father measured the flour with hands that shook only a little, and I pressed the stereotype plates—the testament, all thirty-one pages, cast in thin lead that morning while Tomas kept watch at the door—into the soft dough, sealing each one inside its own dark crust. The metal warmed in the oven and came out harmless, anonymous, wrapped in rye and caraway. Twelve loaves on the table, and inside every second one, a piece of Minister Corvel's last testimony, baked solid as a sin.

The boy sat on the flour sack and watched everything with those grave dark eyes, gripping a spare quoine in his fat fist like a scepter. He was fourteen months old now. He could say *light* and *no* and—because Father taught him, because of course Father taught him—*W*.

"He should stay," Tomas said quietly, for the third time since noon. "A checkpoint is no place for a child."

"He stays with me." I said it the way I set type: firmly, without looking up. "If they take me, they take us both. If he stays here and they come here—" I didn't finish. None of us needed the sentence completed. We had all seen what was left of Delacroix & Sons after the fire; we had all walked past the blackened frame and smelled the char that would not scrub out of the street.

Tomas looked away. He had been looking away a lot since the night I found his altered proofs, since his confession by lamplight—that he had been Corvel's clerk, that he had carried the child through the rain to our door, that the note reading *forget this face* was written in his own careful compositor's hand. Since then there was something raw between us that neither of us could set right, and so we worked around it, the way you work around a damaged letter in a locked forme.

"Two hours past full dark," Tomas said instead. "The curfew patrol changes at the fountain. There's a gap. Not a big one."

"I only need a small one." I pulled the shawl up over my hair. "I've been invisible my whole life, Tomas. Tonight I finally get to use it."

* * *

We went out through the cellar, down the old coal chute steps that Father had bricked halfway shut years ago against exactly this kind of necessity, and came up in the alley behind the cooper's yard, where the world was all shadow and dripping eaves and the distant red glow of the fires that never quite went out anymore. The boy rode against my chest in a sling under my coat, his breath warm on my collarbone, his weight familiar as a hand-press pull. I had carried him through a hundred nights of insomnia like this. Tonight the city carried him too, or seemed to—all its shuttered windows, all its held breath.

Vessany under curfew was a city I almost didn't recognize, and yet it was more myself than the daytime city ever was. Empty streets. Lanterns hooded. The sound of boots somewhere ahead, always ahead, always just around the next corner. This was the city I had lived in secretly for years—the city of things unsaid, of type set but never printed, of glances exchanged over censored columns. Now everyone lived there with me.

The first checkpoint was at the Tanner's Bridge, and we heard it before we saw it: lantern light swinging, the mutter of men who wanted to be anywhere else, the wet slap of the river underneath. I turned down the embankment path and let the dark do its work, walking slow, one hand on the boy's back, counting my heartbeats until the light fell behind us.

The second checkpoint we could not avoid.

It stood at the mouth of Rope Lane where it opened onto the fish market square—a brazier, two militiamen, a sergeant with a list. And no way around except the long way through the slaughterhouse quarter, which would take an hour we did not have and pass three more patrols besides.

The loaves were in my market basket. Six of them. Three with plates inside.

"Stop." The sergeant raised his lantern. He was young—younger than me, which seemed impossible and also exactly right for this revolution—and he had the particular exhaustion of a man who had signed on for glory and received instead cold feet and paperwork. "Curfew papers."

I produced Father's printing license, which was the only paper we possessed that any authority had ever signed, and watched him read it upside down in the lantern light.

"Print shop," he said.

"Almeida & Son." The lie about the *Son* was older than the war. "We do broadsheets. Bills of sale. Nothing political." Every word true, every word arranged to deceive. I was getting frighteningly good at it.

He looked at the basket. Of course he looked at the basket. A woman coming home from somewhere at midnight carries a basket; a basket invites the eye; the eye invites the hand. He reached out—

"Bless me, if it isn't Wendell Almeida."

Mother Ivetta materialized out of the fish market shadows the way only Mother Ivetta could—wide as a doorway, smelling of onions and woodsmoke, carrying a jug in each arm and an expression that suggested she had been standing in the cold for hours purely out of spite toward the weather. Behind her, as if conjured, came three of the market women: Widow Sarei with her shawl knotted over gray hair, fat Marta Odo with her sleeves rolled despite the cold, and young Bess whose brother had disappeared into the committee's cells in the spring and who had not smiled since, but who smiled now, grimly, at the sergeant.

"You know this woman?" the sergeant asked.

"She's my daughter," said Mother Ivetta, without hesitation, without blinking, with such authority that I nearly believed it myself. "Been helping me bake since the bell rang. You think bread appears in the morning by magic? It appears because women like us don't sleep, and soldiers like you—" she looked him up and down, the way she looked at fish she suspected of being yesterday's "—should be grateful."

"We're under orders to search all baskets—"

"Search mine too," said Marta Odo, thrusting her own basket forward, and then Widow Sarei's, and then Bess's, four baskets suddenly presented like a row of drawn knives, and something in the arithmetic of the moment shifted. Two militiamen. Four women plus one. A square full of dark windows behind them, and behind those windows, other dark windows, all the way to the river.

The sergeant looked at the baskets. He looked at the women. He looked, briefly, at me, and I understood that he knew—I would never prove it, but I understood it—that some part of him had done this arithmetic already and arrived at the same answer history was arriving at all across Vessany that winter.

"Get home," he said, to all of us, to no one. "All of you. Curfew's for your own protection."

We walked through the mouth of Rope Lane in a body, five women and a hidden child and twelve pages of dead minister's truth in a basket of bread, and none of us spoke until we were three streets gone, and then Mother Ivetta laughed once, low, like a rock settling.

"They never learn," she said. "They count soldiers. They never count grandmothers."

* * *

The underground press operated out of the back room of a coffin-maker's shop in the Marsh Ward, which struck me as either the bleakest joke in Vessany or the most honest, and I have never decided which.

The coffin-maker himself—a narrow, silent man named Pell—opened the door on the third knock pattern, admitted us wordlessly, and led us down a corridor lined with unfinished boxes to a room where a battered hand press sat between two coffins like a patient between visitors. Its owner was waiting: a stooped woman of sixty named Odile Ferrant, once a printer in her own right, once proprietor of the *Vessany Chronicle* before the bureau confiscated her type, and when she unwrapped the first loaf and broke it open and saw the gleam of lead in the crumb, she put both hands over her mouth like a girl.

"Thirty-one pages," I said. My voice cracked on the number. "Unaltered. Corvel's own text, verified twice against the original packet. Names, dates, signatures. Everything the committee wants softened, and everything the royalists want burned."

Odile lifted a plate from the bread with fingers that trembled slightly, tilted it to the lamp, and read. The room went very quiet. Even the boy, awake now in my arms, seemed to hold his breath.

"My God," she said at last. "He names them all. Both sides." She looked up at me. "You understand what happens when this runs? Not victory. Not justice." Her voice was gentle, which made it worse. "Chaos. The kind that eats its parents."

"I understand that whoever prints it first decides what it means," I said. "And I'd rather it be a coffin-maker than a committee."

Odile studied me for a long moment. Then she turned to Pell. "Paper. Ink. Wake the boys. We run tonight—all night, whatever the press can bear—and finished sheets go out with the milk carts at dawn."

She began pulling off her rings, preparatory to work, an old printer's habit, and paused with one ring half off her finger. "The child," she said. "There's a woman in the Marsh Ward, a laundress, trustworthy. He shouldn't sit beside the press during a raid."

"He doesn't leave my sight."

"Wendell." She used my name like a key. "You've done the hard part. Let someone else carry him an hour."

I looked down at the boy. He had fallen asleep against my shoulder, one fist still closed around nothing, his face slack and utterly untroubled, as though the century belonged to him and always would. And I thought about the note in Tomas's hand: *forget this face.* Eighteen months ago, and the face had become the fixed center of my life, the register mark against which everything else aligned.

"No," I said. "Where I go, he goes. That's the whole point of what we're printing. Nobody gets handed off. Nobody gets forgotten."

Odile held my eyes, then nodded slowly, and took off her second ring.

* * *

We printed until the lamps burned low and the sky behind the single high window went from ink to slate. The press clattered in that coffin-lined room like a heart refusing to stop, and Pell and his boys pulled sheet after sheet, and Odile read proof aloud in a low voice, and I sat on a coffin trestle with my son asleep on my chest and listened to Minister Corvel's testimony fill the room page by page—his steady, damning sentences, his refusal to flatter either faction, his last paragraph, the one I had cut with my own hands into copper and lead:

*A nation is not saved by the story that comforts it. It is saved by the record it does not dare to destroy.*

By dawn the sheets were stacked and sewn into milk-pail false bottoms and flour sacks and prayer book covers, and the milk carts rolled out into a city holding its breath, and Odile gripped my hands across the press bed and said, "Now it belongs to everyone. God help us all."

I walked home through the waking streets with the boy and an empty bread basket and the strange lightness of a thing finally set loose. At the corner of the Paper District I stopped, because there was a carriage standing outside our shop.

Black lacquer. Government crest on the door. And in the doorway, speaking quietly to Father where he stood with the morning invoices in his hand, was a man in a fine gray coat who turned as I approached—a historian's face, courteous and patient, the face of a man who had never needed to raise his voice in his life.

"Mademoiselle Almeida," he said, with a small bow. "I've been hoping to make your acquaintance. May we speak? About the late Minister Corvel—and about what a young woman of your talents might yet become."

Behind him, my father's eyes found mine, and in them I saw that he was afraid, and that he was proud, and that he intended to stand in that doorway for as long as God allowed him.
