## 17. The Victor's Historian

He came on a Tuesday, in the hour when the light through the shop's front windows turned the dust to gold and made even the censor's banned-word list, still tacked by the door, look almost ornamental. Wendell was at the stone, correcting a job forme for funeral announcements—there were so many funerals now that the announcement trade was the last reliable business in Vessany—when the bell above the door rang with a soft, civil note, not the shove-and-jingle of soldiers.

The man who entered wore no uniform. That was the first thing she noticed, and the second was that this meant nothing anymore. The committee's men had learned that cloth tells less than bearing. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, dressed in a good grey coat brushed clean of ash—he must have come through the paper district, where the ruins of the Duarte shop still smoked three days after the fire—and he carried nothing but a walking stick and a leather folio under one arm. His hands were ink-stained at the cuticles. Old stains, worked deep. A man who had spent his life at desks and archives, not presses.

"Signor Almeida?" he asked, and when her father looked up from his ledger, blinking, the visitor corrected himself with a small, courteous bow. "Forgive me. Signora Almeida. I was told the shop answers to the daughter."

Behind her, at the far bench, Tomas went very still over his composing stick. Wendell felt the silence stretch between them like wire.

"You were told correctly," she said. "Though I'm only the type cutter. My father owns the press."

"And yet." He set the folio on the counter with the care of a man laying down a relic. "May I? I am Aurelio Vance. Historian to the Committee of Public Records. You will have heard of the committee."

Everyone in Vessany had heard of the committee. It had been formed six weeks ago, in the first flush after the Bastion fell, its name printed in gold on broadsides pasted to every wall: *the people's memory shall be kept honest.* Since then it had kept honest the names of two hundred arrested men, most of whom had not been heard from again.

"We've done work for the committees before," Father said carefully, from behind his spectacles. "Bills of sale. Notices."

"I don't come for notices." Vance smiled. It was a good smile, unhurried, the smile of a man who had all morning and knew it. "I've come because I understand you are holding something the new republic needs."

*The testament.* She did not look at the type loft. It took everything she had—the whole discipline of a lifetime spent being unremarkable—to keep her eyes on his face, to let them go mildly blank the way a shopwoman's eyes go blank when a customer haggles over pennies. Somewhere above them, on the other side of the floorboards, Anselmo slept in the blanket nest among the spare cases, and she prayed to no particular saint that he would stay asleep, that his breath would not rise up through the boards like smoke through a roof.

"I'm afraid I don't know what you mean," she said.

"Of course not." Vance drew off one glove, finger by finger. "Let me tell you what I do know, and you can correct me where I err. I know that Minister Corvel, before his death, wrote a full account of the palace conspiracy—the names of the royalist captains who arranged it, and also certain men who now sit on committees and call themselves patriots. I know that account survived him. I know it came into this city. And I know—" he paused, and the mildness of it was worse than any threat— "that a child came into this city at roughly the same time. A child of Corvel's blood."

The funeral announcements swam in front of her. She heard her own heartbeat, quick and shallow, and forced her hands to stay flat on the stone.

"That's a great deal of knowing," she said, "for a historian."

"It is my entire profession, signora. Knowing things is all I have ever been good for." He said it without pride or false modesty; it sounded almost like grief. "I have spent thirty years watching men write lies into ledgers and then die, and the lies outlive them, and someone like me comes along afterward to sift the ashes and call the truth recovered. I am tired of sifting ashes. For once in my life I should like to hold the truth while it is still warm."

From the back of the shop, Father said, "If such papers existed, and we had them—which we do not—you'd be asking us to hand them to a government. Which government, signore? The one that shot Corvel was a government too."

"The one that shot Corvel," Vance said quietly, "is dying in ditches as we speak. You've seen the lists. Yes—it was a government. All governments are. But this one, for the first time in the history of this benighted coast, has asked a historian what actually happened, and promised to print the answer." He spread his hands. "I know how that sounds. I know what you think of us. Burned shops make poor arguments for our sincerity. But signora—Signor Duarte refused the committee's contract, and Duarte is dead. Refusal is no longer a position. It is a diagnosis."

* * *

He asked if he might sit. He asked if there was tea. He complimented the specimen book open on the counter—*that italic is your own cutting, isn't it? Remarkable hand*—and Wendell understood, with a cold professional clarity, that none of it was accident. This was a man who disarmed the way other men drew pistols, and he had studied her the way she studied letterforms: every serif of her life noted and catalogued before he ever walked through the door.

He knew about Anselmo. Whatever else happened, that fact sat in her chest like swallowed lead. If Vance knew, the committee knew, and if the committee knew, then the days of hiding the boy among the type cases were numbered—not because anyone would hurt an infant, but because an infant was leverage, and both factions had learned leverage from the same teachers.

"I'll speak plainly," Vance said, when the tea had been poured and mostly ignored. "The committee wants the original testament. Not copies—we know copies exist, or may exist; rumors are the one crop this city grows in abundance. The original, in Corvel's own hand, for the national archive. In exchange—"

"Here it comes," Father murmured.

"In exchange," Vance went on, unoffended, "the Committee of Public Records offers the Almeida press the official commission. All state printing. Declarations, laws, the proceedings of the tribunals, the new constitution when it is written. Your name"—and here he looked directly at Wendell, and his voice dropped into something gentler, almost intimate— "*your* name, Signora Almeida, on the colophon of every document the republic produces. The woman whose typesetters cut the letters of the new nation. There are men on that committee who would kill for that honor, and there are women in this city who will tell their grandchildren they bought bread from you."

She laughed before she could stop herself—a short, ugly sound—and Vance tilted his head, inviting her to share the joke.

"Forgive me," she said. "It's only—I've spent nine years in this shop making myself small. Small enough that censors walked past our window. And you're offering to hang my name on every wall in Vessany."

"Is that so terrible?"

"It's a target," Father said flatly. "Every regime falls. We've watched two fall this year. The man who prints the victor's truth is the first man the next victor visits."

"A fair point, signore, and I won't insult you by pretending history disagrees with you." Vance folded his gloved hands. "But consider what happens if you refuse. The testament surfaces eventually—it always does—and it appears without context, without the archive, without verification. Every faction prints the excerpts that serve it. The royalists publish the pages naming republican conspirators; the committees publish the pages naming royalist captains; within a year no one alive will know what Corvel actually wrote, including us. The document dies of a thousand copies." He leaned forward. "Or: the original goes into the archive, the full text is printed once, verified, whole, with the committee's authority behind it—and it becomes *the record*. Unarguable. Permanent. Signora, you are a craftsman. Which lasts longer—a truth shouted in a square, or a truth set in lead?"

It was, she had to admit, the exact argument she had used on herself. Sitting up nights with the minister's pages, she had told herself the same thing: *set it true, set it whole, let the lead hold it.* And here was a silver-haired stranger handing her own conviction back to her with a government's seal on it, and that was the horror of him, that he was not lying, or not entirely, that the trap was built from true sentences.

Because she had read the testament. All of it. She knew the pages that named royalists and the pages that named republicans, and she knew which ones the committee would want "verified" and which ones would be lost in the archiving, mislabeled, water-damaged, regretted. A sanitized testament in an official archive would outlast every copy of the true one. Permanence was exactly what it would buy. Permanence for the lie.

"You've thought about this," she said. "You've rehearsed me."

"I rehearse every argument I care about," Vance said. "Yes. I confess it. I read Corvel's list of names—or rather, I was shown a summary of it—and I have spent every night since deciding what kind of man I am willing to be." For a moment the polish cracked, just a hairline, and she saw underneath it a tired clerk of history who had catalogued too many graves. "I cannot fix the committee, signora. I cannot fix the age. I can fix one document, one record, one small island of what-actually-happened, and I need the original to do it. Help me build the island. That is all I am asking."

* * *

He let the offer sit while he finished his tea. That was another technique, she supposed—letting silence do the persuading, letting the customer talk themselves across the counter. And it nearly worked. That was the thing she would remember afterward with shame and fascination in equal measure: how close it came.

Because she was tired. Nine years of invisibility, months of terror, a burned district, a friend gone from the market, a child who called her *Wenna* because he had no one else—and here was a lawful, reasonable man offering to make the terror stop. Legitimacy, it turned out, smelled like tea and grey wool and clean gloves. It did not smell like blood at all until you looked at the cuffs.

She caught herself composing the surrender. *I'll bring the packet tomorrow. The original, as requested. And the boy—what arrangement can be made for the boy?* She caught herself measuring Vance's coat for the tailoring of her conscience, and the recognition frightened her more than the censor's men ever had, because the censor had come with soldiers and she had known instantly what she was; this man came with reasons, and reasons wore her own handwriting.

"Wenna—"

Anselmo's voice, thin and sleep-blurred, from the loft.

The word dropped into the room like type from a shaken case. Vance's eyes went up to the hatch. Above it, small feet pattered across boards, and a small voice said, "Wenna, the letters felled down."

Nobody moved. Wendell's heart slammed once, hard, and then—strangely—went quiet. There was no pretending left. She saw Tomas by the far bench with his hand hovering near the iron paper knife, saw Father's knuckles whiten on the ledger, and understood that the next minute would decide what all of them were.

"He's awake," she said, evenly. "My son's son. Orphaned this spring." The lie came out worn and thin, a coin rubbed smooth from handling, and she watched Vance weigh it and find it light.

"The letters fell, did they," he said softly. "Children love type. They always do. Something about holding the alphabet in your fist." He rose, took up his folio, and settled his coat. At the door he stopped. "You needn't answer today. The committee's patience is real but finite—say, three days. Consider, signora, that I am probably the best friend you have in this city. Everything I said about the testament dying of a thousand copies is true. And consider also—" he glanced once, briefly, at the loft hatch— "that the child's safety and the document's fate are, from the committee's perspective, the same question. Keep the papers, keep the heir, and you are a rebel with hostages. Surrender both, and you are the republic's printer, and the boy is simply a printer's grandson. Legitimacy is a lantern, signora. It lights whatever it's held over. I'd think about which way you want it shining."

Then he bowed—actually bowed, to her, to her father, even a small nod toward Tomas—and the bell rang its civil note, and he was gone into the ash-smelling afternoon.

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. Upstairs, Anselmo could be heard patiently restacking his fallen letters, narrating each one to himself. *A. B. Wenna.*

Tomas broke the silence first. "Three days."

"Three days," Wendell agreed.

"He knows about the boy. Which means the committee knows, which means—"

"I heard him, Tomas."

Father removed his spectacles and pressed his thumb and forefinger against the ache between his brows. When he spoke, his voice had the flat calm she had learned, in the bad weeks, to fear more than shouting. "He's right about one thing, you know. The one who holds the original writes the record. Vance understands that. The committees understand that. Even the widow—Corvel's daughter—whoever she is, she understands it." He looked up at Wendell, and behind the clouded lenses his eyes were suddenly, terribly clear. "The question is whether *we* understand it. Whether we mean to write it, or only to survive being written."

Wendell looked up through the hatch at the faint sound of a toddler's voice spelling letters aloud in the dark of the type loft, and she felt the answer assemble itself in her chest the way a line assembles in a composing stick—letter by letter, irreversible, ready for the stone.

That night, long after the lamp in the front room had been trimmed and her father's footsteps had stopped crossing overhead, she heard sounds from the composing room that did not belong to any hour: the slide of cases, the click of type meeting type, the slow deliberate rhythm of a man setting words he never intended to take apart.

Her father's door stood open. His bed had not been slept in.

The composing room lamp was burning.
