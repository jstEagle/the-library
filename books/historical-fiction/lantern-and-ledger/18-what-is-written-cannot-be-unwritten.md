## 18. What Is Written Cannot Be Unwritten

The bolt on the print shop door was older than the revolution, older than the minister who had died for his handwriting, older—nearly—than Senhor Almeida himself, and when he drew it shut that evening it made the same sound it had made for forty years: a sound like a full stop.

He had told Wendell he wanted a night alone with the press to true the platen. She had looked at him the way she looked at misaligned type, as if she could see the lie sitting crooked among the truth, but she had said nothing. That was the trouble with raising a child in a house of letters. They learned to read you.

"Lock it behind me," he said. "And don't come before dawn, whatever you hear."

"Father—"

"Whatever you hear," he repeated, gently, and touched her cheek, and turned away before she could make him say something true.

Through the door he heard her footsteps recede toward the lodging house across the yard, and then the smaller sound beneath her footsteps, which was the child's breathing, carried on the wet air like a second heartbeat. The boy had begun sleeping against her shoulder now instead of the type loft. Good. When the knocking came—and it would come—the child should be far from this room.

He lit two lamps and one candle. The lamps were for the work. The candle was for himself, because a man setting out on a long passage deserves his own small light, one the work cannot claim.

* * *

The testament lay where they had hidden it, inside the hollowed cases under the galley shelf, wrapped in oilcloth like something butchered. He unwrapped it slowly, by lamplight, and let himself look at the pages again as he had not allowed himself to since the censor's visit.

Minister Corvel's hand was beautiful even now, even knowing what the beauty had cost. A narrow, climbing script, each ascender reaching upward as though the words themselves might escape the page and testify aloud. Almeida had set this hand before, decades ago, when Corvel was only a young deputy drafting pamphlets against the grain tariffs, and even then the man wrote like someone leaving instructions for his own memorial.

The document named names. That was its whole terror and its whole worth. It named the royalist ministers who had sold the grain contracts, and it named the republican committee men who had taken royalist silver while shouting for liberty in the squares, and it named them in equal measure, without favor, the way a ledger records debts on both sides of a page. Wendell understood this; he had watched her understand it, there in the composing room, the night she found Tomas's altered passages. His daughter had a compositor's conscience: what was set must be true to copy. Not prettier. Not safer. True to copy.

But the committee did not want true to copy. The committee wanted an enemy list dressed as history. And Tomas—Tomas, who had once been a stranger at their door in the rain, who had given them the child and the packet both—Tomas had reset the passages under orders and under fear, and Almeida could not even hate him for it, because fear was a currency every man in Vessany was spending these days.

No. That was not quite honest either. He could hate him a little. Hate was also a kind of ledger entry.

The old man spread the original pages across the stone floor where the light fell, weighted their corners with quoins, and knelt over them, knees cracking, the way he used to kneel over proofs when his back was young and his eyes were not yet turning the world's fine print into weather.

Forty years he had printed what others wrote. Contracts, hymnals, tide tables, the city directory, wedding announcements for people whose grandchildren now shouted in the streets. Forty years of other people's words passing through his hands clean and unimproved and unbetrayed. He had always thought of himself as a ferryman of language: he carried meaning from one shore to another and asked no questions about why it wished to cross.

Tonight he would carry the most dangerous cargo of his life, and he would ask no questions, because the questions had all been answered already, answered in fire when the Paper District burned, answered in the widow's coin and the censor's list of banned words, answered in his daughter's face when she held the boy and finally admitted she loved him. There are moments when a man's silence becomes a thing he has chosen, and chosen long enough that it hardens into complicity. He had been invisible forty years. Invisibility, he had learned too late to teach it, is a kind of complicity.

Not tonight.

* * *

He began, as he began every job, with the count.

Corvel's testament ran to eleven pages in the minister's hand. Set in the shop's pica body, justified to the forme they could lock up in the old press—which was all the press they had left, the second having burned with the Ferrandis shop across the street—it came to thirty-two lines per page, four pages per forme, three formes. Eight thousand ems, give or take, of the truest sentences ever to pass through the House of Almeida.

He washed his hands first. This was not superstition exactly. Lead remembers touch; grease and sweat foul the matrices; and besides, a man should come to such work clean, the way he would come to an altar. His wife had believed in altars. He believed in type. Between those two faiths their daughter had never stood a chance.

The case stood open before him, the compartments laid out in the order his fingers had known since boyhood, since apprenticeship, since the first time old Ferro—who was Tomas's grandfather, though neither man ever said so—had put a composing stick in his hands and said, *the letters go where the truth goes, boy, and nowhere else.*

Almeida took up the stick in his left hand and the first page in his right, and began.

*In the name of the record, and against all parties, I set down what I have seen and signed.*

The words went into the stick one at a time, upside down and backward, as all truth enters the world before it is printed: reversed, unreadable to the hasty eye, requiring patience and a mirror and a press. He worked the way water works, steadily, without hurry, drawing letters from their boxes—e, e, e, the hungry letter, always running short; t, i, n—the muscles of his hand finding them without instruction from his dimming eyes, because the eyes had failed years ago and it was the fingers that still read.

That was the secret nobody outside the trade ever understood. Compositors do not read with their eyes. They read with their hands. And so his blindness, which the censors and the committees and even his own daughter believed had retired him, was in fact no obstacle at all. The committee's historian had come to the shop weeks ago and looked at the old man's clouded eyes and discounted him entirely, spoken past him to Wendell, offered *her* legitimacy, as if he were furniture. Let them discount him. The world had always mistaken weak eyes for a weak man.

Line by line, the minister rose out of the galley shelf and stood upright in metal.

*I saw Minister Duvane accept the grain contract terms from the Count of Serrelle on the ninth of October, in the west gallery of the Ministry, and I heard him say, laughing, that the poor of Vessany would eat their belts before winter's end.*

*I saw Committee Deputy Maro receive a sealed purse from the same Serrelle agent on the twelfth of December, through the offices of the harbormaster, and I hold the harbormaster's own confession in my keeping.*

*I saw, and I did not speak, because I loved my life more than my country. Let this page stand as my late correction.*

That last sentence slowed his hands. Not because the type was difficult but because the humility of it was unbearable—a minister of the crown, murdered in a doorway for knowing too much, using his last testament to confess his own cowardice before accusing anyone else. Corvel had gone to his grave with his accounts balanced. Almeida thought of his own ledger: forty years of jobs done cleanly, done quietly, done invisibly. What was his confession? Only this: that he had printed ten million true words and never once bled for them.

Until tomorrow. Tomorrow the blood, if blood there must be. Tonight, the lead.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight he stopped to trim the lamp and discovered he had been weeping, or perhaps the weeping had been happening for some time and he had simply declined to interrupt the work for it. Grief is like justification, he thought, wiping his face with the heel of his hand: the spaces between words must expand or contract according to the demands of the line. There was no room tonight for grief at full width. It squeezed itself into the gaps and let the sense stand.

He ate a little bread Wendell had left. He thought of her mother, who had died of a fever in this same building twenty-two years ago, in the room above, while below the presses ran overnight printing funeral sermons for somebody else's dead. He thought of how he had held the infant Wendell in this very room, at this very bench, and how her fists had been so small and already gripping.

Then he thought about what would happen after dawn, and made himself think it through completely, the way he proofed a page—every error hunted, every ambiguity resolved—because a man who knows the cost of a thing should pay it knowingly or not at all.

If the forme was locked and the press dressed when the soldiers arrived, they would destroy it, and the type would scatter, and the sentences would return to the general chaos of lead from which he had raised them. Therefore the forme must be printed before they arrived, which meant beginning at first light, which meant the press would still be warm when they broke the door. Very well.

They would demand the type. He would refuse. He knew this about himself the way he knew the location of the em-quads: without doubt, without drama. He had spent his whole life stepping around conflict the way a compositor steps around a dropped letter, and tomorrow he would step directly into it, and the strangest part was how little it felt like courage. It felt like craft. You do not surrender a locked forme. It is not done. There are rules older than governments, and the guilds kept them, and the rules say: what is set must be printed, and what is printed cannot be unwritten.

Perhaps they would kill him. He turned the thought over and found it surprisingly light, the way lead surprises you, heavier than it looks until you hold it. He was sixty-eight years old. His eyes were going. His wife waited—he permitted himself the belief—in whatever country the faithful dead inhabit, and she had never once in her life asked him to be safe. She had asked him to be honest, which was harder and more dangerous, and he had managed it in everything except the large things, and now, at the end, there was one large thing left.

The boy, though. The boy he prayed about, briefly and without ceremony, the way he did everything: *let the child remember the smell of this room, if not the man.*

* * *

The third forme was nearly set when he heard the footsteps.

Not at the door. Above. On the roof of the paper warehouse across the yard, where the night watch sometimes walked, and he paused, stick in hand, an em-quad suspended between box and line, listening the way deaf men listen better than the hearing.

It passed. Wind in the eaves, a loose slate, the city settling itself like a great animal preparing for the morning's violence. Tomorrow the palace guard wavered or it didn't. Tomorrow the testament ran from a hundred hidden presses or none. He had done what he could about the *or none*. The rest belonged to the city.

He returned to the line.

*I leave these pages to whoever keeps them, trusting not their loyalty but their craft. Ink does not take sides. It takes dictation.*

Yes, thought Almeida. Yes, Minister. That was the whole of the trade, written plain by a dying man who had never set a line of type in his life. He copied the sentence into metal with more care than he had given any sentence in forty years, and when it stood finished in the stick he held it up to the candle—not to read it, his eyes could not, but to feel it, the little ridges of the letters like braille, like a message meant for fingertips, like something a blind man might be permitted to know.

Then he transferred it to the galley, and squared it, and went on.

Dawn was a gray seam under the door when he locked the last forme. Three galleys stood ready on the stone, each holding four pages of the unaltered testament, every name intact, both factions indicted, the minister's confession included, nothing softened, nothing sharpened, true to copy. He dressed the press with hands that shook only slightly, oiled the ways, wetted the first sheet of paper—the good paper, the stock he had hoarded against exactly this—and cut a proper supply of ink, working it smooth with the brayer until it sang faintly under the rollers.

Then he sat down on the stool by the candle, which had burned nearly to nothing, and rested, because the last thing remained and it deserved a fresh man.

The last thing was this: he took a scrap of paper and, in his careful, old-fashioned hand, wrote a single line, and folded it, and placed it under the candlestick where Wendell would find it. He wrote:

*Set true to copy. Forgive your father his slowness; some sentences take a lifetime to compose.*

He looked around the shop. The type cases in their ranks, the hanging galleys, the paper smelling of size and dust, the press itself squatting in the corner like a patient ox—all of it worn smooth by his hands and hers and Tomas's and, before theirs, other hands going back to the founding of the city. A good room. A room where things became real. Whatever happened here in the next hours, the room would remain, and rooms remember.

Far off, in the direction of the palace, a bell rang once and stopped. Wrong hour for bells. Wrong bell altogether—one of the alarm towers, or perhaps a signal, or perhaps merely the city clearing its throat before it spoke the day.

Almeida stood, took the first sheet, and laid it on the tympan with the tenderness of a man dressing a wound or a christening, and reached for the lever.

Outside, boots were coming down the lane. Many boots, in step, unhurried, the sound of an official morning.

He pulled.
