## 19. The Presses at Dawn

### Wendell

The first sheet came off the press at the hour when even the gulls were silent, and it came out damp and warm, like something newly born.

Wendell pulled it from the tympan with both hands, not trusting herself to touch it with fingers that shook. The ink was still wet enough to shine. She held it up to the lamp and read the opening line of Corvel's testament—the true line, the unaltered one, set by her father's hands over nine hours of darkness—and she felt the words go through her like a nail driven clean.

*I write this knowing that no court will hear me and no confessor will absolve me, and therefore I address myself to the only judge left: whoever is holding this page.*

Below, in the press room, the machine breathed and struck and breathed again. Her father had forbidden all of them to sleep. Tomas worked the frisket with the economy of a man who had stopped pretending he might live forever, and Father Almeida stood at the stone, wetting each sheet, feeding each forme, his bad eyes so close to the type that his beard nearly brushed the lead.

They had printed two hundred sheets when Wendell heard the boots.

Not one pair. A cordon's worth, moving up Tanner's Lane in step, muffled no more than discipline required. She knew the sound the way a fisherman knows weather—she had heard patrols pass the shop for years and had learned, with pride, to make herself and everything she loved small enough to be overlooked.

There was no being overlooked tonight. Two hundred sheets of testimony sat stacked on the stone like loaves.

"Back door," she said. "Now."

Her father did not stop feeding the press.

"Papa." She caught his sleeve. "The plates are packed. The child is upstairs with Marta's woman. *Go.*"

He looked at her then, and in the lamplight his cataracts made his eyes look frosted, like windows in winter. "Someone must pull the sheets," he said mildly, as if discussing the weather, as if soldiers were not forty seconds away. "A press abandoned mid-run spoils the forme. Ink dries in the counters. And these words—" he pressed his palm flat against the stack, "—these words have waited three months in hollowed-out type cases for someone to treat them gently. I will treat them gently."

Boots on the lane. A fist against the shutters across the street.

"Wendell." His voice changed—the voice he had used to teach her letters, to correct her kerning, to tell her once that smallness was a kind of armor. "Take the proof copy. Take the boy. You are the compositor now. I am only the old man who owns the shop."

"You're my father."

"Yes," he said, and smiled, and turned back to the press. "That too. Go through the paper storeroom, out the coal hatch, into Mirella's yard. Don't stop for anything you can replace."

She took the proof copy—folded it into the oilcloth sling beneath her breast where it lay over her heart like a second heartbeat—and ran for the stairs.

Behind her, the front door came down.

### Tomas

Tomas Ferro had spent eleven years learning exactly how much weight a door could bear, and he threw himself against the shop's front door with the full knowledge of what doors could bear and what they couldn't.

The first blow from outside split the wood along the grain. He set his shoulder against it, braced his heel on the frame, and became, briefly, a piece of the architecture.

"Ferro!" A voice he knew. Sergeant Ollivier, who bought cheap almanacs off them every January and always overpaid, always with a wink, as though the wink settled some account between them. "Ferro, open in the name of the Provisional Committee!"

"The shop is closed," Tomas called back, and felt absurd, and kept his shoulder where it was.

"We know what you're printing, man. Step aside and be counted as an employee. Stand there and be counted as an accomplice. It's your choice, but make it now."

Through the gap under the door he could see lantern light sliding across the cobbles, six men at least, eight. Through the doorway behind him he could see the press still striking—Father Almeida would not stop, would not even slow—and he understood with sudden, sickening clarity that the old man had never intended to run. That the whole night, the locked door, the lamps trimmed low, had been a craftsman arranging his own final composition: type, ink, paper, pressure. All the elements brought together. All that remained was the impression.

Another blow. The hinges shrieked.

Tomas thought of the card in his pocket—the royalist agent's card, then the committee's summons, then the money he had refused, then the money he hadn't refused fast enough. Eleven years of keeping accounts. Here, at last, was a debt he could settle in a single payment.

"Almeida," he said, without turning his head. "The window in the storeroom. Tell me the girl went through it."

"She went," said the old man's voice, steady under the rhythm of the press. "And the child before her. God goes with them, or someone does; it amounts to the same thing."

"I'm going to hold this door until you've pulled the last sheet."

"Don't be a fool, boy. Pull sheets yourself. There's room for two at the stone."

There was not room for two at the stone. There was barely room for one honest act in Vessany that morning, and Tomas Ferro intended to spend his where it mattered.

The lock gave. The door bowed inward an inch, and through the crack he saw the bayonets, and beyond them, impossibly, the sky going grey at the edges—dawn arriving on schedule, indifferent as water, as if this were any other morning in the history of the port city.

He thought, strangely, of the night he had stood on the widow's step in the rain with a bundle inside his coat and a note written in his own hand: *forget this face.* He had told himself it was mercy. He had known it was cowardice—delegating the impossible to a stranger because a stranger owed him nothing. Fourteen months of watching that stranger carry his burden without complaint.

The door burst inward.

He did not move from the doorway. He was, he discovered, very strong.

### Wendell

The coal hatch dropped her into Mirella Fontaine's washyard, where the laundry lines hung heavy with sheets gone grey from river-washing, and the baby woke and began to cry.

"Hush. Hush, love, hush." She gathered him against the oilcloth and the hidden pages and rocked him among the wet linen like a woman comforting herself with her own shadow. He was fourteen months old; he had opinions about being woken; he expressed them at volume while somewhere behind her, separated by a wall and a lifetime, the press fell silent.

She froze. Waited for the silence to break into shots.

It didn't. Not yet. What broke instead was the bell of Saint-Aubin, ringing the hour, and then—no, she was wrong—it wasn't the hour. Five strokes, then again, then again. Alarm. Someone in the belfry who read testaments, or someone paid by those who burned print shops, or both.

Smoke on the wind. Not her shop's smoke—too far east. The paper district. They had fired it at both ends, then, the way you fire a field you mean to clear.

Wendell Almeida had spent her whole life perfecting invisibility, and standing in a stranger's washyard with a baby on her hip and her father's death sentence drying on two hundred sheets behind her, she finally understood what her smallness had cost, and what it had purchased, and that the ledger between the two had come due.

Marta's woman appeared at the yard door in a nightgown, candle in hand, and did not ask questions, which was its own kind of eloquence. "Mirella says take the river path. The market women are out already—they'll pass you along like a loaf nobody wants to account for."

The market women. The invisible ones. Bakers' wives and fish-gutters and rag-pickers, women whose names appeared on no list because lists were written by men who never looked down.

She went.

The lanes toward the river were chaos braided with purpose. Smoke rolling down from the paper district in fat black ropes; the glow of the fires painting every wall the color of the inside of a furnace; running feet everywhere, most of them fleeing, some of them carrying—she noticed—a surprising number of loose sheets, passed hand to hand, read by matchlight. The testament? No. Too soon. Rumor, then. Rumor outrunning truth the way sparks outrun flame. But the truth was coming. It was in the sling against her ribs, and it would be on every corner of the city by noon.

At the mouth of Cordwainer's Row a checkpoint had grown up around a barricade of overturned carts, and soldiers were turning people back with the flat, bored cruelty of men following orders they expect to disown later.

A fish-wife named Odile—Wendell knew her face from the market, had never exchanged ten words with her—took one look at Wendell, one at the baby, one at the sling, and hooked her arm through Wendell's arm.

"My sister," Odile announced to the sergeant, loud enough to be tiresome. "Come to nurse our mother, God keep her, poor soul, if the smoke doesn't finish her first. You want to answer for that, sergeant? You want to explain to the committee why a dying woman lost her nurse?"

The sergeant looked at Odile, at the basket of smelts she had apparently been waiting all night to use as a prop, at the squalling child, and performed the oldest arithmetic in Vessany: the cost of trouble against the value of obedience.

"Move along," he said.

They moved along. At the corner Odile pressed the smelt basket into Wendell's hands and vanished back into the crowd without giving a name, and Wendell understood that this was how it had always worked, this was how everything had ever survived anything: hand to hand, in baskets, under shawls, unwritten, unseen, until the moment the invisible chose to be seen.

The river path took her south, toward the underground press in the tannery cellar where Tomas had arranged it weeks ago—if Tomas still lived, if anyone at the shop still lived—

She would not think it yet. She would carry the pages. That was the work. Carrying was the work.

Behind her, above the roofs of the paper district, a new column of smoke climbed into the brightening sky, and she knew its origin the way you know your own house in a dream. Tanner's Lane. She stopped walking. The baby, mercifully asleep again, breathed against her neck.

She did not go back. Her father's last lesson stood in her chest like set type, immovable: *Don't stop for anything you can replace.*

Nothing behind her could be replaced. Everything ahead of her could not yet be imagined.

### Father Almeida

The ninth sheet after the door came down was the last one, and Joaquim Almeida pulled it from the press and laid it atop the stack and stepped back, and considered the forme still locked in the chase—Corvel's testament, every word, set true.

The soldiers filled the press room. Their officer was young and frightened, which was the worst combination, and he had a pistol pointed somewhere in the region of Tomas Ferro's spine, and two of his men were already moving toward the stone with the particular confidence of men who have been told exactly what to destroy.

"That's seditious material," the officer said. "Surrender the forme."

Joaquim took off his apron, folded it, and laid it over the stack of finished sheets, protecting them from the dust his own motion raised. An old habit. A good habit. He had taught it to his daughter when she was seven years old, and she had done it her whole life, and someday—if there was a someday, and there would be, because paper floated and fire was local—someday someone would find these sheets with an apron folded over them and understand that a printer had finished his run.

"Young man," he said, "do you know what this is?"

"It's the thing I've been ordered to destroy."

"It's the testimony of Minister Corvel. Written before he was murdered. Naming the men who ordered it—in your faction and in theirs." He watched the young face work through this, watched the arithmetic fail, watched fear win, as it usually did. "You should know what you're burning. That's all. A printer owes his readers that much."

"Surrender the forme, old man."

"No."

He put both hands on the chase—not to defend it, he was past defense, but simply to stand with it, the way a man stands with his family in the last photograph. He thought of his wife, thirty years dead. He thought of Wendell, alive, moving through the city somewhere with his grandchild, carrying the record. He thought of a lifetime of setting other people's words and how strange and right it was that the last words set in this shop belonged to neither of them—to a murdered minister, to history itself, to whoever was holding the page.

The officer gave the order. Joaquim Almeida did not move his hands, and so the bullet that was meant for the air beside his head found the space beside it instead, and the second found nothing beside it at all.

He fell against the stone. The forme held. Type, locked tight, does not scatter when it falls; it keeps its sentence. This, too, he had taught his daughter.

The last thing he heard was the soldiers shouting about fire, and the first thing he had ever truly heard—lead speaking to paper—was already out in the world, two hundred sheets strong, drying in the dark.

* * *

### Wendell

From the rooftop of the tannery, at sunrise, Wendell watched the smoke of her father's shop join the smoke of the paper district and rise into one long gray column over Vessany, and she did not weep, because the child was awake now and watching her face, learning it, the way he had learned his letters from pieces of lead in her father's palms.

*Forget this face,* the note had said. Well. The face had been remembered. The face had been fed and washed and taught; the face had a name now, though she would not give it to any committee; and somewhere below her, spreading through the waking streets faster than any fire, was the other thing that could not be forgotten—two hundred sheets of truth, moving hand to hand, and every hand that took one becoming, however briefly, its author.

She kissed the top of the boy's head and turned toward the cellar door, where the underground press waited, where the plates needed cleaning and the sheets needed folding and the day—the terrible, enormous day—needed printing.

Down in the streets, someone began to ring every bell in the city at once.
