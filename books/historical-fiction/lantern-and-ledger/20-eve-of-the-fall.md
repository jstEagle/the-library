## 20. Eve of the Fall

The first copy left the shop folded inside a fishmonger's apron, and by the time the bells rang nine it had been read aloud twice in the Salt Market and passed, warm from other hands, to a cooper who could not read but carried it anyway, like a relic, toward the ropewalks.

We watched this happen the way one watches weather. From behind the shutters of the print shop, my hands still black to the second knuckle, I watched a woman named Marta—whom I knew only as the wife of a man who bought our cheap almanacs—unfold the testament in the mouth of the alley and begin to read it to anyone who would stand still. Her voice was not loud. It did not need to be. Words that have been forbidden carry their own trumpet.

*They will tell you the minister died of his own despair,* Corvel had written, in a hand so upright and disciplined it seemed to be bracing itself. *I am writing so that you will know he was pushed.*

Marta read until her voice gave out. Then a boy of perhaps fourteen took up the pages where she had stopped, stumbling over *embezzlement* and *armistice*, and the crowd corrected him gently, word by word, the way a village once taught its priests.

My father should have been standing beside me at the shutter. He was instead at the press, running his palms along the frame as though checking a horse for fever, though the press had done its work hours ago and stood cooling in the dark like a spent animal.

"Three hundred sheets," he said, for perhaps the sixth time since dawn. "Wendell. Three hundred."

"I can count, Papa."

"Three hundred sheets, and every one of them a hanging offense." There was something in his voice I had never heard before—not fear, and not pride either, but some alloy of the two that had no name in our language. He laughed, a small dry sound. "Your grandfather printed hymnals. Safe as bread. And here we are."

Here we are. I looked at him—at the milky cataract swallowing his left eye, at the ink worked so deep into the creases of his knuckles that he would go to God with a compositor's hands—and felt something enormous and unnameable rise in my throat. I did not say any of the things I was thinking. In a trade built on exactness, we had both become masters of what goes unsaid.

* * *

Tomas came back from the paper district after midnight smelling of smoke and someone else's wine.

"The whole quarter's awake," he reported, dropping onto the composing stone and rubbing his face with both hands, which smudged it further. He had been doing that all week—the rubbing, the not-meeting-of-eyes—and each time I saw it I thought of the royalist's card he had pocketed and said nothing about. Tonight there was no time for suspicion; suspicion, like everything else, had been overtaken by events.

"Read me the streets," my father said.

Tomas grinned, and there was something feral and young in it that made him look nineteen instead of thirty. "The Ropewalk is singing. Actual singing—some filthy song about the Intendant, four verses, and they've added a fifth since I came past. The garrison on Quay Street turned out at eleven and stood in ranks for an hour, and then—" he spread his hands— "they didn't do anything. They just went back inside. Half of them took off their cockades first."

"Half?"

"You can't shoot a man for reading aloud," Tomas said. "That's the trouble with it. They've been given orders nobody wants to be the first to obey." He reached into his coat and produced a sheet, folded to the size of a prayer book—one of ours, I recognized the impression of our type, our little shop's fingerprint, and felt a jolt of vertigo at seeing it come back to me already transformed. "Found this nailed to the customs house. Someone's recopied page six by hand. All those names. They've underlined them."

Underlined them. Some stranger, some clerk or student I would never meet, had sat down with one of our three hundred sheets and a pencil and drawn lines beneath the names of the men who had arranged a minister's murder. That act—small, private, illegal—struck me harder than anything the committees or the censors had ever done. We had set those letters in lead believing ourselves alone. We were not alone. We had never been alone.

"There's more," Tomas said, and now the grin drained away. "The official gazette came out at dusk. The palace version." He laid it on the stone next to our testament, and the two documents lay there side by side like an accusation and its denial. The gazette insisted Minister Corvel had died of a seizure, mourned him in four hundred words of syrup, and denounced 'the seditious fabrications of anonymous criminals' in the fifth paragraph.

"They misspelled his name," my father said quietly. He had taken up the gazette and held it an inch from his good eye. "Corvel. They've spelled it with an *f*. Corfevel." He set it down with great care, the way one handles something dead. "A chancellery that cannot spell the name of the man it killed. And they wonder why nobody believes them anymore."

* * *

I did not sleep. The child slept, which is the arrangement mothers and fathers accept without negotiation, and I sat in the loft above the type cases with him breathing against my shoulder and listened to the city turn over in its sleep like a man about to wake.

He is nearly two now, and heavy in the way that healthy children are heavy, and I have carried him so long that when I put him down my arms continue to ache in his shape. He sleeps through gunfire now. There has been occasional gunfire for a month; he has learned its rhythm the way farm children learn thunder. Tonight the sounds were different—no shots yet, but boots, many boots, moving in directions boots were not accustomed to moving, and once, around two o'clock, a long rolling murmur from the direction of the palace square, like surf, like a crowd that had decided to stop dispersing.

I thought: *this is happening because of us.* Not because of committees or generals or the price of bread, or not only because of those—but because three hundred sheets had gone out from a failing print shop on Cooper's Lane, and strangers had copied them by hand, and underlined the names, and sung them into songs, and now the truth had more regiments than the palace did.

I also thought: *Papa is downstairs with a loaded pistol he does not know how to use,* and *the widow—or daughter—who called herself Corvel's widow is somewhere in this city tonight, and I do not know whose side the committee believes she is on, or whether anyone is on anyone's side anymore,* and *if they come, I take the boy down the coal chute and through the tanner's yard and I do not look back, I swore it, I swore it to myself in the dark weeks ago.*

But mostly I thought about the cooper. The man who could not read, carrying the folded pages through the ropewalks anyway, handing them on like a coal from a dying fire. All my life I had believed invisibility was safety. I had cut my letters small and kept my eyes down and prided myself on being a woman the soldiers' gaze slid off like rain off oilcloth. And it had taken a murdered minister's orphan sleeping among the type cases to teach me what I actually was: not invisible. Unseen by choice. And a city full of people choosing, tonight, to stop being unseen—the market women, the deaf cooper, the boy reading aloud in the alley with his voice cracking—all of them stepping out of the crowd into the light of whatever came next.

The child stirred and murmured something syllabic and sank back down. I pressed my lips to the crown of his head. He smelled of milk and lamp oil and lead dust, the smell of our whole strange household, and I thought: *your father wrote this night before you could walk, and you will not remember it, and someday I will have to tell you that your inheritance arrived as three hundred sheets of contraband paper.*

Somewhere below, the street door latch clicked.

I was on my feet before my heart finished its next beat, the boy gathered against my chest, one hand finding the coal chute rope in the dark. Then I heard my father's voice, low, and Tomas's answering, and the particular scrape of the cellar door, and I understood: they were moving the forme. Burying the locked chase of type under the floorboards again, in case the night brought visitors. Two old conspirators and a reformed traitor, doing housework for history.

I did not go down. I stood in the loft with the child and listened to the small industry of men protecting words, and through the loft window I watched the sky over the palace district, which had been dark all my life on nights like this and was now faintly, impossibly orange—not fire, I realized, or not only fire, but lanterns. Hundreds of lanterns lit in windows facing the square, the old signal, older than any of the factions: *we are awake, we are watching, we are counted.*

The city had decided. It had simply, quietly, in the course of one night, decided.

* * *

Near dawn, Tomas climbed to the loft. He stood in the doorway with a candle, and for a moment neither of us spoke. The pocket where he kept the agent's card—I found myself looking at it, and he found himself noticing me looking, and something passed between us that was not forgiveness, exactly, but its raw material.

"Quay Street garrison opened its gates," he said. "An hour ago. They let the crowd march through the courtyard and didn't fire. A lieutenant shook hands with the cooper—you know, the one who carries the pages?" He shook his head slowly, as if to settle the facts into place. "It's over, Wendell. Maybe not today, maybe there's fighting still, but it's over. The story got out ahead of the soldiers and you can't outrun it now. Truth moves faster than cavalry. Nobody ever budgets for that."

Below us I heard my father moving among the cases, beginning the day's work out of pure habit—distributing type, breaking up last night's formes, putting every dangerous letter back in its proper compartment. The ledger closed. The alphabet restored to order.

"Wendell." Tomas's voice changed, and I turned. His face in the candlelight was terrible with some knowledge not yet spoken. "There are men asking at the end of the lane. Not soldiers. Committee men, with a carriage. They're asking which house is Almeida's." He swallowed. "And Wendell—they're smiling. They've brought flowers."

The child woke in my arms and did not cry. Outside, far off toward the palace, the bells began—an unfamiliar pattern, joyous, ragged, rung by many hands—and I stood in the loft with my whole world balanced against my chest and understood that the revolution had come to thank us, and that from everything I had learned of revolutions, their gratitude was the most dangerous thing about them.
