## 22. Lantern

The harbor at Sablemère smells of tar and cold salt, and in the eleven years since I first stepped off the packet boat with a child on my hip and a satchel of type against my chest, I have never once stopped being grateful for that smell. It is the smell of elsewhere. Of a place that agreed, without ceremony, to let us be strangers.

The shop is small—smaller even than my father's had been, if such a thing can be believed. A ground-floor room with a bowfront window fogged by the sea air, a secondhand press I bought from a widow who was giving up the trade to marry a fisherman, and a loft above where we sleep when the wind isn't trying to come down the chimney. I named it nothing. My father had believed shops should carry their maker's name like a promise; I had learned in Vessany what promises cost. The sign above the door reads only **PRINTING — BOOKS & BROADSIDES**, in letters I cut myself, in a face plain enough to be honest.

Eleven years. The boy who arrived at our door wrapped in oilcloth, with a note that said *forget this face*, is tall now, all wrists and questions, his hair the dark brown of wet type metal. He has Corvel's brow—I saw it once, in the one portrait that survived, a minister standing stiffly beside a window—and he has my father's habit of squinting at small things as though daring them to be illegible.

This morning he set his first line.

I did not plan it. These things should not be planned; they should arrive the way weather arrives, or grief, or a stranger at your door in the rain. He came down before dawn because he cannot help it—he has my father's dawn habit too, though he never met the man—and found me already at the stone, distributing last night's work back into the case. The lamp was burning low. The harbor bells were counting five.

"Teach me," he said. Not *will you teach me*. Teach me. As though it had been decided somewhere above both our heads, the way everything in my life seems to have been decided.

I put the composing stick in his hands the way my father put it in mine: handle toward the wrist, jaw open, held not gripped. "Left to right," I said. "Upside down and backward. Everything worth saying comes out looking wrong until it's pressed."

He chose the type himself. I watched him run his thumb along the cases—the same gesture, God help me, the same exact gesture, his fingers finding the grooves my father's fingers had worn into ours—and then he went past the jobbing faces entirely, past the display roman I use for playbills, to the old case in the corner under its cloth. The Vessany face. Cut by my father's hand, or by mine when his eyes began to fail and he guided mine along the graver like a man steering a boat he no longer trusted himself to hold.

"What's this one?" he asked.

"The truth," I said, which is what my father used to say, meaning: the oldest thing we own.

* * *

I should set down how we got here, because a ledger left unbalanced is worse than none at all, and this chapter—if chapters are what these years are—is the last page of the account.

We left Vessany on the morning after the presses. I have told that night so many times in my head that it has worn smooth, like a coin passed through too many hands: Tomas holding the door with his shoulder and his whole furious life; the forme of unaltered testament still locked on the bed of the press, sheets coming off wet while soldiers hammered; my father standing between them and the type with his ruined eyes turned toward the sound of boots, refusing to move because a man does not abandon a sentence mid-composition, not even for a bayonet. They tell me—in the way exiles are told things, in fragments carried by sailors and smugglers—that he did not cry out. That he said something about justification. That the soldiers hesitated, one heartbeat, two, as though they understood they were killing not a man but a record, and that some of them would dream about it for the rest of their lives.

Tomas survived. Barely, and bent, but he survived, and it was Tomas who put me and the child and the proof copy into a herring boat out of the paper district, pressing the satchel of type into my hands with both of his. "You'll need letters," he said, "wherever you land," and then, because Tomas could never leave a debt unspoken: "I owed him. Now I owe you. Don't forgive me fast."

It took him nine years to find us. When he walked into this shop last spring, gray at the temples and walking with a stick, the boy looked up from sweeping and said, "You're the man from the stories," and Tomas looked at me over the child's head and said, "She tells them wrong. I'm worse than the stories." Then he sat down on my only good stool and wept like a man paying out a rope, hand over hand, and neither of us said anything foolish or kind, because we were printers, and printers know that some sentences must simply be set and left alone.

The testament itself needs no accounting here. It ran in Vessany on the day the old regime fell, exactly as my father set it—every name, every signature, every confession Corvel wrote in the weeks before they cut his throat, implicating royalists and republicans alike with the terrible evenness of a man who knows he is dying and will not flatter anyone with his last breath. The new government tried to claim it. The old government's ghosts tried to disown it. Both failed, because paper, once loosed, is a bird that answers to no one. Within a month there were copies in four languages. Within a year, schoolchildren in countries I will never see were reciting its opening lines without knowing whose hands had cut the type.

They offered me honors, once. A medal, a stipend, a commission to write the official history of the fall. I burned the contract in the harbor basin the same week I buried my father's ashes beside the river at home—or beside what used to be the river; the city has renamed half its streets since, as cities do, laundering themselves in language. Power corrupts every victor. I have watched it happen here too, gently, gradually, in the new republic across the water whose ministers smile more than the old ones did and jail almost as many. Which is why this shop prints what it prints: almanacs, primers, shipping lists, and—quietly, on the nights the customs men are drunk—pamphlets for people who still believe a press is a lantern and not a mirror.

But that is politics, and this morning was not politics. This morning was a boy, a stick, and forty-one pieces of lead.

* * *

He worked the way beginners work, which is to say slowly, wrongly, and with a concentration so fierce it seemed to heat the air around him. He picked letters upside down, checked each against the copy, muttered when he mistook a b for a q—as every compositor since Gutenberg has done—and started over twice. I sat where my father would have sat, by the window, pretending to read proofs, watching him sideways the way you watch anything you are afraid to jostle.

He had chosen his sentence himself. I want that recorded. I gave him nothing but the case and the craft; the words were his.

They came, in time, from the testament. He has known the document his whole life the way other children know a psalm. I read it aloud to him when he was small—not the names, not at first, but the shape of it, the cadence of a dead man insisting on the record—and later, when he was old enough to ask the right questions, he read it himself, silently, for a week, and then asked me which parts were true. All of it, I told him. That's what made it dangerous.

His sentence was from near the end, where Corvel stops accusing and starts confessing:

**A TRUTH HIDDEN IS A DEBT THAT COMES DUE WITH INTEREST.**

Forty-one characters, spaces included. It took him an hour and ten minutes. When he locked the line into the stick and turned it right way round to read it, his hands were shaking, and he looked at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors and from memory: the expression of a person who has just discovered that a sentence can be *held*—that words have weight, literally, gram for gram, and that whoever holds them holds a little of the future.

"Well?" he said.

"It's tight," I told him, taking the stick. "Your spacing needs work. You've got three ems loose after HIDDEN and you've doubled a t." I ran my thumb over the face of the type, feeling the letters stand up like teeth. "And it's perfect. Don't touch it. First lines should keep their scars."

"Whose type is this?" he asked, and I knew what he was really asking, and I answered it plainly, because he is Corvel's son and deserves plainness the way other children deserve sweets.

"Your grandfather Almeida's. And mine, after his eyes went. And now yours." I put the stick back in his hands. "That's how it works. Type doesn't belong to anyone. It belongs to whatever gets printed with it."

He pulled a proof at noon—ink too heavy, impression uneven, the kind of pull a master would reject and a father frames—and he hung it above the stone where my father used to hang the day's first proof, and he stood looking at it for a long time with Corvel's stubborn brow furrowed and my father's squint, and I stood behind him and did not say any of the thousand things crowding my throat, because he already knew them. Children of secrets always know.

* * *

Now it is evening, and the fog has come off the water the way it does in autumn, folding the masts of the fishing fleet into gray wool, and it is time to light the lantern.

Every printer in Sablemère hangs a lantern when working past dark—an old custom, part signal, part superstition, meant to tell the harbormaster that honest labor is underway and the light should not be reported as trouble. Mine is brass, dented, bought secondhand with the press. For eleven years I have lit it at dusk and hung it in the bowfront window, and for eleven years no soldier has ever looked twice at it. That is the whole miracle of exile, if it is a miracle: to become ordinary. To be a woman of no faction in a town of no factions, printing almanacs, raising a boy, hanging a light.

But tonight I stand at the match a moment longer than usual, because I understand—for the first time fully, the way you understand things only at their ending—that the lantern is not mine. It was never mine.

It was my father's faith, carried out of a burning shop in the hands of his daughter. It was Corvel's testament, carried out of a sealed room in the arms of a clerk who could not destroy a child. It was Tomas's guilt, carried across nine years and a sea. Every hand it has passed through has warmed it and been warmed by it, and tomorrow—next year, someday soon, he is ready whether I am or not—it will pass into the boy's hands, and he will carry it wherever history next decides to break its ledger and start a new page. Perhaps here. Perhaps home, when home is ready for him, which it may be sooner than any of us expects; the pamphlets I print say the ministries across the water are frightened again, frightened the way they were frightened in Vessany, and frightened men always reach for the presses first.

The wick catches. The flame stands up straight and yellow in the glass.

Behind me, the boy is washing the stone, humming—Corvel's cadence, though he doesn't know it, the testament's rhythm worn into him like letters into a well-used face. On the wall above him hang two proofs side by side: his first line, and beneath it, older, ink faded to the brown of dried blood, a sheet pulled at dawn from a press that no longer exists, in a country that has forgotten my father's name but quotes his sentences.

I hang the lantern in the window. The light goes out across the water, small and stubborn, the way all true lights are.

"Leave it burning," I say to the boy. "Night shift."

He looks up, and grins, and reaches for the case.
