## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The road into Bellhollow smelled of salt and coal smoke, and Isandro Vane counted her lies the way other travelers counted their coin.

One: she was Aldous Vane's nephew, come down from Correnvale to repair the observatory clock. Two: she had trained under her uncle these six years, and his death had left her the tools and the trade. Three: her name was Ivo. Short, plain, forgettable — a name that would slide off a garrison clerk's tongue like water off oiled steel.

She wore the name like a coat two sizes too large. She would grow into it, or she would hang in it.

The town rose out of the harbor fog in tiers: fish sheds at the waterline, merchant houses stacked above them, and at the crown of the hill, the observatory, its dome catching the last grey light like a coin held up to an eye that never blinked. Below it, the garrison walls. She made herself look at them only once. The flag on the pole was new; the stones beneath it were old enough to have heard her father's name read aloud from a scaffold.

*Hanged for treason*, the broadsheets had said, seven years ago. *Rebel leader Dain Vane, executed by order of Colonel Harrow.* She'd been fourteen. She'd learned then that grief was a thing you folded small and carried where no one could see it, the way smugglers carry knives.

Now she walked through Bellhollow's gate with that knife-shaped grief against her ribs, and a soldier looked at her papers, and looked at her face, and said, "Clockmaker?"

"Apprentice," she said, letting her voice sit low. "Journeyman, near enough. Master Vane is dead."

"Condolences." He stamped the paper without interest. To him she was a trade, not a person. That was the whole architecture of her disguise, and it held.

* * *

The observatory keeper who met her at the top of the hill was not what she expected. Edda Sollen turned out to be a woman past sixty with white hair cut short as a man's and eyes that did not fix on anything — eyes that moved a half-beat behind the world, tracking sounds instead of sights. Blind, then. Isandro had heard the name whispered among clockmakers: Sollen, who mapped the stars by ear and arithmetic, who had once been famous and was now a rumor kept alive in an observatory nobody visited.

"You're late," Sollen said.

"The road was."

"Roads are always something." The old woman stood aside. "Come in before the damp gets into your gears. Damp is the enemy. Well — one of them."

The interior smelled of brass polish and lamp oil and, faintly, of dust burned hot by lenses long out of use. Isandro followed the sound of Sollen's cane up a spiral stair and into the clock room, and stopped.

The master clock dominated the room like a cathedral dominates a village. It stood three men high, its case black walnut gone silver at the edges, its dial painted with stars instead of numerals because this clock did not merely tell the hour — it governed every clock in Bellhollow. Harbor bells, garrison watches, the townhouse chimes: all of them took their time from this machine, transmitted by telegraph wire and signal flag, so that ships could calculate longitude off the observatory's noon and soldiers could march on schedule and the whole town moved in step to a heartbeat housed here, under the dome.

And the heart was failing. She could hear it from the doorway — a hitch in the escapement, a hesitation like a stutter in a sentence, the kind of fault that began as a whisper and ended, months from now, with the entire town's time drifting into chaos.

"When did it start slipping?" she asked.

"Eleven weeks ago," Sollen said. "It loses four seconds a day. The garrison noticed last month. They wrote to your uncle — to the firm." A pause, precise as a tick. "You came instead."

"My uncle died in the spring. I finished his commitments."

Sollen's blind eyes found her with unnerving accuracy. "How old are you, journeyman?"

"Nineteen."

"Hm." The old woman said nothing else, which was worse than suspicion. She rapped her cane twice on the floor. "The case key is on the hook. Don't scratch the walnut. My husband built that case before he was my husband, and I will haunt you."

Isandro set down her bag and opened it, and the fear receded the way it always did when her hands found their work. This was the country she was a citizen of — the country of wheels and arbors and jeweled bearings, where a girl could be competent without being questioned, where skill was a mask that fit better than any false name. Her father had taught her that much before the army took him: *A craft is a shelter, little star. Build it strong and live in it.*

Her uncle had taught her the rest. Her uncle, who had known exactly whose child he was sheltering, and who had never once asked her to be anyone but what she pretended to be. She owed the dead that much: to do the work perfectly.

She climbed the maintenance ladder and opened the case, and the smell of the movement rose up to meet her — old oil gone varnish-stiff, the green breath of tarnishing brass. The great train lay before her like a sleeping animal. She began the diagnosis the way her uncle had drilled into her: hands still, ears open. She listened to the clock breathe.

There. Third wheel. Something catching, releasing, catching. She eased the wheel free and turned it under her lens, and there it was: a hairline crack in the arbor, invisible to any eye without magnification, widening by fractions with every revolution. Eleven weeks ago someone had over-torqued the great weight during winding — or the metal had simply aged past its endurance, the way everything aged, the way towns aged, the way reputations cracked along lines no one saw until they gave way.

"I'll need to make a new arbor," she called down. "I have stock in my kit. Three days, maybe four, if I machine it properly rather than quickly."

"Properly," Sollen said. "Always properly. The garrison wants it fast. The garrison can want."

* * *

She worked until the lamp burned low, and Sollen sat below with her star tables and read them aloud to herself in a low murmur, running her fingers across the raised notations she'd punched into the paper herself. Twice the old woman asked questions that were not about clocks. Where she'd trained. Whether Correnvale had much call for horology. Whether her family had been in the trade long.

Each answer was rehearsed. Each answer cost her something to deliver smoothly. By the third question Isandro understood that Sollen wasn't making conversation. She was taking measurements — of Isandro, the way Isandro measured a cracked arbor: gently, precisely, looking for the flaw.

"I had an apprentice once," Sollen said, apropos of nothing, as Isandro packed her files away. "Years ago. Brilliant hands. He left the observatory for politics, which I told him was a downgrade." A silence. "He was hanged for it."

Isandro's hands went very still inside her bag. "That's a hard story."

"It's a true one. I find those are usually hard." The old woman rose, cane first. "Your lodgings are through the arch, the old instrument room. There's a stove. Mind the third floorboard; it complains."

At the door she paused, though she could not see the door, and knew exactly where it was. "Master Vane's nephew. You listen to the mechanism the way he used to. Before you touch anything." Then she was gone down the stair, her cane counting the steps like a slow second hand.

Isandro stood alone in the clock room with her heart hammering against the lie in her throat.

*Sollen knew her father.* Had taught him. And had just told her so — or had told a stranger boy named Ivo Vane a sad story about a dead apprentice, which was different, which meant nothing, which could mean everything.

She made herself breathe. She made herself climb back up the ladder and look at the great clock's face — the painted stars, the single hand sweeping toward midnight — and she made herself think like a craftsman and not like a fugitive's daughter. Four seconds a day. A cracked arbor. Three days' work.

She could do the work. The work was safe. The work was all that was safe.

But sleep, when it finally came in the narrow bed in the old instrument room, brought her father's voice saying *little star* in the dark, and the sound of a crowd, and the creak of a rope, and she woke before dawn with her hands aching from being clenched.

* * *

On the second morning, the soldiers came to the observatory.

Not for her — for the view. An officer and two men, measuring the hilltop with a surveyor's chain, muttering about sightlines and signal masts. Isandro stayed on her ladder inside the clock case, filing the new arbor blank with slow, even strokes, and listened to boots on the gravel below.

"The colonel wants the dome flagged by week's end," one of them said. "Himself is coming to inspect. Tomorrow, they're saying."

"He'll want to see the clock working. It keeps his schedules."

"It keeps everyone's schedules. Whole town runs off that thing."

Whole town. Whole garrison. Every bell, every muster, every patrol, every execution — for her mind supplied it unasked — timed to the machine her hands were currently rebuilding. She thought of the crack in the old arbor and wondered how many things in Bellhollow ran on parts that had already failed and simply hadn't given way yet.

They left. She filed. The arbor took shape under her hands, true to within a hair's breadth, and she fitted it that evening by lamplight, easing it home into the train with the tenderness other people reserved for infants. When the escapement took it up, the hitch was gone. The clock breathed evenly again, four lost seconds recovered, the whole town's heartbeat steadied by a nineteen-year-old girl with a dead man's name.

"There," she whispered, to the movement, to herself. "Properly."

From below, Sollen's voice floated up the stairwell. "It sounds right. I could hear it struggling even from here. Now it sounds—" the old woman considered "—honest."

Isandro almost laughed. If the old woman only knew what dishonesty lived under this roof, wearing her nephew's coat.

She slept better the second night. That was the mistake. Confidence is the crack that widens.

On the third morning she cleaned the clock's housing while the oil cured — a job she'd saved because cleaning meant opening drawers, compartments, the dusty cabinets built into the case's lower body where generations of keepers had stored spare weights, old pendulums, ledgers of windings going back decades. She worked methodically, drawer by drawer, wiping each piece and logging it in her book. Weights. Chisels. A broken second-hand from some earlier century.

In the deepest compartment, behind a false panel her fingers found before her eyes did, there was a scrap of paper folded to the size of a coin.

She should have logged it and resealed the panel. That was procedure. That was safety.

She unfolded it.

The paper was ledger-stock, good rag paper, ruled in faded columns — a corner torn from some official account book, years ago. Across it ran figures and short entries in a cramped, slanted hand, dense with abbreviations, columns of numbers beside columns of letters that refused to resolve into anything. Not accounts. Not a letter. Something between — numbers standing guard around words, words hiding inside numbers.

And the handwriting —

She knew it the way you know a scar. She had seen it on the flyleaf of the one book he'd left her. On the note tucked in her shoe the morning the soldiers came, the note she'd read so many times the ink had worn thin: *Be brave, little star. Be clever.*

Dain Vane's hand. Her father's writing, hidden inside the observatory clock, in a compartment sealed years before the observatory hired any Vane but her.

The clock struck ten above her head — steady now, honest, her own work — and the sound went down the hill into Bellhollow, into the garrison, into the ears of every soul in the town whose time belonged to this machine. Isandro knelt on the cold floor with her father's handwriting trembling in her fingers and understood, with total clarity, that she had not been summoned to Bellhollow to fix a clock.

Something had been waiting in this housing, patient as a spring under tension, for seven years.

For her hands.
