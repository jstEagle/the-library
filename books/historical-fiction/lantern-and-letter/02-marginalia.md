## 2. Marginalia

The master clock's housing came apart in the order of its own making. Isandro had learned that much from her uncle before he died — that every machine kept a memory of the hands that built it, and if you were patient enough, it would tell you where it hurt.

She knelt on the observatory floor with the case panels laid out around her like a disassembled argument. Fourteen of them. Oak, iron-bound, each one heavier than decency required, because whoever had commissioned this clock a hundred years ago had wanted it to look like it would survive the end of the world. It nearly had. Two centuries of salt air and neglect had gotten into the joints, and the case breathed damp the way an old man breathes in winter — slow, rattling, reluctant.

Three days she'd been at it. The movement was clean now, or clean enough; the escapement ran true after she'd replaced the pallets and re-cut the suspension. But the strike train still hesitated on the hour, dragging half a second like a man clearing his throat before saying something he'd rather not say, and Isandro did not yet know why.

She did know that the answer was in the housing. It usually was. Dust told stories if you let it.

The morning light through the observatory's east windows came down in long slanted bars, full of drifting motes, and Edda Sollen had already retreated to the far side of the dome with her charts and her brass rules, because blind astronomers did not need light and did not like to be watched working. They had exchanged perhaps forty words since Isandro arrived. That suited her fine. Words were doors, and doors were risk.

She worked the bottom panel free with a driver wrapped in cloth so the screws wouldn't scar. The wood groaned, gave, and came away into her hands with a soft exhalation of dust that smelled of oak tannin and mouse.

And something else. Something sharper. Ink.

She froze.

It was nothing, she told herself. A drop of old ink spilled during some repair, soaked into the grain. Clocks lived long lives and collected everything — hairpins, coins, letters, love notes, dead flies in congregation. She had pulled stranger things from clock cases. Once, in Corvasser, she'd found a wedding ring taped inside a regulator's baseboard with a note in a woman's hand: *He can have the clock. I'm keeping this.*

So she did not let herself think. She thought about screws instead, and torque, and the angle of the driver, and by the time the thought she'd been refusing arrived, her hands were already steady, because her hands had learned long ago that steadiness was a skill you could practice until it stopped being a lie.

She turned the panel toward the light.

Taped inside the recess where the strike-train weight passed — a place no one would look unless the strike failed, and no one repaired clocks who wasn't paid to — was a folded scrap of paper, flattened by two decades of pressure into something closer to board than paper. The tape had dried to amber and crumbled at her touch. The scrap came free.

Her father's handwriting.

She knew it before she knew she knew it. That was the obscenity of the body — it remembered what the mind had spent seventeen years burying. The forward slant, the tall ascenders, the way he crossed his *t*'s with a single impatient stroke that overshot the letter entirely, as if even his alphabet were trying to escape somewhere. She had been seven years old watching him write at the kitchen table by lamplight, pretending sleep so he wouldn't send her to bed, and the last thing she had ever stolen from that house was a laundry list in his hand, which she had carried in her shoe until the sweat dissolved it, and which she had mourned afterward like other people mourned locks of hair.

*Ledger paper.* Ruled columns, faint now, the grid printed in faded violet. Someone had cut a strip from a ledger page and written across it in that impatient hand — numbers, mostly, and fragments of words she couldn't parse, arranged in groups that refused to behave like sentences.

She read it four times. It stayed closed to her.

> 7/3 — K— 44/12 by the *gull* route, mark the second
> H. signs all. H. does not read.
> 9/11 — the third column lies. count what is not entered
> if they take me — L., the west case, under the—

The rest was gone, torn away. The strip ended mid-word.

Isandro sat back on her heels in the dust and the light and did not move for a long time. Somewhere above her, through two floors of the observatory, the great lens gathered morning and did nothing with it. Below, Bellhollow woke: cart wheels, a gull screaming over the harbor, the garrison drum beating the change of watch. Ordinary sounds. Sounds from a world in which scraps of paper hidden in clock cases meant nothing to anyone.

*If they take me.*

They had taken him. They had taken him on a Tuesday in October and hanged him on the Friday of the same week, and she knew both days the way you knew your own birthday, and for seventeen years she had known one other thing with the same granite certainty: that her father had been guilty of what they said he was guilty of, because to believe otherwise was to believe the world was worse than she could afford.

She had been careful for seventeen years. Careful as a profession, careful as a religion. She had buried Vane and dug up Ostrova and finally buried that too and become nobody's daughter at all — an apprentice with a dead uncle's name and a toolbox and a face that people described afterward as *unremarkable*, which was the finest compliment her line of work allowed.

And now here was his handwriting, folded into the belly of a clock in a garrison town, saying *if they take me*, which meant he had known it was coming, which meant —

Which meant she didn't know what it meant. The paper said *H. signs all. H. does not read,* and the paper said *the third column lies,* and none of it opened itself to her, because whatever language these were written in, it was not one she spoke.

The past didn't knock. That was the thing no one told you. It didn't knock and wait politely. It reached through the wall and took hold of your wrist.

* * *

She put the scrap in the inner pocket of her coat, against her ribs, and finished the housing because she was a professional and because her hands needed orders. The strike-train hesitation turned out to be a worn gathering pallet, exactly as boring as such problems usually were, and she fixed it in an hour and hated every minute of the work because her mind was elsewhere, turning the strip over and over like a coin she couldn't spend.

At noon Edda Sollen materialized at her elbow the way she always did — silently, precisely, a woman who navigated rooms by their acoustics the way other sailors navigated by stars.

"The strike is mended," Edda said. Not a question. She'd heard it run at noon.

"Half past will confirm it."

"It confirmed itself. You flinched at noon." Edda's face was turned slightly wrong, three degrees off true, aimed at Isandro's ear rather than her eyes. "You've flinched twice more since. Clockwork doesn't flinch, Miss Ostrova. What did you find in my clock?"

"My uncle's clock," Isandro said. "You inherited it with the observatory, but he maintained it twenty years."

"And he hid things in it. He was that sort of man. So are you, I think." Edda smiled without much warmth in it. Warmth wasn't the point of the smile; accuracy was. "I don't need an answer today. I mention it only so you know I noticed. Blind people are assumed not to notice things. It's our great tactical advantage, and I hate to waste it."

She turned and went, counting her steps along the rail. Isandro stood with the driver in her hand and understood that she had just been measured, weighed, and filed, and that the file was open.

* * *

She should have burned it.

She stood at the small stove in the observatory's ground-floor room that served her as workshop and bedroom both, the scrap in her hand, the fire breathing orange behind the grate, and went through the arithmetic for the fourth time. Burn it, and the past went back to being dead. Burn it, and she walked out of Bellhollow when the repair was done, collected her pay, and disappeared south where nobody had ever heard of Vane.

Keep it, and she was holding a document written by a hanged traitor, in cipher, hidden inside a clock in an army town, referring to someone named H. who signed things without reading them.

She held the paper over the stove. Her hand did not open.

"You're a coward," she told herself quietly, meaning it as praise. Cowards lived. Cowards burned things.

But the strip had her father's hand on it, and there was no fire hot enough for that, not anymore. Seventeen years she had managed the grief by making it small, folding it down tight as a map, and the map had just unfolded itself in her chest and it went further than she did. She couldn't burn the last handwriting of his she would ever hold. That was the whole of it. Everything else was decoration.

She folded the strip along its old creases, which knew her fingers better than she knew them, and put it back against her ribs.

Then she sat down at the workbench with lamp and magnifier and began the slower, colder work: reading it properly. She copied the characters onto fresh paper, isolating each group. Some entries were plainly dates — 7/3, 9/11, the year omitted. Some were plainly numbers. But between them ran a second script, letters that recurred in patterns too regular for names, too varied for figures. *K— 44/12.* *Mark the second.* *Count what is not entered.*

*Count what is not entered.* She wrote that phrase out alone and looked at it a long time. It sounded less like code than like advice. It sounded like something a bookkeeper would say, or a smuggler, or a man keeping honest records of dishonest ones.

Her father had been a coast surveyor, officially. Charts, soundings, tide tables. That was what the trial had said, and what the trial had said next was that his surveys had served the Republican smuggling lines, and that he'd drawn maps for men who moved guns, and that a surveyor's pencil could kill as surely as a musket fired at a picket. Colonel Harrow's name had been on the order. She hadn't known the name then — she'd been seven, and the adults had spelled around it — but she knew it now, because everyone in Bellhollow knew it now, because Harrow commanded this garrison, and because fate, whatever else it was, had a taste for bad jokes.

A surveyor. Keeping counts of what was not entered.

She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the dark bloomed red.

No. She was not going to do this. She was not going to stand in a room in 1847 and build her father an innocence out of half a torn strip of paper, because she had done that as a child every night for a year, built him whole and wronged and coming home, and the building had nearly killed her, and she had sworn off it the way reformed drunkards swore off drink — totally, daily, without exception.

Except.

Except that he had hidden this strip where only a clockmaker would find it. Not buried. Not thrown in the sea. Taped inside the strike train of the town's master clock, where it would sit safe until someone with her exact skills came looking for a hesitation on the hour. That was not the act of a guilty man hiding evidence. That was a message in a bottle, addressed to whoever came next. Addressed, possibly — the thought arrived cold-footed and unwelcome — knowing her uncle. Knowing the trade. Knowing that a Vane-trained hand would someday open that case.

*If they take me —*

They took him. And he had spent his last free weeks writing numbers into the belly of a clock, trusting the future to be curious.

Isandro blew out the lamp, because dawn was graying the window and because she could not look at her own copied notes one more time without either weeping or burning something. She put the copy flat between the pages of her tool inventory, where no soldier on earth would ever voluntarily look, and the original back against her chest.

Tomorrow she would finish the housing. Tomorrow she would be nothing but a competent apprentice again, dull as putty, forgettable as rain. Tomorrow the past could knock all it liked; she would not answer.

But lying in the narrow cot with the observatory ticking overhead — her tick now, her hours, the heartbeat she had wound and set and owned — she listened to it and heard something underneath, faint as a pulse felt through a wristbone. Not a flaw. She checked it against her memory of the train, gear by gear, and it wasn't a flaw. It was rhythmical. Deliberate. A second count laid beneath the first, almost inaudible, the way a coded letter hides numbers inside a laundry list.

Someone had built a whisper into this clock. Maybe decades ago. Maybe with the same tape, the same hands.

Or maybe — and this thought sat her upright in the dark, heart going hard — maybe the whisper had started three days ago, when she first set the pendulum swinging, and the clock had simply begun telling a truth it had been made to keep.

She lay back down. She did not sleep.

Outside, the garrison drums changed the watch, and somewhere below in the sleeping town a bell rang once, uncertainly, and stopped — as if it too had something to say and hadn't decided yet whether now was the time.

In the morning, she decided, she would ask Edda Sollen about her uncle's correspondence. About who else had ever maintained this clock. About what kind of man taught ciphers to surveyors.

In the morning. If the morning let her.
