## 3. The Colonel's Carriage

The knock came at half past nine by the observatory clock — which meant nothing yet, because the observatory clock was the thing I had not finished repairing, and so it might have been half past eight or a quarter to ten in the honest world outside. I had come to think of time in Bellhollow as a room I was still sweeping.

Wren Callis was the one who opened the door. I heard it from my workbench on the gallery above, heard his boots cross the entry stones, and then a voice I did not know said, with the flat courtesy of a man accustomed to doors opening faster: "Colonel Harrow, to inspect the works. His Excellency's compliments."

I want to set down honestly what happened inside me in that moment, because later — when everything else had happened too — people would ask me how I could have stood in front of him and not screamed, not wept, not put a file between his ribs. Here is what happened: nothing. That is the training of fear. My father taught me locks before he taught me letters, and the first law of locks is that panic jams mechanisms. Something in me turned over like a tumbler finding its gate, and I went down the gallery stairs with my hands steady and my mind gone white and quiet as fresh snow.

He stood in the entry hall with his gloves held in one hand, a broad man going soft at the middle, silver at the temples, the kind of face that would be called handsome in a portrait because portraits forgive authority. He was not tall. He was not remarkable. That was the worst of it. Eleven years I had built him out of my nightmares into something with height and teeth, and here was a comfortable colonel smelling of bay rum and wet wool, admiring the stonework.

"Master Vane," he said, reading my uncle's name off the work permit Wren handed him. "You've done the escapement already? The town clock stopped dragging this morning. First time in a month I haven't felt late for everything."

"The drag was in the third wheel," I said. My voice came out level. I have no idea how. "Worn teeth. It'll hold until I cut a replacement."

"And the master clock?"

"Its heart is out on my bench." I tipped my head toward the gallery. "You're welcome to see it, Colonel. Most of it is older than the garrison."

He smiled at that, the small proprietary smile men wear for things they intend to own. "Lead on, then. I'm told the observatory has the best view in Bellhollow, and I intend to confirm it before the army improves it."

*Improves.* Wren's pen stopped for half a heartbeat over the visitor's book, then went on. I marked that. A clerk who flinches at words is a clerk worth remembering, though I didn't yet know why.

* * *

The stairs up to the clock room are stone, spiral, worn concave in the middle by two hundred years of feet. Harrow climbed them breathing hard and joking about it, and I kept three steps ahead so he could not study my face, and all the way up I was doing arithmetic I could not stop doing.

Eleven years ago, in the market square at Coldharbour, forty miles east of here, a man was hanged for treason. I was seven. They would not let me go to the hanging, so I went anyway, small enough to stand between the legs of the crowd, and what I remember is not the drop — I shut my eyes for that — but the drumbeat before it, and a soldier's red coat directly in front of me, and the paper the commanding officer held while he read the sentence aloud. I never saw the officer's face. I saw the paper. I have spent eleven years unable to forget the shape of a sheet of foolscap in a stranger's hands.

The order that sent my father to the rope bore a signature. I learned the name the way you learn a scar's location: by touching it in the dark, again and again, until you know it better than your own skin. *A. Harrow, Col., Comdg.*

And now his boots were on the stairs behind me, and the arithmetic said: he signed it in 1836; he cannot be more than sixty; the man climbing these stairs is fifty at most; the arithmetic says nothing, arithmetic lies, arithmetic is only fear wearing numbers.

I reached the clock room and stood aside and let him come into it, and watched his face while he looked at my work.

The master clock of Bellhollow Observatory fills most of one wall: a case of black walnut, a dial painted with stars instead of numerals — the astronomer's vanity, Edda Sollen calls it, though she cannot see it herself — and beneath the dial the movement, brass and steel, wheels within wheels, the great barrel wound by weights that descend through the building's core like an anchor line into the sea. Half of it lay dismantled across my bench in ordered ranks, each part cleaned and labeled in my own hand. To anyone who understands clocks, a dismantled movement is more intimate than a naked body. Every wear mark tells you where it has hurt. Every repair tells you where someone loved it before you.

Harrow did not understand clocks. But he understood displays, and mine was a good one.

"Well," he said. "Well. This is soldierly work, Master Vane."

I nearly laughed. It rose in my throat like a bubble from a drowned lung — *soldierly* — and I swallowed it and made myself say, "Thank you, Colonel," in the tone of a craftsman pleased by a compliment he doesn't fully follow.

"Order, method, every piece accounted for." He picked up the pallet fork from my bench — picked it up! — and turned it in his fingers, and I watched the most dangerous man in my life hold the smallest part of the largest clock I'd ever serviced, and I thought, with a clarity that frightened me more than anything else in the room: *if I wanted to, I know exactly where to drive this file to stop a man's heart.* Not true, probably. But thinking it steadied me, the way counting does.

"Who taught you?" he asked, setting the fork down precisely where it had lain. He noticed things, this man. I filed that away too, next to Wren's flinch.

"My uncle," I said. "Josua Vane. Dead these two years." Dead these two years was true. Josua Vane had been dead eleven years longer than that, being a fiction I had bought from a dying man in exchange for nursing him through his last winter, along with his papers, his tools, and his name. I had buried the real Josua Vane in an unmarked grave outside Dunmore and taken his place in the world the way you take over a watch: same hours, different hands.

"A family trade." Harrow nodded, satisfied. Men like him love it when the world arranges itself into families and trades. It makes their paperwork easier. "Then you'll appreciate the history. This clock has kept garrison time since before the last rebellion." He said *the last rebellion* the way other men say *the weather.* "There are officers in this town who set their watches by it and always have. When it falters, Bellhollow falters. So I trust you understand the urgency."

"I understand it, Colonel."

"Good." He moved to the window — the great south window, through which the transit telescope once sighted the meridian — and looked down at the town. Rain had moved in from the east, thin and persistent, graying the rooftops. From this height Bellhollow looked harmless: a harbor, a market square, slate roofs steaming, fishing boats drawn up along the quay like spoons in a drawer. "They tell me you came recommended from Dunmore. Letters and all."

"Letters and all," I agreed. The letters were genuine. The dead man had written them himself, in his last lucid week, recommending his nephew for any competent post, and there is no forgery so perfect as the truth written by the wrong hand.

"Hm." He turned from the window and looked at me directly for the first time since the stairs, and I understood that the whole conversation until now had been the approach, and this was the ground. "You'll forgive a soldier's habits, Master Vane. I make it my business to know who works inside my walls. An observatory is a high place. High places see far. You take my meaning?"

"Clockmakers don't spy, Colonel. We're too nearsighted." I said it mildly, professionally, holding up my right hand as if to show the fine tremor that twenty years of close work gives a craftsman. He laughed. It was, I am ashamed to record, a warm laugh, and I hated that it was warm, hated that some traitorous part of me responded to charm in a man whose signature I had traced in candle smoke on my bedroom wall at fourteen, over and over, practicing the strokes of the letters as if I could unwrite them.

But underneath the laugh, he was still looking. Not suspiciously. Assessingly. A man taking inventory. And I stood under that gaze with my heart going like the dead beat of the very clock on my bench — *tick, tick, tick,* each stroke enormous, silent, counted — and gave him back the bland, slightly tired face of a journeyman with sore eyes and a job to finish.

What saved me, I think, was my sex. Or rather, what he believed about it. In his world a woman in a workshop was furniture unless she was trouble, and I had arranged myself carefully as neither: cap low, jacket cut square, voice pitched in the narrow register between deference and boredom. He saw a person named Isandro Vane, nephew of a dead clockmaker, and his eyes slid off me the way water slides off waxed cloth. I had spent eleven years learning to be slid off. It is not a skill I would wish on anyone. It means you practice disappearing in mirrors.

"You've a steady look about you," he said finally, putting on his gloves. "I like steady people. There aren't enough of them." At the door he paused. "When will the master clock run?"

"Three days. Four if the weather turns foul."

"It has turned foul, Master Vane. It turned foul some years ago." He said it lightly, almost playfully, and left, and his boots went down the spiral stairs, and I listened to them diminish the way you listen to a fever break.

* * *

I don't know how long I stood in the clock room after that. Long enough that the rain changed character, from drizzle to proper rain, loud on the observatory dome. At some point Wren Callis came up with the visitor's book for me to countersign — army procedure, everyone who entered the tower was recorded twice now, once by the observatory and once by the garrison, as if a name written twice were somehow safer — and found me sitting on the floor with my back against the walnut case, my hands in my lap, doing nothing at all.

He set the book and pencil beside me without a word and would have gone. Then he stopped. He was a thin, precise young man with ink permanently shadowing the edge of his right hand, and a habit of looking at things sideways as if direct observation were an imposition.

"He comes here often?" I asked, to say something ordinary.

"Quarterly inspections. Sometimes monthly." Wren hesitated. "He liked your work."

"He said."

"You didn't like his liking it." It wasn't quite a question. I looked up at him and he looked at the rain on the window, sideways, and I thought: careful. But I was tired in the marrow, tired in the place where acting stops being performance and becomes weight, and what came out of me was:

"No man should praise a clock the way he praises a gallows."

Wren went very still. For a moment I thought I had shown too much, given the game away to a stranger in a garrison coat — and then I realized, with a strange lurch, that he hadn't even blinked at *gallows*. He had simply absorbed it, filed it, added it to whatever ledger he kept behind his eyes.

"There's rain coming in around the south pane," he said at last, in the tone of a man resuming an interrupted sentence. "The colonel noticed. He'll mention it next visit. You may want to have it seen to before he does." And he went down the stairs, leaving me the visitor's book and the pencil and the strange conviction that something had just been handed to me in code, and that the code was kindness.

* * *

That evening I lit the lamp in the clock room and worked, because work was the only prayer I knew. The escape wheel went back into its train. The pallets took their drop. I did not wind the strike train; I had promised three days and I would keep three days, because a reputation for exact promises is armor, and I needed armor now more than ever.

Because here is what I had not told Wren Callis, and would not tell anyone: Harrow had looked at my hands.

Not my face. My hands, when I'd held them up to show the tremor. And afterward, on the stairs going down, I had heard him pause — one boot on stone, a silence of perhaps two seconds — and continue. Two seconds. Nothing. Except that my father had the same hands as me. Wide palm, short ring finger, a scar across the left knuckle from a slipped chisel, and I had worn gloves in public for eleven years until the summer I decided that a man who hides his hands looks guiltier than a man who bites his nails, and had stopped, and had not thought about it in two years, and now thought of nothing else.

It was nothing. Of course it was nothing. He had paused to adjust his glove.

I sat back on the bench and looked at the dismantled heart of the master clock, all its bright parts ranked and gleaming in the lamplight, patient as bones, and I made myself say the truth of it aloud to the empty room, because my father used to say that a fear spoken plainly loses half its teeth:

"He was in Coldharbour in '36. He signed the order. He stood not thirty feet from me while they hanged my father, and today he shook my hand."

Shaking his hand had been the worst part. I had not mentioned that even in my own thoughts until now. His grip had been dry and firm and brief, the grip of a man who shakes forty hands a day and remembers none of them, and my hand had known his hand, my hand had *known* it, the way a body knows the shape of the thing that broke it, and I had smiled and said good evening, Colonel, and my hand had not shaken at all.

The lamp guttered. Out over the harbor the rain moved in sheets, and somewhere below in the town a bell rang ten o'clock — the town clock, the one I'd freed of its drag, keeping time now on my repaired wheel, marking hours for people who had no idea their day ran through my hands.

Ten o'clock. Three days until the master clock ran. Three days until quarterly-inspection-Harrow returned to check the south pane, and the visitor's book, and whatever else a man inspects when he is deciding whether a town is ready to be improved.

I picked up the pallet fork he had handled, wiped it clean of nothing, and set it back in rank.

And that was when I saw it — or rather, when the lamp caught it, a pale sliver wedged deep in the seam where the clock case met the floor, a corner of paper showing like a tooth. I pried it loose with a fingernail, expecting a scrap of Edda Sollen's star tables, an old invoice, dust made solid.

It was a strip of ledger paper, yellowed, folded small and hard as a seed. Columns of figures ran down it in a cramped, slanted hand — columns of figures with letters threaded through them that made no sense, cipher or shorthand or madness. And beneath the figures, unsigned, undated, a single line in the same cramped hand:

*They count the hours. Let the hours count them.*

I knew the handwriting the way you know a voice through a closed door. Before I could stop myself I said "Papa" out loud, into the empty clock room, into the rain, and the lamp flame bent as if answering, and I sat down slowly on my stool and read the strip again, and again, and the clock I was rebuilding ticked its borrowed, temporary time above my head, and outside, far below, Bellhollow slept in the colonel's peace.
