## 4. Requisition

Maren Ostrow heard the hammers before she saw them.

She was in the bakery on Cordwainer Lane, arguing the price of flour with Halver Brunt, when the sound came through the shutters — three flat knocks, a pause, three more. Not the rhythm of builders. Builders worked in runs. This was measured, deliberate, spaced like a man counting his own heartbeat, and it moved up the lane door by door with the patience of something that had all morning and all the soldiers in the world.

"Notice," Halver said, and the word dropped out of him like a stone into a well.

"They posted notices at the harbor last month," Maren said. "Yellow paper. Nobody read them twice."

Halver didn't answer. He was looking at his hands, which were white to the wrist with flour, as if he'd only just noticed them, as if they belonged to someone whose troubles were smaller. Maren had known Halver Brunt since she was seven years old and had stolen a bun off his cooling rack, and she had never once seen him afraid. She saw it now. It sat on him badly, like a coat cut for another man.

The knocking reached the bakery.

She went to the door because someone had to, and because standing still felt like admitting that doors could now be something to be afraid of. Outside, two soldiers stood in the thin gray light with a nail cask between them and a stack of papers under one arm. The older of the two had the paper raised already, hammer cocked back, before she'd finished opening the door — he'd been nailing notices since before dawn, and the motion had worn itself into him.

"Stand aside, mistress."

"That's my door."

"It's the army's door now." He said it without heat, without even interest, the way a man says *the tide's come in*. That was the worst of it, Maren thought later. Not the rudeness. The boredom.

He nailed the notice at the height of a man's eye, four strokes, brushed the paper flat with his palm, and moved on. The younger soldier handed her nothing. No copy, no explanation, no courtesy. They went on down Cordwainer Lane, and the knocking resumed, three and three, three and three, and behind them every door in Bellhollow grew its pale rectangle like a scar forming.

*By order of Colonel A. Harrow, commanding, the town of Bellhollow and its environs are placed under requisition for the use of His Majesty's forces.* Maren read it twice, then a third time, because the words kept sliding off. *All civil records, ledgers, registers, and rolls are hereby seized to the custody of the garrison clerk.* All markets closed except such as the provost should license. Curfew from dusk bell to dawn bell. Movement beyond the walls forbidden. *Contravention will be treated as aid to the enemy.*

Aid to the enemy. There was no enemy. There was fever at the harbor and ships that wouldn't land, and there was an army that had arrived as if the town were a thing conquered rather than a place where people lived. But the paper said *enemy*, and paper had a way of making itself true.

Maren stood in the street in her apron until her neighbor Pell Wicke came out to read his own door, and then Ostrey from the ropewalk, and the widow Sanne, and slowly the lane filled with people who did not look at each other but all stood facing their separate doors, reading the same sentences, the way a congregation faces its separate prayers.

"It's temporary," Ostrey said. "They did this at Marsh Ford during the border trouble. Six weeks, and gone again."

"Marsh Ford didn't have the fever," said the widow Sanne, who never spoke unless the words were going to cost somebody.

Nobody argued. That was the thing about dread, Maren thought — it didn't need to win the argument. It only needed to be left standing.

* * *

The townhouse had stood at the head of the market square since before anyone living was born, and the ledger had lived in it longer than any single family could remember. Maren had signed it herself, once, the day she took over her mother's stall after the coughing took her — her name, her trade, her street, her origin, *born Bellhollow, of Bellhollow stock*, in her own careful hand, witnessed by the town registrar, old Master Corve, who had pressed sand on the ink and told her she wrote a good honest name. Every soul in Bellhollow was in that book. Three generations of them. Births and deaths and trades and origins, who came from where and married whom and what they owed the common purse, all of it in Corve's spidery register hand or the honest scrawls of people signing what they'd been.

At midday they came for it.

Maren was in the square when the wagon stopped outside the townhouse, and she told herself she was there on errands, that she had salt to buy, and then admitted she had no salt to buy because the market was shuttered under the notice's terms and every stall in the square stood bare as a rib cage. Half the town had found reasons to be in the square. They lined the edges of it quietly, the way gulls line a quay waiting for fish, not daring, not yet, just watching.

Master Corve brought the ledger out himself.

That was the detail Maren would carry. They hadn't dragged him. A corporal had knocked, and Corve — seventy-one years old, gout in both feet, a man who needed two minutes and a rail to descend his own stairs — had gone up and fetched it down himself, carried it out in both arms like a child being given up, and laid it on the trestle board the soldiers had set in the square. He'd even brought the sand caster. He set it beside the book, a small automatic kindness, as though whoever received the ledger next would want to keep the ink properly.

"You'll want the key to the case as well," Corve said. "It doesn't sit right closed without it."

The corporal looked at the old man for a moment, and something crossed his face — brief, quickly put away — that might have been shame. Then he took the key, and the ledger went into a canvas satchel, and the satchel went into the wagon, and a soldier sat in the wagon bed with his rifle across his knees and the book between his boots, guarding names.

"That's us, then," said Pell Wicke at Maren's shoulder, very low. "That's every one of us, in a bag."

She knew what he meant. It wasn't sentiment, or not only. A ledger of names and origins, seized by an army, in a country where twenty years ago men had been tried on less than a page of testimony — everyone in the square could do that arithmetic, and everyone in the square included families who had done it before. Maren's own father had come to Bellhollow from the coast districts in the year of the unrest, and had spent the rest of his life being politely, carefully asked where exactly from, and had learned to answer with a shrug that satisfied nobody and gave nothing away. Names in a book were just names until someone with power decided which names were a problem. Then the book became a map.

Corve stood by the empty trestle a long moment, hands hanging. Then he gathered up his sand caster, which nobody wanted, and walked home through the middle of the crowd without looking at anyone, and his gait was steady, and Maren understood that he had chosen to walk steadily and that it was costing him everything he had.

* * *

By evening the ash metaphor made itself.

There was no fire — the fever ships burned their dunnage at anchor, and the wind brought the smell of it across the town all afternoon, tar and sickly smoke — but a fine gray grit settled on Bellhollow anyway, on sills and lintels and the lips of shutters, and the light through it went the color of old pewter. Maren swept her step before supper and by the time she'd carried the broom in, the step was grained again. She gave up. Some things you couldn't sweep. You could only wait for them to finish falling.

Her son Joss, eleven years old and fearless in the specific way of boys who have never yet met a consequence they couldn't outrun, had spent the day among the soldiers' picket lines selling apples at double price, and came home flushed with profit and importance.

"There's a colonel," he announced over the supper bowl. "Harrow. He came to the square on a horse, gray horse, and all the officers got quiet. Deller says he signed the notices hisself. Deller says—" and here Joss dropped his voice with the relish of a boy handling contraband, "—Deller says there's going to be a ship. One berth ship. For leaving."

"One berth," Maren said. "For a whole town?"

"Not one berth. One *ship*, and the berths go how the colonel says. Deller heard the sergeants talking. Whoever the colonel writes down goes. Nobody else. Fever rules, see — can't take more'n a list, and the list is his."

The bowl in Maren's hands was cheap glazed clay and she had owned it nine years and she had never once noticed its weight until that evening, when she set it down very carefully because her fingers had become unreliable. Around her the kitchen was exactly as it had been that morning: the same crocks, the same smoked ham, the same mended curtain. Nothing had changed, and everything had changed, because this morning the way out of Bellhollow had been the harbor road, open to anyone with legs and a fare, and tonight it was a piece of paper in one man's coat pocket with names on it, and whether your name was there depended on nothing you could earn or carry or plead.

"Deller says old Corve cried," Joss offered, when the silence had gone on too long. "After they took the book."

"Corve kept that book thirty years," Maren said. "Go wash."

When the boy was abed she sat by the shuttered window with a rushlight and let herself think the thoughts she'd been refusing all day, one at a time, like drawing splinters.

The army had come for the fever — that was the story, and perhaps it was even true. Quarantine, patrol, order. But armies did not requisition civil ledgers to fight fevers. Ledgers fought no fevers. Ledgers did one thing: they told you who everyone was. And a colonel who now held every name and origin in Bellhollow in a canvas bag, and also held the only berth out of the plague town, and also — Joss's gossip confirmed what the notices implied — answered to no authority nearer than the coast capital, three days' ride away and getting farther every hour the quarantine held—

Maren blew out the rushlight rather than finish the sentence in her own head. In the dark she listened to the town. It sounded wrong. Bellhollow at night was usually a murmuring place — dogs, drunks, the clock house tolling the hours down from the hill, that great deep voice from the observatory that everyone in town set their lives by without ever thinking of it. Tonight the dogs were quiet, the drunks were quiet, and the clock, when it came, tolled nine into streets so silent the sound seemed to go on afterward, humming in the shutters, in the crockery, in the bones of the houses.

Everyone in town was awake, she realized. Everyone in town was lying in the dark doing the arithmetic she had just done, and arriving where she had arrived, and finding no better answer. Collective dread, settling like ash. You couldn't sweep it. You couldn't even hold it properly; it got into everything, into the bread and the bedsheets and the spaces between people's words, and all you could do was breathe it and wait to learn what it was the prelude to.

* * *

What it was the prelude to showed its first face two mornings later, at the harbor gate.

The quarantine cordon had gone up in a night — rope and staves and a squad of bored-looking soldiers — and the townsfolk had been permitted, licensed, *graciously permitted*, to gather at fifty paces and watch the fever ship's boat come ashore with the week's mail, handled by men in gloves who burned the boat's wrappings afterward in a brazier. And it was there, in that crowd, that Maren first heard the word *list* spoken aloud as a thing that already existed, already written, already full.

"The list is drawn," said the woman ahead of her, in the flat voice of someone repeating news because repeating it was the only way to make it bearable. "Drawn and sealed. Berth allocations posted when the sailing's set. Not before."

"My brother's a cooper," said another voice. "Coopers are useful. Coopers should be on any sensible list."

"Sensible." The widow Sanne, somewhere to Maren's left, with the whole harbor wind behind her voice. "You think it's sensible? I'll tell you what it is. It's favors. It's who dined with whom and who owes whom. It's always been that; now it's just written down and we get to watch."

A soldier shifted along the rope line, and the voices dropped, and the crowd swayed and muttered like water finding its level around a stone. Maren looked past them all at the ship — a brig, small, riding high, room in her hull for a hundred souls and licensed for a fraction of that — and at the garrison officer on the quay with the manifest case chained to his belt, and she thought about names again. About how many kinds of power a name could be. On the requisition notice, a name was a threat. In the seized ledger, a name was a map. On the berth list, a name would be life itself, handed out by favor while the rest of the town breathed fever air and waited.

And underneath the fear, in a place she hadn't visited in twenty years, an older thing stirred — her father's coast-district caution, the shrug that gave nothing away, the instinct that had kept her family unremarkable through the unrest and the trials and the hangings. *Be no one,* her father had taught her, without ever saying it outright, by the example of his whole careful life. *Be no one, and no one comes for you.*

But the army had come for everyone now, and had taken the book that said who they all were, and being no one was no longer on offer. The choice in Bellhollow, starting today, was only ever going to be between the name other men wrote down for you and the name you insisted on for yourself — and Maren Ostrow, sweeping ash from her step, standing at a rope line with the wind off the fever ship in her face, understood with sudden terrible clarity that she was going to have to choose, and soon, and that whatever she chose would cost.

Behind her, up the hill, the observatory clock tolled the half hour, steady as a heart that didn't know yet what was coming.

She turned toward it without deciding to — and saw, halfway up the observatory road, a figure she didn't know: slight, workmanlike, carrying a clockmaker's tool roll, walking fast with the shoulders drawn up as if against weather that wasn't there. A stranger, in a town where strangers had suddenly become the most dangerous and most interesting thing left.

The stranger glanced back once, at the harbor, at the crowd, at the soldier with the chained manifest case — and hurried on toward the clock house, and the great door swallowed them, and Maren stood in the ash-fall wondering, with the particular cold attention of a woman who has just watched something important happen without knowing why, what business a clockmaker had looking at the berth list like that.
