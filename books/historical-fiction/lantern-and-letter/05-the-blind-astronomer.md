## 5. The Blind Astronomer

The astronomer's door was the only one in Bellhollow without a knocker.

Isandro stood on the landing at the top of the observatory's east stair, the scrap of ledger paper folded into a square no bigger than a stamp and pressed against her sternum through two layers of wool, and raised her hand to knock on wood that had never been fitted for visitors. There was no bell-pull either. No nameplate. Just a door of dark pine, swollen slightly with age, that had clearly been meant to open onto nothing but another workroom and had instead become the residence of a woman who did not expect guests.

She knocked anyway. Three times, evenly spaced, the way she'd been taught to test a clock case for hollowness.

"Come in or go away," said a voice from inside — low, dry, unhurried. "But don't stand there breathing at my door. It's a bad habit of the sighted."

Isandro opened it.

The room smelled of brass polish and lamp oil and something older beneath both — paper, decades of it, the sweet dusty rot of records kept past their usefulness. A fire burned low in a grate she could barely see for the stacks. Every surface held instruments: sextants hung from ceiling hooks like sleeping bats, an armillary sphere stood in the corner with one ring dented inward, and along the far wall ran shelves of observation books, their spines labeled in what she guessed was the same handwriting that covered every other surface in the room — because there was writing *everywhere*. On the walls. On the window frames. On strips of card pinned to the curtains. Ink in a hundred different states of fading, all of it large, all of it looping, the script of a woman who wrote so she could find things by touch.

Edda Sollen sat at a table beneath the room's single window, her face turned toward the gray light as though it were something she could drink. She was smaller than Isandro had expected — the observatory's steward had spoken of her with the hushed awe reserved for monuments — with white hair cropped close and hands resting flat on the table as if they were instruments too, calibrated and waiting.

"You're the clockmaker's apprentice," Edda said. "Vane."

*Uncle Corin Vane*, Isandro thought, holding the name up between them like a shield. "Yes, ma'am."

"The master clock has run for thirty-one years without stopping. Then you arrive, and within a week it keeps time better than it ever has." The blind eyes did not turn toward her; they didn't need to. "Either you're very good, or you're very interested in something other than the clock. Sit down. I've made tea I can't taste and won't drink alone."

Isandro did not sit.

She had rehearsed this visit eleven times in her head during the walk up from the townhouse — she had counted the rehearsals the way she counted gear teeth, because counting kept the fear compacted and quiet. In nine of the eleven rehearsals, Edda Sollen refused to help. In the other two, Edda Sollen sent her away and then sent word to Colonel Harrow. Both outcomes began the same way: with a woman who had taught her father mathematics forty years ago, being asked by his daughter — in disguise, under a dead man's name — to read handwriting from a scrap found hidden inside a clock.

There was no version of the question that didn't sound like a confession.

"I found something," Isandro said. "In the clock housing. A piece of paper."

"Paper is common in clock housings. Packing material."

"This isn't packing material."

"Then show me your hands."

Isandro blinked. "My—"

"Hands," Edda said, with the patience of someone repeating an instruction for the fortieth year. "You've been standing in my doorway for ninety seconds with your right fist closed. Whatever you're carrying, you're carrying it the way people carry things they're afraid of. Show me your hands and then sit down, and we'll proceed from there."

Slowly, feeling like a pickpocket caught mid-lift, Isandro opened her fist. She crossed the room — navigating the stacks by instinct and apology — and laid the unfolded scrap on the table in front of the blind woman, where it could do no good at all.

Edda's fingers came forward and read the paper the way Isandro read a dial: edges first, then texture, then the faint ridge of dried ink where the pen had pressed hard. Isandro watched her face for the recognition of the cipher itself, the moment the mathematician mapped the columns of figures and knew them for what they were.

What she saw instead frightened her more.

Edda Sollen went still. Not thinking-still. Struck-still. Her thumb stopped on the third line of numbers and stayed there, pressing into the paper as though she could hold the hand that had written it.

"Where," she said, and her voice had changed — flattened, careful — "did you get this?"

"In the clock housing. Behind the fourth wheel plate. Wrapped in oilcloth."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know," Isandro said. "That's not what I answered."

The fire ticked. Somewhere below them, through three floors of stone, the restored master clock turned through its train, each escape tooth falling into place with a sound too small for anyone but a clockmaker to hear — and Isandro heard it now the way she heard her own pulse.

"Read me the first line," Edda said.

"There aren't any words. It's figures. Columns of—"

"*Read me the first line.*"

"'Fourteen, seven, nineteen. Eleven, two, twenty-two.'"

Edda's hand withdrew from the paper slowly, folding closed on the table, and when she spoke again her voice was steady in the particular way of a person spending everything they have to keep it so.

"I taught arithmetic at the garrison school for eleven years," she said. "Before my eyes finished failing. I had perhaps two hundred students in that time, and I remember perhaps six of them, and one of them was a cooper's son who came to night classes after his shift because he wanted to understand how merchants cheated him at the weighbridge. He had beautiful figures. Round-headed fours. He crossed his sevens — nobody crossed sevens in this province, it wasn't taught here, he'd learned it from a Prussian sailor — and he pressed hard enough on the pen that you could read his sums from the back of the page." Her thumb moved again toward the scrap, not touching it. Hovering. "This man crossed his sevens."

The room did not spin, but Isandro gripped the back of the chair anyway.

"He's dead," she said. Her own voice sounded distant to her, borrowed. "He died eight years ago."

"Yes," Edda said. "I know how he died. Everyone in Bellhollow over forty knows how he died. What I don't know is why a clockmaker's apprentice named Vane carries his handwriting against her chest like a relic, and calls herself by her uncle's name, and repairs my clock better than any craftsman the army ever sent — and stands in my doorway terrified of a blind old woman." She turned her head then, and though the eyes were clouded and aimed slightly wide of their mark, Isandro felt seen down to the bone. "Who are you?"

Every answer Isandro had prepared collapsed at once. She had rehearsed lies the way she'd rehearsed the truth, and now, watching Edda's fingers tremble a half-inch above her father's last written work, she understood that lying to this woman would be not just dangerous but desecrating.

"My name," she said, "is Isandro Vane."

The silence stretched long enough for the clock below to strike the quarter hour — four soft strokes, her own adjustment, her own hands on the hammer.

"Corin's daughter," Edda said quietly.

"Yes."

"The one they recorded as dead."

"The one they recorded as *nothing*," Isandro said. "No record at all. My mother scrubbed me out before the trials even started. I've been no one since I was nine years old. Uncle Corin took me south and gave me his name and taught me gears until I forgot I'd ever had another inheritance." She heard her voice rise and forced it level. "I'm not asking you to believe me about anything else. But that's who I am. And I need to know what he wrote."

Edda rose. It took her a moment — her hip had stiffened, she braced the table — and then she moved through her own labyrinth of instruments without touching a single one, to the hearth, where a tin box sat on the mantel among the stacks. She brought it back and set it beside the scrap of paper with a click.

"Eight years ago," Edda said, "a boy came here in the rain. Sixteen, seventeen years old. He wouldn't give his name and he carried a package wrapped in oilcloth, and he said: *my father said, if he ever couldn't come himself, bring this to the astronomer at Bellhollow, and tell her the sevens are crossed.*" She lifted the lid of the tin. "And I told him to go away, because soldiers had already searched this observatory twice that winter, and I was afraid, and I have been ashamed of it every day since. He left without leaving the package. Or so I believed." Her mouth thinned. "Apparently the family was better at hiding things than I was at receiving them."

Inside the tin, on top of a folded map, lay a second piece of paper. Isandro did not need to be told whose hand it was. The round-headed fours. The crossed sevens. The ink pressed deep enough to read from behind.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"A key," Edda said. "Or half of one. Your father and I designed it together, thirty-eight years ago, for a game — a children's cipher, honestly, so his sister's girls could pass secrets across the cooper's yard. We built it out of clock arithmetic because he thought clocks were honest. Twelve hours and every hour comes back around. No number hides forever." She drew the paper toward her, fingers spreading over the columns. "It seems he remembered the game."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon.

It went slowly at first, because trust does not flow in one direction on demand, and Edda Sollen gave hers out in installments — a rule of the cipher, then a question; a question, then silence while she listened to Isandro's answers with her whole body, reading the vibrations of the room the way she read the page. Where had Isandro trained. Who had signed her journeyman papers. Did she know the sound of Colonel Harrow's voice. Was anyone following her. Each answer seemed to buy the next inch of confidence, and by the third hour Edda had stopped asking questions and started teaching, and teaching, Isandro discovered, was what this woman was *for* — forty years of lectures stored up in her hands, released now in a flood.

"Clock arithmetic first," Edda said, tapping the table. "Everything reduces modulo twelve or twenty-four. Your columns of figures — they look like observations, yes? Declinations, magnitudes. That's deliberate. Any soldier who searches this room finds a page of astronomy and loses interest. But watch." Her finger moved down the scrap. "Fourteen. Fourteen hours is two past noon. Take the twelfth hour off and you have two. Seven stays seven. Nineteen is seven past twelve — seven again. So the line reads: two, seven, seven, eleven, two, ten."

"That means nothing."

"It means nothing *yet*, because you haven't done the second reduction. The second key is the date. Every entry is keyed to the day it was written — he told you that much in the heading, if you can read the heading. The day gives you a number, you subtract it from each reduced figure, and whatever falls below one wraps around the clock face. Twelve hours and every hour comes back around. Nothing hides forever." She paused. "Except people. People hide forever, apparently. Read me the heading again."

"'Third of the ninth,'" Isandro read, "'harbor weights.' Third of September. Three."

"Then subtract three. Two becomes eleven. Seven becomes four. Eleven becomes eight. Two becomes eleven. Ten becomes seven." Edda's finger rested on the margin of the page, motionless. "Eleven, four, eight, eleven, seven. Now — do you have the alphabet wheel in your head, or shall I draw it?"

"Draw it."

Edda drew it from memory, large, in the looping hand that covered her walls: twelve positions, A through L, and then the doubling — position eleven read as *K*, but read backward, from twelve, it read as *W*. Two letters for every hour, depending on direction, which was why the cipher had defeated everyone who'd ever glimpsed it. Direction was set by whether the entry's figure-count was odd or even.

Isandro did the count. Even. Forward.

"Eleven is K," she said slowly. "Four is D. Eight is H. Eleven, K again. Seven is G."

*K-D-H-K-G.*

"That means nothing," she said again, but her voice had gone thin, because it didn't mean nothing. It meant five letters, and five letters was a word trying to happen, and somewhere beneath her ribs the fear had changed species — no longer the fear of being discovered but the fear of understanding.

"It will mean something," Edda said, "when you have the rest of the entries. One scrap is one line. Your father was a methodical man. If he hid one scrap behind the fourth wheel plate—" she tilted her head, and the lamplight slid across her clouded eyes, "—then he hid others. Clocks are full of hiding places. He would have known you'd find them. He was teaching you, Isandro. From the grave, at his own pace, in his own order. The way he taught everything."

Isandro looked down at the scrap — at the crossed sevens, the pressed-deep ink, the handwriting she had last seen on a letter tucked into her boot the night before the soldiers came for their house, when she was nine, when her father had crouched down to her height and said *you are no one now, little star, and no one is safe* — and she understood, with a lurch, that she had spent eight years believing she'd escaped her father's fate when in fact she had been walking directly along its perimeter, circling it, and the circle had just closed.

"He wasn't smuggling," she said. "Everyone said the rebels were smugglers. Gunpowder through the harbor, French coin. But these are harbor *weights*. Weights and measures. He was recording what the harbor weighed."

"He was a cooper," Edda said. "Barrels were his whole world. He knew to the ounce what should come off a ship and what actually did." She sat back. "Do you know what I do all day, apprentice? I measure the sky. I write down where stars are supposed to be and where they actually are, and the difference between those two numbers is the entire science of astronomy. Your father did the same thing with cargo manifests. He measured what the Empire claimed and what the Empire did, and he wrote down the difference." Her voice dropped. "The difference is called evidence."

Outside, the light was failing. Through the observatory's great dome above them — Isandro could feel it now, the shuttered eye of the building, waiting for night — the sky was going the color of unpolished pewter. Down in the town, lamps were being lit along the harbor road, and beyond them, at the water, the quarantine flags hung limp on their masts. She hadn't yet let herself think about the ships. About berths. About lists. One thing at a time; one gear at a time; a clock is assembled in order or it never runs.

"Teach me the rest," she said.

"No," said Edda Sollen.

Isandro's head came up. "*No?*"

"Not tonight, and not alone, and not until we settle terms." Edda's face was unreadable, which Isandro had not thought possible in a blind woman; the eyes gave nothing, so everything had to come from the set of the mouth, and the mouth was set like a vice. "You are asking me to finish what your father started. Do you understand what that means? Eight years ago I turned a child away in the rain because I was afraid, and he is dead, and his father is dead, and I have had eight years to learn exactly what my cowardice purchased. I will not repeat it. But I will also not blunder into it. If we do this, we do it properly. Slowly. With rules."

"Name them."

"First: you sleep in this room from tonight. The observatory is army property and the army doesn't search its own property twice — they searched it in the first week and they'll not waste an officer's dignity on a blind woman's clutter again. You're safer here than anywhere in Bellhollow."

"Second: we decode nothing ahead of what your father intended. His scraps will be ordered — he'll have keyed them. We follow his sequence, not our curiosity. He hid this for a reason, and the reason may be that the wrong entry, found first, gets people killed."

"Third." Edda's hand found the scrap on the table and pushed it, gently, back across the wood toward Isandro. "You tell me the rest of it. Not just the paper. Why a clockmaker's daughter is hiding in an observatory in the very town where her father swung, three weeks before the army requisitioned the civil rolls. Because I am old, girl, but I am not stupid, and I can smell coincidence the way you can smell a burnt gear. Nobody arrives in Bellhollow by accident anymore."

Isandro hesitated. Then she reached into her coat and produced the second thing she had carried up the east stair — the folded requisition notice Maren Ostrow had pressed into her hands that morning outside the townhouse, the one naming the seized ledger, listing every family, every origin, every name the army now owned.

She smoothed it on the table under Edda's fingers.

"The ledger," Isandro said. "They took the civil ledger yesterday. And I need to see it, because my mother's name is in it, and mine should have been, and if my father put anything else in any margins anywhere in this town—" she stopped, and steadied, "—then the most dangerous document in this province is sitting in Colonel Harrow's strongbox. And I need to get to it before he reads what I just spent all afternoon learning to read."

For a long moment Edda said nothing. Then her hand moved across the notice, reading it twice, and when she finally spoke her voice had lost its caution and gained something harder.

"Harrow," she said. "He signed the order, didn't he. Your father's order. That's why you're really here."

Isandro didn't answer. She didn't have to.

"Then we have terms," Edda said. "One more thing, and then you'll make up my bed and we'll sleep, because tomorrow we work. The army keeps a clerk up at the garrison — young man, Callis, transferred from the coast under some disgrace. Before my eyes went I knew his mother; decent family, wrong side of everything. Last month he came to the observatory on requisition business and stood in my doorway doing exactly what you did tonight. Breathing at my door, with his fist closed." She smiled, thin as a knife's edge. "People only hide one kind of thing that way. If you want the ledger, apprentice, the army's own conscience is the crack in their wall — and I think he's ready to be asked."

Down through three floors of stone, the master clock gathered itself and struck the hour — six strokes, even and true, on hands Isandro had set herself.

Somewhere below, in the lamplit streets, an army clerk was carrying a secret home through the ash-smelling dark, having no idea yet that two women in an observatory had just decided his future for him.
