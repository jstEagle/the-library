## 6. Names Without Owners

The requisition order came down at half past eight, and by nine Wren Callis had a headache.

It was not the order itself that did it — orders were weather, in Bellhollow, since the army arrived. It was the handwriting on the routing slip: *Copy complete. Every name, every origin, every entry. Colonel Harrow's personal attention.* Personal attention meant Harrow would read it himself, and Harrow reading something himself meant someone was about to lose a house, a boat, or a life, and Wren's job was to make sure the paperwork for it was tidy.

He took the slip to Captain Dree at the adjutant's office, because procedure was the only weapon he owned and he liked to fire it often.

"Every name," Wren said. "The civil ledger is four hundred pages. The fair-copy alone is two days of work."

"Then you'd best start today," Dree said, not looking up from his tea.

"With respect, sir — the original should stay under seal while it's copied. If I'm carrying it back and forth between the townhouse and this office—"

"It stays *here*." Dree set the cup down. "In the map room. You work here. Door locked, sentry outside. Are you trying to complicate my morning, Callis?"

"No, sir," Wren said. "I'm trying to keep you off a charge sheet."

That earned him a look — the flat, measuring look officers gave clerks who knew too much about how charges worked. But he got his way, which was the whole art of being Wren Callis: never win, never lose, only ever be slightly more inconvenient than the alternative.

* * *

The map room smelled of tallow and old gun oil. A sentry named Brack stood outside with instructions to admit no one, which lasted until eleven o'clock, when three people needed admitting, and after that Brack stopped counting and started shrugging.

Wren cleared the center table himself, unrolled a sheet of clean foolscap, and fetched the ledger up from the strongbox with both hands, the way one carries a sleeping child or a live shell.

He had heard about the seizure secondhand, like everyone. The townhouse on Cordwainer Lane, the mayor's clerk standing aside with his hands open — *nothing here is ours, take what you must* — the soldiers stacking the ledgers into a canvas sack like turnips. He had assumed the civil ledger was exactly what it looked like: names, trades, origins, tax assessments, births and deaths going back ninety years. The town's memory in calfskin. Boring as bread.

He opened it to the first page and began to copy.

By noon he had filled six sheets and learned the town the way you learn a person from their letters. Ostrow, Maren — chandler's widow, Cordwainer Lane, born Bellhollow. Vane, Aldous — clockmaker, deceased 1843. Page after page of ordinary lives written in a dozen different hands across three generations, the ink browning, the margins annotated by clerks long dead. There was something in it that made his chest ache in a way he refused to examine. Four hundred pages of people who had been, until nine days ago, free to be boring.

He was deep in the 1830s when he found the first alteration.

A name — Hessel, Pieter, fisherman, origin listed *Bellhollow* — had been struck through and rewritten. Not overwritten in the original hand; amended in a different one entirely. The new ink was darker, iron-gall, recent. The letters were small and severe, with a downward slash on the terminal stroke that Wren recognized before he could name where he knew it from.

He knew it from requisition chits. From court-martial summaries. From the death warrants that crossed the adjutant's desk for countersignature.

Colonel Harrow wrote like a man carving.

Wren sat very still for a moment, hands flat on the table, listening to Brack shift his weight outside the door.

Then he turned back through the pages he had already copied and looked at them properly.

* * *

There were eleven of them. Eleven entries altered in the same hand, scattered across twenty years of the ledger like burrs in wool. Always the same kind of change: an origin erased and replaced. A man born in Marrow Quay became a man born in Bellhollow. A family arrived from the coast in '29 acquired local roots going back generations. One woman's entire origin line was scraped — actually scraped, with a blade, the vellum thinned to translucency — and rewritten over the scar.

And once, only once, the opposite direction. An entry made *local* by the original clerk had been changed to *foreign*. Vane, Aldous — no. That one stopped him cold, quill dripping onto the blotter.

He read it again. The birth year matched. The trade matched. Clockmaker. Deceased 1843 — but there was a second annotation beneath, smaller, squeezed against the margin: *executed 1838, treason,* in Harrow's slashing hand, dated years before the ledger said he died.

Two dates. Two deaths for one man. Somebody had recorded him dead twice, and both times in the colonel's writing, and neither time correctly.

Wren had spent eleven years in the army's administrative guts. He had processed supply fraud, pay fraud, a captain who sold the same mule to the quartermaster four times. He understood, professionally, the grammar of falsification. You didn't alter records for sentiment. You altered them to make a lie load-bearing. Each of these changes held something up — a confiscation, a deportation, a conviction. Change a man's origin to foreign, and his property became forfeit. Change it to local, and his debts became the parish's problem. Every scrape and rewrite was a theft wearing a clerk's coat.

His own coat, in a sense. That was the part that sat wrong in his stomach. Somewhere in the chain that produced these alterations there had been another Wren — some adjutant's copyist in some garrison year, ordered to make the fair copy, making it, tidying it, initialing it. Making the lie official with good penmanship.

He blotted the drip, carefully, and thought about nothing for as long as he could stand, which was about four minutes.

Then he did the arithmetic that would define the rest of his life.

If he reported the alterations: Dree would thank him, or worse, not thank him, and pass it upward, and it would die somewhere above his head with a note in Harrow's own hand saying *noted, no action required.* And Wren would have signed his name to its burial. He knew exactly how that machinery ran; he had oiled it often enough.

If he kept quiet: he was a party to it. Every hour he sat here copying was an hour the lie cured and hardened.

There was, of course, a third option. It presented itself with terrible clarity, the way all cowardly-brave ideas do: quiet, reasonable, obviously insane.

* * *

He finished the day's assigned pages first. This mattered to him — he couldn't have said why, except that if he was going to commit a crime he wanted his legitimate work done properly first, so that posterity, should it trouble itself, would record that Wren Callis was a good clerk right up until he wasn't.

At seven, Dree dismissed him. At half past seven Wren was eating bad stew in the mess, laughing at a corporal's joke about the quarantine, asking nobody anything. At nine he walked past the map room on a fabricated errand and noted that the sentry was Brack again, and that Brack had a flask, and that the flask was a third empty.

At ten the corridor was dark and the lock on the map room was old — older than the war, older than the garrison's current occupancy — and Wren had picked locks since he was twelve, growing up in a house where his father locked the drink away and lost the key weekly.

Inside, he didn't light a lamp. Moonlight through the arrow-slit window gave him enough: gray shapes, the table, the strongbox. His hands knew the work without light. That was the humiliation of it, he thought, working the pick with his heart slamming — that his hands were braver than he was.

The ledger came out heavy and cool. He carried it to the window slit, where a thin wash of moon fell across the page, and turned to the altered entries one by one.

In daylight they had been suspicious. In moonlight they were damning. He could see now what he'd missed: the scraping wasn't random. It followed a pattern — every altered entry sat within three lines of a marginal mark, tiny ticks and figures in yet another hand, cramped and ciphered, the kind of notation a careful clerk might use for cross-references. Except these weren't references. They looked almost like... tallies. Counts. Someone had been keeping accounts *in the margin*, and someone else had been rewriting the main text to bury them.

Harrow hadn't just falsified the ledger. Someone had caught him at it, in writing, years ago — and instead of destroying the page, had hidden the proof where only a reader would find it, in the one place a censor never looks: the margins.

Wren stood in the dark holding four hundred pages of other people's lives and felt the floor of his orderly world tilt.

He could put it back. Lock the box, go to bed, be a good clerk tomorrow. The thought arrived dressed sensibly, as it always did, and he examined it honestly: *you are one man with a borrowed pick and a flask-drunk sentry, and the man who writes like a carving knife has already hanged people for less.*

Yes. All true. And still.

He unbuttoned his coat, drew out the sheets he had prepared that afternoon in his quarters — plain paper, cut to size, ruled faintly in pencil — and laid the first one over the opening page. Then, with a pencil, moving fast and breathing slow, he began to trace.

Not the whole ledger. God, no — four hundred pages would take a week and get him hanged by Thursday. Just the entries that mattered. The eleven alterations, each copied exactly, scrapings and slashes reproduced as best a pencil could. The marginal cipher-marks beside each one, traced line for line, dot for dot. Dates. Names. Enough that if the original burned tonight, the pattern of the lie would survive in his pocket.

He worked for two hours. Twice, boots passed in the corridor, and twice he flattened himself into the corner behind the map cabinet, ledger against his chest, feeling his own pulse in his teeth. Both times the boots went on. The second time he realized he was smiling, which frightened him more than the boots.

By midnight the duplicate was done: fourteen sheets, folded small, wrapped in oilcloth from his kit. He returned the ledger to the strongbox, relocked it, wiped his fingerprints from the lid with his sleeve, and let himself out into a corridor that smelled of wet stone and lamp oil.

Brack nodded to him, genial and bleary. "Late night, Mr. Callis."

"Requisition reports," Wren said. "You know how the colonel loves his numbers."

"Aye." Brack raised the flask slightly, in salute or apology.

Wren walked back to his quarters with fourteen sheets of oilcloth-wrapped paper against his ribs, and the strange thing — the thing he would think about later, in colder hours — was how light he felt. Lighter than he had any business being. As though the duplicate weighed nothing at all, or as though he had been carrying something far heavier for years and had finally, without quite deciding to, set it down.

* * *

In his room he hid the packet badly, then better, then best: inside the hollowed spine of his mother's psalter, which no army search would ever open, because no army search ever opened scripture. He sat on his cot afterward in the dark and listened to the garrison settle around him — boots, laughter, the harbor bell counting the fog.

Eleven lies. One dead man written dead twice. Cipher in the margins, patient as spiderwork.

Tomorrow he would finish the fair copy and deliver it to Harrow's desk with a straight face and clean penmanship. Tomorrow he would be, for a few more days, exactly what he appeared to be.

But tonight, lying awake, one question kept turning over in him like a coin: the marginal hand was small, precise, unmistakably trained — a clerk's hand, or a scholar's. Whoever had hidden that cipher had known the ledger would be seized eventually, had built the proof to survive it.

Which meant someone in Bellhollow had been waiting years for a man like him to come along and read it.

And Wren Callis, staring at the ceiling, realized with a lurch that had no politics in it at all — only hunger — that he very much wanted to know what the margins said.
