## 7. Fever at the Harbor Gate

The fever came up from the fish quarter the way fog comes off the harbor — quietly at first, then all at once, and then it was everywhere and you couldn't remember when it started.

Maren Ostrow heard about it before she saw it, because in Bellhollow the fishwives were faster than any doctor. She was hauling a crate of salt cod to the Widow Pell's door when two of them caught her at the corner of Rope Walk, heads together, voices low.

"Three down at the Kettle," said one — old Sif, who gutted herring for half the town. "Three in one house."

"The Kettle's crowded," Maren said. "Crowded houses get sick."

"Crowded houses get colds." Sif wiped her hands on her apron as if the news itself had left something on them. "This is blood in the spit, girl. This is the thing that emptied Greyhaven."

Greyhaven was four days' sail north and eleven years gone, and nobody in Bellhollow liked to say its name out loud. They said it now, though. Twice more before noon, Maren heard it, each time softer, each time like a coin dropped down a well.

By evening the doctor's boy was nailing a paper to the chapel door, and by morning there were soldiers on the quay with red paint and brushes, drawing a line across the harbor road that nobody was supposed to cross in either direction without leave.

Maren stood in the crowd at the gate and read the notice nailed above them, because she could read when most couldn't, a fact her mother had spent good money on and Maren had never once regretted.

*BY ORDER OF COLONEL E. HARROW, COMMANDING:*

*OWING TO THE PRESENCE OF CONTAGIOUS FEVER WITHIN THE TOWN OF BELLHOLLOW, THE PORT IS CLOSED TO ALL TRAFFIC SAVE SUCH VESSELS AS SHALL BE LICENSED BY THE MILITARY AUTHORITY.*

*PROVISIONS WILL BE ADMITTED. PERSONS WILL NOT.*

*THE GARRISON WILL MAINTAIN ORDER.*

That last line did more damage than the fever had. *Maintain order.* The words sat on the paper like stones on a grave, and the crowd read them in silence, and then everybody began talking at once.

"They can't close the port."

"They've closed it."

"There'll be a ship. There has to be a ship. They wouldn't strand their own garrison."

"No," said a voice near Maren, dry as old rope. It was the Widow Pell, tiny and bent and older than the war, and she said it just loud enough. "They'd send for themselves first."

Nobody laughed. That was the worst of it. A month ago somebody would have laughed, and told her not to be morbid, and gone home feeling better for having dismissed an old woman. Now the whole crowd stood there thinking, *yes, they would,* and nobody felt better at all.

* * *

The garrison made it official three days later.

Maren was at the well behind Tanner's Row when the drummers came through, beating the assembly, and the crier read the new orders from the steps of the customs house while soldiers held back the gathering street.

There would be one ship. One. The packet brig *Vigilant*, due within the fortnight, licensed to carry out such persons as the military authority should certify — the sick who needed treatment beyond the town, the wounded, officials of the crown, and, the crier read, "*such other persons as the commanding officer shall designate.*"

One berth list. Drawn up at the colonel's discretion. Posted when posted.

"One berth," said the cooper's son beside Maren, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, because he was sixteen and doing arithmetic every soul in the crowd was doing. Bellhollow had four thousand people. The *Vigilant* might carry sixty, seventy at the outside with the fever ward aboard.

Four thousand. Sixty.

Maren watched the numbers land on faces all down the street, watched hope curdle into something else — not despair, not yet, but its ugly cousin. Scarcity. She'd seen scarcity before; every fishing town knew it, the year the herring failed or the ice came early and there wasn't enough to go around and neighbors looked at each other sideways over fences. But this was scarcity with a fence around it, scarcity with soldiers on the gate, scarcity where one man held the whole net and everyone else was fish.

Her mother would have said: *now you find out what people are.*

Maren found out fast.

By nightfall there were women at the garrison gate with baskets. Not begging — offering. Bread, preserves, a knitted scarf, a bottle of plum brandy that had been saved for a wedding. Small bribes, embarrassed ones, left with sentries who mostly wouldn't take them but mostly didn't report them either. By the second day the baskets were bigger. By the third day someone left a silver candlestick, wrapped in flannel, and the sentry who took it inside didn't come back out, and after that the gate had a new rule about packages.

And in the taverns, quieter things changed hands. Names were mentioned. A cousin of a cousin who'd served under Harrow in the border war. A man whose brother drove the colonel's supply wagon. Money moved the way water moves under ice — invisibly, but you could see the cracks where it went.

"It'll be the rich and the connected," Maren's neighbor Tam Voss said at the chandler's, not bothering to lower his voice, which was unlike him. He was a careful man, Tam. Careful men weren't bothering anymore. "The rest of us will stand on this rock until the fever takes its share or the army gets bored."

"You don't know that," said the chandler.

"I know the berth list is drawn up in that man's office," Tam said, "and I know my name isn't written in anything he owns except maybe the fines ledger. Do you?"

The chandler didn't answer. Neither did anyone else in the shop. That silence was the true census of Bellhollow, taken that afternoon: everyone accounted for, no one counted.

* * *

Maren kept her head down and her hands busy, because busy hands kept fear in its box. She mended nets. She salted fish against the weeks when nothing fresh would come in — though nothing fresh was coming in anyway now, the fleet tied up at anchor beyond the quarantine line, the boats forbidden to land their catch, the fishermen standing on their decks looking at their own town like strangers.

She took her sister's children to the well and back and counted doors on the way, because counting doors was easier than counting the black crosses that had begun appearing on some of them — chalk marks the doctor's men made, one per house where fever burned. Four crosses on Tanner's Row by the end of the week. Then seven. The chalk dust got into everything. Maren dreamed of it.

It was on the ninth day that the rumor came through the fishwives again, faster than any official word, and this time it landed in Maren's own chest like a hook.

The berth list existed. It had been drafted. And it was not going to be posted yet — it was being *held*, because holding a thing was worth more than posting it, and Colonel Harrow, whatever else he was, understood value.

"He's letting people sweat," Sif said, in the shadow of the fish shed, where the three of them had gotten good at talking low. "Every day he doesn't post it, another basket comes to the gate. Every day, another candlestick. Why would a man post a list that's earning him a living?"

"That's treason talk," said the third woman, young Petra, who always said that and never meant it.

"It's arithmetic," said Sif. "I count fish for a living. I know when the tally's being cooked."

Maren said nothing. She was thinking about her sister, who had the cough — only a cough, everyone said, only a cough — and two children, and no husband since the wreck of the *Merrow*. Her sister's name would need to be on that list, or those children would grow up in a town with a chalk cross on the door.

She was also thinking — and she hated herself for it, and thought it anyway — that she was a widow of forty-one with strong arms and no dependents, and that if the list was short, then every name on it was a name not on it, and her own name was worth keeping too. That was the sickness the quarantine brought, she realized. Not the fever in the lungs. The fever in the arithmetic. Everyone in Bellhollow was learning to do sums with other people's lives, and the sum always came out the same: *not me. Please. Not me.*

She went home past the garrison wall, and there she saw the colonel himself.

He was on horseback, reviewing the gate guard, and he paused at the pile of unclaimed baskets — the ones the sentries hadn't dared accept — and looked at them for a long moment. Maren was close enough, in the crowd of idlers, to see his face do something complicated. Not greed. Something colder and more tired than that. He looked at the offerings the way a man looks at a bill he intends to pay later, with someone else's money.

Then he turned his horse, and as he turned, a clerk stepped up beside the stirrup — a thin young man with ink-stained fingers and a face like a closed ledger — and handed up a paper, and murmured something, and the colonel read it and nodded once and put it inside his coat.

Maren didn't know why she noticed the clerk. There was nothing to notice. He was grey and quiet and he vanished back inside the wall like a drop of water into a sponge.

But she noticed. Later, she would think often about how she noticed — how on that grey afternoon, outside the garrison gate, with her sister's cough rattling in her ears and the whole town doing its terrible arithmetic, she had marked one small grey man carrying one piece of paper, and filed him away without knowing why.

Perhaps because in a town where everyone had begun to look at everyone else as competition for a berth, the clerk was the first person she'd seen all week whose face showed no appetite at all. Only a kind of grim, private misery, like a man carrying something heavy that he couldn't set down and couldn't bear to hold.

* * *

On the eleventh day, the fever reached the garrison.

Not badly — three soldiers, quickly isolated, quickly (the official word ran) contained. But it changed the air inside the wall. Maren learned this from the fish market, which had become the true newspaper of Bellhollow: the garrison had stopped buying bread from the town ovens. The officers had their own cook now. The colonel ate from sealed stores.

They were afraid in there too. That was worth knowing. Men who are afraid make mistakes, and men who are afraid and powerful make dangerous ones.

On the twelfth day, the crier finally came out. The berth list would be posted at the customs house on Sunday next, he announced, upon completion of certification. Persons whose names appeared would present themselves with their papers. Persons whose names did not appear would remain within the quarantine line until such time as the military authority determined otherwise.

"*Until such time*," Tam Voss repeated, when the crier had gone. "You hear that? Not *until the quarantine lifts*. Until *he determines otherwise*. We're cargo now, and the manifest is one man's whim."

Sunday was five days off. Five days in which every family in Bellhollow would sit at their tables and say their own names aloud, testing them, weighing them, wondering what they weighed in a colonel's hand.

Five days in which Maren Ostrow sat at hers with her sister and the children, and listened to the cough across the room, and did her sums, and found them coming out wrong no matter how she arranged them.

"If it comes to choosing," her sister said on the fourth night, very quietly, so the children wouldn't hear, "you take the berth. Promise me. You've got the strong heart, Mare. You'd live."

"Don't talk foolishness."

"Maren." Her sister's hand was hot — not dangerously hot, not yet, but hot enough to notice, hot enough to lie awake over. "Promise me."

Maren looked at her — at the sharp cheekbones and the stubborn mouth, the same mouth their mother had, the same mouth Maren had — and she lied, easily, the way you learn to lie in a fishing town where bad news is daily bread: "I promise."

Both of them knew it was a lie. Both of them let it stand, because some lies are load-bearing, and the house would fall without them.

That night Maren couldn't sleep, and near dawn she rose and went to the window, and looked out at the harbor where the *Vigilant* would come. The water was grey and flat. Somewhere beyond the point, at anchor in the quarantine roads, the fishing fleet rode at its moorings, forbidden to land, the crews watching their own homes through glasses they'd bought for spotting herring shoals.

And on the observatory hill above the town, the great clock tower stood black against the lightening sky, its dial faintly luminous, marking out the hours for a town that had suddenly run out of time.

Maren watched it tick through the quarter hour — she could see the minute hand move, even at that distance, if she knew where to look — and she thought: *that clock doesn't care whose name is on the list. That clock strikes the same hour for the colonel and for us. Somebody keeps that clock. Somebody winds it and sets it and makes it tell the truth twice a day, every day, rain or fever or requisition.*

It was a strange thing to take comfort in. But it was the only thing in Bellhollow that week that still answered to no one's discretion, and she held onto it the way you hold a rail in rough water.

Two days later, the fishwives brought her stranger news still — news that seemed at first like nonsense, and then like nothing, and then, when she turned it over in the night, like the first crack in a wall she'd thought was solid.

A soldier, drunk on stolen brandy, had been heard complaining in the Kettle that the observatory clock had stopped. Stopped dead. And that the colonel was furious about it, furious out of all proportion — because the garrison's whole schedule, every patrol, every gate rotation, every *certification*, ran on that master clock's time. And because, the soldier said, laughing meanly into his cup, the colonel had ordered the observatory searched top to bottom on suspicion of sabotage.

Searched. The observatory. What in God's name would a blind astronomer and a clockmaker's apprentice have that a colonel feared?

Maren didn't know. But she had lived fifty years in a town built on tides, and she knew what a crack in the seawall meant: the pressure behind it was already enormous, and something, somewhere, had given way first.

She finished her net. She banked her fire. And she lay awake listening to her sister breathe, and waited, with the whole of Bellhollow, for Sunday.
