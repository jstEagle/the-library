## 8. What the Cipher Says

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Isandro finally understood what she was looking at.

She had been at the cipher for three nights now — three nights of Edda Sollen's patient voice in the dark of the observatory, teaching her a system built for men who needed to write numbers that could survive an audit and a search. *The stars don't lie, but clerks do,* Edda had said on the first night. *So my father's generation wrote their figures twice. Once for the tax man, once for God. The second version was the true one.* It was a bookkeeping cipher, nothing more elegant than that: letters standing for digits, digits keyed to the positions of certain fixed stars as they were charted in the observatory's own almanac, the edition printed eleven years ago. Any clerk with a head for figures could learn it in a week. Only someone with the almanac — and the patience — could read it.

Isandro had the almanac. She had more than patience. She had a scrap of paper written in her father's hand, hidden for years inside the housing of a clock he had helped build, waiting for someone to come and wind it back into motion.

She sat at the workbench with the scrap flattened under glass weights, her copy of the decoded lines beside it in her own careful apprentice's script — the script Uncle Aldous had taught her, all upright strokes and no flourish, a handwriting designed to be forgettable. Around her the master clock ticked its enormous slow heartbeat through the tower room, and below, Bellhollow lay under its first week of requisition, doors chalked with company numbers, the civil ledger gone from the townhouse like a tooth pulled from the jaw.

The first entries had been numbers. She'd assumed they were accounts.

They were accounts. That was the horror of it. They were *good* accounts.

*Entry one. Cargo landed east slip, night tide, marked as rope and tar. Actual: muskets, forty crate, powder six. Weighed against manifest signed H.*

Her pen stopped. She read it again. Read it a third time, because the mind protects itself, because a sentence like that will slide off comprehension the way water off oiled leather until you force it to stay.

*Signed H.*

Harrow.

She made herself keep going, line by line, transcribing before she let herself feel anything, because feeling would stop the hand and the hand had to finish. This was the discipline her father had drilled into her before she was old enough to know why it mattered — or perhaps he hadn't drilled it at all, perhaps it was simply his own discipline leaking into her through years of watching him work at the kitchen table, ruling his columns straight, never letting ink blot, *a record is only worth what its accuracy is worth, little bird, remember that*. Little bird. He had called her little bird. She had not thought of that in ten years and now it arrived in her chest like a swallowed coal.

*Entry four. Grain requisitioned from Ostrow holdings at famine price; sold at port price; difference entered as spoilage. Spoilage certified H. No grain spoiled. I counted the sacks myself.*

Maren Ostrow's family. The name went through her like a needle — Maren who had held the door of the chandlery open for her on her first day, Maren whose brother had been pressed onto the harbor watch, Maren who had said *they'll take the ledger next, you'll see* with the flat certainty of someone who has been robbed before.

*Entry seven. The Fenwick petition. Signatures collected, 214 names, presented to garrison command regarding tithe abuses. Petition returned unsigned and unrecorded. Three signatories thereafter arrested on charges of sedition. Charges drafted by same hand as manifest certifications.*

There it was. Not treason. *Bookkeeping.* Her father had not raised a fist or a flag or even a voice — he had kept accounts. Honest ones, in cipher, against a colonel who falsified his own. Arms smuggled in under rope-and-tar marks. Grain skimmed from starving farms. A petition of two hundred fourteen names answered with arrests. And every line of it certified, signed, sealed by the same initial: H.

He had been hanged for testimony. For writing down what was true in a form that could be proved.

Isandro set down the pen. Her hand was steady, which surprised her; she had expected it to shake. Perhaps the shaking was somewhere deeper, in the place where grief had lived so long it had grown a callus over itself, the way hands grow calluses at the lathe. Ten years she had carried this death like a stone sewn into her coat — heavy, familiar, *mine*, without shape or reason, because a rebel's daughter does not get reasons. Rebels are traitors. Traitors die. That is the whole of the arithmetic a child is given.

But here was the arithmetic underneath. Here was entry after entry in her father's small ruled hand, each figure cross-checked, each weight verified, each observation dated to the hour by a man who loved clocks because clocks did not perjure themselves. He had died not for a cause in the abstract but for a column of figures. For the difference between the true weight of a sack of grain and the weight written on a manifest. For forty crates of muskets that were supposed to be rope.

And no one had ever read it. The cipher had gone unread, the scrap hidden inside the clock he'd built with his own hands, tucked behind the strike train where only a repairer would find it — where only *she* had found it. Ten years of silence. Ten years of Harrow rising through the ranks on the strength of a tidy garrison and a hanged traitor, while the proof of everything sat in a clock tower ticking over the town like a held breath.

Grief turned over in her. And something else turned with it, something with edges.

She stood up too fast and knocked the stool back, and stood at the tall window with her palms flat on the cold sill, breathing. Below, lamplit streets in the rain. A patrol crossing the square in step. On the customs house roof, the army flag hung wet and heavy, and beneath it the harbor lay black, where one berth remained and a thousand names wanted it.

Edda found her there. The astronomer came up the stair without her stick tonight — pride, or urgency — and stood a moment in the doorway reading the room the way blind people read rooms, by air and sound. "You've finished," she said.

"I finished."

"And?"

Isandro looked at the papers on the bench, at her father's cipher and her transcription side by side, ten years apart and in the same upright hand, because she had become him without meaning to. "You knew him," she said. "You taught him the stars. Did you teach him this?"

"The cipher? No. His mother was a customs factor's daughter. He learned it young." Edda crossed the room and laid her fingers on the pages without reading them, reading Isandro instead — the set of her shoulders, the breath in her. "Tell me."

Isandro told her. All of it, flat and complete, the way her father would have told it: the arms, the grain, the petition, the signatures. Edda listened without moving, and when it was done she was silent for the length of nine ticks of the master clock — Isandro counted, because counting was what her hands did when her chest was full.

"Two hundred fourteen names," Edda said at last. "I signed that petition. I'd forgotten." Her fingers tightened on the edge of the bench. "They took Joren Vasse that winter. He taught mathematics at the dame school. They said sedition. Nobody said *why*."

"Because the why was in here." Isandro touched the scrap. "And nobody could read it."

"His daughter can."

The words landed and stayed. Isandro looked at them hanging in the air between them, and felt the callus crack — not the grief, the grief would keep, grief always keeps — but the thing grief had calcified into, the smallness, the hiding, the ten years of borrowed names and lowered eyes and *don't look up, don't speak first, be the forgettable girl with the tools*. She had thought survival was the task. She had thought keeping her father's name out of her mouth was loyalty.

It wasn't loyalty. It was surrender with good posture.

"He wasn't a traitor," she said. Her voice came out low and even and entirely new. "He was the only honest clerk in this town, and they hanged him for it, and the man who signed the order is downstairs in the world right now deciding who lives and who drowns, and the ledger that proves it all is sitting in his own requisition office being copied by his own clerk."

"You say that like it's already a plan."

"It isn't a plan yet. But there's a clerk." She thought of Wren Callis — pale, precise, met once across a workbench with ink-stained fingers and eyes that flinched from nothing except kindness. An hour ago she had seen him in the square, copying notices, and seen him pause at one line and look up at the garrison windows before writing on. A man who checked things. A man who noticed alterations, if alterations existed. Her father would have known him instantly. So would she. "A clerk who copies the seized civil ledger by order. If Harrow altered any entry in that ledger — any name, any origin — then the original hand and the correction sit side by side on Wren Callis's desk."

Edda tilted her head. "And if they do?"

"Then we have my father's cipher proving what Harrow did ten years ago, and a duplicate ledger proving what he's doing now, and Maren Ostrow's family proving the grain, and two hundred fourteen petitioners still alive in this town to prove the rest." She gathered the papers, squared their edges, weighted them. The gesture was pure workbench, pure craft, and it steadied her the way craft always had. "Truth doesn't hang people, Edda. Unwitnessed truth does. My father wrote the witness and hid it where no one would look. I'm going to finish the job. I'm going to make them read it."

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a ship's bell rang the change of watch, muffled by rain. One final berth. Dawn coming closer every hour it was rung.

Edda smiled — a thin, fierce, scholarly smile, the smile Isandro imagined she gave a star that finally returned after a decade's cloud. "Your father used to say the heavens keep the only incorruptible ledger. He'd have liked this. Arithmetic as revenge."

"Not revenge." Isandro blew out the candle. In the dark the master clock filled the tower, patient, enormous, keeping the true time whether anyone above it deserved the hour or not. "Correction."

She was halfway down the stair, the scrap safe against her ribs, when she heard boots on the observatory steps below — two pairs, climbing fast, and a voice she knew already by its smoothness calling up through the dark:

"Clockmaker! Colonel Harrow's compliments. He requires your presence at the garrison. Tonight."

* * *

Isandro descended with her tool roll over her shoulder and her face arranged into the blank competence of a tradesman summoned at an inconvenient hour. Fear was there — fear had never left since the day Harrow walked these halls praising her gears — but fear rode behind the craftsman now instead of in front of him. That was new. She catalogued the difference as she stepped out into the rain, past the two soldiers, toward the carriage with its lantern swinging.

Whatever the garrison wanted of her at midnight, it would want it from the clockmaker. Fine. Let it send for the clockmaker.

The daughter of Joran Vane had finished reading.
