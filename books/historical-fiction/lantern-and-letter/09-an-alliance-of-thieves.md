## 9. An Alliance of Thieves

The observatory at night was a different animal than the observatory by day. By day it belonged to the garrison's schedules and Edda Sollen's lectures and the slow arithmetic of gears; by night it belonged to the wind, which came off the harbor and pressed against the dome like a hand testing a lid. Isandro lit no lamps. She sat in the dark beneath the master clock with her father's scrap of paper flattened on her knee, and she waited to find out whether she had just committed treason or merely made two friends.

Both, probably. In Bellhollow, lately, the categories had collapsed into one another.

The knock came at the service door — three taps, a pause, two more. Edda's code, taught to her that afternoon in a whisper while they pretended to discuss escapement weights: *three for the question, two for the answer.* Isandro let her count to ten before she opened it.

Wren Callis came in sideways, like a man entering water he didn't trust. He still wore his clerk's grey, but he'd buttoned a fisherman's oilskin over it, which fooled no one; the man smelled of ink and candle tallow and fear. Under his arm, wrapped in sacking, he carried a parcel the size of a hymnal.

"You weren't followed?" Isandro asked.

"I'm an army clerk," Wren said. "Nobody follows clerks. Nobody sees us at all. That's the one advantage of my profession." He set the parcel down on the workbench and then, as if it had burned him through the sacking, took his hand off it immediately.

Edda was already there — she lived in the observatory's undercroft and needed no door knocked on. She moved through the dark with the certainty of a woman who had stopped using her eyes twenty years ago and had never once missed them. "Sit," she said, to both of them, though she was facing the wrong wall. "Standing meetings look guilty from the street."

"From the street this whole building looks guilty," Isandro said. "It's full of clocks. The garrison thinks clocks are mathematics and mathematics are politics."

"Then we'd best be quick about our politics." Edda lowered herself into the chair by the meridian instrument. "Wren Callis. You knew my brother, I think. Pell Sollen."

Wren went very still. "Pell Sollen was cashiered before I was posted here. Everyone said—"

"Everyone said he falsified supply manifests. Yes." Edda's voice was dry as old varnish. "He did. He also documented who ordered him to, in cipher, because he was a sentimental fool who believed someone would eventually read it. Did anyone ever read it?"

"No one could," Wren said quietly. "It was seized with his effects. I copied the seizure inventory myself, years ago. I remember thinking the pages were nonsense."

"They were mine," Edda said. "I invented that nonsense when I was nineteen and thought I was clever. Which means I am the only person in this province who can read it — and the reason your Colonel Harrow has spent eleven years making sure no one else learned how." She turned her face toward Isandro. "Show him what you found in the clock housing."

Isandro unwrapped the scrap. In the dark it was just texture, a rectangle of rag paper gone soft at the edges. Wren leaned close without touching it.

"That's ledger stock," he said at once. "Civil ledger stock. Heavy weave, blue-ruled margin — see the ghost line? Garrison stock is red-ruled. Harrow changed the stationery order his first month in command. He wanted everything on paper he could recognize as his own."

"You can tell paper by its rules," Isandro said.

"I can tell paper by its rules, its watermark, its weight, and which quartermaster skimmed on the order. It's not a skill. It's a disease." He hesitated. "May I?"

She gave it to him. Watching another man's hands hold her father's handwriting was harder than she'd expected — some animal part of her wanted to snatch it back, the way you guard a wound even from the surgeon. Wren held the scrap up against the faint green glow of the radium dial paint, tilting it.

"The entries are in columns," he murmured. "Amounts, dates, initials. If I were copying this blind, I'd call it cargo tallies. Grain, powder, shot. But the amounts don't reconcile — every column is off by the same fraction. Eleven parts in a hundred."

"Eleven percent," Edda said. "Harrow's cut. My brother recorded it twice before he was caught. The same figure, in the same cipher. It never changes. Greed is the most reliable constant in nature — more reliable than the stars. Stars wobble. Greed does not."

Isandro heard herself speak before she had decided to. "My father wrote this."

The words dropped into the room and rang there. Wren looked up. Edda did not move, but her head tilted a degree, listening the way she listened to clockwork — for the truth under the sound.

"You're certain," Edda said. Not a question, quite. An invitation to be certain out loud.

"It's his hand. I grew up watching it. He wrote his fives with a flat top, like a flag on a pole. He—" Her voice snagged. She pushed past it. "He was hanged as a rebel. For treason. For forging army records, they said. And now you're telling me these records say the opposite. That he wasn't forging anything. He was *copying.*"

"He was documenting," Edda said. "There is a difference, and it is exactly the difference between a rope around your neck and a monument in the square. Harrow hanged him for testimony and called it treason. Then he burned the original pages and assumed the matter closed." She reached out; Isandro placed the scrap back in her palm, and the old woman's fingers moved over it like a blind woman reading a face. "But your father was a clockmaker's son too, girl. He hid things where only patience would find them. Inside the master clock. Where time itself would keep them safe until time itself was ready."

* * *

They worked by one shaded lamp, hooded so the light fell in a coin on the table and nowhere else. Edda recited the cipher key from memory — substitutions built on prime numbers, letters shifted by intervals of two, three, five, seven, wrapping around the alphabet like gear teeth. "My brother couldn't resist making it mechanical," she said. "Every letter advances by the next prime. Break the sequence and the message breaks with it. He thought that was elegant. I told him elegance gets people hanged. Look how right I was."

Isandro transcribed. Wren checked her arithmetic, because the cipher was, in the end, nothing but arithmetic wearing a mask. The lamp hissed. Outside, the garrison drums beat the half-hour, and the master clock above them answered, and Isandro felt the strange doubled pulse of it — two timekeepers, official and true, agreeing for now.

The first decoded lines came up like bodies rising in water:

*Received 14 tons grain, issued 12.6. Discrepancy sold, Vell's Landing.*
*Powder: 40 kegs drawn, 31 delivered. Remainder —*
*Names attached. H. initialled all.*

"Harrow's own hand authorizing the skim," Wren whispered. "On every page. He initialled his own theft because he never imagined anyone would read the margins." He laughed — a small, appalled sound, quickly strangled. "Clerks know something colonels never learn. Nothing is unreadable forever. Paper waits."

"There's more," Isandro said. Her finger had stopped on the last column, the densest one, the one her father had written in a smaller, faster script — the script of a man running out of nights. She ran the primes through it, her lips moving, and the translation assembled itself word by word like a bridge building itself ahead of her feet:

*If found, give to E.S. She will know the hour.*

Edda made a sound Isandro had never heard from her — not grief exactly, something older, a note held down for eleven years finally allowed to move. "To me," she said. "He meant it for me. And it sat in a clock for a decade, waiting for a stranger with clean hands to find it, because everyone who loved me was dead or scattered." She turned toward Isandro with those white, sightless eyes, and Isandro understood that being looked at by Edda Sollen was a thing that happened somewhere other than the eyes. "You have his hands, do you know that? I watched them tonight. The way you brace the small gears. He braced them exactly so."

"I'm not his daughter," Isandro said. "Not officially. Officially I'm Isandro Vane, journeyman, nephew of Aldous Vane of Thornmere." The lie came out smooth and rotten. "Officially, Aldous Vane had no family worth recording."

"Unofficially?" Wren asked softly.

"Unofficially," Isandro said, "my name is the one on a death warrant signed by the man who runs this town. So unofficially, I'd like very much to destroy him."

* * *

It was Wren who brought the room back to earth, the way clerks bring everything back to earth — with inventory. He unwrapped his sacking parcel and laid it open, and there it was: a ledger, civilian-ruled, filled edge to edge with his small upright copyist's hand.

"A duplicate," he said. "Of the civil ledger. All of it. Every family, every origin, every entry — and every entry the colonel altered after seizure, which I've marked in pencil in the margin, with the original text restored from my memory of the pre-seizure copies I filed last spring. I file everything twice. Always have. Habit, not principle." A pause. "Well. Habit that turned out to be principle."

Edda traced the pages with her fingertips, reading the pencil marks like braille. "Alterations in whose interest?"

"His, always. Names struck from the evacuation eligibility rolls. Origins rewritten — three families of Vell's Landing descent reclassified as out-of-province, which strips their claim to relief rations. And one entry—" Wren stopped. Started again. "One entry I haven't marked yet, because I only understood it tonight. A dockhand registered under the name Tomas Ferrow. Age, description, and date of registration all match a boy who was entered in the original ledger under a different surname entirely."

The room went quiet enough to hear the clock's escape wheel ticking, tick by patient tick.

"What different surname?" Isandro asked. Her voice came out flat and careful, a craftsman's voice, and she hated how much effort the flatness cost.

Wren read from his duplicate. "Ferrow. Registered eight months ago, sponsored by no one, listed occupation stevedore. In the original spring filing, the same man appears — I'm nearly certain it's the same man — as Tomas Vane. Of Thornmere."

Above them the master clock began to strike the hour, and Isandro stood inside the sound of it and let it shake her bones, because if she moved she would break. *Tomas.* Her brother, alive, eight months in this town, hauling crates forty yards from the plague ships — and she had walked past dockhands in the street a dozen times with her collar up and her eyes down, guarding a secret that had kept her from her own blood.

"He doesn't know you're here," Edda said. It wasn't a question either. Blind women, Isandro was learning, asked almost nothing as questions.

"He knows his sister was hanged's daughter and got out of Thornmere. He doesn't know where to. If he learned Isandro Vane was in Bellhollow, he'd come to me — and then we'd both be names Harrow could hang. Right now only one of us is." She pressed her palms flat on the table. "Tell me about the berth list. The final ship."

"One berth list, one ship, dawn tide a week from Friday," Wren said. "Allocated solely at the colonel's discretion, which in practice means at the discretion of whoever pays him. I draft the fair copy. I've seen the working list. There are forty-one names for six berths, and thirty-five of the forty-one have made contributions to the colonel's *discretionary fund* — which exists in no regulation I can find and which I personally balance to the penny every Thursday." His mouth twisted. "Tomas Ferrow is not on the working list. No bribe, no sponsor, no patron. He'll watch that ship sail from the quay like everyone else who can't pay."

"Then we put him on it," Isandro said.

"With what?" Wren spread his hands. "Ink? I can write any name you like on any list you like, Miss Vane, and I'll hang for forgery the moment anyone checks the working papers. We have a cipher, a duplicate, and a corpse's testimony. We don't have power. Power is what Harrow has. Power is why we're meeting in the dark."

"Paper *is* power," Edda said. "That's the entire lesson of the last eleven years, child. Why do you think he seized the civil ledger first, before the granaries, before the arms? Names are the only currency this town has left. He stole the mint." She rose, and her hand found Isandro's shoulder with uncanny accuracy. "And we have just taken it back. Three strangers with one ledger between them. That's not nothing. That's how every honest government in history has ever started."

Isandro looked at the two of them — the disgraced clerk still smelling of fear, the blind astronomer standing straight as a mast — and felt the shape of the thing they were becoming. Not a plan yet. Not yet an army. A conspiracy, which was only a plan that hadn't been tested.

"We meet again tomorrow night," she said. "Bring me the working berth list, Wren — a tracing, not the original. Edda, I want the rest of the marginalia decoded by then, every page. If my father died documenting Harrow's thefts, then every theft he documented is a nail, and I intend to use all of them."

"And your brother?" Wren asked gently.

"My brother," Isandro said, "stays a stranger to me a few days longer. After that—" She looked up at the great clock face, pale in the dark, its hands set to the hour she herself had calibrated. "After that, nobody in this town stays a stranger. Not if I can help it."

Below them, far off toward the harbor, a whistle blew — the quarantine patrol changing watch, thin and sharp across the water. And beneath it, almost lost, another sound Isandro had learned to dread: the measured tramp of boots coming up Observatory Hill, two men by their weight, unhurried, official.

Wren killed the lamp. In the sudden black, Edda's voice came soft and steady as a pendulum:

"Well. Someone wants to know what the clock says at midnight. Let's go show them."
