## 10. The Berth List

The colonel wrote the berth list the way other men played cards: with a small smile and one eye on his opponents' hands.

Wren Callis knew this because he was made to sit across the desk from him while he did it, ink pot open, blotting paper ready, ordered to take down each name in fair copy as it was spoken aloud. Not to suggest. Not to read. To write.

"You'll find, Callis," Harrow said, dipping his pen, "that allocation is the purest form of government. Every other kind involves giving people what they want. Allocation involves giving them what you decide they can have." He drew a line beneath nothing at all. "Begin."

"Beginning, sir. First name?"

Harrow consulted a paper before him — not an official list, Wren noted, but a sheet of personal correspondence, its edges soft with handling. "Draymoor. Surgeon Draymoor and household."

Wren wrote it. He knew Draymoor's household numbered nine. The berth — the single civilian berth on the *Providence*, the last ship out of Bellhollow before quarantine closed the harbor for good — had been posted in the gazette as accommodating forty souls. Forty souls, priority to the infirm, the aged, and children under ten. That was the notice nailed to every door by soldiers who wouldn't meet anyone's eyes.

The colonel's first name was a surgeon with no infirm in his house.

"And Mrs. Draymoor's sister, if she's still in town. Vessing. Write Vessing."

Wren wrote Vessing.

* * *

He had been a clerk for eleven years and he had learned early that the ledger never lies but the hand that fills it always might. His father, who had kept accounts for a wool factor in Corvess, used to say a clerk's whole virtue was this: he writes what happened, not what he wishes had happened. It was the only sermon the family owned, and Wren had built his life on it the way other men built theirs on scripture — imperfectly, and with frequent lapses, but never quite abandoning the foundation.

The lapses were the trouble. There had been lapses all week.

It began with the requisition itself, when the civil ledger came out of the townhouse under a sergeant's arm like a prisoner being transferred. Wren had been assigned to receive it, log it, and begin copying it into the garrison's own books — every family in Bellhollow, every name, every trade, every place of origin, going back sixty years. It was a census and a confession both; half the dockside families were listed under names adopted after the Rebellions, when a man's origin could hang him as surely as a crime.

"Copy it exactly," Captain Reeve told him. "Every entry. The colonel wants a clean record."

*A clean record.* Wren had written those words in his own notebook without thinking, and then sat looking at them for a long time, because he had already seen the entries that weren't clean — the ones where a different hand had come through years ago and adjusted things. A toll payment struck out. A shipment weight corrected downward. A name entered twice, once spelled plainly and once in a way that would fail any search. And in the margins, in cramped cipher that looked like bookkeeping and wasn't, little clusters of figures beside certain dates.

He had said nothing. That was lapse the first.

Lapse the second was the duplicate. He hadn't planned it. He had simply, one evening, ruled a second column in his working draft and begun entering the original ledger text there alongside the "cleaned" version the captain wanted — the version with the marginalia omitted and two of the altered entries restored to their falsified state. When he caught himself doing it, his pen hovering, heart going hard, he asked himself the only question that mattered: *is this what happened?*

It was. So he kept writing.

His father would have understood. His father would also have told him he was a fool. Both things could be true.

* * *

"Now," Harrow said, on the second morning of the list, "the general population."

They worked in the colonel's office in the garrison keep, a room that smelled of lamp oil and cold stone, with the harbor visible through a window so grimy the ships looked like sketches of themselves. Quarantine flags flew from the customs house. Below, along the waterfront, the town went about its dying business — carts hushed, voices low, everyone glancing at the water the way a starving man glances at bread.

Harrow had a stack of petitions. They arrived constantly now — pushed under doors, handed to sentries, carried by children too young to know better. Wren had logged forty-one of them himself. Forty-one families, each making the same case in different words: we are old, we are sick, our children are small, we have served the crown, we have nowhere else.

Harrow read perhaps one in five. He read them the way a man reads menus.

"The Ostrows," he said. "Maren Ostrow, laundress. Husband dead, is that right?"

"Yes, sir. Fever, last month."

"Pity. Three children?" He glanced at the petition. "Ages?"

"Eleven, eight, and four, sir."

"Four." Harrow set the petition down and tapped it once with his finger, and for a moment Wren thought — hoped — that some arithmetic of mercy was occurring behind those pale eyes. "Children under ten get priority per the posted notice. But the mother's a widow, no trade the garrison requires, and the eldest is over ten, which complicates the household count. Deny. Next."

"Deny," Wren repeated, writing it. His pen felt heavier than pens should.

"They can appeal through channels."

"There are no channels, sir. You closed the appeals office Tuesday."

Harrow smiled without warmth. "Then there will be very few appeals, won't there. Next."

The next hour was the same hour wearing different clothes. A chandler denied. A rope-maker granted — he supplied the garrison, and the garrison would need rope until the day it sailed. A widow with a consumptive daughter denied; a wine merchant with a cellar full of port granted. Wren took down each ruling in fair copy, and somewhere around the twentieth name he realized he had stopped feeling each denial individually and begun feeling them collectively, as one long continuous sound, like the sea against a hull.

That frightened him more than any single name. A clerk who stops hearing individual names is a clerk who can write anything.

* * *

It was near dusk when the dockworkers came up.

Not literally — Harrow processed the harbor trades as a block, from a muster roll the harbormaster had submitted. Wren recognized the handwriting; old Sorrel wrote his fives like sevens. The roll held thirty-one men, listed by working name and berth assignment, most of them laborers and stevedores who loaded and unloaded everything Bellhollow had ever been.

"Hold," Harrow said, running his finger down the page. "These men work the quay. If the town evacuates, who loads the evacuation?"

"No one, sir. The Providence takes on her own stores by navy crew."

"And until she sails? We're three weeks from departure, Callis, and the garrison still eats." He drew a bracket around the top six names. "Retain these. Skilled or essential. The rest—" He scanned, and stopped.

Wren watched him stop. It was a small thing, a hitch in the finger's travel down the column, less than a second. But Wren had spent eleven years reading hands and faces over documents, and he knew what a hitch meant. It meant recognition. It meant *this name means something.*

"Vane," Harrow said. "Tomas Vane. Dockhand." He said the surname the way a man bites something to test whether it's real gold.

"That's the entry, sir."

"It's a common enough name." Harrow leaned back. "Origin?"

Wren checked the ledger copy — he had it memorized in sections now, which was lapse the third, though he'd stopped counting. "Entered as Corvess-born. No prior record."

"No prior record." Harrow repeated it softly, tasting it. Then, briskly: "Excluded. Dockhands aren't essential past loading day, and I want no ambiguity on that quay. Mark him excluded."

"Sir — he's twenty-two, able-bodied, and the notice says—"

"The notice says what I allocated it to say." Harrow's voice didn't rise; it did something worse, it cooled. "Is there a reason you're interested in this particular dockhand, Callis?"

"No, sir."

"Then mark him excluded."

Wren marked him excluded. The nib scratched and the letters came out even and upright, because eleven years of training does not care what the heart is doing: *Vane, Tomas — excluded.*

But his mind had gone somewhere else entirely. Because he remembered that entry now, the ledger entry, and not for the name alone. Tomas Vaine, Corvess-born, dockhand, lodgings at the Salt Row loft — entered in the civil ledger four years ago, in the census year, in a hand that was neat and careful and slightly too deliberate, the hand of someone writing a false biography and knowing it. Wren had noticed it weeks ago precisely because it was *too* correct, every detail in order, like a room staged for inspection.

And he remembered something else, something that had seemed trivial then: that the entry sat directly opposite a page bearing marginalia in the cipher. Small figures, cramped, beside the date of the Vane registration. Beside the name.

He said none of this. He blotted the line, squared the page, and asked, "Next name, sir?" in a voice that belonged to a man with no interior at all.

* * *

He walked back through the lower town in the blue dark, past shuttered shops and doorways where families sat together in the last light because no one wanted to be alone in a room. A girl was chalk-drawing on the cobbles outside the bakery — hopscotch squares, no one to play them. At the harbor gate, the quarantine chain hung across the water road, and a soldier stood beside it with a lantern, lighting nothing.

Forty souls, the notice said. Priority to the infirm, the aged, and children under ten.

So far the list held a surgeon, a wine merchant, a rope-maker, the colonel's quartermaster, two officers' wives, and a man whose entire qualification appeared to be that he had once lent Harrow money in Corvess — Wren had copied that petition himself and recognized the creditor's seal. Eleven names. Twenty-nine remaining. Four hundred and six households in Bellhollow.

He climbed to his room above the records office and lit his lamp and took out his working draft — the true copy, the one that existed nowhere official — and turned to the section he'd been avoiding since the afternoon. The harbor trades. The dockhand.

*Tomas Vaine, Corvess-born.* Registered four years ago. Lodging at Salt Row. And beside the registration date, in the margin, in cipher: a short run of figures, the longest such run on the page. Whatever the hidden hand had recorded about this man, it had recorded a great deal of it.

Wren sat with his pen above the duplicate column for a long moment.

Here was the shape of it, plain as type: a colonel who falsified records, exposed — if the cipher said what it seemed to say — by whoever wrote those margins. A rebel hanged for testimony disguised as treason; he'd heard the story like everyone else, the clockmaker's son, seven years back, though the trial papers had been sealed, which itself told you something. And now, on the quay of a dying town, a dockhand with a staged biography carrying the same surname, excluded from the last berth by a colonel whose finger hitched at the name.

Coincidence was possible. Wren was a clerk; he believed in coincidence the way farmers believed in dry summers — occasionally, briefly, and never twice in a row.

There was a third person in this equation, he thought. The observatory's new clockmaker. Quiet, precise, watchful — the kind of watchfulness that has a specific object. Wren had passed her twice on the observatory stair and both times she had looked at him the way people look at locked doors: assessing whether they open.

She had repaired the master clock. She handled mechanisms. She had access to the tower where the old astronomer lived — the astronomer who, according to the garrison gossip Wren officially never listened to, had taught the hanged rebel his letters and figures, back before anyone was anything.

He didn't have a plan. He had a duplicate ledger, a conscience with lapses, and a growing conviction that the truth about Bellhollow was currently sitting in pieces inside a clocktower, waiting for someone to assemble it.

Tomorrow the colonel would finalize the list. Tomorrow Tomas Vane's exclusion would go into fair copy, into the record, into permanence — and a twenty-two-year-old with a staged biography would stand on a quay and watch the last ship leave without him, and never know why.

Wren dipped his pen. In the margin of his own private draft, beside the dockhand's entry, he wrote in his smallest, neatest hand:

*Excluded by H. personally. Finger paused at name. Ask the clockmaker.*

Then he blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the harbor chain creak, and waited for morning.
