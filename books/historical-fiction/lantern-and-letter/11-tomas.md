## 11. Tomas

I told myself I was going to the harbor for rope.

That was the lie I chose on purpose, because a good lie needs a true bone in it — I did need rope, waxed hemp for the strike train weights, and the chandler near the fish market sold it cheaper than anyone in Bellhollow. But the truth was that I had heard his voice three days ago through the window of the harbor master's office, arguing about tonnage fees, and I had not slept properly since.

Tomas Vane's voice is lower than mine ever was. He got Father's chest and I got Mother's sharpness, or so our uncle used to say over supper, back when we were children with a father who came home smelling of ink instead of gallows-rope. Three days ago that voice came through a shuttered window and my whole body turned toward it like a compass needle finding north, and I stood in the alley behind the harbor office with a coil of ledger paper under my arm and let him walk past me close enough to touch.

He looked tired. That was the worst of it. Twenty-two years old and carrying crates like an old man, shoulders already set in the shape of the loads he'd carried since he was fourteen, since the day they took Father and I went one way into the hills and he went another way into nobody knew where. Six years. I had imagined him a hundred times and never once imagined him tired.

So: rope. And if the chandler happened to be near the east quay where dockhands gathered at the noon bell to argue about the berth list, then that was only geography.

The town had changed again overnight. It kept changing, the way weather changes, each morning bringing some new weight of soldiers or notices or silence. Quarantine flags flew from the customs house now — yellow jack with its black square, the color of a wound left open. The garrison had chalked lines across the harbor road, and men in gray coats checked papers at the crossing with the bored cruelty of boys given power too early. Nobody crossed without a reason. Nobody lingered without becoming a reason.

I had a reason. A clockmaker's apprentice does not need to explain why she carries tools; her tools explain her. Uncle Aldous's name explained the rest — dead these four years, God rest him, and useful to me still, because no one troubles a dead man's niece. *Isandro* was a name I had worn so long it fit like callus. Sometimes I caught myself thinking in it. Sometimes I woke and could not remember, for a full breath, that there had been another girl before her, a girl who had a brother.

The noon bell rang — my bell, technically, though the harbor bell was a smaller thing rung by hand — and the dockhands came off the quay like water finding a drain. They gathered by the chandlery wall where the sun hit warmest, and I bought my rope and haggled over it longer than I needed to, and listened.

They were talking about the list.

"—posted Thursday," a broad man with salt-cracked hands was saying. "Colonel's own discretion, that's what the notice says. Discretion." He said the word the way you'd spit out a fishbone.

"My brother-in-law paid the harbormaster's clerk two crowns in March," said another. "Two crowns, and now the clerk says March money buys nothing in quarantine season."

"Then your sister drowns slow instead of fast," the first man said, "same as the rest of us."

There was a low ugly laugh, the kind that means no one is laughing. I folded my rope over my arm and looked down the line of faces until—

There.

He was sitting apart, on an upturned crate against the chandlery wall, eating bread with the concentration of someone who could not afford to waste a crumb. Darker hair than I remembered, cut short the way laborers wore it. A scar through one eyebrow I didn't know. But the way he held his shoulders when he chewed — slightly forward, guarding the food, a habit learned young in a house where there were sometimes more mouths than loaves — that I knew the way you know your own handwriting.

Tomas. My brother, six years grown, sitting ten feet from me under another man's name.

Because it would be another man's name. Of course it would. Our family name was worth hanging now; Father had seen to that with his testimony and his rope. Tomas would be called something plain — Ostrow, maybe, or Brandt, one of the names that filled Bellhollow like sand fills a glass. He would have papers forged well or badly, and he would flinch, just slightly, every time a soldier looked too long.

I should have walked away. I want to say that plainly, because everything after came from not walking away, and a person should know their own hinge. I should have bought my rope and gone back up the hill to the observatory, to Edda Sollen and her cipher and the scrap of my father's hand hidden in the clock housing, and I should have kept my brother as a grief instead of making him a risk.

Instead I heard myself say, "You're favoring the left hand."

He looked up. Gray eyes. Mother's gray, not Father's dark. For one second the world narrowed to a needle's point, and I thought — *he knows me, he'll know me, six years isn't enough, blood remembers* — and I braced for my name in his mouth, which would be the end of everything, both our necks in one noose.

But six years is enough. Blood forgets faster than we'd like to believe. He looked at me the way you look at a stranger who has spoken to you unexpectedly: wary, polite, waiting to learn what I wanted.

"Sorry?" he said.

"Your hand," I said. "When you eat. You're favoring the left. Wrist injury?"

He glanced at his wrist as if checking whether it belonged to him. "Old break. Dock work." He started to turn away — dismissal, the small armor of a man with no standing and much to hide.

"Ropemaker," I said, lifting the coil on my arm. "We see a lot of wrists. There's a salve the observatory cook makes, comfrey and tallow. If the ache gets bad before winter."

"Thank you," he said, and went back to his bread, and I stood there like a fool with rope in my arms and my heart going like a struck escapement.

It was then I noticed the paper sticking out of his shirt pocket. A printed notice — the requisition notice, the one nailed to every door in Bellhollow — but creased and refolded many times, soft as cloth from handling. And beneath it, half-visible, the corner of something else. Handwritten. A list? A letter?

And I noticed something else, something that dropped through me cold as harbor water: he was watching the customs house. Not idly. With the flat fixed attention of a man counting guards, timing their rounds, learning a pattern.

My brother was planning something. Or waiting for something. Or running from something, and doing it badly, because Tomas had always been brave in exactly the ways that got people hurt.

* * *

I found out his name by accident, which is how most things in Bellhollow happened that week.

The chandler, a woman named Petra Hoss with forearms like mooring cable, counted out my change and said, without looking up, "Don't mind Corin. He's not unfriendly, just careful. Half this quay is careful lately."

"Corin," I repeated.

"The one with the wrist. Good worker, keeps to himself. Came up from Kesselring way in the spring, no family, no talk." She shrugged. "Everyone's from somewhere. Nobody's from anywhere anymore. That's what the requisition did — made us all strangers with papers."

*Kesselring*. A town forty miles south that I had actually passed through once, which meant if I asked him anything about it I might trip over my own geography. I filed the name away — Corin, of Kesselring, dockhand, careful, alone — and felt the strangeness of holding a stranger's name that was also the most important word in my life.

I spent the afternoon in the clock room and accomplished very little. The great movement lay open around me, wheels and levers spread like anatomy, and I sat among them turning the scrap of ledger paper over and over — Father's cipher, Edda's lessons, the third key she'd given me Tuesday, the one that unlocked the columns of dates. Dates and figures. Smuggling manifests dressed as grain shipments, if Edda's reading held. Proof that Colonel Harrow had been selling army stores out of Bellhollow for years and writing the losses off as spoilage — proof my father had found, copied, coded into the margins of a ledger, and died for keeping.

*Treason was testimony.* I had written that in my notebook like a catechism. But knowing it and feeling it were different countries, and I kept crossing between them badly.

And now Tomas was here, in the same town, inside the same trap — quarantine, requisition, one berth left on the last ship out, allocated by the same colonel who had killed our father. If Harrow's men ran the rolls, if any record of the Vane family surfaced, if my brother's false papers cracked under one bad question —

I put down the scrap and pressed the heels of my hands into my eyes.

"You are a clockmaker's apprentice," I told the room. "You are Aldous Vane's niece. You repair clocks. That is all you are and all you can afford to be."

The clock ticked at me, patient and indifferent, and did not agree.

* * *

I went back the next day. And the next. Rope, twine, a block of tallow, a reel of tarred marline — the chandlery must have thought me outfitting a fleet. Each time, Corin of Kesselring was somewhere in sight of the wall, working or resting or watching the customs house with his flat fixed stare, and each time I manufactured a reason to speak to him. The salve worked — I gave him a twist of paper of it on the second day, and he used it, I could see, which meant he trusted it, which meant he trusted me a little, and I hated myself for how carefully I was building that trust and how much I needed it.

On the fourth day, he spoke first.

"You're the one from the observatory."

"Not the astronomer. Her clockmaker."

"The clock on the hill." He nodded up at it, white against the sky. "People say it hasn't kept true time in years."

"It will," I said. "It's why I'm here."

"They say the army wants it fixed so the garrison runs on schedule." Something moved behind his face — contempt, maybe, or fear. "Whole town's run by their schedule now. When we eat, when we work, when we can stand at our own harbor gates."

"That's what requisition means," I said. "It doesn't ask."

"No," he said. "It doesn't." He was quiet a moment, turning something over. Then, abruptly: "Can I ask you something? You're not from Bellhollow either. You'd know if it's the same everywhere."

"I'm from the coast south of here."

"Do they read the lists out loud? In other towns. The berth lists." His voice was level, careful, a man walking on river ice. "Or do they just post them, so a man finds out his name isn't on them the way you find out anything — alone, reading a wall?"

The question had a wound inside it. I saw it the way you see a flaw in glass, by the way the light bent around it.

"There was a list posted this morning," I said. "By the customs house. Forty-one names."

"I know. I read it." His jaw tightened. "Forty-one names out of nine thousand people. And the names that are on it—" He stopped himself. Started again, quieter. "A friend of mine. He's got a wife and a daughter. Fever's in their street already. He's paid everyone there is to pay. His name's not on it. So I've been wondering what a man does next, when the list is wrong and the man who wrote it owns the ship."

I knew — of course I knew — that there might be no friend. That the wife and daughter might be his own invention, or his own life wearing a borrowed coat. Tomas had lied to me for four days under a name that wasn't his; I could hardly begrudge him one more layer. But it didn't matter, that was the terrible thing. True or invented, the need underneath it was real, and real need was a fire, and my brother had always been a man who walked into fires holding buckets that leaked.

"What would he do?" I asked. "Your friend."

"He'd do something stupid," Tomas said, and almost smiled, and the smile was six years old and broke my heart clean through. "That's his problem. He's stupid-brave. Our father was the same way, he says — the friend, I mean. Died being right about something, is the story. So the son's got it in his head that being right matters. Even now. Even here." He looked at the customs house, at the yellow flag, at the soldiers at the chalk line. "Being right doesn't get you a berth, though. Does it."

"No," I said. "It gets you a grave. Usually."

"Usually." He weighed the word. Then he turned and looked at me directly for the first time, and I understood that everything before had been approach and this was the landing. "You fix the army's clock. You go up that hill every day. You must see things. Hear things." He lowered his voice. "If a man wanted to know when the ship sails — truly sails, not what the notice says — or where the colonel keeps the master roll, the real one, with all the names — a man couldn't just ask. But a man might mention it to someone who moves between the garrison and the town, someone clever, someone with no stake in it. Someone like you."

*No stake in it.* If he only knew. My whole body was a stake; my name was the sharpest one.

"Why would I help?" I said. It came out colder than I meant. Colder than I wanted. But I had to know what he'd do with coldness — whether he'd retreat or push, whether his courage was stubborn or desperate, because those needed different handling and I had one chance to handle it right.

He didn't retreat. He looked at me with those gray eyes, Mother's eyes, and said simply: "Because it's right. And because everyone in this town has somebody who isn't on the list. Even you. Especially you, maybe. People who help other people's somebodies usually have a somebody of their own."

The afternoon light caught the dust between us. Somewhere down the quay a crane groaned, loading cargo no one would be allowed to leave with.

"I can't promise anything," I said. "I can't even promise I'll try. What I can tell you is that the colonel's records are not what they seem. Some of them." I watched that land — watched his face go very still, the stillness of a man hearing a sound in a house he'd thought empty. "If your friend is patient, and stays stupid-brave in private for a few more days, there may be a way to make the list answer for itself. To everyone. At once."

"How?"

"I mend clocks," I said. "Clocks speak to whole towns at a time."

He stared at me. And then, horribly, wonderfully, he laughed — a short startled bark of a laugh, and something in it was eight years old, sitting across from me at supper while Father drew gears on the table in spilled tea and said *a clock is just a truth that repeats itself*. Tomas didn't know why the words had snagged him. He couldn't know. But some part of him, some cellar of memory no alias could reach, had heard our father's voice in mine, and he laughed, and then pressed his hand over his mouth as if laughing were rationed too.

"Forgive me," he said. "It's just — my father used to say something like that. About clocks. He's dead," he added, quickly, unnecessarily. Everyone's fathers were dead that year; it was the safest sentence in Bellhollow.

"Mine too," I said. And it was the truest thing I had said in six years, and he had no way of knowing it, and neither of us could say one word more, and we stood there in the dust with the whole unsayable truth sitting between us like a crate too heavy to lift.

"Few more days," he said finally. "I can give your plan a few days. After that—" He didn't finish it. He touched his forehead, the old-fashioned courtesy our mother had drilled into us both, turned, and walked back toward the quay, favoring his left hand, carrying his borrowed name home like a coat that no longer fit.

I stood a long time with my rope and my secret, watching him go.

* * *

Up the hill, the light was failing and Edda Sollen was waiting in the clock room with the cipher pages spread under her blind hands, which saw more than most eyes.

"Well?" she said. "You've been to the harbor twice since the list posted. You smell of pitch and chandlery. Out with it."

"I found him," I said. My voice cracked on the second word. I hadn't meant it to. "Edda. He's alive. He's here. He's calling himself Corin and he begged a stranger to help his family, and the family is me, and I couldn't—"

I couldn't finish. Edda rose and found my shoulder with her hand the way she found everything, precisely, without looking.

"And you couldn't tell him," she said.

"If I tell him, I kill him. If the colonel learns the Vane girl is in Bellhollow — the daughter, the witness, whatever he thinks she is — he doesn't need a trial. And Tomas wouldn't run. You don't know him. He'd stay and fight beside me and die of it, both of us, and the ledger would burn with us and Father would have hanged for nothing after all." I wiped my face with the heel of my hand, angrily. "So I stood there and let my own brother beg me for help. And I helped. As a stranger. And it's the best I can do, and it's unbearable."

Edda was quiet a moment. Her thumb moved along the edge of the cipher page, reading it the way she read everything.

"When this is done," she said, "when the true hour strikes and the ledger speaks — you'll be able to tell him. Standing in the open, with your own name in your own mouth. That's what you're buying, Isandro. Not the berth. The introduction."

"And if it costs him his life to buy it?"

"Then you'll spend yours making it worth the price," she said, "which is the only inheritance your father left either of you. Now sit. We have the date column half-unlocked, and something in tonight's figures bothers me — the entries stop in March, mid-manifest, as if someone else finished the page in a different hand."

I sat. I bent over my father's coded hand by lamplight, and tried not to think of gray eyes and an old break in a left wrist and a laugh I hadn't earned.

But below us, in the dark, the garrison drums changed their beat — a new signal, shorter, sharper, one I hadn't heard before — and far off toward the harbor, a whistle blew twice and stopped.

Edda's head lifted. "What is that?"

"I don't know," I said. "But the clock room was locked when I came up. And the lock was scratched."
