## 12. The Clock Slows

I heard it before I understood it.

The master clock had a voice, and I knew it the way you know a mother's footsteps on a stair — not the sound itself but its particular rightness, the exact interval between strikes, the faint wooden groan it made in the last second of every hour like a man clearing his throat before speaking. For nine days I had lived inside that rhythm. I ate when the escapement told me morning had settled in earnest. I slept under its long tick like a child under a father's snore.

On the tenth day, at what should have been the ninth hour, the clock cleared its throat and said nothing.

Then it struck once. Only once. And the interval after was wrong — stretched, sluggish, a strike arriving late to its own appointment.

I was up the tower stairs before my boots were fully on.

The clock room smelled as it always did: oil, brass dust, the cold stone breath of the tower itself. But under it there was something else this morning, something sweetish and wrong, and I found the source within a minute. A rag, soaked in tallow and lamp oil, had been wedged between the great wheel and the frame. It hadn't caught fire — thank whatever god keeps fools alive — but its grease had migrated. The teeth of the third wheel were filmed with it, and the film had collected grit, and the grit had worked its way down into the pivot bearings, and the whole train below was dragging through sludge like a cart through February mud.

The clock wasn't broken. It was *slowed*. Deliberately. A clock that stops is an accident; a clock that runs four minutes slow is a message.

I stood there with my hands still dirty from yesterday's work and felt the message land in my chest like a dropped anchor.

Because I knew what ran on this clock now. Since the requisition, since the ledger came out of the townhouse and the quarantine ropes went up along the harbor, everything in Bellhollow ran on the observatory's time. The garrison's patrol rotations. The fever wagons to the lazaretto. The ration queues at the granary gate. The harbor watch's shift changes. Colonel Harrow had said it himself, standing in this very room with his gloved hand resting on the case like a man petting a horse he intended to buy: *A town that cannot agree on the hour cannot be governed.* He'd smiled when he said it. I had smiled back, because that was my job now — smiling at the man who signed my father's death order while my hands remembered how to hold a file steady.

And someone had slowed his clock by four minutes.

Four minutes meant a patrol overlap at the east gate. Four minutes meant a wagon sitting unguarded outside the lazaretto wall. Four minutes meant — my stomach turned over — meant that if anyone in Bellhollow wanted to move unseen, to pass contraband or a person or a piece of paper from one shadow to the next, the garrison's schedule would have a gap in it exactly wide enough.

Someone was using my clock as a weapon. Or testing whether it could be one.

* * *

I cleaned the train for two hours with spirits and linen scraps, working the grease out of the pivots tooth by tooth, and all the while my mind ran ahead of my hands.

It couldn't be Wren. Wren Callis came to the observatory twice a week now, ostensibly to deliver garrison correspondence to Edda Sollen, actually to bring me transcriptions from the seized ledger — the entries he suspected had been altered in the colonel's own hand. He was careful to the point of paranoia. He would never touch the clock; touching the clock drew eyes to the clock, and eyes were the one thing he could not survive.

Edda? She moved through the tower by rope-guides and memory, and she had no reason to slow anything. Her whole life was the opposite of slowing — she wanted the sky measured precisely, wanted the transit instruments true, wanted, more than anything these days, the cipher finished and her dead student's name scraped clean.

That left the town, or the army, or someone I hadn't thought of yet. A townsman with a grudge against Harrow, maybe, wanting to embarrass him in front of his own officers. An officer wanting to embarrass Harrow in front of *his* officers. Or —

Or someone who wanted the garrison to suspect the observatory.

The thought arrived cold and complete, the way the worst thoughts do, and I sat back on my heels among the wheels and let it look at me. Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't escape: hidden inside the clock room, behind a loose panel in the case base, wrapped in oilcloth, was Wren's duplicate ledger. Forty pages in a clerk's small neat hand, documenting every alteration he'd found in the civil record — names changed, origins erased, debts transferred, and threaded through them all, in the marginalia code Edda had taught me, the proof that the treason my father hanged for was nothing but testimony about Harrow's smuggling falsifications. It was the most dangerous object in Bellhollow. And I had hidden it inside the one building whose blamelessness the entire garrison depended on.

If the clock failed, the garrison would come here. If the garrison came here, they would search. And if they searched —

I heard the boots on the stairs before I finished the thought.

* * *

They came up in twos, six of them, and a sergeant whose name I'd learned was Brack — a broad, unimaginative man with a nose that had been broken so many times it had stopped pretending to point anywhere. Behind him, unhurried, came Lieutenant Ashe, whom I liked considerably less because he was imaginative.

"Master Vane." Brack touched his cap. They all called me Master Vane, my dead uncle's name, and I had worn it so long now it fit like an old coat — loose at the shoulders, but warm. "Colonel's compliments. The tower clock is running slow."

"I know," I said. "I've been correcting it since dawn. Sabotage." I said the word flatly, a tradesman naming a defect. "Grease and grit in the train. Someone got in here in the night."

Ashe drifted past me toward the clock case, hands clasped behind his back, and looked at it the way he looked at everything — as though it might confess. "Got in," he repeated. "Through a locked door. In a tower with one stair. On a night when our own sentries report nothing."

"Locks are for honest people, Lieutenant."

He smiled slightly at that, which I took as a bad sign. Smiles from clever men usually are.

"Who has keys?" Brack asked, producing a notebook. This part I could bear; this part was procedure, and procedure had a shape you could hide inside.

"You do. The colonel does. I do." I counted them off on greasy fingers. "And whoever cut a copy, which given what a Bellhollow locksmith charges for good work, I'd estimate half the town can afford."

Brack wrote it down without any sign of humor. Ashe looked at the clock, then at me, then at the clock again.

"The colonel is concerned," he said, "that the fault may not be mechanical."

"The fault is grease, sir. I'm looking at it."

"The colonel is concerned," Ashe continued, with the patience of a man laying cards face-up one at a time, "that the observatory has become — comfortable. Comfortable places collect things." His eyes went around the room. The instrument benches. Edda's star charts pinned in their careful constellations. My tool chest. The clock case itself, tall and dark, its base panel flush and oiled and innocent. "The colonel has asked me to conduct an inventory. Of the tower's contents."

My heart did something complicated and quiet, the way ice makes a sound before it cracks.

"An inventory," I said.

"A list of what belongs to the Crown now, versus what belongs to the Observatory trust. Requisition accounting." He unfolded a printed form with a flourish that was almost apologetic. "You understand. Paperwork is the army's native weather."

I understood perfectly. I understood that somewhere in the garrison a suspicion had formed — perhaps seeded deliberately by whoever greased the wheels — that the observatory was more than an observatory. And I understood that a man like Ashe, given permission to open drawers, would keep opening them until permission ran out.

"Then let's be efficient about it," I said, wiping my hands. "Instruments are the trust's. Furniture is the trust's. The clock is mine by contract — repair and maintenance, per the agreement your quartermaster countersigned. Everything else, ask."

* * *

They searched the way water searches: patiently, finding every low place.

Brack took the lower rooms — the old library, Edda's sleeping cell, the kitchen where I kept my few possessions in a sea chest that suddenly seemed very loud in my memory. Had I left anything in it? The scrap of my father's handwriting was upstairs in my workroom, folded inside a folio of gear calculations, disguised as a note-to-self about pendulum lengths. That was safe. Probably safe. Safe the way a candle in wind is safe.

Ashe took the clock room himself. Of course he did.

I stayed. Leaving would have been worse than staying — leaving was what guilty people did, and besides, my contract gave me the right to stand in this room, and rights were the only currency I had left, and I intended to spend every coin.

He started high. Star charts first; he unrolled three of them and held them to the light as if the stars might spell something. Then the transit instrument, which he examined with genuine interest and asked two intelligent questions about, which unsettled me more than stupidity would have. Then the workbench, drawer by drawer. Files, calipers, the folio of calculations — he flipped it, paused at the folded scrap inside, and my lungs forgot their trade — and read aloud: "'Check pallet stones, third wheel, drag in train.'" He glanced up. "Yours?"

"My handwriting," I said truthfully. It was. I'd recopied the cipher text in my own hand weeks ago and destroyed the original; Edda had insisted, and Edda was right about nearly everything. "I talk to myself on paper. Cheaper than a wife."

Something flickered across his face — amusement, or its professional imitation — and he put the folio down.

Which left the clock.

He walked around it slowly. Ran one finger along the case's molding, the same gesture Harrow had used, and I wondered briefly if ambition taught men to pet things. Then he crouched, and looked at the base.

The loose panel was behind the pendulum's swing, accessible only from the side, invisible unless you knelt exactly where he was kneeling. Wrapped inside it, in oilcloth, forty pages of treason in a clerk's tidy hand.

"The case is original?" Ashe asked. "Eighteen-tens?"

"Seventeen ninety-eight. Recased in 'ten."

"Mm." He pressed one palm flat against the base panel. Not prying. Just pressing, feeling, the way a doctor palpates. "Solid."

"Oak and glue," I said. "Like most honesty."

He stayed crouched a moment longer than comfort allowed. Long enough that I catalogued, in the strange bright slowness of fear, the details of the room — the smell of spirits, the dust motes climbing the window light, the pendulum ticking its patient tick behind him, four seconds out, four seconds back, the steadiest thing in a tightening world.

Then boots on the stairs again, fast, and a soldier's voice: "Lieutenant! Colonel wants you at the harbor gate directly. Ship's come in sight of the roadstead."

Ashe rose. Whatever he had been listening for — a hollow sound, a shifted seam — the ship had outranked it.

"Master Vane." He tucked the inventory form away, unfinished. "We'll conclude tomorrow."

"Any time convenient to the Crown," I said, and gave him my uncle's bland craftsman smile, and held it until the door closed behind him, and held it another ten seconds after that, just to be sure.

* * *

I didn't move the ledger immediately. That was the discipline Edda had beaten into me: *never run from a search, Isandro. A thing moved in panic leaves a wake.* So I sat down at the bench and did real work — finished truing the third wheel, re-oiled the pivots with clean oil, brought the clock's rate back within two seconds a day — and let my heartbeat come down to something resembling a craftsman's.

But my mind kept circling the same fixed point, like a fly at a window.

Tomorrow Ashe would return and finish the inventory, and next time there would be no ship to save us. The panel would not survive a second visit from a man who pressed his palm against oak and listened. Which meant the ledger had to move tonight — out of the tower entirely, somewhere no inventory would think to look. Into Edda's keeping? No; Edda's cell would be searched too, eventually, if the suspicion grew. Out of the observatory altogether, then — to Maren Ostrow, maybe, the cooper's widow who had seen the original ledger with her own eyes and hated Harrow with the clean heat of a woman who'd lost her husband to his requisitions. Or buried in the churchyard. Or sewn into —

The clock struck the noon hour, twelve clean strokes, correct to the second, and the sound went rolling down over the rooftops of Bellhollow, over the quarantine ropes and the fever tents and the harbor where a ship rode at anchor carrying — I didn't yet know how many berths. One, the rumor said. One berth, at the colonel's sole discretion.

Twelve strokes, and beneath them, faint, the town's other bells stumbling to catch up, and I realized with a lurch what I should have realized at dawn.

Four minutes slow. Whoever sabotaged the clock hadn't been trying to help anyone slip past a sentry. They'd been testing *me*. Testing whether the clockmaker would notice, whether she'd fix it, whether she'd say anything — or whether she'd let it run slow and use the gap herself. Every conspirator in Bellhollow was being weighed this morning, and the scale was my clock.

And I had passed the test by repairing it, which meant that whoever watched now knew the observatory's apprentice was either loyal or careful, and both answers would draw the same conclusion:

Worth watching closer.

I looked down at the clock case, at the panel holding forty pages of my father's vindication, and made the decision in the space between one tick and the next.

Tonight. Before moonrise. And God help whoever stood in my way.

Below me, faint through the stone, the town bells finally finished catching up — and somewhere down in the streets, I would learn soon enough, a sergeant was already writing my name in a notebook, and a lieutenant was already composing, in his careful ambitious hand, the sentence that would change everything:

*The clockmaker's apprentice knows more than clocks.*

Down in the tower's shadow, at that precise moment, a knock sounded at the observatory door — three knocks, evenly spaced, the signal Wren and I had agreed meant *urgent, alone, burn this after reading.* I wiped my hands, checked that the panel was flush, and went down the stairs to answer it, each step slower than the one before, because some part of me already understood that the walls were closing, and that the next knock would be Harrow's.
