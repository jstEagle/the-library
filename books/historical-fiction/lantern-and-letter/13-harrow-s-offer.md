## 13. Harrow's Offer

The summons came at dusk, folded twice and sealed with the garrison's stamp — a tower over crossed bayonets, stamped so hard the wax had split.

*Colonel Harrow requests the clockmaker's attendance at Bellhollow House, eight o'clock. The regulator in his study has stopped.*

Isandro read it three times by candlelight, as though repetition might soften it into something other than what it was: an invitation from the man who had signed her father's death warrant, delivered on paper that smelled of gun oil and lampblack.

*Requests.* Harrow did not request. The word was a courtesy laid over a command like a clean cloth over a wound. She had heard how he summoned people — Maren had told her, Wren had confirmed it — always polite, always final, the way a headsman is polite.

She set the note down and looked at her hands. Steady. Good. They were always steady when it mattered; fear went somewhere else in her, into her chest, into her jaw, into the small muscles behind her eyes that ached by morning. Her father had been the same, Tomas said. Tomas didn't know he'd said it. Tomas thought he was telling a stranger at the harbor wall about the man their mother had married before the war took her too — about a steady-handed man who mended watches in a shop that smelled of cedar shavings, right up until the soldiers came.

Eight o'clock. That gave her four hours to decide what she would be when she walked through Harrow's door: a clockmaker, or a daughter.

They were not the same person. Only one of them could survive the evening.

* * *

Bellhollow House had been the wool merchant Aldery's home before the requisition — Maren remembered dancing in its ballroom at a harvest fair, back when the floors were waxed instead of scuffed by boot-heels. Now sentries flanked the door and the merchant's rose garden had been trampled into a drill yard. Isandro gave her name — *Vane, master clockmaker, observatory contract* — to a corporal who wrote it in a book without looking up, and followed a servant down a corridor hung with maps of coastline she was not supposed to study but did anyway. Harbor charts. Depth soundings. One map had a red circle around the last deepwater berth, drawn and redrawn until the ink had bled through the paper.

The study door opened on lamplight and brandy and the tick of a dead clock.

Harrow stood at the window with his back to her, hands clasped behind him — a man built like the furniture around him, heavy and dark and made to outlast whoever bought him. He did not turn when the servant announced her. He let the silence work first. She knew that trick. She used it herself on apprentices who lied about broken pivots.

"Master Vane." He turned. Up close he was older than his portraits in the gazette, gray coming in at the temples like frost on iron. His eyes went to her tool roll, then her face, then past her, cataloguing. "You'll forgive the hour. A stopped clock offends me more than most offenses."

"I'll see what's wrong with it, sir."

"It's a Lachlan regulator," he said, as if that explained something. "Astronomical quality. My aide purchased it from an estate in Corrow. It has never kept honest time."

*It has never kept honest time.* She wondered if he said things like that deliberately, to watch faces. She kept hers still and knelt at the case.

The fault took her ten minutes to find because it was not a fault at all. The escapement had been deliberately retarded — a tiny brass shim wedged against the pallet stone, the kind of adjustment a smuggler makes when he wants the garrison bell ringing late so his cargo moves in the gap between the true hour and the rung one. Someone had slowed this clock by nearly two minutes. Someone in this house understood clocks well enough to lie with them, and had chosen to.

Her blood moved strangely. Here, on the colonel's own shelf, was proof that falsified time ran through everything he touched — and here was the man himself watching her find it.

"The pendulum's suspension," she said, rising. "There's wear in the crutch pin. I can fit a new one tonight if you have wire."

"You have good hands." He said it the way he might say *the tide is high* — flat, factual, a thing already decided. "I've watched you work. Don't look surprised; I inspect everything in this town now, including my tradesmen. You cleaned the observatory's great wheel without scratching the teeth. Do you know how rare that is?"

"Careful isn't rare, Colonel. It's just unpopular."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Amusement filed down until it couldn't cut anyone. "Sit down, Master Vane."

"There's nothing wrong with my legs."

"Sit down." He poured brandy into two glasses without asking whether she drank. She sat. She took the glass because refusing was information and drinking was also information, and she chose which information to give him rather than letting him take it. The brandy was very good and she hated that she noticed.

He settled across from her, and for a while he talked about nothing — the weather turning, the fever reports from the lower town, the lamentable unreliability of army suppliers. All of it scaffolding. She'd built enough of it herself to recognize the frame underneath, and she waited inside it, patient as a mainspring.

Then he said: "You're not from Bellhollow."

"No, sir."

"But you've taken a local name. Vane." He turned his glass once on its base. "Old Bellhollow name, Vane. There was a family of them up Copper Lane, years ago. Fishers, I think. Or fisher-adjacent." His eyes came up, mild as milk. "Relations of yours?"

Every hair on her arms stood. This was the noose — she'd felt its shadow since the requisition, since the ledger with every family's name and origin had gone into army hands, and now here it was, dropping over her shoulders with a question shaped like small talk. If the ledger held the Vanes of Copper Lane, and if any clerk cross-checked names against origins—

"Distant," she said. "My uncle settled here after the unrest. He died in the spring. I took the name for the contract. It seemed tidier than explaining."

"Tidier." He tasted the word. "Yes. I approve of tidy. The whole country's suffering from an epidemic of untidy people." He set the glass down. "Which brings me to business."

Here it was.

"The ship," Harrow said. "You know about the ship. Everyone knows about the ship; it's all anyone talks about anymore, as though talking could make berths multiply. One berth remains unallocated. Mine to give. I give it according to merit." A pause, measured like a beat in music. "And according to usefulness."

She made herself breathe slowly. "What use does the army have for a clockmaker?"

"Not the army. Me." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the lamplight caught the scar along his jaw she'd only seen before on the death order itself — she'd memorized that document's description of him at fourteen, *distinguishing mark, left jaw, sabre*, reading it by candle stub in an attic, hating him with a purity she had never managed since. Hate was simple then. Everything since had complicated it. "I need time kept truly, Master Vane. The evacuation runs on schedules — tides, trains, troop rotations, the fever wagons. When your observatory clock struck wrong last week, half the garrison lost an hour of drilling and a wagon missed the gate. Sloppy time kills people in a quarantine town. I want a clockmaker whose work I can trust absolutely."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, a name on the berth list." He said it lightly, generously, a benefactor bestowing. "Any name. Yours, if you like — a skilled tradesman earns her passage easily. Or a relation's. Family should stay together in calamity, don't you think? I'm told the Vanes have family still in town."

The room tilted very slightly, the way a floor tilts when you realize how far above it you're standing. He knew. Not everything — not yet, or he'd have said it differently, said it with irons instead of brandy — but he knew there was a thread leading from her borrowed name toward someone on the docks, and he was offering to pull it gently, for a price, rather than cut it.

"One condition," Harrow went on. "Discretion. The observatory keeps odd hours and odder company — old Sollen, and that clerk Callis skulking about after hours. I don't ask what you do up there. But whatever you do, whatever you hear, whatever papers pass through your hands—" he spread his heavy fingers, "—it stays in the housing with the gears. You mend clocks. You don't read documents. You're a craftsman, not a historian."

"And if I see something in the course of my work? Something misfiled, say. An entry altered."

His gaze sharpened by a single degree. "Then you'll bring it to me, and be rewarded for your honesty. Misfiled things are my specialty, Master Vane. I collect them."

Silence. The dead regulator stood open on its shelf between them, its pendulum hanging slack, its shim of lying brass still in her apron pocket where she'd put it. Evidence, sitting an inch from her knee, of exactly the kind of falsification Edda's cipher had described — Harrow slowing time itself wherever truth might catch up to him.

She could take the offer. God help her, part of her was already taking it — she could feel Tomas's hands on the dock ropes, feel the salt wind of the last ship, feel her brother alive on open water while she stayed behind in a burning town, anonymous forever because no one would ever again connect the clockmaker Vane to the hanged rebel's daughter. One signature. One silence. Her brother's life bought with the cheapest coin she owned: her discretion.

That was the noose. Not the threat — the kindness. He hadn't threatened her at all. He'd offered her everything she wanted, wrapped around the thing she must never do, so that accepting the first meant swallowing the second whole, and every day afterward she'd keep his secrets to keep her bargain, and every altered entry, every forged record, every name erased would pass through her silence like cargo through a customs house she personally staffed.

He wasn't buying a clockmaker. He was buying an accomplice, and paying her in her brother.

"Colonel," she said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "I'll need to inspect the movement properly before I promise anything. Clocks are like that. You can't swear to a repair until you've seen the whole train."

"Of course." He rose, satisfied, a man who believed the hook was set. "Take the regulator with you. Fit the pin, bring it back Thursday — we'll finalize arrangements then. And Master Vane?" At the door she paused. He smiled fully now, warm as a stove, and it was worse than any glare. "Give my regards to the astronomer. Tell her the army remembers her father's service fondly. Loyalty runs in families, they say. So does the lack of it."

* * *

She carried the regulator through the dark streets like a sleeping child, feeling its dead weight against her chest, feeling the brass shim in her pocket burn colder than the night air. Sentries passed her without challenge; even the checkpoints had learned her face. Safe passage, everywhere, purchased by a man who thought he'd bought her.

At the observatory, Edda was waiting — she was always waiting now, seated by the meridian instrument with her hands folded, her blind eyes aimed at the door as if sight were a formality. "Well?" she said. "He offered you the world with a lock on it. I could hear it in your boots."

"He offered me Tomas."

Edda was quiet a moment. "And what did you answer?"

"That I'd inspect the movement first." Isandro set the regulator on the workbench and took out the brass shim and laid it where Edda's fingers could find it. "Edda. Look at this. Look at what he does to clocks that tell the truth."

The old woman's fingertips traveled the little wedge of brass, reading it like script. Then she laughed — short, dry, terrible. "Two minutes slow. Of course. And he wants you keeping his hours." She tilted her head. "You haven't said yes."

"No."

"You haven't said no."

"No."

"Good," Edda said. "Hold both doors open as long as you can. Because tomorrow, girl, we may need a way through either one of them — Wren sent word an hour ago. He says the berth list is finished, and he says there's someone in the lower town who saw the ledger before the seizure and can swear to what was changed." Her mouth curved, grim as a blade. "An eyewitness. A real one. We go to her at first light."

Outside, far below, the garrison drum sounded the evening call — and Isandro, counting the beats out of habit, felt her stomach drop.

The drums were late.

Two minutes late.
