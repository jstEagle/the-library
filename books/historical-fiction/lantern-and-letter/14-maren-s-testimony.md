## 14. Maren's Testimony

The fever cart came through Cooper's Lane at six bells, and Maren Ostrow counted the bodies the way she counted everything now — because counting was the last work left to her.

Four. Two children, one old man wrapped in sailcloth, a soldier who had caught it from the people he'd been sent to guard. The soldiers didn't take their own dead to the lime pits with the rest. She noted that too. Everything went into the notebook she kept inside her apron, pressed flat against her stomach like a second heart.

She had been keeping accounts since before the requisition — that was the joke of it, if anything in Bellhollow was still allowed to be funny. Twenty-two years as the harbormaster's bookkeeper, then his widow running the same desk out of her front room, and in all that time no one had ever nailed her ledgers shut or carried them off under armed guard. But the civil ledger of Bellhollow — the ledger with every family's name, trade, and origin entered fair and square by three generations of clerks — that one the army had taken on a Tuesday morning, and the town had learned in a single hour what it was to be made of paper that other people could burn.

Maren had seen the original. That was the thing nobody knew but her, and it had been sitting in her chest for eleven days like a swallowed coal.

She had been at the townhouse the morning the seizure order came. Ostrow was her married name; her first husband's cousin had been deputy registrar, and when the soldiers came Maren had been there settling an account of harbor dues, standing close enough to the reading table to see the open ledger while the colonel's aide ran his finger down the columns. She hadn't been looking for anything. You looked at ledgers the way you breathed. And so she had seen, without meaning to, without understanding, the entries in the margin — small notations in a hand that wasn't the registrar's, corrections and amendments beside certain names. Beside the name Vane.

She hadn't known what the marginalia meant then. She still didn't, entirely. But she knew ink, and she knew hands, and when Wren Callis had come to her door three nights ago, gray-faced and whispering about entries altered in the colonel's own writing, the coal in her chest had flared into something with heat and light and a shape.

Now she stood at her window watching the fever cart turn onto Harbor Road, and she made her decision the way she did all her arithmetic: plainly, once, and without going back over the sum.

* * *

Wren Callis came at dusk, which was foolish of him, because dusk was when the patrols doubled. He came the back way, through the drying yard where her late husband's nets still hung in gray tatters, and he knocked on her kitchen door with the rhythm they'd agreed on — two, pause, three — though Maren suspected he'd have knocked however he pleased if his nerve had failed him, and she'd have let him in anyway, because a boy that frightened needed feeding more than scolding.

He was twenty-six and looked forty. The garrison had done that to him, she supposed, the way the garrison had done things to everyone: slowly, in the name of order. He sat at her kitchen table with his cap wrung in his fists and wouldn't take the tea until she pushed it at him twice.

"You understand," he said, "what you'd be saying yes to."

"I understand you've asked me four times and drunk my tea twice." She sat across from him, folded her hands. "Ask me properly and I'll answer properly."

His throat worked. "Mrs. Ostrow. Maren. If the original ledger contained marginalia — notes, amendments, anything written beside the entries — and if those notes were in Colonel Harrow's hand, and you saw them before the seizure—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I need someone besides me to say it. If it's only me, I'm a disgraced clerk with a grudge and a forged copy, and everything I've kept is worth nothing. If it's you — if you saw it with your own eyes, before any of this—"

"Then I'm a witness," Maren said. "That's the word you're circling. A witness."

"Yes."

"And witnesses in Bellhollow, lately, end up in the lime pits or the cellar under the garrison."

"I won't lie to you. Yes."

She looked at him for a long moment. He bore it better than most men would have, holding her gaze instead of his tea, and she thought: *the garrison hasn't finished ruining this one after all.* There was something underneath the fear in his face, some load-bearing beam that the rot hadn't reached.

"Then here is what I saw," she said. "And I'll say it once, and you'll write it down tonight while your memory's fresh, not mine, because yours is younger. Write exactly what I say and nothing more, because I'll not hang for a word I didn't use."

He pulled out his notebook. His hands were steadier now, she noticed. Fear was settling in him rather than rising, the way a house settles — creaking, then quiet.

"The eleventh day of last month," Maren said. "I was at the townhouse collecting the harbor-dues account from Deputy Registrar Quill. The civil ledger lay open on the reading table, volume three, covering the years thirty-one to thirty-eight. A soldier stood at each door. An officer — thin, spectacles, aide to the colonel, I don't know his name — was reviewing the entries against a list he carried. While he worked, I stood within six feet of the table. I am a bookkeeper, Mr. Callis. Ledgers are my trade. I read them the way you read your own name."

"What did you see?"

"Marks in the margins. Small ones, pencil gone over in brown ink so they'd pass for age. Beside perhaps a dozen names across the two pages I could see. Notations of origin — birthplace, family trade, that column the registrar keeps." She closed her eyes and let the memory surface whole, the way she'd been refusing to look at it directly for eleven days, keeping it face-down like a card. Now she turned it over. "Beside the entry for Vane, Daniel — cooper, born Bellhollow — there was an amendment in a different hand. Tighter than the registrar's. Angled. It struck me only because it was *good*, Mr. Callis. Whoever wrote it wrote like a man who'd been trained to write reports. It amended the birthplace. It struck Bellhollow out — lightly, so the strike could be missed — and set another town in its place. A border town. I don't recall which. I recall thinking it was odd, because everyone knows the Vanes, and the Vanes are Bellhollow to the bone."

Wren's pen had stopped moving. "You're certain it was a different hand."

"Certain enough to swear it. The registrar writes round letters, open as a window. This hand was narrow and slanted, with a hard finish on the downstrokes — a hand that presses. And I'll tell you something else I didn't understand until you came to my door." She leaned forward. "The amendment wasn't correcting an error. I know corrections. A correction makes a record *truer*. This one made it worse for the man named. Changing where a man was born doesn't fix his ledger, Mr. Callis. It fixes his *case*. Someone wanted the record to say something it hadn't said, and they went in quietly, after the fact, in a hand chosen to look old."

The kitchen was very still. Out on Harbor Road a patrol passed, boots on wet cobbles, and neither of them moved until the sound faded.

"There were others," Wren said. "In the pages I copied. The same treatment. Names adjusted — origins shifted, trades changed, dates nudged. Every alteration made the person smaller, or foreigner-er, or more transportable. And every alteration in that same pressing hand. I compared it against requisition orders Harrow signed. Mrs. Ostrow — Maren — it's his. It's his hand beyond any doubt I'll ever have."

"Then we're agreed on what we both saw, separately, before either of us had reason to lie." She said it the way she balanced a column: total stated, ready to be checked. "Write it. Date, time, place, what was visible from where I stood, why I was positioned to see it. All of it. A testimony is only as good as its dull parts."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face. "Quill used to say that."

"Quill was right, and they took his ledger anyway." She poured him more tea. "There's one thing more, and I want you to hear it plain, because I've been turning it over for eleven days and I'll not carry it past tonight. When the soldiers seized the ledger, the aide closed it and carried it out under his arm, loose. Not sealed. Not witnessed. Any clerk in that garrison has had eleven days alone with it. Whatever's in that book now, no court in the empire can say what was in it before — except the people of this town, who've kept their own records, their own letters, their own memories, for three generations. They think taking the book took the truth." She tapped the table once, sharply. "The book was never the truth. The book was only where we kept it."

Wren stared at her. Then he bent his head and wrote, fast, the words tumbling off his pen, and Maren sat in the lamplight and watched him do the thing she had spent eleven days deciding to allow, feeling — she searched for the word and found it, surprising herself — *lighter.*

* * *

He stayed an hour, and by the end of it the fear had gone out of the room entirely, replaced by something more dangerous and much warmer.

It was the notebook that did it. He showed her his duplicate — pages copied fair in a clerk's hand, columns and names, and beside certain entries small penciled flags marking the alterations, cross-referenced to dates of Harrow's signed orders. Maren read it like scripture, which is to say critically, hunting errors, and found only two, both trivial, both corrected on the spot.

"You flagged the Vane entry," she said.

"I flagged everything I could match to the colonel's hand. The Vane one was the first I found. It's the cleanest — the amendment contradicts parish baptismal rolls, which are still in the church, unless they've burned those too." He hesitated. "There's a woman at the observatory. The clockmaker working on the master clock. She's been asking questions about that same entry."

Maren looked up. "Has she."

"Not openly. Through the astronomer — Edda Sollen. Questions about cipher, about old notation. About handwriting." Wren's voice dropped. "I think she's the daughter. The cooper's daughter. Living under her uncle's name — the uncle died last spring, there'd be papers enough to bury a girl inside. And I think she's been decoding whatever her father hid in that ledger's margins, and I think what he hid wasn't a confession, Maren. I think it was evidence. Against Harrow. From before anyone knew there'd need to be any."

The lamp flame stood very straight between them. Maren thought of Daniel Vane, whom she'd known slightly — a big quiet man who'd repaired the cooperage hoops for her husband once and refused payment, saying the harbor fed his trade and he paid his debts that way. Hanged on evidence she had never examined, because in those days examining evidence against a rebel's conviction was a good way to stand next to him.

"And the ship," she said. "The last berth. That's his doing too — Harrow holds the list, and the list decides who lives past this winter."

"Harrow holds the list. And there's a girl at the observatory who I believe means to take it away from him." Wren closed his notebook. "They meet at night. The clockmaker, the astronomer, and me. Tomorrow, if I bring them your testimony — your sworn account of the original entries — it stops being a clerk's forgery and a blind woman's theory. It becomes a record. Three sources, independent, predating the seizure."

"It becomes something a person could be hanged for," Maren said. "Which is why you'll write my name in it. Full name, Maren Ostrow, widow of Joss Ostrow, harbormaster's bookkeeper of this town for twenty-two years. If my testimony is worth taking, it's worth signing."

"They could come for you."

"They could come for any of us. They've been coming for us since the notices went up." She rose and took his cup, and stood a moment with her hand on his shoulder, feeling how thin it was under the uniform wool. "Mr. Callis. I watched this town learn it was made of paper. I watched them nail our names to our doors like shipping labels. I am sixty-one years old and I buried a husband and I will not spend what's left of me being cargo. You tell the clockmaker she has my account, signed and dated, and that if she needs it repeated aloud — to officers, to a crowd, to God and the harbor at large — she has only to send for me."

Wren stood. For a moment he looked as though he might say something grand, and to his credit he didn't; he just said, "Thank you," the way a drowning man says it, meaning the whole weight of it.

At the door he paused. "There's something else. Something I shouldn't tell you yet because I only half understand it myself."

"Tell me anyway. Half-understood things are my favorite kind."

"The clockmaker's rigged the master clock's strike. I saw the mechanism — she showed me. The garrison runs its schedules by that bell, and for days now the bell has been telling them what *she* wants it to tell them." He glanced back into the lamplight, and his face had changed — the clerk's careful blankness giving way to something quicker, brighter, half terror and half exultation. "She's been keeping the whole garrison on her time. And tomorrow night, if the plan holds, Edda Sollen reads the true ledger from the observatory balcony at dawn, to the whole assembled town — and the clock strikes the true hour while she does it, so that no one, not even Harrow, can claim the moment was mistimed or the record misfiled."

Maren stood in her doorway long after he had gone, the cold coming off the harbor, listening.

Far off, the garrison bell rang the evening hour — eight o'clock, or what the garrison believed was eight o'clock. Somewhere up on the hill above the town, in the dark observatory, a girl she had never met was holding the entire occupation of Bellhollow in two small hands, letting it run forward minute by minute on borrowed time.

Maren went inside, banked the fire, and took out her own notebook. At the top of a fresh page she wrote, in her neat bookkeeper's hand: *Dawn. Be awake. Wear good boots.*

Whatever came at sunrise, she intended to be standing in the square for it — on her own feet, under her own name, with her testimony in her pocket and her face turned up toward the clock.
