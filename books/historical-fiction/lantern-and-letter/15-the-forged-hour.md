## 15. The Forged Hour

They came for her at first light, as she'd known they would.

Not soldiers this time — a corporal and a boy with a handcart, and the boy was only there to carry the message, which the corporal delivered standing very straight in the observatory doorway as though the words themselves required a parade: *Colonel Harrow requests the clockmaker's attendance at the garrison. The master clock is to be restored to full service by week's end. The garrison's schedules depend upon it.*

*Depend upon it.* Isandro set down her file and looked at him. "The clock depends on its wheels," she said. "Schedules depend on men who read them properly."

The corporal did not have an answer to that, which suited her fine. She packed her tools — the good ones, the ones Edda had given her, the ones that fit a hand smaller than any apprentice's should be — and followed the cart down through streets gone strange with quarantine. Lime wash on every threshold. Sawdust over the gutter outside the cooper's where they said the fever had started. A town holding its breath, and above it all the garrison drum beating the hours that everyone now obeyed because no one trusted anything else to tell them when it was.

The garrison clock had not been sabotaged the way hers had. That was the first thing she understood when they opened the housing and let her look. Hers had been broken by someone who knew clocks — a deliberate severing of the escapement's pallet, clean as surgery. This one had been broken by neglect: dried oil like amber in the bearings, a mainspring fatigued past argument, dirt packed into the going train so thickly the whole mechanism seemed to be moving underwater. It still struck, because striking trains are stubborn things, but it struck late. Eleven minutes late on the hour she watched it, and eleven minutes is enough to move an army.

Eleven minutes had been moving this army for weeks.

She stood before the open case a long time, and the corporal shifted behind her, and somewhere below in the yard a sergeant was bawling at recruits about musket drill, and Isandro Vane — who had been Tammasin Vane once, before her father swung from the gallows at Cragmoor and his children scattered like sparks off an anvil — understood exactly what she was looking at.

She was looking at power.

* * *

She worked through the morning and let herself think about what she was doing, which was itself a kind of luxury. Since the ledger had been seized, since Wren Callis had come up the observatory stair white-faced with the duplicate clutched inside his coat, since Edda Sollen had laid out the cipher key her father had taught her thirty years ago — since all of that, Isandro had felt like a woman running along the top of a wall with fire behind her. Every act had been reaction. Repair the clock because it must be repaired. Hide the ledger because it must be hidden. Decode the marginalia because the truth was there and would not stop whispering.

But here, alone in the garrison clock room with an oilstone and a jar of the colonel's own best sperm oil, she could act first. She could choose.

The plan assembled itself the way mechanisms do — one wheel engaging another, each part obvious once you saw it.

The garrison ran on this clock. She had heard it said a dozen times since the requisition: mess at seven, muster at eight, the harbor gate opened at six and sealed at dusk, the colonel's own correspondence carried at half past nine sharp. And the final ship — the berth ship, the last way out of Bellhollow before the fever closed the port entirely — sailed on a tide, yes, but the garrison's part of it, the manifest, the escort, the opening of the harbor gate for the chosen passengers, all of it keyed to the clock. To her hands.

So she would restore the clock. Beautifully. Perfectly. Every wheel trued, every bearing oiled, the escapement singing the way a good escapement sings, that dry silver whisper like snow falling on snow.

And then she would rig the strike.

Not the going train — that would run true, honest to God and Greenwich both, because if anyone checked her work against a watch or against the sun, it must bear examination. No, the falseness would live in the striking train alone, in the little snail and gathering pallet that counted out the hours. A clock can show one hour and announce another, and almost no one ever looks. People hear the bell. People obey the bell. The hands are decoration; the strike is law.

She would make the bell lie.

How much, and which way? That took longer. Too much delay and the discrepancy would be noticed — a garrison that musters at eight cannot muster at nine without someone asking why. But a few minutes? Minutes were nothing. Minutes were the grease in the machine of habit. If the bell rang four minutes slow, everything in Bellhollow would run four minutes late, smoothly, invisibly, and no schedule would seem broken because every schedule would break together.

Four minutes. Enough for a man carrying a forged berth list to cross the yard between the clerk's office and the harbor gate after the gate's official closing. Enough for Maren Ostrow to reach the observatory from the fish market after the market bell. Enough, if it came to it — and she believed it would come to it — for three conspirators to do their work in the gap between what the garrison thought was happening and what was.

She began.

* * *

It took her two days, and they were the strangest two days of her life, because she had never before committed sabotage with such tenderness.

She cleaned the going train completely. She drew the mainspring and replaced it — there was a spare in the stores, requisitioned from a jeweler's shop on Cordwainer Row, and she tried not to think about whose shop it had been or whether that family's name was on the berth list. She polished every pivot until it shone like wet slate. By the evening of the second day the going train ran with a steadiness that made her chest ache; it kept time within a hair across the whole afternoon, better than it had kept time in twenty years. Any inspector — and there would be inspectors, Harrow himself had said he would inspect — would find flawless work.

Then she turned to the striking train, and here she slowed herself down the way you slow your breathing before threading a needle.

The snail — the spiral cam that tells the count-wheel how many blows to gather for each hour — was mounted on a collet pinned to the hour arbor. Standard work. She unpinned it, and with a graver she cut a new notch pattern into the snail's steps, shallow, precise, each step altered by the exact fraction that would make the gathering pallet release four minutes' worth of teeth early. Then she made the alteration worse than it needed to be, deliberately, crudely, in one place — a small ugly scar in the metal where a careless repairman might plausibly have slipped. A false trail. Let them blame some journeyman dead ten years. Let them blame the war, the damp, the fever, God. Anyone but the small neat woman with the files.

When it was done she stood back and looked at the whole mechanism, true and false together, honesty and forgery living side by side in the same brass body, and she laughed once — a short, appalled sound that echoed in the empty clock room — because she recognized the design. She had seen it before. In the civil ledger, in the colonel's own hand, in entries where the numbers were beautiful and the truth underneath was rotted through. Colonel Harrow had been running a rigged striking train on the town of Bellhollow for years. She was only returning the compliment.

*That,* she thought, winding the great weight back up into its channel, *is what he has made of us. A town full of people altering their own snails.*

* * *

The bell rang at seven that evening, and it rang four minutes after the true hour, and nobody noticed.

Isandro stood in the yard with her tool bag over her shoulder while the sound rolled out over the roofs — that deep bronze voice she had spent two days tuning, saying *seven, seven, seven* into the purple air — and she watched the garrison answer it. Lamps lit. The mess filled. A sergeant glanced at his watch, frowned faintly, wound it, and put it away. The sergeant assumed his watch was fast. Everyone assumes their watch is fast, when the bell disagrees. The bell has the bigger voice.

She walked home through streets already dimming, and the guilt found her at the corner of Rope Walk, where she'd expected it, waiting like a creditor.

Her father had died rather than falsify. That was the story she had built her whole life around — the rebel who told the truth about the smuggling manifests and was hanged for it, whose testimony was called treason, whose daughter had learned to keep her head down and her hands honest. Honest hands. She had sworn on his grave, the little unmarked one behind the meeting house at Cragmoor, that her hands would never learn what his accusers' hands knew: how to make a record say what you wanted it to say.

And now her hands had done exactly that. Beautifully. With craftsmanship.

She stopped under the rope-walk eaves and let the guilt have its say, all of it, the whole indictment: *you are forging, you are deceiving, you are doing to these soldiers' schedules what Harrow did to your father's trial.* The bell's last echo was dying somewhere over the harbor, and the harbor was dark except for the berth ship at anchor with its riding lights burning like a handful of held candles.

Then the other voice, quieter, the one that sounded like Edda: *A clock that strikes a false hour to free true prisoners serves the hour better than an honest clock that hangs them.*

And under that, lower, her father's voice from the coded marginalia, from the pages she had finally learned to read, the entry that had undone her: *They will call my testimony treason. Let the record show what the record shows.*

Let the record show. Her rig was a record too. Four minutes of manufactured slowness, entered into the world's account every hour of every day, accumulating like interest — and what it purchased was time, and time was the only currency left in Bellhollow that wasn't controlled by Colonel Harrow.

She picked up her bag and walked on, and by the time she reached the observatory stair her guilt had settled into something she recognized and disliked in herself: satisfaction. Not joy. She wasn't sure she'd know joy again until this was finished. But satisfaction, the deep craftsman's pleasure of a mechanism working exactly as designed, even when the design was a sin.

Edda was at her table in the map room, her fingers spread on the star charts she no longer needed eyes to read. She tilted her head when Isandro came in — she always knew her tread, said Isandro walked like someone setting type, small careful strikes.

"Well?" Edda said. "Does it sing?"

"It sings."

"And does it tell the truth?"

Isandro hung her bag on its peg. "The hands tell the truth."

Edda smiled, slow and terrible, the smile of a woman who had spent forty years measuring the heavens and knew precisely how much falsehood a well-made instrument could hold. "Then we are ready," she said. "Because Wren sent word from the garrison this afternoon. He copied the final draft of the berth list." Her fingers moved on the chart, finding a place, holding it. "There are twenty names on it, Isandro. Twenty. And Tomas Vane's name is not among them — I checked twice, against the spelling they've given him at the docks. But there is something else. Wren says the colonel has ordered the list sealed and witnessed tomorrow, and the ship sails when the harbor gate opens."

"Which is when?"

"The garrison schedule," Edda said, "runs on your clock."

Isandro stood very still. Outside, the rigged bell was ringing the half hour — sweet, sonorous, four minutes late — and the sound went rolling down toward the harbor like a coin rolled across a table, coming to rest wherever fate wanted it.

Four minutes. Tomorrow, four minutes might be a man's life.

"I'll need to check the strike train tonight," she heard herself say, in a voice calm as a level bubble. "Make certain it holds."

It held. It would hold. She had built it to hold, the way she had built everything lately — true where it must be seen, false where it could serve — and she climbed the dark stair to the clock room and sat beneath the great pendulum's slow swing and admitted, at last, in the privacy of her own skull, what she had known since the first blow of the rigged bell:

She liked it.

She liked holding the hour in her fist. And the liking frightened her more than Harrow ever had.
