## 16. Wren Taken

The berth list lived in a drawer that locked, and the key to the drawer lived in Colonel Harrow's breast pocket, and the breast pocket lived on the man himself from six in the morning until he retired to his quarters at eleven, at which hour the list was carried by Sergeant Pike to the strongbox in the records room and locked there with the requisition seals and the pay chest and the town's confiscated dignity.

Wren knew all of this because knowing things was the whole of Wren Callis's profession. Seven years as an army clerk had taught him that no document was ever truly secret; it was only waiting. Paper wanted to be read the way water wanted to run downhill. You simply had to be patient, and be present, and be nobody — which was the one talent Wren had never had to practice.

He had been nobody for so long it had begun to feel like a vocation.

But the berth list was different. The berth list was not paper waiting to be read. The berth list was forty-one names written in Harrow's own hand, in order of departure, and Wren had seen enough of the colonel's correspondence to know what an original looked like when it passed through his fingers: the ink slightly blotted where he pressed too hard on the downstrokes, the margins crowded with small annotations that were not annotations at all but prices.

*Ostrow — deferred — consideration pending.*
*Vasse — third position — 200 crowns received.*
*Halloran — struck — no consideration.*

The last one Wren had seen three days ago, and it had sat in him like a swallowed stone ever since. Halloran. The ferryman's widow with four children and a fevered daughter. Struck. No consideration.

And beneath all of them, in the position reserved for the clerk who compiled the fair copies, a line Wren was not supposed to have noticed because he was not supposed to have been looking:

*Callis — reserve.*

Reserve meant nothing. Reserve meant you sailed if someone above you died, which during a quarantine was not the long odds it might have been. But it also meant Harrow had thought of him. Had weighed him. Had decided what Wren's silence was worth and priced it accordingly, somewhere between the widow Halloran and two hundred crowns.

That was the moment, Wren would later admit, when being nobody stopped being survivable and started being complicit.

* * *

The copying took four nights of preparation and one night to do.

The preparation was the easy part, the comfortable part, the part that felt like craft rather than crime. He memorized the rotation of the night sentries — Corporal Deyn walked the records corridor at half past the hour and again at quarter to, his boots audible from thirty feet because Deyn favored his left knee and had done since Vellmark. He learned that Pike took the list to the strongbox at eleven and retrieved it at dawn, and that between those hours the strongbox was guarded by exactly one man, Private Lusk, who slept sitting up with his chin on his chest and could be relied upon to sleep through anything short of cannon fire. Wren knew this because he had watched Lusk sleep through a rat chewing through a sack of barley six feet from his head.

He prepared his tools the way he'd been taught to prepare any ledger task: a stub of pencil — pencil, not ink, because ink took time to dry and pencil could be smudged into illegibility with a thumb if the worst happened — and a strip of thin paper folded eight times until it was no wider than his palm, small enough to go into a boot heel, into a seam, into the body itself if it came to that.

And he prepared his excuse, which was the true clerk's art. If found, he was auditing. Quarantine regulations required a certified count of persons allocated transport; the certification required a fair copy; the fair copy required access. It was even true, in the way most good lies are true at their bones. He had drafted the memorandum himself, backdated it five days, and left it in the out-tray where any officer might have seen it and signed it and none had, which proved either that the lie would hold or that nobody read the out-tray, and Wren had built seven years on the certainty that nobody read the out-tray.

At midnight he went to work.

The records room smelled of tallow and damp stone and the particular sourness of sealed paper kept too long in bad air. The garrison cellar ran beneath it — he could hear the drip of the cistern through the floorboards, a slow irregular tick like a clock with a dying heart — but up here, in the room where the town's civil life had been boxed and shelved after the requisition, there was only the sound of Lusk breathing in the corner chair, deep and even, and the scratch of Wren's pencil.

Forty-one names. He copied them all, top to bottom, including the marginal figures — the sums, the deferrals, the little codes Harrow used for payment received versus payment promised. *R* for received. *P* for promised. *N* for nothing, which was its own kind of eloquence.

His hand did not shake. That surprised him. He had expected fear to arrive like weather; instead it arrived like arithmetic, quietly, in the background, while the foreground filled with the work. This was the thing about conscience that nobody warned you about, Wren thought as the names accumulated under his pencil. It didn't feel noble while you were doing it. It felt clerical. It felt like copying.

He was on the thirty-fourth name when the door opened behind him.

Not Deyn's boots. Not the half-past hour or the quarter-to. A different tread entirely, unhurried, with the slight drag of a man who has dined well, and Wren knew it before he turned — knew it the way you know your own signature — because he had catalogued every officer's walk in the first month of his posting, because knowing things was the profession, and because the profession had finally killed him.

Colonel Harrow stood in the doorway with a candle in one hand and no expression at all.

"Callis," he said.

"Sir." Wren rose. His mind performed the audit-memorandum, offered it forward, laid it on the desk like a shield. "Quarantine certification, sir. Regulations require—"

"I know what the regulations require." Harrow came into the room without hurrying. He set the candle down beside the open strongbox, and the light fell across the berth list, across the pencil strip beside it, across the thirty-four names and the marginal figures, and Wren watched the colonel read his own handwriting reflected back at him in graphite and understood, with a clarity that arrived far too late to be useful, that the audit memorandum had never mattered at all.

Because Harrow hadn't come for the list. Harrow had come for something else, and found Wren instead, and was now deciding — visibly, the way a man decides whether to step over a puddle or through it — what Wren was.

"You copy everything," Harrow said. It wasn't a question. It was almost fond. "Seven years. I've read your fair copies. Flawless. A man who copies flawlessly is a man who notices everything." He picked up the pencil strip between two fingers, delicately, as though it were a specimen. "And a man who notices everything is a man I can't leave loose in a plague town with my name in his pocket."

"Sir, I can explain—"

"No," Harrow said mildly. "You really can't."

* * *

The cellar stairs went down farther than the building stood tall. That was Wren's first coherent observation, hunched with his wrists bound before him, counting steps in the dark because counting was what he did instead of screaming. Nineteen steps. The garrison was one story above ground. Somewhere below the frost line, then, older foundations, maybe the old customs house that had burned before the war, its cellars surviving like bones survive a fire.

They put him in the second room along. There were three rooms; he heard the doors. The first was empty — the guard's lantern threw its light across the threshold and showed bare stone, a ring bolt in the wall, nothing else. The second they gave to him. The third stayed shut, and from behind it, faintly, in the pauses between the cistern's dripping, came a sound Wren needed several minutes to identify because he had never heard it made by anything larger than a cat.

A man crying. Quietly. The way you cry when you've been crying long enough to learn economy.

The guard — a broad young soldier with a lantern and a face like a closed shutter — chained Wren's ankle to the ring bolt and left him a bucket, a blanket that smelled of mildew, and a single tallow candle, unlit.

"Why the candle?" Wren asked. His voice sounded strange to him, flattened by stone. "If you're leaving me in the dark anyway."

The soldier considered this. He seemed to consider it honestly, which was somehow worse.

"Captain's orders," he said. "You're to keep your eyes working. You're to be interviewed." And he left.

Interviewed. Wren sat against the wall and let the word turn over. Interviewed was a word with a future in it. Men who were going to hang weren't interviewed; they were processed. Which meant Harrow believed there was something in Wren worth the trouble of extraction, and Wren lay back on the cold stone and tried to think like a document and failed completely, because documents didn't shake, and he was shaking now, now that the arithmetic of fear had finished its sums.

He thought about the strip of paper. It had gone into Harrow's fingers. Thirty-four names, and among them — he was nearly sure, he had been careful, he had been *so careful* — nothing that named Isandro Vane, nothing that named Edda Sollen, nothing that pointed past the garrison's own walls toward the observatory. The duplicate ledger was not in the records room. The duplicate ledger was in a clock case in a tower on the hill, behind a panel Wren had helped loosen, and Harrow did not know it existed.

That was the rope he had to hold onto in the dark. That was the whole rope.

Except that Harrow was clever, and Harrow had said *a man who notices everything*, and a man who notices everything would eventually ask the question Wren was asking himself now, lying in the dripping dark with the stranger weeping softly through the wall: *what else did he notice? What else did he copy?*

* * *

Time behaved badly in the cellar. The cistern dripped on no schedule Wren could trust, and no meals arrived on any rhythm he could count, and eventually he gave up tracking hours and tracked sounds instead, which was the clerk's fallback: catalogue, index, wait.

The crying behind the third door stopped sometime after his second candle. In its place came knocking — not desperate, not rhythmic, just occasional, three soft raps, then silence, then three more, hours apart. Testing, Wren thought. Or praying. Or a man keeping time with the only clock he had left, which was his own knuckles on stone.

On what he estimated was the second day, footsteps on the stairs, and a tray shoved through the gap beneath the door — bread, a cup of water, and, tucked under the cup's edge with a precision that made Wren's chest tighten, a folded scrap of paper.

He waited until the footsteps receded. Then he unfolded it by candlelight.

It was in pencil, hurried, the letters cramped: *Are you the clerk? Did they take the list? Say nothing of others. Burn this. — a friend of the tower.*

Wren read it twice. Then he did the hardest thing he had done since the records room: he obeyed it. No answer. No scratching back. Because the note was right — anything he wrote could be found, and anything found could be read, and everything read led uphill to the observatory, to the blind astronomer and the clockmaker's apprentice and the truth folded inside a clock case like a letter inside a book.

*A friend of the tower.* He held the words the way you hold a coal. Someone outside was moving. Someone outside had found the cellar, which meant the cellar was findable, which meant — and here Wren allowed himself, for the first time, the dangerous luxury of hope — which meant he was not a file closed and shelved. He was a file still circulating. Still capable of being read by the right hands.

He ate the bread. He drank the water. He burned the note in the candle flame and ground the ash into the mortar with his heel.

* * *

The interview came on the third day, and it was not an interview. It was Harrow, alone, no clerk, no guard within earshot, sitting on the single stool in the corridor outside the cell with his sword across his knees like a farmer mending a tool, and talking.

That was the horror of it. He talked. About supply contracts and harbor fees and the price of silence in a garrison town, conversationally, the way a man talks to a horse he's breaking. He never asked Wren a question. He never mentioned the pencil strip. He simply laid out, in the pleasantest terms, the architecture of Wren's remaining options, and at the center of the architecture, delivered almost casually as he rose to leave, was the sentence Wren would carry up the nineteen steps someday or not at all:

"There's a duplicate of my ledger in circulation, Callis. I don't know where. I suspect you do. When I know where — truly know, not guess — you'll walk out of this cellar. Until then, you'll stay, and stay healthy, because dead men can't confirm details." He paused at the stair. "Your friends have three days. So do you."

Then the lantern-light shrank up the stairs, and the door at the top closed, and Wren sat in the ringing dark with his heart going like a trip-hammer, because *your friends* meant Harrow already suspected them — suspected the tower, suspected the clockmaker, suspected the astronomer — and three days was not time, three days was a fuse.

Through the wall, faintly, three soft knocks. Then silence.

Wren pressed his bound hands to his face and began, very quietly, to do the only thing left to a clerk in a cellar: he counted what remained, and prayed it would be enough.
