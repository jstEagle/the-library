## 17. The Exchange

They came for Wren at dusk on the third day of his absence.

I knew it before Edda said it, because I had learned to read the garrison the way I read a clock train — by the weight of its silences. Bellhollow had a rhythm: boots at the half-hour, the harbor bell at the hour, the fever carts creaking up Rope Walk at whatever hour they pleased. For three days one of those rhythms had been missing. No thin clerk with ink-stained cuffs crossing the square at twilight. No knock at the observatory's back stair, no folded paper slipped beneath the door with a copied line of berth list and a scrawled *not yet* in a hand that pressed too hard when it was afraid.

"He's been taken," Edda said. She sat with her hands flat on the table, her face turned toward the window where the last light was going out of the sea. "I felt it two days ago. The town stopped mentioning him."

"Towns stop mentioning people all the time here."

"Not this fast. Not someone who walked the same route every evening." She turned her blind eyes toward me, and I had the old uncomfortable sense of being seen through rather than seen. "You taught me to read a clock by listening, Isandro. Let me listen."

I had no argument. I had only fear, and fear is not an argument; it is a weight you carry while pretending your hands are steady.

The summons came the next morning, delivered not by a soldier but by a boy of perhaps ten who climbed the observatory hill with the solemnity of a child carrying something breakable. He handed me a card — an actual card, cream stock, engraved — as though Colonel Harrow were inviting me to a recital.

*Colonel A. Harrow requests the clockmaker's attendance at the garrison house, eleven o'clock. A matter concerning the master clock.*

"A matter concerning the master clock," Edda repeated after I read it aloud. My voice must have told her more than the words did. "That is either true or it is a hook dressed as bait."

"It's bait," I said. "But I have to take it anyway. If he wants a clockmaker, refusing tells him the clockmaker has something to hide."

"And if he already knows what we're hiding?"

"Then it doesn't matter what I do." I said it flatly, because flatness was all I had. Inside, something was already running the calculation every fugitive runs: how many minutes of ordinary life remain, and what to do with them. "Watch the housing, Edda. If I'm not back by dark—"

"I know where everything is," she said. "Go. And Isandro." Her hand found my sleeve, brief as a struck bell. "Whatever he offers you, remember that he will have priced it against what he thinks you want. Men like Harrow never offer gifts. They offer invoices."

* * *

The garrison house had been a merchant's mansion before the requisition, and it wore its new owner badly. Portraits had come down, leaving pale rectangles on the wallpaper; a chandelier hung dead over a table buried in dispatch boxes. Two soldiers took my coat and my satchel — my tools, my whole livelihood in leather — and left me standing in a room that smelled of cold ash and lamp oil.

Harrow came in without ceremony. He was smaller than I remembered from the inspection at the observatory, or perhaps memory had inflated him the way terror inflates things. Up close he had the neatness of a man who filed himself daily: buttons, collar, the gray at his temples trimmed to regulation. His eyes went to my hands first. Clockmakers' hands. He had noticed them before, I realized. He had probably noticed them the day we met.

"Miss Vane." My uncle's name, worn smooth by three months of use. "Thank you for coming."

"You wrote that it concerned the clock."

"It does. In a manner of speaking." He gestured to a chair and did not sit until I did, a courtesy that felt like a hand on the back of my neck. "Tell me about Callis."

I had rehearsed ignorance on the walk up the hill. Ignorance, mild professional irritation, the vague knowledge that a clerk sometimes brought papers to be stamped. I deployed it now. "Callis? The army clerk? He came once about a requisition form. I don't know him."

"No," Harrow agreed pleasantly. "You don't. Which is why it interests me that he was seen leaving the observatory path at midnight, twice, in the past fortnight. Once carrying a case heavy enough that he switched arms." He let the silence do its work. Silence is a tool too; I recognized the craft even as it was used against me. "A clerk with weak arms and strong opinions. He was found copying the berth list, Miss Vane. Found, questioned, and lodged in the cellar below this floor, where the damp is very good for teaching men humility."

My heart was doing something arrhythmic, some wild unregulated striking that no escapement would ever permit. I kept my hands in my lap. Hands betray. I had learned that at fourteen, watching my father's hands through the bars of a cart.

"That seems a private matter for the army," I said.

"Everything in Bellhollow is a private matter for the army. That is what requisition means." He rose, crossed to the shuttered window, opened it a hand's width. Harbor light fell across the table — across a ledger lying there, open. Not the duplicate. The original civil ledger, seized from the townhouse, its columns of names and origins marching down the page like a census of the condemned. "Do you know what this book is?"

"The town registry."

"It is a weapon," Harrow said. "Every name in it is a man who can be proved to be somewhere he shouldn't be, descended from someone he'd rather forget, owing something he'd rather not owe. Your uncle's name is in it, incidentally. Vane, J., clockmaker, arrived 1839, origin—" he glanced at me, and smiled without warmth, "—well. Origin."

The name Vane on the page looked small and mortal. My borrowed skin.

"Callis made a copy of certain pages," Harrow continued. "A duplicate. We have his testimony to that effect, and his testimony is, at present, the only thing keeping him out of a fever ship. The fever ships are not particular about cargo condition, if you understand me. A man goes aboard in fair health and arrives nowhere at all." He closed the ledger with one finger. "I want the duplicate."

"Why would I have it?"

"Because Callis is a careful man, and careful men don't keep their treasures where soldiers search. They leave them with people they trust. People with locked rooms on hills, and reputations for discretion." He turned from the window. "Bring me the duplicate within a day, and Callis walks free — released quietly, reassigned, alive. Refuse, and I cannot protect him from the consequences of theft of military documents. You see the shape of it. I am not threatening you. I am describing arithmetic."

Arithmetic. I almost laughed. I lived inside arithmetic — trains of wheels, ratios of teeth, every force accounted for, nothing wasted. But this equation had a variable he didn't know about, and the variable was me.

"And if I bring you the duplicate," I said carefully, "what happens to the original entries? The ones he copied?"

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, contained, the flicker of a man whose mechanism has caught on a tooth. "Entries are entries. Paper records what clerks write. Clerks err."

"Clerks rarely err in the same direction twice."

The temperature of the room changed. It did so subtly, the way a clock's rate changes when a bearing begins to wear — imperceptible to anyone not listening for it. Harrow studied me for a long moment, and I understood that I had just spent something I could not mint again. Suspicion is cumulative interest.

"You are a bold tradeswoman," he said at last. "Boldness is useful. So is caution. I would advise the second." He rang a small bell on the table, and the soldiers returned with my satchel. "One day, Miss Vane. The cellar is directly below us. If you stand very still in the observatory tonight, you might even hear him coughing."

It was meant as cruelty. Instead it gave me a map.

* * *

Edda heard the whole account without interrupting, which was her discipline and her kindness both. When I finished she was quiet for the length of four strikes of the tower bell — I counted; counting is how I pray.

"He has the original," she said. "He showed you the original. That means the original still says what it always said, and he knows it, and he needs the duplicate gone because a duplicate proves the alterations were deliberate. One document can be lost. Two documents are testimony." Her fingers tapped the table once. "He isn't buying back stolen property, Isandro. He's buying the death of a witness."

"Wren is the witness."

"We are all the witness. Wren is just the part they've caught." She stood, moving to the clock housing with the sure geography of the blind, laying her palm against the case wood as if taking its pulse. "So we must decide. What is the truth worth? A man's life? Because that is the price on the table. Truth for Wren. Or truth withheld and Wren dies, and the truth survives, and we live with the arithmetic of that."

"I could give him a copy of the copy," I said. "He wouldn't know."

"He'd know within a day. He'll check the paper against the original — the watermarks, the ink age. A forged duplicate bought yesterday is a confession with a date on it." She shook her head slowly. "No. If we give him anything false, it must be flawless, and flawless takes time we may not have, and if it fails, we lose Wren *and* our credibility *and* possibly the observatory. Think, girl. What does he actually get if we surrender a genuine duplicate?"

"Silence. Safety. The alterations die with the copies."

"And what does he lose?" She turned toward me, and her voice dropped into the register she used for students she believed could bear the answer. "He loses certainty. He will never know whether another copy exists. Whether Callis memorized pages. Whether the clockmaker who stood so boldly in his parlor has already sent word beyond the walls. A man who buys back one copy purchases doubt along with it, and doubt is the one thing a falsifier cannot survive. He signed your father's death order on the strength of certainty, Isandro. Certainty is his load-bearing wall."

I thought of the scrap of paper hidden in the clock's housing, my father's cramped cipher marching down its edge. *The treason was testimony.* Eight days ago I had finished decoding the last line, and I still could not say it aloud without my throat closing. He hadn't been smuggling. He had been *documenting* — recording, entry by entry, the colonel's falsified manifests, the ships that left Bellhollow heavier than they entered, the names struck and rewritten in a colonel's hand. My father had died holding a ledger in his head, and now a clerk I barely knew lay in a damp cellar holding its echo.

"What's the truth worth?" Edda asked again, softer.

I went to the window. Below, the town lay under quarantine fog, chimneys smoking thinly, the harbor holding the evacuation ship at anchor like a held breath. Somewhere down there Maren Ostrow was boiling linen and keeping her children away from the gates. Somewhere down there Tomas was hauling rope under a name that wasn't ours, begging strangers to help a family he didn't know was his own. Somewhere below this floor, Wren Callis was coughing.

"If we give him the real duplicate," I said, "we save Wren and we bury Papa a second time. This time deeper."

"Yes."

"If we refuse, we keep the truth pure and clean, and a man drowns in a cellar because of it. Pure and clean." I heard the bitterness arrive in my own voice and let it stay. "Edda, I spent ten years learning that purity is a luxury for people with nothing to trade."

"And I spent forty years learning that traded truth is truth no longer." She came to stand beside me, her shoulder near mine, not touching. "So we are both right, which means we are both wrong, which means there is a third door."

"Which is?"

She smiled — the rare one, the one that made her look like the woman who had once taught a rebel to read the stars. "You are a clockmaker who has already rigged one strike. You told me the garrison's schedules run on your hands now. Then ask yourself what Harrow is really buying. He thinks he is buying a document. But a document is only dangerous because it can be *read*. Because it can be *compared*. Because it exists in quantity, in minds, in margins, in the memory of a woman on a balcony." Her voice gathered speed, the way mine did when a train of wheels finally engaged. "Give him a duplicate, yes. Give him the truest duplicate you can make. And make certain that between tonight and dawn, the truth acquires more bodies than paper — that it lives in Maren's mouth and the sailors' ears and the crowd at the harbor gate — until one surrendered copy is one candle blown out in a room already full of light."

"And if the light doesn't spread in time? If dawn comes first?"

"Then we choose again," Edda said simply. "We are not choosing once, Isandro. We are choosing every hour until the hour itself chooses us."

I stood a long time at the window. Below us, faint through the fog, I could hear the garrison changing the watch — and underneath it, thinner, irregular, a sound I might have invented: a man coughing in a stone room, keeping imperfect time, waiting to learn what his life had been priced at.

"One day," I said. "He gave me one day."

"Then you had better find out," Edda said, "how fast a forgery can be made flawless — and how fast a truth can be taught to fly."

I turned from the window and reached for my tools.
