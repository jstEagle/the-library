## 18. What She Chooses

The forgery took four hours and cost her three drops of blood.

Not from any wound — she pricked her finger deliberately, twice, to age the ink with something organic, the way Edda had taught her. *Old paper remembers,* the astronomer had said, holding a page of the true ledger between fingers that could not see it but read it better than any clerk alive. *It yellows where hands have touched it. It stains where fear has sweated on it. You cannot fake a life in an hour, Isandro. But you can fake a death.*

So Isandro built a death.

She began with paper. Wren had described the ledger stock precisely before his arrest — rag-laid, watermarked with a thistle no one at the garrison had ever bothered to notice — and Maren Ostrow, of all people, produced three sheets of it from beneath the lining of a hatbox her mother had carried out of Kettering twenty years ago. "The townhouse kept scraps," Maren said, pressing them into Isandro's hands with a look that dared her to ask why. "Everyone keeps scraps. That's what Harrow never understood about us."

The ink was harder. The garrison used iron-gall mixed with logwood, Wren had said, and after six months the entries went brown at the edges like leaves. Isandro boiled oak gall and copperas in the observatory's back room while Edda sat by the window listening to the town — to the coughing that was spreading now street by street, to the soldiers' boots, to the gulls that had stopped coming near the harbor because the plague ships anchored there scared even the birds.

"You're shaking," Edda said.

"I'm not."

"Your kettle is."

Isandro looked down. The handle chattered against the pot's rim, a small metallic seizure. She set it aside and pressed her palms flat against the table until they stilled.

*You can fake a death.* But whose? That was the question she'd turned all night, the way she turned a stubborn gear, looking for the flaw. Harrow expected a duplicate ledger — Wren's duplicate, copied in a clerk's hand over months of secret work. If she gave him something too perfect, he'd smell the haste. If she gave him something too rough, he'd suspect the trap.

The answer, when it came, came from the clock itself. A mechanism doesn't hide its age; it wears it honestly. Every repair leaves a scar under the polish if you know where to look — and Harrow did not know where to look. He was a man who read ledgers the way he read faces: frontward only, top line first, trusting the hand that held the pen because he believed all pens answered to rank.

So she would copy the truth — the real entries, the smuggled grain, the falsified tonnage, the names of the harbormasters bought and the witnesses erased — but she would copy them *wrong*. A clerk's honest error here, a smudged figure there. She would transcribe her father's decoded marginalia into the body of the text as though Wren had found it secondhand, garbled, half-understood. Enough truth to be worth trading for. Never enough to be proof.

Because that was the other half of it, the half she said aloud to Edda at three in the morning while the candle gutted: "He has to believe it's real. And then he has to destroy it."

Edda turned her blind face toward the sound of her voice. "And once it's destroyed?"

"Then there is only one copy left." Isandro fed the fire another stick. "The one I'm not going to give him."

* * *

Harrow received her in the garrison's map room at noon, which told her everything about how much he feared contagion and how little he feared her. Two orderlies moved a table of charts aside; a third stood by the door with his hand resting on nothing in particular, near enough to his sidearm to make the point without drawing it.

The colonel looked worse than he had in the autumn. The quarantine sat on him like a coat two sizes too small. His collar was immaculate; his eyes were not.

"The clerk," he said, without greeting.

"Alive when I left the cellar," Isandro said. "Feverish. Your cellar floods at high tide, Colonel — you may want to mention that to whoever keeps your buildings. It's bad practice, drowning prisoners before you've decided whether you need them."

Something moved behind his eyes — irritation, or perhaps respect, which was worse. "You have a merchant's tongue for a clockmaker."

"I have a tradesman's tongue. We learn it from customers who don't pay." She set the wrapped bundle on the chart table between them and did not push it closer. "Before you open it. The terms."

"Terms." He almost smiled. "You're in no position to—"

"With respect, sir, neither are you." Her voice stayed level. Inside, her heart was going like an escapement running hot, each beat catching on the next. "You need this ledger gone more than I need anything. A duplicate, loose, during a quarantine, with your name in the margin of every falsified entry — do you know what your own officers would do with that? Not the town. Your *officers*. The ones who'll write the report if you die of fever next week and someone goes through your papers." She let that sit. "Wren Callis walks out of your cellar tonight. Unharmed. With papers saying his transfer south was approved — you're a man who approves things, it should take you a minute. And the requisition order on the observatory is rescinded. In writing."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I leave, and this bundle stays with me, and we both spend the rest of the siege wondering which of us sleeps worse." She held his gaze. It was the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than the forge-work of the forged pages, because her father had looked at this man across a similar table and lost everything, and some animal part of her expected the floor to open, the guards to move, history to repeat itself with herself inside it.

But Harrow was not facing a rebel now. He was facing a supplier. Men like him always understood suppliers.

"Open it," he said.

She unwrapped the oilcloth. The false ledger lay there, brown-edged, thumb-worn, smelling faintly of the smoke she'd cured it over — three days in a box above the observatory chimney, learning to smell like a thing that had been hidden a long time in a warm place. Harrow turned the pages slowly. She watched him read his own crimes in a stranger's handwriting and saw the moment the fear arrived: a small contraction around the mouth, quickly mastered.

*Good,* she thought. *Believe it. Believe it's the only one.*

"This Callis," he said. "He kept this from his own desk. For months."

"He's a thorough man. It's why you employed him."

Harrow closed the ledger. For a long moment he studied her, and she felt the examination go deeper than it had in the observatory that autumn day, felt him trying to place her face against some older memory. She gave him nothing. A clockmaker's face. Hands stained with ink and brass polish. Nothing else.

"The transfer papers will be drawn up within the hour," he said at last. "The clerk will be released to the harbor gate at dusk. And Vane—" he consulted a card on the table, her dead uncle's name, her borrowed skin, "—you will take no further work in this town. When the last ship sails, whatever happens to Bellhollow afterward, the observatory closes with it. I'd advise you to be on that ship."

"I hadn't planned on staying," Isandro said, which was true, and not.

* * *

They freed Wren at dusk, exactly as promised, because Harrow kept his word the way a debt collector does — punctually, and expecting interest.

Isandro waited at the harbor gate with Maren, watching the soldier escort bring him up from the cellars. He walked badly. Six days on wet stone did that. But his eyes found hers through the failing light and something in them was steady, and he managed, as they passed the checkpoint, a clerk's dry murmur: "Your forgery held. They made me sign a confession saying I copied it. I signed. I have decided," he added, with the ghost of his usual precision, "that God will understand the distinction between copying a lie and telling one."

"Walk," Isandro said. "Confess later."

Maren put his arm over her shoulder and half-carried him up Rope Lane toward the observatory, and the three of them must have made a strange procession — a laundress, a limping clerk, a woman in a dead man's coat — but nobody stopped them. The town was past stopping anyone. The fever had reached the fishermen's rows; you could tell by which doors had chalk marks, and by the silence of the streets where chalk marks clustered.

At the observatory, Edda fed Wren broth and brandy in that order, and Isandro laid out what remained: the true ledger, hidden in the clock's weight channel where no search had thought to look inside the machine itself; the decoded marginalia, copied fair in her own hand; Maren's testimony, witnessed and written; and the schedule, which Wren, still shivering, delivered like a man reading a sentence of his own sentencing.

"The *Meridian*," he said. "Provisioned tonight, tide at dawn. One berth remaining on the civilian list. Harrow has held it open — deliberately, I think. Bidding. There are three families who've offered him everything they have."

"Then it's sold," Maren said flatly.

"No." Wren shook his head, and winced at the motion. "That's the thing. As of this evening it isn't sold. I heard the adjutant. Harrow's keeping it empty. He told the harbormaster the berth is reserved for 'a person of service to the garrison.'" His eyes came up to Isandro's. "He means you. Clockmakers who deliver ledgers are persons of service. He wants you off this rock, Vane. He wants the woman who knows what his handwriting looks like on a boat and out of his report. Your name is already drafted onto the list. All you have to do is not cross it out."

The room went quiet. The fire ticked. Somewhere below, the master clock ran through its train, steady as breath — her clock, rigged to strike false hours for a garrison that trusted it, and tomorrow, if she chose, to strike true.

One berth. One name.

Tomas was a dockhand on the quay lists under the name Aldous Rennick. Tomas had begged a stranger — her — to help his family get aboard. Tomas did not know his sister stood in a room above the harbor holding the single seat out of Bellhollow in her ink-stained hands.

And Harrow knew her face now. Not her name — not yet — but her face, her voice, her trade. Every hour she stayed, the thread between the rebel Vane and the clockmaker Vane grew thinner. If he ever pulled it —

If she boarded, Tomas stayed. If Tomas boarded, she stayed — in a quarantined town, under a colonel who had hanged her father and would sleep no worse for hanging his daughter.

"There's ink on the list," Isandro heard herself say. "A list is just a document. Documents can be amended before the harbormaster seals them." She looked at Wren. "When is the final manifest drawn?"

"Dawn. An hour before tide. The harbormaster signs it at the gate."

"Then at dawn," Isandro said, "I'll be standing at that gate with a pen in my hand."

Maren looked at her for a long moment — the laundress who kept scraps, who understood what everyone carries and what it costs — and then, without a word, she took Isandro's empty teacup away and washed it, which in Maren's language was a blessing and a farewell.

Edda alone spoke it plainly. Blind eyes toward the fire, she said: "You understand what you're giving him. Not just the berth. Your father's name dies with you on this shore unless the clock speaks it."

"The clock will speak," Isandro said.

She went upstairs to the great machine and put her ear against the case, listening to the escapement count out the hours she had left. Fourteen. She had fourteen hours to unrig the strike she had spent a night rigging, to teach the clock to tell the truth again, and to decide — finally, irrevocably — whose name would stand on the manifest when the harbormaster's seal came down.

Her hand was steady now. It had stopped shaking somewhere in the map room, in the moment Harrow had taken the bait, and it had not shaken since.

Resolve, she thought, was like a mainspring. You wound it tight, and tight, and tighter — and the whole time, you were storing up one enormous release.

Dawn was coming. The tide was coming. And below her, asleep in a loft over the ropewalk, her brother was going to wake up on a ship.
