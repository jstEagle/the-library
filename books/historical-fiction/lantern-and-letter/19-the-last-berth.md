## 19. The Last Berth

The harbor at four in the morning was a held breath.

Isandro came down through the fish-stink streets with her tool roll slung across her back and the forged duplicate wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and the fog came off the water in gray sheets, and the lanterns of the *Meridian's Grace* burned small and yellow through it like coins dropped into milk. The ship sat low in the water already, ballasted with the weight of leaving. Ropes groaned. Somewhere on her decks a man coughed, and the cough carried across the whole quay, and no one spoke above it.

The berth list was nailed to the customs post at the foot of the gangway — a single sheet under glass now, because the colonel did not trust even paper to the town. A soldier stood beside it, young, his bayonet fixed more out of habit than intent. Behind him a clerk's table had been set up with an inkpot and a ledger open to the final page, where the last true berth waited like an empty chair at a table.

Wren had told her how it would go. *He'll want you to write it himself,* Wren had said, two nights ago, through the cellar grate after Isandro had walked him out past the guards with Harrow's own signature forged on the release order. *He'll want witnesses. He wants everything witnessed. A man who falsifies records for a living knows exactly what a record is worth.*

Harrow was there. Of course he was. He stood at the head of the quay in his greatcoat with the collar up against the fog, hands clasped behind him, watching the loading with the satisfaction of a man watching a well-run kitchen. Two officers flanked him at a distance. He had not looked at her yet. He was letting the moment arrive on its own schedule; she understood that about him by now. Everything the colonel did arrived on schedule.

She stopped three paces from the table.

"Clockmaker." He turned then, and his face in the lantern light was pleasant, almost warm, the face of a man greeting a tradesman whose bill he intends to pay promptly. "You're early. I admire that. Punctuality is the whole of virtue, in my experience. Everything else is commentary."

"You said dawn."

"The ship says dawn. I merely agree with it." He gestured at the list, at the empty line at its bottom, ruled and waiting. "One name. Yours, as arranged. Write it, and the truth of your clever little forgery stays between us, and the *Grace* takes you out with the tide, and Bellhollow can go on being somebody else's problem."

Behind him, down the quay, the loading went on in silence. Families moving in single file with their bundles tied in bedsheets. An old woman carrying a birdcage with a cloth over it. A boy of maybe six holding his mother's skirt with one hand and a carved horse with the other, gripping both as though the fog might take them separately.

Tomas was not among them yet. Dockhands boarded last; that was the order, and orders were the one thing in this town that still ran true.

Isandro set her hand flat on the table. The wood was cold and wet at the edges. "Before I write," she said, "I want to see the list read aloud. All of it. That was the other half of our arrangement."

"It was not."

"It was implied. You imply a great deal yourself, Colonel. You'd know the difference."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger; she had learned he was too disciplined for anger in public — something more like appraisal, a merchant reassessing a price. He glanced at the officer on his left, then at the crowd below the gangway, forty faces turned toward the customs post in the fog, waiting without hope, because hope had been requisitioned along with everything else.

"Very well," he said. "Sergeant. Read the manifest."

The sergeant took the sheet and read the names out into the fog, unhurried, each one a door opening or staying shut. Isandro listened the way she listened to a clock — not to the sound but to the intervals between sounds, to what was missing. She heard merchant names, garrison wives, a surgeon, the harbormaster's widow. She heard the pauses where bribes had been converted into berths. And then the sergeant reached the end, and folded the sheet, and said, "One berth remaining," and the crowd made no sound at all, which was worse than any sound.

"Your turn," Harrow said.

She picked up the pen. The nib was dry-ish; she touched it to the ink and wiped the excess against the pot's lip, the way Edda had taught her father to prep a pen thirty years ago in this same town, though Edda didn't know yet that she knew that, though nobody knew anything yet, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a weight in a weight-driven train, all of it waiting on the escapement.

She wrote four letters. Not five.

*T-O-M-A-S.*

She wrote the surname after it — Ostrow. Maren's surname. Maren had stood in her workshop two nights ago with rain in her hair and said, *Put him under my name. I've got daughters grown and gone and a dead husband and nobody in the garrison bothers counting what a widow's kin do.* It would buy him hours, perhaps. Perhaps only minutes. But Tomas Vane could not be found on any list, because Vane was a name that ended in rope, and the boy had spent six years learning to be nobody so thoroughly that he'd almost succeeded.

Harrow read the name upside down. She watched him do it — watched his eyes track the letters, watched them stop.

"That isn't your name."

"No."

His gaze came up slowly. And here was the thing she had prepared for and still wasn't ready for: not rage, not the shout of a man cheated, but the terrible quiet of a bookkeeper finding an error in a column he had personally balanced. He looked at her the way he must have looked at her father's ledgers eleven years ago, seeing in the margins what he'd pretended not to see.

"Ostrow," he said. "The clockmaker's apprentice registered under Verrin, uncle to the late Josiah Verrin, clockmaker, deceased." He recited it from memory, and she understood that he had pulled her file that first week, that he had always known her name was borrowed, and had let her work because a skilled clockmaker was worth more loose than questioned. "But you don't write like a Verrin. You write like—" He stopped.

Say it, she thought. Say the name. Eleven years I've paid rent on my father's crime and my mother's silence and my own cowardice, and you will say it for me, you will say it out loud on this quay in front of the fog and the soldiers and the boy with the carved horse.

"Vane," Harrow said.

The name went out over the water and did not come back.

An officer shifted. The sergeant's hand moved toward the sheet. The young sentry at the gangway looked from his colonel to the woman at the table and back, sensing the shape of something without knowing what it was.

"Hanged at Faltermere, autumn of '36," Harrow said softly, tasting each word. "Rebellion, sedition, falsification of garrison records. There was a daughter. The records say the daughter died of fever the following spring." His mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Records are so often wrong. Isn't that what you've been trying to teach me, these past weeks? With your clocks and your clever hands?"

"You taught me," Isandro said. Her voice came out level, and she was proud of that, would be proud of it for the rest of her life however long it lasted. "You taught me what a falsified record looks like. I grew up reading your handwriting, Colonel. In the margins of my father's trial transcript. Every alteration. Every date moved. Every name struck. You signed his death with the same pen you used to sign the smuggling manifests, and you were careless enough to press hard enough to mark the page beneath. My father saw it. That was his treason — he *saw it*, and wrote down what he saw, and you killed him for having good eyes."

The fog moved. Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy clanked twice, patient, indifferent.

"You have proof," Harrow said. It was not a question. It was a man taking inventory.

"I have a ledger."

"Then you have nothing, because if you produce it, you hang beside a memory, and the ship sails without either of you, and I keep my berth, and Bellhollow keeps its silence, because towns always do." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice to something almost intimate, almost kind. "Write your own name, girl. Cross out the dockhand's. I'll pretend I never learned yours. That is the last generous offer I will ever make anyone."

Down the quay, near the warehouse wall, a figure detached itself from shadow — broad-shouldered, cap low, hands scarred from hawser rope. Tomas. Boarded early after all, or come to watch, or come because Maren had gone to him in the night the way she'd promised. He was looking at the customs post. He was looking at her. Six years, and he didn't know her, and he was looking at her anyway, the way you look at a stranger when something in your blood insists otherwise.

He couldn't board until the name was countersigned. She had perhaps a minute.

"No," Isandro said.

Harrow exhaled through his nose. "Sergeant—"

"Before your sergeant does anything permanent," she said, "you should know that the original of my father's testimony — his handwriting, his cipher, pages of it — is in the keeping of a woman who has instructions to carry it aboard the *Grace* if I do not walk away from this table. Edda Sollen. Your astronomer. Blind as a stone, they say, and they're right, and it makes no difference whatsoever, because she has had eleven years to memorize every line of it, and she reads aloud beautifully. Ask yourself, Colonel: what happens to a man who controls the last berth, when the crowd on this quay hears the corrected ledger in his own voice? You taught this town to count. We counted."

It was half bluff and wholly true, and Harrow, who had built a career on knowing which documents existed, could not tell the difference, and that was the whole architecture of her survival: she had learned deception from a master of it.

The sergeant had stopped moving. Harrow stood very still, and the fog dripped off the customs post roof between them, tick, tick, onto the wood, and Isandro thought: *I could regulate you. I could set your beat.*

"Countersign the name," Harrow said finally.

"Swear the berth first. Out loud."

For a moment she thought he would refuse, thought the bayonets would settle it the old way. Then he looked past her, down the quay, at the forty silent faces, at the birdcage and the carved horse and the bundled bedsheets, and whatever arithmetic he performed came out against him.

"The berth is granted," he said. "To the dockhand. Tomas Ostrow." Each word laid down like a coin he resented parting with.

"Tomas Vane," Isandro said quietly, just for him. "When it's safe to be. Tell him his sister kept the clock running."

Harrow's face did not change. But his eyes did, one flicker, the escapement slipping a tooth — and she knew that somewhere behind that stillness a new column had opened in his private ledger, and her name was at the top of it, and the balance outstanding was death. Let it stand. Debts should at least be honest.

She countersigned. Her hand did not shake. She blotted the page, capped the ink, and stepped back from the table, and the clerk called the name, and the gangway boards began to take a dockhand's weight.

Tomas passed within arm's reach of her. He slowed — one heartbeat, two — and looked at her under the brim of his cap, and she looked back, and neither of them said anything, because saying it here would kill them both, and because some recognitions are complete without words. His eyes were their mother's eyes. Then he climbed, and the sailors took his bundle, and the rail of the *Meridian's Grace* had a Vane leaning on it, alive, bound for a country where his name cost nothing.

The mooring lines came off. The fog swallowed the ship a fathom at a time, lantern by lantern, until only the bell buoy marked where she'd been.

Harrow was watching her when she turned around. "You understand," he said, conversational, terrible, "that I cannot permit you to leave Bellhollow now. Or speak. Or exist, ideally."

"I know."

"And yet here you stand, on my quay, with no ship and no protection and a document I intend to find before noon." He tilted his head. "What exactly is it you imagine happens next?"

Isandro looked past him, up the hill, to where the observatory dome caught the first gray light, and beneath it the tower, and beneath the tower the master clock, rigged, wound, waiting, its strike chained to her hand until she chose to let it fall.

"Now?" she said. "Now we find out what time it really is."

She turned and walked toward the hill, and behind her she heard Harrow say, very quietly, to his sergeant: "Follow her."
