## 20. The True Hour

The clock said four minutes to six, and it was lying.

Isandro stood in the dark of the tower with her hand on the escapement, feeling the lie tick through the brass and into her palm like a pulse she'd taught a body to fake. For eleven days the master clock of Bellhollow had struck what Harrow wanted it to strike — curfew at nine by the garrison's reckoning, muster at five, the harbor gates opening on hours that existed nowhere but in the colonel's orders and the wheels she had bent to serve them. She had told herself it was strategy. She had told herself it was a lever, and that levers were not guilty of what they moved.

Now, with the sky going grey over the harbor and the ship's masts visible as black stitches against it, the lever wanted to tell the truth.

Below, through the louvred openings, Bellhollow was gathering. She could hear it — not the usual dawn sounds of carts and gulls and Maren Ostrow swearing at her mooring lines, but something heavier, slower: boots on cobbles, dozens of pairs, then hundreds, converging on the square beneath the observatory hill without being ordered there. Word had gone out in the night the way word always went out in Bellhollow, by kitchen door and dockside murmur, and now the town had come to see whether the story Wren Callis had smuggled out of his own imprisonment was true — whether the girl who kept their clock had kept their names as well.

Edda Sollen stood at the balcony rail above the square, the duplicate ledger bound in sacking under her arm like a loaf she meant to share. The astronomer could not see the crowd. She did not need to. "They're quiet," she said when Isandro came down the stair. "Quiet crowds are either obedient or decided. This one doesn't sound obedient."

"It isn't." Isandro's voice came out steadier than her hands. "Wren is free. He's down there somewhere — Maren found him a coat that fits."

"And Tomas?"

"On the list. In ink. Harrow watched me write it." She had not yet said the rest aloud, not even to Edda, because saying it would make it a thing that had happened rather than a thing she was still outrunning. But Edda turned her blind face toward her with the terrible patience of a woman who had spent forty years listening to what people refused to say.

"He knows," Edda said. "Harrow knows whose daughter you are."

"He knows now." Isandro swallowed. "I bought the name with the truth of my name. It seemed — fair, at the time. Everything else I've traded has been worth less than that."

Edda reached out and took her wrist, the grip precise despite the clouded eyes. "Child. Listen to me, because I am old and I have earned the right to say one blunt thing before whatever comes next comes. Your father wrote those marginalia knowing they might be the only part of him left standing. He did not write them so you could spend your life being careful with them." She pressed the ledger into Isandro's arms. "Go up. Give the town its hour. I will give them the words."

* * *

The mechanism waited for her like an argument she'd already won.

She climbed the last flight into the clock room, where the great dial's back glowed faintly with the coming light, and put her hands into the works she knew better than her own heartbeat. The rigging was elegant — she would grant herself that much. A disengaging pawl, a weighted auxiliary train, a strike governor she had tuned to run two minutes and forty seconds slow for eleven days, so that every bell in Bellhollow had been lying gently, consistently, in service of the colonel's schedule. To release it she needed only to lift the pawl clear and let the governor fall back to its true rate, and the clock would catch up to honesty the way a river catches up to the sea: all at once, and loudly.

But first there was the matter of the six o'clock strike itself, and this was the part she had built alone at night and told no one, not even Edda, because it could not be unmade once known.

Inside the striking train, behind the count wheel, she had fitted a secondary cam — a small brass heart of her own cutting — wired to the observatory's signal line, the same wire that once rang the hour flag for the harbor pilots. When the clock struck six, truly struck it, the cam would close the circuit and the flag would rise. And pinned beneath the flag hoist, folded small enough to hide in a fist, was the last piece: a single sheet in Wren Callis's clerk's hand, copied fair from the duplicate ledger, listing every entry Colonel Harrow had altered — dates, tonnages, names, and beside each one, in the margin, the cipher translation of what her father had written there twenty years ago. Smuggled grain sold as seized contraband. Convict transports rerouted for private freight. And at the top of the page, the death order, dated, signed, and contradicted by its own garrison records in three separate hands.

Edda would read it aloud. But a voice carries only so far over a frightened crowd, and paper carries only as far as a thrown stone. The clock would carry farther. Every soul in Bellhollow had set their lives by that dial for a hundred years — market bells, funeral bells, the bells that told the fishing fleet when the bar was safe. When it spoke this morning, they would look up. All of them. At once.

That was the whole design. Not cleverness. Just attention, aimed.

Isandro checked the escapement oil, checked the governor seating, checked the pawl spring one last time with fingers that were no longer shaking, which surprised her. Fear, she discovered, had a floor. Somewhere between forging a ledger and surrendering her own berth she had passed entirely through fright and come out the other side into a flat, clear country where there was nothing left to protect except the doing.

Four minutes to six. Two, by the clock's lying face; less, by the truth.

She thought of her father for exactly as long as it took to draw one breath — his hands, the smell of solder, the way he'd whistled through his teeth when he worked, off-key, always off-key — and then she stopped thinking about him and started working, because he had never once in his life confused grief with idleness.

She lifted the pawl.

The click traveled up through the frame like a bone setting. Above her, unseen, the minute hand began to eat the distance between the false hour and the true one, faster now, the whole train running hot and honest, and Isandro stood inside the sound of it with her ear against the great wheel and counted her father's testimony home.

* * *

In the square, the crowd heard it first as hesitation.

The bell had been wrong for eleven days and Bellhollow knew it the way a body knows a limping horse — nobody said it aloud, everybody adjusted around it. Curfew bells that rang too early, muster bells that rang too late, the whole town living inside the colonel's stolen minutes. So when the first stroke came at 6:04 by every private watch and sun-bleached instinct in the square, people looked up, and kept looking up, as the master clock struck six strokes for six o'clock at four minutes past — catching up, correcting, refusing to let the lie stand even one hour longer.

Then it went further. It struck seven.

Maren Ostrow, standing at the front of the crowd with Wren Callis pale and coat-swallowed at her shoulder, understood before almost anyone: the girl had rigged it. The clock wasn't late. It was speaking. Seven strokes for a six o'clock hour was not error; it was emphasis; it was a bell clearing its throat.

By the ninth stroke the entire square was looking at the observatory.

And the flag rose.

Not the pilot's hour flag. Something larger, hastily sewn — Maren recognized sailcloth from her own lofts, and felt an absurd, fierce pride about it later — and sewn to it, legible from the harbor steps, a sheet of clerk's script in a hand so fair it could only be Wren's. Too far to read the whole. Near enough to read the heading, which Wren had written in letters a foot high:

THE TRUE LEDGER — READ ALOUD AT THE BALCONY

And below it, smaller, the line Maren would recite for the rest of her life to anyone who asked where she'd been standing:

CORRECTED BY THE HANDS OF T. VANE, CLOCKMAKER.

Vane. Not the dead uncle's name. Not any uncle at all.

Maren grabbed Wren's sleeve. "That's her name. That's — she signed it. She signed her own—"

"I know," Wren said. His voice cracked on it. Eleven days ago he had copied a ledger because it was wrong to do anything else, and he had assumed, in his modest way, that conscience was a private expense. Now it was flying over the harbor on stolen sailcloth. "Look at the balcony."

* * *

Edda Sollen came out onto the balcony leaning on Isandro's arm, and the crowd's noise fell away the way noise does before weather.

She could not see them. She stood at the rail anyway, an old woman in a man's coat with a ledger open on the stone before her, and when she spoke her voice carried the way trained voices carry, up and out, unhurried.

"My name is Edda Sollen. I have kept this observatory for thirty-one years, and in all that time I have told you the truth about the stars, because the stars permit no forgery. This morning I mean to tell you the truth about a book."

She untied the sacking.

"In the year of the rebellion, a man named Aldous Vane was tried and hanged for treason against the Crown, on the testimony of ledgers kept by Colonel Edmun Harrow. Some of you remember him. Some of you are his children. He was a careful man and a good neighbor and he kept notes in the margins of everything, because that is what careful men do." Her thumb found the first marked page. Isandro, half in shadow at the balcony door, watched the crowd watch her, and understood that Edda had chosen her opening the way a surgeon chooses an incision: not the smuggling, not yet, not the numbers. The man.

"What follows is Aldous Vane's marginalia, decoded, read alongside the entries it annotates. It was hidden in this building for twenty years. I helped him cipher it and I am ashamed it took me this long to finish the work." A pause, dry as chalk. "Blindness is a slow profession."

She began to read.

Entry the first: *Grain, 40 sacks, seized at Millbrook, entered as spoiled.* Margin, translated: *"Not spoiled. Sold at Colder Bay, three pounds the sack, proceeds to H. Entry countersigned under duress by the miller, whose son is held at Fort Ann."*

A sound moved through the square. Not shouting. Something worse — recognition. The miller's grandson was standing four rows back.

Entry the fourth. Entry the ninth. The convict transport diverted to carry Harrow's private cargo while its human freight waited eleven days on the quay, of whom nine died, their names read out one by one because Edda insisted names were the unit of truth. And then the last pages, the ones Isandro had decoded at three in the morning with her father's handwriting swimming before her eyes, and Edda's voice did not change register at all, which was its own kind of fury:

*"He means to hang me for what I know. Let the record show I never raised a hand against the Crown. I raised a pen against a thief. If my children read this: the fault was arithmetic, and arithmetic can be corrected. Correct it."*

The clock finished striking as she closed the ledger — twelve, thirteen, fourteen strokes past the hour, the rigged train spending its last accumulated lies in one long bronze sentence — and in the silence after, Isandro stepped out onto the balcony beside the old woman, visible, named, signed.

She had prepared a speech on the stair. It dissolved on contact with the air. What came out instead was plain as a chisel mark:

"My name is Isandro Vane. Aldous Vane was my father. Colonel Harrow offered me a berth on that ship in exchange for my silence, and yesterday I gave him my berth anyway to buy my brother's passage, because I thought that was all I had to trade." She laid her hand flat on the railing, on the iron her father's era had forged. "I was wrong. I had the hour. It belongs to you."

Below, the crowd did not cheer. That was the thing Isandro would remember longest, telling Tomas about it years afterward across water: they didn't cheer, and they didn't scatter. They turned — all at once, like a field of wheat taking wind — toward the harbor gate, where the final ship rode at its single requisitioned berth, and where Colonel Edmun Harrow had just arrived in his carriage to supervise the embarkation of the names he had chosen.

The carriage stopped. Harrow stepped down. He saw the flag. He saw the balcony. He saw, with the particular clarity of a man watching his own signature fly overhead on sailcloth, exactly what had been done and by whom.

His hand went to the berth list under his arm.

And the crowd, four hundred strong and holding the corrected names in their ears, began to walk toward him — slowly, without orders, in the manner of people who have just discovered that the law was on their side all along and merely misfiled.

Beside her, Edda tilted her head, listening to the footsteps. "Well," she said. "Now we learn what the town decides."
