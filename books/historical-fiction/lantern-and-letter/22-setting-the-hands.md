## 22. Setting the Hands

The ship went out on the morning tide with her brother's name written on its manifest and her own struck through in the colonel's careful hand — a line drawn clean as a blade, as if she had never been listed at all.

Isandro stood at the harbor gate to watch it go. She had meant not to. She had told Edda she would stay at the observatory, that there was work, that the clock needed winding before nine. But her feet had carried her down the hill anyway, through streets still scattered with the ash of requisition notices pried from doorposts, past the townhouse where the ledger's empty case stood open like a mouth that had finally finished speaking.

The *Meridian* sat low in the water, crowded to her rails. Maren Ostrow was somewhere aboard with her sister's children; Isandro had seen the woman at dawn pressing a bundle of bread into Wren Callis's hands and saying something that made the clerk laugh — actually laugh, a cracked, disbelieving sound, like a hinge rusted shut swinging free for the first time in years.

Tomas found her at the barrier.

He looked older than he should have. They all did. But there was a moment when he came toward her along the quay, one hand raised against the grey light, and for three steps he was only her brother again, the boy who had once held a candle for her under a blanket so she could read past curfew, the young man who had taught her to gut a fish badly and laugh about it longer.

"You're staying," he said. Not a question.

"Someone has to keep the clock."

"The clock kept fine without you for twenty years."

"It ran wrong for twenty years." She smiled. "Nobody noticed because nobody knew what right sounded like."

He took both her hands. His were cracked from salt work, from hauling lines in weather that would have broken softer men. She memorized them. She had already begun the habit of memorizing things — the exact weight of his grip, the torn thumbnail, the way his eyes went wet without spilling — because she had learned in the last month that everything you love can be taken by a signature on a page, and the only defense is to hold it so completely inside yourself that no ledger, no colonel, no fire can reach it.

"Tomas." Her voice almost failed on the second syllable. "I have to say it now. While I can."

"Don't—"

"I'm your sister." The words came out steady after all. She'd rehearsed them on the stairs of the observatory, alone, at two in the morning, whispering to the dark until they stopped tasting like blood. "Not Isandro Vane, clockmaker. *Sela.* Your sister Sela. Papa used to carry me on his shoulders to the harbor and buy us burnt sugar, and you were jealous, and I never told anyone, ever, because telling anyone would kill us both. That's why I couldn't help you. That's why I let you beg a stranger. It was the only way to keep you alive."

The ship's bell rang twice behind him. A mate shouted something about the tide.

Tomas pulled her in. He held her the way people hold each other when they've run out of time to do it properly — too hard, too fast, his chin pressed to the top of her head, and she felt his breath shudder once and then steady itself, because he was their father's son too, and steadiness was the inheritance the hangman hadn't managed to confiscate.

"I knew," he said into her hair. "Since the first week. When you fixed the harbor master's watch without opening your mouth the whole time. Papa did that. Went silent over any work he loved."

She laughed and cried in the same breath, which she supposed was simply what happened when a person became whole again all at once.

"I wrote your name," she said. "On the list. In my own hand. He'll know. Harrow will know who I am."

"He's in irons in his own cellar." Tomas pulled back and looked at her, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen since childhood — pride, plain and unguarded, the kind you show family because family doesn't need it explained. "Whatever else happens, Sela. Whatever comes next. You gave the town back its names. Papa would have—" His throat worked. "Papa would have signed his own name next to yours. Proudly. Loudly. Probably gotten arrested for the volume of it."

The mate shouted again. Sailors began casting off lines.

She kissed her brother's cheeks, both of them, the way their mother used to, and then she stepped back behind the barrier because if she didn't step back now she never would.

The *Meridian* turned her bow to the open water. The crowd along the quay — thinner now than at the evacuation's height, but stubborn, the ones who'd stayed to see it through — did not cheer. They watched in silence, the way you watch a funeral or an acquittal, and Isandro understood that both griefs and both reliefs were present in that silence, mixed beyond separating, and that this was simply what the end of a siege looked like from the inside.

Tomas stood at the stern rail. He did not wave — waving was for partings you expected to survive casually. Instead he put his hand flat against his chest, over his heart, and held it there until the ship was small enough that she could no longer be sure whether the gesture was his or her own memory inventing it.

Then the fog took the ship, and the harbor was only a harbor, and Bellhollow was only a town again — damaged, diminished, missing half its families, but a town, with its own name intact, which was more than most of its people could yet say for themselves.

* * *

The walk up to the observatory took eleven minutes. She counted because counting was how she thought, and because the number mattered: eleven minutes between the sea and the highest working clock in Bellhollow, and every minute of it uphill, and she intended to make that climb every day for the rest of her life if the town would let her.

Edda Sollen was waiting in the transit room, seated in her old chair by the meridian marks, her hands folded over a cane she'd finally consented to use after the night of the search, when a soldier's boot had caught her hip and sent her sprawling across star charts forty years out of date.

"Well?" the astronomer said.

"Gone. Clean gone. Fog took her inside the hour."

"And your brother?"

"Sailing. Alive. Knowing me." Isandro hung her coat on the peg by the door — her coat, in her peg, in a building she had entered five weeks ago as a thief in her own skin. "Knowing my name. My real one."

"Mm." Edda's blind eyes turned toward her with that unsettling accuracy they always had. "And how does it sit? The name. Worn openly."

Isandro considered the question seriously, because Edda deserved serious answers and rarely got them from anyone else.

"Like a shoe I'd been wearing on the wrong foot for fifteen years. Everything fits differently. Better. And I keep waiting for it to hurt and it doesn't."

"It won't." Edda tapped the cane once against the floor, a habit Isandro had come to read as punctuation. "Names are like clocks, girl. Run them wrong long enough and even the owner forgets the true time. But the mechanism remembers. It always remembers. All you did was stop lying to it."

"That's a terrible metaphor. Clocks don't lie."

"All mechanisms lie when they're broken. Most people are broken mechanisms pretending otherwise." Edda's mouth twitched. "Sit down. We have work, unless you intend to spend the day staring at the harbor like a widow."

"There's no work. There's nothing left to decode, nothing left to forge, nothing left to rig. The truth has been read off a balcony to four hundred people. The colonel is in custody. What possible work remains?"

Edda smiled, and Isandro felt her stomach drop, because she had learned in five weeks that Edda Sollen smiling meant someone was about to discover how much larger a problem was than they'd estimated.

"Four hundred people heard the true ledger read aloud," Edda said. "Four hundred people heard it *once*. From a balcony. In a wind. By an old woman reading marginalia in cipher translation while standing on legs that haven't fully trusted her since the soldiers came." She lifted a folded paper from beside her chair. "What happens in six months, when the garrison sends a new officer? When the Crown's auditors arrive and ask to see the civil records, and the town produces — what? A memory?"

Isandro stared at the paper. "You want it rewritten."

"I want it *written*. Properly. Every name, every origin, every entry Harrow falsified restored and annotated, with the evidence cited, witnessed, and sealed. A document, not a performance. Performances fade. Documents persist." Edda held out the paper. "Wren Callis left this before boarding. Their duplicate ledger, with corrections noted in the margins. They said—" the astronomer paused, and her voice did something complicated, "—they said the town would need a keeper of records, and that they'd known from the first week who it should be, and that they were sorry to leave before seeing it done, but that some people are only brave in short bursts and they'd used up a decade's worth in one month."

Isandro took the duplicate. Wren's handwriting covered it in that cramped, apologetic script — every altered entry flagged, every original value reconstructed from memory and cross-checked against Maren Ostrow's testimony, against the parish rolls Edda had kept from her teaching years, against the scrap of her father's handwriting that had started everything.

At the bottom of the first page, beneath Wren's final note, a space had been left blank. Deliberately blank, ruled off, labeled in Wren's hand:

*Certified correct by the Keeper of Records, Town of Bellhollow.*

Signature line. Date line. Waiting.

"She planned this," Isandro said. "Before she left. Before the exchange, maybe. Before the arrest, even."

"Wren Callis spent years watching powerful men write lies and sign them comfortably." Edda shrugged. "I imagine the thought of leaving behind one honest signature, in one honest book, was the entire point of surviving."

* * *

She worked through the afternoon.

The observatory was warm for once — Edda had permitted fires now that the garrison's coal requisition had collapsed along with the garrison's authority — and Isandro sat at the long table under the east window with the duplicate ledger open before her and ink, blotter, and the good pen laid out like surgical instruments.

It was slow work, and it was sacred work, and she was surprised by how much it resembled clockmaking. Every falsified entry was a slipped gear: the teeth misaligned, the whole train of the record running wrong downstream of the error. Her job was to find where the lie had entered, remove it without damaging the surrounding truth, and reset the mechanism so that time — real time, verifiable time — flowed through it again.

Harrow had been thorough in his corruption and lazy in his craft. He had altered thirty-one entries across six years of the civil ledger: smuggling tonnages disguised as legitimate freight, confiscated goods rerouted through fictitious buyers, and — the entries that had hanged her father — inspection reports backdated to conceal that the cargo being inspected had already been paid for and moved. Her father had caught it. Had documented it, entry by entry, in cipher, in the margins of the very ledger Harrow controlled, believing — rightly, wrongly, it no longer mattered — that the record itself would someday testify.

The record had testified. It had simply needed a clockmaker to give it a voice.

By lamplight she copied. Entry thirty-two, restored: *Ostrow, Maren, weaver, born Bellhollow, resident twenty-nine years* — the word *resident* struck through by Harrow and replaced with *recent arrival*, a change that had stripped the Ostrows of evacuation priority and nearly cost Maren her sister's children. Entry fifty-eight, restored. Entry seventy-one, the Vane entry, and here Isandro set down her pen and sat for a long moment with her hands flat on the table, breathing.

Her father's name. *Aldous Vane, horologist, born Marrowfield.* Harrow had amended it to read *Vane, Aldous, traitor, origin rebel-sympathizing districts,* and the amendment had been enough — one word swapped for another in a dead man's entry, and a family destroyed, and a girl sent into hiding under a dead uncle's name for fifteen years.

She wrote the correction. Her hand did not shake. *Vane, Aldous, horologist, born Marrowfield. Falsely convicted. Record amended by order of the assembled townspeople of Bellhollow, this date, upon evidence certified below.*

Below, the certification block waited. The signature line.

She looked at it for a long time.

Fifteen years of *Isandro*. Fifteen years of answering to a name chosen because it was common enough to hide in, masculine enough to pass a dockside glance, empty enough to leave no trail. Fifteen years of flinching when anyone said her father's name aloud, of signing work papers with a stranger's letters, of being nobody in particular so thoroughly that she'd almost succeeded in becoming no one at all.

The pen hovered.

*Sela Vane.*

She wrote it slowly, letter by letter, the way she'd set a delicate escapement — total attention, no hurry, the knowledge that this particular mechanism, once assembled, would run for the rest of her life. The ink dried darker than she expected. Everything about it was darker than she expected: the weight of the letters, the sound of the name in her own head, the strange vertigo of looking at her father's surname joined to her own given name on an official page and feeling not fear but *arrival*.

She blotted it. She checked it — twice, because good work demanded checking. Then she closed the ledger, tied it with the cord Wren had left, and carried it to the townhouse herself, through the blue evening, and placed it in the case whose glass had been shattered during the seizure, and locked it with a new lock she had cut herself, because if the town was going to trust her with its names she was going to be the one who decided which keys existed.

* * *

That night, for the first time since her arrival in Bellhollow, Isandro climbed the observatory tower with no task waiting at the top.

The master clock filled the stairwell with its breathing — that vast, patient respiration of weights and gears she had lived inside for five weeks, fought for, sabotaged, repaired, and finally set free. She walked the maintenance gallery around its movement, running her fingers along the great wheel rims, listening with her palms as much as her ears. Everything ran true. The strike train, released at dawn from her rigging, had tolling the correct hour ever since, and the town below had begun, tentatively, to organize its days around the sound again. She'd watched it happen from windows: the market stalls opening on the ninth stroke instead of whenever the mood arrived, the ferry adjusting its crossings, children racing the noon count up the hill road.

A town runs on its clock. She'd known that as craft. She knew it now as creed.

At midnight she stopped the pendulum — gently, with her hand, the way you calm a horse — and set the hands forward one minute, and released the pendulum again, and listened as the mechanism absorbed the correction and carried on, unbothered, exact.

Small adjustments. Honest ones. Made openly, recorded, theirs to audit.

From the gallery window she could see the whole town laid out below: the dark harbor, the townhouse with its lit record-room window where a volunteer committee would begin copying the corrected ledger tomorrow for the parish archives, the garrison where officers who had chosen law over loyalty were drafting reports that would travel to the capital by faster ship than any refugee had sailed. Somewhere in those reports, her name would appear. *Sela Vane, clockmaker, daughter of Aldous Vane.* Let it travel. Let it be read in whatever marble rooms had once received Colonel Harrow's tidy falsifications. There were worse things to be known as than a keeper of true records.

Edda appeared at the stairwell door, cane tapping, drawn up no doubt by the brief silence of the stopped pendulum — the woman could hear a missing heartbeat in a building the size of a cathedral.

"Adjustment?" she asked.

"One minute. Forward."

"Honest adjustment?"

"Logged and dated."

"Then I approve." Edda came to stand beside her at the window, a small straight-backed silhouette against the town lights. For a while neither spoke. Below, faintly, the tide worked at the harbor pilings. Above, the sky was doing the thing it did on clear cold nights, thickening with stars, and Isandro thought of all the nights Edda had spent alone under that sky, keeping time by instruments no soldier could requisition.

"They'll want you for the inquiry," Edda said eventually. "When the auditors come. Harrow's trial, if there is one. They'll want the woman who decoded the marginalia to testify."

"I know."

"And your name will be read into the official record. Spoken aloud in a courtroom, in front of strangers, attached to your father's case forever."

"I know that too."

"Frightened?"

Isandro considered it honestly, because Edda deserved honest answers and because she had promised herself, somewhere between the signature and the locked case, that she was done answering dishonestly even in the privacy of her own skull.

"A little," she said. "But it's a small fear now. Like the fear of a high dive you've already jumped once. My body remembers the edge, but the rest of me knows what's at the bottom." She turned from the window. "Besides. I've got work. The town needs its records kept, and the clock needs a keeper, and apparently I'm both. Fear doesn't fit the schedule."

Edda laughed — a dry, delighted bark that echoed up the stairwell and startled pigeons somewhere in the dome. "Your father said almost exactly that, once. Word for word, near enough. *Fear doesn't fit the schedule.* He said it the day he showed me the first cipher entry and asked me to teach him a better code, because the one he'd invented could be broken by a clever child and he had clever children." Her voice softened. "He meant you, you know. In that sentence. Even then."

Isandro looked at the old woman, and at the great clock turning its patient circles behind them, and at the town holding its small lights against the dark below, and she felt the whole shape of it at once — the scrap of paper hidden in a clock housing, the blind teacher, the disgraced clerk, the weaver who remembered, the brother on the sea — every gear engaged, every tooth meshed, the long train of chance and courage running at last to its intended output.

"Yes," she said. "I think he did."

The clock struck midnight — twelve strokes, true and unhurried, rolling down over the roofs and the harbor and the hills, and this time she simply listened, hands empty, name whole, home.

Below in the dark, at the edge of the quay, a lantern was moving. Small, solitary, climbing the hill road toward the observatory, stopping once, twice, as though the bearer were uncertain of the way or gathering courage. Isandro watched it come, and felt no dread — only the old craftsman's alertness, the sense of a mechanism about to receive a new weight.

Someone, it seemed, had come up the hill at midnight to find the keeper of the clock.

She went down to open the door.
