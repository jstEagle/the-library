## 1. The Counting Man

The morning fog came off the harbor the way it always did in Vellamare—slowly, reluctantly, as if even the sea were tired of what the city had become. Aurelio Vance stood at the bottom of Lantern Row with his ledger under his arm and his ink bottle stoppered against the damp, and he watched the mist unravel through the bare branches of the orchard district like something being deliberately undone.

It was the third of Harvestmonth, by the Consulate's calendar, which meant it was nobody's calendar at all down here, where the tenant families still reckoned time by grafting seasons and pressing days. The Consulate's calendar was a thing imposed, like the Consulate itself: clean lines drawn over old growth, straight streets planned through crooked ones that had never quite been straightened. The census was the same kind of instrument. Aurelio had understood this for years without ever letting the understanding take full shape in his mind, the way you can know a stone is in your shoe and keep walking anyway.

He climbed.

Lantern Row rose in uneven steps from the waterfront, cobbled with stones worn concave by two centuries of feet, and on either side the houses leaned together overhead like conspirators. In autumn they would have been canopied in plum and quince; now, in early spring, the trees stood naked along the property lines, black-limbed and patient, their roots buckling the walls of cellars their owners did not legally own. Every house on the row had an orchard claim attached to it—a strip of ground behind, three trees deep, five trees wide—and every one of those claims was now, according to the land registry, held by the Office of Settlement on behalf of the Consulate of Nine.

Aurelio counted heads for the Office of Settlement.

His route that morning took him first to the widow Marchetti's house, third from the corner, where the door opened before he knocked because she had seen him coming from her upper window and had already decided to be gracious about it.

"Signor Vance." She curtsied the way people curtsied to tax assessors and plague doctors—correctly, completely, and with nothing in it. "You'll want tea."

"I'm not permitted to accept—" he began, out of habit, and she was already pouring it.

The interior of the Marchetti house smelled of woodsmoke and dried figs. A boy of perhaps six watched Aurelio from behind a chair leg with the frank hostility children reserve for anyone carrying official paper. On the wall hung a framed embroidery: an orchard in blossom, worked in thread gone soft and gray with age, and beneath it, in cross-stitched letters, a motto in the old dialect that Aurelio could read but was not supposed to be able to. *What is planted is owed.*

"Four persons in the household," the widow said, before he asked. "Myself, my daughter, her two boys. The girl Sera married out last year, so strike her if she's still on your list. She's in Salters' Ward now."

Aurelio wrote it down. He wrote everything down; that was the whole of his trade. Name, age, trade, years resident, relation to head of household. Eleven columns, each one a small act of reduction, a life pressed flat into figures that would travel to the capital and become part of the great sum that justified whatever the Consulate had already decided to do. He had been a counting man for nine years. He was very good at it. He tried not to think about what the numbers were for.

"And your son-in-law?" he asked. "Tomas Marchetti. He's listed at this address as of last winter's survey."

"Gone to the docks," she said. "Work, when there's work. He sleeps here when he can."

"He should be recorded at his principal residence."

Something moved behind the widow's eyes—not anger, exactly, but a kind of weary gatekeeping, the expression of someone deciding how much truth was safe to hand across a counter. "Then record him here," she said pleasantly, "and let the Office send its letters to the docks if it wants him."

He recorded Tomas Marchetti at Lantern Row. It was a small mercy and they both knew it, and small mercies were the only currency left in the orchard district, and both of them pretended the transaction had been nothing at all.

* * *

By midmorning the fog had burned off into a thin, hard sunlight that made the whitewashed walls glare. Aurelio worked his way up the row and then along Fig Street and Press Lane—Press Lane, named for olive presses that hadn't operated in forty years, though the name had a different music to it now, he would think later—tallying households with the automatic rhythm of long practice. Knock, wait, count, write. Knock, wait, count, write. The district answered him with its usual guarded courtesy, doors opening only as far as chains allowed, faces arranged into the neutral mask that people wore for the state's clerks, children pulled backward by the collar out of earshot.

He was forbidden to look too closely. That was the actual instruction, written into his commission in language so bland it took a year or two to notice it: *enumerators shall record household composition as declared by the head of household, without inquiry beyond the schedule.* As declared. Not as observed. The census was not designed to find the truth; it was designed to produce a number that matched the number the Consulate had already published in its almanac. When his totals came in wrong—and they always came in wrong, because the orchard district breathed and shifted and hid its people the way any living thing hides from a net—he was instructed to adjust them to the projection. There was a column for it. Column eleven. *Correction.* He filled it in every quarter, and every quarter he felt something in himself grow a little quieter, a little more carefully dead, like a hearth banked for a winter with no end.

At the top of Press Lane the houses gave way to a yard, and in the yard stood the print shop.

He had passed it a dozen times on previous routes and never thought about it twice—one building among hundreds, tall and narrow, its timber frame silvered with weather, a hanging sign carved with a hand holding a quill. But today the light fell on it differently, and he noticed what he had trained himself not to notice: the shutters.

Every shutter on the street side was closed. Not broken, not weather-warped—closed, and freshly closed; he could see the pale rectangles on the plaster where the sun had faded around them. On a working day, in a working district, every other window stood open to the air. The dyer two doors down had her frames out in the yard. The cooper's apprentices were singing at their staves. Only the print shop sat sealed against the daylight like a face turned away, and from inside, if you stood still in the lane and stopped pretending you weren't listening, came a sound that did not match the silence of the shutters at all: a small, rhythmic, metallic tapping, quick as a wren's heartbeat, stopping the moment his boots scraped the cobblestones.

Aurelio looked at his ledger. He had four households left on Press Lane and then the long climb to the terraces above, and none of it was urgent, and all of it was due.

He knocked at the print shop door anyway.

The tapping did not resume. For a long moment the building simply existed at him, blank and shuttered, and then footsteps crossed a floor inside—measured, unhurried, the footsteps of someone who had taken the time to compose themselves—and the door opened the width of a man's forearm.

Henrik Eriksen was not what Aurelio expected, though he couldn't have said what he'd expected instead. The master cutter was a big man going gray, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who have spent decades bent over fine work rather than heavy work—the strength concentrated, hidden, coiled into the hands. And the hands were remarkable even at a glance: scarred across the knuckles, burn-marked at the fingertips, steady as a mason's level where they gripped the door's edge. His face was ruddy and plain, his eyes pale gray, and his voice, when he spoke, carried the flattened vowels of the northern coast, a foreignness worn smooth by thirty years in Vellamare but never sanded away entirely.

"Yes?"

"Aurelio Vance, census enumeration, Office of Settlement." He showed the tin badge, though the man had surely seen a hundred of them. "I'm completing the spring count for Press Lane. Your household isn't on my schedule."

"No," Eriksen agreed. "It wouldn't be."

There was no hostility in it. That was what unsettled Aurelio—that and the mild, waiting patience of the man's gaze, like a craftsman studying a piece of stock, assessing grain and flaws. Aurelio heard himself say, "Households are added to the schedule when a premises changes use. A commercial property converted to mixed dwelling requires—" He stopped. The sentence was a formality, and they both knew it was a formality, and reciting it felt like performing a card trick for a jeweler.

"My daughter lives above the shop," Eriksen said. "She has for six years. My wife died eight years back." He said it the way he might report the weight of a type case: accurate, unadorned, offering nothing that could be used to pry anything else loose. "Two persons. You may write it however the Office prefers."

"Your name will need to go into the register. Occupation."

"Typefounder. Typeface cutter." A pause so slight Aurelio almost missed it. "Printing is licensed separately. You'll have seen the license."

"I'd need to see it to record the license number."

For the first time, something flickered behind the pale eyes—not fear, Aurelio would think afterward, trying to parse the memory. Calculation. The flicker of a man measuring a distance. Then Eriksen stepped back and the door swung wider, and the smell of the shop rolled out over the lane: hot metal and oil of turpentine, lampblack, the dry paper-and-glue scent of a bindery, and underneath it all something faintly sweet and scorched, like sugar burned in a pan.

"Come in, then. Mind the step."

* * *

The interior was dimmer than the closed shutters explained, because the shutters were only half the concealment. Beyond the front room—where a counter, a scale, and a wall of sample typefaces faced the street—workshop benches ran back into gloom, and above them the ceiling vanished upward into a loft stacked with wooden racks, each rack bristling with thousands of small metal fingers, ranks upon ranks of letterforms catching what little light there was and throwing it back dull silver. Aurelio had seen printing shops from the outside. He had never stood inside one, and the sheer density of it—the sense of a building stuffed solid with language, every word in Vellamare lying dormant in these drawers and cases waiting to be woken—pressed on him strangely, like standing in a granary and suddenly understanding hunger.

At a bench near the back window, a young woman was grinding something against an oilstone with slow, circular strokes. She did not look up.

"Paloma," Eriksen said. "The census man needs the license book."

She set down her burnisher, wiped her hands on her apron with a deliberateness that struck Aurelio as rehearsed—though why a woman would rehearse wiping her hands, he could not then have said—and went to a shelf behind the counter. She moved through the dim shop without seeming to navigate it, touching nothing she didn't need to touch, passing through the cluttered space the way water passes through reeds. She was perhaps his own age, dark-haired, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and steel-gray smudges ground into her knuckles that no amount of washing would ever fully lift; he knew that particular stain from somewhere in his own childhood, some uncle or neighbor, and it woke a tenderness he had no business feeling. Her eyes, when they finally lifted to his, were brown and level and utterly unrevealing. She looked at him the way her father had looked at him: as material to be assessed.

She did not smile. She did not meet his eye for longer than a transaction required. She put the license book on the counter between them, opened to the current page, and stepped back to her bench, and the whole exchange took less time than the widow Marchetti's tea.

Aurelio copied the license number, the issue date, the renewal stamp of the Office of Works. Everything was in order. Everything had the polished, seamless rightness of a machine kept in perfect repair, and it occurred to him—standing in that dim, humming, shuttered room with his pen moving across the page—that the Eriksen household was in order the way a fortress is in order.

"Two persons," he said, writing it. "Yourself and Paloma Eriksen. Ages and birthplaces?"

Eriksen supplied them. Born in Norrvik, on the northern coast, fifty-one years ago. The daughter born in Vellamare, twenty-six years ago. Aurelio's pen paused of its own accord, a hesitation no supervisor would ever see, because the arithmetic of the household nagged at him the way a misprinted line nags a proofreader: a man who had lived in Vellamare thirty years, whose trade required apprentices, whose shop by any commercial logic should employ four hands minimum—and the register would show two. A shop this size, silent, shuttered, staffed by an old man and a daughter who ground punches and never raised her eyes.

"Apprentices?" he heard himself ask. "Most shops this size carry—"

"The trade's changed," Eriksen said. Smoothly. Too smoothly, the answer arriving before the question finished landing, the way a prepared speech arrives. "Machines in the capital now. Cheap work from the south. We do fine cutting only. Fine cutting wants few hands and quiet ones."

From her bench, Paloma resumed grinding. The rhythmic scrape of steel on stone filled the pause, and Aurelio understood, with a certainty that had no evidence behind it whatsoever, that the sound had resumed precisely because he had asked a question. It was cover. It was choreography. These two had performed this scene before, for other officials, other knockings, and every gesture in it—the license ready, the answers oiled, the daughter absorbed in her work and deaf to it all—had been drilled until it ran smooth as a press.

He finished the entry. He blotted it. And because he was a counting man, and because counting men are paid to notice quantities, his eye did one last circuit of the room before he left, and it snagged—snagged badly—on the hearth.

The shop's kitchen hearth, tucked in the back corner behind a partition, held the cold grey ash of a fire that must have been substantial: a great drift of it, heaped higher than a night's burning would leave, and threaded through the ash were fragments that had not fully burned. Corners of paper. A scrap with a wax seal half-melted but legible, red as a wound. He looked at it perhaps two seconds. Perhaps less.

When he raised his eyes, Paloma was watching him. Not her father—her. She had stopped grinding. Her face was perfectly composed, perfectly courteous, and her eyes were saying, with a clarity that needed no words: *you saw nothing. There is nothing to see. Walk out of here.*

He walked out of there.

* * *

The rest of the route went mechanically. Two households on the terraces, one refusal, one dog. He climbed to the ridge road above the district in the late afternoon and ate his bread looking down at Vellamare spread out below—the harbor crammed with grain ships flying the Consulate's nine-towered banner, the rooftops smoking peacefully, the orchard district a patchwork of garden walls and bare trees stitched into the city's eastern hem like a seam someone had forgotten to finish. From up here the district looked harmless. From up here you could believe the almanac's version: a settled quarter, adequately enumerated, orderly.

He took out the ledger to review the day's entries, the way he always did, checking his sums against the light. Two hundred and six souls recorded in the orchard district, spring count. The projection in his instructions said two hundred and nineteen. He turned to column eleven—*Correction*—and sat with his pen poised over it while the shadows came up the hillside and the harbor bells rang the fourth hour.

Thirteen missing persons. Thirteen people the Consulate preferred not to exist, and every quarter he erased them with a stroke, and every quarter the erasure got easier, and he had told himself for nine years that this was the honest work of an imperfect system, the best available arithmetic, the compromise a careful man makes. Sitting on the ridge with the print shop somewhere below him in the blue dusk, its shutters certainly closed again, its hearth certainly burning something it shouldn't, Aurelio Vance found that the compromise had begun to taste like ash.

He entered the correction. Of course he did. He had a lodging to pay for, a post to keep, a mother in Salters' Ward who depended on his wage. He was a counting man, not a judge. The pen moved; the thirteen vanished; the ledger balanced.

But walking down into the city by lamplight, past the first taverns spilling firelight and argument onto the cobbles, he found his mind returning again and again to the same small wrongnesses, stacking them up the way he stacked figures: shutters closed on a working day. A tap of metal that stopped at the scrape of his boots. Answers too smooth by half. A hearth full of burnt paper and a wax seal red as blood. A daughter who ground steel and never met his eye—and who had looked at him, once, directly, with a message in her gaze that no innocent person sends.

And beneath all of it, the thing he had no column for: the arithmetic that wouldn't close. A household of two in a shop built for ten. Thirty years in Vellamare and a name—*Eriksen*—that sounded bought new, like furniture from a catalog, like a story rehearsed until smooth. Like an answer given before the question finished.

He would come back, he told himself. There was an error to correct—an arithmetic error in the day's entries, trivial, the kind of thing any diligent clerk returns to fix. It was even true; he had found it reviewing the sums, a transposition in the Marchetti ages. A counting man corrected his errors. It was practically doctrine.

He did not examine too closely why, of all the errors he had made in nine years of routes, this was the first one he had ever been eager to return for.
