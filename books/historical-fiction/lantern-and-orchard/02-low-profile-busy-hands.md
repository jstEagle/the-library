## 2. Low Profile, Busy Hands

The whetstone sang against the steel, and Paloma Eriksen let it.

She had learned early that a girl bent over her work was invisible. A girl grinding punches at the back bench of her father's shop, sleeves rolled to the elbow, hair pinned up with a sliver of boxwood—she was furniture, she was weather, she was nothing at all. The census men could walk past the window ten times a day and see only what they expected: a daughter doing a daughter's work, hands busy, head down.

So she kept her head down. She had made an art of it.

Outside, the afternoon light came through the front panes in long amber bars, catching the dust that rose whenever Henrik sorted type in his cabinet. The dust of a print shop was not like other dust—it tasted of lead and lampblack, of resin and old rags soaked in solvent. It got into the creases of her knuckles and stayed there for days, so that when she took bread at market the baker's wife looked at her blackened fingers and smiled the smile one gives to tradespeople: *working folk, honest folk, nothing to see here.* Paloma returned the smile exactly as intended. That too was craft.

*Scrape,* said the whetstone. *Scrape.* The punch under her hands was a lowercase g, and the bowl of it had gone soft from years of striking into copper. Her father could have bought new matrices; there were foundries in the capital that shipped them wrapped in oilcloth like relics. But Henrik Eriksen did not order by post if he could help it, did not write letters he did not have to, did not leave a paper trail where a paper trail might be read. So they recut, reground, re-polished. Their type was older than most of the city's presses, and every letter in it had passed through the hands of someone named Eriksen—or someone who had been named something else before that.

Paloma set down the whetstone and held the punch up to the window. The g caught the light along its bevel, clean as a raindrop.

"Mind the counter," her father said from across the room, without looking up. He never looked up. He always knew.

"The counter is true."

"The counter was true this morning." He blew a curl of shavings off the case in front of him. "Steel has moods."

Everything in their house had moods. The press downstairs had moods—the great iron platen groaning on damp days like an old man complaining of his knees. The stove had moods. The door hinge had a mood so particular that Paloma could tell from her bed whether the person coming in at midnight was her father or a stranger, and she had drilled herself, half in play and half not, on what she would do if it were ever a stranger: out the loft window, across the tiles, down the drainpipe into the tanner's yard, where old Basso owed Henrik a favor nobody spoke of aloud.

She went back to the punch. *Scrape.* Invisibility, she had come to understand, was not stillness. Stillness drew the eye the way a stopped clock draws it. Invisibility was motion—ordinary, repetitive, slightly dull motion, performed with the face of a person thinking about nothing more interesting than supper.

Her mother had taught her that, though she'd been small when her mother died and could not now recall her voice, only the lesson, which arrived in memory without words attached: *be busy, be bland, be boring.* Her mother had been very good at it. Paloma sometimes wondered, filing that thought away in the drawer where she kept all the questions her father would not answer, what a woman could have done in her life that required being boring so well.

* * *

After dark, the shop changed.

They shuttered the front windows at dusk—not latched but barred, the way shops did in rough districts, which was true enough of theirs and also useful. Henrik lit two lamps instead of three, leaving the corner behind the standing cases in shadow. And then, twice a week or once a week or sometimes three times in a single week, depending on things he did not explain, he carried the iron brazier from the hearth into the back room, and the smell of burning paper drifted through the whole building like a bitter incense.

Tonight was a burning night.

Paloma sat at the sorting frame with a candle stub and the day's work, distributing type—letters back into their compartments, each in its proper cell, the way a shepherd pens sheep. The work was rhythm itself: pick, glance, place. Pick, glance, place. An e here, an n there, the fussy little punctuation huddled together in their own quarter of the case like nervous relatives. Anyone watching through the window—and no one could, but she checked anyway, the curtain moving just enough—would see a dutiful daughter tidying after the day's trade.

What she was actually doing was listening.

The back room door stood open a hand's width. Through the gap she could see the edge of the brazier, the rim of orange light breathing on the wall, and her father's forearm entering and leaving the frame of view. He fed the fire slowly. That was the thing she noticed, the thing that coiled in her chest tighter every burning night: he fed it slowly, page by page, holding each sheet until the flames took it properly before adding the next. A man destroying evidence in haste burned in fistfuls. A man who watched each page go was reading them. Or remembering them. Or mourning them.

A sheet curled. Through the doorway she saw, for half a heartbeat before it blackened, the ghost of writing—dense lines, a formal hand, maybe a seal at the foot, a dark bloom of wax going glossy in the heat. Then it was ash, sifting down onto the coals like grey snow.

"Henrik," she said. She almost never called him Father inside these walls; outside, yes, Father, loud enough for neighbors, because a fatherless girl working a print shop was a story people told each other, and stories brought visitors. Inside, he was Henrik, and had been since she was twelve, when she'd asked him why they lied to the tax assessor about how many presses they owned and he'd looked at her for a long moment and said, "Because you're old enough now to lie well. Call me Henrik when we're alone. Liars should know each other plainly."

"Burning again," she said now. Not a question. Questions gave him room to deflect; statements did not.

"Mm." Another sheet went into the fire. The arm withdrew. "Old correspondence."

"It had a seal on it."

"Merchants' seals. I correspond with merchants."

"With merchants whose wax smells like the Consulate chancery?"

The arm stopped. For three full seconds the only sound was the fire chewing and the tick of the cooling press below them, iron contracting in the night air. Paloma kept sorting type. Pick, glance, place. Her heart was going faster than her hands, and she made sure her hands didn't show it. That was the discipline: never let the hands tell.

"You count too much," Henrik said finally, and there was something underneath the gruffness that wasn't anger. She'd spent years learning the strata of his voice the way she'd learned the compartments of the type case, and she knew what lay under that particular flatness: fear, and beneath the fear, a tiredness so old it had fossilized into habit.

"I'm a printer's daughter," she said. "Counting is half the trade. How many ems to the line, how many lines to the page, how many pages to the forme." She placed an m in its slot, dead center, perfectly. "You taught me that whatever can be counted can be checked. So I check."

He came to the doorway then. Firelight on one side of his face, lamplight on the other, and between the two lights a narrow band of shadow where his expression lived, unreadable. He was not a large man, but he filled doorways the way good type fills a line—completely, with no gaps left over.

"Some papers are dangerous to keep," he said.

"And some papers," Paloma said, keeping her eyes on the case, "are dangerous enough that keeping copies of them would be worth dying for. Which kind burns tonight?"

He didn't answer. He never answered. But he stayed in the doorway a moment longer than usual, and she felt his gaze rest on her the way a proof press rests on dampened paper—pressure, brief and enormous, taking some impression he did not intend to show her. Then he went back to the fire, and the next sheet went in, and she smelled the wax seal melt and knew that whatever family had owned that seal, its name had just gotten smaller by one witness.

* * *

Later, when the brazier had cooled and the ash was folded into the slop bucket where it would go out with the morning wash water—never the refuse cart, never anything that left the house in a form anyone could sift—Paloma climbed to the type loft.

The loft was hers. Officially it stored the display faces, the big wooden poster types the size of hymnals, stacked on their edges like the books of a giant. Unofficially it was the one room in the house with a lock on the inside of the door, a cot, a window overlooking the tanner's yard, and a loose floorboard under which she kept the things that were hers alone: a broken cameo that had belonged to her mother, three coins struck by a government that no longer existed, and a notebook.

The notebook was the size of her palm and bound in offcuts of morocco leather, stitched crookedly, because she had bound it herself at fourteen. In it she wrote everything. Not secrets—she had none yet, or believed she had none—but observations. The comings and goings of the street. Which census men took bribes and which took notes. The schedule of the harbor patrol, deduced from lanterns. The fact that her father burned papers on nights following certain visits, and that those visits were always from men who arrived in unmarked carriages and left their drivers waiting with the reins wrapped twice around their fists, the grip of men expecting trouble.

Tonight she dipped her pen and wrote: *Burned eleven sheets. One sealed. Father held page seven longest. Count again tomorrow.*

Then she sat cross-legged on the cot and listened to the house settle around her—the beams ticking, the press sighing, the city beyond the shutters doing its nighttime work of noise: a drunk singing three streets over, the watch calling the hour in a voice that cracked, gulls arguing over the fish market leavings. Vellamare at night sounded like a press running. Somewhere, always, something being stamped and sealed and recorded, the city printing itself upon itself.

From below, faintly, the scrape of her father's boots. He was sweeping the back room. He always swept after burning, on hands and knees, with a brush reserved for that room alone, gathering every fleck of char that might hold a legible fragment. She had found the brush once, years ago, hidden in the flour bin, stiff with ash, and had put it back exactly as it lay and never spoken of it. Some discoveries you returned to their hiding places like borrowed tools. That, too, was part of the choreography: the dance they did around each other, both of them pretending the floor was only a floor, the fire only a fire, the family only a family.

But the choreography had a fault line in it, and Paloma stood on it every day and felt it flex.

It was this: she was becoming too good at noticing. The same skill that kept her safe—the counting, the checking, the reading of lanterns and carriage wheels and the weight of her father's silences—kept turning up questions, and questions were a currency she couldn't spend and couldn't stop earning. Why did their type include characters for an alphabet she'd never seen commissioned? Why did Henrik keep one cabinet locked that held no matrices she'd ever been shown? Why, when the Consulate's crest was stamped on proclamations nailed to the fish market posts, did something in her father's face move—not fear exactly, and not hatred, but recognition, cold and old, like a man hearing his childhood language spoken by strangers?

*We are printers,* she reminded herself, lying back on the cot. *We serve the trade. We take commissions. We print bills of sale and shipping manifests and, last spring, forty funeral invitations for the Vasseur family, God rest them, and we ask nothing and we note nothing and we survive.*

It was a good creed. It had kept three generations of them alive, apparently, though of generations past she knew only what the shop knew: that there had always been an Eriksen at this bench, cutting steel, casting type, keeping the presses of Vellamare supplied with honest letters. The shop remembered further back than the family admitted. Type did that. Type remembered every impression ever struck from it, held the ghost of each page in its worn shoulders. Steel had moods, her father said. Steel also had memories.

Below, the boots stopped sweeping. A match flared—she saw its glow under the door seam—and then the lamps went out one by one, and the house went dark in the order it always did: back room, stair, workshop, front. Her father saving the front lamp for last, as he always did, so that any watcher across the square would see the light die at the ordinary hour, in the ordinary sequence, an ordinary shop closing up for an ordinary night.

Paloma blew out her candle and lay in the dark above the sleeping press, and let the tension sit in her chest where it lived, coiled and patient, like type locked in a forme: everything held tight in its place, waiting only for the ink, the paper, and the pressure that would make it all speak.

Tomorrow the census man was due back on their street. She had seen his route card through the window yesterday—had read it upside down at forty feet, another skill she did not discuss—and noted his careful pen, his tired eyes, the way he looked at buildings as if they might testify.

Let him come. She would grind punches. She would sort type. She would be busy, and bland, and invisible.

And if he looked too closely—if he asked one question too many—well.

She was her father's daughter, and she had already begun, without knowing it, to cut the tools she would need.
