## 3. One Question Too Many

The error found him at dusk, three days later, in the census office on Customs Quay, where the lamps burned tallow and the air tasted of wet wool and old arithmetic.

Aurelio Vance had been reconciling his returns against the district rolls when he saw it: the Eriksen household, print shop, Lantern Row. Four souls entered in his own hand—Henrik Eriksen, master cutter, fifty-eight years; Paloma Eriksen, daughter, twenty-three; and a third line he could not account for. *Apprentice, male, nineteen.* He had written it in the field because the boy had answered the door on his second visit, flour-pale with paper dust, and Henrik had said *our apprentice* in that even voice of his, and Aurelio had written it down the way he wrote everything down, faithfully and without thought.

But the household count was four. And he had only ever seen three.

He went back through his field notes. The first visit: Henrik at the counter, Paloma sorting type in the back room, visible through the doorway like a figure in someone else's painting. The second visit: the apprentice—no name given, no name asked for, which was itself unusual, since apprentices were supposed to be registered with the Guild of Printers and the guild rolls were public record. Aurelio had checked them that afternoon, between cups of bitter office tea. No apprentice named under Henrik Eriksen. No apprentice of any name at that address.

Census law required names. He had written *apprentice, male, nineteen* because the man had looked nineteen and Henrik had supplied no other word for him, and Aurelio had let it pass, because by then the rain had started and the shop smelled of hot metal and he had wanted to be elsewhere.

Now, sitting in the yellow lamplight with the district rolls fanned before him like a hand of losing cards, he understood that he had made two errors. One of arithmetic: the tally column summed to five households short for the block, an error trivial enough to correct with a fresh visit. And one of attention: he had counted heads without counting what the heads were hiding.

His supervisor, Magister Corvo, a soft man shaped like a wine cask, glanced up when Aurelio rose to fetch his coat.

"Back out again? Your route's filed."

"An error in the Lantern Row returns. I'll correct it tomorrow."

Corvo waved a hand thick as a ham. "Correct it from memory. Nobody audits the orchard districts until after the settlement."

"The settlement," Aurelio said, and heard how flat his own voice had gone.

"After," Corvo repeated, with the mild finality of a man who had survived thirty years of Consulate administration by never once finishing a sentence that began with *before*. "Go home, Vance. Drink something. You count people too hard; they can feel it through the walls."

---

He told himself he was going back for the arithmetic.

He told himself this again on the walk out, past the tanneries where the wind turned, past the first straggling pear trees of the orchard district with their fruit too small and sour to sell, hanging on branches the tenants were forbidden to prune—the trees belonged to the land, and the land belonged, on paper, to men who had never seen Vellamare. He told himself a third time as he climbed Lantern Row in the grey morning light, breath smoking, boots loud on stones still slick with night rain.

The lie wore thin by the time he reached the door. It was not the sum of five households that had brought him. It was the fourth soul.

He knocked. Inside, the shop sounded different than before—not silent, exactly, but hushed the way a house is hushed when its occupants have been listening to footsteps approach. A long pause. Then bolts, one-two, drawn back with deliberate slowness, and Henrik Eriksen stood in the doorway with his sleeves rolled and his forearms dusted silver to the elbow, type metal ground into the creases of his hands so deeply it looked like age itself had gotten into the skin.

"Magister Vance." Not a question. Henrik's eyes went briefly past Aurelio's shoulder to the empty street, then returned. "Come about your numbers?"

"There's a discrepancy." Aurelio produced his ledger and opened it on the counter, all business, ink-stained thumb holding the page. "Five households short on my block tally. And—" he kept his voice pleasant, administrative, the voice of a man who cared about columns, "—I need to confirm a registration. Your apprentice. Census law requires a name and a date of indenture."

For a moment—half a heartbeat, no longer—something moved behind Henrik's face. Not fear. Something more controlled than fear, something that had been practiced for so long it had become architecture. Then it settled, and the old cutter smiled the smile of a man agreeing that yes, paperwork is tedious, isn't it.

"Tomas," Henrik said. "Tomas Eriksen. My late brother's son. Indentured to me at fourteen—he's twenty-one now, I misremembered his age when you came; he was ill that day, you understand, and I was distracted." He recited it smoothly, each fact fitted to the next like lead type locked in a forme. "No indenture papers. Family arrangement. The guild fees seemed an extravagance for kin."

*A family arrangement.* In Aurelio's experience, the phrase covered everything from genuine tenderness to tax fraud, and usually both. He wrote the name down. His pen moved easily. That ease itself troubled him—how readily the ledger accepted whatever it was fed, how a lie became a fact simply by being legible.

"And Tomas is here now? For the confirmation? Height, occupation, place of birth—I'll need it from him directly."

"He's out," Henrik said. "Delivering proofs to the chandler on Fish Street."

"At this hour?"

"He goes early. He doesn't care for the midday crowds." Henrik's tone did not change at all, and that, Aurelio thought, was the strangest thing about it. Most liars adjusted their voices, pitched them up or softened them. Henrik delivered his fiction with the flat serenity of a man reading a shipping manifest.

Aurelio copied the details into his ledger. Behind him, deeper in the shop, there was the small iron music of type being sorted—the click and slide of sorts dropping into their cases—and beneath it, faintly, a second sound he couldn't identify. Something heavier. A drawer closing. Or not closing quite all the way.

"I'll just confirm with him another day, then," Aurelio said, and turned toward the back room as though to leave that way, an idle redirection, a census man's habit of looking into every room he passed.

"Through here." Henrik stepped sideways, blocking the passage with his body, still smiling, already opening the front door onto the street. "This way saves you the stairs. Mind the step—it rots."

It was done gracefully, without haste. But it was done. And Aurelio, standing on the doorstep with his ledger under his arm, understood that he had just been escorted out of a house whose interior he would now think about every day for the rest of his life.

One thing more. As Henrik's hand came down to close the door, Aurelio caught a glimpse past his shoulder—down the length of the workshop, past the great press with its iron screw gleaming dully like a black sun, to the back room where Paloma Eriksen sat at her case. She wasn't sorting type anymore. Her hands had gone completely still above the open drawer, one sort held pinched between finger and thumb, caught mid-air, frozen the way birds freeze when a hawk's shadow crosses the yard. She was looking directly at him.

Not away, as she had on both previous visits. Not through him. *At* him—with an expression he needed a long moment to read, standing there in the cold with the door swinging shut between them.

It wasn't fear. It was assessment. She was measuring him, the way her father measured metal: testing where the grain ran, whether he would hold an edge or shatter, what he might be worth the trouble of cutting.

Then the door clicked home, and the bolt went across, one-two, and Aurelio stood alone on Lantern Row with a corrected tally in his book and a wrongness in his chest he had no column for.

---

He walked back toward the quay the long way, along the orchard terraces, and tried to make the morning behave like a normal morning.

Tomas Eriksen. Nephew. Unregistered, unindented, conveniently absent. It was possible—all of it possible. Families hid apprentices from the guild to dodge fees; half the small shops in Vellamare did it. The guild fines were steep and the inspectors corrupt, and a man who had buried a brother might well keep the orphan off the books out of grief or thrift or simple stubbornness. There was nothing in any of it that required conspiracy. People lied to the census constantly, and almost always about money.

And yet.

He kept seeing the stillness of her hands. He had spent eleven years counting people, and he had learned that the truth of a household lived not in its answers but in its hesitations—the beat before a wife said how many children, the glance a husband traded with a lodger, the way a family's breathing changed when a certain name was spoken. Most census takers never learned this. They collected facts the way magpies collect buttons, bright and meaningless, and never noticed that the whole story was in the pauses.

The Eriksen house had paused everywhere. It paused around the apprentice who did not exist on any roll. It paused around the back room. It paused, most of all, around its own silence—that deep, maintained quiet of a shop that should have been noisy with work and wasn't, shutters closed on a working day, press unrun, the smell of hot metal fresh but no orders drying on the racks.

A shop that wasn't printing. A printer's house holding its breath.

At the top of the terraces he stopped and looked back. From here he could see the roofline of the print shop, and above it, rising from the loft, a thin thread of smoke from a chimney that served only the upper rooms—a small fire, banked low, the kind of fire a single person keeps burning through a cold morning.

Or the kind of fire a man keeps for burning things.

Aurelio stood in the wind among the unpruned trees, his ledger cold against his ribs, and felt the crack open in him that would never close again. He was an honest man; he had built his whole grey life on the foundation of that honesty, and honesty had always meant accuracy—correct sums, faithful counts, the world recorded as it presented itself. But the Eriksens had shown him something else: that a record could be perfectly accurate and entirely false. Four souls in a household of some other number. An apprentice made of air. A name—Eriksen—sitting in his ledger in his own careful hand, and for the first time in eleven years he wondered what the name had been before, and why the asking of such questions was nowhere in his instructions.

Below him, on Lantern Row, the shutters of the print shop remained closed.

Above him, on the Consulate hill, the banners of the Provisional Authority hung becalmed in the grey air over the Hall of Records, where every true and false fact in Vellamare was stored side by side and none of them, he suddenly understood, were required to agree.

He opened his ledger. Under *Tomas Eriksen, nephew, 21,* he added nothing, crossed out nothing. Instead, in the margin, in the tiny cipher shorthand he used for private reminders—reminders about weather, about routes, about which housewives gave bad tea—he wrote a single note to himself:

*Ask the boy directly.*

Then he closed the book and walked down into the city, past the sour pears and the shuttered shop, carrying his small accurate falsehoods back to the quay like a man carrying water in a cracked jug—wondering, all the while, what it was in that house that needed so badly to stay uncounted, and why the daughter's stillness had stayed with him longer than any face had in years.
