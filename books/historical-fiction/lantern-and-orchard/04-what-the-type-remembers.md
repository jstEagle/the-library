## 4. What the Type Remembers

The cabinet had a key, and the key lived in her father's waistcoat pocket, and the waistcoat lived on his body from the hour he rose until the hour he slept—so Paloma had spent nineteen years treating that cabinet the way she treated the sea: aware of it, respectful of it, never once tempted to swim.

But her father was at the harbor this morning, negotiating the purchase of a crate of antimony with a man who smelled of tar and lies, and the key—this once, this only time—had been left behind in the coat hanging by the stove. She saw it the moment she came down to light the fire: a small brass tooth glinting in the pocket's seam, catching the gray light like a fish turning under water.

She told herself she would only warm her hands.

The stove was cold. The shop around her was still half-asleep, the great press a black shape under its canvas sheet, the type cases stacked in their familiar geography of drawers. Outside, Vellamare was waking in its usual order—the gulls first, then the fish carts, then the bells of Saint Alder counting six—and none of it knew what she was about to do, which was nothing, which was everything, which was to put her hand into her father's coat and take the key out as gently as if it were an egg she'd been asked to mind.

*Just to oil the hinges,* she told herself. The lock had been sticking; she'd heard him curse it twice last week. A daughter could oil a hinge. A daughter could not be blamed for maintenance.

Her hands were steady. That was the strange part. Paloma Eriksen could grind a punch to within the width of a hair, could cut a letter's serif so fine it looked drawn rather than carved, and her hands did not shake for solder or acid or the long slow pressure of the graver. They did not shake now. It was her breath that had gone strange, shallow and quick, as she crossed the floor and knelt before the cabinet in the corner where the shadows pooled deepest.

The lock turned with a sound like a swallowed word.

Inside, she expected type. Old matrices, perhaps, or the tools of the trade that needed guarding—punches were currency in this city, a good set worth more than a fishing boat, and Henrik Eriksen guarded his like a dragon guards gold. She expected steel and brass and the ordinary wealth of a craftsman.

What she found instead was paper.

Reams of it, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, tied with string gone soft with age. Bundles of letters in a hand she didn't recognize, tall and slanted, written by someone who pressed hard enough to score the page. Beneath them, flat portfolios the size of hymnals, their spines cracked, their corners worn round as river stones. And at the very back, wrapped separately in waxed linen, something heavy—a shape she knew with her whole body before her mind caught up, because she had grown up with ten thousand of its cousins.

A punch. But no punch like any she had ever seen.

It was larger than the others, cut from a single bar of steel gone dark with handling, and when she lifted it into the light from the window her stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

A crest. Cut deep, cut masterfully, every line crisp as the day some unknown hand finished it: the olive branch and the three towers, the shield beneath, the motto ribbon curling beneath it all—*Ordo et Copia*—the crest of the Consulate itself, the seal that appeared above every proclamation, every tax notice, every decree that had reshaped the city of her childhood.

Except this one was a *punch*. Not printed. Not engraved on a plate in some government office. This was a cutting tool, meant to strike the design into a matrix, meant to cast type, meant to stamp that crest onto paper a thousand times, ten thousand times, wherever a printer was paid—or persuaded—to make the official mark appear.

And it should not exist. Not here. Not anywhere outside the Consulate's own mint and ministry, where such things were counted nightly and locked behind doors with more locks than her father's cabinet had ever dreamed of.

Paloma knelt there holding the impossible object, and the shop seemed to lean in around her. The press under its sheet. The cases with their sleeping letters. Every drawer of type her father had cut in thirty years of honest work—and beneath all of it, at the root of the root, this.

She thought of the burning barrel behind the shop, the nights she'd woken to orange light dancing on the ceiling of her room and told herself it was only her father disposing of spoiled proofs. She thought of how he flinched—not visibly, never visibly, but in some way she felt through the floor—whenever a Consulate clerk came to collect the printing fees. She thought of her mother, dead nine years now, who had never once spoken of her own childhood, whose answers to questions about the old country had been doors quietly closed.

*Our name,* her mother used to say, when Paloma pushed too far, *is the name we answer to. Isn't that enough?*

She turned the punch over. On the shank, in tiny letters cut near the tang, was a date and a word. She angled it toward the window.

*Vellamare — 41 Anno Fundationis.*

Forty-one years after the Founding. The Consulate was founded fifty-two years ago. This punch was older than her father by eleven years, older than the shop, older than her own family's history as she had ever been allowed to know it.

Someone had cut this. Someone with hands trained exactly as hers were trained, in the same trade, the same craft passed down the same way—someone who had stood in a workshop very much like this one and driven a graver through steel until the Consulate's face emerged. And that someone had kept it, hidden it, buried it at the bottom of a locked cabinet in a print shop in the orchard district, where it had waited through her grandfather's silence and her mother's closed doors and her father's careful, choreographed life.

Through her.

The bell over the door did not ring—it never rang; her father oiled it precisely so it wouldn't—but she heard the latch anyway, the small click she had known since she was four years old, and she knew without turning that it was him, home early, the antimony deal gone sour or gone strange, and she was still kneeling, still holding the crest of the men who ruled them all in her two bare hands.

"Paloma."

Not angry. That was the terrible thing. His voice was quiet and level and tired, the voice he used for delivering bad news to customers whose orders couldn't be saved. She heard him cross the floor, heard the canvas sheet shift as he hung his coat—her theft of the key already forgiven, already folded away as if it had never mattered—and then he crouched beside her, and for a long moment neither of them moved.

He looked at the punch the way other men look at graves.

"You oiled the hinge," he said finally.

"The lock was sticking."

"It was." He took the punch from her hands with a gentleness that frightened her more than anger would have. He held it up to the window light, turning it, and she watched his face do something complicated—grief and pride and fear braided together, none of them winning. "Do you know what you're holding?"

"The Consulate's crest."

"I cut it." He said it simply, and the sentence broke her understanding of the world along a clean seam. "No. That's not true. My father cut it. I re-cut the worn places, twice, when I was younger than you are now. It is the finest work I have ever touched and the worst thing our family has ever made."

"Henrik—" She never called him Henrik. *Papa*, always, or nothing. The formality came out of her unbidden, a stranger's word, because the man kneeling beside her had become briefly a stranger. "Why do we have this? Why does a type founder in the orchard district have the Consulate's seal in his cabinet?"

He set the punch down on his knee and looked at her, really looked, the way he looked at a proof sheet hunting for flaws—searching her face for some error, some immaturity, some reason to close the door again and let her go on living in the house of the story they had built.

Whatever he found, it made him sigh.

"Sit down," he said. "No. Stand. You'll want to stand for this. I stood when my father told me, though God knows why, there's nowhere to run to either way."

He did not unlock anything else. He simply talked, standing beside the covered press with his hands clasped behind his back like a man giving testimony, and Paloma leaned against the type cases and listened while sixty years of careful silence came apart in her father's mouth.

"Our name was not always Eriksen," he said. "That's the beginning and you'll want me to hurry past it, but I won't, because the name matters. Names are the first forgery anyone learns. Before a man falsifies a document he falsifies himself."

The family, he said, had come to Vellamare two generations before the Consulate—cutters of punches, founders of type, a trade carried from the northern kingdoms where their surname had been long and foreign and impossible for harbor clerks to spell. When the Founding came, when the merchant families and garrison captains drew up their compact and crowned themselves Consuls of a free city that had stopped being free in the same breath, the trade guilds had been given a choice: serve, or starve.

"My grandfather served," Henrik said. "He was the finest cutter on the coast, and the new government wanted seals. Proclamations, land grants, charters of settlement—all of it bearing the crest, all of it carrying the weight of law because it carried the mark of authority. And my grandfather cut that authority into steel with these hands' grandfathers." He flexed his fingers, looked at them as if they belonged to someone else. "You know how good we are, Paloma. You've seen what a punch can do. Now imagine that skill pointed at a lie. Imagine the lie coming off the press looking more true than truth, because the impression is perfect."

"He forged land grants," Paloma said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears.

"He made documents that *were* the law, because whoever holds the seal holds the law. The orchards belonged to the tenant families—some had worked the same terraces for a hundred years, since before the Founding, since the old counts. The men who became the Consuls wanted those slopes, wanted the water rights, wanted the labor. So grants were drawn. Grants were sealed. And every grant bore this crest, struck true and clean, because my grandfather's steel was true and clean even when his conscience wasn't." Henrik picked the punch up again, weighed it. "He kept it. Afterward, when he understood what he'd helped build, he kept it as a record. The one original. Everything else the Consulate owns is a copy struck from copies. This is the source. This is the wound itself, in steel."

"And us? Why Eriksen?"

"After the Uprising of the north districts—failed, you'll find no trace of it in any book your census man might read—my father changed the name. Too many people knew what the old name had signed. We became Eriksen the way you become left-handed: deliberately, painfully, forever." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile she had ever seen on him. "I have been Henrik Eriksen for forty years. I am good at him. He is honest, he is dull, he cuts beautiful letters for books nobody bans. But underneath, Paloma—underneath, in the cabinet, in the dark—we are the family that built the thieves' crown. And we are the family that kept the proof."

"Proof of what?" But she knew. She felt the answer rising in her like water finding its level. "Proof of what, Papa?"

"That the grants are false. Original deeds. Correspondence. My grandfather's own confession, written the year before he died, naming names, describing the forgeries stroke by stroke—he was a craftsman; he confessed the way a craftsman confesses, in detail." Henrik wrapped the punch back in its waxed linen with the tenderness of a man bandaging a wound. "Thirty years I have kept it. Thirty years I have told myself that a record no one reads is still a record, that the archive is its own justification, that I am not a coward but a custodian. Your mother believed otherwise. She believed a secret kept too long becomes a grave you're buried in with the person you buried."

The stove ticked as it warmed. Somewhere outside, the fish carts had given way to the school bell, and children were shouting in the lane, and the ordinary morning went on being ordinary, indifferent to the fact that Paloma Eriksen's entire inheritance had just changed shape—from a trade, from a shop, from a name—into a burden with edges.

"Why tell me now?"

Henrik was quiet for a moment. He slid the punch back into the cabinet, among the oilcloth bundles and the cracked portfolios, and turned the key in the sticking lock, and put the key in his waistcoat pocket, and buttoned the pocket, and only then answered.

"Because you opened it," he said. "Because your hands didn't shake. Because sooner or later Sarn's historians will finish rewriting this city, and when they do, the only question that will matter is whether the people who knew the truth were brave in time or brave too late." He touched her cheek, brief and rough, his palm smelling of metal and lamp oil. "Your mother used to say the type remembers. Everything we ever set, every lie and every ledger, it remembers. Steel doesn't forget, Paloma. Neither do I. And now—God help us both—neither do you."

He went back to the stove to heat the solder for the morning's work, moving with the exact economy he always moved with, as if nothing had happened, as if the shop were still only a shop.

Paloma stayed where she was. Behind her the locked cabinet sat in its corner shadow, holding its paper ghosts, and she found she could no longer look at the type cases the same way—every tray of letters suddenly a witness, every character complicit or waiting to testify, she could not decide which.

That evening, when the census taker came up the lane on his rounds with his ledger under his arm and his tired eyes cataloguing shutters and chimney smoke, Paloma watched him from the window and thought: *he counts heads, but he has no idea what heads carry.*

She thought of the punch in the dark, the crest sharp enough to draw blood from the careless thumb.

She thought: *steel doesn't forget.*

And for the first time in her careful, invisible life, Paloma Eriksen began to wonder what it would mean to make the steel speak.
