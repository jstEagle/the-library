## 5. The Orchard Districts

The Bureau of Records occupied the lower floor of the old customs house, a building that had been built to count ships and now counted people instead. Its windows were tall and salt-hazed, and the light that came through them in late autumn was the color of weak tea, falling in long rectangles across the filing tables where clerks like Aurelio Vance bent over their returns like penitents over prayer books.

Aurelio had been filing census returns for eleven years, and he knew the ritual of it the way a sailor knows rope. First the tally sheets, checked twice against his field notes. Then the household abstracts, copied fair into the ledger hand the Bureau demanded—a hand with no flourishes, no character, a hand designed to be forgotten even as it was read. Then the returns themselves, bound in gray string and carried to the map room, where each household would be pinned to its parcel of ground on the great district charts that hung from the walls like flayed skins.

It was in the map room, on the third morning after he had closed out the orchard route, that Aurelio noticed the boundaries.

He had not meant to notice anything. That was the discipline of the work—count, record, do not interpret. The Bureau paid him for accuracy, not for opinion, and in eleven years he had learned that the two were rarely welcome in the same room. But the maps had been replaced over the summer. New engravings, fresh from some office in the capital, and as he stood at the chart of District Nine with a pin between his fingers, preparing to fix the Eriksen print shop to its coordinates, something in the lines troubled him the way a wrong note troubles a musician who cannot say why.

He set the pin down. He leaned closer.

The new map showed District Nine divided into tidy rectangular lots, numbered and initialed, each one assigned by the Consulate's land commission to this holder or that—the names of merchants, of officers, of men whose signatures Aurelio recognized from tax rolls and banquet invitations. Clean lines. Orderly settlement. A district made legible, as the Bureau liked to say, made legible and therefore made governable.

But beneath the ink—if you held the paper at an angle, if you let the tea-colored light rake across it—you could see where the engraver had worked over an older plate. Ghosts lived under the new lines. Curving boundaries, organic ones, following hedgerows and stream beds and the old rights-of-way that tenant families had walked for generations: the lane behind the Ferreiras' press-house, the footpath that cut through what was now lot forty-one, the brook that had once belonged to no one and watered everyone. Someone had scraped the plate and re-engraved it, but scraping is never perfect, and the old orchard boundaries rose through the new survey like drowned fences seen through spring flood.

Aurelio stood there a long moment, the pin forgotten in his fingers.

He thought of the Eriksen shop's shutters. Of Henrik Eriksen's smooth answer about a fourth name in the household, an answer so polished it could only have been rehearsed. Of Paloma's hands going still above her type case—not the stillness of innocence, which fidgets, but the stillness of someone listening to how much danger has entered the room. And beneath all of it, unexamined until now, the memory of documents glimpsed on the shop's back counter when he'd come to correct his error: parchment edges, wax-sealed corners, papers too old and too carefully kept to be anything ordinary.

He had told himself they were private family papers. Wills, perhaps. Deeds. Everyone kept deeds.

*Deeds.*

The word turned over in him slowly, like a stone in a stream bed showing a different face each time the water shifts.

Because if the Eriksens held deeds—and printers, of all trades, understood precisely what a deed was worth as evidence—then those curved ghost-lines on the map were not ghosts at all. They were claims. Old boundaries that said *this ground belongs to the families who worked it*, boundaries that someone in the capital had found inconvenient enough to erase from the very plates used to administer the district.

He did not decide anything, standing there. Decision would come later, and costlier. For now he only did what a census man does: he compared records. He fetched the previous edition of the District Nine chart from the archive cabinet—the one engraved twelve years ago, before the redraw—and laid the two maps side by side on the filing table, weighting the corners with brass objects shaped like fruit, which the Bureau called paperweights and which everyone privately called apples, because the Consulate had commissioned them and the joke wrote itself.

Twelve years ago, the map had shown the orchard commons. Not as commons—the Bureau's vocabulary contained no such word—but as *unallocated parcels, seasonal use, tenants-at-will*. Still, the shapes were there: the long curve of the old boundary running along the ridge, the irregular tongue of land where the cider presses stood, the strip of riverside ground marked *ferry access, all households*. On the new map, all of it had been absorbed into numbered lots. The ferry access was gone entirely; the riverbank now belonged to lot thirty-eight, holder's name Vessant, a customs magistrate Aurelio had once seen drunk at a naming feast.

And here was the thing that made Aurelio's stomach tighten, here was the crack opening wider in the floor of his orderly world: the erasure was not sloppy. It was thorough. Every old path, every shared well, every right-of-way—gone, re-engraved, overwritten. This was not neglect. This was authorship. Somewhere in the capital, someone had sat down with intent and rewritten the ground itself, and the ground, being only ink, had obeyed.

People, Aurelio knew, did not obey so easily. People remembered where the well was.

He copied nothing. That instinct, too, was already forming—the census taker's reflex inverted, the recording hand withheld. But he memorized the overlay: the ghost-boundary along the ridge, matching exactly the line he had half-seen on the edge of a parchment in the Eriksen shop. Matching it the way a key matches a lock it was cut for.

"Aurelio."

He straightened too quickly. Pins scattered from the tray.

Supervisor Doss stood in the doorway of the map room, and Aurelio registered, in the way one registers weather, everything wrong about the scene: Doss never came to the map room before noon; Doss did not like stairs; and Doss's eyes went first not to Aurelio's face but to the two maps lying open on the table.

"Closing out Nine?" Doss said.

"This morning." Aurelio began gathering pins with careful indifference. "The new plates are handsome."

"Mm." Doss crossed the room at his unhurried pace, a heavy man who moved like furniture being rearranged, and looked down at the charts. His shadow fell across both editions, covering the ghost-boundaries as thoroughly as the engraver's scraper ever had. "You'll note the supersession stamp. Old plates are withdrawn from reference. There's a procedure."

"I've noted it."

"Have you." Doss picked up one of the brass apples, turned it in his thick fingers, set it down again on the *newer* map, as if choosing. "Then you'll also note that withdrawn materials are not to be compared against current surveys. It creates confusion. Confusion in a records office is worse than error. Error can be corrected. Confusion spreads."

Aurelio felt the warning arrive the way cold air arrives under a door—no announcement, just a change in the temperature of things. "I wasn't comparing," he said. "I was checking parcel numbers against my tallies. The renumbering runs contrary to the field sequence."

"The field sequence is irrelevant now. The lots are allocated." Doss smiled, and it was the smile of a man offering you the last biscuit on a plate he intended to keep. "You did good work in Nine, Vance. Thorough. The Commission remarked on it—your household counts are precise, your notes are clean." He paused. "Perhaps too clean. You write observations in the margins. Occupations. Condition of dwellings. Whether a household seemed... prosperous, or discontented."

"That's standard annotation."

"It was. It's being phased out. The Commission finds interpretive notes generate questions." Doss let that sit in the room like smoke. "Questions travel upward, Vance. Questions reach men who don't distinguish between the person who asks and the question asked. You're young enough to have a career ahead of you. Don't spend it on margins."

For a moment neither man moved. Through the salt-hazed window, Aurelio could see the roofs of the orchard district sloping down toward the river, and beyond them, faint with distance, the ridgeline where the ghost-boundary ran—the true line, the old line, the line that existed now only in ink that had been officially withdrawn and in the memories of people who had walked it all their lives.

"And if the tallies contradict the allocations?" Aurelio heard himself ask. His voice was level. Eleven years of bureaucratic calm held it level. "If a household occupies ground the new maps assign elsewhere?"

Doss's small eyes regarded him with something that might, in a better man, have been pity.

"Then the household is recorded as displaced," Doss said. "That's what the column is *for*, Vance. We didn't add it last year for decoration." He gathered his coat about himself and moved toward the door, then stopped, one hand on the frame, without turning around. "The Eriksen file. The print shop on Tanner's Row. You returned twice."

"To correct an arithmetic error. Procedure requires—"

"I know what procedure requires. I wrote half of it." Now he did turn, and his face in the tea-light was unreadable as a closed ledger. "The Commission has asked for a supplementary abstract on certain Ninth District properties. Printers, ferries, mills—trades that move paper. Yours will be the first name on it. Make sure the abstract contains nothing that requires explanation later."

He left. His footsteps diminished down the stairwell, each one a period ending a sentence Aurelio hadn't been allowed to hear.

* *

He worked through the afternoon with the mechanical diligence of a man trying to outrun his own thoughts, and the thoughts caught him anyway, the way they always do, in the pauses between tasks.

*A trades that move paper.* So the Commission was watching printers already. Watching them not for sedition—that charge had not yet been invented, or had not yet been needed—but for the older, quieter crime: possession of documents. In a state that ruled by grant and charter, paper was territory. Whoever held the oldest paper held the truest claim, and the Consulate had spent the summer making sure that the oldest paper in Vellamare was the paper they had written.

Except, perhaps, in a locked cabinet in a print shop on Tanner's Row.

Aurelio filed the last of his District Nine returns at dusk. He pinned each household to the new map with brass-headed pins, and his hand did not hesitate—he was too well-trained for hesitation—but something in him catalogued every pin as a small violence. *Displaced.* The column waited in the ledger for these families like an open grave waits for rain.

He locked the map room, returned the withdrawn chart to the archive cabinet, and paused with his hand on the drawer. Procedure said lock it. Procedure said the past was withdrawn from reference.

Instead, before closing the drawer, he did one small thing. He took the old chart out, rolled it loosely, and slipped it inside the hollow cardboard core of a damaged wall-map destined, by every schedule, for the incinerator. Damaged maps were burned weekly. No one audited refuse.

It was not copying. It was not yet treachery. It was, he told himself as he carried it out under his arm into the blue evening, merely a clerk refusing to let a record die unread—which was, after all, the entire nominal purpose of his profession.

But he knew the weight of what he carried. He had counted enough households to know that a man carrying a hidden thing walks differently, and he made himself walk normally, past the sentry at the customs house gate, who glanced at the roll of waste paper and looked away, because sentries do not audit refuse either.

The city smelled of woodsmoke and bruised apples. In the orchard district, lights were coming on behind shuttered windows, and somewhere a dog was barking at something invisible, and down on Tanner's Row, if he walked that way—not that he had reason to, not that a census man had any further business there—the print shop would be dark, or would seem dark, the way shops are dark when the people inside them wish very much to be unseen.

Aurelio did not walk toward Tanner's Row. He walked home. He ate bread and cheese standing up. He unrolled the condemned map on his table by candlelight and studied the ghost-boundaries until the candle guttered, committing them to memory the way he committed everything now—not to paper, never again only to paper, but to that other ledger he seemed to be keeping lately, the one no supervisor could withdraw from reference.

And it was there, in the margin of his mind, beside the ghost-lines and the still hands and the sealed parchments, that a single question settled and refused to leave:

If the Consulate had gone to such lengths to erase a boundary—how much farther would they go to erase the document that proved what the boundary had meant?

Somewhere across the dark city, he suspected, someone was already asking the same question. And someone else—someone with small, steady hands and ink-stained fingertips—was about to start answering it.
