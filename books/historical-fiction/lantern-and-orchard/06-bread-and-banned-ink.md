## 6. Bread and Banned Ink

The market at Saltwharf opened before the sun did, the way it always had, vendors lighting their lamps against the grey and calling out prices to no one. Paloma came down with a basket on her arm and her father's list folded in her pocket—flour, salt pork, lamp oil, the small ordinary liturgy of a household that wished above all things to remain unremarkable. She had learned long ago that a woman carrying bread was invisible. A woman carrying questions was not.

The morning smelled of brine and woodsmoke and the green bitterness of late oranges. Gulls quarreled over the fish stalls. Somewhere a knife rang against a whetstone, keeping time like a heart that would not slow down.

She bought flour from Marta Okoye first, because Marta's stall stood nearest the harbor arch and because Marta never asked anything but *how is your father's hand this week*, which was a kindness disguised as gossip. Paloma answered the way she always answered: well enough, thank you, the cold makes it ache. The lies of Vellamare were mostly small ones, told gently, worn smooth as river stones by everyone who carried them.

It was while she was counting out coppers for the oil man that she heard the boots.

Not one pair—a dozen, maybe more, coming up Cooper's Row in step, the sound of them arriving through her feet before her ears made sense of it. The market did not go quiet; markets never do all at once. But it changed key. The haggling thinned. A mother pulled her boy close by the collar. Paloma watched a fishmonger become very interested in scaling a mackerel he had already scaled twice.

They came around the arch four abreast, Consulate blues with white crossbelts, bayonets fixed, and behind them two men pushing a handcart—and behind *that*, walking between two soldiers with his wrists tied in front of him, was a thin young man she half knew by sight. Ossian. The one who set type for hire when he could get it and printed leaflets when he couldn't. He had ink stains to the elbow, permanent as birthmarks. Everyone at Saltwharf knew his hands even if they'd never learned his name.

And behind him, on the cart, wrapped in sacking but unmistakable to anyone who had grown up sleeping beside one, was a press.

Paloma's basket handle bit into her fingers. She did not move. Moving was the first thing they looked for—the flinch, the turn, the sudden fascination with one's own shoes. She had been taught invisibility as a craft, and now she practiced it standing still: eyes down, face mild, a woman waiting for lamp oil, thinking of nothing but supper.

They stopped at the mouth of the square, where the morning light came strongest, and an officer with a voice like a door slamming read from a paper.

"By order of the Consulate of Vellamare, this man is taken for the crime of unlicensed printing. His press is confiscated. His type will be melted." A pause, and then, because cruelty always wants an audience: "Let all present understand. There is one lawful history of this city. It is written at the Consulate. Anyone found setting unlawful words will share this man's morning."

Ossian's chin was up. That was the terrible part, Paloma thought later. Not the rope or the bayonets or the cart, but the chin, held high, and the way his eyes went across the crowd—not searching for rescue, because there was none to be had, just looking at faces. Recording them. A printer to the end, taking inventory of who had witnessed him.

His gaze crossed hers and moved on. Or perhaps it didn't move on. Perhaps he knew her too—knew the print shop on Tanner's Lane, knew whose daughter stood there with flour on her sleeve. His eyes flickered, once, like a lamp in wind. Then the soldiers turned him and marched him off toward the customs house, and the press went bumping after him on its cart, and the market exhaled and resumed its noise the way water closes over a dropped stone.

Paloma paid the oil man. Her hands were steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have. She took her change, nodded to Marta—who did not nod back, who was staring hard at nothing—and walked home by the long way, through the tannery smells and under the dripping eaves, because the short way passed the customs house and she could not bear to see where they were taking him, or imagine what they would do with his type. Melted lead has no memory, her father said once, when she asked why he kept every broken letter in drawers instead of selling it for scrap. Melted lead forgets everything it ever said.

Her hands were steady until she reached the shop door, and then they were not.

* * *

She did not tell her father. That was the arithmetic she did on the stairs, climbing past the smell of metal polish and old paper: Henrik Eriksen would hear of Ossian's arrest by evening, because everyone would hear of it by evening, and if she told him first he would begin burning papers tonight, and if he began burning papers tonight he would burn the wrong things first, the harmless things, the things that might yet be saved. No. Better to say nothing and watch what he chose to feed the stove. A man reveals his treasures by what he burns last.

So she ground punches until her wrist ached, and sorted type into cases by candlelight, and said nothing, and listened to her father's footsteps overhead, pacing, pacing, stopping, pacing again. He had heard. Of course he had heard. The whole city had heard.

In the afternoon she went out again, because they needed salt and because staying inside the shop felt like sitting in a closed box with the lid nailed down. Also—though she did not admit this to herself until much later—because she wanted to walk past the bakery on Cordwainer Street, where the census man had been seen three mornings running.

Aurelio Vance. She had counted him the way she counted everything, in glances measured out like coin: the tired eyes, the ink-stained cuff, the ledger carried flat against his chest like something he was sworn to protect or ashamed of. He had come to their door in the autumn with his counting book and his soft questions, and Henrik had given him answers polished smooth, and Aurelio Vance had written them down and gone, and Paloma had spent the following week telling herself she had not thought about him since.

The line outside the bakery stretched to the corner. Bread had been short since the dockworkers stopped unloading grain ships, and short bread made patient lines, patient lines full of people who had nothing to do but talk. She took her place at the end, basket on her arm, hood up against the drizzle that had started somewhere between the second thought and the third.

He was already in the line. Three people ahead of her, ledger under his arm, hat dark with rain, looking as though he had been standing there long enough to memorize the cracks in the cobblestones.

Pretending coincidence, she told herself. He is a census man; census men eat bread. So do I. Two people in a queue is not a conspiracy.

But then he turned, as if her heartbeat had reached him through the wet air, and looked at her, and did not look away.

"Signora Eriksen." A beat, and then, quieter, almost wry: "I hope I'm pronouncing it correctly. I checked."

"You checked," she said.

"I check everything. It's the whole of my profession." He hesitated, and she saw the hesitation cost him something—saw the official composure slip sideways like a forme that hadn't been locked properly. "I came back to your shop in the winter. To correct an error in my returns. Your father was... gracious about my carelessness."

"He remembers you," Paloma said, and immediately wished she had not said it, because it sounded like an offering and she had not decided to offer anything.

Something moved behind his tired eyes. "Does he?"

"He remembers everyone who writes things down." She kept her voice level, pleasant, market-day pleasant. "Mostly he distrusts them."

"And you? Do you distrust me?"

The question hung between them with the drizzle. Ahead in the queue, a woman laughed at something, bright and oblivious, and the ordinariness of it made the moment stranger—two people conducting a dangerous conversation in the open air, disguised as small talk, sheltered by the sheer dullness of a bread line.

"I don't know you," Paloma said. "Distrust takes acquaintance."

That surprised a laugh out of him—a short one, quickly caught, but real. "That's fair. I'd argue we're acquainted. I've counted your household twice."

"You've counted our *heads*. That isn't the same thing." She shifted her basket. Rain dripped from the awning between them, ticking on the stones. "Anyone can count heads. My father counts letters. He says a letter is only worth what someone risked to put it on the page."

"Is that what he says?" Aurelio Vance looked at her then fully, and she understood that he understood—that whatever careful choreography her family performed, this man had seen a step out of place and had not reported it, and had come here, to a bakery, in the rain, three days running, to stand near her without reason. "Then he and I disagree about my profession. I count heads because I'm ordered to. But—" he lowered his voice, and the drizzle seemed to lean in with him, "—this morning, at Saltwharf. You were there."

The cold went through her like a blade of ice. "I buy oil at Saltwharf."

"Yes. So did I. I was filing returns at the harbor office." His eyes held hers, steady and unhappy. "They're going to melt his type, signora. All of it. Every word he ever set. I watched them load the crucible carts this afternoon." A muscle worked in his jaw. "I signed the requisition form. My office processed it. It required a signature and I gave one, because that is my profession, and I am beginning to think your father is right about mine."

For a moment Paloma forgot to breathe. Here it was, then, laid out in a bread line like a card turned face-up: a government man confessing complicity to the daughter of a hidden archive, in daylight, within earshot of forty people and a constable drinking coffee under the arcade. Either he was the bravest fool in Vellamare or he was bait.

She should walk away. Every craft of invisibility she possessed said *walk away, smile, buy your loaf, go home, say nothing.*

"The crucible melts the words," she heard herself say. "It doesn't melt the memory of them. Someone who saw the type set could cut it again. Letters are only lead. What they say lives somewhere harder to burn."

He stared at her. Rain ran off the brim of his hat. And in that stare she saw the thing she would spend years afterward trying to name: not lust exactly, not yet, but recognition—the jolt of finding another person who had also been awake all this time, also watching, also keeping a private ledger against the day it might matter.

"That," he said quietly, "is either nothing at all, or the most dangerous sentence spoken in Vellamare today."

"Which is it?"

"I haven't decided." He glanced toward the bakery window, where the loaves sat golden under glass, absurdly peaceful. "I'll decide soon. Things are moving faster than ledgers now. Dock strike spreading to the fruit wharves. Rent refusals in the lower orchards. The Consulate has sent for a historian from the capital—a court man, Sarn—to write the official account of whatever happens next." His mouth twisted. "They want the history printed *before* the events, so the events will know how to behave."

"A historian," Paloma repeated. Something in her chest had gone very tight and very calm, the way it did when her father spoke in code over dinner and she translated without moving her face. "To write one lawful version."

"One lawful version." He nodded slowly. "And every press in the commercial districts will be requisitioned to print it. Including, I imagine, a certain shop on Tanner's Lane with excellent equipment and a master cutter whose hands are known throughout the city." He paused. "You should tell your father to take inventory. Quietly. Of everything he'd hate to lose."

The line moved. The baker's boy called that the dark rye was out, only white, only white today. Paloma stepped forward one place, and Aurelio stepped forward beside her, and for the length of a breath neither of them said anything, and the silence was not empty at all—it was crowded, it was teeming, it was two minds turning over the same forbidden page.

"Signora." His voice had dropped so low she had to read his lips as much as hear him. "When the historian comes—if presses are seized, if men like Ossian start disappearing by the dozen rather than the one—there will be people in this city who think some records ought to survive anyway. Outside the lawful version." He was looking straight ahead, at the bakery door, addressing the rain. "If such people existed, and they needed a census man who knew every warehouse, every cellar, every customs clerk who takes bribes and every one who doesn't..." He let it hang. "They could find me. I am very easy to find. I stand in lines."

"You're asking me to carry a message," she said.

"I'm asking you to remember that I stood in this line. Whatever else happens. Whether you ever speak to me again or not—I stood here, and I said what I said, and it was freely said." Now he did turn to her, and the drizzle caught the lamplight coming on behind them in the baker's window, and his face was open in a way she suspected it almost never was, stripped of the census man, the uniform, the signature on the requisition. "I've been counting heads for six years, signora. I would like, before this city finishes tearing itself apart, to count for something."

Inside the shop, the baker called next. Paloma bought her loaf—white, because there was nothing else—counted out her coins with hands that did not shake, and turned to go.

At the edge of the awning she stopped, just long enough, and did not look back at him.

"Tanner's Lane," she said, pitched for him alone beneath the market noise. "Thursday evenings, my father sends me for lamp oil. The shop stays open late. If a message needed carrying, Thursday would be a poor night to knock—everyone knows we're alone."

Then she walked into the rain, bread warm against her ribs like a second heart, and did not let herself tremble until she was three streets gone.

Behind her, in the line, a government man stood holding coins for a loaf he had forgotten to buy, watching the space where she had been.

And three days later, as the first barges came up the harbor bearing crates stamped with the seal of the Consulate's new court historian, a knock came at the print shop door on Tanner's Lane, at dusk, on a Thursday—earlier than any lamp-oil errand, earlier than agreed, a knock that did not stop, urgent, doubling and doubling again, until Paloma wiped the ink from her hands and opened it onto Aurelio Vance's rain-white face and the words that would undo every careful year:

"They've given Sarn a list of presses," he said. "Yours is second on it."
