## 8. Oath and Appetite

The oath had been administered to him nine years ago in a room that smelled of beeswax and cold stone, by an official whose voice never once rose above the register of a man reading a shipping manifest. *I swear to count truly, to record faithfully, to serve the Consulate of Vellamare in all matters of census and registry.* Aurelio had repeated it with his hand flat on a leather-bound ledger older than his father, and he had meant it—not with any burning conviction, but with the plain, unexamined sincerity of a young man who believed that counting was a neutral act, that numbers were innocent, that a tally was simply a tally.

He had not believed it for some time now. But belief, like a tooth going bad, rots from the inside out, and the outside keeps its shape long after the core has turned black.

Three nights had passed since Henrik Eriksen opened the cabinet.

Aurelio lay awake in his rented room above the chandler's shop on Tallow Street, staring at the ceiling beams, and in the dark behind his eyes the documents arranged themselves again and again like a deck being reshuffled by patient hands. The forged grant of 1187, its seal pressed from a punch cut in the shape of a laurel wreath broken at the north—the same break he had seen in the Consulate's own crest above the customs house door. The original deeds beneath them, written in a clerk's careful copperplate, naming orchard families—Vance had counted those families himself, weeks ago, entering their names into columns without knowing what those names were worth. The letters. He could still see the ink-brown loop of the capital *T* in the oldest one, the way the writer's pen had torn slightly through cheap rag paper when he wrote the word *thieves*.

He had copied nothing. His hands had not gone near his notebook. That much of his oath remained intact, technically, immaculately—and the technicality disgusted him more than open treason would have, because what he had done instead was worse than copying. Copying was evidence, and evidence could be confiscated, burned, denied. What he had done was commit the archive to the one repository no censor could raid: the wet gray sponge of his own memory, which held everything now whether he willed it or not. He lay in the dark and recited it silently, the way a schoolboy recites conjugations. *Grant of the north terraces to the House of Corvane, executed under seal, year 1187. Counter-deed registered in the parish book of Saint Ellery's, witnessed by three names, the third smudged but legible as Marozia. Letter dated spring of the famine year, signed with the initial F., accusing the signatories of the settlement compact of—*

Of theft. Of theft dressed in law, and law dressed in seals, and seals cut by the grandfather of a typeface cutter who had spent thirty years waiting for someone worth trusting and had chosen, God help them all, a census taker.

Aurelio turned onto his side. Downstairs the chandler's tallow vats breathed their faint sweetness through the floorboards, and somewhere a cat argued with a gutter, and none of it mattered, because the whole city was built on a lie and he had spent nine years helping the lie keep its books tidy.

He got up before dawn, as he always did, and shaved, and put on his gray census coat with its dull brass buttons, and looked at himself in the mirror—a narrow face, ink-stained fingers, eyes that had learned to look at people without seeing them and were finding that skill suddenly, horribly difficult to perform. He buttoned the coat to the throat. It felt less like clothing than like a bandage wrapped around something bleeding.

*You took an oath,* he told the mirror.

*Yes,* said the man in the glass. *And the men who administer oaths are the men who forged the grants.*

There was no answer to that. There hadn't been an answer to it for three nights.

* * *

The banquet was held in the Hall of Meridian Trade, under a ceiling painted with allegories of Abundance—a fat-cheeked woman pouring grain from an overturned horn, gulls wheeling in a painted sky so blue it hurt to look at. Aurelio attended because attendance was required of senior enumerators when the Registry Commissioner summoned them, and the summons had come in a hand so elegant it seemed embarrassed by its own message.

The hall blazed with three hundred candles and smelled of roast quail, bay, hot wax, and the particular sweetness of too many cut flowers standing in too little water. Musicians played from a gallery, something lilting and courtly that nobody listened to. Servants moved through the crowd with trays of wine the color of garnets, and everywhere Aurelio looked there were the faces of power: magistrates in chains of office, shipping magnates with rings sunk into their fingers like coins into dough, the wives of both, glittering and bored.

Commissioner Delacroix found him near the third wine table. Delacroix was a broad, pink, comfortable man who had risen through the Registry by the twin arts of never making a mistake and never making a decision, and he gripped Aurelio's shoulder with the warmth of a man handling a well-made instrument.

"Vance! Good man. You've heard the news, of course."

"Which news, sir?"

Delacroix lowered his voice into a register of shared confidence that included, Aurelio noted, everyone within six feet. "The orchard question. Settled. Settled *finally*." He beamed. "New grants confirmed, boundaries regularized, tenancies converted to leasehold under Consulate supervision. Orderly. That's the word they're using tonight—*orderly*. You'll hear the First Secretary use it himself within the hour." He squeezed Aurelio's shoulder again. "Your district reports were exemplary, Vance. Clean arithmetic. The Commission notices thoroughness."

*Clean arithmetic.* Four hundred households counted in the orchard districts, their names entered, their holdings assessed, their rents recalculated—and every column of it now feeding the machinery that would take their land with a signature instead of a sword. His hands had done that. His neat, praised, exemplary hands.

"I only counted," Aurelio said. "I don't set policy."

"No one does, really. That's the beauty of it." Delacroix laughed at his own joke, lifted two glasses from a passing tray, and pressed one into Aurelio's hand. "Drink. Tonight we celebrate good order. Tomorrow—" he waved his glass at the ceiling, at the painted gulls, at all of it "—tomorrow we go back to work. As always."

Aurelio drank. The wine was excellent. That was the obscenity of it: everything about the evening was excellent. The quail arrived glazed with honey and thyme; the bread came in small round loaves still warm, their crusts scored with the Consulate's laurel; even the candlelight was flatteringly warm, turning every face at every table into a portrait of benevolence. And beneath the abundance, if you knew how to listen—and Aurelio had discovered, these past weeks, that he knew—the conversation ran on a single current. He caught fragments at every turn: *the tenant agitators*, *the dock rabble*, *a firm hand*, *before planting season*, *orderly settlement of the orchard question*. The phrase repeated itself through the hall like a bell struck in different rooms, always the same note.

An old magistrate with a voice like dry paper was telling a circle of younger men about the boundary commissions of his youth. "The trouble with the old orchard maps," he said, "is sentimentality. Every terrace drawn by hand, every hedge line disputed by some widow with a story about her grandmother. Chaos. The new surveys—" he tapped the table "—draw straight lines. Straight lines are honest lines."

Aurelio stood close enough to have heard it, close enough that silence would have been a statement, and so he smiled, and nodded, and said nothing, and hated himself with a clarity that was almost restful.

Later, near the tall windows overlooking the harbor, the First Secretary rose, and the hall hushed, and the musicians stopped mid-phrase. He was a spare man with white hair combed back like sea foam and a voice trained to carry without effort.

"Friends. Colleagues. Tonight we mark the close of a question that has troubled this city for a generation—the question of the orchards." Glasses came up around the room like a field of pale flowers. "There were those who said it could not be done peaceably. Those who clung to old papers, old grievances, old maps. But Vellamare is a city of the future, and the future does not read old papers. It writes new ones." A ripple of appreciative laughter. "To orderly settlement. To lawful boundaries. To the Consulate—and may every ledger in this city always balance."

"To the Consulate!" Three hundred voices. Three hundred glasses. The wine caught the candlelight and threw small red lights across the painted ceiling, so that for a moment the allegory of Abundance seemed to be raining blood instead of grain.

Aurelio raised his glass with the rest. He touched it to nothing, drank half, and stood very still while the cheering went on around him, feeling the oath rise in his throat like a bone that had to come up or choke him. *I swear to count truly.* Count truly. He had counted truly. That was the horror—he had been faithful to the letter of it all along, faithful as a knife is faithful to the hand that holds it. Nobody swears an oath to the truth. They swear it to the men who define it, and the men who defined his truth were toasting theft under a ceiling of painted gulls, and tomorrow their historians would write the future's only version, and the old papers—the real ones, the ones that smelled of a locked cabinet and thirty years of a cutter's fear—would rot unread, or burn unread, and the widows' grandmothers' hedges would be straight-lined off the earth forever.

He set his empty glass down on a servant's tray with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something he is afraid to throw.

Across the hall, Commissioner Delacroix caught his eye and raised his own glass again, warmly, approvingly. *Exemplary work, Vance.* Aurelio smiled back. He could feel the smile sitting on his face like a mask carved by someone else, and underneath it his jaw was so tight it ached.

He left before the dessert course. No one of consequence noticed. That, too, was instructive: a census taker could vanish from the Consulate's greatest celebration and the celebration did not dim by a single candle.

* * *

The streets outside were cold after the hall's furnace-glow, and fog was coming off the harbor in slow gray ropes, wrapping the lampposts, softening the warehouse fronts into suggestion. Aurelio walked without deciding to walk, and his feet decided for him: down Meridian Hill, past the shuttered moneylenders, across the canal bridge where the water muttered black under the stones, and up into the orchard district, where the air changed—the fog here carried bruised-apple sweetness from the winter-stripped trees, and the cobbles gave way in places to packed earth, and the houses leaned together like conspirators.

Every window was dark except one.

The print shop sat where it always sat, wedged between a rope-walk and a dyer's, its upper window glowing a low amber against the fog. Not lantern-light moving—steady, working light, the kind that meant someone bent over a bench at an hour when honest shops slept. Aurelio stopped across the lane, hands in his coat pockets, breath smoking, and watched that square of amber as though it were a fire he could warm his conscience at.

He thought of Paloma's hands over the type case, quick and sure, sorting metal the way other women sorted thread. He thought of Henrik's face when the cabinet stood open—old, exposed, relieved, terrified, all four at once, like a man surfacing after thirty years underwater. *I have waited a long time,* the cutter had said, *for someone who counts truly.* The words had seemed like courtesy then. Standing in the fog with the taste of Consulate wine still in his mouth and the First Secretary's toast still ringing in his ears, Aurelio understood that they had been something else entirely. They had been a sentence passed. *Count truly.* He had been given the truth and told, in effect, to do his job—and his job, done truly, had only one possible product.

He could go home. He could file his returns, draw his salary, attend the next banquet, grow old, and let the victors write their history over the graves of people whose names he alone, perhaps, had ever entered honestly into any record. That was the road his oath paved. It was paved and lamp-lit and led downhill all the way, and every respectable man in the Hall of Meridian Trade was walking it comfortably, glasses in hand.

In the amber window above the print shop, the light shifted—a shadow crossing it, slender, unhurried, pausing. Whether she saw him in the fog below he could not tell. The shadow stayed a moment, then moved on.

Aurelio stood in the cold until his feet ached, doing the arithmetic he had refused to do for three nights. If Sarn's historians came—if the presses were requisitioned, if the archives of the city were "regularized" the way the orchard boundaries had been regularized—then the only copy of the truth in all of Vellamare would live behind a false wall in a shop full of flammable paper, kept by an old man and his daughter and, now, by the memory of a government clerk who had toasted the thieves with their own wine.

Two repositories. One made of paper and courage. One made of nothing but a tired man's memory and whatever he chose, starting tonight, to become.

The amber light in the loft window steadied, and burned on.

Aurelio pulled his collar up against the fog, crossed the lane, and knocked softly on the print shop door—once, twice—listening to the sound travel up through the sleeping building like a coin dropped down a well, while somewhere above him the footsteps in the loft stopped, and held, and began, carefully, to descend.
