## 9. Cutting the First Letter

The shop slept the way a held breath sleeps.

Paloma knew every sound of its resting: the settling tick of the type cases as the night cooled, the sigh of damp paper in the drying loft, the groan of the old floorbeam above the press that her father swore was the building remembering its own weight. She had grown up inside that breathing. Other children learned to sleep through church bells or gulls; she had learned to sleep through silence itself, the particular silence of a household keeping secrets.

Tonight she did not sleep in it. Tonight she moved through it, barefoot on the cold boards, with a candle cupped in her hand like something stolen.

Her father's door was shut. Beneath it, no light. He had gone to bed early, which he did now more often than he admitted—going to bed early and lying awake, she suspected, listening to the same silences she listened to. Since the night he had shown them the cabinet, since the letters naming thieves in high places had lain open on his workbench like a wound finally unlaced, Henrik Eriksen had aged in a way that had nothing to do with years. It was the aging of a man carrying something too heavy for one spine.

*Then put it down,* she wanted to tell him. *Let me carry half.*

But he would not. And so she would not ask.

She passed through the workroom, where the presses stood under their dust sheets like furniture in a house of mourning, and climbed the narrow stair to the type loft. The lantern was already there—she had left it that afternoon, trimmed and filled, its chimney polished, because she had known, the way you know a tooth will ache before it does, that tonight would be the night.

The archive lay in the false wall behind the standing type. She had helped her father build that wall herself, three years ago, when the first rumors of Sarn's commission reached Vellamare—a court historian coming to write the orchards out of existence in real time. She had mixed the mortar of it from plaster and printer's ink, so that even if someone broke the wall open they would find nothing but what looked like decades of accumulated grime. Her father had called it cleverness. Paloma had understood, even then, that it was fear wearing cleverness's coat.

Now she pulled the loose case aside and drew out the folder she had marked with a slip of blue thread.

Inside were the documents. The forged grants with the Consulate's seal pressed fat and confident into the wax, grants that had turned forty families' orchards into state property with a stroke and a stamp. And beneath them, older, softer at the folds, the true deeds—the ones Henrik's grandfather, a man named not Eriksen but Eriksson-Vale, had cut the plates to counterfeit and then, in some long midnight of conscience, had hidden instead of used. The confession letter. The punch-cut seal that should never have existed, wrapped now in oilcloth like a relic, which is exactly what it was.

Paloma spread the confession page flat on the composing table and weighted its corners with quoins. Then she sat down before the blank matrix of her own making and picked up the graver.

The first letter she chose to cut was not the first letter of the document. It was not even part of the text proper. It was the small ornamental initial that would head the pamphlet—a lantern, if she could manage it, a lantern with light spilling from its panes—and she chose it deliberately, because the first mark a cutter makes sets the temper of everything after. Her father had taught her that before she could write. *The graver remembers your mood,* he said. *Cut angry and the letter comes out angry. Cut frightened and it comes out thin, apologetic, a letter that wants forgiveness.* Cut steady, he said. Cut like the truth doesn't care whether anyone likes it.

So she breathed until her hands went quiet, and she set the point of the graver against the end-grain boxwood, and she began.

The sound was very small in the great dark loft: a whispering, a fine sand-fall whisper, steel through wood. Outside, Vellamare breathed its harbor breath—rigging clinking in the basin, a drunk singing two streets over, somewhere far off the boots of the night watch going by with their lanterns and their boredom. Any of those sounds could cover hers. None of them would save her if the watch happened to look up at the loft window and see a flame moving where a flame had no business being.

She had thought about that. She had built the shield for the lantern out of a biscuit tin pierced with pinholes, so that from the street the window showed only darkness, only a room full of sleeping type. But she left the shield off tonight. Let the light show. Some stubborn, rising thing in her wanted it to show.

The lantern initial grew under her graver by slow accretion, chip by chip, each cut removing exactly enough and not a hair more. Wood gave up its shape reluctantly; that was why boxwood was precious, why a good punch took days. There was no hurrying a letter into being. You could only persuade it, patiently, out of everything it wasn't yet.

Her mind, freed by the work's rhythm, went where it always went these days: to Aurelio Vance, census man, oath-keeper, the government's own counting fingers, who had stood in this loft four nights ago with his sleeves rolled and ink to his second knuckle and set type beside her with the careful devotion of a man learning a new language. His fingers had brushed hers reaching for the same piece of ten-point. Neither of them had moved away quickly. Her father had watched from the stair and said nothing, and the nothing had been enormous.

Aurelio had memorized pages. She'd seen him do it—eyes moving down the confession letter once, twice, lips still, and then later reciting passages back to her in the dark of the market lane as though testing whether the words survived outside the cabinet. They did survive. That was the thing she kept returning to, the thing that had hardened in her like setting resin: words survived. In memory, in type, in ink. Everything else—the deeds themselves, the wax seals, the whole paper weight of the past—could be burned in an afternoon. Victor Sarn had requisitioned three presses that week alone. The Consulate was not waiting. Why should they?

*Because printing means death,* her father said. Had said. Would say again tonight, when he caught her. She was counting on him catching her.

The lantern's last pane came free at some hour she didn't track, and she held the little block up to the flame and blew the shavings from it, and there it was: light cut out of darkness, negative made positive, a lantern rendered in the space where wood had refused to be. Her chest ached with something that was not quite pride and not quite terror and was, she understood, simply resolve—the physical sensation of it, sitting behind the sternum like a swallowed coal.

She began the second block: the opening line of the confession, set large.

*I, Anders Eriksson-Vale, cutter of letters, do set down in my own hand what I did, and for whom, and what was taken thereby.*

She worked. The candle guttered. Somewhere in the small hours a rain began, soft and businesslike, silvering the loft windows and giving her cover for the sounds she no longer bothered to muffle. Her shoulders burned. A blister rose on her right middle finger and she wrapped it with thread and went on. This was the craft her father had given her, all those years of grinding punches and sorting cases, all that training in invisibility—and invisibility, it turned out, had been apprenticeship all along. She had learned to move unseen so that one day she could make unseen things move.

* * *

He caught her on the third night.

She heard him on the stairs—his weight, his hesitation, the pause at the seventh step where the board complained—and she did not stop cutting. There seemed no point anymore in pretending. When he came into the loft, holding his own candle, and saw the lantern-light of her shielded lamp, the shavings drifted across the floor like strange snow, and the half-formed letters ranked on the table, he stood very still for a long moment.

"You've been at this three nights," he said. Not a question. Fathers know.

"Four," Paloma said. "You slept through the first."

Henrik crossed the loft slowly, the way he approached anything that might bite, and looked down at the blocks. The lantern initial. The opening lines of his grandfather's confession, emerging letter by letter from the boxwood in Paloma's own hand—a hand recognizably descended from his, but harder somehow, denser, cut with less ornament and less mercy.

"Those are for the press," he said.

"They're for the press."

"Paloma." He said her name the way other men said prayers or curses. He set his candle down and took the graver from the table—not snatching it, just lifting it, the way you lift a blade from between arguing friends—and turned it over in his fingers as if checking it for flaws. "Do you know what Sarn's men did to the Aldobrandi press? Not confiscated. Not sealed. They took it apart bolt by bolt in the street and threw the pieces in the harbor, and then they took Matteo Aldobrandi's right hand to the surgeon, and the surgeon amputated two fingers, and everyone understands it was a message." He looked at her, and in the candlelight she saw how deep the lines around his eyes had gone. "Printing this means death. Not maybe. Not possibly. Means."

"I know."

"Do you?" His voice cracked upward. "Your mother died of a fever, thank God, in a bed, with a priest, with me holding her hand. That is the only death anyone in this family has ever been permitted, and you want to spend yours on—" he gestured at the blocks, the papers, the whole buried inheritance—"on paper. On old grievances. On a dead man's guilt."

"Not on paper." Paloma stood. She was not tall; her father loomed over most rooms. But she had spent her life among presses and she knew about force, about how a small pressure applied exactly right could move tons of iron. She stepped close and laid her hand flat on the confession page, on her grandfather's handwriting. "On the record, Father. There's a difference. Paper burns. You taught me that—you burned papers in the yard for years, I watched the ash come down like grey snow. Every document in this cabinet can be gone by morning if one soldier gets curious. But type survives. Ink survives. A thousand copies scattered through ten thousand hands cannot be unprinted. You told me that yourself. You told me that's why we keep the presses oiled."

"To keep the trade alive. To pass inspection. Not to—"

"Not to use them. Yes. And look what keeping them has bought us." She swept her arm toward the window, toward the city beyond the rain. "Sarn is writing the history right now. Right now, tonight, some clerk is fair-copying the official grants with our grandfather's forged seal on them, and in five years no one alive will remember there was ever another version. The tenants will be squatters in their own grandparents' orchards. Forever. Because we were careful." The word came out of her like a stone spat from the mouth. "Careful, Father. Thirty years of careful. And what did careful save? Nothing. It saved nothing and it cost us everything—we lived our whole lives folded up inside a wall of standing type, hiding, and the theft went on anyway. The orchards went anyway. Careful was just a slower way of losing."

Henrik had gone very pale. His hand, still holding her graver, trembled slightly, and she saw him notice it and hate it.

"A record no one reads is just kindling," Paloma said, quieter now. "That's all this archive is, the way we keep it. Kindling for the fire that will burn it. Sarn won't even have to search. He'll requisition the shop, seal the walls, and one day a cart will take the whole false wall to a courtyard and there'll be smoke, and that will be that. Thirty years of truth, warmed nobody, fed nothing, and gone." She took the graver gently back from his fingers. "Unless it goes into type. Unless it goes onto a press. Unless it goes out into the city in enough copies that burning becomes a story they have to deny rather than a thing they can do."

"And you'll be dead," Henrik whispered. "And I'll be dead. And the record will survive us, is that it? Is that the arithmetic?"

"It's not arithmetic." She met his eyes. "It's inheritance. Grandfather cut the lie. Somebody in this family has to cut the correction. I'm the only cutter left."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The rain filled the silence, drumming soft on the roof slates, running in bright threads down the loft glass. Downstairs, faintly, the old floorbeam groaned its nightly complaint.

Henrik looked at the blocks a long time. Then he did something she had not expected: he pulled her father's chair—her grandfather's chair, the one worn to the shape of three generations of backs—to the table, and sat down in it heavily, and picked up the second graver from the rack.

"The 'V' in Vale," he said. "Grandfather cut it with a spur on the left stem. Idiosyncratic. Any examiner who knows the period will look for it." He turned the block of boxwood over, finding its grain, and Paloma saw that his hands had stopped trembling. "If we're forging authenticity, girl, we'll do it properly."

Paloma sat down beside him. Something loosened in her chest that she hadn't known was knotted—grief and relief tangled together, indistinguishable.

"We'll need more boxwood," she said.

"There's a billet in the cellar I've been saving thirty years," Henrik said dryly. "I told myself it was for retirement pieces. Ornamental alphabets. A nice quiet old age." He set the graver's point to the wood. "God forgive us both. We start with the seal. If we print the confession without reproducing the crest, they'll call it forgery. We reproduce it exactly, from the original punch, and they'll have to call it evidence."

They worked until the rain stopped and the sky behind the loft windows went from black to the bruised grey that precedes the gulls. At some point Henrik went down and came back with bread and hard cheese and a jug of small beer, and they ate without stopping work, crumbs falling among the shavings, and it was the most companionable hour Paloma could remember in years—two cutters bent over the same table, the secret finally shared at the level of their hands.

When the light came strong enough to see dust in the air, Henrik stood, stretched until his spine cracked, and surveyed the night's work: the finished lantern initial, the opening lines, and between them, fresh-cut and perfect, the Consulate's crest reproduced from the original punch in all its fat, official authority—the emblem of the state, cut by its enemies, ready to testify against it.

Henrik studied it with a craftsman's bleak satisfaction.

"It's good work," he said. "It would convince me." Then his face changed, and Paloma saw the fear come back into it—but different now, aimed outward instead of in. "There's one problem left, and it isn't type. It's paper. A thousand copies needs a thousand sheets, and no paper merchant in Vellamare sells that quantity to a print shop without the purchase going into a ledger, and every ledger in this city ends up on Sarn's desk within the week. We can cut the letters. We cannot feed them."

Paloma was already shaking her head. "We don't need to buy paper. The census office buys it by the bale—rag stock, good weight, for the district returns. Aurelio told me they've over-ordered this quarter; there's a storeroom at the customs annex with more blank paper than the returns will use in a year, and half of it's scheduled to be pulped as surplus." She paused, hearing how it sounded, and made herself say the rest plainly. "I'm going to ask him."

Henrik looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the first gulls were crying over the basin, and the city was waking up to a day that did not yet know what had been cut in it overnight.

"You're going to ask a sworn officer of the Consulate," her father said slowly, "to steal government paper."

"I'm going to ask Aurelio," Paloma said, wrapping her blistered finger, "to choose."

Down in the street below, passing the shuttered shop on his early round, whistling softly, entirely unaware, came the footsteps of the census man himself—and Paloma blew out the lantern before she could be seen smiling.
