## 10. The Historian Arrives

The historian came to Vellamare on a morning so gray the harbor seemed to have been blotted out with ink, as though someone had already begun drafting the official version of the city and found it easier to start from nothing.

Aurelio Vance learned of the arrival the way he learned most things—through paper. He was at his desk in the census office, transcribing household counts into fair copy, when a clerk from the Procurator's wing came down the stairs two at a time and slapped a broadsheet against the slanted light of the window.

"Read it and weep, Vance. The Consulate's sending us its memory."

The broadsheet bore the Consulate's crest—the orchard tree split by a straight road—and beneath it, in type so fine it looked engraved:

*By order of the Honorable College, Victor Sarn, Historiographer to the Consulate, is empowered to compile, in situ, the definitive record of events pertaining to the Vellamare districts. All civic offices shall render him every assistance. All commercial presses shall be placed at his disposal.*

Aurelio read the last line twice. *All commercial presses.* He thought of the Eriksen shop with its shutters half-closed, the smell of lead and oil that clung to the street outside like weather, Paloma's hands moving through the type cases with the certainty of someone counting her own heartbeats. He thought of what he had memorized in that back room—the grants with their impossible signatures, the deeds older than the Consulate itself, the letters naming thieves by their first names.

"All commercial presses," the clerk repeated, grinning. "You know what that means? No more pamphlets about grain prices. No more ballads. Sarn's going to write us a history where everything that happened, happened politely."

"Where is he lodging?" Aurelio asked, because it was the kind of question a diligent census man would ask.

"The Gilded Anchor. He arrived on the *Meridian*, first-class cabin, six trunks and a secretary." The clerk leaned in, lowering his voice with relish. "They say he wrote the account of the Saltmarsh Rebellion. You know how that one ends? With the marsh dwellers grateful for drainage."

Aurelio knew how it ended. Everyone knew how it ended, because there was no other version left to know.

He went back to his transcription. His pen moved; his mind did not. In the margin of his fair copy, without quite deciding to, he drew a small square—a forme, locked and waiting—and then scratched it out so hard the nib tore the paper.

* * *

He told himself he was walking the long way home. The long way home passed the print shop, which was not an excuse but had become one through repetition, the way a path becomes a road becomes a street.

It was late afternoon, and the light lay along the orchard district at a low angle, setting fire to the last leaves. The trees here were old—older than the tenancy laws, older than the redistricting, older, some said, than the Consulate—and they stood in the yards of tenant houses like grandparents who refused to be introduced properly. Apples had begun to fall. They rolled into the gutters and no one collected them, because under the new boundaries the fruit belonged to men who had never walked these streets, and picking it had become, by degrees and decrees, a small crime.

At the corner of Binder's Row, the Eriksen shop stood with its shutters drawn to exactly the width they always were: open enough to be honest, closed enough to keep the light off the paper stock. But today there was something different. A notice had been nailed to the doorpost, crisp and official, its wax seal still glossy.

Aurelio crossed the street and read it.

*Notice of Requisition. By authority of Victor Sarn, Historiographer to the Consulate, the press and premises herein are required for state service effective immediately. All matrices, type, and apparatus to remain on premises. Access restricted.*

Below it, someone had already scrawled a word in charcoal and smeared it halfway out again. Aurelio could still read it: *Thieves.*

He stood there long enough that the door opened—not the shop door but the narrow side door, the one that led up to the living quarters—and Paloma stepped out onto the stoop with a pail in one hand, as if she had been about to fetch water and had simply paused. She saw him. She did not pretend otherwise, which was itself a change; three months ago she would have found something urgent to do with her eyes.

"They came at noon," she said quietly. "Two clerks and a soldier. They measured the press."

"Measured it?"

"Wrote down its dimensions. Counted my father's type cases. Asked him how many formes he could set in a day." Her mouth tightened around the word *formes*, and Aurelio understood that she was telling him this in a language only the two of them spoke now, a language built out of the things they could not say aloud on a public stoop. "He answered them very politely. My father can be very polite when he wants to frighten someone."

"Did they take anything?"

"Nothing. That's what worries him." She glanced up and down Binder's Row, where a fishwife was arguing with a porter and nobody was listening to anybody else. "A requisition that doesn't take isn't a requisition. It's a claim. They've put their hand on the shop without lifting anything, so that when they want to lift, no one can say it wasn't theirs already."

Aurelio looked at the notice again. The seal was the split-tree crest, pressed deep, the wax fat and confident. He had seen that same crest on the forged land grants in Henrik's cabinet—he had held the original deeds beside them and watched the crests agree with each other perfectly, because of course they agreed; the same hand had cut both seals across three generations. Somewhere behind that door was a steel punch that could make the government's own signature tell the truth or the lie, whichever the holder wished.

And now the government had claimed the shop that held it.

"When does the historian arrive at the shop?" he asked.

"Thursday. To inspect." Paloma set the pail down on the stoop, deliberately, the way her father set down a heavy forme. "Aurelio. What does a court historian do with a commercial press?"

Aurelio thought of the clerk's grin, of the Saltmarsh account, of a thousand pamphlets pulped and a single official narrative bound in calfskin and distributed to every school in the provinces. What did a historian do with presses? A historian silenced them. Or worse—a historian used them, set his own type in another man's shop, and made the machines complicit.

"He writes," Aurelio said. "That's the trouble. Everything he writes will come off presses like yours, in your city, with your name on the door. So that later, when anyone asks where the true account was printed—" he gestured at the notice, "—the answer will be *here*. It will look like the city consented."

Paloma was quiet. Down the row, the fishwife won her argument and the porter shrugged and life went on being ordinary, which suddenly seemed like a fragile, temporary condition, like ice before thaw.

"He'll want to meet the census takers too," she said at last. "Sarn. He'll need our numbers for his book. Births, households, holdings." She looked at him then, fully, and there was something in her face that was not fear exactly—it was the expression she wore over the type case when her hands went still. "Will you give them to him?"

"I gave them to the Consulate already. That's my oath."

"And after the oath?"

The question hung between them in the smell of apples and ink. Aurelio found he could not answer it on a public street, in daylight, with a requisition nailed four feet away. He picked up the pail and handed it back to her, and the brief weight of it passing between their hands felt like a promise neither of them had dared to draft.

"After the oath," he said, "there's a man."

* * *

Victor Sarn received the civic officers of Vellamare on Friday evening in a rented salon at the Gilded Anchor, and Aurelio, summoned among them as keeper of the district returns, understood within the first minute that he was in the presence of the most dangerous kind of official: one who genuinely loved his work.

Sarn was perhaps fifty-five, spare and silvered, dressed well but not richly, with the deliberate modesty of a man who wanted his intelligence to be the loudest thing in the room. He had a habit of tilting his head when you spoke, as if your words were specimens he was pinning. Around him, his secretaries had already transformed the salon into something between an office and an archive—maps pinned to the walls, folios stacked in labeled columns, a long table laid out like a dissecting board with the city spread across it in documents.

"Gentlemen." Sarn's voice was mild, unhurried, almost warm. "Thank you for coming. I'll be brief, because I know you all have duties, and duties are what I've come to honor." He smiled at the assembled officers—census men, tax assessors, the harbormaster's deputy—and Aurelio watched each of them soften a little, the way wood softens near steam. "Vellamare is about to pass through a difficult season. There is talk in certain quarters—I will not insult you by pretending I haven't heard it—of unrest. Of grievances. When such seasons pass, cities are always left with two things: what actually happened, and what people remember happening. My task is to make sure those two things are the same thing."

*Or,* Aurelio thought, *to decide which of them gets to survive.*

"To that end, I require accuracy." Sarn touched the stack of folios nearest him. "Master Vance. You conduct the household returns for the orchard districts."

"I do."

"Good work. I've read your fair copies. Meticulous." The compliment landed like a hand on the shoulder—warm, proprietary, a mark of ownership. "Meticulous men are the backbone of honest history. I may have questions for you. Small ones. Dates, names, boundary lines."

Something moved behind Aurelio's ribs at the phrase *boundary lines*, slow and cold, like a fish turning in deep water.

"The boundaries were redrawn last year," Aurelio said carefully. "My records reflect the current survey."

"Yes. The current survey." Sarn tilted his head. "Though I'm told the old orchard families keep their own notions of where the lines run. Sentimental geography." He said it lightly, almost fondly, the way one speaks of children who believe in the moon's face. "Sentiment makes poor borders but excellent fuel. I'd like to understand it while it's still only sentiment."

No one else in the room heard anything wrong in that sentence. Aurelio heard it the way one hears a floorboard creak overhead at night—with total attention, and no way to unhear it.

The meeting wound on: schedules, requisitions, the harbormaster's deputy promising cooperation with shipping manifests. At the door, as the officers filed out, Sarn caught Aurelio's sleeve—gently, courteously, a gesture any observer would read as collegial.

"A moment, Master Vance." He lowered his voice. Up close, his eyes were pale gray and very patient. "The Eriksen print shop. You know it? Your route passes it, I believe."

"It's on my circuit, yes."

"A family business. Three generations, the registers say. Remarkable continuity, in a city where everything else keeps changing hands." Sarn smiled. "Old Henrik Eriksen cut type for my predecessor's commission, years ago. Fine work. I'm told the family keeps excellent archives. Printers usually do—they're the only tradesmen who understand that paper outlives everyone." He released Aurelio's sleeve and stepped back. "When I visit Thursday, it would ease matters if the shop's neighbors understood the requisition is an honor, not a punishment. A word from a trusted census man carries further than a posted notice. People trust the man who counted them."

*I am the man who counted them,* Aurelio thought. *I counted their hearths, their children, their dead. And you would spend that trust like coin you found on the ground.*

"I'll consider what I can say," Aurelio replied. "People are frightened just now. Words land differently."

"Of course." Sarn's smile did not change, but something in it acquired depth, the way water acquires depth. "Frightened people are why I'm here, Master Vance. History is a lantern. Held steadily, it calms everyone." He inclined his head. "Held carelessly, it shows them more than they wished to see. Good evening."

* * *

Aurelio did not go home. He walked, and the walk took him, as walks had begun to do, toward Binder's Row—but the shop was dark when he arrived, shutters drawn full for the first time he had ever seen, and he stood across the street in the falling dark feeling the shape of what was coming close around him like a change in pressure.

Thursday. Four days. In four days, Victor Sarn would stand inside that shop with his measuring eye and his patient voice, and he would see the false wall of standing type—or he would not—and he would count the type cases—or he would ask Henrik to—and somewhere in that room, in the space between a courteous question and a careful answer, the whole hidden archive would either stay buried or come into the light, and if it came into the light it would burn, along with the press that might have printed it, along with whatever the Consulate decided to call the burning.

Aurelio took out his notebook, out of habit, and stood writing under a streetlamp while moths struck the glass above him. Not the archive—he would never write the archive—but the schedule, the numbers, the shape of the danger, translated into the only cipher available to him: bureaucratic fact.

*Requisition effective immediately. Inspection Thursday. Presses at historian's disposal. All commercial presses.*

He stopped. Read the last clause again. *All commercial presses.*

Not one shop. Every shop. Which meant that whatever the Eriksens chose to do—whatever pages Paloma might already be dreaming of cutting in the nights ahead—there would soon be nowhere in Vellamare to print it except under Sarn's own eye. The clock wasn't ticking anymore. It had been ticking since noon, when two clerks measured a press and wrote down its dimensions, and none of them had known it.

A shutter shifted across the street. Just slightly—one leaf of the print shop's closed shutters easing inward, the way an eyelid eases. Behind it, faintly, a lamp moved. Then a second lamp, lower down, toward the rear of the building, where the type loft window faced the yard.

Someone inside was awake. Someone inside was working.

Aurelio closed his notebook and stood a moment longer in the moth-whirring dark, a government man loitering outside a requisitioned press, committing to memory the exact hour when everything began to run out.
