## 11. Searches and Small Mercies

The knock came at the hour when the shop was at its most innocent.

Paloma had arranged it so without meaning to—without knowing she would need to. Morning was the honest hour of a print shop: the smell of ink and lampblack still thick from the night's work, formes broken down or waiting, the great hand press open and gleaming like something confessed. No secrets could live in daylight. She had believed that once.

Three knocks, spaced like a man tapping a wall to hear where the hollow places were.

She looked at her father across the composing stone. Henrik Eriksen did not stop what he was doing—a quoin key turning in his blunt fingers, tightening a lockup that needed no tightening—but his eyes went pale as wet paper. In thirty years of careful living he had rehearsed this moment the way other men rehearsed their own funerals, and now it stood in the doorway of his shop wearing a sergeant's braid.

"Paloma." His voice was low and even. "The door."

She opened it to grey light and four soldiers in Consulate blue, and behind them, standing slightly apart as though he wished the street would take him back, Aurelio Vance.

He wore his census coat—the plain grey of the Enumeration Office—and he carried his ledger under one arm like a shield he did not quite believe in. Their eyes met for half a heartbeat. She saw everything in that half-heartbeat: the apology before any crime had been committed, the terror he was holding very still, the question *will you trust me* that he had no right to ask and asked anyway.

"Master Eriksen." The sergeant stepped forward. He was a broad man with a face like a closed ledger himself, all columns and no commentary, and his voice had the flat courtesy of orders being read aloud rather than felt. "Sergeant Doss, Third Garrison. By requisition of the Historian's Office, all commercial presses in the Orchard District are subject to inspection. We're looking for seditious matter. Unlicensed type. You'll open your premises."

"Of course." Henrik's voice was warm, unhurried, the voice of a man interrupted at bread-making. "Gentlemen, mind the floor by the press—there's oil there that will ruin good boots."

It was beautiful, Paloma thought later. It was terrible. Her father moved through the shop like a host at a feast, naming every object before the soldiers could name it themselves: *the galley, the imposing stone, the paper stock, my grandfather's press, older than the Consulate itself.* Naming was a kind of owning. Owning was a kind of innocence. And all the while the archive sat six feet behind him, behind a false wall built of standing type.

Her father had built the wall two winters ago, and only three people alive knew it was not a wall. From the outside it was nothing but a shelf of locked cases, cases full of metal that had been set and never distributed—pages upon pages of some old book, dead language, dead project, the sort of thing every print shop accumulates like scar tissue. The cases were heavy. The cases were real. Behind them, a panel swung on hinges cut into the frame, and behind the panel lay thirty years of truth: forged land grants with the Consulate's own seal, original deeds in tenant hands' names, letters that named thieves as founders.

Aurelio knew. That was the arithmetic she could not escape. Aurelio had stood in the loft with her fingers touching his over the composing stick, had read the first proof sheet with his own eyes, had carried its memory home in his skull like contraband. If he chose, in the next ten minutes, to be the man his uniform said he was, they would all hang from the customs house beam before the week was out.

She watched him not choose it. She watched him do something worse and better: nothing. He simply stood by the door with his ledger, a grey man in a grey coat, part of the furniture of the state.

Doss's men began badly and improved. The young one—nineteen at most, cheeks still soft—went straight for the type cabinets and started pulling drawers like a child raiding a sweet stall.

"Careful, boy," Henrik said mildly. "That drawer is Minion, sorted by frequency. You'll give me a week's work untangling it, and I'll charge the garrison for my time."

The soldier looked at Doss. Doss looked bored. "Sort it back," he said, and the boy retreated as though the drawer had bitten him.

They worked outward from the press. Paloma kept her hands busy because her hands had been trained to be busy—invisibility was a craft, her father said, and crafts were practiced. She sorted type. She wiped ink from the press bed. She let the soldiers see her doing the ordinary labor of an ordinary daughter, and she kept her breath even by counting sorts in her head: *e, t, a, o, i, n.* The most common letters lived nearest the compositor's thumb. There was a mercy in that, too—if you had to reach blindly for comfort, comfort should be close at hand.

One of the soldiers found the cabinet where the punch-cut seal had once slept, before Paloma had moved it into the false wall with the rest. He rapped his knuckles along its side.

"What's behind these?"

"Cases," Henrik said. "Standing type. An old folio history we set twenty years ago and never sold. Dead stock. Every shop has some."

"Open one."

"Break the lock if you like. But you'll be the one who sets those pages right again." Henrik smiled, and it was a marvel to Paloma, that smile—it reached his eyes, it crinkled them with genuine amusement, and underneath it her father's heart must have been going like a trip-hammer. "Metal remembers, Sergeant. Type is the most honest material in the world. It holds whatever was set in it, forever, until someone takes it apart letter by letter."

Something flickered in Doss's face—not suspicion exactly, but the discomfort of a man who suspects he is being lectured and cannot prove it. "I don't care about your sermons. Open it."

Henrik produced the key from the chain at his waist—he had worn it there for years, in plain sight, the best hiding place in Vellamare—and unlocked the end case. The soldier pulled out a chase of standing type, dense as a brick, lines of some dead argument about grain tariffs. He turned it over. He put it back.

"Dead stock," he agreed, wiping his hands on his trousers as though the metal had dirtied them.

Paloma breathed again.

But Doss was methodical; that was the trouble with him. Bored men miss things; thorough men find them eventually, even behind walls of lead. He walked the perimeter of the shop with his knuckles out, tapping. Paloma heard the rhythm of it—plaster, plaster, plaster, wood—and felt the whole world narrow to the width of her father's false panel. Tap. Tap. Tap.

At the wall, the sound changed.

Not much. A stranger would have heard nothing. But Paloma had grown up inside that frame; she knew its voice the way she knew her father's. Where the panel began, the tap went hollow by a hair, by the thickness of a lie.

Doss heard it. She watched him hear it. His knuckles stopped. He tapped again, twice, deliberately, and tilted his head like a man listening to a clock.

"Hollow here," he said. "What's behind the shelving?"

"Cinder fill." Henrik did not miss a step. He was at the wash-up sink, cleaning a roller, his back half-turned—an old man at his chores. "This quarter was a warehouse row before it was anything else. The whole party wall is filled with rubble. Knock anywhere along it."

Doss knocked anywhere along it. Plaster, plaster, plaster. Hollow. Plaster. Hollow. The cinder story held everywhere except where it mattered, and Paloma understood suddenly that her father had designed even that—that the hollow places were scattered like decoys, that a wall which sounded solid everywhere would have drawn attention precisely where it was silent.

Still Doss stood there. Still his hand rested against the shelving, palm flat, as though he could feel the truth through the wood by touch alone.

"Sergeant." Aurelio's voice, from the doorway. It was the first word he had spoken since entering, and it came out dry, official, faintly apologetic—the voice of a clerk correcting an error in a form. "If I might save you some trouble."

Doss turned. Everyone turned. Paloma kept sorting type and gave Aurelio nothing, not even the edge of her eye, because whatever happened next had to look like nothing.

"The Enumeration Office surveyed this property in the spring census." Aurelio opened his ledger with the crisp movements of a man whose paperwork has never once been questioned. "Structure of record: print works, ground floor plus type loft, single-party construction, party walls rubble-filled per the district survey of—" he turned a page, and Paloma marveled at the page-turn, at the small theatrical machinery of bureaucracy deployed like a barricade— "the year of the Harbor Works. The hollow readings will be consistent throughout. Old buildings ring like bells. If you'd like, I can note your structural objection in my report, and the Office can send an engineer."

An engineer. The word dropped into the room like ice into water. An engineer meant a second visit, a warrant, a delay—and also a chance for the whole fragile arrangement to crack wide open. Paloma saw Doss weigh it and saw what tipped the scale: not fear of the engineer, but contempt for the census man. Nobody wanted extra work. Nobody wanted to be the sergeant who delayed the Historian's schedule over a hollow wall in a printer's shop full of nothing but honest lead.

"You surveyed this property?" Doss said.

"I survey forty properties a day, Sergeant." Aurelio's shrug was perfect—tired, precise, the shrug of a man who counted heads and cared for nothing else. "I couldn't tell you what's behind anyone's walls. I count who sleeps inside them. That's all my office does."

*That's all my office does.* Paloma set the sentence down somewhere permanent in herself, next to her memory of his fingers near hers over the composing stick. It was a betrayal of his uniform, small and complete, and there would be no taking it back—not ever, not if he lived to be ninety. A man who lied for you once owned you the rest of your life, or belonged to you; she was not yet sure which way it went.

Doss grunted. He took his palm off the shelving.

And then, because the gods of printers love irony, the youngest soldier sneezed.

It was the paper dust. Fine as flour, it hung in the morning light of the loft ladder, and the boy inhaled it and sneezed with the violence of youth, and the sneeze startled him backward into the stack of fresh printed sheets Paloma had left drying on the rack. They went down in a soft white avalanche—broadside announcements, licensed ones, a funeral notice for a harbor alderman, utterly innocent—and the boy floundered among them apologizing while Doss snapped at him and Henrik said *leave it, leave it, boy, they were drying anyway,* and the moment broke like a rope under load.

For one instant, in the middle of the fluttering paper, Aurelio caught her eye again. Something almost human crossed his official face—almost a smile, strangled at birth—and Paloma had to turn away and pretend to scold the soldier, because if she laughed now she would laugh the way people laugh at funerals, and it would not stop.

* * *

They were gone within the hour. Doss left a receipt for the inspection, signed against the Historian's Office requisition number, and a warning that they would return, and a look at Henrik that promised he personally intended to find out what rang hollow in this shop if it was the last thing he did on earth. The door closed behind the blue coats. The street sounds resumed—carts, gulls, the distant strike of the dockworkers' hammers, which had taken up a new rhythm lately, slower, angrier, like a heart deciding something.

Paloma waited until the footsteps faded entirely. Then she walked to the front door and shot both bolts, and hung the CLOSED FOR REPAIRS board in the window, and stood with her forehead against the cool glass watching the empty street.

Behind her, she heard her father sit down. Not in a chair—in the compositor's stool, abruptly, the way a man sits when his legs have been holding more than legs hold.

"He tapped the wall," Henrik said. "Twice. Deliberately."

"He'll come back," Paloma said. She did not turn around. "That look wasn't curiosity. Curiosity goes away. That was a debt he thinks we owe him."

"And yet." Her father exhaled, long and shaking. "And yet we are still standing in our shop, and the wall still keeps its secret, and it is because—" he stopped.

Paloma turned. Aurelio had not moved from the doorway. He stood with his ledger still under his arm, and now that the audience was gone, the performance fell off him like a coat off a hook: his shoulders dropped, his face went grey and young, and his hand, when he lifted it to push hair from his forehead, was not quite steady.

"You lied to them," Henrik said quietly.

"Yes."

"You know what that costs. A census man who misdirects a garrison search—if Sarn hears of it, if Doss mentions the clerk who was too helpful—"

"I know what it costs." Aurelio's voice cracked on the last word and he let it. Perhaps that was the bravest thing, Paloma thought: he didn't rebuild the smooth surface. He let them see the crack, because they deserved to see it, because a lie offered smoothly would have been worthless. "I've known since the banquet, probably. Since before. Sir—I stood in that doorway and listened to that sergeant tap your wall, and I had a ledger full of ways to stop him, and I used every one, and I would use them again tomorrow. So either I'm a traitor now, or I finally stopped being one. I've decided it's the second. You don't have to decide it for me."

The shop was silent. Somewhere above them, in the loft, a pigeon shifted its weight on the skylight, and a fine sift of dust came down through the lamplit air.

Henrik rose from the stool. He was not a tall man, and age had begun to fold him inward like a closing book, but crossing that floor he seemed to Paloma to grow, letter by letter, the way a line grows in a stick until it fills the measure. He stopped in front of Aurelio Vance and looked up at him for a long moment.

"My grandfather hid in a cellar for nine years," Henrik said at last, "with papers that could have freed half this district, because he trusted no one. He died with the truth still locked in tin boxes, and the boxes went to my father, and my father died the same way, and the boxes came to me. Thirty years I have fed that wall and told myself patience was courage." He shook his head slowly. "Patience was just fear wearing better clothes. This morning a government man lied to a sergeant for us, and I find I'm done being afraid alone. Come upstairs. Both of you. There are things in that wall you should see with your hands, not just your eyes. If we're to be hanged together, we may as well know exactly what for."

He turned toward the shelving, toward the lock hidden in the frame, toward thirty years of buried light.

Paloma caught Aurelio's arm before he followed. Her fingers found his sleeve and stayed there longer than the gesture required, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

"That sergeant will tell someone you were here," she said softly. "Word travels faster in this city than type ever could."

"I know."

"And when Victor Sarn hears that the census man who lingered at our counter was the same man who vouched for our walls—"

"I know, Paloma." He covered her hand with his, briefly, warmly, like a man sealing wax over a letter. "Then we'd best make sure that whatever comes out of your father's wall gets printed before it gets buried. Haven't you been telling me? A record no one reads is just kindling."

From behind the false panel came the sound of hinges, soft as a held breath released—and her father, kneeling in the dark he had built, beginning at last to pass the truth out into the light, box by box, like a man handing down the family silver from a burning house.
