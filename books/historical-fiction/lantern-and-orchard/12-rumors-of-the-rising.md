## 12. Rumors of the Rising

The strike began on a Tuesday, the way catastrophes often do—without ceremony, without thunder, with nothing more than a foreman's whistle that never blew.

Aurelio heard the silence from the customs house steps, where he had gone to deliver his quarterly returns. The harbor had a voice he knew the way a man knows his own breathing: the groan of winches, the slap and suck of water against pilings, the bawling of gulls over fish heads, the endless argument of stevedores. That morning the voice was gone. Three hundred dockworkers stood along the quay with their hands in their pockets, watching the cranes the way one watches a sick relative—not with hatred, but with the terrible patience of people who have decided to wait out a thing.

No crates moved. No herring came ashore. The *Meridian Star* sat at her berth with her holds full and her crew leaning on the rail, idle as a painted ship.

"Third port this month," said the customs clerk beside him, a soft man named Bello whose face always looked freshly ironed. "Corvessa last week. They say the tenants in the upper orchards aren't paying rents either. Just—refusing." He said the word *refusing* the way other men said *plague*.

"Refusing what?" Aurelio asked, though he knew.

"Rents. Tithes. The survey fees." Bello lowered his voice, though there was no one near enough to hear. "They stand in their doorways when the collectors come and they say nothing at all. Nothing. You can't arrest a man for silence, Vance. The magistrates tried it in Ferrow's Cross and the whole district walked out after them."

Aurelio signed the receipt Bello slid across the counter and did not trust his own hand to write more. Above the customs house, the Consulate flag hung limp against its pole, and beyond it, terraced up the hills like green amphitheaters, the orchards stood in blossom—the pear trees white, the late apples still clenched pink in their buds. It was the season when the districts should have smelled of honey and cut grass. Instead the air carried smoke from cook-fires lit early and fed sparingly, the smell of households counting their stores.

He thought of Paloma's hands moving through the type case, sure as a surgeon's. He thought of Henrik Eriksen's confession locked behind a false wall: *we were the thieves' clerks; we made the theft legal.* Thirty years that paper had waited in the dark. Now the city itself seemed to be waiting for it, though no one outside the shop yet knew why.

The truth, Aurelio thought, walking back down toward the census office with his collar up against the harbor wind, is a seed. It keeps. But there comes a season when it must go into the ground or rot in the sack.

* * *

The census office occupied the ground floor of the old wool exchange, and it smelled of dust, lamp oil, and the particular despair of arithmetic done by candlelight. Aurelio shared a room with four other tally-men, each bent over his own district like a priest over his parish. Their pens scratched. A fly died slowly against the window glass. Through the wall came the muffled voice of Supervisor Corvaglia conducting an interview, and then—unusually—Corvaglia himself, appearing in the doorway with a look on his broad face like a man who has swallowed something that intends to stay swallowed.

"Vance. Upstairs. Ten minutes. Wear your good coat."

"The good coat" meant officials. Aurelio climbed the stairs past the map room, and it was the maps that stopped him—new maps, unrolled and pinned over the old ones, still smelling of fresh ink. He had redrawn boundaries himself often enough to read them at a glance, and what he saw turned his stomach with its quiet efficiency. The old orchard boundaries were gone. Not adjusted—gone. The winding lines that had followed hedgerows and streams and the remembered edges of family plots for ninety years had been replaced with clean rectangles, parcels numbered, sized for auction. The names of the tenant families had been struck through in red and beneath them, in the tidy italic of the Survey Office, new designations: *Parcel 14, vacant. Parcel 15, vacant. Parcel 16, vacant.*

Vacant. As if the people who had planted those trees, pruned them through forty winters, buried their dead beneath the north walls—as if all of that could be erased by an adjective.

He found the same word waiting upstairs. The room beyond the map room had been converted overnight into something between an office and a stage set: a long table, a decanter of pale wine, and Victor Sarn, court historian, seated at the head of it with papers fanned before him like a card sharp's hand.

Sarn was younger than his reputation, silver at the temples before fifty, dressed with the studied negligence of a man who wanted you to notice how well his clothes fit. His eyes were the color of weak tea and about as warm. He did not rise.

"Census taker Vance." He pronounced the name correctly, which somehow felt worse than getting it wrong. "You've been walking the orchard districts for—" he consulted a sheet, though Aurelio would have wagered anything he already knew the answer— "eleven months. Sit. Drink. Tell me what the districts feel like."

"Feel like, sir?"

"I can read the harvest figures myself. What I cannot read from this desk"—Sarn tapped the papers—"is mood. Temperature. Whether the air in Vellamare smells of patience or of pitch. You walk the lanes. You knock on doors. People tell census takers things they would never tell a magistrate. So." He folded his hands. "Pitch, or patience?"

Aurelio took the chair because refusing it would have been its own report. The wine was a Vellamaran white, cold and faintly sweet, the taste of a better year.

"Both, sir. In different quarters. The dockside is angry but leaderless. The orchard tenants are calm, which worries me more. Calm men have plans."

Something flickered behind Sarn's tea-colored eyes—approval, perhaps, or appetite. "And the print shops? You called at the Eriksen establishment during your rounds."

The question arrived so smoothly that only afterward, walking home in the dark, did Aurelio understand it had been the point of the entire meeting. Everything else had been scenery.

"A census call, sir. Four souls in residence, corrected to three upon verification. Typefoundry and letterpress, licensed, taxes current."

"No pamphlets. No unauthorized jobbing work?"

"None that I observed."

Sarn smiled, and the smile did not reach further than his lips, which were thin as a ledger line. "Good. Because I am about to make your rounds simpler, Vance. By order of the Second Consul, all commercial presses in Vellamare are to be requisitioned for state work within the fortnight. There will be a history published—an account of the present disturbances, their causes, their resolution. My account." He said it without vanity, the way a man says *my name*. "The public requires clarity. Clarity requires monopoly."

"And the presses return to their owners after?"

"After." Sarn poured himself wine and did not offer the bottle. "You'll assist with the enumeration of press equipment. Inventories, output capacities, which masters keep which fonts. A man of your exactness." He raised his glass a half-inch, a toast to exactness. "Vellamare is going to be a difficult city this summer, Vance. The wise will be very precise about which side of the paper they're standing on."

Walking home, Aurelio did the calculation he had been avoiding since the customs house. Requisition within a fortnight. Sarn's history drafted now, typeset the moment the presses changed hands, printed in thousands before the first ripe apple came off the bough. Every door in the city nailed with the victors' version: *foreign agitation, criminal conspiracy, orderly settlement.* And behind a false wall in a print shop on Tanner's Lane, the true grants aging in the dark, one season older, one season closer to being kindling.

Paloma had said it herself, weeks ago, her hands ink-black to the wrist: *a record no one reads is just paper.* She had not yet convinced her father. Aurelio understood now that time had finished the argument for her. There were perhaps three weeks left in which caution meant anything at all.

After caution, there was only courage, or surrender.

* * *

That evening the rumors came down off the hills like weather.

He heard them at the corner tavern where he ate most nights, a low-beamed room that smelled of tallow and fried onions, where the talk usually concerned football and the price of tar. Tonight the talk had a different grain. At the far table, the rope-makers' men spoke of the strike in voices pitched just under the landlady's hearing. Near the fire, an orchard man named Teodoro—a broad, slow-spoken tenant from the upper pear terraces whom Aurelio had counted two winters running—held forth to a circle of listeners, holding his cup like a chalice.

"No violence," Teodoro was saying, and his voice carried the weight of a man repeating something agreed upon by many. "That's the discipline. Nobody throws a stone. We simply stop. Stop hauling, stop paying, stop signing. Let them send their clerks to an empty ledger and see what their ink is worth then." A rumble of assent. "But hear me—" and here he leaned forward, and the firelight caught the gray stubble on his jaw— "when the moment comes, the whole city will need to know *why*. Not just us. Not just the districts. The fishwives and the rope-walk and the guild apprentices—they'll need it written plain, in words any man can read at a doorway, or this rising dies in a ditch like the last one."

"And who'll write it?" someone asked. "The Consulate's scribes?"

Teodoro smiled slowly. "There are other pens."

Under the table, Aurelio's hand closed around his own knee and held on. *Other pens.* He thought of the composing stick, of Paloma's breath in the lantern light of the loft, of pages set letter by letter from a confession thirty years old. The rumor had not reached Tanner's Lane yet—but it was traveling toward it, the way floodwater travels toward a cellar. And somewhere across the city tonight, Sarn was writing his own version by lamplight, smooth and authoritative, calling the strike foreign agitation, calling the tenants criminals, preparing to pour his version into every press in Vellamare like lead into a mold.

Aurelio paid for his supper and stepped out into streets that smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Lamps were being lit along the canal. Somewhere a drum was beating—not a festival rhythm, something slower, a heartbeat rhythm—and a knot of apprentices stood listening to it with their caps in their hands.

Three weeks. Perhaps less.

He turned toward Tanner's Lane and walked faster, and behind him the drum went on beating, patient as a tide, keeping time for whatever was coming.
