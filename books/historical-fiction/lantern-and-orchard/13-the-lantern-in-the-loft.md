## 13. The Lantern in the Loft

The type loft smelled of oil and cold iron and, beneath it, the faint sweetness of apple peel that had dried into the floorboards years before Henrik converted the space. Paloma lit the lantern at dusk because dusk was the safest hour—too late for neighbors to be abroad with honest errands, too early for the watch to grow suspicious of light itself. The flame caught, steadied, and threw the room's shadows upward: the racks of standing type like a forest of dark saplings, the composing stick waiting on the bench, and Aurelio Vance, sleeves rolled to the elbow, studying a galley of sorts as though it were scripture written in a language he was only beginning to read.

"You hold it wrong," she said, not unkindly.

"I hold it as I saw you hold it."

"And you watched me from across a market square, through a crowd, while soldiers were seizing a press." She took the stick from his hands. Her fingers brushed his—knuckles ink-stained, palms softer than hers, a government man's hands that had nonetheless learned, in three nights of this, to move among metal letters without flinching. "You were watching the press. Not me."

"Both," he said quietly. "I've stopped pretending otherwise."

She turned back to the case before he could see what her face did with that. The type case lay open before them like a city seen from above—its compartments divided by thin wooden walls, each bay holding its small population of letters: the *e*s crowded thick as tenements, the *z*s and *q*s sparse and lonely in their corners. Lowercase below, capitals above. Her father's arrangement, her grandfather's before him, unchanged for sixty years. She knew it blind. She could stand here in absolute darkness and find any letter in the alphabet by touch alone, and some nights she thought that was the truest thing anyone could say of her: she knew where every letter lived.

"The page," she said. "Read me the line."

Aurelio unfolded the copy—the fair transcription they had made together two nights past, in ink that would dissolve if wet, so that no legible draft remained anywhere in the shop. He read slowly, carefully, in the low voice he used when he was being careful about something other than words:

"'Be it known to all who come after: that the grants bearing the seal of the Consulate were made by the hand of Aldous Eriksen, called then Aldous Verrin, cutter of seals, in the year of the old reckoning 1741—'"

"Stop." She had found the compartment. Her fingers walked the rows of type, selecting, tilting each piece so she could feel the face of the letter against her thumb. "I set the numerals first. Old figures are harder—they're taller than the lowercase, they want more leading or they'll crowd."

He watched her work. She could feel the weight of his watching the way one feels weather changing—not unpleasantly, but with consequence.

* * *

They worked by a choreography neither of them had designed. He read; she composed. When a line was set he took the stick from her—carefully, always carefully now—and justified it with a brass slug, tapping the letters down with the flat of a bodkin until they sat flush and even. Then he slid the line onto the stone galley and locked it there with a quoin, and she began again.

It was slow work. A hand compositor working steadily might set a thousand ems an hour. Paloma had never been steady; she was fast, and speed bred errors, and errors in this particular document were unforgivable in a way that went beyond craft. A transposed figure in a land grant date—a 4 where a 1 should stand—was not merely a mistake. It was a gift to Sarn, to the Consulate, to every man who would seize on a single flaw to declare the whole record false. The truth, she was learning, had to be set more carefully than lies, because lies came pre-forged and truth had to be built letter by letter out of a person's own two hands.

So they went slowly. And slowness, in the lantern light, with only the two of them and the ticking of the cooling stove below, became something else.

"Tell me about your father," Aurelio said, somewhere past midnight. He said it the way he asked census questions—gently, from an angle, never directly at the thing itself.

"He cut punches. Like me, but better. He could cut a letter so fine you'd need a lens to see the serifs." She fitted an *n*, an *o*, another *t*. "My mother died when I was nine. He raised me in this loft, more or less. I slept up here summers, when the shop below got too hot. I used to lie under the roof beam and read the type cases by moonlight—there's a window facing east, and on clear nights the letters cast shadows long enough to spell things across the floor. Nonsense things. My own name, once. I thought it was a message."

"Was it?"

"It was moonlight and lead." She smiled despite herself. "But I decided it meant something, and I've been deciding things mean something ever since. It's how you survive in a house full of secrets—you make meaning out of whatever isn't secret."

"And what does tonight mean?"

She looked up. He was very close—closer than the work required, close enough that she could smell lamp oil on his collar and beneath it something plainer, soap and wool, the smell of a man who lived alone and kept a clean, spare life the way he kept his ledgers. His eyes in the lantern light were the color of strong tea, warm at the edges.

"Tonight means," she said, "that we're setting sedition by hand, and my father is downstairs pretending to sleep, and if a single neighbor hears us moving type at midnight we're all three of us finished. And it means I don't mind."

"No?"

"No. That frightens me a little. Minding is safer."

He reached past her—to the case, ostensibly, to the compartment of commas—and his arm settled along the bench beside hers, and neither of them moved away from the contact. Their hands met over the open compartment, both reaching for the same mark, and stayed there a moment too long: her fingers roughened from the graver, his still carrying the ghost of official ink, resting together on the small cold shoulder of a comma.

"Aurelio." She said his name deliberately, fully, for the first time. Not *the census man*. Not *Vance*. "When this is printed—if it's printed—what will you be? You can't go back to counting. They'll know."

"I know."

"What will you do?"

He was quiet a long moment. Below them, a floorboard creaked—Henrik, walking in his sleepless circuit from bed to window to bed—and then stilled.

"I'll do what you're doing," he said finally. "Set the record straight. However they let me, however they don't. I counted four hundred households in the orchard district, Paloma. I wrote down their names and their trades and how many children, and I filed those pages, and men in the capital are using pages exactly like mine to decide which of those families exist and which don't. I'd like to spend the rest of my life correcting the arithmetic."

Her throat tightened. She bent over the case and set three letters badly and had to distribute them back and begin again, because her hands had gone unsteady in a way they never did over mere metal.

* * *

What Paloma did not see—but what the stairwell saw, and the half-open door at its foot—was her father.

Henrik Eriksen had come up an hour past midnight intending nothing more sinister than a pipe and a look at the moon. He had stood in the shadow of the doorway with the door drawn to behind him, unseen, unannounced, an old habit from thirty years of listening at his own house for sounds that should not be there.

What he heard was his daughter laughing. Low, brief, quickly smothered—but laughing, in the type loft, at midnight, over documents that could hang them all.

He watched. He told himself he was watching the work, checking the justification of the lines, the evenness of the word spacing. He was a craftsman; it was natural to check. But the work was good—better than good, the girl had inherited his eye and surpassed his patience—and so he ran out of professional excuses within minutes, and went on watching anyway.

He watched Aurelio Vance take the composing stick from her hands and hold it a heartbeat longer than the transfer required. He watched Paloma lean into the lantern light to point out a flaw in an old-style figure, her shoulder nearly touching the man's chest, neither of them acknowledging the nearness and both of them arranging themselves around it like water around a stone. He watched them set the line that named their family's crime—*made by the hand of Aldous Eriksen*—and he watched his daughter's fingers choose each letter of the confession as tenderly as if she were dressing a wound.

Thirty years ago, Henrik had loved a woman in this loft. He had set type with her by candlelight in the months before the Consulate's men came asking questions the first time, and he had learned then what he was watching now learn again in reverse: that danger does not cool love, it concentrates it, the way fire concentrates ore. Two people setting forbidden words by lamplight will fall in love if they are young, and if they are not young they will fall into something older and worse, and there is no preventing either, and he had never once regretted it.

But this one was a government man. This one had come to their door with a census book and an oath in his pocket, and Henrik had spent three decades teaching his daughter that the state's affection was a lure hung over a trap. And now the state's own clerk stood in the loft holding his family's confession in brass and lead, and looked at Paloma the way—

The way, Henrik admitted, standing in the dark with his unlit pipe going cold in his hand, that an honest man looks at a thing he intends to keep.

He had tested the boy. Not with questions—with silence, with errands, with the deliberate mis-statement of a date to see whether Aurelio would catch it or flatter it. Aurelio had caught it. He had caught it twice, the second time gently, in front of Paloma, saying only, "I think the grant reads 1743, sir—the 4 is cut narrow," and had not used the error to purchase standing, had not seemed to want standing at all. That was the test passed that mattered. Ambitious men corrected you to rise. Honest men corrected you because the record mattered more than their comfort.

And yet. And yet the boy still wore the uniform of the thing that had stolen their name, burned their kin's papers, and redrawn every map in the district. Love did not launder that. Only action would, and the action had not yet been asked of him.

Henrik put the pipe in his pocket unsmoked. Let them have the lantern and the loft and the hours before dawn. He would ask everything of Aurelio Vance soon enough—all of it, before this was done—and the boy would either stand or break, and no amount of watching from doorways would change which.

He went down the stairs as quietly as he had come, and Paloma, pausing with a ligature between finger and thumb, felt the house settle around her and mistook it for the wind.

* * *

Toward the fourth hour they finished the second page, and Paloma did what she always did after locking up a forme: she pulled a proof, because no compositor trusts locked type until paper has told it the truth about itself. She inked the brayer, rolled it across the standing letters in slow strokes, laid a sheet of cheap proofing paper over the galley, and pressed it down with the back of a spoon because the hand press below was far too loud for this hour.

She lifted the paper. In the lantern light, wet and gleaming, the words stood up off the page.

Aurelio read them upside down, the way compositors learn to, and she watched him go pale in the wrong direction—pale rising rather than draining, the blood leaving his lips and flooding his face, fury and awe arriving together.

"That's my signature," he said. "Not mine—" He touched the line where his own name appeared, typeset in the witness clause they had added: *attested by Aurelio Vance, census taker, who has seen the originals.* "You set my name into it."

"You attested it."

"I attested nothing formally. If they catch this, that name makes me—"

"Makes you what you already decided to be, the night you lied to a sergeant for us." She held the proof out to him. "I won't print a man's name without his knowing. So know it."

He took the sheet. His hands were not quite steady, and she saw that he understood, fully and at last, what she had understood since she found the seal punch in the cabinet: that paper is not innocent, that a name in ink is a nail driven into a life, and that he had just let her drive it.

"They'll burn presses for this," he said.

"They'll try."

"And you?"

She took the proof back from him, looked at it a moment longer—at the confession of her great-grandfather, at her own name nowhere on it, at his name standing witness—and fed it into the stove. It flared green and then orange, and the words curled, blackened, and were gone, all but the smoke.

"Me," she said, "I'm already ash and lead. What's left is the printing."

Above the loft, in the chimney's throat, the wind changed. Down in the street, faint but unmistakable, boots rang on cobbles in the rhythm of a patrol that did not pass by—patrols passed by; this one slowed—and stopped, briefly, outside the shuttered front of the shop, before moving on toward the docks.

Paloma pinched out the lantern. In the darkness she found Aurelio's hand in the dark among the type cases, and held it, and neither spoke, listening to the boots fade, both thinking the same thought and neither daring to say it aloud: that someone had heard the press, or seen the light, or been paid to look—and that the clock Sarn had brought to Vellamare had just begun, in earnest, to run.
