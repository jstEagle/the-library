## 14. Sarn's Offer

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy in Consulate grey who did not wait for Aurelio to sign for it. The paper was heavy stock, the kind used for proclamations and death notices, and the handwriting was not a clerk's hand at all but something deliberate and beautiful, each letter formed with the patience of a man who had spent his life making words behave.

*Mr. Aurelio Vance is requested to attend upon Mr. Victor Sarn, Historiographer to the Consulate, at the Custom House, Thursday next, at the hour of ten. Attendance is understood to be voluntary.*

Aurelio read that last line three times. *Understood to be voluntary.* In Vellamare, he had learned, everything the state called voluntary arrived with an escort or left with consequences. He held the summons over the stove flame out of old habit—he had burned enough marginalia in his career to know the choreography—but stopped before the corner caught. Burning a summons from Sarn would be its own confession. Instead he set it beneath his census ledgers, weighted flat under the district maps whose redrawn boundaries still troubled him like a stone in the shoe, and went on counting heads.

Two days. He spent them the way a man spends the days before a tooth is drawn: ordinarily, deliberately, with exaggerated attention to small things. He finished his route through the Salt Quarter, tallying widows and dockhands and one family of nine living above a rope-walk where the smell of tar seeped through the floorboards into their bread. He ate dinner at the same chophouse, at the same corner table, and noticed—because noticing had become both his trade and his affliction—that the man reading a newspaper two tables away had been there yesterday also, and had turned pages without appearing to read them.

He walked past the print shop twice without stopping. The shutters were closed, as they always were now since the searches; the painted name *ERIKSEN & SON* flaking above the door, the *& SON* older than any son the current Henrik had ever had. Once, passing, he heard the faint rhythmic knock of a press running behind the brickwork, muffled almost to nothing, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Or perhaps he imagined it. He had begun imagining a great many things in that neighborhood.

Thursday came grey and windless, gulls hanging motionless over the harbor like scraps of paper nailed to the sky.

* * *

The Custom House had been built by men who wanted history to feel inevitable. Its colonnade was thick as tree trunks, its floors polished marble worn into shallow rivers by a century of feet carrying goods and taxes and grief up to the assessors' windows. Aurelio climbed the stairs past the customs clerks, past the surveyor's office, past a corridor of doors with brass plates naming offices of record, registry, tariff—and at the end of it, newly installed, a plate so fresh the screws still shone:

V. SARN — HISTORIOGRAPHER TO THE CONSULATE

A clerk in livery opened the door before Aurelio could knock, which meant the clerk had been watching him climb the stairs, which meant Sarn had arranged even the choreography of arrival.

The room had been a map room once; you could see the ghost-rectangles on the walls where chart cabinets had stood until very recently. Now the space was filled instead with books—crates of them, half unpacked, spines in six languages—and a long table laid out not with maps but with documents, each squared precisely to the table's edge. Beyond the table, windows overlooked the harbor, and beyond the harbor, hazy in the morning light, the orchard districts: the terraced slopes, the smudge of blossom just beginning, the rooftops of the tenants' rows stitched between the trees like seams.

Victor Sarn stood at the window with his back to the door, hands clasped behind him, a compact man of perhaps fifty dressed not in official grey but in scholar's black, cut well, brushed well, wearing authority the way some men wear cologne—applied sparingly, unmistakable at close range.

"Mr. Vance." He did not turn yet. "Do you know what you're looking at? What I'm looking at?"

"The harbor, sir."

"The harbor," Sarn agreed. "And above it, four thousand acres of fruit trees that have produced revenue for this city for a hundred and forty years, and which are about to produce something else entirely. Unrest, Mr. Vance. The word has a certain drama to it, but the substance is simpler. Some people will decline to pay rents they owe, and other people will be asked to remind them, and a third group will write down what happened. That third group is my responsibility." Now he turned, and his face was mild, lined, almost kind—the face of a favorite tutor. "And your records, I'm told, are among the finest raw material in the city."

"I do my work thoroughly, sir."

"So I hear. Sit, please."

They sat. A servant had left coffee, and Sarn poured two cups himself, unhurried, and Aurelio understood that the pouring was part of the architecture—that every detail of this meeting had been composed like a document, margins justified, no stray mark left uncorrected. He drank because refusing would itself be a data point.

Sarn began, as Aurelio might have guessed, with the census.

"Eleven years you've kept the rolls for the eastern districts. Your error rate is negligible. Your field notes—" Sarn touched a folder on the table; Aurelio's own handwriting looked back at him from the tab, copied, excerpted, annotated in that beautiful patient hand "—are, frankly, better than most historians manage. You notice things. Household composition. Land use. Who owns, who tends, who has quietly moved off a property while still paying its taxes." A thin pause. "You notice things, Mr. Vance, and the Consulate values men who notice things."

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me yet. Thanking me comes later, if at all." Sarn smiled to show the remark was a joke, and showed simultaneously that it was not. "Here is my situation. I have been commissioned to write the definitive account of the settlement of the orchard question—the land grants, the consolidation, the transition to managed estates. It will be printed here, in Vellamare, on the presses I am presently requisitioning, and it will be distributed to every school, every magistrate's bench, every parish chest in the province. Fifty years from now, when someone wants to know how this city came to be ordered as it is, they will open my book." He said it without vanity, which made it worse; he said it the way a mason says *I built that wall*. "History, Mr. Vance, is not what happened. History is what can be proven to have happened. And proof is a matter of archives."

"And archives are a matter of custody," Aurelio said, before he could stop himself.

Something flickered behind Sarn's eyes—not anger; appraisal. "Yes," he said softly. "Exactly that. Custody." He let the word sit between them like a coin on a table. Then, briskly: "Which brings me to why you're here. I need eyes inside the printing trade. Not spies"—he raised a hand against the word—"colleagues. The printers of this city are craftsmen, and craftsmen are honorable, but craftsmen are also, historically, the most easily seduced class of men in any polity. Give a printer a grievance and a case of type and he will make you a revolution by Friday. My book must go to press undisturbed, and afterward, the presses must return to honest commerce. If anyone in that community is approached by agitators—if seditious material is being set, or stored, or circulated—I want to know about it early. Quietly. Before it becomes a matter for soldiers, who are not quiet."

He slid a paper across the table. Aurelio looked down at it and saw his own future written in careful clauses: a promotion to Chief Registrar of the Eastern Districts, a salary that would have made his mother laugh in disbelief, an immunity clause broad enough to drive a cart through, and a schedule of reporting obligations described in language so gentle it might have been a subscription to a literary periodical.

"It is voluntary," Sarn said. "I want that understood. No man is compelled to inform on his neighbors in a free city. But free cities survive, Mr. Vance, because some men volunteer."

Aurelio looked at the paper for a long moment. Through the window behind Sarn, the orchard slopes caught a band of sunlight, and he thought—with a clarity that frightened him—of Paloma Eriksen's hands moving over the type case, quick and sure, sorting letters the way she might sort seeds. He thought of Henrik's smooth answer to the question about a fourth family member, and the false wall he had not seen but now knew the dimensions of, and the forme of true grants that might at this moment be locked in iron somewhere below those closed shutters.

He thought: *if I refuse outright, I die slowly, and the archive dies with me.*

He thought: *if I accept, I am theirs.*

He reached for the only ground between.

"Sir," he said, "I'm a counting man. I count what exists. I've never been asked to count what doesn't, and I'd be poor at it—you don't want a poor informant, believe me. Agitators don't file returns with me. They avoid me the way water avoids oil." He turned the paper slightly, as though studying it. "What I can offer is this: my records are complete. If anything changes in the printing quarter—new apprentices, unusual purchases of paper, households that shift overnight—it will surface in the next enumeration, and you'll have it, because I give the Consulate everything the census finds. Everything it finds." He met Sarn's eyes. "That's the whole of my oath, sir. I never swore to see around corners."

Silence. The harbor clock struck the quarter-hour below them, and a gull screamed somewhere in the rigging of the world.

"You misunderstand your own profession," Sarn said at last, pleasantly. "A census taker who sees nothing is a contradiction in terms. I've read your notes. You see everything." He rose, and Aurelio rose with him, and Sarn extended the paper again. "Take it home. Consider. There is no deadline—but of course, events have their own schedules, and I would rather have you inside the tent than wondering about the weather."

Aurelio took the paper. Refusing to take it had felt, in the half-second available, like a door slamming; taking it felt like a door opening onto a stair he could not see the bottom of. He tucked it inside his coat, over his heart, where the master pages would later lie sewn into the lining, though he knew nothing of that yet.

"Mr. Vance." Sarn's voice stopped him at the door, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. "One more thing, purely professional curiosity. In your rounds of the printing quarter—purely as an observer of households—have you formed any impression of the establishment of Eriksen and Son?"

The question hung there, patient, beautiful, lethal. Aurelio felt his pulse in his teeth. He gave it the full weight of eleven years of bureaucratic tedium.

"A tidy shop," he said. "Shutters closed most days since the searches. An old man, a grown daughter. They keep their accounts current and their opinions to themselves, which is more than I can say for most of the quarter. The daughter does the books, I believe." A pause calibrated to boredom. "There was a discrepancy in my first count—a fourth name I couldn't reconcile—but the old man explained it and the arithmetic resolved. Clerical error on my side. It happens."

"Doesn't it," said Sarn, and smiled his tutor's smile. "Clerical errors are the historian's bread, Mr. Vance. We live on them. Good day."

* * *

Outside, the wind had come up at last, pushing clouds in from the sea in long ragged banners, and the smell of rain and brine and, faintly, from the east, of blossom. Aurelio walked back across the city without hurrying, the promotion paper stiff against his ribs like a splint, and took inventory of what he had done.

He had refused. Obliquely, in the grammar of the empire, but refused: he had offered the state only its own ledgers back, and nothing of the shop, nothing of the wall, nothing of the lantern-lit loft where a woman he could not stop thinking about was setting truth letter by letter into metal. He had bought himself a measure of cover with the story of the clerical error—which now, he realized coldly, existed nowhere except in his mouth, and which Sarn would verify against the original census returns within the day, and which would therefore become either his shield or his gallows depending on what Henrik had actually told him that afternoon in the print shop, and whether the old man's smoothness had matched Aurelio's paperwork.

But refusal, he understood, walking, was not safety. Sarn had not needed his answer. The meeting had been the trap's first jaw, and his equivocation the second, and they had closed around him gently, without sound. From this hour he would be watched with new attention; his route would be mapped, his visits catalogued, his coffee companions noted by men who turned pages without reading them. Whatever he did next—to the Eriksens, with the Eriksens, for the Eriksens—would be done under glass.

And yet, beneath the fear, something in him had settled, the way a bone settles back into joint. For eleven years he had carried his oath like a coat that fit badly, and today, in a marble room overlooking stolen orchards, he had finally learned what it cost to take it off. He had lied to the Historiographer of the Consulate. There was no walking that back, and he found, testing the fact like a tongue against a missing tooth, that he did not wish to walk it back.

At the corner of Rope Lane he stopped, ostensibly to adjust his collar, actually to look down the street to the print shop with its flaking sign and shuttered face. No smoke from the chimney. No light in the loft. But he knew—they were there, behind the brick and the standing type, doing whatever careful people do in the last quiet week before history arrives with soldiers.

He should walk away, he told himself. Report nothing, visit no one, keep his head down through the spring, retire on his pension to some inland town where the only pressing question was the price of hay.

Then the shutter on the shop's upper window shifted—an inch, no more—and through the gap he caught the gleam of lamplight and, briefly, the silhouette of a woman bent over her work, hands moving, cutting something out of metal that would outlive them all.

Aurelio pulled his collar up against the coming rain, turned toward the eastern districts, and began, with the unhurried stride of an innocent man, to plan a completely unofficial visit.

Behind him, two streets back, a figure in a fishmonger's apron turned the same corner he turned, at the same distance he had kept all the way from the Custom House, and did not turn off when he did.
