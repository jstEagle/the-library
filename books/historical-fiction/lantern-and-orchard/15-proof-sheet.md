## 15. Proof Sheet

The press had a voice, and Paloma had spent her whole life learning its moods.

Her father's old hand press—cast before she was born, its iron frame worn smooth at the points where three generations of Eriksen hands had gripped it—spoke differently at different hours. In the morning it groaned like an old man rising from bed. In the heat of afternoon it ticked as the platen expanded against the screw. But at night, past midnight, when the shop was shuttered and the lamp in the loft turned low, the press went quiet as a held breath, and every sound it made became a confession.

She had oiled it twice that evening. She had packed the tympan with fresh frisket paper and trimmed the edges clean, because a ragged frisket left ghost-marks along the margins and ghost-marks were how careless printers died. She had inked the forme with a ball that her own hands had stuffed and stitched, working the brayer across the surface until the type glistened black as harbor water at night.

And now she stood over it, palms flat on the coffin press's rails, and listened to the silence of Vellamare at one o'clock in the morning.

Above her, through the loft floor, she could hear Aurelio breathing. He was supposed to be watching the street from the slatted window, counting footsteps the way he counted households, but she knew by the rhythm of him that he was listening down through the boards at her, and she let herself be comforted by it, which frightened her more than the soldiers did. A woman alone could be careful forever. A woman who wanted someone beside her began taking risks to keep him there.

The forme lay in the bed of the press: four pages of the true record, set in type she had cut with her own graver over nineteen nights. Not cast type—cut type, each letter carved fresh from type-metal blanks so that no foundry in the city could trace the order, so that even if Sarn's men seized every mold in Vellamare, these letters would belong to no register but theirs. Her thumbs still carried the ache of it. There was a groove worn into her right thumbnail where the graver handle had pressed, night after night, and she had caught herself touching it during the day like a rosary bead, a small secret wound that meant *I am doing something.*

The page her grandfather had written sixty years ago—the confession, the naming of names, the account of how the Consulate's founders had come to the Eriksens' shop with gold and threats and left with a forged seal on forged grants—was locked upstairs in the cabinet. But it lived now in metal. Eleven hundred words. Fourteen nights of cutting. Her eyes had learned to see letters everywhere: in the cracks of the ceiling plaster, in the arrangement of olives on a plate, in the wake a gull drew across the harbor sky. Henrik had watched her work without speaking, and once she had looked up and found his face wet, and neither of them had said anything about it, because in their family grief was handled like type—quietly, carefully, with clean hands.

*Pull the bar,* she told herself. *Or don't. Once you pull it, there is no unprinting.*

She thought of the pamphleteer at the market whose press the soldiers had broken apart with hatchets while the crowd watched and did nothing, because doing something was how you ended up under the hatchets too. She thought of the standing wall of dead type behind which the archive hid, thousands of letters that spelled nothing, guarding thousands of words that spelled everything. She thought of her father saying, years ago, in the voice he used for lessons: *A record no one reads is just kindling.* And she thought of Aurelio's census ledger, with its neat columns and its careful silences, and understood suddenly that the whole city was a misprinted page—that somewhere beneath the official text, the true line was waiting for someone bold enough to correct it at the press.

Paloma Eriksen pulled the bar.

* * *

The press spoke.

It was not the soft domestic sound of jobbing work—calling cards, broadsheet almanacs, the hundred harmless things the shop printed to stay invisible. It was the deep full-voiced pull of a real impression, platen meeting forme meeting dampened paper with a sound like a door closing firmly in a large stone house. The whole frame shuddered. Ink smell bloomed up around her, linseed and lampblack and something older underneath, the smell of the archive itself, as though the documents had exhaled.

She released the screw, lifted the frisket, peeled the sheet from the tympan.

And there it was.

Ink on paper. Legible. True.

*To all who shall read this record:* —her grandfather's words, her grandfather's hand rendered in letterforms she had given him, letters she had cut straighter than any he'd have used, because grief makes better gravers than comfort does—*the grants by which the Consulate claims the orchard lands were cut in this shop, by my hand, at the order of men who owned neither the land nor the honesty to steal it openly...*

She read it standing in the lamplight with the proof trembling slightly in her fingers, and the words did not feel like history. They felt like a voice. That was the thing about print that no one who hadn't set type could understand: the dead do not speak from manuscripts, which are private things, folded things, things that can be denied. The dead speak from print. Print is a voice that keeps talking after the mouth is stopped. Somewhere in the city tonight, in ten houses she could name and twenty she couldn't, other presses sat idle or broke or printed lies—and here was one sheet of honest paper, warm from the platen, holding the truth the way a lantern holds flame.

She hung it on the drying line with two wooden pins, and stood back, and looked at it, and laughed—a single startled syllable, quickly strangled—and then heard her father's step on the stair.

He came down in his nightshirt with his coat thrown over it, gray-haired, barefoot, moving fast for a man of sixty-one. He had pretended to sleep; she had known he wouldn't manage it. He stopped at the foot of the stairs and looked at the drying sheet the way sailors look at land.

"You pulled a proof," he said.

"Someone had to see whether the letters stand."

"They stand." He crossed the room slowly. He took the bottom edge of the sheet between finger and thumb, tilting it to the lamp, reading with the unconscious lip-moving concentration of a man who had spent his life judging proofs. "They stand," he said again, uselessly, and then his voice failed altogether.

Paloma had seen her father weep exactly twice: when her mother died, and once, silently, at the market the day the soldiers broke the pamphleteer's press, when he'd thought no one was looking. This was different from both. This was not grief. He pressed the heel of his hand against his mouth and his shoulders shook, and the sound that got past his hand was closer to laughter than sobbing, a cracked and disbelieving joy, and Paloma understood that she was watching a man read his own father alive again.

"Here," Henrik managed, touching the page with one fingertip, not quite letting himself smudge it. "*My name was Aldric Voss-Eriksen, and I cut this seal knowing it was false.* Sixty years. Sixty years that sentence has been sitting in the dark in that cabinet, and now—" His finger moved down the lines. "Now it says itself. Now anyone can read it. Any child. Any clerk. Any tenant in the orchard districts who was told the papers say the land isn't theirs." He looked up at her, eyes streaming, and he was smiling, and the smile was the most frightening thing she had ever seen on him, because it was fearless. "You've killed us, little one," he said gently. "And it was worth it. God forgive me, it was worth it."

"Don't say killed."

"I'm a printer. I say what's true." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, leaving a faint gray smear of ink along his jaw that he would wear for days without noticing, the mark of their trade, the mark she'd have known him by in any crowd in the world. "How many can you pull tonight?"

"Before the ink smell leaves the room? Two. Three if I risk the second damping."

"Then pull them. I'll watch the window."

"No—you'll go back to bed and be innocent if they come."

"Paloma." He put both hands on her shoulders. Up close she could see how old he had become in the last month, since Sarn arrived and requisitioned the commercial presses, since the shop had begun printing the historian's flattering pages by day and hiding the true ones by night. "There is no innocence left in this house. There is only what we do next. Pull your sheets. I'll stand at the window, because my eyes are better in the dark than yours, and because I want to see the street while my father's words are being born. Let an old man have that."

She pulled three sheets. Each time the press spoke its great stone-house word into the sleeping shop, she flinched, and each time the flinch came later and smaller, her body learning what her mind already knew: that quiet had been a kind of death too, just slower. The three proofs hung on the line like laundry, like flags, like three small black-and-white dawns. She blotted the forme, covered the type with the press board, raked the sand over the hearth where she'd heated nothing, and banked the lamp to a bead.

Henrik stayed at the window a long time. When he finally came away from it, he touched the topmost proof once, very lightly, the way you touch the forehead of a sleeping child.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we decide how many the world gets."

* * *

The trouble came at half past seven, with the bread.

Mrs. Callas on the ground floor baked on Thursdays, and the smell of her oven rose through the floorboards into the shop like a summons, and for thirty years the ritual had been the same: Paloma downstairs with a warm coin, Mrs. Callas pressing an extra roll into her hands and complaining about the price of flour and asking nothing, ever, about the household above her, because in Vellamare the courtesy between neighbors was built of questions politely never asked.

But this Thursday Mrs. Callas opened her door before Paloma had finished knocking, and her face was wrong—too alert, too interested, the face of a woman rehearsing a question she'd been composing since dawn.

"Late night up there," Mrs. Callas said. Not a question. A statement wearing a question's coat.

"The press runs when the orders allow, you know how it is."

"At one in the morning?" Mrs. Callas's eyes went past Paloma's shoulder, up the stairwell, hungry. "That wasn't jobbing-work noise. My husband worked the ropewalk thirty years; I know a big press pulling a real impression when I hear one through a wall. That was a full form. A big one." She lowered her voice, and here it was, the thing she'd climbed toward all morning: "They're saying at the fish stalls that the historian's men are paying for information. About unlicensed printing. Paying real money, Paloma. Enough to leave this street." She said it fast, the way people give warnings they're ashamed of, thrusting the knife out handle-first. "I'm not saying it was anything. I'm saying walls talk, and some ears get paid."

Paloma kept her face arranged into mild, stupid patience—the face she had practiced her whole life, invisibility as craft. "Thank you for the warning, Mrs. Callas. If I hear any strange printing, I'll tell my father." She paid for the bread. She took the extra roll. She climbed the stairs with her heart going like the press at full pull.

Aurelio was already in the loft, dressed, pale, the census ledger open on the table before him like a shield he no longer believed in. He'd heard. Of course he'd heard; the walls talked both ways.

"How much did she hear?" he asked quietly.

"One impression clearly. Maybe parts of the others."

"Then we assume the fish stalls assume the worst." He stood and paced the length of the loft, three steps, turn, three steps, the walk of a man doing arithmetic he hated. "Sarn pays informants out of his travel budget—I've seen the requisitions. By tomorrow evening there could be a sergeant at your door asking why a licensed jobbing shop pulled a full forme at one in the morning. Licensed shops don't run at one in the morning, Paloma. Licensed shops sleep."

"Then we finish before they ask." She said it before she knew she was going to say it, and having said it, felt the strange calm that follows a decision. "The remaining pages. All of them. Not one letter a night anymore—every night, both of us, until the whole record stands in type. And then—" she gestured at the three proofs hanging on their line, white as moths in the gray morning light coming through the slats, "—then we scatter it. Four shops. You said four shops would take formes."

Aurelio stopped pacing. He looked at the proofs, and she watched him do the thing he did, the census-taker's thing, tallying: three sheets, eleven hundred words, one neighbor bought or buyable, one historian with a budget for ears, one city with maybe six weeks left in it before the victors wrote it all their way.

"If we speed up, we make mistakes," he said. "Mistakes in type mean re-cutting, and re-cutting means noise, and noise means—"

"Mistakes also mean the record dies in a drawer with my grandfather." She took the extra roll out of her basket and put it in his hands, an argument disguised as breakfast, an intimacy disguised as charity. "You taught me the arithmetic yourself, Aurelio Vance, the night you told me about the redrawn maps. A record no one reads is kindling. Well. So is a record that comes too late."

He held the roll and didn't eat it. Through the slatted window the street was filling with ordinary Thursday light—milk carts, gulls, a squad of Consulate infantry tramping toward the docks where the strikers had been three days now, their boots making a sound like a slow drum. Somewhere down that street, Mrs. Callas was telling her husband what she'd heard, and her husband was deciding what it might be worth.

"My grandfather's name was Aldric," Paloma said, to fill the silence, or to break it. "Aldric Voss-Eriksen. I cut his words last night and I never knew his face. Isn't that a strange thing? I know his hand better than his face."

"He knows yours now," Aurelio said simply. "Everyone will."

Below them, faint through the floorboards, they heard Mrs. Callas's door close, and then open again, and footsteps hurry away toward the fish stalls faster than any appetite for herring required.

Paloma looked at the three proofs turning gently on their line in the draft from the window—her grandfather's confession, legible, true, born—and understood that the hour of secrecy was ending, and whatever came next would come fast.
