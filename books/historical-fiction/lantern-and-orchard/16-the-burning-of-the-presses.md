## 16. The Burning of the Presses

The order came down on a Tuesday, in the gray hour before the markets opened, when the fog off the harbor still lay against the lower streets like a sheet drawn over the city's face.

Aurelio saw it first as a change in the clerks' behavior. The counting house had a particular silence for ordinary days—a shuffling, coughing, pen-scratching quiet that he knew as well as his own breathing—but that morning the silence was different. Tighter. The junior clerks moved with their eyes down, and old Maret, who had served three supervisors and outlived two of them, was locking documents into the iron cabinet with a key she usually left in the lock all day.

He should have understood then. He told himself afterward, many times, standing in the ruins of what he had been, that he *should* have understood.

Supervisor Delacroix summoned him at half past seven. The man's office smelled of cold coffee and sealing wax; through the window behind him, the orchard district climbed its hill in terraces, the bare winter trees stitched in rows like stitches in a wound.

"Vance." Delacroix did not look up from the paper in front of him. "You know the district better than any man in this office. Your routes, your household returns—you've walked every street from Saltgate to the top of Terrasse Row."

"I have."

"The Historian's office has issued a requisition. All commercial presses in the third and fourth districts are to be secured for state use. Effective today." He slid a folded broadsheet across the desk. "Seizure orders. You'll accompany the provost's men. Your familiarity with the premises will expedite matters."

Aurelio took the paper without reading it. His hands were steady—he noted this with a strange, distant pride, the way one notes the behavior of someone else's body—and his voice, when he found it, was the flat administrative voice he had worn for eleven years like a coat.

"How many shops?"

"Nine that we know of. There may be others operating without license." Delacroix finally raised his eyes, and there was something in them Aurelio could not name—not suspicion exactly, not yet, but the careful blankness of a man testing a scale. "Sarn wants them inventoried by nightfall. Type, formes, stock, presses. Everything."

"And the printers themselves?"

"Detained for questioning where they resist. Sarn prefers cooperation, naturally. A press that surrenders quietly can be put to work printing the truth." He smiled thinly at his own phrasing. "You'll report to Sergeant Broch at the customs yard at eight."

Aurelio bowed the small bow the service required and walked out through the clerks' room, past Maret's locked cabinet, past the maps on the wall—the redrawn maps, the orchard boundaries erased under washes of pale ink—and down the stairs into a morning that smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

* * *

He read the seizure order properly on the customs yard steps, standing among soldiers who smelled of wet wool and lamp oil.

That was the detail that undid him first: not the language of the order, which was the usual bloodless bureaucratic poetry—*whereas the peace and good order of the districts require*—but the barrels. Six of them, stacked by the yard gate, stenciled with the Consulate crest. He asked a quartermaster what they held, already knowing.

"Lamp oil and pitch, sir. For the unserviceable material."

Unserviceable material. He turned the phrase over as the column formed up—two sergeants, fourteen men, Broch at the head with his helmet pushed back and his face red from the wind off the water. Unserviceable meant whatever Sarn decided it meant. A press could be pressed into state service or declared unserviceable in the same hour. Type could be melted. Paper burned. And a family—

Henrik Eriksen, who had shown him the archive with shaking hands after thirty years of silence, saying *I have waited for someone worth trusting*, and meaning, God forgive him, meaning *you*.

Paloma, whose fingers had touched his over the composing stick in the lantern light, who had cut letter after letter through the winter nights until her thumbs cracked and bled and she wrapped them in linen and kept cutting.

The forme was finished. Three days ago he had held the proof sheet in his hands—*the true land grants, typeset and legible*—and Henrik had wept over his grandfather's confession set in ink, and the noise of the press had carried to the neighbors, and word had begun to travel, because in Vellamare everything traveled eventually except mercy.

They were going to burn it. Burn the shop, burn the type, burn the family if the family objected, and then write in the official history that no true record had ever existed, that the orchards had always belonged lawfully to the Consulate, that the tenants were agitators, foreigners, ghosts. Sarn would print the lie on presses taken from honest men, and the smoke of the burning would smell like nothing at all by spring.

"Ahead, then," Broch said, consulting a list. "First stop's Gaspard's bookbindery, then the chandler on Rue Verte who does job work, then—" he ran a blunt finger down the page, "—the Eriksen typefoundry, upper Orchard Street. Last of the morning. Big operation, that one, according to the Historian's people."

Last of the morning. Not last of the day—the list went on into the afternoon, five more names below Eriksen's, which meant the soldiers would be hours at the shops above it, cataloguing, arguing with printers, loading wagons.

Which meant there was time. An hour, perhaps two. Enough, if the fog held and the streets stayed empty and no one marked a census taker walking fast uphill against the grain of the morning.

Aurelio folded the order and put it inside his coat, against his heart, where later he would pretend he had kept it as evidence rather than as a wound.

"Sergeant," he said. "I'll walk ahead and confirm the premises. Save your men the trouble if any of the addresses on that list are wrong. My routes are older than the Historian's information."

Broch squinted at him. For a moment—one long breath, one heartbeat, the whole rest of his life balanced inside it—Aurelio felt the man's gaze go through him like a probe through cloth.

"Suit yourself," Broch said finally. "Flag us if anything's amiss. We'll follow the route."

"Yes," Aurelio said. "I expect you will."

And he turned uphill into the fog, walking at the pace of an official man, while behind him the soldiers checked their oil barrels and their irons, and the city lay silent under its white sheet, and somewhere above him on Orchard Street a lantern was surely already lit in a loft window, and a girl was cutting letters that would outlive them all if only they could live out the day.

* * *

The fog thinned as he climbed. By the second terrace it hung in rags between the trees, and the bare orchards stood around him like crowds of patient witnesses, their branches black and intricate against the whitening sky. He passed the house where the widow Corvi had given him salted plums his first week on the route; passed the stairway where children had chalked a lantern on the wall and some other hand had scrubbed at it and failed to erase it entirely; passed the shuttered bakery where the strike had begun, and felt the district watching him—not with hostility, never that, but with the wary attention of people who had learned what a government man's footsteps sounded like when they meant harm.

He took the last flight of stairs to Orchard Street two at a time.

The shop's sign swung in the damp air: *E. ERIKSEN & SON — TYPEFOUNDERS AND PRINTERS*, the paint faded, the letters cut deep enough to survive decades of weather. Below, the workshop windows were dark. But he had learned the building's grammar in these weeks of secret compositing, and the grammar told him things: the second-floor shutters closed but not latched, the faint smell of hot metal that meant the forge had been fired within the hour, and—most telling—the delivery cart gone from its usual place beside the door, though the wheel ruts in the mud were fresh and deep, loaded-heavy.

Someone had already begun to move the formes.

He knocked the pattern they had agreed on: twice, pause, once. Nothing. Again. Above him a floorboard creaked, and then Paloma's voice, low and sharp through the shutter crack:

"Who is it?"

"You know who it is." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Open the door, Paloma. Please. I haven't got time to stand here being mysterious."

Bolt, chain, bar—the door opened in stages, and she stood in the gap with a candle in one hand and a pry-bar in the other, her sleeves rolled, metal filings silvering her forearms, her eyes going past his shoulder to the empty street before they came back to his face. Whatever she found there made her step back and let him in without a word.

"They're coming," he said, before the door was fully shut. "Today. This morning. Seizure of every press in the district—yours is on the list. They've got oil and pitch in barrels at the customs yard, Paloma. Whatever they can't carry away for Sarn's own printing, they mean to burn. And they're not stopping at presses."

She set down the pry-bar very carefully, the way she did everything, as if even in catastrophe her hands refused to make noise. "How long?"

"An hour. Perhaps less. I told the sergeant I'd verify the addresses ahead of the column." He heard how it sounded, what he had just confessed to, and did not look away from her. "I've chosen, if that's what you're wondering. I walked up this hill faster than any loyal man would have."

Something moved across her face—relief, terror, and beneath both a fierce bright thing she did not let herself show—and then she turned and shouted up the stairwell, all craft abandoned: "Papa! It's today!"

* * *

Henrik came down from the loft like a man descending into his own funeral, and yet there was no fear in him that Aurelio could see—only a terrible clarity, the clarity of someone who had spent thirty years expecting this morning and had, in a way, made peace with it long ago.

"The cart's already gone twice," he said. "Two of the four formes are distributed. Marot has one, the woman Osei at the chandlery the other. They're hidden in legitimate stock—job work, almanacs. If the soldiers search those shops casually, they won't find them."

"But?"

"But the master copy is still upstairs. And the third forme." Henrik's jaw worked. "The false wall will hold a search, maybe. It held the last one because they didn't know to look. But if Sarn's people come with the Historian's knowledge—with measurements, with a floor plan—" He stopped. Looked at his daughter. Looked at Aurelio. "Then it holds nothing."

"Then it doesn't matter whether it holds," Paloma said. "It matters what's not behind it when they open it. Papa. The cart makes one more trip. You and I can carry the third forme to the cooper's yard in twenty minutes if we leave now."

"And the master?"

"I'll take the master," Aurelio heard himself say.

Both of them turned. In the dim workshop, among the smells of ink and hot lead and lamp oil waiting somewhere downhill in barrels, the words seemed to hang in the air like set type, unalterable once cast.

"The soldiers know my face," Aurelio went on, and his voice was steadier than he had any right to expect. "They'll see me on the street and think I'm doing my duty—verifying the address, preparing the inventory. No one stops a census man. I'm the most invisible person in this city right now, and the most trusted, and I intend to spend both." He looked at Henrik. "Where do you want it?"

"Not with you," Henrik said slowly. "If they take you—"

"If they take me, they take me. But they won't find it, because I won't be carrying it sewn into my coat like a fool in a ballad. I know a place." He did, too—he had known it since his first week on the route, a dry cavity behind the loose keystone of the aqueduct arch on Terrasse Row, a place children used for secrets, a place no soldier would think to look because no soldier had ever been a child in this district. "I'll move it tonight, once the fires are lit and everyone's watching the smoke instead of the streets."

"The fires," Paloma repeated softly.

"They'll burn what they find. That's why what they find must be nothing." Aurelio turned to Henrik. "Break the wall open yourselves. Take out anything with a name on it—letters, deeds, the confession pages. Scatter the rest. Let them find an empty cavity and a guilty-looking wall; let them wonder. Empty rooms frighten Sarn more than full ones. An empty room means someone knew."

For a long moment the old cutter looked at him—looked at him the way he had looked at the proof sheet, the way a man looks at something he had stopped believing he would live to see—and then he nodded, once, and became motion, and the workshop erupted into purposeful quiet.

* * *

There was no time for tenderness, and they took it anyway.

Paloma walked him to the door with a parcel of type wrapped in sacking—the master sorts, the letters she had cut with her own bleeding thumbs, the alphabet of the true record—and pressed it into his hands as though handing him a child. Her fingers were shaking now, finally, now that the work was done.

"Aurelio." She said his name the way she set type: precisely, with total attention. "When this is over—"

"When this is over," he said, "I'm going to walk up this hill in daylight, at a loiterer's pace, with nothing in my hands and nowhere to be. And you're going to be annoyed at me for wasting time."

Her laugh broke in the middle, which was the bravest sound he had ever heard. She kissed him once, hard, tasting of metal and candle smoke, and pushed him out into the fog.

He was halfway down Orchard Street when he heard the boots.

They came up through the mist in formation, sixteen pairs, the oil carts rattling behind, Broch's voice carrying flat and official through the white air. Aurelio stepped aside into a doorway, composed his face into the blank courtesy of the service, and waited for the column to reach him.

"Sergeant." He fell into step as Broch drew level, matching the man's stride, wearing his uniform of lies like a coat that fit perfectly and weighed nothing and everything. "Premises confirmed. Eriksen's, upper Orchard Street. Doors unlocked, no resistance expected. Though I'd caution your men about the type loft—narrow stairs, and the floor's none of it trustworthy."

Broch grunted and waved the column forward.

Aurelio stood in the fog and watched the soldiers climb toward the shop where the man who had trusted him was even now tearing open a false wall, and he did not pray, because he had resigned from praying along with everything else. He simply stood, and counted, the way he had been trained to count, the way he would count for the rest of his life:

Sixteen soldiers. Two carts. One hour of daylight left to hide a truth in.

And behind him, downhill, the whole sleeping city that did not yet know what was coming—what was already printed, already cast in metal, already scattered in pieces across four shops and a cooper's yard and the hollow heart of an aqueduct, waiting only for dawn and ten thousand doors.

He turned and walked toward the aqueduct, and did not look back at the smoke.
