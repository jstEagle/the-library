## 18. Henrik's Stand

The knock came at the wrong hour.

Henrik Eriksen knew every hour of his shop the way a sailor knows the moods of a sea—the creak of the floorboards settling after midnight, the particular hush of four o'clock when the harbor fog pressed against the shutters and made even the lead in his type cases seem to breathe. This hour, just past dawn but before the market bells, belonged to no one. It was an empty hour, and into that emptiness came fists on wood, three blows, then three again, deliberate as a metronome.

He had been awake all night. He had been awake most nights since Paloma pulled the first proof from the press and wept over it with ink on her cheek, and Henrik had known—had known with the certainty of a man who has spent thirty years listening for footsteps—that the noise had traveled. Sound always travels. That was the whole purpose of printing, after all: to make a small sound in one room and have it arrive in ten thousand rooms. He should have remembered that the principle cut both ways.

The last forme had gone out the back not twenty minutes earlier. Aurelio had carried it, wrapped in oilcloth beneath his coat, sewn against his ribs like a second heart. Four shops across the city held their copies now, locked in iron frames, waiting. And this shop held the rest: the false wall of standing type, the archive behind it, the confession of Henrik's grandfather written in a dead man's hand—all of it stacked like powder in a magazine, waiting for the spark that was currently knocking on his door.

"Henrik." Paloma's voice from the stairs, low, urgent, already dressed—he could hear it in her step. She had learned to sleep clothed these past weeks, the way soldiers do. He hated that she had learned it. He hated more that he had been the one to teach her, without ever meaning to, simply by being a man who burned papers after dark.

"Stay up there," he said. "The loft."

"Papa—"

"Paloma." He said her name the way he said it only twice in a life: once the day she was born, and now. "The window. The rope is coiled by the north casement. You remember the drop to the cooper's yard."

Silence from the stairwell. Then, quieter, terrible in its obedience: "I remember."

* * *

He opened the door because refusing to open it would only make them break it, and broken doors announce things to neighbors, and neighbors were still, despite everything, a kind of currency he could spend. The sergeant who stood in the gray light was young—younger than Paloma—and wore the Consulate's new cockade, the one with the orchard branch crossed out by a black bar. Behind him stood six men with muskets, and behind them the street, and behind the street the whole machinery of Victor Sarn's tidy history, arriving at last to collect its footnotes.

"Master Eriksen." The sergeant consulted a paper. "By order of the Provisional Commission for Public Order, this premises is to be searched for seditious materials. You will open your workrooms."

"My workrooms are open," Henrik said. "They have been open for forty years. Any printer whose workrooms are closed is a printer with something to hide, and any printer with something to hide is out of business within the month. Come in. Mind the inkstone; it stains worse than sin."

The sergeant did not smile. Young men sent to do old men's cruelty rarely smiled; they saved their faces for afterward, when they would need them blank. He stepped past Henrik into the shop, and the smell hit him—Henrik watched it hit him—the smell of oil and metal and paper and lamp-black, the smell Paloma had been bathed in since birth, the smell that Henrik's own father had carried into his grave and that his grandfather had carried across a border under a different name entirely.

Eriksen. He had worn the name so long it fit him like his own skin. But names are typeset too, he thought, watching the soldiers move through his life with bayonets. Cast in someone else's foundry. His grandfather had been Eriksen only after the crossing, after the fire, after the family that had served as engravers to men who stole whole districts decided that some inheritances must be carried in secret or not at all. *We were not thieves,* the old confession read, *but we engraved the thieves' documents, and the shame of our hands bought us silence, and silence is a debt, and debts come due.* Thirty years Henrik had waited to pay it down. It appeared the payment would be collected today, in whatever coin he had left.

They worked through the front room quickly—composing sticks, galleys, the job cases of ordinary commercial type, nothing there but orders for wine labels and funeral announcements. The sergeant flipped through a stack of broadsheets with increasing frustration. Henrik followed at a courteous distance, hands loose at his sides, heart going like a drum in a procession.

"You print pamphlets?" the sergeant asked.

"I print what people pay me to print. Pamphlets pay poorly. Weddings pay well. Grief pays best of all—I've set more death notices than scripture." He let the bitterness show, the genuine article, the weariness of a tradesman. "If you're looking for sedition, Sergeant, you'll find my margins full of it. Every complaint about rents I ever heard passed through this shop on its way to being a bill of lading."

One of the soldiers laughed—a short, surprised bark—and the sergeant silenced him with a look. Good. A laughing soldier was a half-defeated one. Henrik had learned that in another lifetime, or so it felt: that authority fears laughter the way water fears oil.

Then the sergeant turned toward the back room, toward the false wall, and Henrik felt the morning tilt.

* * *

It was good work, the wall. He had built it himself, alone, over eleven nights two winters past, when his back was younger and the stakes seemed theoretical. A frame of standing type—cases upon cases, locked chases stacked to the ceiling, the great silent forest of metal that every print shop accumulates like a tree accumulates rings—and behind it, a cavity of three feet, plastered and shelved, holding everything: the forged grants in Sarn's own commission's hand, the original deeds with tenant families' names, the letters, the seal, the confession. Enough paper to hang every founder of the Consulate posthumously. Enough truth to fill a thousand presses.

The sergeant put his hand flat against the standing type and pushed.

The wall gave nothing. It was built to give nothing. But Henrik saw the calculation move behind the young man's eyes—saw him register the depth of the room versus the width of it, the way a man measures a coffin by eye without meaning to—and Henrik understood that the arithmetic was already done, that they would find it, that the only question left in the world was how many minutes he could buy.

Minutes. Aurelio had perhaps reached the second canal by now, if the fog held. Paloma needed four minutes to reach the window and six more to descend, if she went slowly enough not to break her neck and fast enough not to be seen. Eleven minutes, then. He had spent thirty years waiting; surely he could spend eleven minutes.

"Sergeant," Henrik said, and his voice surprised him by sounding almost gentle, "how old are you?"

The question landed strangely, as he'd intended. The young man frowned. "Twenty-three. What does—"

"When you were born, I cut the type for your parish's baptismal rolls. Little letters, your name among hundreds. Do you know what I remember about that job? Nothing. Not one face, not one name. Forty years of cutting letters for other people's lives, and it's all nothing." He moved, casually, toward the corner where the hand press stood, putting himself between the sergeant and the wall the way a shepherd stands between wolves and the fold—not to stop them, only to fix their attention. "But I'll tell you what isn't nothing. Paper keeps. Ink keeps. Whatever they've told you about the orchard question—about orderly settlement, about foreign agitation—it will keep too, longer than you live, longer than the men who told you. And someday a boy of twenty-three will read it and know who lied."

"Stand away from the press," the sergeant said, but his voice had changed pitch, and two of the soldiers shifted uneasily, and Henrik thought: *there it is. The crack. Every wall has one.*

"The press stays where it is," Henrik said. "So do I."

What happened next happened quickly and slowly both, the way deaths do. The sergeant ordered the search of the loft. Boots on the stairs. And Henrik—Henrik did the thing he had decided, somewhere in the deep ledger of himself, he would do if this morning ever came: he picked up the composing stick lying on the nearest stone, a humble tool, a stick of iron and wood that had set a hundred thousand honest words, and he threw it through the front window.

Glass exploded into the street like a shout. Heads turned along the lane; a fishwife began to cry out; somewhere a dog took up the alarm. Because a crowd, Henrik knew, was also a kind of wall, and crowds were slower to breach than doors. Two soldiers rushed to the broken glass. The sergeant shouted orders that tangled with the fishwife's outrage. And in the confusion above—Henrik did not see it, but he heard it, and hearing it was enough—the soft scrape of a casement opening, the whisper of rope over sill.

Go, little one. Go.

* * *

They beat him at the base of the false wall, which was fitting; he had built the wall, and it was right that it should be the last thing he touched. He did not remember much of it afterward—or rather, there was no afterward to remember in—but in the last interval of clarity he registered strange, small things: the taste of blood with iron in it, familiar, the same iron as his type; the sergeant shouting *break it open, break it open* with a hysteria that sounded almost like fear; and through the shattered window, beyond the soldiers' backs, the cooper's yard where a figure in a dark shawl dropped the last arm's length from a rope and vanished between the barrels, quick as a compositor's finger lifting a finished letter from its case.

Gone. Out. Into the city that was already rising—because he could hear it now, under everything, under the shouting and the splintering wood: bells, wrong bells, alarm bells from the harbor quarter, and beneath the bells a low continuous roar like surf, the sound of ten thousand people deciding at once that they had read enough history written by thieves.

The soldiers tore the wall apart with crowbars and musket butts. Standing type cascaded across the floor in a bright metallic flood, forty years of his craft scattered like a spilled purse, and through the widening gap they saw the shelves, the papers, the sealed archive—and the youngest soldier, the one who had laughed earlier, went pale as fresh proof sheet when he understood what he was looking at.

Henrik Eriksen sat with his back against the ruined wall, among the letters of his life, and smiled at them.

"It's true," he said. Blood at his teeth. "Every word of it. And it's already out of my hands. Print is patient, boys. It waits for you at the end of every lie."

The sergeant raised his pistol. Outside, the bells went on and on, and the crowd-roar swelled, and somewhere across the rooftops a girl with ink-stained fingers was running east with her father's name and her own, carrying the lantern into whatever came next.

Henrik closed his eyes and thought, absurdly, of the first letter he had ever cut: a lowercase *e*, aged nine, crooked-eyed, his grandfather's hand over his. *Cut it clean,* the old man had said. *Someone will read it long after we're gone.*

He had cut it clean. They all had.

The shot joined the bells.
