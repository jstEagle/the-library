## 22. Lantern and Orchard

Spring came to Vellamare the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and not to everything at once.

Paloma noticed it first in the mortar of the shop's front step, where a thread of green had forced itself up through the cracks over the winter, patient as anything she'd ever set in type. By March it was a small defiance of chickweed, and she found she could not bring herself to pull it. She stepped over it each morning instead, on her way to unlock the door her father had locked for thirty years.

The sign above the door was new. *Eriksen* was gone—not erased, never that, but folded away like a letter kept too long against the skin. In its place, cut by her own hand in a face no one in Vellamare had ever seen: **PALOMA OKONKWO-VANCE**. No—she had argued with herself about this for weeks, standing on a ladder with a brush and a pot of black while Aurelio held its feet steady below. In the end she had chosen simply **P. ERIKSEN**, because grief is a kind of inheritance too, and because Henrik would have scolded her for vanity. The letters were tall and narrow, with a lantern-shaped counter in the A—a small hollow light where the eye could rest. She had named the typeface *Lantern* the night she finished cutting the last punch, alone at the bench with the oil lamp turned low, her thumb still aching from the graver, and she had said the name aloud to the empty room because there was no one else to say it to anymore.

That was February. Now it was April, and the smell of the harbor had changed—less tar and brine, more blossom drifting down from the terraced orchards above the city that had not been burned after all. Some of them. Enough of them.

She unlocked the door and stood a moment in the doorway, letting the morning move through her. The press room smelled of ink and walnut oil and, faintly, of smoke that would never fully leave the plaster no matter how many times she whitewashed it. She had stopped trying. The stain above the false wall—the wall that had hidden the archive, the wall her father had died facing—was shaped like nothing at all and like everything, depending on the hour of day and the state of her heart. Some mornings it was only water damage. Some mornings it was a man's shoulders, broad and stubborn, planted before a door that would not open.

Today it was only water damage. That was a mercy. She took mercies where they came.

The new assembly had returned the shop to her in January, along with a document she kept framed behind the counter: an official acknowledgment, signed by three clerks and one minister, that the seizure of presses during the uprising had been "irregular." Irregular. She had laughed when she first read it—a short, ugly sound—and then she had wept, and then she had hung it on the wall anyway, because the words were true even if they were too small, and small true things were what she dealt in now. Her father had spent his life cutting letters large enough to be read across a market square. She had learned from the pamphlets that truth did not need to shout. It needed only to survive, and to be legible.

The hand press stood where it always had, cleaned and oiled, its iron arm polished by her sleeve every time she reached past it. Beside it, the galley of the master pages—her father's grandfather's confession, the true grants, the letters naming thieves—locked in their forme, waiting. She printed fifty copies every month and gave them away. Not nailed to doors anymore; those days were done, or transformed. Now they went into hands: schoolteachers who asked, tenants who came to the shop with old papers of their own and trembling questions, one gray-haired woman who had walked four hours from the hill districts just to touch the page that said her grandmother's name spelled correctly, with the accent mark the Consulate's clerks had dropped for sixty years.

*We are keeping the record,* Aurelio liked to say, in his census-taker's voice, the one that made the enormous seem manageable. *Not winning anything. Keeping it.* And then he would smile his tired smile and add, *Winning can look after itself.*

She heard his boots on the step now—heavy, unhurried, the tread of a man who had learned to stop sneaking and found he preferred it. She did not turn around. She let him come to her, which was a luxury she allowed herself exactly once a day and intended to keep allowing for as long as the world permitted.

"You're early," she said, sorting type she had already sorted.

"I couldn't sleep. The plum tree kept me up."

"The plum tree."

"It's gossiping," he said solemnly. "With the pear. About us."

She laughed despite herself—she was learning to do that again, though the laugh still came out rusted at the edges—and turned into his arms. He smelled of cold air and turned earth. Under his coat, she knew, was the notebook. He carried it everywhere still, the old census ledger with its columns and its corrections, the one with the fourth family member penciled and struck through and penciled again. Some men wore medals. Aurelio wore his arithmetic.

"Is it time?" she asked against his shoulder.

"It's past time. The ground's been ready for a week. I've been waiting for you to stop pretending you have work."

"I have work."

"You have forty sorts out of order and a customer at noon. Come on, Paloma."

* * *

Behind the press room, through the yard where the soldiers' cart had once backed up to the loading door, there was a rectangle of earth Aurelio had dug over in March while she watched from the window and pretended not to cry. It lay there now, dark and turned, fenced with salvaged laths from the crates the pamphlets had shipped in—one thousand copies, then five thousand more when the other printers ran their formes, the seed scattered to four shops and then to forty, until Sarn's men could not burn them faster than the city could read them.

They knelt side by side in the dirt.

"Apple here," Aurelio said, patting the ground. "Two plums. The pear goes by the fence so it has something to lean on. And—" he hesitated, and she saw his throat work, "—the cherry by the wall. If you still want it."

Henrik had loved cherries. This was not a fact Paloma had known until after he died, when a neighbor—an old woman named Maren whose attention had nearly ruined them all, whose testimony afterward had helped save them—had come to the door with a basket of preserved fruit and a story: that years ago, before Paloma was born, Henrik Eriksen had climbed Maren's cherry tree like a boy to steal fruit for his young wife, and had been caught, and had paid for the whole season's harvest rather than confess, and had eaten cherries every spring of his life afterward with the particular satisfaction of a man who had bought his own crime honestly.

"He'd hate this," Paloma said, pressing the bare-root cherry sapling into the hole Aurelio had made. "All this ceremony. He'd say a tree is a tree, get on with it."

"He'd say that," Aurelio agreed, shoveling soil around the roots. "And then he'd come out every morning to check on it and pretend he was checking on the fence."

The laugh again, easier this time. They worked in the rhythm they had learned in the type loft during those last terrible weeks—her hands and his, close enough to touch over the shared task, sometimes touching, never needing to make a point of it. Love, she had decided somewhere in the winter, was mostly this: another pair of hands on the same work. Everything else was ornament.

They planted six trees in all. Apple, apple, plum, plum, pear, cherry. When they finished, the yard looked sparse and foolish, six sticks in straight lines, and Paloma thought: *this is what a record looks like at the beginning.* Nobody applauds a composing stick half full. Nobody salutes a seedling. You do the work anyway, in the dark, with your hands, and trust the season.

Aurelio sat back on his heels and wiped his forehead with his wrist, leaving a streak of mud, and looked so much like a man counting households on a weary route that her chest went tight. "You know what I keep thinking?" he said. "The first day I came here. Counting heads. I wrote 'four persons' in my ledger and I didn't even know how right I was. Four persons. You, your father, your mother's memory, and the secret."

"And you," Paloma said. "You just hadn't been counted yet."

He looked at her then, really looked, the way he had learned to look at documents—with patience, with the willingness to see what was actually written rather than what the column expected—and she felt the familiar warmth climb her throat. They had not married. There had been talk of it, gentle talk, deferred by funerals and tribunals and the sheer labor of rebuilding a print trade out of confiscated wreckage. But she had sewn her name into his coat collar without telling him, and he had carved her initial into the handle of his best pen, and between them these quiet acts had the binding force of any contract the Consulate had ever forged, and more honesty than most.

"Come inside," she said, standing and brushing off her knees. "I want to show you something before Mrs. Ondel arrives."

* * *

On the workbench, under a cloth, lay a proof sheet.

It was not the grants. Those were finished, sacred, reprinted monthly like a rite. This was new—set in *Lantern*, the first complete page ever pulled from the face she had spent the winter cutting, punch by punch, matrix by matrix, until her thumbs were permanently ridged with graver scars that matched her father's.

Aurelio lifted the cloth and read. She watched him read, the way she always did: the slight forward lean, the lips moving on the difficult names, the moment his eyes reached the bottom line and stopped.

The page was titled, simply, *A Record of the Orchard Districts of Vellamare*. Below it, in smaller type: *being a true account compiled from original deeds, testimonies, and the census notes of A. Vance, with corrections.*

"You're printing the census," he said quietly.

"I'm printing *your* census. Every household. Every name the redrawn maps erased. The tenant families, the rents they paid, the boundaries as they were before the Consulate's clerks got hold of them." She touched the edge of the sheet. "Your supervisor told you to stop looking too closely. So I'm going to look closely enough for both of us, and put it where nobody can unsee it. The pamphlets won the argument. Books win the century."

He was silent a long moment. Outside, gulls argued over the harbor, and somewhere up the hill a church bell rang the quarter hour, and beneath all of it, if a person listened very hard, the city itself seemed to be making a low continuous sound—not anger anymore, not exactly. More like a press running. Like ten thousand impressions being taken of something that had finally, at last, been set in type.

"Henrik would have hated the serif on that R," Aurelio said at last, and his voice cracked on the second word, and she put her hand over his on the proof sheet, and neither of them said the rest of it, because the rest of it was already in every letter on the page: *for the man who kept the record thirty years in a locked cabinet, who died facing a wall so the words behind it could live.*

"He'd have hated it," Paloma agreed. "And then he'd have printed with it anyway, and complained the whole time."

The bell above the shop door rang. Mrs. Ondel, punctual as ever, come for her monthly copy and her gossip and, secretly, Paloma suspected, for the company—the old woman had lost a son in the fighting at the customs house and did not like her own kitchen's silence. Through the front window Paloma could see the street: cobbles washed clean by last night's rain, blossom from the upper orchards drifting down like slow pale type falling through the air, and beyond the rooftops, the harbor, and beyond the harbor, the road to the capital, where the Consulate still sat in its marble rooms drafting its careful, shrinking concessions.

The victory was partial. She knew it the way everyone in Vellamare knew it—in the price of bread, in the empty chair at the tribunal, in the way Sarn's name was still spoken in certain drawing rooms with respect instead of contempt. The districts had their boundaries back on paper. The capital kept its armies. Somewhere north, historians were already writing the official account of a rebellion that had, inconveniently, published its own footnotes.

Let them write it. There were presses now in four cities that owned copies of the formes, and a girl in Vellamare who could cut type in her sleep, and a former census taker who had memorized an entire archive and could reproduce it from memory onto any paper in any kingdom, and six young trees in a muddy yard that would bear fruit, God willing, long after every clerk and colonel currently drawing a salary from the Consulate had gone to whatever accounting awaited them.

"Paloma." Aurelio's voice, low. "The cherry tree. What will we do when it fruits?"

She looked at him—at the mud on his wrist, at the pen scarred with her initial in his breast pocket, at the whole improbable architecture of a life built out of grief and ink and the stubborn decision to keep counting heads honestly—and she smiled.

"We'll eat them," she said. "Every spring. With the particular satisfaction of people who bought their crime honestly." She took down the key to the press. "Now come help me. The record won't keep itself."

And outside, in the yard, the cherry tree stood in its fresh-turned earth, leaning toward the light, already beginning—quietly, invisibly, in the dark—to grow.
