## 1. The Weight of Glass

There were one hundred and six steps from the foot of the Gannet Point lighthouse to the lamp room, and Mirabel Vane had counted them so many times that the number had stopped being arithmetic and become a kind of heartbeat. One hundred and six steps, twice a night, and once more at dawn if the weather was mean enough to warrant it — which at Gannet Point, in November, it almost always was.

Mirabel climbed them now with the oil can balanced on one hip and the trimming kit in a leather roll under their arm, taking the stairs at the unhurried pace of someone who had long ago stopped pretending there might be anything waiting at the top but work. The stairwell smelled of brass polish and burnt paraffin and, faintly, of salt that had worked its way into the very stones of the tower over sixty years and would outlast every keeper who ever drew wages there.

Outside, the wind was doing what it always did in the last week of November: rehearsing.

The old fishermen down in the harbor had a name for these rehearsals. They called them the choir practices, because they said the great storms came to Gannet Point the way choristers came to chapel — early, in ones and twos, clearing their throats — before the whole terrible congregation arrived together sometime between the feast of Saint Andrew and the new year. Mirabel had lived through four of those great storms since coming to keep the light. The fifth had taken Ede.

They did not think about that on the stairs. Thinking about things on the stairs was dangerous; a keeper's feet needed to be dull and their mind duller, or else one hundred and six steps became a hundred and six opportunities to miss one and break an ankle on the iron treads, alone, with nobody due to notice for eleven hours. This was the kind of arithmetic Mirabel permitted themselves. Practical sums. Oil remaining: sufficient. Wicks remaining: three. Days since anyone had spoken to me about anything other than oil and weather: I have stopped counting, which is itself a kind of counting.

At the top, the lamp room door opened onto wind with a sound like a held note finally released, and Mirabel stepped into the glass drum at the crown of the tower and shut the world back out behind them.

The light itself — the great Fresnel lens, eight feet of stacked crystal prisms arranged around the flame like a glass beehive — was already lit and turning, throwing its beam out across the black water in the patient, revolving sweep that had been Gannet Point's whole argument with the sea for longer than anyone now living could remember. *Here is where the rocks are,* the light said, once every fourteen seconds. *Here is where the rocks are. Here is where the rocks are.* It was not a complicated argument, but it was a sincere one, and it never slept, and Mirabel loved it the way you love anything that has asked nothing of you but everything you had.

"Evening," Mirabel said to it.

This was not madness. Or if it was, it was a very tidy madness, of the sort the Board of Trade would have approved of. A keeper who spoke to the lamp was a keeper who noticed when the lamp sounded wrong, and after eleven years Mirabel could hear a wick burning crooked from the foot of the stairs. Besides, Ede had talked to it constantly — full conversations, arguments even, standing there with their sleeves rolled up telling the lens about the price of mackerel or the sermon at the chapel or the state of Mirabel's boots — and the light had never once answered them either, and Ede had gone right on talking, cheerful as Sunday, until the day the sea took them and the light went on saying *here is where the rocks are* to no one at all.

So. "Evening," Mirabel said, and set about the work.

The work was this: first, the oil reservoir, checked and topped from the can, the gauge read and noted. Then the wicks, trimmed square and true with the little brass-trimmed shears that had come with the station before Mirabel was born. Then the clockwork that turned the lens — wound, always, though tonight it wanted only half its measure. Then the glass.

The glass was the part Mirabel did not hurry.

Every surface of the lens had to be wiped free of the fine salt haze that crept in through seams no engineer could ever quite defeat, because a film of salt as thin as breath could steal a quarter of the light's reach, and a quarter of the light's reach, out past the reef where the water broke white over the Teeth, was the difference between a ship standing off and a ship standing into trouble. So Mirabel took the chamois and began at the lowest prism and worked upward, pane by pane, in slow circles, and the lamp room filled with the small squeaking music of cloth on crystal, and outside the wind rehearsed, and below the black water moved, and above the clouds tore open now and then to let down a handful of cold stars.

It was, Mirabel reflected — polishing, breathing, not thinking — the best hour of the day. Every day. The only hour in which nothing was required of them except care, and care was the one thing they had in inexhaustible supply, with nowhere else to spend it.

That thought tried to become another thought. Mirabel polished faster.

* *

The storm that had made Gannet Point famous — famous being perhaps too generous a word for what happened when London newspapers printed your harbor's name next to the words *forty souls lost* — had come ashore nine years before Mirabel ever saw the place. The fishermen still told it in the Anchor & Chain on nights when the beer was good and the weather was bad: how the sea had risen out of a clear afternoon like a thing insulted; how the water had come up Harbor Lane to the second step of the chapel; how the *Providence Jane* had gone onto the Teeth with all hands because her master had trusted his reckoning over the light. Forty souls. The number got said in a particular voice, the way you'd handle a cracked bell.

And every winter since, the sea came back to check whether the town had gotten comfortable.

Mirabel had arrived in the spring after the fourth such storm, young for a principal keeper and doubly young-looking for a widow — widower — whatever word the forms wanted; Mirabel had taken to writing *spouse deceased* in the margin and letting the clerks make of it what they would. Ede Vane had signed on as assistant keeper the same season, which was how the Board had learned that married couples could keep a light for the salary of one man and half a boy, and had considered this economy brilliant rather than cruel, which told you most of what you needed to know about the Board.

Ede. Who had sung to the lens. Who had planted — absurdly, gloriously — a row of sweet peas along the seaward wall of the keeper's cottage, knowing the salt wind would kill them by July, planting them again every April anyway, on the grounds that *a man ought to lose something small every year so he remembers how*. Who had gone out in the longboat with the relief crew the November before last when the *Merry Alice* signaled distress off the Teeth, because Ede had never in their life met a distress signal they didn't answer personally.

The light had swept the water all that night. It had done everything a light could do. It had shown exactly where the rocks were.

Mirabel had stood at the rail of the gallery until dawn, keeping the light with hands that belonged to somebody calmer, and at dawn the sea had given back one oar, half a boot, and nothing else, in the flat indifferent manner of the sea settling its accounts.

Afterward, people had been very kind, in the way of small towns, which is to say they had left things. A loaf of bread appeared on the doorstep. A jar of honey. Once, disconcertingly, a live hen, which Mirabel had kept, named Biscuit, and which remained the longest continuous conversation Mirabel had had with any living creature in two years.

Kindness, Mirabel had discovered, was nearly unbearable. Kindness required a response, and responses required words, and words required a person to say them, and the person Mirabel had been — the one who sang off-key on the gallery and argued with Ede about whether gulls had opinions — had gone out with the longboat too, in every way that mattered. What remained was a keeper. Keepers kept. Oil, wicks, glass, log. There was peace in it, Mirabel told themselves. There was even happiness, of a kind, the happiness of a well-run mechanism, and if sometimes the silence of the cottage felt less like peace than like a held breath — well, that was the wind. At Gannet Point it was always the wind.

The last prism gleamed. Mirabel stepped back, gave the lens a final critical look, and allowed it the small nod one professional gives another.

* * *

Downstairs, in the watch room, Mirabel wrote up the log by lamplight while the kettle muttered on the stove.

**Nov. 24th. Wind SW freshening, gusts to 40 kt by midnight. Sea moderate, heavy swell setting in from the SE. Barometer falling steady since noon. Lamp lit 6 hrs 12 min, burned clean. Clockwork wound. No vessels passed.**

Then, beneath the official line, in smaller letters, because the official ledger was the Board's property and inspected twice a year by men who believed the sea was a logistics problem:

*Gannets riding the swell past the Teeth again — third evening running. Old Tam Ollie says birds sitting on water mean a blow coming inside the week, and Brine Ollie (his brother, and a liar) says the opposite, so between them we know nothing, comfortably.*

*A light showed east-by-southeast around nine, low and green-ringed. Not the Kestrel — she's laid up. Might have been a coaster standing too far south for comfort. Watched her a quarter hour. She corrected. Whoever you are, sir, you're welcome.*

Mirabel hesitated, pen hovering, and then added:

*Cold tonight. First real cold. Should lay in extra coal against the frost.*

It was a foolish entry. The logbook was no place for coal inventories. But there was a pleasure in it, a small smuggled humanity, like leaving a coin under a loose flagstone for whoever came after. Someday some other keeper would read these margins and know that a person had sat here, not just a function. That mattered to Mirabel in a way they couldn't have defended before the Board, and therefore didn't try.

Biscuit scratched at the door to be let in from the coal shed, and Mirabel let her in, and she installed herself on the worn rug before the stove with the entitlement of royalty, and Mirabel put the kettle on properly and looked at the shelf where the cups lived.

There were two cups on the shelf.

One was Mirabel's — stout, brown, chipped at the rim, perfect. The other had been Ede's: pale blue with a painted wren on the side, because Ede had bought it at the harbor fair from a potter who swore the bird was a swallow, and Ede had insisted on the wren, and the two of them had laughed about it for years whenever anything small and stubborn needed describing. *"Wrennish,"* Ede would say of a contrary wind, of a determined weed, of Mirabel before breakfast. The cup hadn't been used in two years. It hadn't been thrown away either. It sat on the shelf collecting a fine dust that Mirabel noticed weekly and did not remove, because removing it would be a decision, and Mirabel had arranged their entire life, with great discipline, around making as few decisions as possible.

The kettle boiled. Mirabel took down the brown cup. And then — automatically, absently, the body remembering what the heart had forbidden — reached for the blue one too, set it beside the first on the tray, spooned tea into both.

Stopped.

Stood there in the small warm circle of the stove's light with a spoon in one hand and steam climbing past their face, looking at the two cups the way a man looks at a trap he has walked into with his eyes open.

The wind found the chimney and moaned down it, low and long, like something trying a door.

"It's habit," Mirabel said aloud, to the stove, to the cat, to nobody. "Nothing but habit."

Biscuit blinked at them with the frank disbelief cats reserve for human self-deceptions offered at close range.

Mirabel put the blue cup back on the shelf. Dusted it, first, without deciding to — the chamois was still in their pocket, and the wren came up bright and blue under the cloth, absurdly vivid, small and stubborn as its namesake — and then set it carefully, facing outward, where the stove-light caught it. Made one cup of tea instead of two. Drank it standing at the window with the shutter open a hand's width, watching the beam go round: *here is where the rocks are, here is where the rocks are.*

Beyond the reef the swell was building, long and slow, the sea gathering itself the way a congregation gathers. Old Tam Ollie's birds had known it all week. By Thursday, maybe Friday, the choir practice would end and the great storm would come up the coast with its whole terrible congregation, and Gannet Point would do what Gannet Point always did: stand, and shine, and count its losses after.

Mirabel washed the single cup, hung it on its hook, banked the stove, and climbed the hundred and six steps once more before bed to see that all was well. All was well. The light turned. The sea rehearsed.

And far down the dark coast road, unseen in the wind and the spitting rain, a supply boat was fighting its way toward the harbor with news that would arrive, wet and shouting, before morning — though Mirabel, banking the lamp and speaking to no one, knew none of it yet, and slept the sleep of the diligent, with two cups drying on the shelf behind them: one used, one shining, both waiting.
