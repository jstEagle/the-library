## 2. Names in the Log

The logbook of the Gannet Point light had a prescribed form. Mirabel Vane had copied it out by hand the first week they arrived, four years ago now, because the printed forms from the Trinity office always arrived damp and stuck together like leaves after rain. The columns were strict: *Date. Hour. Wind. Barometer. State of Sea. Lights Observed. Remarks.*

It was the last column that Mirabel had come to regard as their private country.

The official instructions were clear on the matter. *Remarks shall pertain to the operation of the light and matters of navigation.* Nothing about gulls. Nothing about the color of the sea at dusk. Certainly nothing about people, except insofar as people endangered themselves on the water, which was a matter of navigation and therefore permitted.

Mirabel dipped the pen, waited for the excess ink to run back into the well, and wrote:

*Jan. 9th. Wind SW freshening by evening, veering WSW near midnight. Barometer falling slow, 29.4. Sea moderate with a long swell out of the south that I do not care for. Lamp trimmed and lit at 4:40, glass polished. No lights observed but the starboard riding light of the* Margaret Jane *out of Penzance, standing well off the shoal as her master ought to know better than to trust his memory of it in this light.*

Then, in the smaller script that had become a second language, squeezed along the margin where the inspector's eye would slide past it without catching: *MJ's cook waving from the rail — new red muffler, someone loves him.*

There. That was the day recorded properly.

Mirabel blew on the page once, closed the book, and sat a moment with both hands flat on its cover, as though keeping something small and warm from escaping.

The gallery above creaked its old complaint against the wind, and below, down one hundred and six steps, the kitchen held the ghost of this morning's tea and the smell of lamp oil that lived in everything now, in the wood, in Mirabel's own hair, in the very bread. Ede had said once — laughing, oiling their hair back off their forehead with hands black to the knuckle — *You'll never get it out of us, Van. We'll smell like the lamp at our own funeral.* It was meant as a joke about the future. Mirabel had not known then that it would turn out to be a kind of prophecy, delivered cheerfully, like all Ede's best lines.

One cup this morning. Mirabel had caught themselves reaching for the second hook on the third morning after, and had trained the hand out of it since, mostly. Mostly. This morning the hand had gone straight to the single cup like an honest animal, and Mirabel had felt — not grief exactly, but the shape where grief would be if they let it in, the way you feel the dent in a cushion where someone used to sit.

Peace, they called it. They were quite firm about calling it peace. A lighthouse was a good place for peace: the rhythm of the work, the great lens turning its slow silver thought overhead, the sea saying the same sentence over and over until you stopped trying to hear meaning in it. Four years of that could sand a person smooth. Mirabel sometimes thought of themselves as a piece of sea-glass — worn down, frosted over, no longer sharp enough to cut anything, least of all memory.

The trouble with sea-glass was that it had been something else first.

* * *

At noon the supply boat came around the point, late and laboring, riding low.

Mirabel saw her first as a smudge against the grey water and knew her before any flag showed — the *Puffin*, Mr. Quill's tub, the only vessel foolhardy enough to make the winter run from Falmouth with cargo nobody strictly needed and everybody counted on. Tea, salt, rope, paraffin, the post, and whatever gossip Quill had netted on the way, which he considered part of the freight and charged nothing for, being unable in any case to keep it.

By the time the *Puffin* bumped against the landing stage below the lighthouse path, half the town had found reasons to be on the quay. That was the arithmetic of Gannet Point in winter: a supply boat was a festival, a newspaper, and a church social compressed into forty feet of leaking oak.

"Keeper!" Quill bawled up the steps, a barrel-chested man with a beard like a storm-tossed thicket and a voice built for contradicting weather. "Your paraffin! And a letter! And — " here he dropped his volume to a theatrical murmur that carried perfectly to everyone present, "— news."

"News," said Marta Tull, arriving at the rail with flour to her elbows, "is not freight, Ollie Quill, and you'll not make us stand in the wind for it. Out with it while we carry your crates."

Quill came up the steps two at a time with a crate under each arm, set them down by Mirabel's boots, and produced from inside his coat a letter and a face arranged carefully into seriousness.

"Trouble down coast," he said. "Helford way. And lower still."

Marta stopped mid-reach for a crate. "What kind of trouble?"

"Water." Quill said it plainly, which from Quill was itself alarming; he dealt chiefly in embellishment. "The marshes are over their banks and the low streets of the villages are standing in it. Three weeks of these sou'westerlies and a spring tide coming on top. They've boats tied to bedroom windows down in some of those places, Mirabel. Boats to bedroom windows."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The wind filled the pause with its usual commentary.

"That's Helford," said Cobb Fenwick at last, from the back, arms crossed. "That's not us. Different river, different banks."

"Aye," Quill said, "and the same sea."

He handed Mirabel the letter — it was from the Trinity office, Mirabel saw, which made it business — and busied himself with the crates while the little crowd on the quay absorbed the news the way sand absorbs water: slowly, unevenly, some of it running off, some sinking deep.

Mirabel broke the seal where they stood. The letter concerned stores, an inspection scheduled vaguely for spring, and a paragraph near the end that Mirabel read twice:

*You will further note reports of unusual tidal action on the southern coast. Keepers are instructed to maintain particular vigilance regarding their barometric readings and to record any anomaly with precision. The Board has the matter under consideration.*

*Under consideration.* Mirabel looked up at the horizon, where the long southern swell Quill's boat had been punching through all morning rolled in, patient and enormous, like something that had been sent ahead of something else.

"You'll stay for tea?" Marta was asking Quill. "I've a batch just out —"

"I'll stay for three batches," said Quill, "and tell you everything twice."

* * *

The afternoon went to unloading. Mirabel hauled crates up the lighthouse path with the rest of them, because a keeper who stood apart on ceremony would soon find the whole town standing apart from them, and besides, the work was good. Work was always good. Work asked nothing about the past and took no interest whatsoever in the hour after midnight when the wind got into the tower and sang.

Tam and Wren were not yet in Mirabel's life. That came later, with the flood and the knocking at the door. On this afternoon the lighthouse was still a place of one, and Mirabel climbed back up the hundred and six steps as the light went coppery, carrying the letter, a tin of tea, and the strange heaviness that comes of other people's bad news arriving in your own house.

They lit the lamp at 4:40 exactly. They polished the glass. They wound the clockwork that turned the lens, listening to it take up its patient ticking, and then they sat down at the desk in the watch room and opened the logbook to the day's entry.

*Jan. 9th, continued. Supply boat Puffin arrived 12:10, departed 3:55. Paraffin received, 6 tins. Post received, 1 item, official.*

Then the pen hovered. Then it moved.

*Remarks, unofficial:* (the word was there every time, like a signature, like a confession) *Mr. Quill reports flooding on the southern coast, villages standing in water, boats tied to bedroom windows. The Board writes that the matter is "under consideration," which is what boards say when they mean they have put a paper in a drawer. The swell out of the south has not gone down since noon. I have lived beside this sea my whole life and it has two voices, the ordinary one and the other one, and today I heard the other one under the first, the way you hear a second person breathing in a room you thought held only you.*

Mirabel stopped. Read it back. Did not strike it.

And then, because the habit was stronger than the dread, the small script crept on down the margin, recording what the ledger forbade:

*Marta Tull gave Ollie Quill three loaves and pretended she hadn't counted them, and he pretended he hadn't noticed, and both of them would die of shame if either admitted the other was kind. Cobb Fenwick stood at the back of everything as usual, but I saw him give his spot nearest the fire on the Puffin to young Silas Reeve, whose hands were blue, and if you repeat that I will deny writing it. Mrs. Penhale asked after me. I said all was well. She looked at me the way people do.*

The way people do. With pity, was what she'd looked like, and concern, and the terrible patience of someone waiting for you to stop performing wellness.

Mirabel capped the inkwell harder than necessary.

Below, the sea kept talking. Above, the lens turned, pouring its beam out over the water in long white strokes, and Mirabel sat in the sweep of reflected light and listened to the tower tick and thought about boats tied to bedroom windows, and how far away that seemed, and how the sea did not recognize distance as a kindness. Water was not a local event. Water was one thing, everywhere, all one body, and when it rose anywhere it had merely risen somewhere else first and was on its way.

The barometer stood at 29.1 now, and falling. Not fast. But steadily, the way a guest descends stairs who intends to stay.

Mirabel wrote the reading in its column. Official. Precise. Correct.

And then, in the margin, small as a secret: *Ede would already know what it means. Ede read weather the way Marta reads dough. I am reading it alone and pretending that is the same thing.*

The pen scratched. Outside, the first spit of rain came against the glass, fine as sifted flour, and the wind backed a point to the south and gathered itself, quietly, like a man rolling up his sleeves.

Mirabel trimmed the wick a final time, watched the flame stand up bright and steady in its great cage of crystal, and told the empty room, as they told it every night, "All's well."

The room, being a room, said nothing back. But far down the coast, in places whose names Mirabel knew from charts and from Quill's stories — Helford, Gweek, the low green lanes going under — the water was rising in the dark over doorstep and sill, and somewhere in a village called Durgan a boy and a girl were sleeping with their grandmother between them and the sound of it, none of the three yet knowing how the next tide would spend them.

The lamp turned. The log lay open on the desk, its margins filling, night by night, with everything the official record would never hold.

In eleven days, the knock would come at the lighthouse door.
