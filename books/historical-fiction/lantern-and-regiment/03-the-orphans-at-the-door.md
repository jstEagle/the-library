## 3. The Orphans at the Door

The knock came at half past two in the morning, which was the hour Mirabel had long ago decided belonged exclusively to God, to gulls, and to no one else.

They were on the second landing with a rag and a tin of whiting, polishing the brass collar of the great lens, when the sound reached them — three knocks, small ones, spaced like someone who had to gather courage between each. Not the urgent hammering of distress. The polite knocking of people who feared they were imposing even as they drowned.

Mirabel set down the rag. They stood very still for a moment, the way one does when a house that has been silent for a year makes a noise it has no business making.

"Nobody's there," they told the lens.

The lens said nothing, being glass, but it caught the lamplight and threw it out over the water in that patient golden wheel it made every night, and Mirabel went down the stairs anyway, one hundred and twelve steps, counting them as always because counting kept other thoughts from getting in.

The door at the foot of the tower opened onto wind and rain and two children.

* * *

The girl was perhaps eleven, though she held herself like a woman of sixty who had been badly used by her investments. She stood in front of her brother with her arms spread wide, as if she could absorb whatever the lighthouse might do to him first. The boy was small enough that Mirabel's first thought — absurd, unhelpful — was that he looked like something the tide had brought in and not yet finished arranging. His lips were blue. He was shaking in a way that Mirabel knew from long experience was past shivering and into something the body did when it was quietly giving up an argument.

"We knocked," the girl said, "because the church is flooded."

"The church," Mirabel said.

"And the schoolhouse. And Mrs. Pellow's. And—" Her voice snagged on something and she hauled it back like a net. "Our gran's cottage is gone. Off the Point. It just went."

Mirabel looked past them, down the dark slope toward the town. From here, in ordinary weather, you could see the lights of Gannet Point strung along the harbor like beads on a wire. Tonight there were fewer beads than there should have been, and where the lower street had been there was only black water with a pale skin of foam moving on it, and the rain coming down in ropes.

"You're the Tallow children," Mirabel said. It came out before they'd decided to say anything at all. "Wren and Tam."

The girl blinked. "You know us?"

"I know everyone." Mirabel stepped back and pulled the door wider. "In. Quickly, before the sea changes its mind about you."

* * *

What followed was, Mirabel reflected later, less like hospitality and more like salvage.

There were no blankets in the tower room below the lamp — Ede had taken the good ones on the last voyage, and the ones left in the chest smelled of camphor and of a grief Mirabel did not open on weeknights. There was oil, plenty of oil; there was tea, half a tin of it, hoarded against some emergency Mirabel had privately assumed would be their own death and not this; there was bread gone hard as a hymnal, which softened beautifully in hot milk if you had milk, and Mirabel found, to their surprise, that they did.

The boy — Tam — sat wrapped in a blanket on the bench by the stove, dripping onto flagstones that had not seen another human footprint since the mason had laid them, and said nothing. He said nothing so completely, so thoroughly, that Mirabel began to feel their own silence all around the room like a draft.

"He doesn't talk," Wren said. She said it the way one reports weather, or the price of coal. "Since Gran. He talked before. He talked a great deal before, actually. You couldn't get him to stop." She smoothed his wet hair back from his forehead with a hand that shook only slightly. "The water took the cottage while we were on the hill. We watched it go. He was holding her hand and then he wasn't."

"I'm sorry," Mirabel said, and heard how thin the words were, two pennies offered where a debt was owed. They put the mug of sweet milk into Tam's hands and closed his fingers around it. "Drink that. All of it. Your sister too."

Wren drank hers in four swallows and then looked ashamed of having done it, sitting up straight as a mast.

"There's more," Mirabel said, which was a lie, there was exactly one more mug's worth, and poured it anyway.

* * *

By the time the stove had done what stoves do and the children's color had come back from blue to merely pale, the sky outside the seaward window had gone the color of wet slate, and the rain had settled in for the long argument it intended to have with the coast.

"You can't go down tonight," Mirabel said. "Or tomorrow, likely. The lower road's under. Where were you thinking of going?"

"The Harrow house," Wren said. "Aunt Sef. She'll take us."

"Aunt Sef lives past the cut, doesn't she? On the far side?"

Wren nodded, and then, catching something in Mirabel's face, nodded again, harder, as though agreement were a rope she could pull herself across the flood with.

"That road's gone or near enough," Mirabel said. "And nobody's rowing anywhere in this. So." They rubbed a hand across their face and felt the grit of lamp-black still in the creases. "You'll sleep here tonight. In the tower. There are bunks — well. There is a bunk, and there are blankets that smell like a chemist's shop, and tomorrow, if the water's dropped, I'll walk you round to your aunt myself."

"One night," Wren said. She said it quickly, like snatching at a coin before someone took it back. "We won't be any trouble. I can work. I've gutted fish since I was seven and I can mend nets and I know my letters."

"I don't doubt it."

"Tam can carry things. He's strong for his size."

"I don't doubt that either." Mirabel stood, and found themselves standing in the particular posture of a person who has just realized the house has opinions. "One night," they said, mostly to the stove. "Upstairs with you, both. The stairs are steep, so hold the rail. And mind — the lamp turns above you all night. You'll hear it. Everyone hears it the first night and thinks they won't sleep, and everyone sleeps."

Tam looked up at them then — the first time those grey eyes had lifted off the middle distance since the door — and Mirabel saw in them something they recognized with a sick lurch: the look of a person standing inside a life that had ended without informing them. Mirabel knew that country. They had been living in it a year.

"Go on," they said gently. "Up you go. I'll bank the fire."

* * *

They did not sleep, of course. Mirabel lay on the narrow bunk in the watch room with the children in the next — Wren curled around her brother like a comma around a word — and listened to the great lamp turning overhead, its gears murmuring through the floorboards like some enormous patient animal digesting the night.

It occurred to Mirabel, somewhere past three o'clock, that they had spoken more words tonight than in the whole of the previous month, and that most of them had been lies: *there's more*, *one night*, *everyone sleeps*. A keeper ought to keep a better account.

It further occurred to Mirabel that when Wren had said *our gran's cottage is gone*, Mirabel had thought — instantly, shamefully — *Ede's coat was in that cottage.* No. That was wrong. Ede's coat was here, in the chest at the foot of the bunk, where it had been for a year, because Mirabel could neither wear it nor give it away nor burn it. The thought had been something else, something worse: that the sea had now taken a grandmother's cottage and two children's whole world, and had done it within sight of a light that was supposed to mean safety, and that Mirabel had been upstairs polishing brass while it happened, and had known none of it.

The beam wheeled. The rain wrote its long nonsense on the glass.

Below, faintly, Tam coughed in his sleep, and Wren murmured something to him — a word, maybe two, the kind of thing sisters say without waking — and the tower, which for three hundred and seventy-odd nights had contained exactly one heartbeat and a clockwork mechanism, now contained four heartbeats and a mechanism, and the mechanism seemed, somehow, quieter.

Mirabel slept at last a little before dawn, and dreamed of nothing at all, which was itself remarkable, and woke to find a small paper boat sitting on the floor beside the bunk, folded from the flyleaf of the old supply ledger with a precision that would have shamed a sailmaker. It was pointed toward the door. As if it were leaving. Or as if it were showing the way out.

Downstairs, the kettle was already on. Someone small and determined was trying to reach it.

"Hold on—" Mirabel took the stairs two at a time. "That's scalding. What did I say about—"

Wren stood at the stove with two mugs already filled, wearing an expression of tremendous reasonableness. "I was going to bring yours up."

Mirabel looked at the mugs. Two of them. Set out side by side on the scrubbed table, steam rising, the morning grey and clean beyond the salt-fogged glass.

For a moment — just a moment — they saw another morning, years ago, two cups set out the same way, and Ede's voice saying *you always pour mine short, you think I don't notice* — and then the moment passed through them like cold water and was gone, and the two cups on the table were simply two cups, steaming, waiting, belonging to two living children who had nowhere else to be.

"Sit down," Mirabel said, and their voice came out rougher than the tea. "Both of you. And after breakfast — after breakfast, we'll see about the road."

But they both of them knew, and the lighthouse knew, and the sea out beyond the Point knew best of all, that the water had not gone down. Far from it. Even as they drank, a supply boat was beating up from the south under shortened sail, carrying the first news of what the flood had done to the towns down the coast — and the first hint of what was coming for Gannet Point, riding in on the same grey water, wearing red coats.
