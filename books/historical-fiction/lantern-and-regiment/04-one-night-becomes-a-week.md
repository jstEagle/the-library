## 4. One Night Becomes a Week

The morning after the storm, Mirabel Vane woke to the sound of someone else breathing in the lighthouse.

It was a small sound, really — the quick, shallow rhythm of a child asleep in the storeroom below, and beneath it the slower snore of an even smaller child curled against Wren's side like a whelk in its shell. But to a person who had spent three hundred and forty-one nights listening to nothing but wind, sea, and their own heart doing its stubborn work, it might as well have been a full orchestra tuning up.

Mirabel lay still in the narrow bed and told themselves the arrangement was temporary. The children had eaten. They had slept. Come morning — this morning — they would walk down to the harbor road and find some aunt or cousin or kindly neighbor with room by a fire, and the lighthouse would return to its proper silence, which was not loneliness at all but peace, thank you very much.

Then Tam appeared at the bedroom door holding one of Mirabel's boots, both hands wrapped around it as though it were a rescued kitten.

"It's wet," he said.

"It is," Mirabel agreed, sitting up.

"I dried it."

"You did." The boot was, in fact, warm from the stove. Mirabel put it on. It fit better than it had in months, though that was probably sentiment.

Wren was already in the kitchen — Mirabel's kitchen, the little iron stove room at the tower's base — standing on a stool and stirring the porridge with the gravity of a harbormaster. She had found the salt without being told. She had also found the honey, which had been hidden behind the lamp oil specifically so that no one would find it, and had applied it to her brother's bowl with the generosity of a woman spending money she'd just stolen.

"One night," Mirabel said, mostly to themselves. "You said one night."

The porridge, for the record, was excellent. Better than Mirabel's. This was annoying, and Mirabel resolved to mention it never.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about children: they are weather. They come into a house the way fog comes off the sea, without knocking, without reason, and suddenly everything is damp and changed and you cannot remember what the view looked like before.

By the second day, Wren had reorganized the storeroom. By the third day, she had opinions about how Mirabel coiled line ("too loose, it tangles"), which stung precisely because she was right. By the fourth day Tam had claimed the corner by the south window, where he sat folding scraps of paper into boats and setting them on the sill in silent flotillas of two and three.

And Mirabel — fool, sentimental fool, keeper of exactly one night's promise — began teaching them things.

It started innocently enough. A wick needed trimming twice a watch; Wren asked why; Mirabel answered; and then, because the girl was looking at the great lens the way Mirabel had once looked at it, hungry and awed, Mirabel found themselves saying, "Well. If you're going to ask, you may as well learn properly."

He taught her the brass scissors first — how dull blades crush a wick instead of cutting it, and a crushed wick smokes, and smoke dirties the glass, and dirty glass dims the light, and a dimmed light has killed men on black water. Wren repeated this back like scripture. Then he showed her the lens itself: how to touch it only through the chamois, how to breathe sideways while polishing so no breath-fog bloomed on the glass, how each of the great prisms was ground so the smallest flame became a beam that could reach a ship five miles out in the worst soup the winter could brew.

"Why does it matter so much?" she asked, on the fifth day, her sleeve rolled up, her small hand steady on the chamois.

"Because somebody's father is out there," Mirabel said. "Somebody's daughter. Every night, somewhere past the point, there's a boat with people in it who can't see anything except what we give them. That's the whole job. You don't get tired of it. You don't get careless. Because they can't afford for you to be either."

Wren considered this with the seriousness of a judge sentencing a man to the gallows or the crown.

"Like Gran," she said. "She used to say the light was the town's promise. That as long as it burned, nobody got to say Gannet Point didn't care."

Mirabel's throat did something complicated. "Your gran sounds like she knew lamps."

"She knew everything." Wren said it simply, without drama, and went back to polishing, and Mirabel turned away to check the oil reservoir for no reason whatsoever, because there was a thing happening behind their eyes that they refused to permit.

Tam was harder. Tam said nothing — hadn't said a word since the flood took his grandmother's cottage, and possibly since before that; Wren wasn't sure and Mirabel didn't press. But the boy watched hands. He watched Mirabel splice a rope and his whole body leaned toward it like a plant toward sun, so on the sixth day Mirabel put a length of line in those small, cold hands and showed him the overhand knot.

Tam's fingers were quick. Quicker than Mirabel's, if truth be told. Within two days he could coil a line in flat, perfect circles — "like a cinnamon bun," Wren said, and Tam almost smiled, which was the closest thing to speech anyone had gotten out of him. He learned the bowline. He learned the figure-eight. He sat by the hour with his paper boats and his rope, and when the wind came up hard against the gallery glass he would look up, every time, toward the sea, with an expression Mirabel recognized because they wore it too: the expression of someone listening for a name.

"He's not mute," Wren said quietly, one evening, while Tam slept. "Gran said grief sits on some tongues. Hers sat on hers after Grandad drowned. She didn't speak for near a year." Wren poked the fire. "She talked plenty after."

"Did she," Mirabel said.

"Aye. Mostly complaints about fish prices." And Wren smiled, and Mirabel laughed — actually laughed, a rusty, unpracticed sound that startled them both — and then stood abruptly and announced that the lamp glass wanted checking, though it was four hours until lighting time and they both knew it.

* * *

On the seventh day, Mirabel came down from the gallery at dawn and nearly tripped over a basket.

It sat on the doorstep, square and honest: two smoked herring, a loaf still warm enough to fog the wicker, and a twist of paper holding salt. No note. Mirabel stood over it for a long moment, then carried it inside and set it on the table and looked at it the way one looks at a letter from a stranger who knows too much.

"That's Mrs. Pellowe's basket," Wren said, appearing at their elbow. "I know the weave. Her boy nets herring off the north rocks."

"How do you know Mrs. Pellowe's baskets?"

"Everyone knows everyone's baskets here. Gran said a town's like a net — you can tell who tied which knot." Wren took the loaf and began slicing it with the confidence of ownership. "There'll be more."

"There will not—"

There were more. The next morning: eggs, six of them, tucked in straw. The morning after: a jug of milk and, inexplicably, a jar of pickled onions that made Wren cheer aloud and Tam clutch to his chest like treasure. Fish, bread, butter wrapped in cabbage leaves, a bundle of candles, once — memorably — an entire apple pie with a note pinned to it that read, in a fisherman's fist: *For the keeper and the young 'uns. The light was kind to my boys Tuesday night.*

Mirabel read that note eleven times. *My boys.* Someone had been out in the dark on Tuesday, in the squall, and had come home because the beam had reached them, because Mirabel had trimmed the wick and polished the glass and done the work that was not work but promise. And someone had climbed the path in the cold to leave pie.

"They're thanking you," Wren said, watching Mirabel's face.

"They're thanking the light."

"No," Wren said, with the terrible precision of nine-year-olds everywhere, "they're thanking *you*. Gran always said the lamp doesn't trim itself." She hesitated, then added, softer: "People are glad you've got us. I think they were worried. About you. Before."

"Worried," Mirabel repeated. "I keep the finest light on the coast. What is there to worry about?"

Wren looked around the kitchen — at the single chair, the single cup, the single of everything — and had the wisdom, rare in the young, to say nothing at all.

* * *

That night, after the lamp was lit and turning, Mirabel sat at the table with the log book open and wrote the weather, the wind, the visibility. Routine. Doctrine. And then, as had become habit, added the small forbidden marginalia: *Mrs. Pellowe's herring, generous. Pie from the Cobb family boat. Wren trims a wick cleaner now than I do, though I would sooner swallow the chamois than tell her.*

They stopped. Read it back. Heard Ede's voice in their ear, amused, fond: *You're keeping two ledgers, love. One for the Admiralty and one for the truth.*

"Ede," Mirabel said to the empty chair across the table, "there are children sleeping under this roof."

The chair said nothing. Chairs are famously reticent. But the house did not feel empty, was the trouble. The house felt inhabited — by snoring and paper boats and a girl who hummed while she polished glass, badly, all wrong notes, gloriously. The silence Mirabel had mistaken for peace for a year had been, it turned out, only silence. This was something else. This had weight and warmth and crumbs in it.

*Temporary,* Mirabel wrote firmly, at the bottom of the log page, where no inspector would ever see it. *One week at most.*

Then Mirabel blew out the candle, listened a moment to the two sets of breathing below stairs, and — telling themselves it was merely efficient — moved their boots closer to the stove so they'd be warm for the morning.

In the morning there were eggs again, and Tam had folded a new boat and set it on Mirabel's log book, and Wren had already gone up to the lamp room without asking, because she knew the way now, because she belonged to the routine the way the routine was beginning to belong to her.

And far down the coast, past the flooded fields, past the drowning lanes — though no one in Gannet Point knew it yet — men in red coats were striking camp, and an order was being folded into a dispatch case, and the name *Gannet Point* was traveling north toward London in ink that would not wash out.

But that was tomorrow's weather. For one more morning, the porridge was good, the light held, and the keeper of the Gannet Point lamp set out three cups without thinking, and caught themselves doing it, and — for the first time — did not put the third one back.
