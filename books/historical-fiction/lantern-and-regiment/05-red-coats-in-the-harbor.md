## 5. Red Coats in the Harbor

Sergeant Nathaniel Hollis had been a soldier for nineteen years, and in that time he had learned to read a town the way other men read weather. Most towns announced themselves long before you reached them — by the smell of their chimneys, the state of their fences, whether the dogs barked at soldiers out of fear or merely out of habit. Gannet Point, as the transport boat came around the headland through a thin gray drizzle, smelled of woodsmoke and salt fish and, faintly, of bread. The dogs did not bark at all. They sat along the seawall in a row, watching the red coats come ashore with the patient disapproval of cats observing new furniture.

"Cheerful place," said Private Dunn beside him.

"It's raining," Hollis said.

"It's always raining, Sarge."

"Then you've no basis for comparison."

Hollis stepped onto the cobbles of the harbor slip and felt immediately that he had made some kind of error, though he could not have said what. It was not hostility. Nobody shouted. Nobody threw anything, which put Gannet Point ahead of three towns he'd requisitioned in the last year. It was something quieter than hostility and harder to answer. A woman mending a net looked up at him, met his eyes, and went back to her net — not quickly, not slowly, just with the unhurried confidence of someone who had decided he was not worth interrupting her work for. A boy of perhaps ten stood on an upturned crate and watched the soldiers unload crates of forms and ink and a portable writing desk, and his expression was not fear. It was arithmetic. He was counting them.

Hollis had seen that look before, in a village in Ireland, on the face of an old priest who counted the soldiers coming up the lane and then counted again, slower, as if hoping the number would come out different the second time.

The customs house stood at the head of the harbor, a squat stone building with a slate roof and a sign whose paint had given up sometime in the previous decade. Inside it was dry, which after four days on the transport was nearly enough to make Hollis sentimental about it. Captain Voss had already claimed the back room for himself. Colonel Ashworth would arrive by morning with the rest of the column; tonight it was Hollis's job to make the building ready to receive him, which meant, in practice, to make it ready to receive paper.

There was more paper than Hollis had ever seen assembled for so small a place. Requisition orders. Census abstracts. Tonnage tables. Blank manifests in triplicate. A sealed folio from the Admiralty marked MOST SECRET that Voss handled like it might bite. And beneath it all, stacked in oilcloth, the reason they had come: the evacuation schedules for the flooded coast, forty miles of drowned farmland and half-drowned villages to the south, with Gannet Point — its harbor, its warehouses, its one deep-water pier — designated as the staging ground for the whole wretched business.

"Set the manifests on the dry shelf," Voss said, without looking up. He was a thin man with the complexion of uncooked pastry and the manner of someone perpetually surprised that the world continued to require his attention. "And send someone for the town records. The parish ledger, the customs rolls, the household registers. All of it."

"All of it, sir?"

"The colonel wants a complete accounting of the population by tomorrow noon. Names, trades, ages, households." Voss turned a page. "We can't evacuate what we can't count, Sergeant."

No, Hollis thought. You can't. He'd learned that lesson two years ago, in a river town where the census had missed eleven families living on barges, and those eleven families had spent three days on a quay in the rain while officers argued about whether they existed. He had been the one sent to tell them to wait. He still remembered a girl holding a chicken under her arm like a doll, asking him if the chicken could come.

He sent Dunn and two others out into the rain with instructions to collect the town's records, and then he unpacked the writing desk, squared the ink bottles, and tried not to listen to the sound of boots going door to door in a town that smelled of bread.

* * *

They brought the ledger back within the hour, and Hollis knew before he opened it that something was wrong with it — or rather, that something was right with it in a way that made him uneasy. It was not an official document. Official documents had margins and uniform hands and the smell of government about them. This was a fat, salt-stained book bound in oiled canvas, the kind fishermen used for catch tallies, and it had been carried to the customs house by a delegation: a broad woman with flour on her forearms who introduced herself as Marta Tull, baker, and a lean old man with a crab-white scar down his cheek who did not introduce himself at all but stood with his arms folded, watching.

"This is the town book," Marta said. She set it on the table with both hands, the way you set down something alive. "Every household. Who lives where, who's kin to who, who owes the doctor, who's owed by the cooper. Births, deaths, marriages, boats lost, boats built. My grandmother started it. I keep it now."

"You keep the town's records," Hollis said. "Not the parish?"

"The vicar keeps souls. We keep people." She said it without any particular defiance, as though stating that the tide came twice a day. "There's a difference."

Hollis opened the book. The hand changed every few pages — grandmother to mother to daughter, decades of it — but it never became careless. And in the margins, in smaller script, were the things no clerk would have written: *Ede Vane taught Tam Cobb's boy to swim, June '69.* *Old Nance's hip bad since the winter of the great swell — she'll not manage stairs.* *Marta's oven cracked; shared with the Fenwicks until mended.* Whole lives annotated like footnotes. He found himself reading a page about herring quotas and learning that a man named Brine Ollie — no Christian name recorded, and Hollis suspected none existed — had once navigated the inner reef blind in fog to bring a doctor through, and that the entry was initialed by three different keepers of the book across thirty years, each confirming it as if it were scripture.

"What is this?" Dunn asked, peering over his shoulder.

"It's a census," Hollis said.

"It's a mess, is what it is. Half these notes aren't even proper entries."

"No," Hollis said quietly. "It's the only proper entry I've ever seen." He closed the book. "Thank you, Mrs. Tull. The army will take custody of this for the duration."

"And when's the duration done?" the scarred old man asked. First words he'd spoken.

"When the evacuation's complete."

"And till then? When we want to know who owes who for a barrel of nails?"

"Then you'll apply to this office. In writing."

The old man laughed — one short bark, entirely without humor. "In writing. Aye. That'll suit us. Half this town can't write and the other half writes like crabs walking." He touched the ledger's cover once, lightly, the way you'd touch a horse you were handing over to a stranger, and walked out into the rain. Marta lingered a moment longer.

"Sergeant." She had read his rank off his sleeve without asking. "That book's not paper to us. It's the town remembering itself. You mind how you handle it."

"I will, ma'am."

"That's what they all say." But she went, and left the smell of bread behind her, and Hollis sat looking at the ledger for a long moment before entering it into the requisition log: *One (1) volume, household register, condition worn, contents complete.*

Then, because no regulation forbade it and because it seemed important, he added: *Handle with care.*

* * *

By evening the town had arranged itself around the occupation the way water arranges itself around a rock — not yielding, exactly, simply flowing past and wearing it down by patience. Soldiers were billeted in the wool warehouse. A cook discovered the bakery and came back speaking of the Tull woman's seed loaves in tones usually reserved for religious experience. Two privates got into a friendly argument with fishermen about the best bait for winter cod and lost, comprehensively, and were better liked for losing. The dogs, having concluded the soldiers were furniture, began sleeping against their boots for warmth.

Hollis walked the harbor wall after supper because he slept badly in new places and because walking let him think. The rain had stopped. Out at the point, the lighthouse turned its beam with the slow regularity of a heartbeat — sweep, dark, sweep, dark — and he found himself timing his steps to it without meaning to.

"There's your trouble," said a voice.

Brine Ollie was sitting on a bollard with a pipe, having appeared from nowhere in the manner of men who own no fixed address. "That light," he said, nodding toward it. "Been burning every night for years. Never missed. Storm took the roof off the oil store two winters back and it never missed. There's folk in this town that measure everything by it. Supper by the first sweep, bed by the third." He sucked his pipe. "You lot come ashore with your papers, and the first thing you'll want is to know everything. But there's things that light knows about this coast that your colonel's maps don't. Mind that."

"Is that a warning, Mr. Ollie?"

"It's a tide table, Sergeant. Warnings are for things you can avoid."

Hollis left him to his pipe and his bollard and walked on, and the beam swept over him, warm and impersonal, and he thought: *sweep, dark, sweep.* A town measured by a light. A census kept in margins. People who counted soldiers twice, hoping the number would change.

Back at the customs house, Captain Voss was awake and unhappy, which Hollis gathered was his permanent condition. "The household register is inadequate," Voss said. "It's annotated. It's sentimental. Half the entries are in different hands. The colonel requires a clean manifest — names, ages, trades, nothing else. You'll begin transcribing a fair copy tonight."

"A fair copy without the annotations, sir?"

"Without the annotations. Marginalia is not data, Sergeant."

Hollis looked at the open ledger, at the small careful script in the margins — *Old Nance's hip bad since the winter of the great swell* — and understood, with a clarity that settled in his chest like cold iron, precisely what was being thrown away. A list of names could be counted. The margins could be *saved*. And the colonel's arithmetic, whatever it proved to be, would run on the numbers alone.

"Yes, sir," he said. "I'll begin tonight."

He copied until the candle guttered, transcribing the clean facts and leaving out the lives, and near midnight he caught himself pausing over one entry — *Vane, M., lighthouse keeper, widow/er, one (1) household* — and beneath it, in the margin, in a hand steadier than most: *Keeps the light. Hasn't missed a night since Ede went out with the *Providence*. Sends bread down to Old Nance Tuesdays, says it's the bakery's idea.*

Hollis looked at that line for a long time. Then he dipped his pen, and instead of copying it, he wrote it out separately, on a loose scrap of his own paper, and folded the scrap into his breast pocket, where regulations had nothing whatsoever to say about what a sergeant was permitted to keep.

Outside, the beam swept the harbor — sweep, dark, sweep — and somewhere up the coast, he knew without knowing how, the water was already rising.
