## 6. The Ledger with All the Names

The summons came at half past seven in the morning, which was an hour Mirabel Vane had long ago claimed as their own — the hour between dousing the lamp and winding the clockwork, when the tower belonged to no one but them and the gulls.

It came in the form of Private Denny, who was nineteen if he was a day, whose boots were polished to a shine that suggested terror rather than pride, and who stood at the foot of the lighthouse path breathing clouds into the cold like a small locomotive.

"Begging your pardon," he said, when Mirabel opened the door with a polishing cloth still over one shoulder like a sash. "Sergeant's compliments. The colonel requests your presence at the customs house."

"Requests," said Mirabel.

"Yes'm."

"At half past seven in the morning."

"Yes'm." The private hesitated. "He said — I'm to say it exactly as he said it — 'at once, and tell the keeper that precision is a courtesy owed both ways.'"

Mirabel looked at him for a moment. Somewhere behind them, inside the tower, Tam dropped something metal, and Wren said *shh* in the fierce whisper of a girl who had learned in one week to run a household of two-and-a-half.

"I'll want ten minutes," Mirabel said. "There are wicks that don't care about colonels."

Private Denny looked profoundly relieved, as though ten minutes were a mercy he could carry back with honor. "Yes'm. I'll wait here. I'm not meant to come in."

"No," said Mirabel. "I imagine there's a great deal you're not meant to do."

They left him standing very straight on the path, and went up the stairs two at a time, because grief had made their body lean and duty had made it strong, and in the lamp room they trimmed what needed trimming, wound what needed winding, and paused — just for a moment — with their hand flat against the warm brass of the lens housing, the way one might touch a shoulder.

"They've come to count us," Mirabel told the glass. "And they've asked me to hold the book."

The glass, being glass, said nothing. But it held the morning light and threw it out to sea in a great slow wheel, and Mirabel found, descending the stairs again, that they had stopped shaking. That was the trouble with towers. They taught you steadiness whether you wanted it or not.

* * *

The customs house had been built in a fatter, more confident decade, when Gannet Point believed itself a port of consequence. It had a pediment, four columns, and a damp cellar where the harbor's paperwork had quietly rotted for forty years. Now red-coated soldiers stood on either side of its doors like gateposts, and the smell of wet wool and gun oil had replaced the older smells of tar and tobacco.

Inside, Sergeant Hollis waited for them at the foot of the stairs. He looked, Mirabel thought, like a man who had not slept well in a bed that deserved better.

"Keeper Vane." He touched his cap. "Thank you for coming."

"You didn't give me far to fall back on."

"No, ma'am. We generally don't." There was no apology in it, but no satisfaction either. It was simply the weather of his profession, reported honestly. "He's in the survey room. I'll warn you now — he'll be civil. That's not the same as kind, but in my experience it's the best thing on offer this far from London."

"That," said Mirabel, "is the most honest sentence any soldier has spoken to me since the boats landed."

Hollis almost smiled. Almost. Then he opened the door.

Colonel Ashworth stood at the head of a long table, and on the table lay the town.

Not the town itself — though Mirabel's stomach made the error anyway, some animal part of them flinching as at a wound — but the ledger of it. The great parish register that had lived, until three days ago, in the vestry of St. Bride's-under-the-Cliff, bound in cracked leather, swollen with a century of entries: baptisms and banns, births and burials, the comings and goings of every soul from Cobb Fenwick's grandfather to Wren and Tam, whose grandmother had entered them herself in her careful schoolmistress's hand, *foundlings, kept, God willing.*

Water had been at it. The regiment had carried it up the coast by boat, and somewhere in the crossing the sea had reached through a tarpaulin or a careless hand and drunk deep. Whole pages had fused into brick. Others had bloomed with mold like gray lichen. The ink of the earlier decades — iron gall, stubborn stuff — had survived. The later entries, written in cheaper ink, were fading even as they lay there, ghosts going ghostlier.

"You are the keeper of the light at Gannet Point," Ashworth said. It was not quite a question and not quite a greeting. He was a tall man built like something drafted rather than grown, all straight lines and right angles, and his voice had the peculiar quality of being neither loud nor soft — merely final.

"Fifteen years," said Mirabel.

"Then you'll know why you're here." He turned the damaged ledger toward them with one finger, delicately, as a surgeon turns a limb. "This is the census of record for the parish. Every household, every occupant, every trade, every age. My orders require an accurate manifest of the civilian population within seventy-two hours. What I have is—" he paused, selecting the word with visible distaste— "*porridge*."

"It got wet," said Mirabel.

"It got wet." He let the flatness sit between them. "Half these names are illegible. A quarter are gone entirely. And yet Sergeant Hollis tells me — and the harbormaster confirms, and the vicar confirms before remembering he's supposed to be difficult about it — that you personally know every household on this coast. Who lives where. Who has married whom. Who has died since the last entry and who has been born. Who keeps goats. Who owes whom."

"The harbormaster talks too much."

"The harbormaster," said Ashworth, "talks precisely enough. Sit down, Keeper."

Mirabel sat. The chair was government oak and offered no opinion.

Ashworth laid a fresh ledger beside the drowned one. New binding, good paper, ruled lines waiting like empty berths. Beside it, a stoppered bottle of ink, three pens, and blotting paper. Everything arranged with the tidy menace of a well-run artillery battery.

"You will reconstruct the register," he said. "Household by household, in your own hand, verified against your own knowledge. Where the old book survives, you will confirm it. Where it doesn't, you will supply it. Names, ages, trades, numbers."

"And if I decline?"

"Then I will send clerks door to door with a printed form, and they will get it wrong, and wrong entries in a requisition become wrong rations, wrong berths, wrong decisions, and I will note in my report that the keeper of the light declined to assist and that the resulting errors are accordingly not the Army's." He said it without heat, the way a man reads coordinates aloud. "I am not threatening you, Keeper. I am describing arithmetic."

"Everything," said Mirabel slowly, "is arithmetic with you."

"Yes," said Ashworth, and did not appear to consider this an insult. "That is why I am trusted with it."

* * *

Mirabel looked down at the drowned book, and the drowned book looked back.

Here was the Tull family page — Marta's mother's entry in Marta's mother's hand, and beneath it Marta's own marriage line, and beneath that the smudge where the water had taken the names of her children and left only their birthdays, like candles blown out. Here was the Fenwick page, fat with Cobbs and Fenwicks going back past anyone's grudge. Here — and Mirabel's breath caught despite themselves — here was the entry in Ede's hand, eleven years old now, *Vane, Ede, mar. Vane, M., Gannet Pt. chapel,* the ink gone brown, the letters still upright and sure, because Ede had written everything the way they'd sailed, straight and unafraid.

Ede had signed this book. Ede's name was dissolving off this page while Mirabel watched.

And here was the thing that settled cold in their chest, colder than the room: whoever wrote the new book would decide what the old book had said. Where the water had eaten a name, the writer supplied one. Where the water had blurred a number, the writer chose one. Every gap in the ruin was a door, and Ashworth was asking Mirabel to walk through each one carrying a pen.

"You understand what you're asking," Mirabel said.

"I'm asking for an accurate population manifest."

"You're asking me to write down my neighbors' names in a book that will decide who eats, who ships out, who gets counted and who gets missed. You're asking me to make the town legible to men with chalk." They kept their voice level. Fifteen years of speaking to storms had taught them how. "I know everyone, Colonel. You said it yourself. Which means everyone will know it was my hand."

Ashworth studied them for a moment — the first time he had properly looked, Mirabel thought, rather than assessed. His eyes were pale gray and patient as tide charts.

"Do you imagine," he said, "that I don't know the same thing? Do you think I summoned you despite that knowledge, rather than because of it?" He closed the ruined ledger gently, with something that was almost respect. "I have clerks who can copy. I have sergeants who can count. What I do not have is a single other soul on this coast who can be *trusted to be right.* Sympathy makes errors, Keeper. So does spite. You have fifteen years of logbooks in that tower, and Hollis tells me they are accurate to the minute and annotated beyond regulation — wind, wrecks, weddings sighted from the gallery, God knows what. You write things down because it matters to you that things be true. That is the only virtue I require."

"And if being true and being merciful point different directions?"

"Then that," said Ashworth, "will be a problem for whichever day it happens." He uncapped the ink and set the bottle before them, precise as a chess move. "Begin with the harbor row. Work outward. You'll take your meals here; the Army provides. Hollis will attend you."

At the door, Mirabel paused.

"The children," they said. "Wren and Tam. Their grandmother wrote them into that book herself. *Foundlings, kept.* If the water took that page, I'll be writing it again. I'd have you know that before I start."

Something crossed Ashworth's face — too quick to name, gone before it arrived. "Foundlings, kept," he repeated, as though tasting the phrasing. "Then write it so. Accuracy, Keeper, cuts both ways. That is the whole point of it."

* * *

Hollis brought tea, which regulations had nothing whatsoever to say about either, and set it at Mirabel's elbow without comment.

For an hour, the only sounds were rain starting against the pediment windows, the scratch of the pen, and the small sighs of a dying book giving up its dead. Mirabel worked the way they trimmed wicks: steadily, completely, mourning afterward.

*Harbor row. Tull, Marta, baker, aged 34; children, three, names restored from memory and Marta's own mouth, which had shouted them across the yard often enough.* *Fenwick, Cobb, fisherman, aged 41; wife, sons two.* On and on, name after name, each one a face arriving unbidden — the ferryman who cheated at cards, the widow who sang to her hens, the boy who had once put a crab in Mirabel's boot and received in return a lecture that was still, by family legend, quoted at Christmas.

By noon Mirabel had filled nine pages, and understood fully, in the marrow, what they had agreed to. This was not a census. It was a target list drawn in a friend's handwriting. Every true thing they wrote made the town easier to count, and everything counted was easier to move, and everything moved was easier to spend.

Hollis watched them pause over a blank stretch where the floodwater had taken an entire household — the Pells, up on the cliff road, three generations under one roof — and saw the keeper's pen hover.

"Ma'am," he said quietly. "For what it's worth — I copied what I could of that page before it stuck together. It's in my pocket, not the file. Regulations don't cover it."

Mirabel looked up at him. Outside, the rain thickened, and beneath the rain, faint but unmistakable, the sea was working itself up to something. The beam would be needed early tonight.

"Sergeant," Mirabel said, taking the scrap he offered, "you keep things in your pocket the way I keep things in my tower."

"Yes, ma'am," said Hollis. "Somebody has to. The colonel only counts."

And Mirabel Vane dipped the pen, and wrote the first Pell back into the world.
