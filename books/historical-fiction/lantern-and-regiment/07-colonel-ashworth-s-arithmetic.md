## 7. Colonel Ashworth's Arithmetic

Colonel Edmund Ashworth had three habits he permitted himself and no others: tea taken scalding at six o'clock precisely, a walk of exactly four hundred paces along whatever quay or parade ground was nearest, and the solving of small sums in his head when conversation bored him. The first two had survived the crossing from the mainland. The third was working overtime.

The customs house at Gannet Point had been built by a man who believed in the dignity of trade and the unimportance of weather, which is to say it leaked. Rain came through the roof in three distinct places — Ashworth had mapped them the way other men mapped enemy positions — and dripped onto a floor that smelled of salt herring and old rope no matter how many times his orderly scrubbed it. He had requisitioned the building on a Tuesday. It was now Friday, and he had already formed the opinion that the entire peninsula was conspiring against him, gently, the way water conspires against stone.

He stood at the window and did the arithmetic again, because the arithmetic was the only thing here that behaved.

One ship. *HMS Meridian*, a coastal steamer of four hundred and ten tons displacement, currently wallowing at anchor in the harbor like a fat man at a funeral she'd rather not attend. Her certified capacity for an evacuation run: two hundred souls if pressed hard, and Ashworth intended to press. Two hundred and forty if they stood shoulder to shoulder and said nothing about it afterward.

Population of Gannet Point and the flooded villages downcoast, per the confiscated town ledger: three hundred and sixty-two.

The difference was one hundred and twenty-two people, and the sea was coming back for all of them.

"Sir." Sergeant Hollis appeared at the door with the particular stiffness of a man carrying news he hoped would be refused. "The lighthouse keeper's come. Vane."

"Send them in."

Ashworth turned from the window and arranged himself behind the customs officer's desk, which was too large for him and too small for the maps he'd spread across it. On those maps lay his whole campaign: the coastline in faded ink, the flood line marked in red as of last week, and the projected storm line in pencil, drawn twice because he hadn't trusted his own hand the first time. Both lines agreed. That was the trouble with mathematics. It never flattered you.

Mirabel Vane came in the way, Hollis had reported, that a cat enters a room full of dogs — without hurry, without apology, and with a clear assessment of every exit. Ashworth had read the keeper's file on the crossing. Widowhood — or widowerhood; the file was maddeningly vague and Vane's manner gave away nothing — one year past. Sole keeper of the Gannet Point light through the worst winter in thirty years, with a logbook the naval survey office had once described, in a marginal note Ashworth had found genuinely delightful, as *"more accurate than our own instruments, and considerably more literate than our own officers."*

Precision. He could work with precision.

"You'll take tea," Ashworth said. It was not quite a question.

"I'll take a chair," said Vane, "if there's one that doesn't drip."

Ashworth indicated the chair beneath the dry corner of the ceiling. He noted, filing it away, that Vane sat down without being asked twice, crossed their arms, and looked at him with the level patience of someone who had spent years waiting out storms and considered him a small one.

"You know why you're here," Ashworth said.

"The sergeant said the ledger."

"The ledger is ruined." He pulled it toward him — the town's great book of households, births, debts, grudges, everything a community wrote down about itself over eighty years — and turned it so Vane could see what the water had done. Pages fused into bricks. Ink bloomed into grey flowers. Half the names along the shore villages gone entirely, dissolved into a brown tide-line across the paper. "Salt water is thorough. I'll grant it that."

Vane touched the edge of the ruined book with one finger, lightly, the way you'd touch a body to confirm what you already feared. Something moved behind their face and was put away again.

"The regiment keeps records too," Ashworth went on. "Ration returns, muster rolls, pay books. Do you know what I've learned in fifteen years of soldiering, Keeper?"

"That paperwork wins wars?"

"That paperwork is the only honest witness a war has. Everything else lies — the survivors lie out of grief, the dead can't correct the record, and the generals lie out of habit. But a well-kept ledger doesn't care whose feelings are hurt. It says what happened." He tapped the ruined book. "This was a good ledger. Now it's a good paperweight. And I am short one hundred and twenty-two names' worth of certainty."

"And you've heard I keep lists."

"I've heard your logbook could navigate by. I've heard you know every household on this coast by name, by boat, by debt, and by grudge. I've heard—" he allowed himself the ghost of a smile, "—that when the baker's boy drowned his father's skiff two winters ago, you logged it, with the cause, within the hour."

Vane didn't smile back. "Marta Tull told you that."

"Marta Tull tells everyone everything. It's why the town functions. You'd be amazed how much intelligence work is just listening to bakers." Ashworth leaned back. Outside, the rain found a new seam in the roof and began a slow percussion on the map table's far corner. He ignored it. "Here is my position, Keeper, stated plainly, because I find plainness saves time even when it costs comfort."

He laid it out the way he laid out everything: end to end, no decoration.

"One ship. Four hundred and ten tons. She can carry two hundred and forty souls in one lift if we load her like a cattle boat and nobody brings furniture. The next great storm — and there will be a next one, the barometer has been falling for three days like a man jumping off a cliff — arrives in roughly eleven days, give or take a day the instruments won't swear to. The *Meridian* cannot hold station in that weather. No ship this size can. After the storm, half this coast will be underwater and the other half will be unreachable, and whatever tonnage I have left will be needed for soldiers, not fishermen."

"So the sum is simple," Vane said.

"The sum is simple. The remainder isn't." He watched their face. "Three hundred and sixty-two souls. Two hundred and forty berths. One hundred and twenty-two remainers. That is the arithmetic, and I did not invent any part of it. I inherited it. If you want to be angry at something, be angry at tonnage. Tonnage doesn't listen, but at least it doesn't pretend to."

For a long moment Vane said nothing. The rain counted for both of them.

"And you want me," they said finally, "to decide which hundred and twenty-two."

"No," said Ashworth, and meant it, and saw that meaning it changed nothing. "I want you to write down who exists. Every soul, every household, every child, every grandmother, every drunkard and saint between the point and the mill creek. Accurately. Completely. In a hand that a tired clerk can read at night by bad light. The *deciding* will be done by criteria — age, skill, health, family unit integrity. Criteria are impersonal. Names are not. I need the names before I need anything else in this world, Keeper, and I'm told you're the only person alive who carries all of them."

"And after I write them?"

"After you write them, the criteria do their work, and the list goes to the ship, and the ship goes to the mainland, and the ones who remain go up the hill to the garrison line where we'll hold as long as holding is possible." He said it evenly. He had learned long ago that softening a sentence only postponed its weight; better to let it land whole and let the listener deal with it at their own speed.

Vane looked at the ruined ledger again. Then at the maps. Then, briefly, at Ashworth himself, with an expression he couldn't entirely classify — something between pity and appraisal, as though he were the item under examination.

"You've done this before," they said. Not a question either. These two, Ashworth reflected, were going to communicate exclusively in statements shaped like questions.

"Bhutan frontier, 'sixty-three. A river valley, a failed harvest, and a column that could carry nine hundred where twelve thousand needed carrying." He heard his own voice go flat and distant and did nothing to stop it. "I was a captain then. My colonel handed me a muster book and said, *Ashworth, count them.* I counted them for eleven days. I can still recite the number. Nine hundred and six. I remember the six because the six were the ones I argued for at three o'clock in the morning while grown men wept at me, and I got four of them aboard, and I have thought about the other two every year since, usually in February, usually at night."

"Why tell me that?"

"Because you should know what you're agreeing to. You think I'm handing you a pen. I'm handing you February. I'm handing you the rest of your nights." He straightened the corner of the map, unnecessarily. "You'll write the names true, Keeper. That's all I ask. Write them true, and hate me for what comes after, and we'll both survive it in our separate ways."

Vane stood. They stood the way they seemed to do everything, without hurry and without permission, and Ashworth found himself standing too, which annoyed him.

"I'll need paper," they said. "Good paper. And a room that doesn't leak."

"You'll have the back office. The roof above it is sound; I checked personally." A pause. "Keeper. One thing more."

They stopped at the door.

"The fishermen have been to see me. Delegations. Petitions. One man — Fenwick, a fishmonger with the temperament of a wet cat — suggested that a man in my position might drown accidentally." Ashworth said it dryly, almost fondly. "And the same afternoon, an old woman left a loaf of bread outside my door with no note, which frightened me considerably more. This town is going to make its case to anyone who'll listen, and some of them will bring bread and some will bring threats, and most will bring both. I'm telling you now: sympathy is a fine quality in a neighbor and a fatal one in a registrar. When you sit down with that pen, I need the woman who logs a drowned skiff within the hour. Not the woman who leaves her lamp burning for a lost boat."

Something flickered across Vane's face at that — quick, private, gone. As though he'd reached into a pocket and found it empty when he was certain he'd left something there.

"I don't leave the lamp burning for lost boats," Mirabel Vane said quietly. "I keep it lit for the living. So they can come home."

And they were gone, boots measured and even on the wet stones outside, leaving Ashworth alone with his maps and his dripping ceiling and the strange, unwelcome sensation of having been corrected by the help.

* * *

That evening he walked his four hundred paces along the harbor quay, hands clasped behind him, hat collar up against the drizzle. The *Meridian* rode at her anchors, deck lights burning yellow through the murk. Along the seafront, the chalked numbers were already appearing on doors — Hollis's men marking households with their berth allotments, neat figures in white that glowed faintly in the wet dark like a species of luminous fungus. *Four.* *Two.* *Five.* He watched a woman come out of a cottage, look at the number on her door for a long time, and go back inside without touching it.

He did the arithmetic again, out of habit. Three hundred and sixty-two. Two hundred and forty. The remainder stood in the rain with him, invisible, patient, everywhere.

At the end of the quay he paused, as he always paused, at the exact four-hundredth pace, and looked up at the Gannet Point light sweeping its white arm across the water — steady, unhurried, indifferent to tonnage, counting nothing.

*Keep it lit for the living,* the keeper had said.

Ashworth turned back toward the customs house, and it occurred to him — not for the first time, but more sharply than usual — that the hardest part of the work ahead wouldn't be the arithmetic at all.

It would be that tomorrow morning, when Mirabel Vane sat down in the back office with fresh paper and a sound roof, the first name they would write was their own — *essential personnel, one keeper* — and the second and third would be two children whom his sergeants had reported, in a footnote of a routine patrol log, as *resident at the lighthouse, status unclear.*

Status unclear. Ashworth disliked footnotes. By morning, he intended to make that one very clear indeed.
