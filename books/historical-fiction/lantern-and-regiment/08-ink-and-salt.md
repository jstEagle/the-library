## 8. Ink and Salt

The customs house smelled of cold tea and wet paper, which Mirabel thought was a fair description of the whole enterprise of government: something meant to be nourishing that had been left out in the rain.

They had been given a desk in the corner of the main room — an actual desk, with a drawer that stuck and an inkwell chipped at the lip, positioned beneath the one window whose glass had not yet surrendered to the storms. Outside, the harbor lay gray and restless under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Inside, Sergeant Hollis stood at parade rest three paces away, watching them the way a man watches a dog he has been told might bite.

"You'll want to begin with the harbor households," Hollis said. "Colonel's orders. Waterfront first, then the hill."

"I know how a ledger is built," Mirabel said. "I've kept one for eleven years."

"Of course." A pause. "The colonel said you'd say something like that."

"The colonel," Mirabel said, dipping the pen, "seems to have anticipated me at every turn. It's almost flattering."

Hollis did not smile. That was the trouble with soldiers, Mirabel had decided; they were trained out of smiling so thoroughly that when they finally attempted it, the result frightened livestock. He was young under the weathering, though — thirty perhaps, with a face that hadn't yet decided what it believed about the work it was doing.

Mirabel opened the fresh ledger to its first clean page. The regiment's copy of the town record sat beside it, swollen fat with seawater, its ink bloomed into gray ghosts. Half of Gannet Point lived now only as smudges. The colonel's arithmetic needed numbers, and numbers needed names, and there was exactly one person in this room who carried all of them in their head, filed away over years of lamp oil deliveries and bread debts and funerals and christenings and one memorable feud involving a crab pot.

So Mirabel wrote.

*Tull, Marta.* Baker. Widow of Aldous Tull, lost twelve years back off the Serrin shoals — no, wait, that wasn't right; Aldous had come home from the Serrin shoals and died two winters later of his lungs, which the sea took credit for anyway. Marta baked on Thursdays and Sundays and gave away her ends-of-loaf to anyone who looked hungry, which was everyone, which was why she was poor, which was why she'd once tried to pay Mirabel in rolls for trimming her wicks and Mirabel had pretended the rolls were fair payment because refusing kindness is its own cruelty.

The pen paused above the page.

There was no column for any of this. The colonel's ledger wanted: *Name. Trade. Age. Dependents. Fitness for labor.* Four lines per soul, as though a person were a barrel to be inventoried.

Mirabel wrote *Tull, Marta, 51, baker, dependents: none,* and then, in the margin, in smaller script because it was nobody's business and everybody's business both: *Gave away her last flour in the flood week. Feeds the town whether the town can pay or no.*

* * *

By midmorning the page held nine households, and Mirabel had learned something they did not enjoy learning.

Writing a name was easy. The hand moved; the ink dried; done. What was not easy was the moment after, when the name sat there looking up at you and you understood that somewhere upstairs a man with a compass and a ruler was going to draw lines around these words, and the lines would become berths, and the berths would become boats, and the boats would leave, and some of the names would be on them and some would not.

*Cobb Fenwick, 44, fisherman, dependents: wife, two sons.*

Mirabel sat back and regarded Cobb's name with the expression one reserves for a stain that won't come out.

Here was the thing about Cobb Fenwick: he was a hard man, and he had been made hard honestly, by weather and loss and the particular bitterness of a man who worked harder than his luck deserved. He had called Ede a fool for running the point in that last storm — loudly, in front of half the harbor — and when the sea brought back nothing but a boot and the ship's bell, Cobb had been the one who dove until his lips went blue, trying to find more. He'd wept at the funeral. He'd also never once apologized, and he'd spent a year afterward glaring at the lighthouse as if it had personally swallowed his grievance.

Mirabel wrote, in the margin: *Dove for three days looking for my Ede. Would deny it if asked. Grudge between us older than either of us remembers starting it.*

Then, after a moment, added: *But he dives for people. Remember that about him.*

Across the room, Hollis shifted his weight. "You're writing in the margins."

"I am."

"The colonel specified a clean transcription."

"The colonel," Mirabel said, "specified that I know everyone. This is me knowing everyone. If he wants a list without people in it, he's welcome to hire a stranger, and I wish him joy of the stranger's guesses."

Hollis came around the desk — slowly, the way men approach things they're not certain they want to look at — and read the margin notes. His jaw did something complicated. "Fenwick dove for your spouse?"

"Three days. In January water."

"And you still—" He stopped himself, which Mirabel appreciated. Men who stopped themselves mid-question were at least capable of learning where questions shouldn't go.

"He's a neighbor," Mirabel said. "Neighbors are allowed to be difficult. That's most of what being neighbors means."

* * *

At noon, a soldier brought bread and cold mutton, and Mirabel discovered they could not eat it.

Not from principle — grief had burned through their principles long ago, leaving only practicalities. No: every bite seemed to require naming, inwardly, someone who had less. The Tulls' oven was cold because the flood had taken their coal. The Pell children were eating nettle soup. Old Sorrel Brack, who was ninety-one and blind and could still mend a net better than any man on the coast, had eaten nothing yesterday but what his granddaughter smuggled from the army stores.

Mirabel pushed the plate toward Hollis instead. "You eat. You lot marched here; I only sat down."

"That's against—"

"Sergeant. I am a civilian performing conscripted labor under protest, in a requisitioned building, in a drowned county. I promise you the regulations have already given up on us both."

Something happened to Hollis's mouth then. It was brief, crooked, gone in a second — but it was unmistakably the ghost of a smile, and Mirabel counted it a small victory, filed alongside the other small victories: the fire lit in the customs house grate despite the colonel's fuel economy, the window cracked open against the smell of mildew, the extra candle burning past regulation hours because reading by bad light gives a person a headache and headaches make for sloppy ledgers, and sloppy ledgers, Mirabel had told the colonel to his face, get people killed.

The colonel had looked at them for a long moment and said, "Continue, Keeper Vane," in the tone of a man allowing a fox to count the hens because the fox was, annoyingly, the best counter available.

* * *

It went faster in the afternoon, which was worse.

Speed arrived when Mirabel stopped fighting the margins and let the memories pour. Each name uncorked others. *Brine Ollie, trade: none admitted, occupation: whatever he says it isn't.* The whole room smelled faintly of the sea anyway, salt in the plaster, salt in the air from the open window, and it got into the ink somehow, or seemed to — each letter drying with a faint crystalline shimmer at the edges, as if the ocean were signing its objections.

By four o'clock Mirabel's hand ached and the ledger held sixty-one households, and they had laughed twice and wept once and hoped the sergeant hadn't noticed the weeping, and knew perfectly well he had, and found they didn't entirely mind.

"The Pell family," Hollis said quietly, reading upside down, which he apparently could do, which explained several things about good sergeants. "You've written 'the boy Tam used to be friends with.'"

"Jem Pell. Yes."

"But Tam—"

"Tam Vane is the boy currently living in my lighthouse," Mirabel said, "along with his sister Wren, since the flood took their grandmother's cottage and everything in it. Jem Pell is his friend. They dug bait together. Jem taught Tam to whistle through his hands. If you separate those boys onto different ships, Sergeant, I will consider it a personal injury, and I keep a very long memory, as you may have noticed."

Hollis stood very still. Then he said, in a voice carefully stripped of anything at all: "I don't assign the berths, Keeper."

"No," Mirabel agreed. "You just carry the paper."

He flinched. Actually flinched, a small involuntary movement, like a horse troubled by a fly it couldn't reach. And Mirabel, who had spent a year becoming an expert in the many shapes guilt takes — had worn most of them personally — recognized the fit of the coat immediately.

"You didn't know," Mirabel said, gentler. "When you took this posting. You thought it was coastal survey, or grain requisition, or something with forms in triplicate and honest boredom."

"I thought," Hollis said, "that I was keeping order during an evacuation. Order. People lined up, people boarded, people safe." He looked at the ledger, at the margins full of bread debts and dead wives and boys who whistled through their hands. "Nobody said anything about deciding."

"I'm not deciding," Mirabel said. "I'm writing."

"With respect, Keeper Vane — look at what you write."

And that was the whole of it, laid out plain between them on the desk, next to the cold tea and the salt-stiff pages: that a list is never only a list. That every line is a lever, and someone else's hand always works the fulcrum, but the ink is yours, and the ink does not wash out, and somewhere down the coast or up in heaven or nowhere at all, Ede was presumably watching their spouse transcribe neighbors into a machine and wondering where the person they married had gone.

Mirabel capped the inkwell. "We resume tomorrow," they said. "Same time. Bring better tea; yours tastes like punishment."

"It is army tea," Hollis said. "That's what it's for."

For one absurd moment, standing in the ruined customs house of a drowning town, the two of them nearly laughed together. Nearly. It passed, as such things do, leaving only the echo, which was perhaps worth having anyway.

* * *

Mirabel walked home along the harbor road as the light failed, and the chalk marks were everywhere now.

White numbers on doorposts, on shutters, on the lintel of the chapel. *3. 2. 4. 1.* The regiment's tally of souls per household, chalked up like scores in a game nobody had agreed to play. An old woman stood before her own door rubbing at the number with her thumb, though chalk doesn't yield to thumbs, and she kept rubbing anyway, small circular motions, like polishing a lens that wouldn't brighten.

At the lighthouse, Wren had supper on the table — burnt barley stew, but hot, and set out with a ceremony that suggested she'd arranged the bowls three times. Tam sat in the corner folding paper, his fingers quick and silent, little white hulls accumulating around him like snowdrifts.

"Well?" Wren demanded, the moment the door shut. "Did you write us?"

Mirabel hung up their coat. "I wrote everyone. Alphabetical by street, as the old ledger ran. You're between the Nances and the Pells."

"What did you put?"

"Dependents: two. Trades: none listed, ages eleven and eight." Mirabel sat down heavily. "Wren—"

"You put something in the margin." She wasn't asking. She had her grandmother's eyes, and her grandmother's habit of seeing straight through the back of a person's skull. "Everyone knows you can't help yourself. Marta says you once logged a wedding in the weather report."

"It was relevant," Mirabel said with dignity. "The groom was seasick. That's weather-adjacent."

Tam looked up. He didn't speak — hadn't spoken since the water took Granny Maren — but he pushed a paper boat across the table toward Mirabel, the way he did most evenings now, one small folded hull offered without explanation and taken without question. Mirabel picked it up. Inside, faintly, in pencil: a name. *Pell, Jem.*

Not Wren. Not Tam. Not *Vane.* Jem Pell, the friend, the boy across the harbor.

Mirabel looked at the child, and the child looked back with an expression far too old for eight, and Mirabel understood that Tam had grasped the arithmetic of the thing days before any adult in the house had dared say it aloud. The boat was not a wish. It was a question, folded small enough to survive the answer.

Outside, the wind was rising out of the southeast, and far below on the gallery rail, unseen in the dark, a girl was already planning her own errand among the chalked doors — because Wren had seen something from the lamp room that day that she had not yet told anyone, and it was burning a hole in her the size of Gannet Point itself.
