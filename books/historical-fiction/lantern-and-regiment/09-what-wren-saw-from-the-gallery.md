## 9. What Wren Saw from the Gallery

Wren had decided that the lamp room was the best room in the world, and she intended to keep this opinion even if it got her in trouble.

It wasn't just the view, though the view was a good part of it. From the gallery — one hundred and six steps up, she'd counted them twice and gotten one hundred and five both times, which she suspected meant she'd miscounted twice rather than the stairs being dishonest — you could see the whole of Gannet Point laid out like a map someone had made of a place they loved. The crooked streets. The harbor with its boats bobbing like toys left in a bath. The smoke rising from chimneys in thin gray ribbons, each one marking a house where somebody was burning something for breakfast.

Mirabel said the gallery was no place for children in weather like this. Wren had pointed out that the weather was exactly why the gallery existed, because otherwise how would anyone know what was coming? Mirabel had looked at her for a long moment with an expression Wren couldn't read — something between exasperation and surrender — and then said, "Hold the rail. Both hands."

That had been three days ago, and now holding the rail was Wren's official duty, or close enough to one that she performed it with great seriousness while Tam sat on the landing below, out of the wind, folding his paper boats. He still didn't talk. But he folded. He could fold a boat small enough to sit inside another boat, and he did, and when Wren asked him why, he just looked at her until she stopped asking.

Below her, the town went about its morning. And below her, too, came the soldiers.

They'd been in the harbor four days now, red coats gone brownish at the hems from the salt spray, and Wren had learned their rhythms the way she'd learned the tides. They rose early. They marched down Fore Street in pairs. They carried things: crates, coils of rope, long poles with hooks on the end, and — this was the thing that made her stomach do something complicated — buckets of chalk.

Not chalk like school chalk. Chalk like mason's chalk, great fat sticks of it, white as bone, and they used it on doors.

Wren watched Private — she thought his name was Denny, he was the young one who smiled at Tam once and then looked guilty about it — watch him stop at the Pellow house first. Old Mrs. Pellow answered the door before he knocked, arms crossed, chin up, the way she stood when she was about to win an argument. Denny said something. Mrs. Pellow said something back, longer. Then Denny crouched and drew on her door: a number. *Four.* Big white strokes, taller than Tam.

Four what? Four people allowed to stay? Four sacks of flour requisitioned? Wren turned it over in her head while Denny moved on down the street, and the numbers accumulated like gulls on a mooring post. Three at the Tulls'. Five at the Harrows', though there were seven Harrows, unless old Nan Harrow had been counted as half, which would be its own kind of insult. Two at the net loft. One at the chapel — one! — which meant either the army had opinions about God or the chapel's number meant something else entirely.

She kept a running count in her head, the way Mirabel kept the log. By midmorning she had forty-one numbers, and forty-one houses, and the arithmetic didn't sit right in her chest, like a coat buttoned wrong.

Then Denny reached the Quill house, and Wren forgot all about arithmetic.

The Quills lived at the bottom of Fisher's Row, in the blue-shuttered cottage with the anchor over the door that Mr. Quill had carved himself and painted three times because salt ate paint faster than pride. Their daughter Ivy was Wren's friend — her best friend, if Wren were honest, which she tried not to be, because best friends got taken away by floods and grandmothers' cottages got eaten by the sea, and it hurt less to keep friendships slightly unlabeled, like cargo without papers.

Ivy's family was nine people. Grandfather Quill, who was mostly deaf and mostly wonderful. Mr. and Mrs. Quill. Ivy. Her brothers Jem and Peter and little Alfie, who was four and believed everything Wren told him, which was a responsibility she took seriously. And the twins, born last spring, who mostly slept and screamed in shifts.

Denny stood in front of the blue door a long time. Longer than he'd stood anywhere else. Wren leaned so far over the rail that her ribs pressed the cold iron, and she watched him raise the chalk, and lower it, and raise it again, and then Mr. Quill came out and put himself between the soldier and the door, not angry, just *there*, the way a rock is there in a current.

They talked. Wren couldn't hear a word of it — the wind up here took voices and shredded them — but she could see the shape of it. Mr. Quill's shoulders. Denny's cap coming off, going back on, coming off again. And then Denny did something Wren had not seen him do at any other door.

He walked away without writing anything.

No number. No mark. Just the ghost of chalk dust on his fingers and a door left blank, and Wren stood there with the wind pulling her hair and understood, in the way children understand things — completely and all at once and without any of the words — that a blank door was worse than a numbered one.

A number was a ration. A blank was a question nobody wanted to answer.

* * *

"Mirabel," she said that evening, carefully casual, the way you approach a cat you want to pick up, "what do the chalk numbers mean?"

Mirabel was trimming the wicks, sleeves rolled, face lit gold from below in a way that made them look like a painting of themselves. They didn't stop working. "Who told you about the numbers?"

"Nobody. I saw them. I see everything from up here." She climbed onto the workbench stool, which was against the rules, and Mirabel let it go, which meant Mirabel was tired. "Mrs. Pellow got a four. The Harrows got a five but there are seven of them. And the Quills—" Her voice caught on the name and she pushed through it. "The Quills didn't get anything at all."

The scissors paused above the wick. Just for a breath. Then they finished the cut, precise as ever.

"It means berths," Mirabel said. "Places on the evacuation ships. How many of a household the army intends to carry."

"And a blank?"

Mirabel set the scissors down and finally looked at her, and whatever was in their face made Wren wish she hadn't asked, and made her glad she had, both at once.

"A blank means they haven't decided yet," Mirabel said. "Or it means they've decided and don't want to write it where people can see."

"The Quills are nine people."

"I know."

"Ivy taught me to gut a mackerel. She can mend nets better than men twice her size. Alfie is *four*, Mirabel. You can't put a four-year-old in the arithmetic column and leave him blank."

"I know," Mirabel said again, quieter, and turned back to the lens, polishing a spot that was already polished, which was what Mirabel did instead of shouting or crying or throwing things, and Wren was beginning to build a whole dictionary of what Mirabel did instead of those things.

"They're going to leave them," Wren said. "Aren't they. The ones with blanks. When the ships go, the blanks stay."

The cloth stopped moving.

"Go down and check on your brother," Mirabel said gently. "It's past his supper."

Which was not an answer, and was therefore an answer, and Wren went down the hundred and six steps with each one landing harder than the last, and found Tam on the landing surrounded by his paper fleet, and sat down beside him and didn't say anything, because he'd taught her that silence could hold more than words if you packed it right.

Tam looked up at her. Then he picked up one of his boats — a new one, folded tight as a fist — and held it out.

Inside, when she unfolded it careful as a moth's wing, was a name written in his careful, terrible handwriting: *IVY QUILL.*

Wren's throat closed. She refolded the boat and put it in her pocket, where it stayed for the rest of the night, and most of the next day, and might have stayed forever except that Wren Vane was not built for keeping things in pockets when they ought to be kept somewhere useful.

* * *

Here is what nobody in authority seemed to understand about Wren: she was small, she was quiet when she chose to be, and she had spent two years living above a bakery in Saltmarsh learning exactly how much a person could observe while appearing to observe nothing. Adults talked over children the way water talks over stones. It was practically an invitation.

So when she announced at breakfast — bread and dripping, Mirabel standing at the window with a cup of tea going cold — that she was going to walk down to the harbor to look at the boats, and Mirabel said, "Stay off Fisher's Row," and she said, "Why?" and Mirabel said, "Because I asked you to," and she said, "All right," and meant none of it — nobody thought anything of it.

The customs house sat at the head of the quay, a squat stone building that had spent its whole life disapproving of the sea. Since the regiment came, it had grown a flag, two sentries, and a smell of pipe tobacco that drifted out its windows like the building itself had taken up smoking to cope.

Wren did not walk up to the customs house. That was the first rule of looking at something: don't look at it. She walked past it, twice, at a stroll, eating a heel of bread she'd saved, and noted the following. Sentries changed at some point between the church bell striking eight and the bell after it — she'd need to pin it down. The side door, the one facing the fish sheds, had a latch instead of a lock, because the army had requisitioned a customs house and not bothered to requisition a proper locksmith. And the cellar hatch, low on the seaward wall, was propped open with a brick to air the place out, which meant the soldiers were using the cellar for something damp, probably records, probably *the* records, because everyone in town knew the old ledger had been carried off there and everyone also knew the sea got into everything at Gannet Point eventually, given time and inclination.

On her third pass, a soldier said, "Lost, little miss?" and she gave him the look Ivy's grandmother used on gulls — flat, ancient, unimpressed — and said, "Waiting for my aunt," and he nodded and went back to his pipe, because adults would believe absolutely anything a child said in a bored voice.

Her aunt did not exist. Her aunt had never existed. Wren felt only mildly bad about this.

* * *

She went back the next night.

This required lying to Mirabel, which she hated, and doing it well, which she was ashamed to find she could. She said she'd wake early and wanted to sleep in the big chair by the stove, and Mirabel — who trusted her, who had taken them in off the rocks and fed them and taught Tam to coil line and never once asked for anything back — Mirabel banked the fire and hung her own coat over Wren like a blanket, and Wren lay in the warm dark feeling the lie sit in her stomach like a swallowed stone, and waited until the breathing upstairs went slow and deep, and slipped out into the cold.

The town at night was a different animal. Streets she knew by daylight turned strange, puddles holding pieces of sky, shutters creaking their private opinions. She kept to the shadow side of the lane, heart going like a gull in a gale, and told herself the whole way down that this was foolish, dangerous, exactly the sort of thing that got children washed off breakwaters — and kept walking, because Ivy was nine people and a blank, and because Tam had written a name on a paper boat, and because somebody had to know what the numbers meant before the numbers became true.

The side latch lifted without a sound. Of course it did. Nothing about the army was built for this coast yet; they'd brought their locks from inland, and their boots, and their certainty, and none of it fit quite right.

Inside, the customs house smelled of wax, tobacco, and cold stone. Moonlight came through the front windows in pale slabs, and Wren moved along the wall between them, one slab at a time, listening. Above her, floorboards ticked. Somebody slept up there — she could hear the particular rhythm of it, slow and even, a man-sized rhythm. The sergeant, probably. Hollis. The tall one who stood at the back of every gathering with his hands behind him like a man waiting to be told what his hands were for.

The records were where she'd guessed: the cellar, down stairs that complained under her feet no matter how she placed them, each step a small betrayal. She froze at the bottom, counted ten, twenty, thirty. The floorboards above stayed silent.

Moonlight found the cellar through the propped hatch, and by it she saw the table. On the table, the book.

It was bigger than she'd imagined — a ledger fat as a Bible, swollen with damp, its corners blooming gray. Beside it lay fresh paper, pens, a pot of ink with a skin on it like a dead thing's eye. And stacked neat as sliced bread, pages and pages of new writing: columns of names, columns of numbers, a town translated into arithmetic.

She found the Quills on the fourth page. Nine names in Mirabel's hand — she knew it instantly, that upright, patient script, the same hand that wrote the lighthouse log, the same hand that had written *wind NW, moderate, gulls restless* every day of Wren's new life. Mirabel had written every soul in Gannet Point into this book. And beside each name, in a different hand — a harder hand, square and certain — the numbers.

Beside *Quill, E., fisherman*: nothing. A space, ruled and empty, like a grave dug in advance.

Beside *Quill, Ivy*: nothing.

Beside *Quill, A. (child, 4)*: nothing.

Wren stood in the cold cellar with her fists clenched and her eyes stinging and understood that the blankness wasn't neglect. It was a decision wearing a disguise. Somewhere in this building, in some other book or some other head, the answer already existed, and the empty spaces on these pages were just the polite version of it.

She heard the floorboard overhead shift.

Not much. A weight changing sides in sleep. But enough. Enough to turn her blood to seawater. She memorized the page number — fourteen, top corner, faint — and the shape of the columns, and she backed up the complaining stairs with her heart in her ears, and let herself out the side door into the night, and ran.

She ran all the way to the lighthouse path before she remembered to breathe, and she stopped there, bent double in the dark, gasping, and felt in her pocket for the paper boat with Ivy's name in it, and held it, and made herself a promise with no witnesses but the moon:

They were not going to be a blank. Not Ivy, not Alfie, not any of them. Wren didn't know how yet. But she knew the book now. She knew where the town lived, in ink, in a cellar, in a stranger's arithmetic — and knowing a thing's hiding place was halfway to stealing it.

Up the hill, the Gannet Point light swept its slow circle over the water, over the town, over her, steady as a heartbeat, and Wren Vane wiped her face with her sleeve and started climbing.

Tomorrow, she'd find out what the numbers added up to. Tomorrow, the colonel was announcing the list.

She intended to be listening.
