## 10. The Berth List

Wren got her listening spot by claiming it before dawn.

The customs house had a broad stone stoop, and the stoop had a corner behind a rain barrel where a girl of eleven could tuck herself flat as a shadow and watch the whole square fill up. She'd been there since the sky was still the color of dishwater, huddled in Ede's old fishing blanket — no, not Ede's, she corrected herself; Mirabel's spouse had been dead a year before Wren ever climbed the lighthouse path, but somehow everything in that house felt borrowed from someone who mattered — with Tam's hand curled sleepily in hers.

Tam was awake now. He'd woken without a sound, the way he did everything, and stood beside her peering through the gap between barrel and wall.

"Too many," he said.

It was two words. From Tam that was a speech. And he wasn't wrong. The square was packed shoulder to shoulder: fishermen still smelling of tar and salt, baker's boys, the ferry wives with their shawls pulled tight against the wind off the harbor, Marta Tull standing straight-backed near the chapel steps with flour dust still ghosting her sleeves because she'd come straight from the ovens rather than miss a word. Even Cobb Fenwick had turned out, arms folded like a fence around himself, wearing his best coat and worst expression.

Above them, on the customs house steps, stood Colonel Ashworth in his red coat, immaculate, with Sergeant Hollis a pace behind holding a ledger under one arm like a man carrying something that might bite.

"Everyone in Gannet Point," Wren whispered, "and half of Salt Row besides."

Ashworth did not shout. That was the first thing everyone noticed, and the thing they talked about for years afterward — that he never once raised his voice, and every word landed anyway, the way a stone dropped down a well lands at the bottom no matter how long it takes.

"People of Gannet Point," he said. "You have been asking what the numbers add up to."

Beside the rain barrel, Wren went very still.

"I will tell you plainly, because you have earned plainness." He lifted one gloved hand, and Hollis stepped forward and opened the ledger across a small table that had been set out for the purpose, weighted at its corners with four stones against the wind. "This coast will flood again. Not may — will. The surveyors give us days, not weeks. The Admiralty has sent one ship. The *Providence* carries six hundred souls at safe tonnage."

He paused. Somewhere in the crowd a baby fussed and was hushed.

"Gannet Point holds nine hundred and forty-one souls."

Nobody made a sound. Nine hundred and forty-one. Somebody had counted them. Somebody had sat in a cellar with a water-damaged book and counted the town the way you count coins you know are too few. Wren thought of Mirabel bent over that table in the customs house night after night, pen scratching, and felt sick.

"The *Providence* makes three runs if the weather permits," Ashworth went on. "Three runs carry eighteen hundred. That leaves one hundred and forty-one unaccounted for, and I do not intend to leave them to arithmetic. A second vessel is being sought. Until one is found—" and here his eyes moved over the crowd, unhurried, missing nothing, and Wren pressed herself flatter behind the barrel though there was no reason on earth he could see her— "we must decide who boards first."

A voice from the crowd — Cobb Fenwick's, naturally, because Cobb had never met a silence he didn't want to fill. "Decide how?"

"With a priority list." Ashworth nodded to Hollis, who unrolled a paper and pinned it to a board with a hammer blow that cracked across the square like a shot. "Berths will be allotted by category. Skilled trades essential to resettlement: smiths, carpenters, masons, midwives, surgeons. The young — those under twelve, whose lives are longest ahead of them. The useful — those with trades the new settlements lack. Families will be kept together wherever the numbers allow."

*Wherever the numbers allow.* Five little words, and Wren heard the whole town hear them at once, a sort of collective indrawn breath, like the sea pulling back off the rocks.

"The list will be posted in three days," Ashworth said. "Objections may be submitted in writing to this office. Rioting, hoarding, or interference with the evacuation will be treated as sabotage against His Majesty's forces. That is all."

And that was it. No trumpet, no drum. He simply gathered his coat about him and went inside, and left nine hundred people standing in a square with the number six hundred hanging over them like weather.

* * *

For a moment nobody moved. Then the square broke apart the way ice breaks — not all at once, but along lines nobody had known were there.

"Under twelve," Wren heard a woman say, gripping her son so hard the boy squeaked. "Did you hear? Under twelve boards first."

"First run or last run?" someone demanded. "There's a difference between first and last when there's a storm coming, and don't tell me there isn't."

"Skilled trades. *Skilled trades.*" Old Cobb Fenwick had found his voice fully now, and it carried. "My grandfather built half the boats in this harbor. My family's been here two hundred years. Two hundred years, and some clerk's going to tell me whether I'm *useful*?"

"You're a fisherman, Cobb," said a woman near him — Nettie Quayle, who sold cockles and had opinions the way other people had hair. "Half the town's fishermen. What happens when six hundred berths meet four hundred fishermen?"

"What happens," Cobb said, "is we look at who's been here longest, that's what—"

"Or who needs it most," Nettie snapped.

"Or who can pay," said somebody else, very quietly, and the whole square went quiet again, because everyone had thought it and nobody wanted to be the one who'd said it out loud.

Wren watched it happen. She watched neighbors look at each other sideways — really look, measuring — for the first time in her life. Mrs. Penhale the seamstress suddenly looked at the cooper's wife like a woman counting buttons. Two brothers from Salt Row stood with their arms crossed facing opposite directions. And in the middle of it all, Marta Tull climbed up onto the chapel steps, raised both flour-white hands, and shouted, "Stop! Stop this, all of you — we've buried half this town already this winter, are we going to bury the rest of each other before the sea even gets its chance?"

For a heartbeat it almost worked. Heads turned. Shoulders eased.

Then Cobb Fenwick said, loud enough for everyone, "Easy for you, Marta. You'll be on any list ever drawn. Who bakes bread for a garrison?"

And Marta Tull, who had fed half the town for nothing since the floods began, went white, and the square started talking again, ten conversations at once, none of them kind, and Wren felt Tam's small hand tighten around hers until it hurt.

"We're under twelve," Tam said. It came out flat, the way he said everything now, but his grip said the rest. "We board first. We go first and you—"

"Shh." But he'd said it, and now she had to say it too, or leave it sitting between them like a stone. "I'm eleven. You're seven. They'd take us and leave—" She stopped.

Leave Mirabel.

The keeper of the light was neither under twelve nor, as far as Wren knew, skilled in any trade the new settlements lacked, unless you counted keeping a lamp lit, which apparently the Army considered *essential* — one keeper might stay, the chalked notice on the lighthouse door had said, ONE KEEPER, ESSENTIAL POST — but that was staying, not going, and staying was its own kind of drowning when the water came up the hill.

They didn't talk about it. There was nothing to say that wouldn't break something. They just held hands harder and slipped out from behind the rain barrel and went home up the cliff path, past the offerings — a loaf wrapped in cloth, a jar of honey, a coil of good line — that the fisherfolk still left at the lighthouse door as if the light were a saint and Mirabel its reluctant priest.

Mirabel was waiting for them with soup.

* * *

"They posted the categories on the church door," Wren reported, spooning soup with the grim efficiency of a child who has learned that eating is a job. "Smiths, shipwrights, masons, midwives, surgeons, schoolmasters—"

"Schoolmasters," Mirabel repeated faintly. "Gannet Point hasn't had a schoolmaster since old Mr. Prigg fell off the pier in 'sixty-eight."

"He counts anyway. He's on the mainland already, which is cheating." Wren set down her spoon. "Mirabel. What are we?"

The question hung in the lamplit kitchen of the lighthouse, among the copper pots and the smell of oil and the great lens overhead turning its patient circle. Tam looked up from his paper boats. He'd been folding them all week, one after another, and lining them along the windowsill like a fleet waiting for orders.

Mirabel set down the ladle very slowly.

"You," Mirabel said carefully, "are children. Under twelve. First category, first boat, first run."

"And you?"

"I am the keeper of the light. Essential post. One keeper remains."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Mirabel agreed, after a moment. "It isn't."

Tam pushed a paper boat across the table. On its folded hull, in careful pencil, someone had written a name. Wren leaned over to read it and her stomach dropped: *Marta Tull.*

"There's more," she said. "At the square. After the colonel went in, people started — I don't know. Measuring each other. Cobb said the ones who'd been here longest should go first. Someone else said whoever pays. Marta tried to stop it and Cobb threw it in her face, about the bread." Wren looked up. "They were friends. Were. This morning."

Mirabel was quiet a long time. Outside, the beam swung round and round, touching the water, touching the town, touching the water again, steady as anything, as if the whole coast weren't coming apart at its seams.

"When I was recording ships in the log," Mirabel said finally, "there was a rule. Weather, position, heading, tonnage. Facts only. But every keeper I ever knew wrote a bit extra in the margins. *Sloop *Kitty*, master drunk, crew singing.* Things like that. Because the facts keep the record, but the margins keep the truth." Mirabel looked at the paper fleet on the windowsill. "That colonel has a ledger full of facts. What he doesn't have is margins. And a town is mostly margins, Wren. A town is who leaves bread at whose door. The day we start reading each other like a column of figures is the day Gannet Point stops existing, even if every soul in it survives."

"Then stop him," Wren said.

"It isn't that simple."

"Why not? You know everybody. You wrote the book. You could—"

"*Enough.*"

It wasn't shouted. It was worse than shouted — quiet and cracked down the middle, and Wren actually flinched, because in a year of living next door to grief and then a month of living underneath it, she had never once heard Mirabel Vane raise a voice.

Mirabel pressed both hands flat on the table, and for a moment looked older than the lighthouse.

"I'm sorry," Mirabel said. "I'm sorry. You're right, and I'm frightened, and those two things came out of me in the wrong order." A breath. "Go up to bed. Both of you. Tomorrow I'll think better."

Tam gathered his boats without being told twice. At the foot of the stairs he paused, looked back, and said, "Stay."

One word. His whole argument, his whole fear, folded into it like a name into paper. Then he climbed into the dark, and Wren followed, and neither of them saw Mirabel sit alone at the table for a long while afterward, head in hands, while above them the lamp turned and turned and asked nothing of anyone.

* * *

Down in the town, the square did not empty when the soldiers went indoors. It thinned, and curdled, and rearranged itself into knots of two and three, and the knots spoke low.

By the bakery, Marta Tull scrubbed flour from her hands that wasn't there and listened to her neighbor Bess Cordery explain, with terrible gentleness, that Bess's daughter was a midwife, and midwives were second category, and surely Marta understood that a midwife saved lives, whereas bread—

"Bread keeps people alive too," Marta said.

"Yes," Bess agreed. "But it's not on the list, dear, is it."

In the Anchor's End, Cobb Fenwick bought drinks for men he'd feuded with for twenty years, and laid out his case like fish on a slab: roots, blood, generations, the Fenwicks before the church was built, and if usefulness was the measure then measure him, he'd crewed boats since he was six. Around him men nodded, and behind their nodding they were doing sums — because Cobb had a wife and three daughters, four berths, and every berth he took was a berth gone, and love and arithmetic had never shared a room comfortably.

And outside, at the edge of the lamplight, Sergeant Hollis stood with his hands clasped behind his back and his face arranged into official blankness, and listened.

He heard a woman ask another woman, casually, horribly, whether her husband's cough had gotten worse — because sick men, surely, were less *useful*. He heard two brothers agree to claim different households, so as not to be counted twice against each other. He heard a boy of maybe fifteen solemnly swear to lie about his age downward, and his father correct him — upward, boy, upward, fifteen reads as strong labor — and the boy's face as the lie settled on him like a coat too big.

Hollis had served in India. He had seen plague camps and famine roads. He had thought he knew what a list could do to people.

But he had never seen it done to a town this size, this slowly, in front of him, with tea still being offered across fences in the mornings.

He walked back toward the customs house past the harbor, where the *Providence* rode at anchor, black against the last grey light, big enough to hold hope and not nearly big enough to hold a town. He stopped at the rail and stood there a while.

Inside the customs house, a single candle burned in a high window. Behind it, he knew, the colonel worked late over the lists, refining his categories, sharpening his arithmetic, doing what he believed was duty.

Hollis thought about the woman with the cough. He thought about the boy learning to lie about his own strength. He thought, unexpectedly, about the lighthouse on the hill, and the keeper up there who knew every name in this town not as figures but as faces — who had written, in the margin of a requisition form, no bigger than a pencil whisper, *Mrs. Quayle, sells cockles, feeds stray cats, do not let her pride refuse help.*

Three days until the list was posted.

Hollis turned up his collar against the wind coming off the water, and went inside, and did not write down everything he had heard.

Some things, he was beginning to think, belonged in the margins.

* * *

At the lighthouse, long after the children slept, Mirabel climbed to the gallery and stood in the cold looking down at the town. Lights still burned in windows that should have been dark hours ago. Nobody was sleeping in Gannet Point tonight; the whole town lay awake counting — counting souls, counting berths, counting neighbors, and finding the sums short.

Six hundred seats. Nine hundred and forty-one souls.

Mirabel took out the little notebook — the private one, the one with the margins — and read names by the light spilling from the lamp room. *Marta Tull. Nettie Quayle. Brine Ollie, sleeps rough, knows the reef better than any chart.* Every name a face. Every face a debt or a kindness or a grudge thirty years old.

Tomorrow they would start coming to the door. Not to the lighthouse door with loaves and honey — to Mirabel, directly, hat in hand, eyes down, asking. Asking the only person in Gannet Point who knew everyone to please, please remember that they mattered.

And Mirabel would have to look at each of them and say *I cannot,* or worse, say nothing at all.

Far out past the reef, the sea grumbled against the dark like something impatient. The next storm was gathering itself beyond the horizon, patient as arithmetic.

Three days, the colonel had said.

Mirabel closed the notebook, went inside, and trimmed the wick.
