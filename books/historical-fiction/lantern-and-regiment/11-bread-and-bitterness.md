## 11. Bread and Bitterness

The offerings had stopped.

Mirabel noticed it the way one notices a clock that has gone quiet — not all at once, but as an absence with weight to it. For a week after Wren and Tam had come to stay, the lighthouse doorstep had collected small tributes like a shrine: a twist of smoked herring, a jar of honey so dark it was nearly black, a bundle of candles someone had clearly made themselves and been too proud of to leave anonymously (the initials *E.P.* were carved into each one, which rather defeated the purpose). Mirabel had pretended not to know who left what, and the town had pretended not to notice Mirabel pretending, and between those two politenesses a kind of warmth had grown that Mirabel had refused — absolutely refused — to name.

Now the doorstep held only salt spray and a dead gull's feather, and Mirabel understood why before they'd walked halfway down the cliff path. The chalk marks had done it. The numbers on the doors had done it. The moment the army put arithmetic on the town, the town stopped giving things away for free.

The bakery smelled the way it always had — of woodsmoke and scorched flour and Marta Tull's stubborn refusal to let a war or a flood or His Majesty's own red-coated regiment interfere with the morning loaves. But the queue outside it was different this morning. It stood in clumps, not lines. People were talking with their shoulders hunched, the way people talk when they are saying things they don't want the wind to carry.

Mirabel had come for bread. That was the whole of the errand. Two loaves, because Wren ate like a growing gull and Tam had lately developed an appetite that seemed, given his silence, almost defiant — as though eating were the one argument he could still win.

"Marta," Mirabel said, ducking under the lintel. "Two of the dark ones, if they've survived your customers."

"Survived?" Marta Tull was a broad woman built like her own ovens, with forearms that could have kneaded dough through a hull. She did not smile. That was the first wrong thing. In thirty years she had never once handed Mirabel bread without some remark about lighthouse folk and their fancy appetites, or the time Mirabel had paid her in foreign coins salvaged from a wreck and she'd spent a fortnight trying to convince a skeptical fishmonger that Portuguese currency was legal tender in Gannet Point.

She wrapped the loaves in silence.

"Marta."

"Don't." The word came out flat as a rolled crust. She set the loaves down and kept her hands on them, as though Mirabel might snatch them and bolt. "Don't say my name like that. Like we're just two women at a counter."

"We are two women at a counter."

"You're the keeper of the list." Her voice dropped, and the bakery seemed to lean in around them — the boy at the back shoveling peat into the oven, the girl scraping trays, all of them suddenly very busy and very attentive. "That's what they're calling you. Down the harbor. The keeper of the list."

"I'm the keeper of a light. The army keeps its own lists."

"With whose hand? Whose ink?" Marta finally let go of the bread and wiped her palms on her apron, over and over, as though the dough wouldn't come off. "They say you wrote every name yourself. Every household. They say Colonel Redcoat trusts nobody else's figures but yours."

"They say a great deal down the harbor."

"My Sam is fourteen." It came out of her like something she'd been holding behind her teeth since the chalk went up on her door. "Fourteen and strong as an ox and he can mend a net blindfolded, and they've got him down for a berth same as me, but there's four of us, Mirabel, and our number says three. Three." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, like a door swinging on a rusted hinge. "I've baked bread for this town since before you kept the light alone. I fed your Ede every market day, God rest them, and never once took full coin. And now I'm standing here asking you — begging you — to look at a ledger and find me a line where three can become four."

The bakery was very quiet. Even the fire in the oven seemed to have drawn its breath.

Mirabel looked at the loaves between them and found they could not pick them up. Their hands would not do it. To take bread from Marta Tull now felt like taking a bribe, or worse, like accepting payment in advance.

"Marta, I don't choose—"

"No. You don't. That's what you'll say. I know that's what you'll say, because it's what's true, and truth is the only coin you've ever dealt in." Marta's eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "But you *see* the list. You hold the pen. When they ask you whether the Tull boy is skilled, whether he's useful, whether he's worth his berth — you'll answer, won't you? They'll ask, and you'll answer, and whatever you say will be written down in your fine straight hand and it will be *so*."

"And what would you have me say?"

"The truth!" She slammed her palm on the counter; the loaves jumped. "That Sam Tull can splice cable better than any soldier the army ever shipped! That my ovens could feed a regiment if they'd take them! That we are *useful*, Mirabel, that we are worth the tonnage—" She stopped herself. Stopped, and looked down at her own hands, and Mirabel watched her hear her own words and flinch from them. *Worth the tonnage.* As though her son were cargo to be justified by weight.

"That's what it does to us," Marta said, quieter now. "That's the filthiest part of it. They've got us measuring our children in pounds and feet and calling it sense. And you're the only one in this town they'll listen to."

* * *

Mirabel carried the loaves out without paying. Marta hadn't asked for coin, and Mirabel hadn't offered, and both of them had understood the transaction perfectly: the bread was not free, and it was not a gift, and neither of them could say what it was.

It was in the square, adjusting the paper-wrapped loaves under one arm, that Cobb Fenwick fell into step beside them.

Cobb had the particular talent of certain men for appearing larger indoors than out and larger outdoors than either. He was a boat-owner, a net-magnate by the standards of Gannet Point, which meant he owned two boats and everyone owed him money on both. He and Mirabel had been rivals since childhood, in the way of children who grew up on the same short street with nothing to divide but temperament — Cobb hoarding, Mirabel keeping, and each convinced the other's virtue was merely vice wearing its Sunday clothes.

"Lighthouse keeper," Cobb said, in the tone other men used for *good morning*.

"Cobb."

"Funny thing." He walked with his hands clasped behind him, a man strolling, a man with nowhere urgent to be, which from Cobb meant everything was urgent. "Funny thing about that list of yours. My name's on it. My wife's name's on it. Our girls." He smiled at a passing neighbor, raised his chin, waited until the man had gone by. "And yet somehow, funny thing, the Fenwick household's number is smaller than the Harrow household's. And the Harrows owe me eleven pounds."

"The numbers aren't mine, Cobb."

"No. No, of course not. The army's numbers. Very fair, the army." He stopped walking. So did Mirabel, because the alternative was walking on without him, and something in Cobb's stillness suggested that would be unwise. "Here's what I keep coming back to, though. Keep coming back to it nights. The colonel — tall fellow, looks like a church with the paint gone — he says you know every soul in this town. Says your word decides what goes in the margins. And margins, keeper, are where the moving happens. A name nudged up. A household recounted. A trade mentioned that wasn't mentioned before." He leaned in, and his breath smelled of nothing at all, which meant he'd been careful, which meant this was planned. "You could mention things."

"I write what's true."

"Then write more of it. Write it louder. Write it for the households that deserve it." He straightened, resumed his stroll, drew Mirabel along by sheer momentum. "Because here's the other funny thing. Things go wrong for folk these days. Boats get requisitioned. Stores get inspected. Chalk gets rewritten. And a man in my position — a man with boats, with crews, with cousins in the customs house — well. A man like that finds out things. Finds out, say, what a certain lighthouse gets up to after dark. Who comes and goes. What's smuggled up the cliff path in the night." He said it lightly, airily, a man discussing weather. "Wouldn't want anything found out. Wouldn't be good for the children."

The word landed exactly where he'd aimed it. Mirabel felt it strike home below the ribs, where all the true targets are.

"There are no children at the lighthouse," Mirabel said. "There is a keeper and a lamp."

"Course there aren't." Cobb beamed. "And I never said otherwise. Funny how a man hears things, though. Well." He touched his cap. "Bread looks heavy. Mind you don't drop it."

He strolled off toward the harbor, whistling, and Mirabel stood in the square holding two loaves like a verdict, understanding that they had just been threatened with the two things they loved most in the world — the light, and the children they refused to call theirs — and that the threat had been delivered politely, in daylight, by a man who would call himself a friend of order.

* * *

Marta caught up with them at the well.

Not Cobb's way — no falling into step, no pleasant menace. Marta came around the corner of the chandlery at a walk that was nearly a run, and she had her purse in her fist, and she thrust it at Mirabel right there in the open street where anyone could see, which was perhaps the point, or perhaps she simply no longer cared who saw.

"Take it."

"Marta—"

"It's not much. It's forty-one pounds and it's everything I've got that isn't in flour, and I know it isn't enough, I know what they're charging down the coast for places, I've heard, God forgive us all, I've *heard*—" Her voice cracked and she pressed on through the crack. "But take it. Take it and when they ask you about the Tulls, remember the woman who fed your Ede for nothing for twenty years, and find us a line. Find my boy a line."

Mirabel looked at the purse. It was soft with age, mended at the seam with red thread, the kind of object that holds not money but a life's small accumulations — and Marta was holding it out in the street like a beggar, this woman who had never begged for anything, who had once told the entire congregation at chapel that charity was just pride with its collar turned around.

"Marta." Mirabel did not take the purse. "If I took it, I'd be no better than the colonel. Worse. He counts honestly."

"Then don't take it." Her hand trembled; the purse trembled; somewhere behind them the well chain rattled as someone drew water, indifferent as the sea. "Just remember it exists. Remember what I'd have paid. That's worth something, isn't it? Knowing what a thing's worth even when nobody buys it?"

And that — that was worse than Cobb's threat, somehow. Cobb had aimed at fear, and fear Mirabel knew how to stand against; you stand against it the way you stand against weather, by refusing to be moved and counting the hours. But Marta was aiming at love, and love had no counterweight that Mirabel possessed. Love just sat in the chest getting heavier.

"I'll remember," Mirabel said. "I remember everything. That's the whole curse of me."

Marta searched their face for a long moment — looking, Mirabel supposed, for the lie, or the mercy, or whichever one she feared more. Then she closed her fist around the purse and turned away, and her shoulders, which had been squared for battle, came down around her like a house settling.

Mirabel watched her go and thought: *she thinks I refused her because I'm honest. She doesn't know I refused her because I'm already planning to steal from the army, and I won't have her money mixed up in it.*

Which was, Mirabel admitted, walking home with cold bread under one arm, a very convenient sort of honesty. The kind that costs nothing until it costs everything.

* * *

The cliff path home was steep enough that Mirabel allowed themselves, as a rule, three rests disguised as views. Today they took five.

From the second landing they could see the harbor, and the evacuation ship riding at anchor like a black tooth in the bay, and the little clusters of townsfolk on the quay who no longer waved at passing boats but counted them. From the fourth landing they could see the customs house, and the sentry at its door, and the window of the room where the ledger lay — *their* ledger now, their handwriting, their neighbors' lives in their own straight ink — and Mirabel made themselves look at it, because a keeper who can't look at a thing has no business keeping it.

By the sixth turn, the thought had assembled itself fully, the way fog assembles over cold water, without any single moment of decision:

*Marta wants a line moved. Cobb wants a line moved. Half the town wants a line moved, and the other half wants the whole list burned. And Brine Ollie says there's a boat.*

Mirabel stopped on the path.

They hadn't spoken to Ollie about it yet — not properly, not past the half-drunk muttering at the Anchor last week, *there's ways through the reef as aren't on no chart the navy ever bought*. But the thought had been sitting in Mirabel since then like a wick waiting for a flame, and now, standing on the cliff with Marta's trembling hand and Cobb's smiling threat still hanging in the sea air, the flame arrived.

Forty souls, Ollie had said. Forty more berths that existed on no manifest, answered to no colonel, appeared on no list. Forty names that need never be weighed against each other at all.

It was madness. It was smuggling, which was madness's respectable cousin. If Ashworth discovered it — and Cobb, damn him, had practically announced he was in the business of discovering things — it would not be a fine or a reprimand. It would be a rope, or a cell, or a firing squad on the shingle, and the children—

The children.

Mirabel climbed the last stretch fast, as though the thought might outrun them if they dawdled, and pushed open the lighthouse door expecting the usual quiet: Wren reading, Tam folding, the great lens turning overhead with its sound like a slow heart.

Instead the tower was silent in the wrong way. Wren stood at the foot of the stairs, pale, clutching the banister, and Tam's chair by the stove was empty, and on Mirabel's desk — the desk where the ledger drafts lay — sat a small folded paper boat, white and precise, with a name written inside in a child's careful hand.

Wren looked up at Mirabel, and her face said plainly that whatever was coming next had already begun without them.

"He's been writing them all afternoon," she whispered. "Mirabel — he's written *every name in the town*."
