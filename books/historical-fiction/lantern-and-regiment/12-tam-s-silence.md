## 12. Tam's Silence

The boat sat on my desk like a small white accusation.

I did not touch it at first. I had learned — in the year since Ede went out with the tide and did not come back — that some things on a desk are better left unexamined until you have braced yourself for them. A bill. A letter from the Board of Trade. A folded paper vessel containing, according to Wren, every name in Gannet Point.

"Every name," I said.

Wren nodded. Her knuckles were white on the banister. "I counted the ones he threw away. The ones that weren't good enough. There's a whole fleet in the kindling box." She swallowed. "Mirabel, he used your ink. He climbed up and took your ink."

That was the part that frightened her, I think — not the boats themselves but the ink. Tam was six years old, and he had not spoken since the water took his grandmother's cottage off the shingle at Marrow Lane with her in it, and here he had gone about his business like a little clerk, helping himself to supplies, working through an afternoon with the patience of a man settling accounts.

"Where is he now?"

"The gallery," Wren said. "He took the lamp oil can but only a little of it, I watched him, he was careful." She said this as though carefulness from Tam were somehow worse than recklessness. Perhaps it was. Reckless children can be caught. Careful ones have already thought three steps past you.

"Stay by the stove," I told her, and she stayed, because Wren understood orders even when she hated them, and I climbed the stairs two at a time with my heart going like a pump handle.

* * *

Tam was sitting cross-legged on the gallery deck with his back against the lantern housing, sheltered from the wind by the masonry parapet, and around him — arranged in rows with a precision that made my chest hurt — were paper boats.

Dozens of them. White ones from the good writing paper, gray ones from newspaper, one made from the brown paper that had wrapped our last delivery of lamp oil. They sat in ranks on the wet stone, sails down, hulls up, waiting for something.

Tam himself was folding another. His fingers moved with a sureness that no six-year-old should possess over paper — crease, turn, crease again — and his face was the face he always wore now: not sad exactly, not empty, but *elsewhere*. A boy standing on a shore watching a ship that has his whole family aboard.

"Tam," I said.

He didn't look up. He finished the fold, pressed the keel flat with his thumbnail, and set it in the row with the others. Then he picked up a fresh sheet.

I crouched beside him, joints cracking, and looked at what I could see of the nearest boat. Written inside its hull, in Tam's careful, laborious hand — the letters of a child who has learned to write but does not yet trust it — was a name.

*Marta Tull.*

I opened the next one. *Cobb Fenwick.* The next. *Silas Prout, fisherman.* Another: *Mrs. Ennor and baby Rose.* Another: *Brine Ollie (real name Oliver Brine, he says backwards).*

Every name in the town. Every name I had spent these long weeks putting into the regiment's ledger with a soldier's pen and a traitor's heart. Here they were again, folded small, set out on stone under a gray sky, in the handwriting of a boy who could not say a single word of them aloud.

"Tam," I said again, softer. "What are they for?"

He stopped folding. For a moment I thought he might answer — his mouth worked, his breath caught — and then he did something worse than silence. He pointed.

He pointed past the parapet, past the rocks, out to where the harbor water heaved gray and swollen under the lowering clouds, and then he brought his hand down slowly, slowly, until his finger touched the stone of the gallery floor, and he laid the newest boat down upon it, gently, the way you would lay a flower on a grave.

Then he looked up at me, and his eyes asked the question his voice could not:

*If the sea gives them back, will anyone?*

I am forty-one years old. I have trimmed lamps through hurricanes. I have signed away my neighbors' futures in a customs house with steady hands and called it duty. But I sat down on the cold wet stone of my own gallery, next to a silent child and his fleet of paper souls, and for a long time I could not do anything at all except breathe.

* * *

We came down before dark, the boats carried in my coat in armfuls, because rain was coming and Tam watched me tuck each one safe with an anxiety that told me plainly: these mattered. These were not play. Whatever ceremony he had been building toward on that gallery, the boats themselves were sacred cargo.

I cleared the corner of my desk — moved the ledger drafts, moved the ink, moved Ede's logbook that I still kept within reach the way other widows keep a wedding ring — and I set the fleet there, ranked in rows on the scarred oak, forty-one boats in all.

Forty-one. I counted twice. It was close to the number of names in the berth ledger. Close enough that my hands shook doing the arithmetic.

Wren stood beside me, arms crossed, in the way she had adopted lately — a girl of nine trying on the posture of a much older woman and finding it fit alarmingly well.

"He started after we came back from the square," she said. "When they chalked the numbers on the doors. He stood looking at the Harrow cottage for ever so long. The one with the four crossed out and the two written under."

I knew the one. Everyone knew the one. The Harrows had erased the army's own figure and written a smaller one beneath so that the difference — two berths' worth of arithmetic — might flow to a neighbor. Sergeant Hollis had stood in the lane and watched them do it, and had said nothing, and walked away with his collar up as though the wind were in his face from all directions at once.

"And then?" I said.

"And then he came home and got the paper." Wren's voice dropped. "Mirabel, he isn't playing. I know play. That wasn't play."

No. It was not play. It was a prayer, or a protest, or a ledger of his own — and God forgive us all, but in Gannet Point that winter, ledgers were the only language anybody trusted anymore. The colonel had his. I had mine. And now a six-year-old who had lost his words to the sea had built one out of paper and set it on my desk, where the other ledgers lived, as if to say: *here is mine, and mine keeps everyone.*

"What do we do?" Wren asked. It was not really a question. What she meant was: *what happens to us?* Because that was the fear underneath, wasn't it — the fear I had been stepping around for days the way you step around a hole in the stairs you've decided not to fix. The lighthouse was essential. One keeper might stay. And Wren's name and Tam's name sat on the evacuation list, written in my own hand, assigned to berths on ships that would carry them away to the mainland and to whatever came after, and nothing in any regulation said those berths would be on the same ship, or anywhere near each other, or anywhere near me.

They had made a home here without asking. And they had heard, children always hear, that homes are not among the categories the army recognizes. Skilled trades. The young. The useful.

"They're staying for supper," I said, which was not an answer either, and Wren knew it, and let it go with a look I felt on my back all the way to the stove.

* * *

Supper was barley soup and the heel of Marta Tull's loaf, toasted, and Tam ate his share and half of mine before I noticed and pushed the bowl across. Afterward Wren washed up — she had claimed the washing-up in week one and defended it like territory ever since — and Tam sat at the table with his hands folded, watching the paper fleet on the desk across the room.

I trimmed the lamp. I checked the clockwork. I wrote the weather in the log — *wind SW freshening, sea high, glass falling* — and I found myself pausing over the page, because there was a habit growing in me that the official ledger forbade and my own private one indulged, and tonight I wrote beneath the barometer reading: *41 paper boats on the keeper's desk. All accounted for.*

It was while I was grinding the day's coffee — a luxury I permitted myself because grief without small ceremonies curdles into something worse — that I heard it.

A sound from the desk. Small. Deliberate.

Tam had climbed onto my chair. He had taken the topmost boat from the fleet — *Marta Tull*, the first one, the one he'd made first if the wear on its folds meant anything — and he was holding it out to me.

Not giving it. Offering it. There is a difference, and every fisherman's widow in Gannet Point knows it; you learn it the hard way, standing on a quay with your arms full of things that belonged to the drowned.

"For me?" I said.

He nodded once.

I took it carefully. Inside, in that laborious hand: *Marta Tull, baker. Good at bread. Kind.*

There was more written under the name than I had seen on the others. Little notes, in a child's spelling that broke my heart in ordinary ways — *kind*, spelled with a K that leaned; *good at bred* — and beneath that, in smaller letters, pressed so deep the pencil had nearly torn the paper:

*dont send her away from her granchildren*

I read it three times. Then I looked at Tam, and Tam looked back at me, and his eyes said what they had been saying all along, what the whole fleet on my desk had been shouting in the only voice it had:

*I know what you're writing in that book at night. I know what the numbers mean. Please remember that every one of them is a person who is loved, and please, please don't forget that when you hold the pen.*

"Marta's grandchildren," I said, my voice coming out rougher than intended. "Jonah and Pip. Yes. They sleep in her loft when their father's boat runs late." Tam nodded, solemn, satisfied that the record was correct. That the annotation had been received and entered.

And then — I will write this plainly or not at all — he reached out and put his small hand flat on the closed cover of the regiment's draft ledger, and left it there a moment, the way you'd rest your hand on a horse you were trying to gentle.

Then he took it away, slid down from the chair, and went to stand by Wren at the window, and the two of them watched the dark water together while I stood in the middle of my own kitchen holding a paper boat with a baker's name inside it and understanding, at last and completely, what I had become.

I was not the keeper of the light. Not anymore, not only that. Somewhere between the requisition order and the ink-stained pages, I had become the keeper of the list. And a child who could not speak had found the one way left to make sure I never kept it carelessly.

* * *

I did not sleep well. Few of us did, that winter, but this night was particular. I lay in my bunk above the lamp room listening to the wind work at the gallery rail, and every time I closed my eyes I saw the fleet on the desk, ranked and patient, waiting for its launching.

Somewhere past two in the morning I gave it up and came down for warm milk, and found Wren awake too, sitting on the cold hearth with her knees drawn up.

"He thinks if he writes everyone down," she said, without preamble, "the sea will give them back. Like it took them." She turned her chin onto her knees. "That's why the boats. He folds the people and puts them on the water in his head, and then—" Her voice snagged. My brave girl, nine years old, translating her brother's silence nightly because no one else could. "—and then the sea gives them back. That's what he told me with his eyes. Before."

"Wren—"

"You won't put us on different ships." She said it fast, the way you say a thing before courage leaves. "Whatever the colonel says. Whatever the list says. You won't. Because Tam can't—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, and this was the worst of it, this was the sentence I will hear on my deathbed: "Tam can't say stay. But if they separate us he'll never say anything again. Not ever. He'll go somewhere inside where even I can't follow, and I'm the only one who knows the way, and you can't put me on a boat that goes somewhere else from him. You *can't*."

"I know," I said.

"Do you?" And there it was — the question with teeth, asked by a child because the adults in Gannet Point had gotten too polite to ask it of each other. "Because you keep saying *one night*, and it's been weeks, and we wash your dishes and Wren — I — trim the wicks better than you do, you said so, and the fishermen leave fish at the door and you pretend you don't know who leaves them, and none of that is *one night* anymore, Mirabel, and you know it, and you still haven't said what happens to us."

The fire ticked. Above us the lamp turned, steady, faithful, keeping its idiot perfect schedule while the world underneath came apart.

"No," I said finally. "I haven't said. Because everything I could say right now would be a lie, and you'd catch it, and you shouldn't have to catch lies from me too. So I'll say this instead: whatever happens, however the lists fall, I will not let them be separated. Not the two of you. That is not a promise I can guarantee with ships and colonels — but it is a promise I will spend everything I have keeping. Do you believe me?"

She studied me for a long moment with those grave gray eyes, weighing me the way she weighed everything now, having learned early that adults' words float and their actions sink.

"Provisionally," she said at last.

From the great maritime vocabulary of a nine-year-old, there are worse verdicts. I laughed — actually laughed, a rusty sound that surprised us both — and she almost smiled, and above us the beam swept round, round, round, out over the water where the real boats waited, the wooden ones, the ones whose passenger lists were being drafted in a customs house by lamplight.

* * *

In the morning there were two new boats on the desk.

Tam must have risen before dawn, worked in the blue-gray quiet while I slept. He had not disturbed the fleet. He had simply added to it, and placed the two newcomers apart from the others — set slightly forward, slightly apart, the way a proud fisherman moors his own boat nearest the steps.

I opened the first one. *Wren Vane* — for so the children had named themselves, quietly, weeks ago, without asking permission, and I had pretended not to notice because noticing would have required answering. Inside: *Wren. Nine. Can trim wicks better than Mirabel. Knows the way everywhere.*

The second: *Tam. Six. Quiet. Folds good boats.*

And beneath, in letters pressed so hard the pencil had snapped, a line that was less an annotation than a plea, a prayer, a small boy's entire soul folded into seven words:

*dont send us away from mirabel*

I stood in my kitchen in the early light with two paper boats shaking in my hands, and outside, far off down the coast road, I heard a sound I had been dreading for days: boots. Many boots. The regiment moving through the lanes with paint and chalk, marking the doors, numbering the town.

The arithmetic was about to be written on every threshold in Gannet Point.

And somewhere in that marching column, a sergeant with an uneasy conscience was carrying the chalk.
