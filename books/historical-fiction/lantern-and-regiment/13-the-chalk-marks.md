## 13. The Chalk Marks

Sergeant Elias Hollis had been a soldier for nineteen years, and in nineteen years he had learned that the worst orders were never the loud ones. The loud ones — *charge*, *hold*, *retreat* — at least told you plainly what they wanted of your body. It was the quiet orders that rotted a man. The ones delivered in a colonel's level voice over a table covered in paper, the ones that said *chalk* and *numbers* and *households* and never once said the word they were all dancing around, which was *who lives*.

So it was Hollis who walked the streets of Gannet Point on a grey Tuesday morning with a stick of chalk in his pocket and a ledger of figures under his arm, and it was Hollis who stopped at the first door — the Tulls', the bakery, smelling of yesterday's bread even now — and stood there like a man who has forgotten how to knock.

"Number of berths per household," Ashworth had said. "Chalk it on the lintel where the boarding parties can see it. Two digits, no more. We are not running a parish fête, Sergeant."

No, sir, Hollis had thought. A parish fête would at least have had cake.

He chalked the first number. *3*. The Tulls were three: Marta, her husband, and the grandmother who was, by all accounts, the reason the bread was worth eating. Three. He looked at the number and thought of the grandmother's hands in flour and decided, in the small private parliament that every soldier convenes behind his own eyes, that he did not care for his government.

* * *

By noon the town wore its arithmetic.

It was strange, Hollis thought, how quickly people learned to read it. He had expected tears, and there were tears, but mostly there was a terrible quiet competence. Folk came out of their doors and looked up at the lintels the way farmers look at the sky. A woman on Harbour Row — Mrs. Penty, the ledger said, four in household, *2* berths — stood with her two eldest and her two youngest and did not say anything at all, which was worse than saying something.

A boy of perhaps ten followed Hollis for three doors, watching the chalk with the flat attention of a child who has already understood the thing and is now only checking the adults' work.

"Sir," the boy said finally, at the Fenwick gate, "what happens to the numbers that don't fit?"

Hollis crouched down, which was against several regulations and in favour of several better ones. "What's your name?"

"Penty. Josiah."

"Josiah. The numbers are just — they tell the boats how many they can carry. That's all a number is. It doesn't decide anything by itself."

Josiah considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "My da says a number never did anything by itself. It's always a man holding it."

Hollis had no answer to that, because it was true, and because the man holding this particular number was him, and because his hand had gone slightly damp around the chalk. He ruffled the boy's hair, which was also against regulations, and walked on.

* * *

The trouble — the real trouble, the kind that gets into a man's boots and won't shake out — began at the Cobb place on Fisherman's Walk.

The Cobbs were six. Old Cobb himself, who was not to be confused with Cobb Fenwick the rival and general menace — the town apparently contained two Cobbs out of spite — his wife, their son and daughter-in-law, and two grandchildren. Six souls, *2* berths chalked on their lintel, because old men and old women were, in the colonel's arithmetic, ballast.

Hollis had finished the whole street and was doubling back with his tally when he saw them: the son, a big weathered fisherman with hands like spades, standing at his own door with a rag in one hand and a fresh stick of chalk in the other.

Hollis stopped in the shadow of the wall and watched.

The son wet the rag and erased the *2*. Then he wrote, in careful, schoolroom strokes, a *1*.

Then his wife took the chalk from him and erased the *1* and wrote — Hollis had to squint — a *0*.

They stood back and looked at their own door, at the zero sitting there like a small round mouth, and the wife said something Hollis couldn't hear, and the son laughed, a short awful laugh, and said something back, and then they went inside and shut the door on their own arithmetic.

Hollis stood in the lane for a long while.

He understood it, of course. That was the devil of it. The Cobbs had looked at the numbers and seen that the Penty family next door had a nursing mother and a newborn, and that the widow Ackland two doors down had nobody at all, and they had done the only sum that made sense to them, which was to subtract themselves. They had not asked permission. They had not appealed to the colonel. They had simply taken up the chalk — *his* chalk, the army's chalk, the instrument of the requisition — and turned it around.

A number never did anything by itself. It's always a man holding it.

Or, apparently, a family un-holding it.

Hollis walked back to the customs house and stood in the doorway with the wet sea-wind at his back, and Colonel Ashworth looked up from his maps with those grey, patient eyes, and asked, "The street is marked?"

"The street is marked, sir."

"Discrepancies?"

And here it was, the moment, the little hinge on which a man's whole remaining self-respect swings. Hollis thought of the zero on the Cobb door. He thought of the rag and the careful schoolroom strokes. He thought of Josiah Penty's face, and of the fact that somewhere in his breast pocket was a tally sheet that could, with one sentence, send a boarding party to overrule a family that had chosen to give itself away.

"Minor discrepancies, sir," Hollis said. "Folk counting their households wrong. Grandmothers left off. That sort of thing. I'll make corrections as I go."

Ashworth nodded, satisfied, and returned to his maps, and Hollis went out into the cold and discovered that he had just told his first lie in nineteen years of service, and that it had come out of him smooth as oiled rope, and that he felt — this was the part that frightened him — *lighter*.

* * *

He began bending things that same week, and he found, to his further alarm, that he was rather good at it.

It was not forgery. He was too much of a soldier for forgery; his hand shook at the very thought of it. It was subtler. It was the art of the accurate inaccuracy. A household of five recorded as six, because the eldest daughter had been away at her aunt's in Millhaven and might, mightn't she, come home before the boats. An old man's trade amended from *fisherman, retired* to *fisherman, active — netmender, essential to fleet refit*, because the colonel's own priority list said skilled trades boarded first, and if the colonel wanted to be governed by his list then the list could learn some manners.

He adjusted the widow Ackland's age downward by nine years, which she would have enjoyed enormously had she known, and which moved her from the *ballast* column to the *infirm but manageable* column, and he told himself the difference between those two columns was the whole of civilization.

"You're spending a lot of time on the door-to-door, Sergeant," observed Corporal Reeve, the young one, the one with the cowlick and the incurable habit of noticing things.

"Verification takes time, Corporal."

"Seems like every time you verify a house it comes back with one more person in it than it had before."

"Folk move about. Babies get born. It's a whole coast of them."

Reeve squinted at him. Hollis stared back with the expression he had perfected over two decades, the one that communicated *I am thinking about nothing and you should move along*, and it worked, as it always worked, and Reeve went off to bully the harbour stores, and Hollis sat down and put his head briefly in his hands and laughed at himself, alone, without much humour.

* * *

The lighthouse keeper, he noticed, was watching him.

He shouldn't have been surprised. Mirabel Vane missed nothing; it was in the nature of the profession. Hollis had been up to the lighthouse twice — once to inform the keeper that the light was now designated an *essential navigational aid under military requisition*, which Mirabel had received with a look that could have preserved fish, and once to ask, on the colonel's instruction, whether the keeper knew every household in the town.

*I know everyone* had been the answer, delivered flat as a slate, and the colonel's eyes had lit up like a man finding money in an old coat.

Now Hollis came up the path on a Thursday with his tally book and his guilty conscience, ostensibly to verify the lighthouse household, and Mirabel met him at the door with two cups already set out, which was somehow more disarming than a rifle.

"You look like a man carrying wet sand," Mirabel said, and poured the tea.

"I'm carrying figures."

"Same thing. Heavier." Mirabel sat, and looked at him across the little table with the lamp-room's great eye turning slow and golden above them, and said, "You've been chalking doors."

"I have."

"And the numbers on the doors don't match the numbers in your book."

Hollis's tea went very still in his hands. "The keeper keeps a close count."

"I keep every count in this town. It's the whole of my character, I'm afraid. The Cobbs wrote a nought on their own door." Mirabel said it without drama, the way one reports weather. "The Penty boy asked a soldier what happens to the numbers that don't fit. And the widow Ackland is sixty-eight in your book and seventy-seven on the parish roll, and I'd wager my lamp she's delighted about it."

The wind turned the light above them. Hollis set down his cup.

"Are you going to report me, keeper?"

"To whom? The colonel?" Mirabel almost smiled; it was a narrow thing, more crack than smile. "I'm the one who has to write his ledger. Every name in my hand is a life in his arithmetic. You think I'd hand him one more honest man to subtract?" Mirabel looked out the seaward window, where the grey water was building its greyer argument. "I'd hand him a lie first. I'd hand him a hundred."

"Careful, keeper. That's near treason."

"That's near arithmetic," said Mirabel. "Drink your tea, Sergeant. It's going cold, and cold tea is the one cruelty this house won't stand for."

* * *

That night, in the barracks room that had been the harbourside school until the school was requisitioned along with everything else, Hollis sat with his tally book and his reports and did the thing he had been circling for days.

He bent the biggest thing yet.

The colonel had ordered a summary — households, souls, berths, surplus — a single page of figures that would go up the chain and fix, in official ink, exactly how many of Gannet Point's people were surplus to the tonnage of one ship. Hollis wrote the page. He checked every figure twice, the way he'd been taught, the way he'd taught others.

Then he added a footnote.

*Discrepancies persist between parish rolls and field tallies,* he wrote, in his best round hand. *Recommend a full recount before any list is sealed. Recount to include all households, all ages, all trades. Estimated time: no less than a fortnight, given weather.*

A fortnight. The next great storm was coming — the old fishermen could smell it, they said, in their knees and their nets — but a fortnight was a fortnight. A fortnight was a grandmother's berth and a newborn's berth and a zero on a door rewritten back into a number that meant somebody's da was coming off that rock alive.

It was not forgery. It was not even a lie. Every word of it was true; the discrepancies *did* persist, mostly because he had manufactured them with a pencil and a bad memory. That was the beauty of it, and the horror. He was learning that a man could hollow out an order from the inside and leave it standing, looking official, and no one would know until the thing was opened up and found empty.

He signed it. *E. Hollis, Sgt., verification detail.* His hand did not shake. Nineteen years of practice held the line.

Corporal Reeve, passing his door with a lantern, stuck his head in. "Still at it, Sarge?"

"Reports write themselves, Reeve."

"Mine never do. Mine sit there like cats, daring me."

"Yours are honest, then. That's the difference."

Reeve grinned, saluted with the lantern, and went on, and Hollis sat alone with the lamp turned low and thought about the Cobbs' door. He thought about the rag, and the wet chalk, and the zero. He thought about how the army had come to this town with its lists and its tonnage and its reasonable, terrible sums, and how the town had answered with a stick of chalk and a family that had subtracted itself to save its neighbours, and how the army's whole magnificent machinery had, at the last, been outmaneuvered by arithmetic performed in love.

He thought, too, about the keeper's face in the lamplight. *I'd hand him a hundred.*

And he thought — this came last, quietly, the way the tide comes — that when the recount he had just invented was finally demanded, and the colonel turned to the one person in Gannet Point who knew everyone, everyone's age, everyone's trade, everyone's grandmother and grudge, that the keeper would be standing in the customs house with a pen in hand and a town's fate in the inkwell.

And that the colonel had no idea what he was about to hand Mirabel Vane.

Hollis blew out the lamp. In the dark he could hear the sea working at the harbour wall, patient as a clerk, and somewhere up the coast the great storm gathering its papers, and he fell asleep for the first time in a week without dreaming of chalk.

He was woken before dawn by Corporal Reeve, lantern swinging, rain already loud on the requisitioned roof.

"Sarge — you'd better come. The cellar at the customs house is under water, and the colonel's asking for you, and sir—" Reeve swallowed. "Sir, it's the ledgers. The old town ledger's soaked through. Half the names are gone."
