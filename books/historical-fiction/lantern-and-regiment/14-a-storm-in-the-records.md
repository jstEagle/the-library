## 14. A Storm in the Records

The squall came out of the southwest like a debt collector — no warning, no courtesy, and it took whatever was loose.

Mirabel felt it first as a change in the lamp's draft, that faint shudder in the glass that meant weather turning ugly somewhere beyond the horizon. By the time they'd climbed down from the lantern room, the rain was coming sideways off the harbor, and the gulls had all gone mad or sensible, depending on your view of gulls.

"Tam," Mirabel called into the warm dimness of the kitchen, "get away from the window."

"I'm not *at* the window," Tam said, who was at the window.

Wren didn't even look up from her mending. "He's at the window."

"He's at the window," Mirabel agreed, and lifted him bodily by the collar of his coat, which was Marta Tull's doing — she'd knitted it two sizes too large on the theory that children grow faster than wool wears out. Tam hung in Mirabel's grip like a very small, very indignant anchor.

"The boats," he said. It was one of perhaps nine words he'd spent since the flood, and he spent them carefully, like coins.

"The boats are hauled up above the tide line. Brine Ollie saw to it before anyone asked him to." Mirabel set him down and pointed him toward the hearth with the firm gentleness of long practice. "The sea can have its tantrum without our supervising."

But Mirabel watched the window themselves, after that, when they thought the children weren't looking. The harbor was a churn of gray, the fishing fleet bobbing at their moorings like corks in a washbasin, and beyond them, riding at anchor with its lamps lit against the gloom, the army transport — that great blunt fact that had been sitting in Gannet Point's water for eleven days now, eating coal and patience in equal measure.

Eleven days of ledgers. Eleven days of names. Mirabel's hand still ached at night from the writing of them, though whether from the labor or from what the labor meant, they couldn't have said.

* * *

At the customs house, Sergeant Hollis heard the squall arrive as a rattle of sleet against the shutters and thought, not for the first time, that Gannet Point weather had the manners of a pickpocket.

He was in the upstairs office, transcribing Colonel Ashworth's morning corrections onto the fair copy of the berth list, when Private Dunmore came up the stairs two at a time, wet to the eyebrows and breathing like a man delivering a baby.

"Sergeant. The cellar."

"What about the cellar?"

"It's the sea, sir. The sea's in the cellar."

Hollis put down his pen with the deliberate care of a man who wanted very much to throw it. The customs house stood at the head of the harbor steps, old as the town, its foundations half-buried in the shingle where three generations of clerks had stored records, spirits seized from smugglers, and — in the current emergency — the original household ledger of Gannet Point, along with every scrap of paper the requisition had generated. The cellar had been dry when Hollis inspected it. The cellar had been dry an hour ago.

It was not dry now.

The storm surge had found some ancient drain, some forgotten culvert running down from the high street, and pushed seawater up through it in a brown, reeking flood that stood shin-deep across the whole of the stone floor. Crates floated. A barrel of confiscated rum — Hollis noted its position for later, purely administrative purposes — bobbed gently against the stairs like a fat, contented duck.

And in the corner, in the crate marked in Ashworth's own hand *PRINCIPAL RECORDS*, the water was doing its slow, patient work.

"Get it out," Hollis said. "Get it all out. Now. Everything above the waterline first."

They worked like men bailing a sinking ship, because that was precisely what they were doing, only the ship was made of paper. Hollis waded in to his knees hauling crates while Dunmore ferried armloads up the stairs and laid them across the customs tables in dripping ranks. The ledger itself came up last, because it was heaviest and because Hollis could not make himself hurry it — the book that held every name in Gannet Point, every household, every child, every berth. Water had gotten into it despite the oilcloth. Water always got in. It had swollen the binding until the covers gaped, and when Hollis opened it with the delicacy of a surgeon, he found the first forty pages fused into a single sodden brick, the ink blooming outward in gray flowers, name dissolving into name dissolving into nothing.

Half the book. The front half — the harbor district, the fisher rows, the lanes below the church. All of it gone soft as porridge.

Behind him, Dunmore said, in the hushed voice men use in churches and cellars, "Sergeant. Is that bad?"

"Dunmore, if you ever make sergeant, remember this moment, and never ask a question whose answer you already know."

* * *

Colonel Ashworth arrived within the hour, having been rowed ashore through weather that would have kept any reasonable man aboard, which told Hollis everything about how the colonel ranked reason against command.

He stood over the ruined pages for a long time without touching them. Rain dripped off his greatcoat onto the floorboards. The office smelled of wet paper and wood smoke and, faintly, of the sea itself, which had come uninvited into the heart of the army's business and showed no inclination to leave.

"Drainage," Ashworth said at last. "From the high street."

"Yes, sir. Old culvert. The town maps don't show it."

"No," Ashworth said. "They wouldn't."

There was something in his voice that Hollis did not like, a flatness like ice forming. The colonel turned from the table and looked out the window at the harbor, where the fishermen's boats strained at their lines and the townsfolk moved behind their shuttered windows like shadows behind frosted glass.

"Who knew the records were kept there?"

"The requisition order was posted, sir. Public notice, as required. And the cellar's been in use eleven days — carts going in and out twice daily. Half the town could tell you where we keep the ledger."

"Could they tell me where the culvert is?"

Hollis hesitated. "Sir, I'd imagine the people who've lived here fifty years could tell you where every drop of water in this town goes. That's rather the nature of living in a place."

Ashworth nodded slowly, and the nod was worse than shouting. "A town that knows where the water goes. And a cellar full of the only document deciding who leaves on that ship. How convenient that the water should find it."

"You're not suggesting—" Hollis stopped himself, which was a discipline he practiced daily in this posting.

"I'm suggesting nothing, Sergeant. I'm observing that a document has been destroyed, that its destruction benefits persons unknown, and that I am not a man who believes in coincidence at this tonnage." Ashworth peeled off his wet gloves, finger by finger. "The town will say the sea did it. The sea is very convenient. The sea cannot be arrested."

"And if it *was* the sea, sir? If it was nothing but weather?"

"Then the weather has cost us the harbor district's record, and I will rebuild it from whatever source remains." Ashworth's eyes went, briefly, to the window — toward the lighthouse, white and steadfast at the end of its spit, its beam just visible now in the thickening afternoon. "Vane reconstructed it once from memory. Vane can do it again. Send for them."

"They're keepering a light in a rising storm, sir."

"The light will survive an hour without them. The manifest may not survive another tide." Ashworth drew on fresh gloves, dry ones, and Hollis understood the audience was over. "Double the guard on this building. Two men inside, day and night. Nothing else enters that cellar — no crates, no barrels, no *rum*" — this with a glance at the report Hollis had prudently not yet filed — "and no water. If God sends another flood, Sergeant, you will stand in it and hold the books above your head."

"Yes, sir."

"And Hollis." Ashworth paused at the door. "Post a man on the high street. Where the culvert comes down. I want to know if anyone goes near it."

Hollis saluted, and held the salute until the colonel's boots had faded down the stairs, and then stood alone in the dripping office surrounded by the sodden remains of Gannet Point, and permitted himself one long breath.

Because here was the thing he could not say aloud: he had seen the faces of the fishermen that morning, hauling their boats up ahead of the squall, cursing the weather with the cheerful fluency of men who had cursed it all their lives. He had seen Cobb Fenwick arguing with the tide itself. Not one of them had looked like saboteurs. They had looked like people trying to keep their town dry.

And here was the other thing he could not say aloud: he was not entirely sure he'd have reported it if they had.

* * *

Mirabel came down from the lighthouse through rain that hit like flung gravel, Wren insisting on carrying the lantern and Tam insisting on carrying Wren's spare mittens, which was the arrangement the three of them had silently evolved: everyone carried something, so no one could be sent back.

The customs house guard at the door — young Dunmore again, blue-lipped and miserable — waved them in with visible relief at the sight of another human face.

Inside, the destruction was laid out like an autopsy. The rescued papers hung in rows from a line strung across the office, pegged by their corners, dripping. The great ledger lay open under a lamp, its drowned pages spread as far apart as they would go, and Mirabel stopped dead at the sight of them.

Forty pages. Forty pages of households Mirabel had written out by hand, name by name, doorway by doorway — the Harrow lane families, the net-loft men, old Mrs. Petchey who fed stray cats and grudges in equal measure — all of it gone to gray bloom, ink bleeding into ink until the page read like weather.

"Oh," said Wren softly, beside them. She had gone very still, the way she did when she was seeing more than she let on.

"Stand there a moment longer and drip on the floor, why don't you," said Colonel Ashworth, from the shadow by the hearth. There was no heat in it. Somehow that was worse.

Mirabel hung their coat, sat where indicated, and bent over the ruined book. Their fingers hovered above the swollen paper without touching. "How much is legible?"

"The back half. The cliff district, the farms, the upper town. The harbor rows are gone." Ashworth came to stand opposite, and lamplight caught the exhaustion carved under his eyes — the colonel had not been sleeping either, Mirabel realized, and was surprised by the small unwelcome pang of fellow-feeling. "You wrote these pages once from memory. I'm told you can write them again."

"I can." Mirabel turned a fragile leaf with one fingertip. "Though I'll tell you plainly, Colonel, what I told you before: memory isn't ink. Memory remembers the woman, but it may forget whether she's widow or wife, and the difference matters to your arithmetic."

"Then we shall hope your memory is better than your pessimism." Ashworth pulled a chair around and sat, and for a moment the two of them faced each other across the drowned book like mourners at a wake. Outside, the squall threw sleet against the shutters in long rattling volleys. "You understand what this means."

"That the list is thinner than it was."

"That the list is *provisional*. That every name I cannot verify is a name my superiors will strike for safety's sake, and struck names do not board ships." Ashworth's voice stayed level, but his hand, resting on the table, had closed slowly into a fist. "I built this evacuation on the premise that the numbers were true. Someone — something — has made them untrue, and I have four days before the great storm arrives to make them true again."

"Four days," Mirabel said. "And a town that watched its own ledger drown."

"A town," Ashworth said, "that I am beginning to think would cheer the drowning." He rose abruptly, buttoned his coat. "Begin tomorrow at dawn. You'll work here, under guard, where the records are. Bring whatever notes you require."

"And my post?"

"Will be tended by whoever tends it when you're not there. You have children in your keeping, I'm told." Something flickered across his face — not quite softness; more like a man noticing a door he'd assumed was painted on. "Keep them clear of this building, Keeper. After tonight, I can no longer vouch for what this town thinks of it."

Mirabel walked home through the tail of the squall with a lantern in one hand and a child's mittened hand in each, and said nothing, and thought a great deal.

Because Ashworth had said *sabotage*, and doubled his guards, and posted a man on the culvert — and all of it aimed at the wrong thing entirely. But underneath the wrongness, Mirabel had seen the truth of it plain as the drowned pages: the list was provisional now. Every name uncertain. Every berth negotiable.

Which meant, though neither the colonel nor the sea nor the sergeant knew it yet, that the game had changed — and somewhere down the harbor, in a hut that smelled of tar and secrets, an old smuggler was about to become the most important man in Gannet Point.

Wren squeezed Mirabel's hand in the dark, and said nothing at all, which from Wren was louder than shouting.
