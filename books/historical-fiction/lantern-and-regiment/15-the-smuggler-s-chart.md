## 15. The Smuggler's Chart

The hut at the end of the shingle spit did not have a name, because a thing with a name can be found on a map, and Brine Ollie had spent seventy years making sure that nothing he loved could be found on a map. The town called it Ollie's hut, which was accurate and useless, and this suited everyone.

Mirabel knocked the way you knock on a door that has never once been opened for a knock — twice on the wood, once on the post beside it, an old signal from the days when excise men walked the spit and a knock on the post meant *friend* while a knock on the wood meant *run*. They hadn't used the code in forty years. Ollie answered to it anyway.

"Keeper." He filled the doorway like weather. He was mostly beard and boots now, the middle of him having quietly surrendered to age without informing the rest of him. "Come about the drowned ledger, have you."

Mirabel blinked. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because everybody comes about something lately, and you're not the begging sort, and you're not the threatening sort, so it's either the ledger or you've finally come to tell me my lamp oil's been short since Michaelmas and I'd have noticed if you were here to scold me, because you'd have brought the invoice." He stepped aside. "In, then. Mind the eel."

"The — what?"

The eel turned out to be a bucket of seawater by the door, occupied by a moray the size of Mirabel's forearm, who regarded them with the flat professional disinterest of a customs officer.

"He came in on the last spring tide," Ollie said, as if that explained anything. "I'm calling him Alderman."

"Why?"

"Because he takes a cut of everything that comes through here and does no visible work."

The inside of the hut was dim and smelled of tar, hemp, old tea, and — beneath all of it, faint but unmistakable — salt-soaked paper. Charts hung from every beam, some of them older than the town's church, their ink gone brown as dried blood. A stove ticked in the corner. Two stools stood by it, and Mirabel understood, with a small lurch, that Ollie had been expecting company long enough to put out two seats.

"You knew I was coming."

"I knew *somebody* was coming." Ollie lowered himself onto his stool with the sound of a ship taking green water. "Water's ruined your ledger. Ashworth's guards are walking the culvert like it's a parade ground. And you, Vane, have been standing at your gallery window every evening for three nights looking down the harbor at my end of it, which is flattering but not subtle." He poured tea into tin mugs without asking. "You're a careful woman, but you look at things like a man reading a will. Everybody in Gannet Point knows what that means just now."

Mirabel sat. The tea was strong enough to stand a spoon in and tasted faintly of the kettle's previous life, which had possibly involved pitch. It was, nonetheless, the best thing they'd drunk in weeks, and they said so.

"Liar," Ollie said, pleased.

They let the silence sit for a moment, the way you let dough rise. Then Mirabel set down the mug.

"How many names are on the berth list, Ollie?"

"Two hundred and eleven souls fit on the *Providence*." He said it flatly, without looking up. "And there are four hundred and six of us. I count, Keeper. Counting is most of what smuggling ever was — knowing how much fits where, and what happens when it doesn't."

"One hundred and ninety-five people left over."

"On paper."

"On paper," Mirabel agreed. "But paper is all Ashworth has. Paper is all any of us have right now. The cellar flood took half the original ledger, and whatever he reconstructs from what's left—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "Ollie, I wrote those names. Every one. I know which houses hold six and which hold two. I know that the Pellow twins are four months old and that Widow Sennet won't leave her loom and that Cobb Fenwick would trade his own mother for a berth and then complain about the price of passage." Their voice cracked on something between anger and grief. "I am the arithmetic now. Do you understand? Whatever I write becomes true. One hundred and ninety-five people are going to be subtracted, and I'll be holding the pen."

Ollie looked at them for a long moment. Then he got up, joints cracking like rigging in a squall, and went to the back wall where the oldest charts hung behind a curtain of oiled canvas.

"I'm going to show you something," he said, "that I haven't shown a living soul since the year your Ede was learning to walk. You'll want to remember that, afterward, when you decide what kind of person I am."

He drew the canvas aside.

Behind it, the wall was covered — not hung, *covered*, edge to edge — in charts. Not the Admiralty's clean engraved sheets but hand-drawn ones, layered, annotated, corrected across decades in different inks: depths in fathoms marked in one hand, currents in another, notes in a third that read like poetry written by an accountant. *Shingle shifts NE after gales. Reef breathes. Trust the light, not the chart.*

Mirabel stood slowly. "That last note—"

"Aye. That's yours, or near enough. Your Ede and I traded soundings for thirty years. Best range markers on this coast were never buoys, Keeper. They were always your lamp." Ollie tapped the wall, one callused finger landing on a stretch of water east of the harbor mouth that the Admiralty charts marked with a single confident word: *REEF.* "Everyone in this town believes there's one way through Gannet Reach, and that it's suicide in winter, and that the *Providence* anchors outside it because she must. Everyone believes that because I spent thirty years and a fair amount of money making sure the Admiralty believed it too."

Mirabel's mouth went dry. "There's another way."

"There's another *everything*." Ollie pulled down one particular chart, yellowed soft as cloth, and spread it on the table between the mugs. Drawn across it in waterline profile ran the reef — and through its heart, a channel no wider than a cart track, snaking between teeth of rock, marked with soundings in Ollie's crabbed hand. At the head of it, in ink faded nearly to ghost: *the Smuggler's Gate.*

"It runs a boat-length and a half wide at low water springs, and it moves — not much, a yard maybe, but a yard matters in there. You steer it by transit: bring the Gannet Point light down onto the black rock below the old signal station, hold that line, and turn when the white streak on the north head opens. Your Ede ran me through it eleven times, dark nights, no questions asked, and never once scraped paint." Ollie's voice did something complicated around the name. "It wants a helmsman who knows it and a crew with hands like nerve. But it exists. It has always existed."

"And?" Mirabel said, because Ollie was still talking like a man circling his real meaning.

"And," Ollie said, "there's a boat."

He let that land. Outside, the wind worried the tarred shingles, and somewhere under the floorboards the eel shifted in his bucket with a sound like a sigh.

"Not a navy boat. Not a revenue boat. Not on any register, because registering a boat is how you lose a boat." Ollie traced his finger along the harbor plan to a cove south of the point — a notch in the coastline so small the mapmaker of 1843 had dismissed it as shading. "Kestrel. Sixty feet on the waterline, built in Fowey the same year as the *Providence*, by the same yard, out of the same timber — and nobody living knows it but me and now you. She's been laid up in Smuggler's Hollow these nine years under canvas and tarpaulin, and before you ask: yes, she's sound. I've kept her bilges dry and her timbers sweet the whole time, because a man who keeps only one secret isn't really keeping secrets at all, he's just waiting to be robbed. She'll carry forty souls, wet and miserable and alive, if she carries them gently."

Forty more. Mirabel did the arithmetic before they could stop themselves — they couldn't seem to stop doing it anymore; the colonel had gotten into their head like damp into plaster. Four hundred and six souls. Two hundred and eleven berths. Forty more made two hundred and fifty-one, and one hundred and fifty-five left over instead of one hundred and ninety-five. Still monstrous. Still arithmetic. But forty names was forty families' worth of faces, forty reasons at the chapel meeting not to draw lots against each other like enemies. Forty was not salvation. Forty was a door.

"Ashworth doesn't know she exists," Mirabel said slowly. "If we take her out full of townsfolk—"

"—he'll call it theft of crown property within a requisitioned zone, or piracy, or witchcraft, whichever reads worse in his report," Ollie finished comfortably. "Aye. There's the price, Keeper. There's always a price. Nothing free on this coast but the view."

"What else?"

Ollie counted on fingers gone knotted as tree roots. "One. She needs a crew — six hands minimum, seven better, and every one of them has to be someone whose absence from the *Providence*'s list won't start questions, because the crew goes on a manifest too, these days, God help us. Two. She needs piloting through the Gate, and there are perhaps three people alive who can do it in weather, and I'm one, and my eyes aren't what they were when your Ede was alive, and the other two are dead. So it's me, or it's nobody, unless—" he looked at Mirabel over the rim of his mug, sly and sad at once, "—unless somebody's been standing at that lamp every night for fifteen years watching the sea the way a surgeon watches a wound, and learned the reef's breathing without ever sailing it."

"I'm a keeper, Ollie. Not a pilot."

"You're the only soul in this town who knows what the light looks like from the water side of every rock in the Reach, because you've drawn it, night after night, in your log, in the margins, where the official form says *remarks*." He leaned forward. "Don't think I haven't read your remarks, Vane. Half this town's survival lives in your remarks."

Mirabel said nothing. It was true, and they both knew it was true, and there was a strange shame in it — that all these years of writing down small human details the ledger forbade had been training for exactly this, though they'd never known for what.

"Three," Ollie said, and his voice lost its slyness entirely. "Time. Kestrel needs her seams worked and her gear bent on, and that's five days' honest labor for five good hands, three if the hands are desperate. And the great storm—" he nodded toward the window, toward the western sky, where a bruise was already forming above the horizon that Mirabel had been pretending not to see for two days, "—is coming early. Barometer's falling like a stone down a well. My knee says Thursday. My knee has lied to me exactly twice in seventy years, and both times it apologized."

Thursday. Three days. Hollis's falsified report had bought the town three days — Mirabel didn't know that yet, wouldn't know it until later, but the numbers were going to meet in the middle like two tides, and the meeting place would be the harbor, and it would be crowded.

"So," Ollie said. "Door of hope. Price and deadline attached, as doors go." He refilled the mugs. "Ask me the question you actually came to ask, Keeper. You've been polite for ten minutes and it's wearing on you."

Mirabel wrapped both hands around the mug. "Will it work?"

"No," Ollie said cheerfully. "Not as planned. Plans never work; I've run forty years of plans and not one of them survived contact with the sea. What works is preparation, luck, and people willing to be braver than the plan requires." He drank. "Will it carry forty more souls clear of this coast before Thursday? Aye, if the light holds, and the hands hold, and nobody talks. Which brings us to the last price, and it's the steep one."

"Silence."

"Silence. You, me, and whoever crews her — that's all who can know, and not one soul more, because this town loves its neighbors the way it loves gossip, generously and immediately. If Cobb Fenwick hears the word *Kestrel* before Thursday, Ashworth hears it by supper, and she'll be confiscated and broken up for firewood by Friday, and I'll be transported, and you'll be—" he waved a hand, "—whatever they do to keepers. So. Can you keep it? Truly? Because I've watched you carry this town's secrets in your ledger for years and never once spill a drop. But this one's heavier, and the spill drowns people."

Mirabel thought of the two cups on the table at home — three cups now, and two small chairs dragged up to the table, and Tam's folded boats stacked by the window like a fleet waiting for orders. Thought of Wren's face when she'd seen the chalk marks on the Pellow cottage. Thought of the hundred and ninety-five.

"I kept one secret for fifteen years," Mirabel said quietly. "That I talk to a dead woman every night while I polish the lens. I can keep yours."

Something moved behind Ollie's beard that might have been a smile or might have been grief wearing a smile's coat.

"Then we'd best get to work," he said. "And Keeper — the crew. Choose them tomorrow, quiet as church mice. And choose somebody young and quick and trustworthy for the small errands. Running messages, fetching stores, that sort of thing. Somebody nobody notices."

Mirabel opened their mouth to say that they knew exactly nobody who fit that description, and closed it again, because that wasn't quite honest, was it. There was a girl in the lighthouse who could cross a rooftop like a rumor and had already, unbeknownst to anyone but herself and God, developed opinions about lists.

They walked back along the shingle in the gathering dusk, the chart folded against their chest inside their coat, over the heart, where it beat a little against their ribs with every step. Behind them the hut light went out — Ollie never showed a lit window after dark, not in seventy years, not even for the eel.

At the top of the lighthouse path, Wren was waiting on the step with a lantern and an expression that meant she had counted the minutes and had opinions about the total.

"Well?" she said.

Mirabel looked down the dark harbor, at the black water hiding a boat nobody believed in and a gate nobody had charted, and felt — for the first time since the cellar flood — something that was not dread. It took a moment to recognize it. Hope, it turned out, felt almost identical to fear. Same racing pulse. Same tight throat. The difference was only in which way it made you move.

"Wren," Mirabel said, unlocking the door, "how would you feel about running an errand tomorrow? A secret one."

Wren's whole face changed — the way a harbor changes when the tide comes in.

"I thought you'd never ask."
