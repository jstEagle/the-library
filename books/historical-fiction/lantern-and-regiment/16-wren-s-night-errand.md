## 16. Wren's Night Errand

Wren had learned three things in her nine years at Gannet Point, and all of them were useful for breaking into the customs house.

First: soldiers march on a schedule. Second: grown-ups never look up. Third: if you carry something that looks like you belong somewhere, people assume you do.

She had learned the first thing by watching from the lamp gallery, which was where she did most of her watching now. The sentries changed at the half hour past every even hour — she'd counted, and counted again, and then counted once more just to be sure, because Tam said her counting was "enthusiastic" and she wanted to prove him wrong. The boots came down Harbor Lane at a certain rhythm, stopped at the customs house door, and there was a scrape of a match and a glow behind the frosted window while whoever was coming off watch lit whatever it was soldiers smoked when they thought nobody could see them. Then the new sentry took his post, and the old one walked away, and for about forty seconds the customs house stood with no one watching its front at all.

The second thing she'd learned from Gran, who used to say that children were invisible because adults kept their eyes at the height of their own troubles. Gran had said it about shopkeepers, mostly, when Wren wanted sweets. She hadn't meant it to be useful for this.

The third thing she'd learned only recently: Mirabel Vane carried an oil can into the customs house twice a week and came out with nothing, and nobody ever once asked to look inside the oil can. Nobody looked at oil cans. Oil cans were furniture. If you were a girl walking along Harbor Lane after dark carrying a tin of lamp oil — and there were tins of lamp oil everywhere in Gannet Point these days, stacked outside doors like loaves of bread — you were not a girl. You were part of the street.

So Wren waited until the lamps were trimmed and the supper dishes done and Mirabel sat at the desk below with the great ledger open, pen scratching, scratching, writing names. Writing *their* names, among others, though Wren didn't know yet what that meant. She knew only that Mirabel wrote and wrote and never smiled, and that Tam had taken to sitting on the bottom stair watching, folding his paper boats, silent as he had been since the water took Gran's cottage and everything in it except the two of them.

"I'm going out," Wren announced, in the voice she used for going out, which was the same voice as always so that it wouldn't be suspicious.

Mirabel looked up. There was lamplight on their face and tiredness under their eyes like bruised fruit. "Boots buttoned?"

"Yes."

"Coat?"

"Yes."

"Back before the light needs trimming?"

Wren hesitated one beat too long, and she saw Mirabel notice it — saw the pen stop. But whatever Mirabel thought, they let it go, because that was the arrangement they'd all agreed to without saying it aloud: the lighthouse was a home shaped exactly like a favor, and favors didn't come with questions.

"Before trimming," Wren said.

* * *

The trouble with stealing a list is that first you have to know where it lives.

Wren had spent four evenings learning the customs house from the outside, which was easy, and one evening learning it from the inside, which had been an accident involving a loose cellar hatch, a curious nose, and a very fast retreat that she chose not to count as practice. What she knew was this: the front room held desks and maps and Sergeant Hollis's cold tea, which he left on the windowsill until it went grey, and the back room held the strongbox, and the strongbox held the ledger, and somewhere near the ledger — folded into it, or beneath it, or locked in the little drawer of Ashworth's own desk, which was the biggest desk and faced the door — was the berth list.

She knew the berth list existed because everyone in town talked about nothing else. She knew it was sealed because Cobb Fenwick had said so loudly enough to shake the baker's sign loose. And she knew that her name and Tam's name were written somewhere on it, because Mirabel had gone quiet in a particular way — the way of someone carrying something heavy up many stairs — ever since the day the colonel's clerk had brought the fair copy across the square.

What she did not know was where on the list those names sat. High or low. Boat or no boat. Together or apart.

That was the question she intended to answer tonight, and she intended to write the answer down, because words on paper were true in a way that words in your head were not. Gran used to say that too. *If it matters, write it down, love. Memory is a liar with good manners.*

Harbor Lane at night smelled of wet rope and coal smoke. The chalk marks glowed faintly on the doors as she passed — numbers, each household's allotted berths, tallied up by soldiers who had never eaten at any of these tables. The Pellow house: 3. The Tull bakery above: 4, and Marta had five children, which everyone pretended not to have noticed. Wren walked past them all with her oil can held against her hip like a talisman and her heart going like a gull in a storm.

At the corner by the cooperage she stopped and did her counting one more time. Quarter past the hour. The change would come at the half. Fifteen minutes to walk twenty steps, which left her — she worked it out on her fingers — rather a lot of minutes inside.

She needn't have hurried. She needn't have been afraid at all, as it turned out, but she didn't know that yet, so her hands shook as she set down the oil can beside the customs house step, tucked into the shadow where the other tins stood, and tried the front door.

Locked, naturally. Soldiers locked things; it was most of what they did.

But the customs house had been a fishing warehouse before it was a government building, and fishing warehouses had windows built for hauling barrels through, and one of those windows — the small one beside the coal chute, high up, painted shut by decades of salt air — had been loosened by the winter storms. Wren knew because she'd watched the paint crack flake away over three nights of wind. She climbed the coal chute with her palms black, hooked her fingers under the frame, and pulled until something surrendered with a sound like a fish being gutted, and then she was through, and inside, and standing in the dark among the smells of paper and ink and strangers' boots.

Her eyes adjusted. Moonlight came through the frost-furred glass in thin grey ribbons. The front room was exactly as she'd imagined: desks, maps weighted down with stones, Hollis's teacup on the sill like a small abandoned grave. She moved between the desks the way she'd seen cats move along the harbor wall — low, fluid, apologizing to nothing.

The back room door was closed but not locked. Of course it wasn't locked. Who would lock it? Only a child would climb through a window. Only a child would want the berth list badly enough to bleed her fingers on a coal chute for it.

Ashworth's desk faced the door, big and bare and terrible. She opened the drawers one at a time, slow as tide. Paper. More paper. A tin of pencils, sharpened to vicious points. A Bible, which surprised her until she saw it had never been opened — the spine uncracked, the gold leaf perfect, the kind of Bible bought for a desk instead of a life. In the last drawer, flat and official-looking, a folder stamped with the War Office crest.

Her mouth went dry. She drew out the folder and opened it on the desktop, and there it was.

The berth list. Sealed with wax, but the wax had been pressed only at the corners, and Wren had watched Mirabel steam open envelopes at the kitchen table often enough to know that wax was a promise made to other adults. She peeled it back carefully, the way you peel a limpet off a rock, whole and unhurt.

Names. Columns of them. She found the town's names in her head first — Marta Tull, and her brood; the Pellows; old Sam Brine; Cobb Fenwick — and then ran her finger down the columns looking for the ones she'd come for.

*Vane, M.* — keeper, essential, retained.

And beneath it, in the column marked for the second transport:

*Tam Vane.* No surname of their own; Mirabel must have given them hers. Wren stared at it. Somebody had written *Tam Vane* in a fine clerk's hand, and next to it a number, and the number was low. Low like the bottom of a well.

She read the notes beside the names without understanding them fully and understanding them completely at the same time. Skilled trades ranked high. Young and able ranked middling. Orphans — the word sat there, plain, in the remarks column — *orphaned minors, dependent placement, separate transports advised.*

Separate transports advised.

Tam on one boat. Her on another. Or neither, or both, depending on arithmetic that had never once asked either of them a question.

Wren sat down on Ashworth's chair, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her, and she looked at the list for a long time. Somewhere outside, boots passed on Harbor Lane, and she froze with her hand flat over Tam's name, as though her palm could hold it up in the water.

Then she did what she had come to do. She had brought paper folded small in her coat pocket, and a pencil stub Gran had given her two years ago, and she copied. Not everything — she wasn't fast, and her hand was shaking, and the letters came out crooked — but the order of it, the shape of it, who was high and who was low and who was marked *separate*. She copied the numbers by the chalked houses and compared them in her head to the families she knew, and by the time she reached the bottom of the page she had stopped shaking, because anger is steadier than fear and lasts longer.

It was while she was copying the third column that the floorboard creaked behind her.

She turned, and the moonlight showed her Sergeant Hollis filling the doorway, and every plan she had ever made emptied out of her head at once.

He had his cap in his hand, which somehow was worse than if he'd had a pistol. His face did what faces in Gannet Point did when they were deciding something — it went through weather.

"You're the lighthouse girl," he said quietly.

Wren put her body between him and the list, which was ridiculous, because he was a foot soldier and she was nine years old and the list was already in her memory, but bodies do what they do. "I'm taking my brother's name home," she said, and her voice cracked on *home*, and she hated it, and she lifted her chin anyway. "You can arrest me. But I'm not giving it back."

Hollis looked at her for a long moment. Then he crossed the room — she flinched — and he did not touch her, or the list, or the folder. He picked up Ashworth's cold pipe from the desk edge, turned it over, and set it down again, as though he needed his hands to be busy while his mind finished its argument.

"The cellar hatch," he said. "Third night running somebody's been at that window. I've been standing in the yard listening to you breathe for ten minutes."

Wren's stomach dropped through the floorboards and kept going. "Then why—"

"Because if it were me," Hollis said, "and it was my sister's name on that list, I'd have come through the coal chute too. Louder." He glanced at the folder, at the peeled wax, at her crooked pencil lines, and something in his jaw tightened. "You'll want to press that wax back warm. Do it with your thumb, quick, before it cools. It won't fool the colonel if he looks close. But he doesn't look close. He trusts the seal more than he trusts people. That's his whole mistake."

Wren stared at him. "You're letting me go."

"I'm seeing a girl leave by the window she came in," Hollis said, "because I was on the far side of the building checking the cellar, and saw nothing." He held out his hand, palm up, and for one horrible moment she thought he meant to take her copy. Instead he nodded at it. "May I?"

She gave it to him. She couldn't tell you why. Perhaps because he'd said *my sister* the way Gran used to say *our Wren*, like a fact about himself and not a complaint.

Hollis read the page by the moonlight, slowly, his lips moving slightly. When he reached the line about separate transports, he closed his eyes briefly, the way Mirabel did when the lamp flared wrong. When he reached the bottom of the page, he handed it back.

"There's a family on here," he said, "the Pellows. Five of them, listed as four. The mother sews; the clerk counted her trade as the father's, doubled. Mistakes happen in ledgers." He met Wren's eyes. "Mistakes happen. Remember that, when you're deciding what to do with what you stole."

"What am I supposed to do with it?" Wren whispered.

"That," said Hollis, "is above my rank." And then, almost smiling, in the voice of a man handing a child a lantern in a high wind: "Mind the window frame on your way out. It bites."

* * *

She went out the way she'd come in, pressed the wax warm with her thumb as instructed — it held, mostly, a little smudged at the edge like a thumbprint left on purpose — and dropped onto the cobbles with her heart still hammering but her fists full of truth. She retrieved her oil can. She walked home along Harbor Lane past the chalked numbers, and this time she read them differently, because now she knew which numbers were lies and which were mercies and which were simply mistakes that might, if a person were brave and quick and small, be corrected.

At the lighthouse door she stopped, breath steaming, and looked back once at the dark bulk of the customs house, where a grey-faced sergeant was presumably pouring his cold tea out the window and reporting to no one that he had seen nothing at all.

Inside, the stairs wound up into the smell of lamp oil, and below, Mirabel's pen was still scratching. Tam sat on the bottom stair with a fresh paper boat in his hands, and when he saw her he held it up — a question, the only language he had.

Wren knelt down and took it, and unfolded it carefully, and inside, in Tam's careful blocky letters, was a single name: *Marta.*

One of the boats for every soul in town, Mirabel had told her he was making. Boats with names inside, waiting.

"We have to talk to Mirabel," Wren said. "Tonight. All of us." She smoothed the paper flat against her knee, and her voice came out lower than she meant it to, harder, older. "I know where our names are on the list, Tam. And I know how to get them higher."

Above them, unseen, the great lens turned its steady circles. Below them, the pen scratched on, writing the town into fate one name at a time. And Wren climbed the stairs toward the lamplight with a stolen page against her ribs, feeling less like a thief than she had all night — feeling, in fact, for the first time since the water came, like a keeper of something herself.
