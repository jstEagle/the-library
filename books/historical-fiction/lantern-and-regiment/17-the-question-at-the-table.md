## 17. The Question at the Table

The storm that evening was only a rehearsal of storms — a gray wind worrying the harbor, rain like thrown gravel against the north panes — but it drove everyone indoors early, and by the time Mirabel climbed down from the lamp room, the tower smelled of wet wool and burnt porridge, which was to say it smelled of children.

Wren had attempted supper. This was generous of her. The porridge sat in its pot with the texture and general disposition of harbor mud, and Tam sat across from it, regarding it the way he regarded most things: silently, patiently, as though waiting for it to confess.

"There's salt in it," Wren said defensively. "You're always saying things want salt."

"I say that about soup."

"Porridge is just sad soup."

Mirabel laughed — an actual laugh, unguarded, the kind that arrived before permission did — and then felt it catch on something on the way out, because there were four spoons on the table. They counted them without meaning to. Four. When had that happened? When had the arithmetic of this table changed from *mine* to *ours* without their signature anywhere in the margin?

They ate anyway, the porridge improved by butter Marta Tull had left wrapped in cloth at the door, along with a loaf whose crust cracked like ice going out in spring. Tam ate three pieces. He always ate as if the bread might be reclaimed.

Outside, the light turned above them, steady as breathing. Inside, the stove ticked. And Mirabel found, watching Wren scrape the pot for Tam's benefit while pretending she didn't want any more herself, that they could no longer put off the thing that had been sitting at this table for days now, patient as a third child, waiting to be fed.

*The list.*

* * *

It had come that morning, folded inside the requisition papers Sergeant Hollis delivered with his hat in his hands and his eyes anywhere but Mirabel's face. Colonel Ashworth's final schedule of berths, preliminary draft, subject to certification. Mirabel's name appeared exactly once, in the column marked ESSENTIAL PERSONNEL: *Vane, M. — Keeper, Gannet Point Light. One (1) keeper may remain at station through evacuation period.*

One keeper. One. The army's arithmetic was precise about everything except what it cost.

And below, in the columns for dependents and minors, in Miravel's own careful reconstructed hand — for it was Mirabel who had written every name in that ledger, Mirabel who had given the colonel his ammunition — two entries:

*Wren, age 11. Berth 14, transport vessel.*
*Tam, age 7. Berth 31, transport vessel.*

Different vessels. Different ships, sailing different watches, bound for the mainland at different hours, to be received by different clerks into different registers, and God alone knew when or whether those registers would ever be read side by side.

Mirabel had stood in the customs house doorway holding the paper and understood that they had spent a year learning to be alone again, and had finally, finally begun not to be — and the same hand that had rebuilt the town's names had written these children onto boats that went separate ways.

* * *

Now the bowls were empty and Tam was folding. He folded constantly these days — scraps, wrappings, the margins of old newspapers — small paper boats lined up along the windowsill like a fleet waiting for weather. There were dozens now. Mirabel had stopped asking about them. Something in Tam's stillness said *not yet*, the way the sea says it, and you learned to wait.

"Wren," Mirabel said. "Tam. There's a thing we need to talk about. Properly. At the table, where talking is supposed to happen."

Wren looked up. She had her grandmother's trick of going very still when something mattered, which was unsettling in a child and would be formidable in an adult. "Is it the list?"

"It's the list."

"I already know what's on it," Wren said. "Berth fourteen. I can count, and I can also read upside down, if anyone's wondering."

"You read my copy?"

"I read everybody's copy. Cobb Fenwick leaves his on the bakery counter like he wants people to argue with him." She shrugged. "I know Tam's on thirty-one too, if that's the part you're working up to."

Tam's hands went quiet on his paper. That was all. Quiet hands, and eyes that came up to Mirabel's face and stayed there, and Mirabel understood with a lurch that this silence was not absence — it had never been absence — it was attention, total and terrible, the attention of someone who had lost everything once already and was counting what remained.

"Yes," Mirabel said. "That's the part." They laid the schedule on the table between the salt cellar and the butter dish, official paper among domestic things, and hated how at home it looked. "Here's where we are. The army says the lighthouse must stay lit through the evacuation — every boat, every run, needs the light to find the channel. So one keeper stays. One. That's me, because I'm the keeper, and because—" they stopped, started again. "Because nobody else knows the lens. It has to be me."

"Obviously," Wren said. "Nobody else can do it."

"And your names are on the transports. Both of you. You go on the boats, Wren, and you go on another boat, Tam, and the boats sail for the mainland, and when the water goes down—" Mirabel heard their own voice doing what it always did with hard news: wrapping it, cushioning it, making it smaller than it was. They made themselves stop. Ede had never once lied to Mirabel with kindness; it was the least they could do in return. "No. Plainly. We agreed, all three of us, that this table doesn't get soft words anymore. Plainly, then: the next great storm is coming within the fortnight. The boats leave before it. If the waters don't recede quickly after — and Brine Ollie says they may not, not till spring — then the mainland is months away, not miles. Months. And you'd be there. And I'd be here. And there are two ships."

Tam's folded boat creased under his thumb. A small sound, like a knuckle.

"The colonel offered me a bargain," Mirabel went on, because plainly meant plainly, all the way down. "I survey the reef passage for his Kestrel scheme, and he guarantees your berths. Guaranteed places, safe passage, no lottery, no list-shuffling. But the guarantee comes split — Wren on the first transport, Tam on the second, each with a clerk's receipt, each entered in a different book. He'll keep you safe. He will not keep you together."

"Why?" Wren asked. Not angry. Worse — genuinely asking, the way she asked about tides and lens-works, wanting the mechanism of it.

"Because separated people cause less trouble. Because two children alone in a strange port are manageable, and two children together are a household, and households make claims." Mirabel's jaw ached; they realized they'd been clenching it since morning. "That's the arithmetic he uses. I'm telling you because you live under it same as I do."

Wren looked at the paper a long moment. Then she did the thing that frightened Mirabel more than any storm: she thought it through. Out loud.

"So if we take his bargain, we're safe and apart. If we don't, we're on the open list, together maybe, unless the list changes, which lists do." Her finger traced the columns. "And you stay here either way, because the light has to burn, and there's one berth at the lighthouse and it's yours, and nobody's even pretending there's a version where you come on a boat, because the colonel needs you more than you need him."

"That's near enough the shape of it."

"No," Tam said.

Both of them turned. Wren's mouth opened and stayed open. In all the weeks since the flood — since the cottage gone, since the grandmother gone — Tam had not spoken. Not a word, not a cry, not even in sleep, or so Wren swore. The doctor on the supply boat had called it shock that would pass, and passed it hadn't, and the whole town had learned to read the boy the way sailors read water: by surface signs, by what moved underneath.

"No," Tam said again. His voice was rusty, small, pitched low like something recovered from deep water. He pushed back his chair, took his folded fleet from the sill, and brought it to the table. Dozens of paper boats. He began arranging them — not randomly. Mirabel saw it as his hands moved: rows. Manifest rows. Names inked tiny inside each hull, and Mirabel knew those names, knew every one, had written half of them in the ledger themselves.

"He's been folding the whole town," Wren whispered. "Every person on the list. He folds them so—" She stopped. Looked at her brother. "So they don't get lost? Is that it? So somebody's keeping track?"

Tam shook his head once. He picked up two boats — one marked with a small w, one with a t — and set them apart from the rest. Then he pointed at the window. At the sea beyond it, black and working under the wind. Then he pointed at the two boats, and then at himself, and then at Wren, and then he crossed his arms, and his chin came down in the exact stubborn set of his grandmother, whom Mirabel had loved and argued with for thirty years over the price of mackerel.

"He's saying no to being sent away," Wren translated, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, eleven years old and carrying weight no eleven-year-old should be issued. "He's saying no to different boats."

Tam nodded. Then he did one thing more. He took the two small boats, walked to the hearth, and set them on the mantel above the fire — beside the chipped cup that Mirabel had never taken down, Ede's cup, the one that had stood empty on this shelf for a year because Mirabel could not bear to fill it or remove it. Tam placed his paper fleet's smallest vessels next to Ede's cup, deliberately, like setting a place.

Then he came back and sat down, and looked at Mirabel, and waited.

* * *

The lamp turned overhead. Somewhere below, the tide worked at the rocks, patient, indifferent to schedules and tonnage and the difference between essential personnel and everyone else. Mirabel sat with their hands flat on the table because if they didn't give them a job they might have reached for these children and held on until the wood broke.

"Do you know what I promised myself," Mirabel said at last, quietly, "when I took you in? One night. I told myself: one night, dry beds, hot food, and in the morning their people will come. Nobody came, and I kept telling myself one more night, and somewhere in there I stopped telling myself anything, because telling yourself things is how you lie politely." They exhaled. "I've been lying politely for a month. Tonight I'm done."

"Good," said Wren, fierce and wet-eyed.

"Plainly, then. All of it." Mirabel turned the schedule around so the children could see it whole. "If you board those transports, I may not see you again until spring. If you board them separately, you may not see *each other*. If the storm comes early — and storms this season have been liars about their timing — the second ship may not sail at all. These are not my fears talking. These are the numbers. Ashworth's own numbers, and I've checked them twice, because checking is the one thing I'm good for."

"Then we don't board," Wren said simply. "We stay here. Three of us. You said one keeper may stay — fine, you're the keeper, we're your—"

"Your what?" Mirabel asked, gently, and watched the word die in her mouth. *Dependents. Minors. Entries.* The army had words for children and none of them fit.

"—family," Wren finished, glaring, daring anyone at the table to make it small. "We're your family. And families aren't cargo. There's no column for us."

"There isn't," Mirabel said. "That's the whole trouble. Every list ever made has a column for the useful and a column for the young and a column for the old, and nowhere — nowhere — a column for *belonging*. Ashworth can't weigh what you are to me. Neither can I. That's why the question won't sit still." They rubbed their eyes. "And here is the unbearable bottom of it, Wren, and I want you to hear it straight because you're owed straight: whatever I decide, I decide alone. The colonel takes my word. Your names rise or fall by my hand, my signature, my bargain or my refusal. Two children's whole lives, sitting in one keeper's pen. I didn't ask for it. I took the ledger, and the ledger took you."

Tam slid one of his paper boats across the table. It bumped against Miravel's knuckles and stopped. On its hull, in careful pencil: *M.V.* The keeper's own initials. Tam tapped it, then pointed at the lighthouse beam sweeping past the window, then folded his arms again.

Wren stared at it. "He's saying you fold yourself into a boat too. He's saying everyone should get one."

"He's saying," Mirabel said slowly, understanding arriving like cold water, "that nobody should be left off the manifest. Not even me." They picked the little boat up. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. "Oh, Tam. Child. Paper doesn't float in that sea. Paper sinks."

Tam looked at them with the patience of the deeply certain, and Mirabel could almost hear the answer he wasn't giving: *so does stone, if you build it right. So does iron. Somebody decided those things float, and built accordingly.*

Somebody decided. That was the whole of it, wasn't it. Lists, berths, bargains, chalk marks on doors — all of it somebody deciding, and calling the deciding arithmetic afterward so it wouldn't feel like choosing.

"Well," Mirabel said, and set the little boat down beside the salt cellar, where it rocked faintly with the stamp of surf below. "Then I suppose the only honest answer to the colonel's bargain is the one you gave, Tam. No. Not yet refused out loud — I'll refuse out loud tomorrow, and he'll be furious, and fury from that man is survivable. But refusing his bargain means your names go back on the open list, and the open list is weather. So between tonight and the sealing of that list, we find another way. Ollie's Kestrel. Marta's lots. Hollis's conscience, if it holds. Something. Some third column that isn't in anybody's book yet."

"A column for belonging," Wren said.

"If I have to write it in myself."

Tam, who had not spoken a second time and did not, reached across and took back his paper boat — the one with M.V. on the hull — and instead of returning it to the fleet, he pressed it into Mirabel's palm and closed Mirabel's fingers over it, both his small hands, deliberate as a christening.

Keep it. Keep me afloat. Keep us all.

Outside, the wind rose a note. Down in the harbor, invisible in the dark, the transports rode at anchor with their empty berths, forty souls short of the town that filled them, and somewhere in the customs house a sealed envelope waited for a signature that would make the arithmetic true.

Mirabel closed their fist around the paper boat and felt it crush slightly, and hold.

"Tomorrow," they said to the children, to the light, to the listening dark. "Tomorrow we make our own list."

But even as they said it, the lamp swept the window and showed them, far out past the point, a riding light where no boat should be — and Mirabel's stomach dropped, because they knew that anchorage, and it belonged to no fisherman, and dawn was a long way off.
