## 18. Ede's Coat

The coat had been hanging in the back of the sea chest for a year and three days.

Mirabel knew the number precisely because Mirabel knew everything precisely. It was the only kind of arithmetic they trusted anymore — days counted, wicks trimmed, gallons of oil logged in a hand so neat it looked printed. Precision was a wall you could live behind, if you built it high enough and kept the mortar wet.

They had gone into the chest that morning for something practical. The requisition notice had said the lighthouse stores would be inventoried by week's end, and Mirabel intended to have every bolt of canvas and spare burner accounted for before a red-coated clerk could find them wanting. So: down on their knees in the cold room beneath the tower, lifting out oilskins, storm shutters, the spare signal flags gone soft with mildew — and there at the bottom, folded the way Ede always folded things, corners tucked like a letter being sealed.

Ede's sea coat.

It was wool the color of wet slate, double-breasted, with brass buttons worn to a dull gold where Ede's hands had worried them for eleven years. There was a burn mark on the left cuff from the night the stove flared. There was salt in the seams that no amount of brushing would ever take out. Mirabel lifted it and it was heavier than wool had any right to be, the way certain things are heavy — not with weight but with everything that has happened inside them.

"You're in my way," Mirabel told it, which was absurd, because it was a coat, because coats do not stand in doorways or hum off-key or argue about whether the second wick needs trimming when the first will plainly do. "You are a coat," Mirabel said, more firmly, as though the coat might contest the point. It did not. That was the trouble with it. It never argued anymore.

Mirabel sat down on the chest lid with the coat across their knees, and the inventory could wait, and the clerk could go hang, and for a long while the only sound in the lighthouse was the sea saying the same thing over and over in its patient, terrible voice.

* * *

They found the letter the way you find things you have spent a year not looking for: immediately.

It was in the inside pocket, folded in quarters, addressed on the outside in Ede's looping hand — *For M., if I'm late.* Not *if I don't come back.* Ede had never once said *don't come back.* "I'll be late," Ede would say, shrugging into this very coat, "keep my supper honest." As though drowning were a delay, a bad road, a long queue at the customs house. As though death were merely poor punctuality, forgivable if you kept the plate warm.

Mirabel's thumb hovered over the fold. A year and three days, and they had not read it. They had told themselves this was restraint. Dignity. Grief borne upright, like a keeper bears weather. But sitting there in the cold storeroom with the coat warming against their knees, Mirabel understood suddenly and completely that they had not read it because reading it would make Ede dead in a way that ledgers and lamp oil could not fix. Everything else could be managed. A letter is not manageable. A letter talks.

They opened it anyway. The sea had been talking all year; one more voice hardly mattered.

*M.,*

*You'll be cross with me for writing this instead of saying it, but you know how I am with doors. If I try to say a true thing standing in front of you I end up telling you the gutters want clearing instead.*

*So here is the true thing.*

*I've been watching you keep the light these five years and I know what you think it is. You think it's a job. You think it's lists and oil and glass and the logbook, and you're very good at all of it, better than me, and you think that's the whole of it. But I've seen you climb the stairs at midnight when there's nothing wrong just to look at her turn, and that's not a job, love. That's a vow.*

*Keep the light, and it keeps you. Your grandmother said it and mine said it and we laughed about it our first winter here, remember? You said it sounded like something painted on a mug. Well. I've been out in boats half my life and I'll tell you what I've learned: every light on this coast is somebody's promise that the dark isn't empty. And every keeper who tends one is kept. Held. You can't hold a light up all night without it holding you back. That's not poetry, that's physics, or near enough.*

*So if I'm late — when I'm late — don't you dare hide in the work. Don't trim your grief like a wick, small and steady and safe. Burn big. Let the thing be what it is. And mind the second cup.*

*All my love, longer than the list of ships,*
*E.*

*P.S. The gutters do actually want clearing.*

Mirabel read it twice. On the second reading, somewhere around *don't trim your grief like a wick*, a sound came out of them that they did not authorize — something between a laugh and the noise a hull makes when it takes water — and then they were crying, properly, hugely, the way they had not let themselves cry at the funeral, at the inquest, at any of the forty-nine mornings since when they had stood at the rail and watched the sea and felt nothing they permitted themselves to name.

They cried for Ede, who had been right about everything including the gutters. They cried for the second cup, which they had caught themselves setting out last spring and quietly retired to the back of the cupboard like a scandal. They cried for Wren and Tam asleep upstairs in the keeper's bed, two children the sea had already robbed once, whom Mirabel had taken in "for one night" nine nights ago and had not yet said aloud — not to them, not to Marta Tull, not to God or the Admiralty — that they did not intend to give them back.

And underneath all of it, plain as chalk on a door, they cried because they finally saw the shape of what they'd been doing. The trimming. The smalling. The steady flame turned down low so it couldn't flare or gutter or fail. A year of keeping the light like a penance instead of a promise. Hiding in duty the way Ede had known they would, because Ede had known them better than anyone alive, and now better than anyone dead.

"I'm sorry," Mirabel said to the coat, wiping their face on their sleeve like a child. "I'm sorry, I'm doing it again even now, I'm sorry—"

The coat, being a coat, offered no absolution. But the storeroom felt less like a tomb than it had an hour ago, and that was something.

* * *

They had met in a squall, which Mirabel considered, in hindsight, entirely characteristic.

Mirabel was twenty-four and newly posted to Gannet Point, convinced that loneliness was simply the price of competence. Ede Marlowe was the youngest deckhand on the supply boat *Kittiwake*, sent ashore with the monthly crate and a message that the boat couldn't lie off in the weather. By the time the exchange was done the squall had closed the harbor mouth, and Ede stood dripping in the lighthouse doorway grinning like the weather was a personal gift.

"Looks like I'm yours till morning," Ede said. "I'll try to be quiet."

"You will do nothing of the sort," Mirabel said, because they had already learned this about Ede within four minutes. "You'll talk the whole night and touch every instrument I own."

"Your barometer's sticky," Ede agreed. "And your logbook hand's beautiful but you press too hard. You'll ruin pens."

They had married fourteen months later in the chapel at Gannet town, and half the fishing fleet attended, and Cobb Fenwick got spectacularly drunk and wept and declared them the two most stubborn people on the coast, which everyone present correctly identified as the highest compliment Cobb possessed. Their grandmother's saying had been stitched on a sampler that hung by the kitchen door — *KEEP THE LIGHT AND IT KEEPS YOU* — and Ede had pointed at it on their wedding night and said, "There. Vows done. Everything else is commentary."

Five years. Five years of shared watches and arguments about wicks and Ede teaching Tam's grandmother's boy to coil line on the gallery, years of the particular warmth of a house where someone is waiting to hear how your day went, even if your day was identical to the day before. Then Ede took the *Providence* run down the coast in November because the pay was good and the storm was, everyone agreed afterward, nobody's fault. The sea does not accept blame. It accepts only cargo.

Mirabel climbed the stairs that evening with the coat over their arm and hung it on the peg by the lamp room door, where it had hung for eleven years, where it belonged. Then they went down to the kitchen and opened the cupboard and took out the second cup — brown glazed, chipped at the lip, Ede's favorite — and set it on the table.

Then, after a moment, they set out two more cups beside it. Small ones. The tin ones with the painted gulls.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked, and small feet came padding down the spiral stair. Wren appeared in the kitchen doorway with her hair escaping its braid and the particular expression of a girl who has been listening at doors, which she had, and was not ashamed of it, which she also was not.

"You've been crying," Wren said. Not accusing. Observing. She had a keeper's eye already; Mirabel had noticed it and refused, until now, to be moved by it.

"Yes," Mirabel said. "I found Ede's coat."

Wren came and looked at the three cups on the table, and then at Mirabel, and did the arithmetic children are so mercilessly quick at. "Is that Ede's? The one the sea took?"

"The same."

Wren pulled out a chair and sat down uninvited, the way family does. "Tam folds boats for people the sea took," she said. "He doesn't say names. But I counted. There's one he made twice and folded smaller than the rest. I think it's Gran. And now there'll be one for Ede." She paused. "If you tell him the name. He won't ask. He never asks. But he'd want it right."

Mirabel looked at this child — nine years old, orphaned by flood, conscripted by circumstance into a lighthouse full of another family's grief — and felt the last of the wall come down with a sound only they could hear.

"Ede," Mirabel said. "Ede Marlowe. Spelled like the bird, E-d-e. They'd have liked being a paper boat. They liked anything that floated."

"Everybody here likes things that float," Wren said sensibly. "It's a coastal town."

Tam appeared behind his sister, silent as fog, clutching his paper and his little bone folder of a fingernail. He looked at the second cup on the table. He looked at Mirabel's face, still blotchy. He sat down, took his paper, and began to fold — slow, precise creases, a craftsman's care — and when he finished he held the small white boat out across the table without a word.

Mirabel took it. Turned it. And there, folded inside the hull where no one would see unless they looked, Tam had written the name himself, laboriously, in pencil: *EDE.*

"He asked me how to spell it," Wren whispered. "Two days ago. He's been waiting for you to be ready."

Mirabel held the boat the way you hold something breakable, which is to say the way you hold everything that matters. "We'll float it together," they said. "First calm tide. All of them. The sea gives back what it takes, if you ask it properly."

Tam nodded once, gravely, and for the first time in nine days he smiled — small, crooked, gone in a moment, but real. Wren put her hand briefly on her brother's shoulder, and Mirabel thought: *this is what I was keeping the light for all along, I simply didn't know the names yet.*

Then the knock came at the door.

Three knocks, spaced like a drumbeat, and through the kitchen window Mirabel saw the lantern-light of a soldier on the path, and behind him the customs house blazing yellow against the early dark, and their stomach dropped with the certainty of a sounding lead.

Wren tensed. "They've never come up here at night."

"No," Mirabel said. They set the paper boat carefully beside Ede's cup, where it rocked faintly in the draft like something alive. "Stay with Tam."

They opened the door onto wind and lamplight. The soldier was young, apologetic, rain streaming off his cap brim. "Keeper Vane? Beg pardon, sir — ma'am — the Colonel's compliments. He requires you at the customs house tonight, first bell. Says there's a matter of tonnage." He hesitated, visibly wishing to be anywhere else on earth. "Says it can't wait for morning. Says—" the young man swallowed— "says the numbers have changed."

Behind Mirabel, in the warm kitchen, the paper boat named Ede sat between three cups, and out beyond the point the sea went on saying the same thing over and over, patient and terrible, counting down.
