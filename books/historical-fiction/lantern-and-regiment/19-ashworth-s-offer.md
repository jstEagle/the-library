## 19. Ashworth's Offer

The summons came at dusk, folded into the hands of a private so young his chin had not yet decided whether it wanted a beard. He stood on the lighthouse step dripping rain and would not come in, though the wind was flinging spray clear over the gallery rail and down upon them both.

"The colonel requests the keeper's presence. Customs house. Eight o'clock."

"Requests," Mirabel said.

The private looked at Wren, who was watching from the doorway with her arms crossed in a way she had learned — Mirabel realized with a small shock — from watching Marta Tull argue with the fishmonger. "It's an order dressed up polite, miss," he said, to Wren rather than to Mirabel, and then seemed alarmed at his own honesty, touched his cap twice as though one touch hadn't done the job, and splashed away down the path.

Wren shut the door. Tam was sitting by the stove folding paper — he was always folding paper now, boats of every size, some no bigger than a thumbnail, lined up on the sill like a fleet waiting for weather. He didn't look up.

"You don't have to go," Wren said.

"I do, though."

"He can't make you."

"No," Mirabel agreed, pulling Ede's old coat from its hook — not the sea coat, the good wool one, the one for town — "but he can make everything else worse while I stand here being brave about it. There's a difference between courage and stubbornness, and I've been told the difference is mostly how tired you are."

"That doesn't sound true."

"It isn't. It's something my mother said when she didn't want to admit she was scared." Mirabel found their hat. "Watch the lamp. Trim at nine, quarter-turn on the wick, and if the glass fogs, breathe on it and wipe *with* the curve, never across. You know all this better than I do by now."

"Tam will be here."

"Tam will be here," Mirabel said, and felt the truth of it settle somewhere under the ribs, warm and heavy as a stone.

* * *

The customs house had once been the finest building in Gannet Point, back when Gannet Point had fancied itself a port. Now its pilasters were salt-eaten and its floorboards bowed like the deck of an old ship, and the army had filled it with trestle tables and oil lamps and the smell of wet canvas. Soldiers worked at the tables copying lists, and their pens made the only sound, a dry whispering like sand running out.

Colonel Ashworth stood at the head table with the ruined ledger open before him. The flood-damaged original — the regiment's copy, salvaged from the cellar after the squall — lay spread in sections, its ink bloomed to gray ghosts, half the pages fused into pulp that smelled of rot and brine. Beside it sat Mirabel's reconstruction, bound in sailcloth, every name legible, every household accounted for.

"Keeper Vane." Ashworth did not look up. "You've been writing marginalia again."

Mirabel came to the table. In the margin beside *Pike, Josiah, cordwainer, wife, three children*, in Mirabel's small neat hand: *mends boots for half the harbor; youngest afraid of gulls.* Beside *Tull, Marta, baker*: *widow; feeds the schoolchildren who cannot pay; do not let her pride stop you.*

"It's not marginalia," Mirabel said. "It's the record. A list of names is just arithmetic wearing a coat. The margins are what makes it people."

Ashworth finally raised his eyes. He was a man built like a surveyor's instrument — straight lines, right angles, everything load-bearing — and his eyes were pale as winter water. But there was something tonight behind them that had not been there before. Mirabel had spent a year reading nothing but weather and water, and they recognized the look. It was the look of a man who had checked his figures twice and found them correct and hated the answer.

"Sit down, Keeper."

Mirabel sat. One of Ashworth's officers brought tea, actual tea, army tea, strong enough to trot a horse across, and left without a word.

"Do you know why I requisitioned this coast?" Ashworth asked.

"To evacuate it."

"To evacuate it *in order*." He laid one finger on the damaged ledger. "Chaos kills more people than water does. I have seen a landing stage in panic. It is not a scene I will describe to you over tea. Order is the only mercy large enough to cover a whole population at once, and order requires accurate figures." He turned a page of the drowned book. Names surfaced and sank in the stain. "One transport ship, *Providence*, eleven hundred tons. Two hundred and sixty berths fit to carry souls. The Kestrel situation notwithstanding—" a flicker, there and gone, of something that might have been irony or might have been threat "—the mathematics do not care what we wish."

"And yet here you are wishing at me."

Something moved at the corner of Ashworth's mouth. It might, in a younger man, have become a smile. "Here I am."

He drew a chart from beneath the ledgers and unrolled it, weighting the corners with an inkwell, a pistol, a biscuit tin, and Mirabel's own teacup. The reef ran along the eastern approaches like a signature scrawled by a drunkard. The Kestrel's channel — Brine Ollie's channel, the smugglers' passage that Mirabel and Hollis and old Ollie had sounded by night two weeks past, the passage that existed in no naval chart and no official record — was marked in fresh pencil, tentative as a confession.

"I am given to understand," Ashworth said, "that certain soundings were taken recently. At night. By persons whose names appear nowhere in any report I have received."

Mirabel drank their tea and said nothing, which between a lighthouse keeper and a colonel is a full paragraph.

"The great storm comes within days. Every barometer on this coast agrees, and barometers are the one thing on this coast that has never lied to me. When it arrives, the *Providence* cannot hold station off an open roadstead. She must work inside, through the reef, or she cannot work at all — and if she cannot work, the berths she carries are worth exactly nothing, and the people they were meant for drown on a beach with their papers in perfect order." He tapped the penciled channel. "I need this passage surveyed properly, buoyed, ranged, and proven. I need someone who knows this water the way your log knows weather. And I need it before the storm."

"So ask Ollie."

"Ollie is seventy years old, drinks his ballast, and cannot see ten feet in fog. Hollis has the nerve but not the knowledge. The pilots at the harbor are loyal to the harbor, and I do not trust loyalty under pressure; I trust competence." Ashworth sat back. "I trust you, Keeper. That should alarm us both."

Outside, the rain thickened against the windows. Somewhere a soldier dropped a stack of forms and swore softly, and another laughed, briefly, and was silenced.

"What's your price?" Mirabel said.

"My offer." Ashworth corrected the word the way he corrected everything, precisely, without apology. "Your cooperation in surveying and ranging the reef channel, under my direction, beginning tomorrow night. Your continued custody of the light, which I am formally designating the primary range marker for the evacuation approach. And in exchange—"

He turned Mirabel's own ledger around and opened it to a page near the end. *Vane, M., keeper of the light.* Below it, in the space where dependents were listed, two names in Mirabel's hand: *Wren, age 11. Tam, age 7.*

"—the children receive guaranteed berths. First-class priority. Not subject to revision, not subject to lots, not subject to Cobb Fenwick or any other man's argument. Paper signed by me, countersigned by the Admiralty agent aboard. Whatever else happens in this town, whatever chaos the final week brings, those two names travel. Guaranteed."

The lamps whispered. Mirabel looked at the two names and felt the terrible warmth of them, the way a drowning man feels warmth and knows it may be the fire and not the sun.

"And yourself?" Mirabel said quietly.

"Irrelevant."

"You're on the list. I wrote you on the list."

"You wrote me on the list because you write everyone on the list. It's a compulsion with you. I find it useful and slightly mad." Ashworth closed the ledger gently, the way he handled nothing else. "The keeper of a range light stays with the light during operations. That is not sentiment; it is doctrine. You stay. They go."

"They go together?"

There it was. The pause. Small — half a breath — but Mirabel had spent a year listening for the things the sea said between waves, and heard it plain.

"The berths are on different vessels," Ashworth said. "The *Providence* takes the priority manifest. The second transport, *Resolute*, follows when she's loaded. Berth allocations are assigned by tonnage and timing, not by preference. The girl goes first sailing. The boy follows on the second."

"They're seven and eleven."

"They are seven and eleven, and alive, which is a condition this coast will not guarantee them past Thursday." Ashworth's voice stayed level, but it lost its polish, the way ice loses its shine when it begins to move. "You want to know what I think, Keeper? I think you believe love is a mooring line. Tie two things together and neither drifts. I have buried a wife and two daughters, and I will tell you what I learned at their graves, which is the only inheritance they left me: when the water rises, the mooring lines are the first thing to snap, and the snapping kills the ones who held on. Separate them. Get them out. Let them hate the arrangement from safe harbors on opposite sides of the channel, and let them grow up to curse my name, and let them *live* to do it."

The words sat in the room like cold iron. For a moment the colonel was not an instrument at all but a man holding an instrument, and his hands were shaking very slightly, and he despised them for it.

Mirabel thought of Tam's paper fleet on the windowsill. Forty-odd boats now, one for every name, each with a name written inside in pencil, because Tam had said — had finally said, one word, *stay*, and then more words, haltingly, like a man walking on a broken leg — that he meant to float them all so the sea would give them back. Every name. Not the useful ones. Not the young ones. All of them.

And Wren, who had stolen a berth list once already and would steal it again, who organized the landing of her own imagination nightly at the kitchen table with saltcellars for ships.

Separate them.

"Guaranteed berths," Mirabel said slowly, "on different ships. And if I refuse?"

"Then the berths fall back into the general allocation. Lots, merit arguments, Fenwick's lawyers if he finds a lawyer, the whole ugly machinery." Ashworth began rolling up the chart. "I am not threatening you, Keeper. I am describing Tuesday."

Mirabel stood. Their legs felt like someone else's legs, borrowed and badly fitted. "I'll give you your answer at the light. Tomorrow. I keep a lamp burning over a reef, Colonel; I don't make decisions in the dark, even when the dark is offering me exactly what I want."

Ashworth studied them for a long moment. Then he took a sheet of paper, wrote two lines, signed it, and slid it across: *Berths reserved, Vane, W. (11) & Vane, T. (7), per arrangement pending keeper's confirmation.* He blotted it, folded it once, and held it out.

"A reservation," he said. "Not a bargain. The bargain waits for your answer. I am told—" and there, unmistakably, was the ghost of humor, dry as August "—that I am capable of learning manners. From a lighthouse keeper. God help the Army."

Mirabel took the paper. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

At the door, Ashworth spoke again, quieter. "For what it's worth — and I know what it is worth, which is little — the margins in your ledger. The girl afraid of gulls. The baker who feeds children free. I read them all last night. Twice." He looked at the drowned ledger on his table as though it were a grave. "My daughters would have liked your handwriting, I think. My wife's slanted the same way. Leftward. As if always reaching for someone just out of frame."

Mirabel said nothing. There was nothing in any language that fit the moment, and they were too tired to invent one.

The rain took them home.

* * *

The lamp was burning steady when Mirabel climbed the tower, and Wren was at the lens with the polishing cloth, breathing fog onto the glass and wiping *with* the curve, precise as a surgeon. Tam had fallen asleep on the settee below, one hand still curled around a half-folded boat, and the unfinished fleet stood guard along the sill.

"Well?" Wren said, without turning. Eleven years old, and she asked it the way Marta asked about flour prices — flat, prepared for bad news, unwilling to be managed.

Mirabel sat down on the gallery steps and told her all of it. The guarantee. The two ships. The separation. Wren listened with her whole body gone still, which was how she listened to anything that mattered, and when it was finished she polished one more slow arc of glass before she spoke.

"He'd split us up."

"Yes."

"Because it's safer."

"Because it's safer."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a harbor master weighing a dangerous cargo. "Tam floats his boats so the sea gives them back," she said at last. "All of them. He counts them every morning. If one's missing he makes another with the same name so the number stays right." She set down the cloth. "If we go on different ships, one of us gets given back and the other doesn't. He'll count. He'll know."

Mirabel looked at her and felt something crack — not painfully, not entirely; more the way river ice cracks in spring, groaning before it gives.

"And you?" Mirabel said. "What do you want?"

"I want everyone on the boats." Wren said it simply, without drama, the way you'd state the tide table. "That's the wrong answer, isn't it? Marta says I'm like you. Stubborn in the direction of other people."

Below them, in his sleep, Tam murmured something and turned over, and one of the paper boats trembled in the draft from the lamp room door.

Mirabel unfolded Ashworth's paper and read the two names again, the guarantee that was also a knife, the mercy that came pre-broken. Tomorrow night the survey of the reef began, and with it the answering of the question. But standing there in the warm circle of the light, with the beam sweeping its slow patient circle over black water, Mirabel understood suddenly — with the clarity the sea grants only to the exhausted — that the question was not *will you take the bargain.*

The question was whether a bargain that saved the children by breaking them was a rescue at all.

Down in the town, a single window in the customs house still burned. Ashworth, awake. Somewhere between here and there, Brine Ollie was drinking his ballast and keeping his channel secret. And out past the reef, far off in the dark, the first long swell of the great storm was already gathering itself, patient as arithmetic, on its way to Gannet Point.

The light turned. The light turned again. And Mirabel reached for the log, and wrote: *Wind rising from the southeast. Barometer falling fast. Decision owed.*
