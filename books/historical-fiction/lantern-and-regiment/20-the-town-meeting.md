## 20. The Town Meeting

The chapel had never been built for this many people and it made no pretense otherwise. It had been built, some seventy years before, for a congregation of perhaps sixty souls who could be relied upon to sit still and think about heaven. On the evening the town gathered to argue over the berth list, there were better than two hundred packed between its walls, standing along the aisles, crowding the doorway, spilling out the windows onto the sills like bread rising past the lip of a tin.

Mirabel arrived early — earlier than anyone but the sexton, who was setting candles with the grim efficiency of a man preparing a church for a funeral that hadn't decided yet whose it would be. Mirabel took a seat at the back, on the end of the last pew, where the draft came through the door and where, Mirabel had calculated, it would be easiest to leave without being noticed.

That calculation told Mirabel something about themselves, though they declined to examine it just then.

Wren and Tam had wanted to come. Wren especially, chin up, insisting she had as much right as anyone since her name was on the list. But Mirabel had said no, and had heard how their own voice sounded saying it — not like a keeper's voice at all, but like Ashworth's — and so the children were home at the light with strict instructions about the lamp and a promise from Tam, given with his eyes rather than his mouth, that they would be obeyed.

The pews filled around Mirabel the way water fills a hold: steadily, then all at once. Marta Tull came in smelling of flour and woodsmoke, her hands still dusted white despite the hour, and squeezed Mirabel's shoulder in passing without a word. Old Brine Ollie sat near the front, which surprised no one; smugglers, Mirabel reflected, always liked to sit where they could see the exits. Cobb Fenwick took his place beside his wife and daughters with his arms folded, already looking like a man who had come not to listen but to be disagreed with, and to enjoy it.

At half past six, Sergeant Hollis entered alone, red coat brushed, boots loud on the stone floor, carrying the ledger under his arm the way one carries a sleeping infant or an unexploded shell. He set it on the communion table and stood beside it, and did not sit down, and did not speak, and managed by doing nothing at all to make the whole room feel as though the army were present even though only one man was.

Then the Reverend Mr. Quill climbed into the pulpit, looked out at the sea of faces, and said: "We are gathered to discuss the list."

Which was, Mirabel thought, rather like saying *we are gathered to discuss the weather* during the middle of a hurricane.

* * *

Mr. Quill read the terms aloud first, because someone had to, and because the terms were easier to bear when spoken in a church voice, flat and liturgical, as if they had been handed down rather than written up.

One ship. The *Providence*, out of Harwich, requisitioned and refitted, capable of carrying one hundred and eighty souls in safety, two hundred if one did not much care about safety. Gannet Point and the villages down the coast held, by the regiment's count, three hundred and eleven persons fit to travel. The storm — *the next great storm*, the colonel's phrase, repeated now until it sounded like scripture — was expected within the week. There would be no second sailing.

"The list," Mr. Quill concluded, "has been drawn up according to the priorities established by Colonel Ashworth. Skilled trades essential to settlement. Children under twelve. Persons of demonstrated usefulness." He paused. "I am instructed to say that appeals may be submitted in writing."

A woman in the third row — Widow Prue, whose husband had drowned the same winter Ede had, and who had never once in nine years let the sea hear the end of it — stood up slowly, leaning on the pew rail.

"Appeals," she said. "To whom?"

"To the colonel," said Mr. Quill.

"Written," said Widow Prue, "in what ink? I've seen the list, Mr. Quill. My name's not on it. Neither is my sister's. Neither is old Josiah Blount's, and that man built every roof in this town, including the roof of your chapel, if you'll look up and notice." A few heads did look up. "Whose handwriting decides whether I see spring? Because it isn't mine."

"It is the colonel's arithmetic," said Mr. Quill gently, "and we are not here to dispute arithmetic—"

"No," said Cobb Fenwick, standing, and the room shifted toward him the way a crowd shifts toward a fight. "We're here to fix it."

Cobb was a big man gone soft in the middle but not in the voice, and he had spent thirty years as a fisherman arguing with the sea and winning often enough to believe he could win anything. He planted himself in the aisle.

"I'll tell you what's wrong with that list," he said. "It's backwards. It puts the young first and the able second and everybody else nowhere. Now I love my girls" — a gesture at the pew, where three Fenwick daughters sat with identical expressions of mortification — "but my girls can't haul a net. My girls can't build a wall on the mainland when we get there. You know who can? I can. Tam Ossler's father could. Half the men in this room can. If we get one hundred and eighty seats, then by God those seats go to the people who can make something of them, not to infants who'll need carrying the whole way."

"Cobb," said Marta Tull, from two rows back, in the voice she used for telling customers the bread wasn't ready yet, "you are proposing to put your own name above your daughters'."

"I'm proposing sense," said Cobb. "You want sentiment, join the choir."

There was laughter at that — short, unhappy laughter, the kind that lives in rooms like this one — and then a fisherman named Aldous Grebe stood and said his mother was seventy-eight and had buried four children and he would carry her to the boat on his back if it came to that, and Cobb said that was exactly the problem, that everyone in this room would carry somebody, and the arithmetic didn't care who carried whom, and Aldous said something unprintable about Cobb's arithmetic, and for a moment it seemed the meeting might become something other than a meeting.

Mr. Quill banged the pulpit with his hand. It was not a gavel and did not sound like one, but it served.

* * *

They argued for two hours.

They argued about age — the young first, because they had the most years to lose, versus the old first, because the young could survive another storm and the old could not. They argued about trade — the smith against the schoolmistress, the midwife against the mason, each profession championed by the people who practiced it or loved someone who did. They argued about family — whether berths should be kept whole, households together, or broken apart and scattered across the manifest like seed. And underneath every argument, unspoken but audible, ran the other argument, the true one, which was simply: *not me*. Not my name. Please. Not mine.

Mirabel sat at the back and listened and said nothing.

It was not that Mirabel had nothing to say. That was precisely the trouble. Mirabel had more to say than anyone in the room, because Mirabel knew every name on that list and every name off it, knew which households had flour and which had none, knew that Josiah Blount's hands shook too badly now to build so much as a birdhouse, knew that Widow Prue's sister was blind in one eye and stubborn in both. Mirabel knew, better than the colonel ever could, exactly what each life weighed.

And that knowledge sat in Mirabel's chest like ballast, because Mirabel also knew that the list had been reconstructed in their own hand. Every name. Their pen. Their ink. When Widow Prue demanded to know whose handwriting decided whether she saw spring, the answer, God forgive them, was sitting in the last pew pretending to be furniture.

Sergeant Hollis stood near the table the whole time, and once — only once — his eyes found Mirabel's across the crowded chapel, and there was something in them that might have been a question. Mirabel looked away first.

* * *

It was Marta Tull who broke the deadlock, and she broke it the way she broke everything: plainly, with flour still on her hands.

She waited until the age argument had circled itself hoarse and the trade argument had begun repeating itself, and then she rose, smoothed her apron, and said, "May I?" in a tone that did not require permission.

"Marta," said Cobb warily, "if this is about the bread—"

"It is never about the bread, Cobb, the bread is fine." A ripple of real laughter this time, grateful for any port. Marta turned to face the room. "Here's what I see. We have been at each other's throats for two hours and nobody has moved an inch, because every one of us is arguing for the people we love, and you cannot beat a person who is arguing for the people they love. There's no winning it. So stop trying."

She held up her hands, palms out, flour drifting faintly in the candlelight.

"Lots," she said. "Draw names. Every soul in this town, in the hat, one hundred and eighty slips pulled fair, in front of witnesses, children and grandmothers and fishermen all equal. No trades. No ages. No lists made by soldiers or—" she paused, and Mirabel felt the pause like a hand on the back of the neck, "—or anyone else. Let chance do what we cannot. Because chance doesn't love anybody, and that's the only fairness left to us."

The chapel went quiet in the particular way a room goes quiet when an idea lands that everyone has secretly been waiting for.

"That's madness," said Cobb immediately, but softer than before, testing the ice. "You'd send a doctor's place to a draw? You'd trust a child's life to luck?"

"I'd trust it to luck before I'd trust it to a list," said Marta. "Your list, my list, the colonel's list — every list ever written has a hand behind it, Cobb, and every hand has a reason, and every reason is somebody's grandmother going in the water. Luck has no reasons. Luck is the only honest man in this room."

"She's right," said Aldous Grebe, standing. "Lots."

"My mother," said Aldous's neighbor, "would never agree—"

"Ask her," said Marta.

And then it spread, the way things spread in small towns, which is quickly and unevenly and with everyone talking over everyone else. Lots. Lots, said the widow Prue. Lots, said the young Tull boys. Even some of Cobb's own faction began nodding, and Cobb stood in the aisle watching his position erode like a dune in weather, and finally he threw up his hands and said, "Fine. Fine! Draw your lots. But when the boat lands and nobody on it knows which end of a hammer holds the head, don't come crying to me," and sat down to a scattering of applause that was mostly relief.

Mr. Quill turned to Hollis. "Sergeant. Would the colonel entertain such a proposal?"

Hollis looked at the ledger on the table. He looked at it for a long moment — long enough that Mirabel, watching him, thought: *he wants to say yes.* Then he straightened.

"I'll convey it," he said. "I make no promises. The colonel's instructions are specific."

"His instructions," said Widow Prue clearly, "drown people."

No one contradicted her. Mr. Quill closed the matter with a prayer, which was brief, and a benediction, which was briefer, and the chapel emptied itself into the cold dark street by twos and threes, voices low, arguments continuing in murmurs all the way down the lane.

* * *

Mirabel was among the last out, having learned long ago that leaving early drew attention and lingering drew none. The sexton was snuffing candles. Hollis was gathering the ledger, and as Mirabel passed the communion table, the sergeant spoke without looking up.

"They'll ask you," he said quietly. "About the lots. Whether the record supports them. Your hand's the only one the colonel trusts with names."

"I know."

"Do you?" Hollis did look up then, and his face in the candle-end light was younger than his rank. "Because I watched you sit through two hours of that and never open your mouth, Keeper Vane, and I couldn't tell if it was wisdom or something else."

Mirabel stood with a hand on the pew rail and considered several answers. *I know every one of them by heart. I could stand up and tell this town the truth of every name. I could have said any of it and said none.*

"You couldn't tell," Mirabel said at last, "because neither could I."

Hollis nodded slowly, as if this were an answer, which perhaps it was.

Outside, the wind had come around to the northeast, and it smelled of deep water and coming trouble. Down the hill, the harbor lights bobbed. Above them all, steady as a heartbeat, the Gannet Point beam swept its slow circle, keeping its word to nobody in particular.

Mirabel walked home with their collar up and their shame riding close as a coat, and it was not until the lighthouse door opened and Wren's face appeared, anxious, searching, that the full weight of the evening settled.

"Well?" Wren asked. "What happened? Are there lots? Is it decided?"

"There will be lots proposed to the colonel," Mirabel said, hanging up their coat. "Nothing is decided."

"But your name's on the list," Wren said, and it wasn't an accusation, it was worse, it was simple fear. "Yours and ours. Isn't it?"

Mirabel looked at the child, at Tam curled in the chair with a paper boat folded flat in his fist, waiting, both of them waiting, and understood that the silence they had kept in the chapel tonight had not been neutral after all. Silence had a hand behind it too. Silence was somebody's grandmother going in the water.

"Yes," Mirabel said. "It's on the list. And tomorrow I'm going to do something about it."

They did not yet know what. But three days' hard sailing to the northeast, the barometer at the Harwich naval yard had already begun its long, deliberate fall.
