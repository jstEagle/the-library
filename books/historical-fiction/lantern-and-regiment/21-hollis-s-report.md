## 21. Hollis's Report

Sergeant Elias Hollis had written four hundred and eleven reports in his eleven years of service, and he could not remember a single one of them.

That was the trick of army paperwork, he'd long ago decided. It was designed to be forgotten. You wrote the weather in code, the casualties in columns, the disposition of forces in language so dry it could be stored next to the gunpowder without risk. A good report was a report that no one ever read twice. A perfect report was one that no one read at all.

He sat now at the customs house desk — his desk for the duration of the requisition, though it had been old Mr. Pellow's desk for thirty years before that, and the wood still smelled faintly of tar and licorice — with a blank sheet before him and a candle burning down to its last inch. Through the shutters he could hear the wind worrying at Gannet Point like a dog with a bone, and beneath that, farther off, the deep groan of the sea working itself up to something.

Three days. That was what the barometer said, if you knew how to beg it for the truth. Three days, perhaps four, until the great storm came down out of the northwest like a debt collector.

And Colonel Ashworth believed they had six.

Hollis dipped his pen. Stopped. Dipped it again.

The trouble was this: the colonel's schedule was built on the meteorological observations of Lieutenant Prout, who had taken his readings from the harbor station, which stood forty feet above the waterline behind a hill, and who had never in his life been on open water during winter. Prout read instruments. Instruments were polite. The sea, Hollis had learned in twelve years aboard troop transports and coastal packets, was not polite. It was a creditor, and it kept its own books.

Hollis had grown up in a fishing town himself — a smaller one, up the coast, mostly gone now to sandbars and memory — and he knew the signs the way Prout never would. The gulls had gone inland two days past, all at once, like a congregation called by a bell only they could hear. The tide had come up the harbor steps three stairs higher than its marks. Old Brine Ollie had been seen standing on the seawall at dawn, looking north, spitting into the wind, which Hollis understood from the fishermen at the quay was roughly equivalent to a general issuing orders for retreat.

The town knew. The town always knew. And the town was watching the customs house windows and counting the days against them, and every hour Ashworth spent reorganizing his loading parties was an hour the sea took off the other side of the ledger.

So: the report.

Officially, Hollis was to file tonight's dispatch with the district command at Marlowe Head, confirming readiness and requesting final sailing orders for the evacuation ship *Providence* on the colonel's timetable — six days hence. It was a simple report. He had drafted such reports in his head already, easy as reciting a psalm.

Unofficially, Hollis was about to commit a crime for which the army kept a wall and a row of posts, and he found, examining himself honestly as he tried to, that his hands were almost steady.

Almost. His right hand had developed a small tremor somewhere around supper, and he chose to believe it was the cold.

* * *

He began the way all lies begin, with truth.

*Report of Sergeant E. Hollis, 4th Company, on requisition operations at Gannet Point, this 14th day of—*

He paused at the date. Then he wrote the wrong date. Not a wild one — nothing that would leap from the page — but three days forward, dated as though the dispatch had been delayed in writing, held up by weather, by the press of duties, by the thousand small frictions of an understaffed operation. A delayed report was unremarkable. Every quartermaster between here and the capital filed late reports. It was practically a regimental tradition.

*Conditions at Gannet Point continue to deteriorate,* he wrote. *The outer road is now impassable to wheeled traffic owing to flooding between the mill and the salt meadows. Resupply by cart will not be possible after tomorrow. All movement of stores must be completed by boat or not at all.*

That much was true. He had watched the water take the road that very afternoon, quietly, without drama, the way water does things — one moment cobbles, the next a brown mirror with fence posts growing out of it.

*Further: the harbor pilotage is degrading. The channel markers have shifted in the current; the eastern buoy dragged its mooring sometime in the night and now sits half a cable west of its charted position. I have ordered it reset, but the ground swell makes the work slow and dangerous. In my assessment, safe embarkation of the transport will require a minimum of three additional days beyond the date presently scheduled, and even then only in fair conditions.*

There it was. The lie, sitting in the middle of the paragraph like a stone in bread. The buoy *had* dragged — he'd seen it himself that morning, and it was true that resetting it would be slow. But it did not require three days. It required an afternoon and two strong men and a vocabulary that would make a chaplain weep.

Three days. He wrote the number and felt it settle over the town like a blanket over someone sleeping.

Because that was the arithmetic of it, the terrible plain arithmetic he couldn't stop doing in his head: six days meant the *Providence* sailed with whatever the priority list allowed, in good order, on schedule, and left the rest of the town standing on the quay to watch her go. Six days meant Cobb Fenwick's family got their berths and Marta Tull's neighbors did not, and the whole rotten business proceeded exactly as the colonel had drawn it up, tidy as a parade ground.

But three days — three days changed everything. Three days meant the storm arrived while the ships were still loading. And when the storm arrived, schedules died. Schedules were paper, and paper did not survive weather. When the great storm came down on Gannet Point with both vessels still at anchor and the whole town crowding the harbor, there would be no time for lists, no time for tonnage calculations, no time for the colonel's beautiful priorities. There would only be room. Fill the boats or drown. Every soul the hulls could hold would go, because no officer on God's green earth would stand on a dock in a hurricane and tell a mother she hadn't been marked with sufficient chalk.

The storm, Hollis reflected, was the most honest officer in the district.

He blew out a breath and kept writing, because a lie this size needed scaffolding.

*I would further note that the civilian population has grown restless regarding the berth allocations. Several households have removed themselves from the register rather than accept partial allocation. This has created confusion in the manifest. I recommend a full recount of the household returns before any final list is certified, which I estimate will require no less than seventy-two hours.*

That part wasn't even entirely false. Wren Vane — no, not Vane, the girl was a Quill or a Tarrow or something, he'd have to check the ledger, and the fact that he thought of her as *Wren Vane*, as though she belonged to the lighthouse, told him something about himself he decided not to examine just yet — anyway, the girl had been seen near the customs house after dark more than once, and half the chalk numbers on the doors had been rubbed out and rewritten by hands unknown, usually smaller ones. The register was a mess. Any competent clerk would demand a recount.

He was, Hollis realized with a strange lurch, committing treason in the style of a man asking permission. *Begging your pardon, sir, but might we delay the drowning of several dozen civilians so that the paperwork may be brought up to standard?* It was the most sergeant thing he had ever done, and he had once spent an entire afternoon convincing a corporal that mucking out the officers' latrines built character.

*In summary:* he wrote, *operations at Gannet Point are proceeding, but the timeline presented in previous correspondence does not reflect conditions on the ground. I take full responsibility for the delay in reporting these facts. Recommend revised sailing orders accordingly.*

*I take full responsibility.* Four words. If it unraveled — if Prout checked the barometer log against the dates, if the buoy crew talked, if the colonel rode out to the mill road and found the flood had conveniently dried up overnight — those four words were what stood between Hollis and the firing squad, and they weighed about as much as a moth. But they were his, and he signed beneath them, *E. Hollis, Sergeant,* in his careful clerk's hand, and set the pen down.

The candle had burned to a puddle. Outside, the wind had found a loose shutter somewhere along the front and was playing it like a bad fiddle.

He sealed the report, addressed it to Marlowe Head, and set it under the inkwell where the morning rider would find it.

Then he sat back in Mr. Pellow's licorice-smelling chair and did the other thing he'd been putting off, which was thinking.

* * *

Here was what nobody warned you about treachery: it didn't feel like anything. He'd expected — well, he didn't know what. Trumpets, maybe. Or at least a sensation of falling. Instead there was just the desk, and the cold, and the fact that tomorrow he would get up and polish buttons and call the roll and salute a colonel whose orders he was quietly sawing through at the joints, and none of it would show on his face, because sergeants' faces are government property and don't belong to them.

His father had been a fisherman. Had been lost, in fact, in the same kind of storm that was coming, the year Hollis was nine — gone with eleven other men off a boat called the *Kindly Fortune*, which was the kind of name fishermen gave boats the way soldiers gave guns girls' names, out of hope rather than sense. Hollis remembered the aftermath more than the loss: the way the village women had stood in the rain counting men off the returning boats, over and over, as though the count might improve with repetition. It never did. Forty-one souls in that fleet. Twenty-nine came home.

He had been counting people ever since. It was why he was good at manifests. It was also, he suspected, why the chalked numbers on the cottage doors made him feel like a man walking through a graveyard reading the stones aloud.

And it was why, when he'd watched the Alder family erase their own mark on the door of Number 9 and rewrite it smaller — giving their berths away, quietly, in chalk, to the widow Pellow next door — something had cracked in him that four hundred and eleven reports had never touched.

The army had taught him obedience. His father's sea had taught him arithmetic. Somewhere between the two, Elias Hollis had apparently become the sort of man who falsified dispatches, and the remarkable thing was how little argument he'd put up.

He banked the fire, checked the sentry rotation, and stepped out into the street.

The town was dark except for the light. Of course. The light was always burning — that great slow wheel of brilliance turning above the point, sweeping the water, sweeping the roofs, sweeping the customs house, again and again, patient as a lighthouse keeper's heart. Hollis had heard the townsfolk talk about Mirabel Vane the way sailors talk about weather: with respect, familiarity, and a certain fatalism. *The keeper won't leave. The keeper never leaves.*

He looked up at it a long moment. Somewhere under that beam, he happened to know, there was a woman who kept a second ledger inside the official one — a ledger with annotations in the margins, little human notes the army would have struck through in red ink. *Marta Tull, bakes for the whole street, hands hurt in cold. Tam, seven, hasn't spoken since the flood. Wren, twelve going on forty, watches everything.* Hollis had seen enough pages of it to understand that Mirabel Vane was fighting the same war he was, from a different trench, with different weapons.

Maybe that was why he did it. Or maybe it was the shutter fiddling in the wind, and the smell of salt, and being nine years old on a dock counting boats.

Reasons were for confessions. He had a report to pretend he hadn't written.

* * *

Morning came up gray and hard, with the wind backing around to the northwest exactly as the gulls had promised. Hollis stood at attention in the customs house while Colonel Ashworth read the dispatch by the window, the seal broken, the colonel's face doing what colonel's faces are trained to do, which is nothing.

"Delayed," Ashworth said at last.

"Yes, sir. Conditions, sir."

"The buoy."

"Dragged its mooring, sir. Ground swell's heavy. My men got one line on her yesterday and lost it." This was true, in the sense that two of his men had indeed gotten wet and miserable attempting it, and had used language that would require its own report.

Ashworth turned the pages. Hollis watched him read the sentence he'd built the whole structure around — *safe embarkation will require a minimum of three additional days* — and felt the moment hang the way the buoy hung on its chain, swinging between holding and dragging.

"You're certain of this assessment," Ashworth said. Not a question. Colonels don't ask questions; they issue statements shaped like questions and wait to see what breaks.

"I grew up on this coast, sir. Thirty miles north of here. I've seen what a northwest gale does to a harbor that looks tame." He kept his voice level, bored even, the voice of a man discussing firewood. "I'd rather report three days late than sail three days early, sir. With respect."

A long silence. Rain began ticking against the shutters, soft at first, like fingers testing a lock.

"Prout reads the pressure steady," Ashworth said.

"Prout's station sits behind a hill, sir."

Another silence, longer. And then — and Hollis would replay this moment for the rest of his life — Colonel Ashworth looked out the window at the harbor, at the white lines starting to show beyond the mole, at the gulls huddled inland like refugees, and something moved behind his eyes that was not suspicion. Something older and wearier. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who had once, long ago, before the maps and the ledgers, stood on a coast in bad weather and understood it.

"Very well," Ashworth said. "Draft revised orders. The *Providence* holds until the seventeenth. And Sergeant—"

"Sir?"

"If this delay costs us the window, it will be the last report you file."

"Understood, sir." And because he was apparently determined to hang himself completely: "Though if we lose the window, sir, I doubt either of us will be filing much of anything."

For one heartbeat he thought the colonel might strike him. Then — there was no other word for it — the corner of Ashworth's mouth twitched. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, visiting from some earlier decade.

"Get out of my office, Sergeant."

Hollis got out. He made it as far as the quay before his knees informed him of what his face had refused to feel, and he gripped the bollard and breathed the cold salt air and laughed — quietly, helplessly, alone in the rain — like a man who had just walked across a frozen river and heard the ice hold.

Three days. Seventy-two hours bought with ink and nerve.

Behind him, the customs house door opened. It was Private Dunn, breathless, cap in hand.

"Sergeant — you'll want to see this. Keeper Vane's at the door, and she's brought that smuggler fellow, Ollie. Says they need to speak with whoever handles charts." Dunn swallowed. "She says — she says there's a boat nobody's counted. And she wants to talk reef passages."

Hollis straightened his collar, tucked the frozen river of his nerves away where no one could see them, and turned toward the customs house with the rain running off his chin.

"Well," he said. "Then I suppose we'd better hear about the boat."
