## 22. Rehearsal at the Reef

The tide was going out when Mirabel climbed down from the gallery and found Brine Ollie waiting at the foot of the tower with a lantern in one hand and a look on his face like a man about to confess to something he'd been proud of for forty years.

"You came," Mirabel said.

"Course I came. You think a man offers the only secret of his life and then sleeps easy?" Ollie spat thoughtfully over the seawall. "I've been awake since noon. My wife thinks I've taken up religion."

Marta had gone aboard the Kestrel's route in her own way — she'd packed them bread, salt fish, a jug of hot coffee strong enough to strip paint, and a folded blanket "for the sergeant, he looks cold, they all look cold, poor lambs." Hollis arrived last, out of uniform, in a fisherman's jersey two sizes too big that made him look like a boy dressed for church. He carried a sounding lead coiled over one shoulder like a soldier's strap, because that was the only way he knew how to carry anything.

"No one followed me," he said. "I want that on the record."

"There's no record," said Mirabel. "That's rather the point."

"Then let it be on some record."

Ollie snorted and started walking. "Come on, then. Water won't hold its breath for us."

They went along the cliff path north of the point, single file, lanterns hooded, past the wreck of the old cod stages and down a gully that smelled of bladderwrack and cold iron. The Kestrel lay where Ollie had kept her these fifteen years — wedged in a shingle cove behind a headland the charts marked as worthless, which was exactly how Ollie liked his boats. She was a topsail schooner, forty feet if she was an inch, black-hulled, tarred to the eyes, with a name board so weathered it read *KE—REL* and might as well have read nothing at all.

"She floats?" Hollis asked dubiously.

"She *flies*, Sergeant. When she's allowed." Ollie patted the hull like a horse. "She's faster than anything Ashworth requisitioned, and she draws six foot light, four and a half if you take her in greedy and don't mind her grumbling. The reef passage off Gannet Point — the one every chart calls suicide — there's a channel through it. Deep enough. Wide enough. I've run it by night more times than I'll admit under oath."

"Why has no one charted it?"

"Because the men who knew it made their living on things that weren't charted," Ollie said cheerfully, "and the men who could have charted it would have taxed it." He looked at Mirabel then, and his humor dropped away like a coat. "But a smuggler's memory dies with the smuggler, and I'm seventy-one next March. So we sound it tonight, proper, mark by mark, and we write it down honest, and it stops being mine and becomes everybody's. That's the price. That was always the price. I just didn't know I'd be willing till now."

Mirabel took out the notebook — Ede's old survey book, actually, salvaged from the lighthouse stores, its first half filled with tidal calculations in a hand Mirabel hadn't opened in a year. There was room enough after that. There always was, in the end, for new things written after old ones.

"Then let's begin," Mirabel said. "The lamp's lit. It'll turn above us all night."

* * *

Here is what a rehearsal at sea sounds like: the creak of a lead line paying out, the call of depths in voices pitched low, and between each call, the long breathing silence of water that has not yet decided whether to be kind.

They worked from the Kestrel's dinghy, because Ollie refused on principle to risk the schooner until they knew the bottom. He rowed; Hollis swung the lead; Mirabel wrote. And above them all, sweeping the dark every seven seconds and a half, went the beam of the Gannet Point light — that great white arm turning in the sky like something patient and enormous keeping count.

"Mark," Hollis called, and brought the lead up dripping. "Four fathom. Fine grey sand and shell."

"Four fathom, sand and shell," Mirabel repeated, writing. "Bearing?"

Hollis squinted up at the tower, a black finger against the stars. "Lamp bears due south when the flash comes. Put that down too."

"I'm putting everything down."

"Good." Hollis hauled in the line again. "Ashworth will want everything down. If this is going to convince him — and it has to convince him, Vane, he doesn't believe in hope unless it's numbered — then it wants to look like a gunnery report. Depths. Bearings. Tidal set. Time of slack water."

"And you learned all that where? Sounding leads and tidal sets?"

"Sapper detachment, before the regiment. I built bridges nobody thanked me for." He swung the lead again, and the line sang out and slapped the water. "Six fathom. Shingle."

"Six, shingle," said Mirabel. And then, because the night was dark and the work was steady and honesty comes easier rowing than it does ashore: "Why are you doing this, Sergeant? Truly. You could lose everything. Your report already bends three ways from Sunday. This is another bend entirely."

Hollis didn't answer right away. He paid out the lead, let it touch, brought it back. "Five and a half. Rock and weed."

"Bearing," said Mirabel patiently.

"Lamp bears east-northeast at flash." He wound the line. "My mother came from a fishing town. Not this one. Norfolk way. Small. Cold. The kind of place the government remembers when it wants anchorage rights and forgets when it wants relief money. When I was nine the herring failed, and the army came — not to help. To keep order while people starved politely." The lead went out again. "I joined the army anyway. It feeds you regular, the army. But I never forgot what the red coats looked like from the other side of the harbor. From below, looking up." Another cast. "Seven fathom. Sand. Good sand, deep channel sand — this is the throat of it, Ollie!"

"Aye, that's her mouth," Ollie called from the oars, pleased as a man showing off grandchildren. "Keep sounding. She narrows past the third rock — you'll feel her pinch."

And pinch she did. Twice more they crossed the channel, working its width by cross-bearings on the light, and twice the lead told the same story: a deep lane through the reef, perhaps thirty yards wide at the narrowest, entered on a bearing of the lamp due south, threaded between two rocks that Ollie called the Old Man and His Wife ("because they've been quarrelling about whose fault the shipwrecks are since before my grandfather").

Mirabel wrote it all. And somewhere around the fourth crossing, with wet ink and numb fingers and the beam sweeping overhead once again — *there, and gone; there, and gone* — the realization arrived, quiet as the tide itself:

Every name in the ledger was a number to Ashworth. Every number was a soul to the town. But the light — the light was neither. The light was simply true, seven and a half seconds at a time, and any boat in any weather that could find it could find its way. For thirteen years Mirabel had polished that lens and trimmed that wick and called it duty, routine, the small stubborn maintenance of grief. It had never once occurred to them that the thing they kept was not a monument but an instrument. That the light did not merely *mark* the coast. It could *lead* through it.

"Ede knew," Mirabel said aloud, without meaning to.

"Eh?" said Ollie.

"Ede used to say — keep the light, and it keeps you. I always thought it meant the other way round. Comfort. Company." Mirabel looked up as the beam swept past, white and certain. "It meant this. It meant the light keeps *them*. Anyone out there. It was never about me at all."

The lead splashed. Nobody said anything for a moment, which is the correct response to a person realizing their whole life's work means more than they thought.

"Five fathom," Hollis offered gently. "Sand and broken shell. Bearing, lamp southwest at flash."

* * *

They rested in the lee of the headland around midnight, drinking Marta's coffee from the same tin mug passed in a circle, because Ollie declared that men who shared a mug couldn't inform on each other and Hollis, after a visible struggle with regulations, agreed this was probably legally sound.

"The trouble," Ollie said, "isn't the channel. Channel's honest. Trouble is the running of it. Forty souls aboard, night water, weather coming — someone has to con her through who knows her moods. I can pilot. But I need a helmsman I trust and I need the light burning steady the whole passage. Not flickering. Not dark for one second while somebody changes a mantle. One bad flash and we're on the Old Man."

"The light will burn," Mirabel said.

"It always burns," said Ollie. "But Thursday night it has to *matter*, if you take my meaning. Whole different animal, a light that matters."

Mirabel took his meaning. They had been keeping the light against emptiness for thirteen years. In four days — three now — it would be kept for forty lives threading a gap in the rocks, and the difference between those two things was the difference between practice and prayer.

"What about the colonel?" Hollis asked. "Even with your report perfect, even with depths and bearings and slack-water tables, he can refuse. He's got a requisitioned harbor, a manifest, and a schedule. A smuggler's schooner full of civilians is not in his arithmetic."

"No," Mirabel agreed. "But forty berths are. And he hasn't got them anywhere else. We bring him the numbers and let him do what he loves best." They almost smiled. Almost. "He'll hate it. He'll inspect it. He may insist on sailing with us, God help us. But Ashworth is not a cruel man, Sergeant. He's a precise man, and precision, given true numbers, has nowhere to hide."

"That's generous," said Hollis.

"He buried a family. I buried a spouse. Generous is what's left when you've done both." Mirabel said it evenly, and watched the sergeant decide not to ask, and was grateful.

They sounded the approaches once more as the tide turned, marking the outer leading line — a bearing that put the lamp dead astern of the Old Man rock — and then rowed home through the first grey suggestion of dawn, the Kestrel rocking gently behind them in her secret cove like a dog pretending it hadn't been waiting up.

At the top of the cliff path, Hollis paused. "Three days," he said. "My report buys us three days. After that, Ashworth expects the manifest sealed and the first loading begun, whatever the weather says. Whatever *anyone* says." He looked out at the light, still turning above them, faithful as ever in the fading stars. "So whatever else needs doing — do it fast."

* * *

Mirabel climbed the tower as the sun came up, bone-tired, salt-stiff, happier than they had any right to be, and found the kitchen table occupied.

Tam sat at it, very upright, surrounded by paper.

Not one boat. Not a dozen. Boats covered the table, crowded the dresser, filled the basket by the stove — dozens upon dozens of little folded vessels, each one creased sharp as a ledger line, each one with a name pencilled inside the hull. Wren stood over him with her arms crossed, wearing the expression of a girl who had decided this was fine and was daring anyone to say otherwise.

"He's been at it all night," Wren said. "Since you went out. I helped fold. Some of them are mine."

Mirabel set down the survey book slowly. "Tam. What are all these?"

Tam looked up. He didn't speak — he hadn't spoken since the flood, not once in all these weeks — but he picked up the nearest boat, opened it carefully along its creases, and turned it so Mirabel could read the name inside.

*Mrs. Tull.*

He set it down and took the next. *Cobb Fenwick.* The next. *The Pike brothers.* The next. *Wren.* The next. *Tam.*

Mirabel's hands were still cold from the sea and suddenly colder. "You've made one for everyone," they said quietly. "Everyone in the ledger."

Tam nodded. Then he pointed, one small blunt finger, out the seaward window — at the harbor, where the evacuation ship lay at anchor, and beyond it the open water, grey and enormous — and then at the boats, and then at the water again, and made a motion with both hands like something setting out and coming back.

Wren translated, in the flat voice she used when she was most frightened. "He wants to float them. All of them. At the harbor. Before the ships go." Her arms tightened. "He thinks if he puts all the names on the water, the sea will give them back. Like it's supposed to give Grandmother back. Like—" She stopped. "Like if the sea holds every name, it can't keep any of them."

Tam watched Mirabel steadily, waiting, the way children wait when they have asked the only question there is.

Mirabel looked at the fleet of paper — every neighbor, every friend, every debtor and grudge, every soul in the town folded small enough to hold — and understood that a nine-year-old had drawn the only chart of the situation that made sense. The adults had lists and tonnage and priorities. Tam had simply put all the names on the water together, where they belonged, and waited for them to come home.

"Yes," Mirabel said. Their voice cracked on it. "Yes, we'll float them. Every one."

Tam's face did not smile, exactly. But something in it unclenched, and he turned back to his folding, and outside the light went on turning above the reef, marking a channel that three tired people had measured in the dark — a way through, for anyone brave enough to follow it.
