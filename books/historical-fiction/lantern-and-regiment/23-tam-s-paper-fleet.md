## 23. Tam's Paper Fleet

The storm was still three days out — Hollis's falsified report had bought them that much, though only Mirabel knew the price of it — and the morning came up gray and soft as wool, the kind of light that made Gannet Point look almost gentle. The sea lay long and green under a sky the color of old pewter. Somewhere below in the town, Marta Tull's ovens would be going, and the smell of bread would drift up the cliff path like an apology.

Mirabel climbed the spiral stair with a bucket of lamp oil and a rag, because that was what mornings were for. Trim the wick. Wipe the lens. Log the weather. One hundred and eighty-two steps, each one worn smooth by Ede's boots before they'd ever been worn by Mirabel's own.

Except that for eleven days now, the steps had been wearing other boots too. Small ones.

"Wren!" Mirabel called down the stairwell, out of habit rather than need. "Don't let him near the oil store again!"

"I'm *supervising*!" Wren's voice floated back, indignant, from somewhere around the watch room. "That's practically a job! You could pay me!"

"With what? Buttons?"

"Buttons are legal tender if you decide they are. That's basically what you do with names on lists."

Mirabel paused halfway up, one hand on the cold iron rail, and considered whether an eight-year-old girl had just described the entire machinery of government better than Colonel Ashworth ever had. She had. That was the trouble with children. They saw the bones through the skin.

Tam, at least, was quiet. Tam was always quiet. He hadn't spoken since the water took his grandmother's cottage off the shingle at Marrow Lane — not one word in three weeks, not when the soldiers chalked numbers on doors, not when the bread ran short, not when Mirabel sat with him at night and told stories about Ede until their own voice went hoarse against his silence. The doctors' men said grief did that to some children. Sealed them up like a bottle. You could only wait for the tide to work the cork loose, or not.

So Mirabel waited. And trimmed the wick. And logged the weather.

But this morning, when Mirabel pushed open the door to the lamp room, Tam was already there.

He was sitting cross-legged on the floor beneath the great lens, which turned above him slow as a planet, throwing its long white blade of light out over the gray water even in daylight — because Ashworth's orders were explicit: the light would burn day and night until the evacuation was done, a range marker for the reef passage, a promise and a threat all at once. And in that turning light, Tam was surrounded by boats.

Hundreds of them. Maybe a thousand. Little paper boats, folded from every scrap of paper a boy could beg or find — the backs of ration chits, the flyleaves of hymnals (Wren would have opinions about that), the margins of the *Gannet Point Gazette*, even, Mirabel noticed with a small internal flinch, a corner torn from a supply manifest bearing Sergeant Hollis's own neat signature. They covered the floor of the lamp room in fleets and squadrons, whole armadas of them, white and gray and faintly tea-stained, riding an ocean of flagstones.

Tam looked up. His face was thin and grave, and his hands — his clever, restless hands, the ones that couldn't stop moving even in sleep — were folded around one last boat, unfinished.

Mirabel set down the oil bucket very slowly, the way you'd move near a wild bird.

"Well," Mirabel said, when the silence had stretched long enough to hurt. "That's quite a navy."

Tam didn't answer. But he didn't look away either, and after three weeks of a child who looked through people rather than at them, being *looked at* felt like a kind of speech.

Mirabel lowered themselves to the flagstones, joints cracking in protest — forty-one years old and feeling ninety — and picked up the nearest boat. It was folded with astonishing precision, crisp as origami from a picture book, the hull sharp, the sail perfect. On its side, in Tam's careful pencil, was written a name.

*Tull.*

Marta Tull. Who left warm loaves at the lighthouse door twice a week and never once asked why Mirabel's ledger had grown so thick.

Mirabel set it down and picked up another. *Fenwick.* Cobb Fenwick, who had threatened them in the street outside the bakery, whose breath had smelled of fear and cheap gin, whose name Mirabel had written low on the priority list with a hand that shook and hadn't stopped shaking since.

Another. *Ollie.* Brine Ollie, smuggler, keeper of charts, holder of the Kestrel's secret.

Another. *Hollis.*

Mirabel's throat went tight. They looked across the fleet at the boy, and the boy looked back, and above them both the lens turned and turned, laying its blade of light across the water where the real ships waited.

"Tam," Mirabel said gently. "What are these?"

And Tam Vane-or-nothing-lately, six years old and silent as the drowned, opened his mouth and said:

"Stay."

One word. Cracked and rusty, like a hinge on a door long shut. But it was a word, it was *his* word, and Mirabel felt it go through them like a fishhook.

Then he held up the unfinished boat in his hands, and said, in a whisper that seemed to cost him everything: "*Please* stay."

*

They came down the stairs together, Tam's hand in Mirabel's, and found Wren in the kitchen pretending she hadn't been crying, which fooled nobody because her eyes were red as gulls' feet and there was a half-folded boat on the table in front of her with *Vane, T.* penciled on the side.

"You know about them," Mirabel said.

"He folds at night," Wren said, chin up, defiant, terrified. "Under the blanket. With your spare candle stub. I thought — I thought it was helping him. It was helping him. Don't be angry at him."

"I'm not angry." Mirabel sat down heavily at the table. The kitchen smelled of yesterday's bread and lamp oil and salt, and the kettle ticked as it cooled, and outside the gulls wheeled over the gray water crying their one opinion about everything. "I don't understand yet. That's different from angry. Sit down, both of you. Tell me about the fleet."

Wren sat. Tam sat pressed against Mirabel's side, which he had never done before, small and warm and solid as a coal.

"It started with Gran," Wren said. Her voice had steadied; she was twelve, and she had learned in three weeks what some people take thirty years to learn, which is how to carry things without letting anyone see you carrying them. "After the cottage went. He kept saying — well. Not saying. He'd make the shape of it with his hands. Boats. Water. I thought he wanted a boat, so I got him paper, and he folded one, and then he wrote Gran's name on it — *Maren Vane* — and he took it down to the tide line and put it in the water and watched it go."

She stopped. Swallowed.

"And then he came back and folded another one," she said. "And another. And I counted them last night while he was asleep, because I count things now too, it's a thing we do, and—" Her voice cracked, and she pressed on anyway, because Wren did not crack, not in front of witnesses. "There are two hundred and sixty-one. He's been asking me how to spell the hard ones. *Fenwick* took three tries."

Two hundred and sixty-one. Mirabel knew the number the way you know your own pulse, because they had written it themselves, in ink, in the reconstructed ledger that now lived under triple guard in the customs house cellar. Two hundred and sixty-one souls remaining in Gannet Point and the scattered farms along the coast. Two hundred and sixty-one names, and berths for one hundred and ninety, and arithmetic in between that no colonel's compass could square.

Every name in the ledger. Every single one. Folded into paper and waiting.

"What does he do with them?" Mirabel asked quietly. "The ones he puts in the water?"

Tam spoke without lifting his head from Mirabel's sleeve. His voice was still rusted, still barely more than breath, but he used it, and that alone was worth more than the Kestrel and her forty berths.

"The sea gives them back," he said.

Wren's hand crept across the table and took her brother's.

"That's what he believes," she said. "You put the name in the sea, and the sea holds it, and when the storm's done the sea gives it back. That's why he has to fold all of them. If even one name isn't in the water, the sea won't give *that one* back. He explained it to me with his hands before he could talk. I understood. Mostly." She glared at Mirabel with sudden ferocity, daring them to laugh, daring them to pity, daring them to do anything but take it seriously. "It's not silly. It's *true*. Things you give away come back. That's true of bread and it's true of names and I'll thank you not to say otherwise."

"I wasn't going to say otherwise," Mirabel said, and found their voice had gone strange and thick.

Because here was the thing, the terrible shining thing: the boy was right. In every way that mattered, the boy was right, and he had understood in six years what the whole apparatus of the Crown and the Army and Colonel Ashworth's beautiful merciless arithmetic could not grasp. A list was not a number. A name was not a tonnage figure. A name was a thing you gave to the sea and trusted the sea to return, and the entire evacuation — the ships, the manifests, the sealed berth list under Wren's pillow even now, copied in her small fierce hand — was nothing but a town trying to give itself to the water and get itself back.

And one keeper, who had spent a year mistaking loneliness for peace, who had hidden a heart behind ledgers and lamp oil, who had set out two cups every morning for a dead spouse until eleven days ago when the cups had become four without permission — one keeper had signed the list. Had certified it accurate. Had written *Fenwick* low and *Tull* lower and had told themselves it was the arithmetic's fault, not theirs, that ink was just ink.

Tam had folded the same list by hand, one boat at a time, and called it mercy.

"Tide's coming up around four," Mirabel said. Their voice held. Barely. "Low wind. Good floating weather."

Tam lifted his head. For the first time in three weeks, his eyes were not empty. They were enormous, and brown, and full of something that looked terribly like hope, which is the heaviest thing a child can carry.

"All of them?" he whispered. "Today? Before the storm?"

"All of them," Mirabel said. "Before the storm. Every name. We'll float the whole fleet together — you and me and your sister. And then, when the sea gives them back..." They hesitated, because the next part was a promise and promises were load-bearing walls now, and Mirabel had learned in the last month exactly how much weight a promise could hold. "When the sea gives them back, we'll be here on the shore to receive them. All three of us. That's how returning works. Someone has to stay and catch."

Tam studied them with the grave, measuring stare of a harbormaster appraising a vessel. Then he nodded, once, the way Ede used to nod — *aye, that'll hold* — and went back to folding, and his little tongue poked out the corner of his mouth in concentration, and Mirabel Vane stood up abruptly and said they needed to check the gallery railings and escaped up the stairs before anyone could see what their face was doing.

Their face, it turned out, was doing a great deal. Alone in the turning light, gripping the cold brass rail of the gallery with the whole gray sea breathing below, Mirabel Vane wept — really wept, for the first time since the news had come, since the empty coat, since the first terrible morning of setting out two cups and drinking both — not with the dry tearing grief of the last year but with something else underneath it, something warmer and more frightening. Because the boy downstairs had spoken, and the word had not been *mama* or *gran* or *why*. The word had been *stay*. And Mirabel understood, standing in the light Ede had taught them to keep, that they had already answered. Three days from now, when the boats loaded in the dark, there would be no question left. Some part of them had known it since the moment a half-drowned girl had banged on the lighthouse door with her brother under her arm.

Some names you write in ledgers. Some you fold into boats and give to the sea. And some — the truest ones — you write nowhere at all, because they're not entries. They're keepers.

*

They floated the fleet at four o'clock.

Word had traveled, as word does in a town of two hundred and sixty-one souls — Wren had told Marta, Marta had told everyone, and by the appointed hour the tide line below the lighthouse path was lined with townsfolk, quiet as churchgoers, hats in hands. Brine Ollie came up from the Kestrel's hiding cove smelling of tar and secrecy. Even Cobb Fenwick appeared at the edge of the crowd, arms crossed, jaw set, saying nothing, staying anyway — which was, Mirabel reflected, the most honest prayer the man had offered in years.

Tam walked the water's edge with the gravity of a bishop, placing the boats one by one onto the incoming tide. *Maren Vane* first, of course. Then *Tull*. Then *Fenwick*, and Cobb's chin trembled and he pretended it was the wind. Then *Hollis*, and a certain red-coated sergeant, watching from a discreet distance in civilian oilskins he clearly thought constituted a disguise, turned sharply away and blew his nose with military precision.

Boat after boat after boat. Two hundred and sixty-one. The tide took them all — out past the breakwater, into the wide pewter evening, a whole town's worth of names sailing together on the flood, white sails catching the last light. Nobody spoke. The gulls cried their one opinion. And Tam stood hip-deep — Mirabel's hand fisted in his collar the entire time, ready to haul him back — and watched every single one go, and whispered, over and over, so softly only Mirabel could hear:

"Gives them back. Gives them back. Gives them back."

The last boat was the one he'd finished in the lamp room that morning. He saved it for the end, and when he finally bent to set it on the water, Mirabel read the name penciled on its side and felt their heart stop and restart like a lamp in wind.

It said *Ede*.

"He's in the water already," Tam said, matter-of-fact, looking up. "Gran told me. People in the sea help the other boats. That's what they're for."

The tide took Ede's boat out past the breakwater, out past the fleet, sailing faster than all the rest as if it had somewhere particular to be, and Mirabel stood on the cold sand holding a six-year-old's shoulder and a year's worth of grief that had finally, finally changed its shape — and did not see, because grief makes poor watchkeepers, the rider coming fast along the cliff road from the customs house, or the way the rider's lantern swung like an alarm bell, or the way Sergeant Hollis broke from the crowd at a run, all disguise abandoned, shouting words the wind tore apart before they arrived.

By the time the words landed, the tide had carried the fleet beyond recall, and the light in them — the light in *everyone* — guttered.

Ashworth knew. The rider brought the message whole: *Cobb Fenwick talked. The Kestrel is compromised. The colonel demands the keeper at the customs house within the hour.*

Tam's small hand tightened in Mirabel's. Wren went pale and stepped in front of her brother, an old instinct wearing new armor.

Above them, indifferent and faithful, the great lens began its turn, sweeping its blade of light across the darkening water toward the reef — toward the passage that now, perhaps, only Mirabel and a dying smuggler and one traitor in the town fully understood.

"One hour," Hollis panted, reaching them. "Keeper — he means it. And ma'am—" his eyes flicked, apologetic, to Wren, to Tam, "—he asked for the children's names specifically."

The sea, faithful to its promise, was already giving the boats back. The first of them came tumbling ashore at Mirabel's feet, sodden and stubborn, sail still standing.

*Maren Vane.*

Mirabel picked it up, wrung it out with steady hands, and handed it to the boy.

"Go home," they said. "Light the lamp in the kitchen window. Whatever happens tonight — keep it burning till I'm back. That's your post now. Both of you. Keepers keep."

And they turned up the cliff path toward the customs house, toward Ashworth, toward the arithmetic, walking into the wind.
