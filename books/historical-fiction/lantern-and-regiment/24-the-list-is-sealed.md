## 24. The List Is Sealed

The wax was red, of course. Mirabel had expected nothing else. Armies did their mourning in red.

They had been summoned to the customs house at nine o'clock sharp, which in Colonel Ashworth's arithmetic meant eight-fifty-five, and so Mirabel arrived at eight-forty to find the colonel already at his desk with the great ledger open before him like a patient on an operating table, and Sergeant Hollis standing at parade rest by the window, and two soldiers Mirabel didn't know flanking the door as though the book itself might attempt escape.

"Keeper Vane." Ashworth did not look up. He was reading the final page with a pencil held between two fingers, not writing anything, just holding it, the way a man holds a knife he hasn't decided whether to use. "You will observe that the list is complete."

Mirabel observed it. Two hundred and eleven names, entered in Mirabel's own hand over nineteen days, each one certified against the parish records, the tithe rolls, and the memory that lived behind Mirabel's eyes like a second lamp. Two hundred and eleven names out of three hundred and forty souls. The rest were chalked into the margins of the town's future in small grey numbers: *remain. essential. await relief.*

Await relief. As if relief were a ship that existed.

"I observe it," Mirabel said.

"The Board requires certification." Ashworth turned the ledger around and pushed it across the desk, along with a pen that was far too fine for the work — black lacquer, gold nib, the kind of pen that signed things away from a great distance without ever getting its hands wet. "You wrote these names. You will attest they are true."

"And if I attested they were false?"

Hollis shifted by the window. One of the soldiers at the door made a small sound, like a cough strangled in infancy.

Ashworth looked up then, and for a moment something moved behind his face — not warmth, never warmth, but the recognition of a fellow professional attempting the same trade with different instruments. "Then you would be lying to His Majesty's government, and I would be obliged to note it. And you would sign anyway, because the alternative is that I find another hand to certify what your hand knows better than any in this county. The list does not change, Keeper. Only the name beneath it does."

It was honestly said. That was the worst of Ashworth — he never lied about the trap. He simply described its dimensions with the precision of a man measuring for a coffin, and let you climb in yourself.

Mirabel picked up the pen. It was heavier than it looked. Everything in this room was.

* * *

The night before, there had been paper boats.

Tam had left one on the desk again — the fourth this week — folded from a page torn out of the old almanac, and inside, in a child's careful pencil, a single name: *Marta Tull.* Mirabel had unfolded it standing at the window with the lamp turning overhead, and found that their hands were not entirely steady, because Marta Tull's name sat thirty-first on the berth list, and thirty-one was safe, and Tam did not know that, and Mirabel could not tell him, because telling him meant explaining why Wren's name sat at one hundred and ninety-eight and Tam's at one hundred and ninety-nine, in the long grey country of *await relief*, and some truths you cannot hand to a child of seven any more than you can hand him the sea.

Instead Mirabel had folded the almanac page back into a boat and set it on the sill of the gallery window, facing the water, and said aloud to no one — to Ede, if Ede was anywhere in the salt wind — "He thinks the sea gives them back."

And the sea had said nothing, being, as ever, the only honest party in Gannet Point.

Wren had been quieter lately too, which for Wren was a thing like a lighthouse going quiet — noticeable, wrong, and ominous. She had taken to asking questions with a terrible casualness. *What time do the soldiers change guard? Is the customs house cellar really flooded still? Do you think Sergeant Hollis sleeps, or does he just stand somewhere?* Mirabel had answered none of them directly and all of them indirectly, by saying "Go to bed," with increasing frequency and decreasing conviction.

Now, standing in the customs house with a gold nib drying in their grip, Mirabel understood exactly what the child had been doing with all those questions, and understood also that it was too late to stop her, and that perhaps — the thought arrived unbidden and would not leave — perhaps it should not be stopped at all.

"You hesitate," Ashworth observed.

"I'm ensuring the ink takes," Mirabel said. "A signature that fades is worse than none. Ask any keeper who's ever tried to claim oil stores off a smudged requisition."

Something that was almost a smile touched the corner of Ashworth's mouth and was firmly escorted away. "Certify."

*Vane,* Mirabel wrote. *Keeper, Gannet Point Light. I certify the within register true and complete according to my knowledge.*

Every word of it was true. That was the trick of it, wasn't it. The list was accurate. Every name spelled right, every household counted right, every age and trade and infirmity recorded with a loving exactness that Mirabel hated themselves for. The list was true the way a knife is true. It cut cleanly because it had been made well.

Mirabel set down the pen.

Ashworth dripped wax onto the page — a candle stub produced from a drawer, heated over a spirit lamp until the wax went the colour of a wound — and pressed his seal into it with both hands, leaning his weight on it, a man closing a vault. Then he closed the ledger, and Hollis bound it in oilcloth and cord, and carried it to the iron chest in the corner, and locked it, and pocketed the key.

"The manifest goes aboard the *Providence* tomorrow morning," Ashworth said. "Boarding begins at first light the day after. You will post the certified numbers in the square by noon today, so that the town may make its arrangements." He said *arrangements* the way other men said *funeral*. "Keeper Vane. A word on your way out."

The soldiers withdrew. Hollis lingered, was dismissed with a flick of two fingers, and went — though Mirabel noted, filing it in the same place where Tam's paper boats lived, that the sergeant paused at the door a half-second longer than regulation required, and that his eyes went once to the iron chest, and once to Mirabel, and that his face did the thing faces do when a man is composing his features and doing a poor job of it.

Alone, Ashworth stood at the window. Below, through the streaming glass, the harbor lay grey and restless, and the *Providence* rode at anchor looking, from this height, like a toy that had been left in a bath.

"A year ago," Ashworth said, "I kept a similar register. In India. Fever season." He did not turn around. "Three hundred names. I knew four hundred and twelve, and struck eighty-two. My clerk certified it. A local man, very precise, very decent. He shook my hand afterward and thanked me for the fairness of the process." Now he turned. "Two years later I learned he had drowned himself in the river behind his own house. I have often thought about what he must have been carrying, walking home that evening with his hand shaken."

Mirabel said nothing. There was nothing in the room to say that wasn't either a lie or a confession.

"I am not telling you this for sympathy," Ashworth said. "I am telling you so that you understand I know precisely what I have asked of you, and what it costs, and that I will not insult you by pretending otherwise. The arithmetic is mine. You are only the instrument."

"Yes," Mirabel said. "That's what I keep telling myself."

For a moment — one moment, no longer — the colonel's eyes held something that in a better man might have been called regret, and in this man could only be called inventory. "Noon," he said. "The square. Post the numbers."

* * *

Mirabel posted the numbers.

They did it themselves rather than let a soldier do it, nailing the sheet to the board outside the chapel while the town gathered in the kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the shoulders. Marta Tull read her own name and put her apron over her mouth. Old Cobb Fenwick found himself at sixty-four and laughed, one short bark with no dog in it, and walked away down the lane with his fists in his pockets. Somebody's mother fainted and was propped up against the chapel wall like a sack of meal. A boy of twelve ran his finger down the columns twice, found his family whole and berthed, and then stood there weeping anyway, because he'd found his friend's family missing, and grief is generous that way — it arrives even for the spared.

Mirabel walked home through it. Nobody spoke to them. Nobody spat, either, which Mirabel supposed was progress of a sort, and the thought was so bleakly funny that they nearly laughed in the street, and decided that a keeper laughing alone in a ruined town might be the last straw for somebody, and kept it in.

At the lighthouse door there were offerings again — a loaf, a twist of tea, a jar of honey gone cloudy with cold — left, Mirabel understood now, not in gratitude but in petition. The fisherfolk had stopped leaving thanks weeks ago. These were prayers, addressed to the one person in Gannet Point whose hand had touched every name. Mirabel carried them inside and set them on the table next to Tam's latest boat, and stood there a long while, lamp oil on their sleeves, bread in their arms, feeling the full weight of what it is to be trusted by everyone and answerable to no one but the dead.

* * *

That evening Wren came up to the lamp room while Mirabel trimmed the wicks, and stood watching the flame with her arms crossed in a manner she had certainly learned from Hollis, and said, "When does the manifest go aboard?"

"Tomorrow morning."

"And it's sealed. With wax."

"With wax."

"And nobody can look at it after it's sealed?"

"That," Mirabel said carefully, "is generally what sealing means."

Wren nodded, absorbing this, the way she absorbed everything: silently, completely, and with consequences that would arrive later, usually at night, usually involving property that was not hers. She helped trim the lower wick without being asked, her small fingers quick and sure on the brass — she'd learned that much in a week, and learned it better than Mirabel had taught her, because children learn hands faster than teachers can teach them — and then she went down the stairs, and Mirabel heard her pause on the landing, and heard Tam's voice say something low, and heard Wren say, lower still, "*Not yet.*"

Not yet. Not *no*. Not *we can't*. Not yet.

Mirabel stood in the turning light and felt the truth of it settle over them like the storm-smell that comes ahead of the wind: the list was sealed, yes. Sealed in wax, locked in iron, bound for the *Providence*. But a seal is only a promise that a document hasn't changed. It says nothing — nothing at all — about what a document becomes after it's opened. Or before it reaches its ship. Or whether every page in a ledger must necessarily survive the crossing.

Downstairs, Mirabel took out the logbook and made the night's entry. Wind northwest, freshening. Barometer falling — falling faster than it ought, faster than the Admiralty's little printed curve allowed for, and Mirabel wrote the number twice and underlined it, because the storm the colonel had scheduled for Friday was reading Thursday in the glass.

Then, below the weather, in the space where the official regulations said *remarks*, Mirabel hesitated — and wrote:

*Tam folded a boat tonight with Marta Tull's name in it. He believes the sea gives back what it is given. I have decided to act as though he is right, since every adult in this county appears determined to prove otherwise.*

It was not a remark the Board would recognize. It was, however, true and complete according to Mirabel's knowledge, and they had signed their name to enough truth today to know the feel of it.

They blew out the work lamp, and in the dark the great lens turned above them, throwing its beam out over the reef, over the anchorage, over the black water where a smuggler's boat named Kestrel waited under Brine Ollie's tarpaulin for a crew that did not officially exist — and Mirabel climbed to the gallery and stood watch, and began, quietly, methodically, in the only language they had ever trusted, to plan how a sealed list might be persuaded to become a different story entirely.

Below in the town, a light moved along the quay road toward the customs house. Small. Hurried. The shape of it — quick, slight, skirts of a coat flying — was unmistakable to anyone who had spent three weeks teaching a girl to handle brass.

Mirabel watched it go, and did not raise the alarm, and found that their heart, for the first time in a year, was not lonely at all.

It was terrified. It was complicit. And it was, at last, entirely awake.
