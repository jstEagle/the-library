## 25. Cobb's Betrayal

Cobb Fenwick had never in his fifty-one years been good at waiting, and he was catastrophically bad at it now.

He stood at his window on the third morning after the town meeting, watching the rain come down in long gray ropes, and told himself — as he had told himself a hundred times since the lists went up — that there had been some mistake. That the arithmetic would be corrected. That a man who had kept the biggest boatyard south of the harbor for thirty years, who had built half the hulls riding at anchor out there, who had employed half the men whose names *had* made the list, could not possibly have been marked with a chalk three.

Three berths. For four Fenwicks. And not even the first three — the list had him down behind the cooper, behind the schoolmistress, behind young Ned Pellowe who couldn't yet shave.

"It's the yard," his wife said from behind him, not unkindly. She was mending a net by the fire, though there'd be no fish to catch if they all drowned. "They want hands that can work the mainland. You've got hands, love. They just built things instead."

"Built things," Cobb repeated. "Aye. Built every blessed thing they're sailing away on, and they'll thank me for it from the deck while I stand on the quay."

He said it loudly enough that his daughter Susannah, thirteen and sharp-eared as her mother, looked up from her book. His son Jem, nine, did not look up from anything anymore; since the flood took the Tull boy's grandmother's cottage and very nearly took the Tull boy too, Jem had gone quiet in a way that frightened Cobb more than any storm.

"We'll be fine," Susannah said, in the tone of a girl comforting a much older person. "The lightkeeper won't let them leave anyone."

"The lightkeeper," Cobb said, "signed the list."

That was the wound of it, the one he turned over at night like a stone. He'd known Mirabel Vane twenty years. They'd quarreled about mooring rights and crab pots and once, memorably, about a church pew. But he'd thought — he'd have sworn on the yard itself — that Mirabel Vane would no sooner write a neighbor's name low on a death-list than cut off their own hand. And then he'd stood in the square and heard the keeper read the names out in that level, careful voice, and his had come where it had come.

What Cobb did not know — what nobody knew but three people and a smuggler — was why the numbers were what they were, or what Mirabel Vane had spent two sleepless nights arranging beneath them.

* * *

It was Susannah, of course, who found the first thread.

She came in at dusk, salt-wet to the knee, and announced over supper: "Brine Ollie's been up to the lighthouse twice this week. After dark. I saw his lantern on the cliff path."

"Eh?" said Cobb. "Ollie's got no business up there. Ollie's got no business anywhere but his hut and his bottle."

"He had a chart with him," Susannah said. "Big one. Rolled up under his coat. And Mrs. Tull was coming down the path as I was going up, and she had flour on her sleeves at suppertime, which means she'd been baking for somebody, because she doesn't bake for us anymore." She said this last without bitterness, only observation, the way children record the world when the world has stopped making sense. "And Tam Vane — well. Tam. He was on the gallery with a spyglass, looking at the reef. At night. In this weather."

Cobb set down his fork.

Now. A man can live his whole life with a certain kind of mind and never know it's a certain kind of mind until the moment comes to use it. Cobb Fenwick had a wrecker's mind — no, that's unfair, he'd never lured a ship onto the rocks in his days — call it a salvager's mind. Thirty years of reading water, of knowing where cargo hid and how men moved when they were moving something they shouldn't. And everything Susannah had just described — night visits, hidden charts, the reef surveyed by spyglass, bread carried up a cliff path — added up, in a salvager's mind, to exactly one thing.

"There's another boat," he said slowly.

Susannah blinked. "What?"

"Think it through, girl. Why else sound the reef? Why else would Marta Tull be baking for a garrison that feeds itself? There's a second vessel. Unregistered. Off the books." His heart had begun to hammer, and he found, to his own surprise, that he wasn't angry. Not yet. What he felt first was something worse: hope. Wild, specific, greedy hope. "Forty souls, maybe more. Somewhere. And Vane knows where."

"They wouldn't hide a whole boat from everyone," Susannah said. "Would they?"

"They'd hide it from the army," Cobb said. "Which is everyone, these days."

He sat back. Outside, the rain worked at the windows like something patient. And the hope curdled, as hope does in a frightened man, into its other shape. Because if there was a second boat, and forty more berths, then the question became not *is there room for my family* but *who decided who fills it* — and the answer, he already knew, would be the same answer as every other answer in Gannet Point that winter: Mirabel Vane, with the pen.

"And we're marked three of four," Cobb said quietly. "While Vane plays ferryman for favorites."

"You don't know that."

"I know Marta Tull's baking goes uphill and mine doesn't." He stood, and kissed the top of his son's head without quite seeing him, and took his oilskins down from the peg. "I'm going to the Anchor."

"Cobb," his wife said — and here is a thing worth recording: she said it gently. She knew her husband. She knew the difference between his anger and his fear, and she had been watching the fear grow in him for a month like black ice. "Whatever you're thinking of doing—"

"I'm going to have a drink," Cobb said, "with men who still talk to me."

* * *

He learned the shape of it at the Anchor, though not the substance. The substance came later, and from an unexpected quarter, because the world that winter had developed a habit of handing Cobb Fenwick truths at the worst possible moments.

The public bar was half-full of men too tired to drink properly. Talk died when Cobb entered — that had been happening since the lists — and he bought a round anyway, and presently old Hodge the netmender, three cups past caution, leaned close.

"Boats is leaving that aren't on no manifest," Hodge murmured. "That's all I'll say. That's all anybody'll say. But you mark me — when the storm comes, there'll be more souls off this rock than Ashworth's paper says there are. And good luck to 'em."

"More souls," Cobb repeated. "Off the *rock*. So there *is* a second vessel."

Hodge suddenly found his cup fascinating. Around them, the bar had achieved a silence so complete that the fire sounded loud.

Cobb laughed — one short, ugly bark — and set down his own untouched cup. "Right," he said. "Right. Don't tell Cobb. Cobb's only built your boats and patched your hulls and lent half of you money you never paid back. Cobb can drown on paper." He raised his voice, and hated himself even as he did it, and did it anyway. "Well, I hope she rides sweet, whoever she is. I hope she rides sweet through the reef, because I'm the man who *buoyed that channel*, and there's a shelf off the Kettle Stone that'll open her hull like a tin of sardines if her pilot's half a fathom wrong!"

He walked out into the rain before anyone could answer, which was fortunate, because what he wanted, standing in the dark with the water running off his hat brim, was not a drink at all.

He wanted to go home. He wanted to wake his wife and say *there's a way, there's a way, I've found the edge of it.* And underneath that, quieter, in a voice he didn't recognize as his own: *or you could make sure your family is on whatever leaves this harbor, however it leaves.*

Fear, gentlemen and ladies, does not make men evil. It makes them exact. It strips away everything except the arithmetic, and what remains is a man standing in the rain calculating, coldly and precisely, the price of the only currency he has left.

Cobb Fenwick stood in the rain for a long time. Then he turned, not toward the Fenwick house, but toward the customs house, where a light still burned in the colonel's window.

* * *

Sergeant Hollis saw him coming, and did not like it.

Hollis was on the customs house steps with his collar up, nursing the particular misery of a soldier standing guard on a coast he'd come to love in a cause he no longer believed in. He recognized Cobb at fifty yards — the rolling seaman's walk, the fury in the shoulders.

"Not tonight, Mr. Fenwick."

"Aye, tonight." Cobb stopped at the bottom step. Rain streamed between them. "I want to speak to the colonel."

"The colonel sees petitioners at ten o'clock."

"This isn't a petition."

"Then it can wait till ten o'clock."

Cobb climbed one step, close enough that Hollis could smell the Anchor's whisky on him and see, beneath the anger, the thing that actually drove him. Hollis had seen it in a hundred faces by now. It was fear. It was always fear.

"Mr. Fenwick," Hollis said, lowering his voice, "I'll tell you something for nothing. Whatever you've come to sell, don't. There isn't a man in this town who'd thank you, and there isn't a soldier here who wants to hear it, and the colonel—" he hesitated, and gave away more than he meant to, "—the colonel already suspects. Anything you add will only speed up a thing that's better slow."

For one moment — Hollis would remember this all his life — Cobb wavered. The big man's face worked, and Hollis watched thirty years of a town pass behind his eyes: the yard, the boats, the christenings, the quarrels, the day the flood came and Cobb's crew had pulled three families off the chapel roof with their own hands.

Then the fear won. It usually does. That is the whole tragedy of it, and it fits in a single sentence.

"My family's marked three of four," Cobb said. "You fix that, and I'll fix your suspicion."

* * *

Colonel Ashworth received him in the map room, where the charts of the coast hung like flayed skins and the lamp burned low. Ashworth did not offer a chair. He listened, standing, with his hands folded behind him, while Cobb laid it all out: the night visits, Brine Ollie's chart, the sounding of the reef, Marta Tull's flour, the child with the spyglass. He left out Hodge and the Anchor, and he left out Susannah, and those two omissions were the last scraps of the man he'd been.

"A second vessel," Ashworth said at last. "Unregistered."

"She exists, Colonel. I'd stake the yard on it. And she's meant to run the reef passage — the passage I buoyed, if you'll recall, the summer of '66. Which means whoever pilots her needs the light as a range marker, which means—" Cobb's throat closed, and he forced it open. "Which means Vane."

Ashworth was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked against the shutters. When he spoke, his voice had the terrible mildness of a man confirming a sum.

"Four Fenwicks. Three berths."

"Four," Cobb said. "All four. On the evacuation ship. First loading. Written orders, sealed."

"And if I do this, and your information proves false?"

"It won't."

"If it proves true," Ashworth said, "and I move to seize this vessel before she's needed — and souls who might have lived on her die in my custody instead, or drown waiting — that will be on your ledger, Mr. Fenwick. Not mine. I want you to understand the transaction plainly. You are not buying berths. You are buying them with other people's chances. Do you accept the terms?"

And here is the thing that must be said about Cobb Fenwick, because history — real history, the kind that gets written into ledgers — prefers its betrayers simple, and he was not. He stood in the lamplight with his family's lives held out before him like a hand of cards, and he did not feel clever, or vindicated, or safe. He felt sick. He thought of Jem asleep upstairs. He thought of the Tull boy's paper boats. He thought, absurdly, of the pew he and Mirabel Vane had fought over, and how they'd ended up sharing it, one winter, when the church was full of funerals.

"Aye," Cobb said. "I accept."

"Then you will go home," Ashworth said, "and you will speak of this to no one, and you will act surprised when the soldiers come. Your orders will be delivered by morning." He turned back to the maps, dismissing him — then paused. "Mr. Fenwick. The keeper of your light has lost a spouse to that sea. Did you know that?"

"...Everyone knows that."

"And yet they stayed at the lamp. Every night. Through all of it." Ashworth did not turn around. "I have spent this entire requisition trying to determine what that is worth, and whether it can be counted. You have just answered the second question. It can. That is the most depressing discovery of my career. Good night."

* * *

Cobb walked home through the rain and did not go inside at once. He stood in his own doorway, dripping, looking down the hill at the lighthouse, where the beam swung out over the black water, steady as a heartbeat, the way it had swung every night of his life — the way it had guided his boats home through worse storms than this, year after year, asking nothing.

Inside, Susannah was still awake. He could see her candle under the door. He thought about going up, telling her, confessing it to someone, letting it become a shared weight instead of a private stone.

Instead he went to the window and watched the light turn, and told himself the oldest lie in the drowning man's book: *it won't come to anything. They'll find another way. No one will really suffer for it.*

Up the hill, in the lighthouse, Mirabel Vane trimmed the wicks and knew none of this — knew only that the barometer had fallen all evening, faster than it should, and that the glass had begun, very quietly, to sing.

The great storm was coming early.
