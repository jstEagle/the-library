## 26. Interrogation by Lamplight

The summons came at dusk, delivered by a private so young his chin had not yet decided on a beard. He stood at the lighthouse door dripping rain and trying to look official, and Wren looked at him the way she looked at gulls that landed where they shouldn't.

"Colonel Ashworth requests the keeper's presence at the customs house," the boy recited. "Immediately."

"Requests," Mirabel said, drying their hands on a rag that smelled of lamp oil and brass polish. "That's a generous word for it."

Tam appeared at Mirabel's elbow, silent as ever, and pressed something into their palm before they could go — a folded paper boat, small enough to hide in a fist. Inside, when Mirabel glanced later, was a single word in Tam's careful hand: *come back.* The child had found his voice only days ago, one word at a time, and he spent them like coins he could not spare.

Mirabel tucked the boat into their coat pocket and walked down into town through the wet dark, past houses whose chalked numbers glowed faintly in the lamplight like wounds that had been counted.

* * *

The customs house had been built to flatter trade and now served the army, which had solved the problem of its grandeur by covering everything in canvas and crates. Ashworth had established himself in the back room, where the great ledgers lived, and he had arranged the interrogation — though he would never call it that — with the same care he gave to tonnage calculations.

One lamp. Two chairs, set facing. A table between them, and on the table, three objects laid out like instruments before surgery.

A coil of tarred rope, naval pattern, cut clean at one end.

A chart of the reef passage, unmarked on its face but bearing, along its lower edge, a series of pinpricks that any fool could read as soundings.

And a page torn from a notebook, covered in a fisherman's blunt handwriting, listing stores: water, biscuit, oil, lamp.

"You've been busy," Ashworth said, without greeting. He did not offer a chair. He remained standing, hands clasped behind him, a man who had learned that sitting invited comfort and comfort invited error. "Sit, if you like. I know you've been on your feet since noon."

"I'll stand," Mirabel said. "It keeps me honest. Sitting is how you get people to agree to things."

Something flickered at the corner of Ashworth's mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one, packed away with other effects he no longer used. "Then we understand each other already. You'll have guessed why you're here."

"The rope is Brine Ollie's. The chart is mine. The list of stores is Cobb Fenwick's, because Cobb has always been a man who writes down what he ought to burn." Mirabel kept their voice level, almost conversational. "You're wondering what I intend to do about it."

"What I *know,*" Ashworth said, "is that there is an unregistered vessel moored in Cutter's Hole. What I *suspect* is that she is intended to carry forty souls out of my jurisdiction without my authority, through a passage no pilot will admit exists, guided — this is the part I find most interesting — by the light of Gannet Point. My light. Requisitioned light." He let that sit. "Requisitioning a requisition. There's a word for that, Vane. Several words. Most of them end in front of a tribunal."

"And yet you haven't arrested me."

Ashworth turned to the shelf behind him and took down the ledger — *the* ledger, the one Mirabel had rebuilt name by name through those long weeks, every household of Gannet Point entered in their own careful hand, and beneath each entry, in the margins where a clerk would have left blank space, Mirabel's annotations.

He opened it to a page near the middle and set it on the table, angling the lamp toward it.

"*Marta Tull,*" he read. "*Baker. Widowed twice, both husbands drowned, which she says proves the sea owes her money. Hands that have fed half this town through two bad winters. Cannot swim, refuses to learn — 'the sea can chase me if it wants me.'*" He looked up. "Is that accurate?"

"To the letter. She said it at her second husband's wake. I wrote it down because I was afraid of forgetting the laugh she laughed after."

"*Cobb Fenwick. Smuggler when it suits, churchwarden when it pays, brave in the way of men who are loud before danger arrives and quiet during. Owes me eleven shillings and an apology, and I shall collect neither.*" Ashworth's finger rested under the line. "You knew him. You knew exactly what he was. And you put him in your book anyway, in your own hand, with your own judgment attached."

"A ledger that only holds numbers isn't a ledger. It's a cage with the door painted on."

"Don't philosophize at me, Keeper. I have been philosophized at by better-educated people and it did not slow them down." But he said it without heat, and he turned another page, and then another, and Mirabel realized with a small cold shock that he had been reading the annotations — all of them, or enough of them — and that he had not slept, judging by the candle stubs crowded around the desk like a congregation.

"*Wren Ashby,*" he read quietly. "*Eleven years old. Climbs anything, including sense. Her grandmother taught her letters before the cottage went, and she reads aloud to her brother at night so he hears words instead of water. Fiercer than she knows. Will keep.*"

The room was very still. Somewhere beyond the canvas partitions, a soldier coughed, and boots passed, and the rain worked patiently at the roof.

"You wrote *will keep*," Ashworth said. "About a child. As though she were a hull."

"As though she were a person who will endure. It's the highest thing I know how to write about anyone."

"And Tam?"

Mirabel hesitated. "You won't find Tam's annotation. I couldn't finish it. There are things I don't have the right to write down until they've happened, and that boy surviving this winter is one of them."

For a long moment Ashworth said nothing. He closed the ledger and laid both hands flat upon it, and Mirabel watched the man do arithmetic — not with numbers this time, but with something the army had never issued him a form for.

"My wife kept a garden journal," he said abruptly, as if the sentence had escaped custody. "Seven years of it. Rainfall, planting dates, which roses the frost took. When she died — the fever year, 'sixty-eight — I read it straight through in one night, cover to cover, and I want you to understand that I learned nothing useful from it whatsoever. No horticultural knowledge survives in that book worth the paper." His voice stayed perfectly flat, which was how Mirabel knew the cost of it. "What I learned was her. The woman, in the margins, between the entries. She wrote things like *the beans failed but Thomas ate dirt in the row anyway and laughed.*" He looked up. "Thomas was our son. He was four. He died the same month she did."

Mirabel did not speak. Some rooms punish speech.

"So when I tell you I have read your marginalia, Keeper, you may believe I understood precisely what I was holding." He straightened. "It changes nothing."

"It changes nothing," Mirabel repeated. "You sat up all night with a dead woman's garden journal, and you stand there and tell me it changes nothing."

"It changes nothing about the arithmetic!" And there it was at last, the crack in the plaster, brief as lightning. Ashworth mastered himself the way he mastered everything — quickly, completely, and visibly at a price. "There is one ship. She lifts anchor in fifty hours whether we are ready or not. She carries nine hundred tons, and souls weigh the same as ballast, however much you annotate them. The Kestrel, even loaded to her gunwales with your forty, leaves two hundred people standing on this rock when the storm comes. Two hundred, Vane. Your smuggler's boat is not mercy. It is a rounding error wearing a hero's coat. So you will tell me where she sails from, who crews her, and what passage you mean to run, and I will decide — *I* will decide — whether it fits the plan or breaks it."

"No."

"No?" Ashworth's eyebrows rose a fraction. People had said many things to Colonel Ashworth in his career. *No*, delivered mildly, by a lighthouse keeper in a coat patched at both elbows, was evidently new.

"No, Colonel, I won't tell you. Not because I don't trust you — though I don't — but because you've asked the wrong question." Mirabel stepped forward to the table at last, and laid their hand on the ledger, beside his. "You keep asking how many the boats will hold. Ask instead who they are. Because I can answer that one all night, and you cannot."

"Enlighten me."

"Marta Tull bakes bread for children who aren't hers. If she drowns, the first loaf baked in whatever town you ship these people to will taste of absence, and nobody will know why. Old Sam Pellowe can read weather in his knees, and his knees are more accurate than your barometer, and if he drowns, the next flood takes whoever settles wherever you settle them, because no one else will hear it coming. Young Nell Quill can mend a net in the dark. Josiah Crane can deliver a baby in worse than the dark. Widow Ahearn speaks three languages and sings in a fourth, and her songs are the only reason some of your soldiers haven't deserted yet — yes, I've noticed, I keep a light, I see everything that moves on this coast." Mirabel's voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing about it, Ashworth thought later; it never rose. "Every name in that book is load-bearing, Colonel. Pull one out and something above it comes down. That is the arithmetic I keep. Yours counts bodies. Mine counts what the bodies hold."

Ashworth was silent for the length of ten heartbeats. Then he said, quietly, "The beans failed but Thomas ate dirt in the row anyway and laughed."

"Yes," Mirabel said gently. "Exactly that."

They stood there a moment, two people with their hands on the same book, and the lamp between them burned steady, and outside the storm rehearsed its lines against the harbor stones.

Then Ashworth withdrew his hands, and the moment closed over like water.

"Here is what happens," he said. "I do not arrest you, for three reasons. First, because the town will not survive watching their keeper marched away in irons, and a riot on the quay costs more berths than the Kestrel ever could. Second, because I still require your signature certifying the final list, and a prisoner's signature is worth less than none. And third—" he paused, and did not finish the third reason, and Mirabel understood it anyway: *because I sat up all night reading your book and I am no longer entirely sure which of us is right, and a man who is unsure must not act finally.*

"Instead," Ashworth continued, "you will return to Gannet Point. You will keep your light. And you will be watched. Sergeant Hollis will establish a detail at the lighthouse — ostensibly to assist with storm preparations. You will not leave the point without escort. You will not signal the harbor. You will not send messages by child, by boat, by bird, or by any other cleverness your coast breeds like weeds." He held up the page of Fenwick's handwriting. "This came to me this evening. From a man trading fear for berths. I want you to know what it cost him, and what it bought, and I want you to consider whether the Kestrel plan survives being known."

"Cobb sold us out."

"Cobb purchased his family's places with your secret. Whether the purchase clears depends on what I do next, and what I do next depends—" Ashworth stopped. Looked at the ledger. Looked at the window, where the rain ran down like the sea trying again. "Depends on factors," he finished, which was not what he had meant to say, and both of them heard it.

Mirabel picked up the coil of rope, weighed it, and set it back down. "May I say one thing before I go, Colonel? Off the record. Unannotated."

"If you must."

"Ede — my spouse — used to say that a man who counts everything is usually counting the wrong thing last." Mirabel pulled on their coat. "Count the storm, Colonel. Count it honestly. It doesn't care about your tonnage, and it is coming early."

At the door, Ashworth spoke once more, and his voice had gone quiet and strange. "Vane. The girl. Wren. *Will keep* — you wrote it as certainty. How do you know?"

Mirabel smiled, and it was Ede's smile, borrowed and worn soft. "Because she has already stolen something from this building and gotten clean away with it, Colonel. Check your berth list. Or don't — she'd take that as encouragement."

And they walked out into the rain, leaving the colonel alone with his lamp, his ledger, and the sudden, unwelcome suspicion that he had just been interrogated too.

Behind him, on the desk, the candle stubs guttered. Ashworth did not move for a long time. Then he opened the ledger to the page marked *Fenwick, Cobb*, and read the line about the eleven shillings and the apology, and reached for his pen, and — finding himself, for the first time in thirty years of service, unable to decide what a record was *for* — wrote nothing at all.

Out on the cliff path, Mirabel felt the paper boat crackle in their pocket, *come back*, and quickened their step toward the light — where, in the gallery above, a small figure was already scanning the customs house windows, counting the candles, planning the night.
