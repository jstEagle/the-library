## 27. Wren Steals the List

Wren had learned three things about Sergeant Hollis since the soldiers came to Gannet Point.

First: he polished his boots at exactly half past six every evening, sitting on the customs house steps where the lamplight fell just so, and he took longer over it than any man needed to take over boots.

Second: he could not resist a child who looked cold. This was not a weakness he advertised. It was a weakness he had, which was better.

Third: on Thursday nights, after the changing of the watch, he walked the harbor road alone, all the way to the breakwater and back, and he stood looking out at the water like a man reading a letter he couldn't answer.

Wren knew these things because knowing things was what she did now. It had started with chalk marks and grown from there, the way a leak starts as a drip and ends by taking the roof off. She was nine years old and she had learned that adults believed children were furniture — present, occasionally in the way, but never actually *seeing* anything. She had decided to let them go on believing it for as long as it suited her.

Tonight it suited her very well indeed.

She crouched behind Marta Tull's woodpile, watching Hollis's lantern bob along the harbor road toward the breakwater. Half past eight. He'd be gone fifteen minutes, twenty if the wind was bad enough to make him feel philosophical. Wren didn't know many soldiers, but she'd watched this one enough to know that wind made him stand stiller, not quicker.

The customs house door was locked. Wren had counted on that too.

She went around the back, past the barrels of requisitioned salt fish, to the little window that sat low in the stone — the one the clerks used for passing out forms without getting up, because soldiers were lazy even when they were terrifying. The latch inside was a simple hook. Wren had spent two evenings last week fishing for it through the gap with Tam's hatpin, and Tam had stood lookout both times without being asked, holding his coat closed against the wind, saying nothing, because Tam said nothing about anything anymore except — lately, sometimes — with his hands.

The hook gave. The window swung open with a creak that stopped her heart for a full three seconds. Then nothing. No boots, no shout, no lantern swinging back up the road.

She wriggled through headfirst, hung by her knees for a moment like a bat, and dropped onto the clerk's desk, scattering a pen tray with a clatter she smothered with her whole body, lying flat across the mess of papers like a starfish made of guilt.

The customs house at night smelled different than by day. By day it smelled of ink and wet wool and the colonel's pipe tobacco, sharp and official. At night it smelled of the sea coming up through the cellar grates — salt and rot and the memory of the flood that had drowned the first ledger and started all of this. Somewhere below, water dripped in a slow rhythm, *plip... plip*, like the house itself counting.

Wren slid off the desk. Her eyes were getting used to the dark. There: the colonel's office door, at the end of the hall, shut tight. And beside it — no. Not beside it. Inside it.

That was the problem. That was the whole entire problem.

*The list is sealed.* Ashworth had said it in the square, loud enough for everyone: sealed with wax, signed by Mirabel Vane, locked in his office until the boats came. Nobody would know their fate until the moment they were ordered into it. That was the cruelty of it, Wren thought. Not just deciding who lived where — deciding that nobody got to *know*. You'd walk to the harbor carrying your bundle and you wouldn't know until you saw the manifest whether you were boarding or being left.

Well. Sealed was a word for wax. Wax could be lifted. Anyone who'd ever stolen jam from a sealed crock knew that.

But the door was locked too, and Wren had no hairpin trick for a proper lock, and she was beginning to understand that tonight's plan had a hole in it shaped like a door when —

Boots. On gravel. Coming around the front of the building.

Wren dove under the clerk's desk, folding herself into the dark space beneath it among the dust and a dead moth, and pulled the chair in close behind her like a curtain.

Hollis came in the front door. She heard him shake rain off his cape — when had it started raining? — and stamp his boots, and then he did something strange. He didn't go upstairs to the guard room. He stood in the entry hall for a long moment, breathing, and then he said, quietly, to nobody:

"Two hundred and eleven."

Wren frowned in the dark. Under the desk, she mouthed the number back. Two hundred and eleven.

Then his boots came down the hall — toward her — and paused right at the clerk's desk, so close she could have reached out and touched his heel. Her heart was hammering so hard she was sure it would rattle the floorboards. Above her head, Hollis sighed, a long tired sound, and picked up the cold teapot, and poured himself a cup of yesterday's tea, and drank it standing there, an inch of oak plank away from a nine-year-old burglar.

He set the cup down. He walked on, past the colonel's office — pausing there too, one boot squeaking, a silence long enough to mean something — and continued to the records room at the back, where the duplicate ledgers lived, and began, of all things, to work. Wren heard him sit, strike a match, light the little lamp they kept on the records table, and start turning papers. He was checking manifests. Rechecking them. She'd seen him do it before, always at night, always alone, always with that same look of a man reading a letter he couldn't answer.

And then Wren understood something that changed the shape of the whole night.

He'd left the front hall lamp unlit. He always lit it. Every night, without fail, so the corridor wasn't pitch dark when the watch changed. Tonight he hadn't. Tonight he'd walked straight past the colonel's door in the dark, drunk his terrible tea, and gone to the back room where he could hear everything and see nothing.

Maybe it meant nothing. Maybe the man was just tired.

Or maybe — and Wren held this thought the way you hold a live coal, carefully, ready to drop it — maybe Sergeant Hollis, somewhere between the breakwater and the front door, had decided he needed twenty minutes of not seeing anything.

She crept out from under the desk on hands and knees, keeping below the line of the records room doorway, and crossed the hall to the colonel's office like a crab with a mission.

* * *

The lock was iron and it was serious and it defeated her completely, which was fine, because she hadn't actually come for the lock. She'd come for the window.

Ashworth's office had a window too — bigger than the clerks', facing the seaward wall, because the colonel liked to look at the harbor while he did his arithmetic. Wren had noted it weeks ago, filed it away with everything else. The trouble was the sill was ten feet up and outside it was nothing but wet stone wall, and Wren was nine, and gravity had opinions.

But the woodpile behind the salt barrels put her within reach of the drainpipe, and the drainpipe — she'd tested it Tuesday, hanging from it with both hands while Tam watched anxiously from the shadows — held.

Getting out through the clerks' window and around the building in the rain without making noise took her four minutes and cost her a scraped elbow. Climbing the drainpipe in the dark with wet hands took six more and cost her most of her courage. But at the top, braced on the narrow sill with the rain running down her collar, she found the window latched only by the simple swivel catch that colonels who trusted walls used, and her fingers found it, and turned it, and the window swung open inward onto darkness and the smell of pipe smoke and sealing wax.

She slipped inside and landed soft as an apology.

The office was black except for the gray glow of the storm-light through the sea-facing glass. She could make out the great desk, the chair, the map of the coast pinned to the wall with its red lines like wounds. And on the desk, square and heavy and exactly where rumor said it would be, the berth list — folded once, sealed along the edge with a fat drop of green wax, the regimental stamp pressed into it.

Sealed was a word for wax. Wren had brought a knife. Not a big one — the little paring knife Marta Tull kept for apples, borrowed without asking, which Wren was beginning to suspect was going to become a theme of her life. She warmed the blade against the lamp chimney — unlit, but the metal remembered the day — no, that wouldn't do; she simply worked the thin edge under the wax slowly, patiently, lifting it whole the way Tam lifted shells off rocks, and it came up clean as a dream, one perfect green wafer with the stamp intact, and the paper beneath opened without a tear.

She spread the list flat on the desk and read it by the gray light, and her stomach did something complicated and cold.

There it all was. Every household in Gannet Point, numbered, berthed, sorted. *Tull, M. — baker — 4 berths — priority trade.* *Fenwick, C. — 3 berths — note: petition pending.* Down the columns, names she'd known her whole life reduced to ink and arithmetic. And near the bottom, in Mirabel's careful hand, the entry she'd been afraid of and had come anyway to see:

*Vane, M. — keeper — retained, essential post (lighthouse).* And beneath it, added in someone else's ink, someone else's hand: *Dependents: 2. Berths: granted per bargain of the 14th — separate vessels.*

Separate vessels.

Wren read it three times. *Separate vessels.* Tam on one boat, her on another, and Mirabel not listed as going anywhere at all. The bargain. The thing Mirabel had traded the reef survey for, the thing they'd told the children about in careful gentle words at the kitchen table — *you'll be safe, that's what matters* — and here it was in ink: safe, and split, and scattered across the sea like cards from a dropped deck.

Her own name looked small. *Wren — age 9 — berth 3, Kestrel.* Tam's name smaller. *Tam — age 6 — berth 11, transport Mary Ellery.*

No. She pressed her fists against her eyes until the gray light bloomed. No, this wasn't — she hadn't come to cry about it. She'd come to *copy* it.

Because here was the thing Wren had figured out, the thing that had kept her awake two nights running while Tam folded his silent paper boats: a secret list is a weapon. A shared list is just news. Ashworth thought the seal made him strong. But a town that knows its numbers can argue with them. A town that doesn't know can only wait. And waiting, Wren had learned from watching her grandmother die and her cottage drown and her brother go quiet, is how people drown on dry land.

She had no pencil. She'd planned for that too — she had Tam's stub of charcoal from the boat drawings, and she copied the list onto the backs of six sheets of paper she'd smuggled in her coat, writing fast and crooked in the dark, missing letters, not caring. Names. Numbers. Boats. When she finished, she refolded the original, breathed on the underside of the wax wafer, and pressed it back down. It held. It wouldn't fool anyone who looked hard, but it might fool everyone until morning, and morning was all she needed.

The drainpipe down was worse than the drainpipe up. The window closed. The wax unbroken. And Sergeant Hollis was still in the records room, still turning pages, when Wren slipped out through the clerks' window, replaced the hook with the hatpin, and dropped into the rain.

As she ran, bent double, along the wall toward the salt barrels, she passed the records room's little window. Through the gap in the shutter, lamplight lay yellow across the floor, and she saw Hollis's shadow on it, seated, unmoving. And she would have sworn — then and always after — that as she passed, the shadow's head turned slowly toward the window, held there a moment, and turned deliberately away.

* * *

Tam was waiting in the boathouse, as arranged, sitting on an upturned dory with his knees drawn up, dry under an old tarpaulin. When Wren tumbled in, dripping, he didn't ask *did you get it*. He just held out his hands, and she gave him the charcoal copies, and he looked at them for a long time — Tam, who didn't read much yet but who knew every name in town the way he knew the shapes of boats — and then he nodded once, gravely, like a magistrate.

"Paper," Wren whispered.

He reached under the tarpaulin and produced it. Weeks of it. Weeks and weeks of it. Folded paper boats, dozens of them, stacked in careful ranks inside an old flour sack — the fleet he'd been building all this time, one boat for every name, though nobody had known that but him. Wren stared at the sack and felt her throat go tight.

"We'll need more," she managed. "For the copies."

Tam tipped the sack toward her, an offer and an argument in one gesture. Then he unfolded one boat, smoothed it flat, and held out the charcoal.

They worked by no light at all, by touch and habit, copying the list over and over until the words blurred and Wren's fingers were gray to the wrist. Tam folded each finished page into a boat — his folds had gotten beautiful, crisp as origami, grief taught him precision — and by midnight they had forty-one paper boats, each one carrying the same cargo: every name in Gannet Point, every berth, every number, every cold piece of the colonel's arithmetic laid open to the weather.

"Now," Wren whispered, "every doorstep."

* * *

It took them until nearly dawn.

They split the town into halves — Wren took the harbor side, Tam the lane side, though she trailed him twice without his noticing, because he was six and the night was enormous. They moved door to door in the rain, laying a paper boat on each step, weighting some with pebbles against the wind. At the Tulls', Wren hesitated, then placed hers dead center, square to the door, because Marta deserved exactness. At the Fenwicks', she almost didn't knock-flat the boat at all — Cobb had threatened Mirabel, Cobb had sold his neighbors short — but in the end she set it down anyway, square and fair, because the list belonged to everyone, even the ones she couldn't stand. Especially, maybe, the ones she couldn't stand. Fairness that stops at your friends isn't fairness. Her grandmother had said something like that once, about bread.

At the lighthouse path, she saved the best boat for last — the one she'd folded herself, double-folded hull, the copy with the fewest mistakes — and set it on Mirabel's doorstep, weighted with a white pebble from the beach, the kind Ede used to collect. Let Mirabel find it at first light. Let them read their own name, and the children's names, and *separate vessels*, and know that the whole town would be reading it too, all at once, together.

By the time the sky went the color of dishwater, forty-one boats sat on forty-one doorsteps in Gannet Point, and the rain was easing, and Wren stood at the top of the harbor road with Tam's cold hand in hers and watched the first windows light up.

First one lamp. Then another. Then a door opening — Marta Tull, in her shawl, bending to pick up the little boat on her step, unfolding it, standing very still. Then her voice, carrying down the lane in the quiet before dawn, rough with something that wasn't anger:

"WILLIAM! Wake up. Wake up — it's here. It's *here*, love, we can *see it*—"

Doors opening all down the street now, one after another, lamps blooming like the town catching fire gently, from the inside. People standing on their thresholds in the rain-washed morning, holding their paper boats, calling to each other across fences and lanes — *what does yours say? who's on yours?* — voices joining, overlapping, rising, until the sound coming up out of Gannet Point wasn't weeping and wasn't whispering but something Wren had never heard the town make before, something with a spine in it.

Down at the customs house, she saw, lights were blazing too. Soldiers on the steps. A sentry running for the colonel's quarters at a pace that suggested the morning had already gone wrong.

Tam tugged her sleeve. He pointed at the harbor, at the gray water, and then he opened his mouth — Tam, whose voice had been a locked room for months — and said, clear as a bell across the waking street:

"Everyone knows now."

Wren squeezed his hand and grinned at him, rain-wet and fierce and nine years old, as below them the whole town stepped out of its doors holding its own name in its own hands.

"Yes," she said. "And now we see what they do with it."

What they did with it, starting at dawn and not stopping, was begin to walk — all of them, every doorstep emptied of its boat and its family behind it — toward the harbor, where the evacuation ship rode at anchor, and where Colonel Ashworth stood on the customs house steps holding a broken seal in his fist, watching two hundred and eleven souls come down the hill together.
