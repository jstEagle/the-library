## 28. The Town That Would Not Be Numbered

Mirabel woke to a silence that was not silence at all.

The lighthouse had been theirs long enough that every sound in it had a name: the groan of the tower in an easterly, the tick of the cooling lamp, the particular creak of the third stair that Ede had always meant to fix and Mirabel had never been able to bring themselves to. But this was a new sound — or rather, the absence of one — and it pulled them up out of sleep with their heart already going.

No soldiers on the lane below.

For three days, since Wren's paper boats had bloomed on every doorstep like strange white flowers, the patrol had come past the lighthouse at first light and again at dusk. This morning the lane held nothing but gulls and the smell of coming weather — a smell Mirabel knew the way other people knew their own hands. Low sky. Green light. The sea pulling back from itself.

They dressed in the dark, careful of the children sleeping in the loft — Tam curled around a fleet of folded boats, Wren with her boots still on, as if she expected at any moment to be needed somewhere urgently, which, Mirabel reflected, she generally was — and went down into the grey.

The harbor was full.

Not of boats. Of people.

They stood along the quay in ranks that had no order and all the order in the world: fishermen with their oilskins still dripping from the morning's last haul, the baker Marta Tull with flour on her sleeves because apparently the end of the world was no reason for the bread to go unbaked, old Brine Ollie leaning on his crooked stick with the expression of a man who had seen every kind of trouble the coast could invent and found most of it overrated. Children. Grandmothers. The choir, someone had fetched the choir, which struck Mirabel as either the most hopeful or the most defiant thing they had ever seen.

Nobody spoke. Nobody wept. They simply stood, four hundred souls arranged along the stone, facing the *Persephone* where she lay at anchor with her boats lowered and her gangway down and her captain visible at the rail, looking — Mirabel thought — like a man who had been handed a puzzle box by God and told the tide would not wait.

At the head of the quay stood Colonel Ashworth, red coat immaculate against the grey, and beside him Sergeant Hollis, whose face did what Hollis's face always did, which was to be a wall with something moving behind it.

Mirabel came down the path slowly. The crowd parted for them without being asked — not deference exactly, more the way water parts around a known stone — and closed again behind.

"Keeper Vane." Ashworth did not turn. His voice carried the way cold iron carries sound. "You will inform these people that boarding begins now, by the list."

"They know the list," Mirabel said. "I wrote it."

"Then you will inform them that refusal constitutes obstruction of a military evacuation, and that I am empowered to—"

"With respect, Colonel." Mirabel stepped up onto the low sea-wall so that they stood a head above him, which was not a thing one did to a colonel, but the sea-wall belonged to Gannet Point and so, this morning, did everyone standing on it. "You're empowered to do a great many things. What you are not empowered to do is count."

That got him to turn. His eyes were pale and patient and terribly tired, and Mirabel saw — as they had begun to see over these weeks, in lamplight, across ledgers — the arithmetic running behind them, tireless as a millwheel.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You counted us. Four hundred and eleven souls, tonnage for three hundred and six, berths assigned by usefulness. You made a sum of us. And sums have answers, Colonel. But towns aren't sums." Mirabel looked down the quay, at the faces turned up toward the two of them. "Ask them."

Ashworth asked them, in his way. He raised his voice and said, "The ship cannot carry you all. Every soul who refuses a berth is a soul who drowns when the storm comes tonight. Is that what you want? To drown together?"

And Marta Tull — broad, floured, unafraid — answered for all of them, in the voice she used for telling customers the bread wasn't ready yet and they'd simply have to wait.

"We don't mean to drown at all, Colonel. We mean to leave together, or not leave at all. There's a second boat. There's the Kestrel, and there's forty more of us she can carry, and there's a keeper here who knows the reef better than the reef knows itself. Your sum was wrong. It left out the Kestrel, and it left out us."

"The Kestrel is an unregistered vessel crewed by smugglers, and the passage through the reef has never been sounded under load—"

"It's been sounded," said Brine Ollie, pleasantly. "Three nights ago. By moonlight, with your own sergeant holding the lead line. Lovely lad. Very thorough."

Mirabel watched Hollis not move. It was a masterful performance of stillness. Only his ears went slightly pink.

Ashworth's jaw tightened. For a long moment the only sounds were the halyards knocking against the Persephone's mast and the gulls arguing overhead about whose fault everything was.

"You realize," he said at last, quietly, to Mirabel alone, "that if I order my men to load these people by force, some of them will resist, and men will be hurt, and the loading will take until noon, and the storm will take the rest."

"I know."

"And if I stand aside and let them attempt the reef in open boats in rising weather, half of them may drown within sight of their own chimneys."

"I know that too."

"Then what," said the Colonel, and here his voice did something Mirabel had never heard it do, which was to fray, very slightly, at one edge, "do you propose?"

* * *

What Mirabel proposed was this:

All hands to both vessels. The Persephone takes her three hundred and six — no list, no priority, no tradesmen before grandmothers, just the manifest of a town leaving home. The Kestrel takes the remaining hundred-odd, crowded past all decency, and runs the reef behind the light. And the light stays lit, and the keeper stays at it, until the last hull clears the point.

"You'll need a pilot for the Kestrel," Ashworth said. "Ollie is too old, and half your fishermen are drunk by nine, and—"

"Hollis sounded the passage," Mirabel said. "Hollis can pilot it."

Every head on the quay turned to the sergeant, who wore the expression of a man who had just discovered that his conscience had been taking notes.

"That is not—" Hollis began.

"He's army," Ashworth said flatly. "He's not a sailor."

"He held a lead line in the dark for three nights and memorized every sounding," Mirabel said. "I've watched him do it. He recites them in his sleep. Ask him."

"I did not recite them in my— I reviewed them. Aloud. Once." Hollis cleared his throat. "Colonel. With respect. The soundings are correct. I'd stake my commission on the channel."

"You haven't a commission, Sergeant, you have a grudge against comfortable beds." Ashworth pressed two fingers to the bridge of his nose. When he took them away, the frayed edge was gone and the iron was back, but it was a different iron somehow — heated, perhaps. "Very well. Both vessels. Full complement. If the Kestrel founders on the reef with a hundred souls aboard, Keeper Vane, I want you to understand that every drowned name will be written in a ledger you built."

"Yes," Mirabel said. "I've thought about that. Most nights."

"And you proceed anyway."

"We proceed anyway."

Ashworth looked out at the town — at the choir, who had begun, softly and without anyone's permission, to sing something old about the sea giving back what it borrows; at Tam Vane's small hand gripping the hem of Marta Tull's apron; at Wren standing on a bollard like a girl inspecting her own navy — and something passed over his face that Mirabel would spend a long time afterward trying to name. It was not surrender. It was closer to recognition: the look of a man encountering an equation he could not solve, and finding, to his own astonishment, that he did not entirely mind.

"Mr. Hollis," he said. "Pilot the Kestrel. Take Ollie aboard as adviser and keep him away from the rum. The Persephone loads in family groups, oldest aboard first, and if any man under my command marks a door or reads a number aloud again, I will personally see him posted to a lighthouse so remote he'll forget the word 'order.'" A pause. "Keeper. Light your lamp. Whatever hour it wants lighting."

"It'll want lighting by four," Mirabel said. "Maybe earlier. The barometer's falling like a repentant man."

"Then we have until four." Ashworth turned to his waiting officers and began, in a voice like a well-oiled hinge, to issue orders — and Gannet Point, which had spent a month being counted, began instead to board.

* * *

It went, Mirabel thought later, the way such things go: not cleanly, not quietly, but with a strange fierce tenderness that nobody had planned.

Marta loaded her ovens' whole morning's work into baskets and refused payment from everyone, which caused a small crisis among the townsfolk, several of whom felt that dignity required paying even now. Cobb Fenwick came down the quay with his family and his chin up, having traded his secret for their places and found that the secret had bought him a seat on a boat that was now carrying everybody regardless, and stood for a moment in front of Mirabel with his jaw working like a man chewing a sentence that wouldn't go down.

"I'd have let them drown, maybe," Cobb said finally. "Some of them. The ones who—" He stopped. Started again. "You didn't put me off the list. After."

"You were on the list before you ever betrayed anything," Mirabel said. "Names don't work the way you think they do."

Cobb nodded twice, sharply, the way men nod when they cannot speak, and took his wife's arm, and boarded.

Tam would not get in the boat. This became apparent at the eleventh hour, when the Kestrel's crew — six smugglers of advanced years and cheerful disrepute, who greeted their sudden legitimacy with the delight of actors handed a better play — began passing children down over the side, and Tam planted himself on the quay with his fists in his pockets and his mouth set in the line it had held since the flood took his grandmother.

Wren knelt in front of him. "Tam. We talked about this."

He shook his head.

"You promised me."

Another shake, smaller.

Wren leaned close and whispered something, too low for the wind to carry it to Mirabel on the sea-wall — though Mirabel caught the shape of it, three words, and one of them was *stay* — and whatever she said broke him loose, because he unfolded one fist and pressed something into her palm: a paper boat, small and perfect, and then climbed into Marta's arms like a much younger boy than he was, and buried his face in her shoulder, and did not look back, because he was eight, and looking back was more than eight years could hold.

Wren stood a moment with the paper boat in her hand. Then she looked up at the lighthouse — at Mirabel — with an expression that contained, fully formed, the woman she was going to become, and shouted over the wind:

"You keep it lit! All night! I'll know if you don't!"

"I should hope so!" Mirabel called back, and Wren grinned, and went over the side, and was aboard.

By two o'clock the Persephone sat deep and heavy in the water, black with passengers from truck to waterline, and the Kestrel rode beside her like a faithful dog packed past sense, and the sky to the southeast had gone the color of a bruise deciding where to land.

* * *

On the customs house steps, Ashworth signed the final manifest himself, in a hand that had grown less precise as the day went on, and found that the document now read less like an evacuation and more like a census of a town in exile. Four hundred and eleven names. Every one accounted for. He checked twice, because precision was the only prayer he knew, and then folded the pages into his coat, over his breast, where a man might carry letters from home.

Sergeant Hollis found him there.

"Kestrel's ready, sir. Ollie's aboard. He says — and I quote — 'if the young officer steers where I spit, we'll live.'"

"A rousing endorsement." Ashworth buttoned his coat. "Your orders are simple. Clear the point, round the reef on the range of the light, and make for the mainland anchorage. Do not improvise. Do not be brave. Be accurate."

"Yes, sir." Hollis hesitated. "And you, sir? Persephone's berth list has room for one more officer."

"My place," Ashworth said, "is with the operation until its conclusion. The conclusion includes the light going dark on an empty coast. Someone senior should witness that the requisition was executed in full." He said it in the voice he used for reports, but they both heard what was underneath it, which was plainer: *someone must stay, because I sent them all away, and I will not be a man who counts people onto ships and then sails off before knowing how the sum comes out.*

Hollis saluted. Halfway through the salute, without quite deciding to, he added: "Sir — the keeper's good people. In case that's useful information, wherever you've filed it."

"Noted, Sergeant. Go."

Hollis went, at a run, down to his impossible boat.

Ashworth stood on the steps and watched the harbor empty. It emptied strangely — not like a wound, not like a defeat, but like a held breath finally let out. Families went over the sides in bunches. The choir kept singing until someone's husband told them, lovingly, to save their breath for bailing. And through all of it the crowd moved with a steadiness that no list had given them, because they had chosen it, all of it, together, and it is a curious fact of human beings that they will walk calmly into a storm they have agreed upon who would riot over one imposed upon them.

The last boat left the quay at half past three. At ten minutes to four, the first spits of rain came slantwise out of the southeast, hard as flung gravel.

And above the point, right on time, the great lens of the Gannet Point light swung its beam into the thickening murk and began to turn — steady, unhurried, indifferent to arithmetic — and Ashworth, alone on the steps of an empty customs house with a storm coming down on him like the fist of God, realized that he had gotten exactly what his orders required.

Everyone saved, and himself marooned with the one person in Gannet Point who had never once obeyed him.

He picked up his case and started up the cliff path toward the lighthouse, coat collar up, rain in his eyes, and told himself firmly that the next twelve hours would be a matter of procedure.

The barometer, had he consulted it, would have disagreed.
