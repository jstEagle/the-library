## 29. Hollis's Choice

Sergeant Elias Hollis had served the Crown for nineteen years, and in that time he had learned three things with absolute certainty: that orders were to be obeyed, that obedience was what separated a soldier from a mob, and that a man who started asking questions about his orders would very soon find himself with no orders at all — only questions.

He was thinking about all three of these certainties at half past five in the morning, standing on the harbor quay of Gannet Point with forty men behind him and an entire town in front of him, none of whom were moving.

"Disperse them," Colonel Ashworth had said. His voice had not been angry. That was the worst part. It had been the voice of a man reading out figures from a column. "By force if necessary. We sail when the manifest is full, and the manifest is full when they board."

The town stood shoulder to shoulder along the quay and up the sloping street beyond it, every soul Hollis had spent two weeks cataloguing in ink. The baker Marta Tull stood at the front with flour still on her apron, as though she'd come straight from her ovens, which Hollis suspected she had; there were loaves stacked in baskets at her feet, and she was handing them down the line to children the way a quartermaster distributes rations — calmly, without comment, as if bread at dawn before a possible massacre were simply how things were done in Gannet Point.

Behind her, old Brine Ollie leaned on a gaff he probably wasn't supposed to have. Behind him, Cobb Fenwick — who had sold them all out and knew it, and whose face had the gray look of a man who has discovered that treachery buys you nothing but a front-row seat — stood with his family pressed close against him.

And everywhere, everywhere, chalk numbers. On doors, on shutters, on lintels. *Three. One. Five.* The arithmetic of the evacuation, written on every home like a diagnosis.

Except that overnight, the numbers had changed.

Hollis had seen it first thing, walking the town before dawn to check the pickets. Doors that yesterday read *two* now read *one*. A house marked *four* now bore a careful, smaller *three*, the original figure scrubbed at the edges where someone had licked their thumb. And on the customs house wall itself, in a child's hand — he knew the hand; he'd have known it anywhere by then — someone had written across the whole broad face of the building, in letters two feet high:

**ALL OR NONE.**

"You see this?" Private Denny said beside him, nodding at the wall. He sounded almost admiring. "They've all done it, Sarge. Every door. They've cut themselves down so somebody else can go. You can't fill a manifest off a town that keeps shrinking itself."

"I can see it," Hollis said.

"What do we do?"

What do we do. Nineteen years, and nobody had ever asked him that question in quite that way — not *what are your orders*, Sergeant, but *what do we do*, as if the answer might be something other than the orders.

"Fall in," Hollis said. "Bayonets fixed. But—" and here he heard his own voice drop, heard it become something private, meant only for the file of men around him, "—fixed and *down*. Nobody levels anything unless I say the word. And I am not going to say the word."

Denny looked at him. Young Denny, who'd joined up at seventeen because his father's farm had drowned too, though not by salt water — by debt, which drowns slower and takes longer to notice.

"And if the colonel says the word?"

Hollis looked out at the town. At Marta Tull tearing a loaf and giving half to a soldier at the end of the picket line, who took it because refusing seemed somehow more mutinous than accepting. At a girl — Wren, the lighthouse girl, the one who moved through this town like weather — standing on a barrel with her brother's hand in hers, watching the soldiers with the flat patient gaze of someone waiting for adults to arrive at an obvious conclusion.

"If the colonel says the word," Hollis said, "then the colonel will have to be looking at me when he says it."

* * *

The sun came up over the flooded fields east of town, turning all that stolen water to hammered copper, and still nobody boarded.

Ashworth came down to the quay himself at seven. Hollis watched him cross the square at his usual pace — unhurried, precise, a man who believed pace was a form of authority — and noted that the colonel had stopped carrying his ledger under his arm. He carried nothing. Perhaps he had realized, somewhere in the night, that documents were no longer the currency here.

"Colonel," Hollis said, saluting.

"Sergeant." Ashworth surveyed the crowd, the chalked doors, the enormous child's lettering on his own headquarters. Something moved behind his eyes — Hollis had learned to read the small weathers of that face — but the voice stayed level. "They believe this is a negotiation."

"No, sir. I don't think they believe anything of the kind. I think it's a fact they're stating."

"A fact." Ashworth turned to face the town, and raised his voice, and it carried the way trained voices carry, flat and far across water. "People of Gannet Point. The ship *Providence* carries two hundred and ten berths. This town holds two hundred and sixty-one souls. The mathematics have not changed since yesterday, and they will not change tomorrow. Board now, in the order of the list, or the list will be enforced."

From the crowd, Marta Tull spoke — not loudly, but she didn't need to; the morning was windless and her voice went straight down the quay like a stone dropped down a well.

"Enforced how, Colonel? Will you count us onto your boat while the rest stand on this quay and watch? Will you post guards on the ones left behind? Will you write their names in your book one last time, for the record?"

"The record," Ashworth said, "is what survives. I am trying to ensure that two hundred and ten of you survive."

"And we're trying," Marta said, "to ensure that two hundred and sixty-one do. You keep saying the boat won't hold us all. We're saying you haven't looked hard enough for another boat. There *is* another boat. Everyone knows it. Your own sergeant knows it. Ask him."

Every head among the soldiers turned toward Hollis. Two hundred and sixty-one pairs of eyes, plus forty.

He felt it like a hand on his collar.

Ashworth did not ask him. That, too, Hollis noted. The colonel simply looked at him for a long moment — the look of a man filing something away in a drawer he would open later, alone — and then said, quietly, for Hollis alone:

"My orders stand. Clear the quay. Begin loading with the priority names. If they resist, resist them. I will have this harbor functioning by noon."

"Yes, sir," Hollis said.

It was the correct answer. It was the only answer a sergeant could give. And as Ashworth walked back up toward the customs house, Hollis stood on the quay with the correct answer in his mouth like a coin he couldn't swallow, and thought about Ede Vane.

He'd never met Ede Vane. Ede was a year dead, drowned somewhere off this coast, and everything Hollis knew of him came secondhand — from Mirabel's careful silences, from a coat hanging by the lighthouse door that even the children stepped around, from a single line Hollis had once read upside-down on Mirabel's desk in a letter too old and softened to be anything but sacred: *keep the light, and it keeps you.*

That was the whole difference between them, Hollis thought. Mirabel had been given a light to keep and had understood it was a covenant. Hollis had been given orders to keep and had understood them as — what? A ladder. A wall. A way to be a good man without ever having to decide what goodness was.

Nineteen years, and the orders had always decided for him.

Until now.

* * *

At eight o'clock, the wind changed.

It came around to the northeast all at once, the way weather does on this coast when it means business, and the copper light on the floodwater went gray, and every fisherman on that quay lifted their head at the same instant like a flock of birds. Hollis saw Marta Tull close her eyes. Saw old Ollie spit into the water and mutter something that had the rhythm of a prayer wearing a curse's clothes.

The great storm was coming early. Everyone on that quay could taste it — iron and iodine and pressure, the flavor of a sky deciding.

Which meant the machinery of the requisition was jammed twice over now: once by a town that would not be numbered, and once by a heaven that kept its own ledger and was about to audit everyone.

Ashworth reappeared at the customs house door and gave the order a second time, formally, in front of witnesses, so there could be no confusion afterward about who had given it and who had received it.

"Sergeant Hollis. Disperse the crowd. Load the vessel according to the sealed manifest. By force."

And he handed Hollis the duplicate list.

That was the detail Hollis would remember afterward — not the bayonets, not the crowd, not the storm-light — but the moment when Colonel Ashworth held out the folded paper, the certified copy of the berth list, the document that decided everything, and waited for his sergeant to take it. Because a dispersal required a manifest. Because the army did nothing, ever, without its paper.

Hollis took it.

The paper was warm from the colonel's hand. That seemed impossible and yet it was true; the paper was warm, and weighed almost nothing, and contained two hundred and ten lives arranged in descending order of usefulness, and it trembled slightly in the wind coming off the northeast like a living thing trying to get free.

"Sir," Hollis said. "Before I carry out this order, I want the record to show that I asked a question."

"You may ask it."

"There's a second vessel. The *Kestrel*. She can take forty more, maybe fifty, through the eastern passage. The passage is soundable. The light at Gannet Point can mark the range. I know because I helped sound it." He heard his own voice go steady, steadier than it had any right to be. "I'm telling you this on the record, sir, because I should have told you days ago, and I didn't, because I was following orders instead of doing my job. My job was never to make lists. My job was to look after people. I appear to have confused the two."

A silence. The wind pulled at both their coats.

Ashworth's face did something complicated and brief — Hollis would think about it for years — something that was not anger at all, but recognition, the look of a man seeing a mirror crack diagonally.

"That," Ashworth said quietly, "is insubordination."

"Yes, sir."

"But not the act itself. Disperse them, Sergeant."

"No, sir."

The word came out of Hollis the way a tooth comes out — awful, inevitable, followed immediately by an astonishing lightness. Nineteen years of yes, collapsed into one syllable of no, and the sky did not fall. Only the wind rose, and the town held its breath, and forty soldiers behind him stood very still, and not one of them — he could feel it at his back, the whole silent weight of them — not one of them moved to obey the colonel instead.

Ashworth looked past him at the men. "Corporal Ames. Take the manifest and—"

"Colonel," Hollis said, and unfolded the paper.

He held it up so the town could see it — two hundred and ten names, the arithmetic of who deserved to live, printed in his own careful clerk's hand — and he held it into the wind, and struck a match from his pocket, and touched flame to the corner.

It caught fast. Paper always does; it wants to burn. Hollis had learned that as a boy, burning letters from a girl in Portsmouth. Names curl quickest of all. He watched *Fenwick* blacken and lift, watched a column of skilled trades bloom orange and fly apart into sparks over the harbor, and the sparks went up and inland over the roofs of Gannet Point like a swarm of tiny desperate lanterns, and the town made a sound — not a cheer, nothing so simple — a sound like a held breath finally let go.

"The manifest," Sergeant Hollis announced, in the parade-ground voice nineteen years had built for exactly this purpose, "has been destroyed by fire. The duplicate is illegible. The original, I am reliably informed, was damaged by flooding some days ago and is likewise illegible." He raised his voice further, over the wind, so it reached the customs house steps and the man standing on them. "I regret to report, Colonel, that this requisition cannot proceed. There is no list. There is no legal basis on which to compel any person to board in any order whatsoever. There is only a ship, a storm, and a town. I suggest, sir, that we fill the ship."

The paper burned down to his fingers and he let the last of it go, and the ash went spinning out over the water.

For a long moment, Colonel Ashworth stood motionless on the steps of the customs house. Behind him, through the window, the ruined original ledger lay open on his desk where he had sat with it half the night — Hollis had seen the lamp burning — its margins crowded now, God knew why, with the colonel's own handwriting in the spaces around the names, small annotations in a neat military script, as if a man had been unable to stop himself from getting to know the dead.

When Ashworth finally spoke, his voice was almost lost in the wind.

"You understand," he said, "what happens to you."

"I do, sir."

"Court-martial. Possibly worse. The storm may be court enough for the Crown's purposes — men have been shot on less pretext, in worse weather, by officers with less patience than I have."

"Yes, sir," Hollis said. "But it'll take you till after the boats sail to convene it. And I'd rather hang in a town that's empty than live in one that's full of graves."

Something happened to the colonel's face then. It did not soften — Hollis doubted it could soften anymore, whatever had been carved away from it in whatever year had emptied his eyes — but it *hesitated*. The jaw worked. The eyes went, just once, quickly, to the great white tower above the town, where the lamp room stood dark against the storm-gray sky, waiting.

"Get those people aboard," Ashworth said at last, in a voice gone quiet and strange. "Both vessels. All of them. If the mathematics fail, Sergeant, then let them fail completely. Get *everyone* aboard."

He turned and went inside, and closed the door, and did not arrest anyone.

* * *

Afterward there was no time for anything but work.

The quay erupted into motion — the good kind, the kind Hollis had spent two weeks longing to see. Fishermen ran for the *Kestrel*; women formed lines passing children hand over hand down to the boats like buckets at a fire; Marta Tull shoved her remaining loaves aboard the *Providence* with the fierce efficiency of a woman arming a garrison. The chalk numbers on the doors suddenly meant nothing and everybody, and the town tore itself loose from its own arithmetic and became, in the space of ten minutes, simply a crowd of people saving each other.

Hollis worked the gangplank of the *Kestrel*, counting souls aloud because counting was the only language he had, and finding for the first time in his career that the number didn't matter. Forty. Fifty-five. Sixty. The boat rode lower and lower and old Ollie stood at her tiller laughing like a man possessed.

It was young Denny who came pelting up the quay through the first stinging sheets of rain, pointing east past the point, where the sea had gone the color of wet slate and was standing up in ridges.

"Sarge!" he shouted. "Sarge — the *Providence*'s signal — she says she can't hold station! She's dragging anchor already!"

Hollis looked east. Beyond the harbor mouth, the big ship was yawing at her cable, and beyond her the horizon had disappeared entirely behind a wall of moving water, and above that wall, above the town, above everything, stood the dark tower of the lighthouse with its lamp room cold and unlit —

— except that even as he watched, even through the rain, a spark woke high up in the glass. A small yellow eye, opening in the dark. Someone was climbing the stairs. Someone was going to light it.

The beam swung, found the reef, and began to turn.

"Hollis!" Marta Tull shouted from the *Kestrel*, hauling Tam Vane against her side with one arm and reaching the other hand back toward the quay. "We're running the passage — we need the light on the range — *go!*"

The sergeant of Her Majesty's 44th Foot, wanted man, destroyer of manifests, turned and ran for the lighthouse through the rising storm, and the last thing he heard behind him was the wind, and under the wind, faint and clear, a child's voice cheering.
