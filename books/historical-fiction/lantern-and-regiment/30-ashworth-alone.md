## 30. Ashworth Alone

The customs house at Gannet Point had been built by an optimist.

Colonel Edmund Ashworth noted this as he climbed the stairs to his borrowed office for the last time that evening — the wide windows meant to catch sea light, the broad desk meant to catch contracts, the deep cellar meant to catch prosperity. Whoever had raised these walls had imagined a future of cargo manifests and cheerful tariffs. Instead the building now held a garrison, a requisitioned town's worth of confiscated papers, and one colonel who could not sleep.

The lamp on the desk threw a small circle of yellow against the dark. Outside, wind worried at the shutters like a dog nosing at a door it knew would open eventually. The storm was still two days out by the Admiralty charts — three days, if Sergeant Hollis's amended forecast was to be believed, and Ashworth had begun, uneasily, to believe it. He had not yet decided what he believed about Hollis's other amendments.

He sat down. He told himself he would spend the hour reviewing tonnage figures, which were honest and did not look back at him.

Instead, his hand went to the ledger.

Mirabel Vane's reconstructed ledger lay where it always lay now, within reach of his right hand, the way another man might keep a Bible or a pistol. It was bound in salt-stiff leather, its spine repaired twice with waxed thread, and it had been written in over perhaps forty years by at least three hands before Mirabel's took it over entirely. The regiment's own copy — printed forms, ruled columns, official ink — sat beside it like a tax collector seated next to a poet.

Ashworth opened it again. He told himself it was diligence. He was fairly sure diligence was not the word.

* * *

He had first read it as a professional exercise. A keeper who knows every household by heart is an instrument; instruments must be calibrated. He had wanted to see how accurate the reconstruction was, how much of it could be trusted, where sentiment might have crept into the columns like damp into plaster.

Sentiment had not crept. Sentiment had moved in, paid rent, and redecorated.

The names were all there, in a fine, upright hand — every soul in Gannet Point and the scattered holdings down the coast, entered with a precision Ashworth would have praised in any quartermaster. Ages. Trades. Household counts. Berth-relevant particulars. It was exactly the document he had demanded, and it was nothing like the document he had demanded, because in the margins, in the same careful hand, the keeper had committed treason.

Not the legal kind. The worse kind.

Beside *Tull, Marta — baker, widow, 44*, in smaller script: *"Kept the ovens going through the flood week. Fed eleven people off bread and stubbornness."*

Beside *Fenwick, Cobb — fisher, 51*: *"Owes me for lamp oil since '68. Will never pay. Would still light his way home if I saw him adrift. This is not a virtue. It is just true."*

Beside *Harkess, the elder — netmender, 79*: *"Mends nets faster than men half his age. Says his hands remember what his head forgets. If we are deciding who is useful, someone should ask him what he thinks useful means."*

Ashworth had read those marginal notes the first time with the cold satisfaction of a man finding a subordinate's insubordination documented in their own hand. Here it was, proof that the keeper could not be trusted with arithmetic. Here was the evidence for why the final list must be sealed by military authority alone.

He had carried the ledger to his quarters intending to write up the irregularity. He had sat down, uncapped his pen, and found himself reading the notes instead. All of them. There were hundreds. He had read until the candle drowned in its own wax, and then he had sat in the dark listening to the wind, feeling something shift in his chest that he refused, on principle, to name.

* * *

Now, on this second night of sleeplessness, he turned the pages more slowly.

It was the annotations that undid him, though he would have shot any man who said so. Not the facts — facts were his trade, and Mirabel Vane's facts were impeccable. No. It was the smallness of them. The particularity. Somewhere between the third household and the thirtieth, the ledger stopped being a census and became — there was no other word for it — a parish register of the living. Every name came trailing its weather.

*Pike, Jonah & Pike, Sal — fisherfolk, 31 and 29. Four children. Sal sings when she gutts herring; you can hear her from the quay on a good day. Jonah cannot swim.*

Ashworth had stared at that last clause for a long time. *Jonah cannot swim.* What possible column required that? None. No form in the Adjutant-General's office had a field for whether a man could swim. And yet Ashworth had thought about Jonah Pike four times since reading it — once while reviewing the loading plan, once during supper, once while shaving, and once, just now, unbidden, imagining a big quiet man going into grey water with his arms full of child and knowing it, knowing it, and going anyway because the children went first.

That was the trouble with the annotations. They did not argue. They simply stayed.

He turned another page. The paper had gone soft at the corner where a thumb had worn it — Mirabel's thumb, presumably, returning again and again to some entry. He smoothed the page and saw which one.

*Vane, Ede — keeper, lost at sea, 1870. Spouse of the compiler of this ledger.*

Below it, in the margin, in handwriting so controlled it trembled only if you looked very hard:

*"Ede used to say the light doesn't choose whom it saves. I am trying to learn to be a light instead of a heart. I am not learning fast enough."*

Ashworth closed the ledger.

He got up and stood at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, which was how he stood when he did not trust himself to do anything else with them. Below, the town lay dark except for the lighthouse, whose beam swept past the window every seven seconds with the regularity of a heartbeat. He had counted it often. Seven seconds. A whole town's safety measured out in sevens.

"You should not be reading that book," he said aloud to himself, because the room was empty and a colonel may as well be honest with one listener. "You are commanding an evacuation, not conducting a séance."

But the truth was that the ledger had become a séance, and not only for the dead of Gannet Point.

* * *

There was a drawer in his dispatch case that he had not opened since Portsmouth. He opened it now.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was a small photograph and a school exercise book. The photograph showed a woman with a long face and an expression of patient amusement, standing beside a boy of perhaps eight who was scowling at the camera with tremendous commitment, as though the camera had personally wronged him.

His wife, Charlotte. His son, Thomas, who had scowled at everything, including — especially — his father's absences, and who had been owed, at the time of the fever, one fishing trip to the Norfolk coast that Ashworth had postponed four times for the convenience of the War Office.

The exercise book was Thomas's. Arithmetic, mostly. Ashworth had taken it from the effects because he had not been able to bear leaving it, and had not been able to bear looking at it, and so it had traveled with him for six years in a state of suspended grief, like a letter never posted.

He set it on the desk beside Mirabel Vane's ledger.

The two books looked absurd together — the schoolroom hand with its laborious sums, the keeper's hand with its upright names and its margins full of humanity. But they were the same kind of book. That was what he had understood tonight, finally, and could not now unknow. They were both ledgers of what could not be recovered. One recorded a boy's attempts to make numbers come out even. The other recorded a keeper's attempt to make a town come out alive.

And here was the difference that mattered, the difference that had kept him at this desk past midnight: Thomas's sums could not be corrected anymore. The Gannet Point ledger still could. Every page of it represented lives that had not yet been spent, berths not yet filled, a storm not yet arrived. The book was open. The ink was wet.

Which meant, Ashworth thought, staring at the beam sweeping its seven-second circuit, that he was currently the man holding the pen.

* * *

He tried, as he always tried, to return to the arithmetic, because the arithmetic had never lied to him.

One ship. The *Providence*, 214 souls at maximum safe loading given stores and ballast. Town population, per his own survey: 261. Shortfall: 47 souls, plus whatever the Kestrel might carry if she existed, which she officially did not, and which unofficially was the subject of reports he had chosen not yet to act upon. Deadline: the great storm, two days out, or three, depending on whose forecast one trusted — and there was the small hot coal of doubt about Hollis again, banked but not extinguished.

Forty-seven souls short. He had said the number aloud in the chapel square and watched a town hear itself counted. He had slept perfectly well that night, or nearly — he had told himself he had, and the telling had almost held.

Almost held. Because the arithmetic was sound. That was the devilish thing about it. Tonnage was tonnage. The sea did not take IOUs. If you put 300 souls aboard a ship rated for 214, you did not get 300 saved; you got some larger number drowned, and probably the ship too, and then the shortfall became infinite. He had explained this to Mirabel Vane in the customs house with the patience of a man explaining gravity to a bird. The keeper had listened with those steady grey eyes and said only:

"And after the numbers, Colonel? When the boat has sailed and the list is signed. What does the arithmetic weigh then?"

"Nothing," Ashworth had answered. "Arithmetic weighs nothing. That is its virtue."

He had believed it when he said it. He was no longer certain he believed it now, sitting alone with two dead families' worth of handwriting under one lamp.

Because here was what the margins had done to him: they had put weights on his numbers. *Jonah cannot swim.* How many pounds did that weigh? *Sal sings from the quay.* And that? *Fed eleven people off bread and stubbornness.* At what point did stubbornness convert to tonnage?

The War Office had formulas. Ashworth had helped draft two of them. None of them contained a field for singing.

* * *

There was a knock — soft, apologetic, the knock of Private Dunmore, who knocked the way other men curtsied. Ashworth closed both books before admitting him.

"Beg pardon, sir. Sergeant Hollis's compliments. He's finished the reef soundings report and asked would you want it tonight or morning."

"Tonight."

Dunmore handed over a folder, hesitated with the magnificent awkwardness of the very young, and said, "Sergeant also says — he says the tide tables came in from the mainland packet, sir. He says they don't match his figures."

"Do they match mine?"

"No, sir."

"Thank you, Dunmore. That will be all."

The private withdrew. Ashworth sat looking at the folder without opening it for a moment, because he had learned to smell a falsified document the way a horse smells a storm, and this folder smelled of one. Hollis's tide tables "not matching" anyone's was either incompetence — and Hollis had never been incompetent in his life — or a soldier buying time with forged arithmetic, and risking a court-martial, or worse, to do it.

Three days, Hollis's amended forecast claimed. Three days before the great storm.

Ashworth opened the folder. The tide tables inside were beautifully rendered, authoritative, and wrong. He knew they were wrong because he had memorized the almanac figures weeks ago. Someone had added thirty-six hours to the safe-weather window, and the forgery was excellent, and the fact that it was excellent told him exactly who had made it.

He should have Hollis arrested in the morning. Refusal of orders, falsification of naval documents in an active evacuation — the Articles were clear, and the penalty was the kind that ended careers with a drumbeat. Any competent officer would act immediately. Hesitation, his instructors at Sandhurst had said, is how disasters begin.

Hesitation is how disasters begin.

Ashworth looked at the lighthouse beam passing the window. Seven seconds. Sweep. Seven seconds. Sweep. He thought of Thomas scowling at the camera, of Charlotte's patient amusement, of a margin note that said *I am trying to learn to be a light instead of a heart,* and he heard himself answer his instructors across twenty years:

Perhaps. But sometimes, Colonel, hesitation is the last honest act available to a reasonable man.

He did not arrest Hollis. He did not endorse the forged tables either — he was not yet that far fallen. He placed them in the drawer with the photograph and the exercise book, which was becoming, he realized with grim humor, less a dispatch case than a reliquary of things he could not bring himself to either use or destroy.

Then he picked up Mirabel's ledger one more time, opened it to the flyleaf, and read the single line written there in the keeper's hand, which he had somehow never noticed before, or had noticed and fled from, and could flee no longer:

*"For whoever keeps this book after me: these are not entries. They are neighbors. Mind the difference."*

Outside, far out beyond the point, the sea changed its voice.

Ashworth had served on three coasts and knew every note the water owned — the mutter, the growl, the long Atlantic roar. This was none of them. This was the sound the sea made when it drew breath to speak, and it had come early, hours early, days early, and every tide table in his drawer, forged or honest, had just been declared obsolete by an authority higher than the Crown.

He was moving for the door before the thought finished forming, calling down the stairwell for Dunmore, for his horse, for Hollis —

— because the storm was here, and the list was sealed, and the boats were not loaded, and somewhere below in the dark a girl with stolen paper and a sergeant with forged tides and a keeper with a lamp were all, at this very moment, running out of seven-second intervals.

The lamp swept the window one last time. Then the wind took the glass, and the night came in like a verdict.
