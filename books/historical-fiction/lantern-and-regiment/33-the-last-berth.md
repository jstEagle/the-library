## 33. The Last Berth

The wind had stopped being weather and become a fact of the world, like gravity or grief.

Mirabel Vane stood at the harbor wall with Tam's hand in theirs and could not hear the sea so much as feel it — a vast animal turning over beneath the town, shaking the stones under their boots. Spray came over the harbor wall in sheets now, not in bursts, and every sheet left a film of salt on Mirabel's lips that tasted like the inside of Ede's coat pocket, like all the winters they had ever known pressed into one.

"Keeper!" Brine Ollie bellowed from the Kestrel's deck, his voice arriving sideways on the gale like a thrown hat. "She'll hold for maybe ten more minutes! Then she holds for nobody!"

The little smuggler's boat bucked against her lines like a pony that smelled home. Forty souls were already crammed aboard her — Marta Tull's nieces, the Hollis boy, half the Tull cousins, old Mrs. Penty wrapped in three blankets and complaining about it, which everyone took as a good sign. The evacuation ship *Providence* stood further out, rolling black and enormous against the white chaos, her boats ferrying loads through water that stood up in walls.

They had been loading since midnight. It felt like a year.

Tam tugged at Mirabel's hand. He didn't speak — he hadn't spoken more than a handful of words since the flood took their grandmother — but he had learned to say things with his grip, and this grip said: *I am frightened, and I am not letting go.*

"I know," Mirabel said, kneeling down into the wind so their faces were level. Rain ran off the end of Mirabel's nose onto the boy's cheek. "I know, my lad. Listen to me. Do you see Marta? There, by the rail, with the yellow scarf?"

Tam looked. Marta Tull was braced against the Kestrel's gunwale, holding out her arms toward the quay with an expression Mirabel had last seen on her face when she'd pulled two drowned rats of children out of a cellar last autumn and fed them bread until they remembered how to be people.

"You're going to walk to her, and you're going to let her catch you, and you're going to hold on until your arms ache. Can you do that?"

Tam shook his head.

"No?"

He pointed — up the hill, back toward the lighthouse, its beam swinging wild and magnificent above them, cutting lanes through the rain. His finger moved between the light and Mirabel's chest, and then he made the gesture he'd been making all week: hands cupped, then opened, like a lamp being lit.

*I stay with you.*

Mirabel's throat closed. Of all the terrible arithmetic the winter had taught them — tonnage, deadlines, names weighed against names — nothing was as heavy as this: a seven-year-old who had decided, silently, methodically, that the way to keep a family was to refuse to leave one.

"Wren will be there," Mirabel said. "Wren will be waiting at the other end, and she'll want to know where you are every single minute, and if you're not there she'll come looking, and I can't fight Wren, Tam, nobody can."

His chin wobbled. His grip tightened.

From the top of the harbor steps came the sound of running feet, and Wren arrived out of the rain like something the storm itself had sent — soaked through, hair plastered flat, eyes enormous and furious and terrified all at once. She skidded to a stop beside them.

"The *Providence*'s last boat is going back for six," she shouted. "Sergeant Hollis says six, no more, he counted them himself. And Mirabel—" She stopped. She looked at Tam. She looked at the boat. Mirabel watched her do the sum, watched her arrive at it, watched her face go pale under the salt.

"There's one berth left on the Kestrel," Mirabel said quietly. It wasn't a question; Ollie had told them an hour ago, shouting it over the rail with a strange gentleness, the way you tell someone a death. One place. The Kestrel could take no more after this, and the *Providence* would have to weigh anchor within the hour or founder on the outer shoals.

Wren grabbed Mirabel's sleeve with both hands. "You're getting in it."

"I'm not."

"Yes you ARE—"

"Wren." Mirabel crouched, taking both her shoulders, rain hammering the space between them. Somewhere behind them a soldier slipped on the wet stones and swore; somewhere ahead, the sea threw itself against the wall again and the whole world went white. "Look at me. Somebody has to keep the light. If that goes dark tonight, the *Providence* loses the range marks, the boats lose the channel, and everything we've done — the list, the ledger, Hollis burning the manifest, all of it — means nothing, because nobody finds the harbor. You understand? The light isn't a job tonight. It's the only rope tying all those boats to land."

"So send a soldier!"

"They need every man hauling boats. And no soldier knows the lens, and no soldier knows the reef, and—" Mirabel smiled, and was surprised to find the smile was real, cracked and wet but real. "And no soldier is me."

"That's not FAIR—"

"No," Mirabel agreed. "It isn't. Most true things aren't fair, Wren. That's how you know them."

She was crying now, though the rain made a decent disguise of it, and Mirabel gathered her in — this fierce, thieving, list-stealing girl who had copied a sealed manifest by candlelight because she couldn't bear a world where some names mattered less than others — and held her while the storm tried its best to take them both apart.

"I'm coming back," Mirabel said into her ear. "Do you hear me? The relief ship comes when the storm breaks. Two days. Three. And when it comes, I'll be standing on that gallery, and I'll be able to see the mainland, and I'll watch for the Kestrel's sail the entire way. So you'd better make good time, or I'll write in the log that you dawdled, and logs are forever."

A sound came out of Wren that was half laugh and half sob, which Mirabel considered the finest music the winter had produced.

"Now," Mirabel said, standing, lifting Tam up into their arms. The boy was shivering violently. "Marta! MARTA TULL!"

At the rail, the baker's wife turned. Even at forty yards, even through a gale, her voice carried like a bell. "You get those children to me, Mirabel Vane, or I'll come back for you myself, and I won't be kind about it!"

"Wouldn't dream of it!"

Mirabel carried Tam down the slick steps to the Kestrel's side. Hands reached up — Ollie's, a soldier's, Marta's, brown and strong and certain — and Mirabel passed the boy across the gap between the quay and the rail the way you pass anything precious across a gap: quickly, before fear can change your mind.

Marta caught him and folded him into the yellow scarf and held on exactly as instructed, until his arms would have to ache. Tam looked back over her shoulder, cupped his hands, opened them. *Lamp.* Mirabel answered the same way. *Lamp. Always.*

Then Wren climbed the rail herself, because Wren did not wait to be lifted anywhere in this life, and stood for one moment balanced on the gunwale with the storm tearing at her, looking back.

There was no time for speeches and neither of them would have wanted one. But Wren shouted one thing, and the wind carried most of it away, and what reached Mirabel was: "—two cups! Don't forget! We'll fill them when we come back!"

And then she dropped to the deck, and the lines came off, and the Kestrel swung her bow toward the reef passage with the Gannet Point beam laying a trembling golden road across the water for her to follow.

* * *

Afterward, Mirabel could never remember climbing back up the harbor steps. What remained was fragments: Cobb Fenwick's face at a porthole of the *Providence*, watching the town he'd bargained with disappear into the dark without hatred in it anymore, only awe. Sergeant Hollis on the quay, counting heads with a voice gone hoarse, refusing to board until the count matched the count in his head — the same sergeant who had burned a manifest that morning rather than let the machinery work, standing now in the ruin of his career as calmly as a man mending a net. And the moment, the one Mirabel would carry longest, when the last shore boat pushed off from the *Providence* toward the ship, and a soldier's lantern swept the emptying quay, and Hollis looked straight at the lighthouse hill and raised two fingers to his forehead — not quite a salute, not quite goodbye. A promise to file no report.

Then the quay was empty, and the town was empty, and the storm had Gannet Point to itself.

Mirabel climbed.

The path up the headland was a river now, and the wind came off the sea with the weight of falling timber. Twice Mirabel went down to hands and knees and crawled. The lighthouse appeared and vanished behind curtains of rain, and each time it reappeared Mirabel thought — absurdly, tenderly — *there you are,* as if greeting an old friend who had waited up.

Inside, the tower groaned around them like a ship's hull, but the stairwell was dry, and the lamp room above was a small round country where the storm existed only as sound and vibration and the endless wheel of the light. Mirabel shed the soaked coat — Ede's coat, the one with the letter still folded in the pocket, read at last and carried now like a second heartbeat — and set to work.

Wick trimmed. Oil topped. Lens polished, though the sea spray had found even here, frosting the glass with salt that had to be wiped away in slow circles, the way Ede had taught them twenty years ago, laughing, saying *you don't wipe a lens, love, you introduce yourself to it.*

"Introducing myself," Mirabel told the glass. "Mind the children tonight. Mind them better than I did."

The great lens turned above the town, above the reef, above the black tumbling water where two boats full of everyone Mirabel loved were running the passage between the teeth of the rocks. From the gallery rail, straining against the wind, Mirabel could just make it out: the Kestrel's tiny light, sliding along the golden road, wavering, steadying, wavering —

— and clearing the far buoy. Clearing it, and standing out toward the open sea and the dark shape of the *Providence*, safe past the reef, safe past the arithmetic, safe.

Mirabel put both hands on the cold iron rail and let out a breath they felt they'd been holding since October.

"Well," they said to the storm, to the tower, to Ede, to nobody. "That's that, then."

It was not that. It was very much not that, because at that moment the door at the bottom of the stairwell banged open with a crack of wind and rain, and heavy boots began to climb, and Mirabel turned to find Colonel Ashworth ascending into the lamplight — soaked gray, coat torn at one shoulder, his cap long gone, his face carrying the expression of a man whose ledger has just been thrown into the sea.

He stopped at the top of the stairs. Water pooled around his boots. For a long moment the two of them simply looked at each other while the lens wheeled overhead and the storm hammered the glass like a creditor at the door.

"The *Providence* couldn't hold station," Ashworth said at last, and his voice was raw, stripped of every ounce of parade-ground brass. "She's run for the lee of the point. My launch swamped trying to reach her. There is no other boat." He paused, and something almost like a laugh moved through him without warming anything. "It appears, Keeper, that I have requisitioned myself into your lighthouse."

Mirabel looked at him — this man of terrible, reasonable sums, who had numbered a town and been undone by a child with a stolen list — and saw that underneath the uniform he was only another soul the sea had decided not to ask.

"There's tea," Mirabel said. "And one cup. But I've a feeling we'll manage."

Ashworth stared. Then, slowly, unbelievably, the colonel sat down on the lamp room floor with his back against the warm iron of the housing, a soldier at rest for the first time in a winter of war against weather, and outside the great light turned, and turned, and held.
