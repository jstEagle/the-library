## 34. One Keeper, One Night

The Kestrel went out with the tide and the town on her back, and the sea closed over the place where she had been as if she had never been at all.

Mirabel stood at the base of the tower until the wind made standing a kind of vanity, and then climbed — hand over hand up the wet iron rail, past the first landing, past the second, into the lantern room where the great lens turned and turned and asked nothing of anyone but oil and attention.

Ashworth was already there.

He stood at the seaward glass with his coat plastered to his shoulders, his hat gone somewhere between the harbor and the rocks, watching the last white smudge of the boat's sail dissolve into the rain. He did not look like a colonel. He looked like a man who had been left behind by his own arithmetic.

"You should have gone with them," he said, without turning.

"One keeper stays," Mirabel said. "That was always the arrangement. The light is essential. I am the light's."

"That was *my* arrangement. It was designed to keep one useful man alive, not to strand him." Ashworth's voice was flat, pitched under the wind rather than against it. "I signed that order myself."

"Then you'll have to live with your signature. I've lived with worse ones." Mirabel moved past him to the lamp, checked the wick out of pure habit though they had trimmed it an hour ago, and found their hands were shaking so badly that the little brass key chattered against the housing like teeth.

Ashworth noticed. He said nothing about it, which was, Mirabel would come to understand over the course of that night, the closest thing to kindness the man had ever learned to offer.

* * *

The storm arrived in earnest at half past ten, if the clock in the watch room could be believed, and after that time stopped being a thing anyone could measure. The barometer had fallen so far that the needle lay against its stop like a fainted woman. The rain came not down but sideways, in sheets that struck the lantern glass with a sound like handfuls of gravel thrown by someone very large and very angry, and beneath it all was the sea — not waves now but a single continuous concussion, the whole weight of the ocean picking itself up and hurling itself at Gannet Point again and again, so that the tower trembled on its foundations like a dog dreaming.

They kept the light.

There was no question of anything else. The boats were out there — the Kestrel with forty souls, the evacuation ship *Providence* holding station beyond the reef with two hundred more who hadn't fit anywhere better — and every one of them was navigating blind except for this: one beam, sweeping the dark once every seven and a half seconds, saying *here, here is the land, here is where the rocks are not.*

Mirabel worked and Ashworth worked beside them, and it was strange, Mirabel thought, how quickly the body teaches itself a new rhythm. They fell into it without discussion. When the reservoir needed refilling from the store tank below, Ashworth carried the cans while Mirabel managed the funnel, both of them braced against the walls as the tower swayed. When spray found a seam in the glazing and began icing the outside of the seaward pane, Ashworth held the lantern while Mirabel went out onto the gallery rail — clipped on, Ede's old harness, Ede's old knots — and scraped it clear with the flat of a knife, three inches from eternity, while the colonel of Her Majesty's 44th Regiment gripped the back of their coat in two fists like a man holding onto a kite string.

When Mirabel came back in, soaked through and grinning in spite of everything, Ashworth looked at them as though seeing a new species.

"You've done that before," he said.

"Every storm for eleven years."

"And he let you?" And then Ashworth stopped, because he heard what he'd said — *he* — and understood, the way men do, one beat too late.

"My spouse," Mirabel said, hanging the harness back on its peg. "Ede. Yes. Ede let me do a great many things. That was the trouble with Ede, according to everyone in the town, and the very best thing about Ede, according to me."

* * *

At some hour that might have been midnight or might have been the end of the world, there came a lull — not a true lull, the sea never truly lulled on such a night, but a settling, the way a horse settles between bucking — and in that settling they went down to the watch room to check the log and drink something hot that Marta Tull's bread oven had no part in making. Ashworth produced, from some interior pocket of his ruined coat, a flask of brandy, and poured a measure into the tin cup Mirabel handed him without comment.

Mirabel set out two cups. Then, catching themselves, laughed — a short, cracked sound.

"What?"

"Habit," Mirabel said. "For a year I set out two cups every night. Ede's cup. Couldn't stop. Couldn't bear to stop, really — stopping felt like a second drowning. And tonight, of all nights, my hands do it again without asking me." They looked at the two cups sitting on the chart table, steaming, identical, absurd. "Drink yours. Ede never liked brandy anyway. Said it tasted like furniture polish."

Something happened to Ashworth's face then. It was small. If you had not spent a year learning to read faces across an empty table, you would have missed it entirely. But Mirabel saw it: the flinch of a man who has been handed something he does not know how to hold.

"I had a family," Ashworth said, to the cups.

The wind filled the silence for a moment, testing it, finding it would bear weight.

"A wife. A daughter. Fever, in Delhi, the same week. Eleven years ago this autumn." He turned the flask in his hands, not drinking. "Eleven years, Vane. You've counted yours. I know you have; I've read your margins. I count mine too, only I do it in ledgers of my own. Regiments transferred. Stations accepted that no sane man accepts. I put four thousand names on lists since Delhi, and every list was correct, and every correct list took me further from the day they died, because as long as the arithmetic is perfect, no one asks the man doing it whether he is."

"And are you?"

"Am I what?"

"Asking yourself."

The lamp turned above them. Seven and a half seconds of light, seven and a half seconds of dark. Out in all that blackness, two hundred souls were riding on the difference.

"No," Ashworth said finally. "I am very careful never to ask myself. That is what the army is *for*, Vane. It is a machine so large and so loud that a man cannot hear himself ask." He did drink then, one swallow, wincing. "Your annotations. In the ledger. *Marta Tull, bakes for the wake when no one asks her to.* *Cobb Fenwick, pays his debts late but pays them.* *The Halloran boy, afraid of deep water, which is a hard fear to have in a fishing town.* Do you know what I thought when I first saw them? I thought: sentiment. I thought: this keeper will be a problem. Sentiment ruins lists."

"And now?"

"Now I think—" He stopped. Started again. "Now I think that I have spent eleven years writing names that meant nothing to me, and it turns out that was the easier way to spend them. Your names cost you something every time you wrote one. Mine cost nothing. And I told myself that was discipline."

"It wasn't," Mirabel said gently.

"No. It wasn't."

* * *

The squall line hit them at what the clock insisted was three, and it was the worst hour of the worst night in a generation, and there was no more talk.

The tower shook. The glass sang. Twice the lamp guttered when the draught reversed itself down the ventilators, and twice one of them was at the wick within seconds — once Ashworth, once Mirabel — hands steady in the way hands only become when steadiness is the only thing left. At the worst of it, when the sea came over the gallery in a solid green wall and the whole world was water and roar, Mirabel found themselves shouting the numbers of the rotation aloud — *seven and a half, seven and a half* — like a prayer, like a heartbeat, and realized dimly that Ashworth was shouting them too, in time, his parade-ground voice cracking on the halves.

And then, near dawn, or what the drowned clock claimed was dawn, the *Providence* fired a rocket.

They saw it from the gallery — one red star climbing out of the blackness beyond the reef, bursting, falling. Signal code. Ashworth read it aloud, hoarse, disbelieving: *"Vessel safe. Standing off. God keep the light."*

Two hundred souls. Holding station. Alive.

Mirabel sat down right there on the wet iron plates of the gallery and began, helplessly, to laugh, and the laughing turned halfway through into the other thing, and Ashworth — Colonel Ashworth, keeper of lists, man of perfect arithmetic — did the only correct thing left in his inventory. He sat down beside them on the flooded iron, in the rain, and put one awkward hand on one shaking shoulder, and kept it there until it was over.

"The boats," Mirabel managed. "The Kestrel—"

"We won't know till landfall. Nothing we can do from here but burn the oil and keep the turn." A pause. "So we keep the turn."

* * *

Later — much later, in the grey false morning with the storm blown out to a long exhausted moan — they sat in the watch room among the wreckage of the night: empty oil cans, the two tin cups, Tam's paper boats scattered where the draught had taken them, sodden now, their folded names illegible. Ashworth picked one up and smoothed it flat with enormous care, the way a man handles a document.

"There's a name inside every one of these," he said.

"Yes."

"Whose handwriting?"

"Tam's. He hasn't spoken since the flood took his grandmother — until two days ago, when he said one word. But he writes." Mirabel took the flattened boat, dried it as best they could on their sleeve, and set it upright on the chart table where it could stand. "He folded one for every soul in the ledger. He was going to float them. So the sea would give them back."

Ashworth was quiet for a long time.

"When this is over," he said, "there will be inquiries. Boards. My report will be read by men who think exactly the way I thought in October." He looked at the little paper boat, then at Mirabel. "Whatever that report says — whatever I have to write in it to keep us both from the firing squad — I want you to know that the true record is the one with the margins. Yours. Not mine."

"Ede used to say a name is the smallest boat there is," Mirabel said. "You can carry a whole person in one word, if you fold it right."

"Your Ede sounds like they'd have made a better colonel than I ever did."

"They'd have made a terrible colonel. Refused every order that was tidy and cruel. But they'd have liked you, I think. Underneath." Mirabel almost smiled. "Ede liked people best when they were breaking."

Outside, the light of the actual dawn crept grey along the water, and the sea — spent, scoured, still snarling at the reef out of habit — began, very slowly, to lie down.

And then Wren's voice came up the stairwell, ragged and triumphant and impossibly alive: "*She made it! The Kestrel made the mainland — Marta's signal flags — all forty — all forty of them!*"

Mirabel was on their feet before the echo settled, and Ashworth was close behind, and neither of them remembered, ever afterward, which of them reached the gallery door first.
