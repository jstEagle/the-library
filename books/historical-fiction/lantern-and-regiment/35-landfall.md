## 35. Landfall

The sea stopped trying to kill them at roughly the moment Wren stopped being afraid of it.

She noticed this somewhere past the reef — or rather, she noticed that she had not noticed the reef going by. All night she had braced herself for it, for the black teeth of rock that Brine Ollie swore would take the Kestrel's belly if the light so much as blinked. But the light had not blinked. Somewhere behind them, through a wall of rain that would have frightened God Himself, Mirabel Vane had kept the beam turning, steady as a heartbeat, and the reef had slid past beneath their keel like a sleeping animal they'd tiptoed around.

And then there was simply water. Ordinary water. Water that rocked the boat instead of trying to bury it.

Wren sat in the stern with her back against the mast and Tam asleep against her side, his paper boats safe inside her coat where she'd buttoned them away from the spray. Forty-one souls crowded into a hull built for thirty, and nobody had complained once. Marta Tull held a stranger's baby. Cobb Fenwick — who had sold them all to the colonel and then wept openly when Ashworth's men let him board anyway — sat with his hands folded like a boy at chapel, saying nothing to anyone, which was the most useful thing he'd done all winter.

"Land," said Brine Ollie, who had not slept in two days and looked like something the tide had rejected twice.

Wren stood up too fast and had to sit back down again. But there it was: a gray smudge low on the horizon, resolving by degrees into hills, into trees, into a harbor wall with lamps along it, real lamps, human lamps, the kind that didn't turn.

The mainland.

She had imagined this moment perhaps a hundred times during the voyage, usually while bailing seawater with a bucket meant for fish guts. In her imagination there was always weeping, and falling to knees, and someone singing a hymn. What actually happened was that forty-one people went very quiet, and old Mrs. Pellow began, softly, to laugh — a creaking, rusty sound, like a gate that hadn't been opened in years — and couldn't stop.

* * *

The trouble started before the bow even touched the pier.

"Nobody's expecting us," Marta said, appearing at Wren's elbow with the practical frown she wore like other women wore shawls. "There's no requisition order here. No colonel. No manifest." She said *manifest* the way you'd say something you'd stepped in. "They're going to look at a leaking smuggler's boat full of half-drowned fishermen and ask questions."

"Then somebody has to answer them," Wren said.

Marta looked down at her. Wren was eleven years old and came up to Marta's apron strings, and she knew exactly how ridiculous it was when she squared her shoulders. She did it anyway.

"You," Marta said slowly, "have been spending entirely too much time around that lighthouse keeper."

"I learned from the best," said Wren, who had also learned from the worst — Colonel Ashworth's arithmetic had taught her plenty about how the world worked, mostly what not to copy — and then the keel ground against the pier timbers and there was no more time for talking.

Brine Ollie threw lines. Fishermen caught them. The mainland dockhands came running in oilskins, shouting questions — *where from, how many, whose authority* — and got in one another's way, because a crowd of rescuers is just a crowd until someone organizes it.

Wren stepped onto the pier.

Her legs did something untrustworthy after three days at sea, so she grabbed a mooring post and held on, which had the accidental effect of making her look like she owned the dock. The nearest dockhand, a big man with a beard full of sleet, turned to her because she was the first person standing still.

"Now then, little miss, you'll want to get indoors—"

"Gannet Point," Wren said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Forty-one survivors off the Kestrel. There are more coming behind us on the evacuation ship if she made the crossing — I count one hundred and six names on the list, but the list is three days old and some families have had babies since, so call it a hundred and ten. They'll need hot food, dry blankets, and a building with a floor, because half these people haven't stood on anything that didn't move since Tuesday."

The man blinked. Sleet collected on his eyebrows.

"And you are?"

"Wren Vane."

She hadn't decided to say it. It simply came out of her mouth, whole and true, the way Tam's word *stay* had come out of him in the lighthouse kitchen the night everything changed. Vane. Not born to the name, no more than Tam was, but Mirabel had written their names in the ledger with every other soul in Gannet Point, annotations and all — *Wren, quick with figures; Tam, does not speak, listens better than any of us* — and a name written in a ledger is a name that belongs somewhere.

The bearded man's face did several things at once. "Vane," he repeated. "You wouldn't be any relation to the keeper out at Gannet Point? Ede Vane's widow?"

"My parent," Wren said, which covered a great deal of territory and none of it falsely.

"Well," said the man, and turned, and cupped his hands, and bellowed across the whole harbor in a voice that scattered gulls for half a mile: "*Gannet Point survivors! Get the customs hall open! Wake the baker! Somebody find Dr. Enys!*"

* * *

It is remarkable, Wren thought later, how much can be accomplished by a person who looks like she knows what she's doing.

She didn't, particularly. She knew lists. That turned out to be enough.

The customs hall smelled of rope and lamp oil and decades of herring, and within an hour it held the entire population of Gannet Point, wrapped in whatever blankets the mainland could produce, drinking broth that Mrs. Pellow had appointed herself to supervise and salted to within an inch of its life because, as she announced to the room, "we've been drinking rainwater for a week, you can taste it properly or not at all."

Wren commandeered a corner of the counting table, a stub of pencil, and a sheet of paper, and began doing what she had practiced on stolen nights in the customs house at home: writing names.

Not berths this time. Blankets. Broth. Who was hurt — old Sam Tredaway's ribs, cracked against the Kestrel's rail in the crossing; a baby born somewhere over the reef who had been, until four days ago, only a rumor in the margin of a ledger. Who was missing — and this column she kept short, because the ship behind them might still arrive, and every name she wrote there felt like giving up on it.

Tam sat beside her on a crate and handed her things. He still spoke perhaps once a day, one word at a time, rationed like hardtack. But he had taken up his fleet again — real paper now, salvaged from the hall's own records, which Wren chose not to feel guilty about — and he folded each boat flat and passed it to her, and each one had a name inside, and each name was someone sitting in this very room, warm and accounted for.

"That's Mr. Cobb," Wren said, taking one. "He's right there by the stove."

Tam nodded gravely and set the boat upright on the table anyway, facing Cobb, like a small paper sentry assigned to watch him.

"Fair enough," Wren admitted.

* * *

The evacuation ship came in at dusk.

Wren heard it before she saw it — the harbor bells, and then voices, and then the particular sound a town makes when a boatload of its own arrives out of a storm: a rising noise, half cheering and half crying, impossible to tell apart and no need to.

She ran out into the sleet with everyone else and stood on her toes at the end of the pier, scanning faces as they came down the gangway. Marta's sister. The Hollis family — no; Sergeant Hollis was not among them, and Wren's stomach tightened, and then she saw his wife's face and understood that he wasn't among them for reasons the family already knew, and she made herself look away and make room in her heart for both the joy and the grief, because that was another thing the winter had taught her: they come in the same crate.

Then she saw them.

Two soldiers in a greatcoat that fit neither of them, supporting a third between them — a tall man, gray-faced, one arm bound up tight, frostbitten fingers visible where the bandage ended. His uniform had lost its buttons. His eyes found hers across the crowd the way a compass finds north.

Sergeant Hollis.

"He stayed at the harbor wall," one of the soldiers told Wren later, in the customs hall, while the doctor sawed off frozen boots. "Held the loading line in the worst of it. When the last boat pulled clear he was still on his feet. We carried him aboard over his objections. He objected *at length.*"

Wren wrote his name at the top of her list, underlined it twice, and added in the margin — in the style of a certain ledger she intended to grow up and keep one day — *Sgt. Hollis: stubborn. Useful. Should be thanked by everyone in this room individually.*

* * *

But there were two people the ship did not bring, and the whole town knew it, and nobody said it, which was somehow worse than saying it.

Mirabel Vane. And the man who had been their enemy, stranded together on a rock with the greatest storm in a generation coming down on them like the fist of heaven.

"They'll be dead by morning," said Cobb Fenwick, into his broth, and got such a look from Mrs. Pellow that he apologized to the broth.

"They will not," Wren said.

She said it with the flat certainty of a girl who had watched that light hold through the reef passage in conditions that had sunk boats full of men who knew the water far better than any storm did. She said it because Tam was listening, and because Tam's hand had gone very still over his paper fleet, and because some things you say out loud so they have permission to become true.

"The relief ship goes out when the weather breaks," she said. "First light, day after tomorrow. I asked. I've been asking since we landed. And when it goes out—" she looked around the customs hall, at the blankets and the broth and the hundred-odd souls of Gannet Point, alive, all of them, every single name on the list and a few besides — "it goes out with provisions, and a proper crew, and I am going on it."

"You most certainly are not," said Marta.

"Yes I am."

"You're eleven."

"I'm the one who stole the berth list," Wren said, "and copied it, and put it on every doorstep in town in paper boats, and if I hadn't, half the people in this room would be chalk numbers on a door instead of complaining about the salt in this soup. So yes. I'm going. Someone from Gannet Point should be standing on that gallery when the relief ship comes round the point, so the keeper knows we came back." She swallowed. "So they know the light worked."

The hall had gone quiet. Even Mrs. Pellow had stopped supervising.

It was Tam who settled it, the way Tam settled things — without a word. He picked up two paper boats from his fleet, the ones with no names in them yet, the blank ones he'd been saving, and set them down in front of Wren. One he turned to face her. One he turned to face himself.

*Mirabel,* said his single word, hoarse from disuse. Then, pointing at the second boat, at himself: *Me too.*

Wren's eyes stung. She blinked it away, because she was holding a pencil and a list, and a girl with a list does not cry in front of the cargo.

"All right," she said. "Both of us. Marta, you're in charge of the rest of them till we're back."

"I never agreed—"

"Marta." Wren looked up at her, and tried to put into her face everything she'd learned from watching a lighthouse keeper refuse to flinch for a year. "You left offerings at our door for a month before you ever knocked. You think I don't know who takes care of people in this town?"

Marta pressed her lips together, and her eyes went bright, and finally she said, gruffly, "Back by the second morning or I'm sending Cobb after you."

"*That's* the threat?" said Cobb.

"It's working on me," said old Sam Tredaway from his corner, holding his ribs, "and I've known him forty years."

* * *

They provisioned the relief ship themselves, Wren and Tam, walking the docks at dawn with a list in her fist and a fleet of paper boats buttoned inside her coat — one for every soul still out at Gannet Point, including, though she hadn't told anyone this part, one small blank boat with no name at all yet, because Wren had a suspicion about the tall gray-faced man the keeper was keeping company with out there in the dark, and suspicions deserved room in the margins.

The sky was breaking open in the east, thin gold under the ragged clouds. The sea had gone calm as a church floor.

"Ready?" Wren asked.

Tam held up his boat. Inside it, in careful pencil, he had written a new word — his third ever, and the longest: *home.*

Wren took his hand, and they walked up the gangway together, carrying the story ashore.

Behind them the harbor bells rang out, and ahead of them the tide ran fair toward Gannet Point, toward the rocks, toward a light that had burned all night and, God willing and the wind permitting, would still be burning when they rounded the point — and if it wasn't, Wren decided, squaring her shoulders at the horizon, then she would climb the tower herself and relight it, because that was what Vanes did.

What she could not know — what no one on that deck could know — was that the light was burning still, and that beside it, wrapped in a dead soldier's coat, a colonel was learning at last what a name was worth; and that the relief ship was not the only thing sailing toward Gannet Point that morning.

The tide, as it happened, was bringing the whole story home.
