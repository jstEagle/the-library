## 36. The Morning After

The storm did not end so much as it grew tired of itself.

Mirabel felt the change in the lamp room before they saw it anywhere else — a loosening in the wind's grip on the glass, the way the whole tower stopped shivering and settled into a long, exhausted sway, like a horse coming back to the stable. The rain went from horizontal to merely determined. And then, somewhere past what Mirabel judged to be four o'clock, though their pocket watch had drowned twice and disagreed with itself, there came through the roar a silence with edges to it. Gaps. The sound of the sky remembering how to be sky.

Mirabel had not sat down in nine hours. They had not needed to. There had been wicks to trim and glass to wipe and the great lens turning above them like a patient heart, and every time Mirabel's knees suggested rest, they thought of two small vessels out in that black water — one fat and slow and full of everyone they knew, one lean and desperate and running the reef — and the suggestion was overruled.

*Keep the light, and it keeps you.*

Ede had said it once, laughing, oil on their hands, years ago. Mirabel had spent a year believing it was a pretty thing people said to widows. Tonight it had been instructions.

When the first true light came — gray at first, then the color of pewter, then, unbelievably, gold along the eastern rim — Mirabel stepped out onto the gallery and held the rail with both hands because their legs had opinions of their own.

Gannet Point looked like a town that had been picked up by a giant, considered, and put down again slightly wrong. The harbor road was a brown river still draining itself. A fishing smack sat in the middle of Marta Tull's garden, politely, as if waiting to be offered tea. Half the sea wall was gone; the other half looked smug about it. And the customs house — the customs house had lost its roof entirely, its ledgers and lists and chalk-marked arithmetic scattered to the four winds or soaked into pulp, which struck Mirabel as the sea filing its own objection to the paperwork.

But the lighthouse stood. Of course it stood. It had been standing for ninety years against worse than this, and it would stand for ninety more, and this morning Mirabel loved it the way you love a stubborn old relative who turns out, in the end, to have been right about everything.

They were still holding the rail, doing sums of gratitude, when they saw it.

A ship, standing in toward the point under shortened canvas, wallowing a little in the leftover swell but making honest headway. Navy lines. A gun deck. And at her masthead, breaking out crisp and official in the new wind, the pennant of the Relief Service.

"Late," Mirabel told it, and their voice came out cracked as an old bell. "You're late."

The ship, being a ship, did not apologize. But she ran up a signal flag that meant, in the polite grammar of the sea, *Are you in need of assistance?*

Mirabel looked down at themselves. Nine hours of lamp oil, salt spray, and terror. Their coat — Ede's coat, actually, because they had put it on at some black hour without deciding to — hung off them like a sail with the wind gone out of it. Their hands were steady now, which was somehow the strangest part. Steady all night when it mattered; only shaking now that it didn't.

"No," Mirabel called, uselessly, across a mile of cold air. "We are in need of breakfast."

Then, because the light must answer even when the keeper is being flippant, they hauled the signal halyard and ran up the flag that meant *Light operational. All clear.* The last word was a lie and a prayer at once, and both halves of it made Mirabel grin until their face hurt.

* * *

The relief ship anchored in the outer roads and sent a boat. By the time it grounded on the shingle below the lighthouse path, half the town that could walk had come down to meet it — which is to say perhaps forty souls out of three hundred, wet to the ribs, hollow-eyed, counting each other like misers counting coins.

Marta Tull found Mirabel at the top of the path. She looked ten years older than she had three days ago and twenty years stronger, and she had Tam asleep against her shoulder with his cheek pressed into her neck, his fist closed around a paper boat gone soft as cloth from seawater.

"He wouldn't sleep on the Kestrel," she said. "Wouldn't sleep on the big ship either. Kept saying he had to stay awake till he saw the light." Her voice did something complicated. "He counted your flashes, Mirabel. All night. Out loud. I think half the boat slept better just hearing him do it."

"How many?" Mirabel asked, because someone had to, and because they had spent nine hours not knowing and the not-knowing had been the worst work of the night.

"All of them." Marta said it plainly, the way she announced bread coming out of the oven. "All of them, Vane. The Kestrel took water over the reef but Brine Ollie threaded her through like he was born on it, which I suppose in a manner of speaking he was. The big ship lost her main topsail and nothing else. Not one soul." She looked at Mirabel for a long moment, and whatever she saw in their face made her reach out and grip their wrist, hard. "Not one. You hear me?"

Mirabel heard her. It simply hadn't finished arriving yet. Some news takes the long road.

"I'll want my bakery back by Thursday," Marta added briskly, dashing at her eyes with a wrist that smelled of salt and yeast, and walked on down toward the soldiers and the boats and the unbearable miracle of a complete census.

Wren did not sleep against anyone's shoulder. Wren came up the lighthouse path at a dead run, hair full of brine, took the last six steps in one leap, and hit Mirabel at waist height hard enough to matter.

"You kept it lit," she said into their coat. It wasn't thanks. It was verification. Wren trusted nothing she hadn't checked herself, a quality that had lately saved several hundred lives.

"I kept it lit."

"The range marker held. Ollie said the bearing never wandered, not even in the worst of it. He said—" she pulled back, and her chin was trembling and her voice refused to, "—he said whoever was on the lamp that night deserved a medal, and Cobb Fenwick said the navy should give them one, and Cobb Fenwick has never said anything nice about anybody."

"Cobb Fenwick," Mirabel said carefully, "traded secrets to save his family."

"And then he rowed the Kestrel's spare boat through the reef gap in the dark when Ollie's hands gave out." Wren shrugged, eleven years old and already wise to the terrible arithmetic of human beings. "People are more than one thing. You taught me that. Sort of. By accident."

She looked past Mirabel then, up at the tower, and her voice dropped to the register she used for serious business. "Is Colonel Ashworth still here? Tam said he saw him carrying wood up the stairs at dawn. Tam says lots of things now," she added, with fierce pride, "he says them constantly, it's wonderful, we can't get him to stop."

"He's here," Mirabel said. "Sleeping, last I saw. On the floor of the oil store, if you please, like a tramp."

"He won the war and slept on the floor?"

"He'd dispute nearly every word of that sentence."

* * *

Colonel Edmund Ashworth had indeed been relieved of his command, and had learned of it in a manner he would later describe, in his report, as *irregular*, and in private, to no one, as correct.

It had happened at mid-morning, when the relief ship's senior officer — a Commodore Pell, dry as biscuit and considerably less forgiving — had come ashore, reviewed the situation, reviewed Ashworth, and reviewed twice more the account of a sergeant who had burned a government manifest and declared it illegible rather than enforce it against three hundred civilians standing in a rising tide.

Hollis had expected a cell. He got instead a long stare, a question — *"And the manifest burned well, Sergeant?"* — and a transfer to the relief operation, where men who could count and disobey in the right order were apparently in short supply. Hollis reported to the landing party looking as though he hadn't decided whether to be proud or court-martialed, and had been too busy since to settle the question.

Ashworth himself, stripped of authority by a single sentence from Pell — *"You will surrender the shore party, Colonel, and consider yourself returned to port for inquiry"* — had done the thing that most surprised Mirabel, though by then perhaps it shouldn't have.

He had climbed the lighthouse stairs.

* * *

Mirabel found him at dusk, sitting on the gallery steps outside the lamp room door, having carried up an armful of split wood earlier — Tam had sworn to it — and fed the little iron stove that took the chill off the watch room. He was not in irons. He was not under guard. He was a colonel of engineers with no command, watching the sun go down over a drowned town he had tried to save by numbering it.

"You're alive," he said, without turning. "Pell's surgeon wanted to examine you. I told him you'd refuse."

"I'd have refused."

"I know. I've read your file. Stubbornness is listed under qualifications."

Mirabel lowered themselves onto the step above him. For a while neither spoke. Below, the relief ships' boats went back and forth, ferrying the town to shelter on the mainland, and the sounds rose faintly: oars, orders, a child laughing somewhere, which after the last week sounded like a foreign language spoken fluently.

"The inquiry," Mirabel said at last. "It won't go well for you."

"It will go precisely as it goes." Ashworth turned his hat in his hands — he had taken it off, which Mirabel had never seen him do indoors or out. "I was given arithmetic and asked to make it humane. That cannot be done. I knew it could not be done. I did it anyway, because a man who refuses an impossible order is relieved, and a man who obeys one is merely promoted later, after everyone else has paid." He paused. "Sergeant Hollis understood that a week before I did. I intend to say so, formally, at the inquiry. It will be the least useful thing anyone says there."

"And the list?"

"Burned with the duplicate. The original—" he almost smiled, a small dry movement, like frost forming, "—the original dissolved in the cellar flood, along with my copy, my orders, and, I am given to understand, my career. The sea has audited us, Keeper Vane. We are all of us unaccounted for."

Mirabel looked at him sideways. Exhaustion had planed something off the man overnight — not the spine, that was intact, but the varnish. What was left underneath looked startlingly like a tired person.

"You reread it," Mirabel said. "My ledger. In the customs house. Before the storm."

Ashworth was quiet long enough that the lens above them swept around, and its beam passed over the water, over the boats, over the two of them sitting in its blind warm shadow.

"Your annotations," he said finally, "are a violation of record-keeping practice. *Marta Tull, bakes for the school children free on saints' days. Cobb Fenwick, feuds with his brother, would drown before asking him for help — note, ask the brother first if Cobb is ever missing.* You annotated a census like a parish priest."

"Somebody had to remember the parts that aren't numbers."

"Yes." He set his hat on his knee and looked at it. "My wife kept a book like that. Household accounts. Every entry had some marginal nonsense — *Jemmy's boots, again, the boy grows like weeds.* I laughed at her for it. I told her a ledger was a ledger." His thumb moved over the brim, back and forth, a small tide of its own. "She and Jemmy went down with the *Providence* off Harwich, eleven years ago. There was a list of survivors. My name was not relevant to it. I have spent eleven years learning everything about lists except the one thing worth knowing."

Mirabel said nothing. There was nothing that could be said that wasn't already written, in different ink, in the margins of the world.

At length Ashworth stood, and straightened his coat, and became almost — not quite — a colonel again.

"I'm ordered back to port tomorrow with the first transport. There will be an inquiry, and I will answer honestly, and honesty in such rooms is a form of ruin. I find I don't much mind." He hesitated, and the hesitation cost him visibly, and he paid it anyway. "I want one thing. Not clemency. Not a character reference. One page of your ledger."

Mirabel blinked. "One page."

"Any page. Whichever you choose. With the annotations intact." He met Mirabel's eyes, and behind the engineer's flat gray certainty there was something older and hungrier and very quiet. "The official record will keep the numbers. Someone ought to keep what the numbers were for. I have room in my kit for one page, and I have earned — I believe I have earned — the right to carry a reminder of what I nearly did with three hundred lives."

Mirabel studied him a moment longer. Then they nodded once, keeper to keeper, and went down the stairs to fetch the book.

They chose the page themselves, later, by lamplight, with care. Not a dramatic page. Not the page of the final berths, or the storm, or the signing. They chose a Tuesday from early autumn, ordinary as bread: weather noted fair, three coasters logged passing east, and in the margin, in Mirabel's small square hand:

*Marta Tull's oven cracked; whole town smells of her testing loaves. Old Sal caught eight mackerel and gave six away, claims she caught twelve. Wren (age 11) trimmed her first wick — steady hand. Tam folded a boat from the supply invoice. It floated.*

Ashworth took it with both hands, read it twice, and folded it along its existing creases with the precision of a man handling something explosive or holy.

"Thank you," he said. It came out rough. He cleared his throat and failed to improve it. "For the light. For the ledger. For—" he gestured vaguely at the whole ruined, rescued, impossible evening, and abandoned the sentence as beyond engineering.

"Come back in spring," Mirabel said. "The mackerel run in April. You can count them yourself."

Something moved through the man's face like weather changing. "That," he said, "is the first invitation I have received in eleven years that was not also an obligation." He put on his hat. At the stairhead he stopped, back straight, voice level, and delivered his final report of the Gannet Point requisition to the keeper of the light:

"The tower stands. The lamp burns. The garrison is evacuated. Log it however you like, Keeper — God knows you'll log it better than I would."

* * *

Night came down clean for the first time in a week, stars in fits and starts between the departing clouds, the sea working off its temper in long slow swells. Mirabel climbed to the lamp room and began the ritual — glass wiped, wicks trimmed, oil measured — and found their hands doing it without instruction while the rest of them stood somewhere far away, in a chapel, in a customs house, on wet rocks in the dark holding a sleeping boy.

On the desk lay the log, open. Beneath it, the reconstructed ledger, salt-stained, water-warped, half its pages ghosted with the shapes of words the sea had drunk — and yet legible, mostly, because Mirabel had written everything twice, in the habit of a lifetime, because paper lies but habit doesn't.

Tomorrow the relief officers would want the ledger entered into the official record. There would be forms. There would be questions about annotations, irregularities, unauthorized marginalia. Somewhere ashore, three hundred people were waking in borrowed beds, and among them a girl who had stolen a list and a sergeant who had burned one and a boy who had folded a boat for every name in the town, planning to give them all back to the sea so it would return them — and it had, it had, every single one —

Mirabel dipped the pen.

The log waited, open at the fresh page, its ruled columns empty: *Date. Wind. Weather. Remarks.*

Remarks.

Mirabel looked at the column a long time. Then they wrote the date, and the wind — *moderating, NNW, dying with the storm's manners* — and under *Weather* they wrote *clearing,* and then they moved the pen to the remarks column and let it hover, because what belonged there tonight was not weather at all, and the official rules said otherwise, and Mirabel Vane had spent one entire winter learning exactly which rules the sea respects.

The pen touched the page.
