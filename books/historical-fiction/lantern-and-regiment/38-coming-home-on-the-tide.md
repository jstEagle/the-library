## 38. Coming Home on the Tide

The water went out the way a guest leaves who has overstayed his welcome — gradually at first, and then all at once, leaving mud.

Mirabel saw it from the gallery before anyone in the town could have seen it from anywhere, because that was the privilege and punishment of living ninety feet above everything: you were always the first to know. The harbor had been a brown lake for weeks, lapping at the chapel steps, drowning Marta Tull's ovens, turning the customs house into an island where Colonel Ashworth's soldiers stood their pointless watch like chess pieces nobody had bothered to put away. And then one morning the lake had a shoreline again. Then it had a beach. Then, three days after that, it had a smell — salt and rot and, underneath it, something older and cleaner: the smell of Gannet Point being itself again.

Mirabel trimmed the wick anyway. The storm had passed; the evacuation had succeeded; there was no ship expected until the relief vessel came Thursday. But Ede had taught them that a light was not kept for the ships you expected. It was kept for the ones you didn't.

"You're doing it again," Wren said from the doorway of the lamp room, arms crossed, in the particular way she crossed them that meant she was eleven going on forty. "Trimming when there's nothing to trim."

"The lens doesn't know that."

"The lens is glass."

"Glass remembers," Mirabel said, which made no sense whatsoever, and Wren rolled her eyes and let it stand, because in the weeks since the boats had gone, the two of them had developed an entire language of things that made no sense whatsoever and meant a great deal.

Tam was below, in the kitchen, folding paper. He folded paper the way other children breathed — constantly, unconsciously, sheets of it appearing out of nowhere, each one becoming a small white boat. Since he had spoken his word — *stay*, one syllable that had cracked the winter open like river ice — the boats had changed. Before, every boat had carried a name inside it: names of the lost, names of the departed, names Tam had copied off the berth list in careful pencil. Now the boats were empty. Or rather — Mirabel had checked, guiltily, the way one checks — they weren't carrying names of the missing anymore. They were blank. Waiting to be filled.

The tide was coming back for the town. Everyone knew it would be days yet before the road dried enough for carts, but the fishing fleet had never left — the fishermen had refused berths on the evacuation ships with the same stubbornness they refused everything — so it was Brine Ollie who brought the news, poling his flat-bottomed punt up what had been the harbor road two mornings ago, shouting before he was even within hailing distance:

"She's receding! Water's down past the baker's step! By Sunday you could fry a kipper on the cobbles!"

He'd said it as though frying kippers on cobbles were the town's highest civic ambition, and maybe it was. Maybe it had always been.

* * *

Sunday came, and with it the boats.

They came across from the mainland in ones and twos at first — the Kestrel making three trips, her smuggler-built hull proving sturdier than anything His Majesty's dockyards had ever produced, which Brine Ollie mentioned approximately forty times per crossing. Then the big evacuation ships came too, the ones that had carried away nearly four hundred souls in the dark of the great storm, and the harbor — half-mud, half-water, wholly itself — filled with sails the way the chapel filled on Easter: everyone at once, jostling, calling, someone always singing slightly out of tune.

Mirabel watched from the gallery, and beside them stood Wren and Tam, and none of them spoke, because some sights are too large for speech.

The first person off the first boat was not a dignitary or an official of any kind. It was a child — one of the Pike girls — who leapt onto the quay before the gangplank was properly down, ran twenty feet inland, stopped, turned around, and screamed at the top of her lungs: "IT'S STILL HERE!" And then the whole boatload came off at a run, and the boat behind it, and the town poured back into its own streets like water finding its old channels.

"They're running home," Tam said. It was only the fourth or fifth sentence anyone had heard him speak voluntarily since his grandmother drowned, and each one still landed like a coin dropped in a quiet church.

"Yes," Mirabel said.

"Not to houses. To the town." He considered this with the gravity he gave everything. "Houses can be built again. I asked Marta."

"What did Marta say?"

"Marta said the houses missed us worse than we missed them."

From a nine-year-old, delivered flat as a ledger line, it was somehow the truest thing anyone had said all year. Mirabel looked at the streets filling up below — people standing in doorways, testing hinges, weeping over garden fences, arguing cheerfully about whose chimney had fallen — and thought: *Ede, look.* Not because Ede had loved this town particularly, though he had. Because Ede had believed, with a certainty Mirabel had spent a year resenting, that keeping a thing was not the same as owning it, and that a light kept burning long enough would bring its people home.

The light had burned. The people had come home. Mirabel was not yet ready to call this proof, but they were no longer prepared to call it coincidence either.

* * *

Marta Tull reopened the bakery on Monday, which surprised no one, because Marta had once reopened it during a fire, and by Tuesday morning the queue stretched past the ruined fountain, and by Tuesday noon she had run out of everything twice and was baking in defiance of both supply and sense, handing loaves across her counter with flour to her elbows and joy all over her face.

"Vane!" she bellowed when Mirabel came in — Wren and Tam in tow, because the children went where bread was, as iron filings go to a magnet. "Get in here before the mob eats my counter. First loaf of the new oven goes to the keeper of the light. That's law now. I've decided. It's on the sign."

There was indeed a sign, freshly painted, hanging crooked over the door: *TULL'S BAKERY — FIRST LOAF TO THE LIGHT*. Beneath it, smaller, in different handwriting that was unmistakably Wren's: *and second loaf to whoever holds Marta's ladder.*

"I held your ladder for ten minutes," Wren protested. "Ten whole minutes."

"And worth every minute," Marta said, pressing a second loaf into her arms. "You'll note I wrote it down. That's how laws work. Ask the colonel."

At the name, a small silence moved through the shop — not a cold silence, exactly. More like the pause when someone mentions a relative everyone has complicated feelings about. Ashworth was still at the customs house, relieved of command but not yet recalled, awaiting the inquiry that everyone agreed would come and no one could quite wish speedily upon him. He had, in the weeks since the storm, done a thing nobody expected: he had stayed. He walked the flooded streets in his immaculate coat taking notes — not requisition notes, the townsfolk noticed, but survey notes, drainage notes, notes on which wells were fouled and which foundations had held. He was, several people observed uneasily, doing arithmetic again. But it was arithmetic aimed at rebuilding, and even Cobb Fenwick allowed that this was better use of a soldier than chalk marks on doors.

Even Cobb.

Cobb Fenwick came into the bakery while they were still there, and the room did that thing rooms do — the conversational equivalent of everyone suddenly studying their shoes. Cobb had traded the Kestrel's secret for his family's berths, and everyone knew it, and Cobb knew everyone knew it, and he walked through the town like a man crossing a frozen pond, listening for the crack under every footstep.

He saw Mirabel. He stopped. He opened his mouth, closed it, opened it again, and finally said, in the voice of a man defusing his own reputation: "Heard your lamp held all night. Through the worst of it."

"It did."

"My girls were on the Kestrel when she took the reef passage." His jaw worked. "Ollie says he steered by you. By the light."

"He steered by the light. I just kept it lit."

"Aye, well." Cobb looked at the floor, the ceiling, the bread. "That's the same thing, near enough." He bought a loaf, paid without haggling — the town would talk about *that* for a month — and paused at the door. "I did wrong, Vane. I won't pretend otherwise and you'd see through me if I tried. But my girls are alive, and—" He stopped, started again. "I'm on the repair crew for the seawall. Come spring. If the light needs stone carted, or oil, or — anything. You'll have it. No charge, ever."

"That's very—"

"Don't make a speech," Cobb said fiercely, and fled, and the whole bakery exhaled, and Marta said, "Well. There's a thing I never thought I'd see: Cobb Fenwick paying full price," and the laughter that followed was the kind that heals more than it wounds.

Wren leaned against Mirabel's arm. "Are you going to forgive him?"

"I forgave him somewhere around the third week of watching him carry sandbags alone," Mirabel said. "Don't tell him. He's much easier to manage ashamed."

* * *

The days that followed had a rhythm that Mirabel distrusted precisely because they trusted it.

Morning: trim the lamp, log the weather, walk the path down into a town that grew louder daily. Hammers everywhere. The sound of Gannet Point that season was nails — ringing off the chapel roof, the fish cellars, the schoolhouse, twelve hammering at once some days until the whole headland rang like a struck bell. Afternoon: Wren on the gallery learning the lens — she had a knack, a steady hand and an instinct for the mechanism that made Mirabel's chest hurt in a way they were slowly learning wasn't grief. Evening: supper at the table that used to seat one.

It was on one of these evenings, soup and fresh bread, Tam folding a boat between bites with one hand, that Wren said, casually, dangerously: "The relief ship comes Thursday."

"Mm."

"With the new supplies. And the inquiry officers." She tore her bread with great concentration. "And probably orders for the colonel to go somewhere else."

"Probably."

"And Hollis." She said the sergeant's name with the elaborate indifference of someone who had definitely not been asking Brine Ollie about the man's posting. "Hollis might get reassigned. Or promoted. Or court-martialed, Ollie says, for the manifest business, except Ollie says nobody upstairs will ever admit the manifest was illegible because then they'd have to explain why, and explaining why means explaining the list, and—"

"Wren."

"—and explaining the list means explaining *me*," she finished, chin up, daring Mirabel to make it a problem.

Mirabel set down the spoon. "Sergeant Hollis committed no crime that I witnessed. A manifest damaged by seawater is a tragedy of weather. I'll say so to whoever asks. So will half the town, and the other half will say it louder."

Wren studied them for a long moment, the way she studied the lens — looking for flaws, finding the mechanism sound. "You'd lie for him."

"I'd tell the truth *carefully*," Mirabel said. "It's a skill. Your grandmother had it, I suspect, and passed it down."

Something flickered across Tam's face at the word grandmother — not pain, not anymore, or not only pain. He set his spoon down, reached into his pocket, and unfolded a paper boat. This one was not blank. Inside, in his careful pencil, was written a single word, and he slid it across the table toward Mirabel without ceremony, the way he did everything that mattered.

Mirabel opened it. The word was *home*.

"That's the one for the lighthouse," Tam said. "For the ledger. When the sea gives them back, they need to know where to go."

Mirabel looked at the small paper boat, at the boy who had folded a fleet to save a town, at the girl already calculating Thursday's tides, at the table set for four where it had been set for one — and found that their throat had closed entirely, and that the soup required no further eating, and that some words must simply be nodded at, which is what they did.

Later, washing dishes, Wren appeared at their elbow with the expression she wore when about to be practical, which was her most frightening expression. "Thursday," she said. "When the ship comes. We should set out cups."

"Cups?"

"For tea. On the table. In case people come up the path. People come up paths when ships come in." She was already counting on her fingers. "So — you and me and Tam, that's three. Hollis might come. And Ollie. And Marta always sends someone. So we'd need... how many cups do you actually have? Because I looked, and it's two, and two is a number with a history in this house, and I think the history could use revising."

Mirabel stood with a dish towel in their hands and felt the whole strange architecture of the last year settle around them — the grief that had been a load-bearing wall, the loneliness that had been a locked door, the two cups that had been a shrine — and understood that the child had just proposed renovations.

"There's a crate in the cellar," Mirabel said at last. "Ede's mother's china. Six cups. I couldn't—" They stopped. Started again. "I couldn't unpack it before."

"And now?"

Mirabel looked out the window, down the darkening path toward the town, where forty lamps were being lit one by one in forty windows, each flame a small act of faith that morning would come and find someone home.

"Now I think we count heads first," Mirabel said, "and cups after. Go ask Tam how many boats he'd need for everyone we know."

Wren grinned and thundered down the stairs, and from the kitchen came Tam's voice, grave and clear: "All of them. I'll need all of them. And more paper."

Mirabel hung up the towel, took down the logbook, and began to write the day's entry — weather fair, wind southwest, tide receding — and then stopped, pen hovering, remembering a promise made to themselves on a gallery rail weeks ago, and added beneath the official lines a single unofficial sentence, in the margin, where the annotations lived:

*Town returning. Count so far: everyone.*

Outside, the lamp turned above the healed roofs, and Thursday was coming, and the house would need every cup it had.
