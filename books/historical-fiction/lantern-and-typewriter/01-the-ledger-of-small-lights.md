## 1. The Ledger of Small Lights

The lamps were lit before dark, because dark came early in October and because Matron Cobb believed that a ward without light was a ward already half surrendered. Freya Ashgrove trimmed the wicks along the long room, moving between the iron cots with a brass taper, and watched each small flame take hold and stand up straight against the draft that crept under the door like a living thing.

Outside, the guns had settled into their evening argument — not the screaming barrage of an attack but the low, patient exchange that went on all night, every night, as though two enormous animals lay facing one another across the mud and growled in their sleep. She had stopped hearing it months ago. That frightened her more than the shells did, when she let herself think about it: how quickly the soul made its accommodations.

"Private Hollis wants his blanket straightened," said Tam from the doorway, a boy of seventeen doing his best to fill out a uniform two sizes too large for him. He carried a bedpan in one hand and a look of terrible earnestness on his face, as if both were matters of state. "He keeps asking."

"I'll come."

Hollis lay three beds from the stove, his left leg gone above the knee, wrapped in bandages that would need changing again before midnight. He was twenty-two years old and looked younger lying down, his hands folded on his chest like a choirboy's. When she bent over him he caught her wrist — gently, always gently now, the dying learned gentleness — and said, "Sister. Sister, I want to ask you something, and I don't want you to say it's all right if it isn't."

Freya sat on the edge of the cot. The springs complained beneath her. "Ask me anything you like, Private."

"My mum's in Norwich." His eyes were very clear tonight; she had seen that clarity before and knew what it meant, though the doctors still spoke hopefully at rounds. "I've wrote her eleven letters since I come out here. Eleven. And I never once put down my proper name in full, because we was told not to, in case of — well. In case." He swallowed. "It's Albert Edward Hollis. Not just A. Hollis like on the tag. Albert Edward, after my grandfather and my uncle both, and my mother will want it said proper when they tell her. Will you remember it? If — afterward?"

The question sat in the air between them, small and enormous.

"I'll remember it," Freya said.

"You're not just saying."

"I am not just saying."

He nodded then, satisfied, and let his head sink back into the pillow, and within the hour he slept, and by morning the clarity had gone somewhere else entirely.

* * *

That night, after lights-out, after Matron's final round and the soft percussion of Tam's boots going down the corridor to the boiler room where he slept, Freya took the hymnal from beneath her mattress and carried it to the little alcove off the supply room where the carbolic and the lint were stored.

She had chosen the hymnal deliberately, months ago, from a crate of comforts sent out by some church in Kent. *Hymns Ancient and Modern*, bound in black cloth gone grey at the corners, its spine cracked from use. No one questioned a hymnal. Sisters were expected to have them; chaplains pressed them into soldiers' hands at the gate; they were as unremarkable as boots. Inside, past the gilt-edged psalms and the sewn-in ribbon, someone with a razor blade had hollowed a rectangle from the pages — ninety sheets deep, cut clean through the heart of the book, so that *Abide With Me* and *Rock of Ages* formed walls around a space the size of a cigarette tin.

Into that space fit the ledger.

It was a school exercise book, quarter-bound in marbled card, forty-eight pages, bought for twopence in Poperinghe by a girl who told the stationer it was for keeping accounts of the linen. In a sense this was true. It kept accounts of things the army counted differently.

Freya sat on an upturned crate with the hymnal open across her knees and the exercise book balanced on her thigh, and by the light of a shielded candle — a biscuit tin with a slit cut in the lid, the trick Tam had taught her — she turned to the page marked with a sliver of red thread and began to write.

*Albert Edward Hollis. Private, 7th Norfolks. Died 14 October 1917, wounds (left leg, gas gangrene), No. 3 Casualty Clearing, near Ypres.*

Below it she added, because she had begun adding such things and could no longer help herself: *Asked for his blanket to be straightened. Asked to be remembered. Mother in Norwich. Wrote eleven letters home.*

Then she waited, as she always waited, until the candle guttered low, listening to the building settle and the guns mutter, and thought about what the official record would say.

She knew what it would say, because she had learned to read dispatches the way other people read weather. Somewhere in a bureau in Rouen or London a clerk would enter: *A. Hollis, 7/Norf., died of wounds* — or possibly not even that. Possibly the regimental returns would show him as *missing, later presumed dead*, which spared everyone the trouble of a date. Possibly, if the paperwork of the last fortnight held true to form, he would not appear anywhere at all except in the ledgers of graves registration, a number attached to a cross in a field that his mother would spend the rest of her life unable to find on any map worth buying.

Eleven men had died in this station in the last nine days. The dispatch list posted outside the adjutant's office named four.

Freya had not set out to keep a counter-record. That was the thing she could never explain later, when explaining mattered — that the Lantern had begun not as defiance but as arithmetic, a nurse's ordinary habit of counting what passed through her hands. In her first weeks she had simply noticed the gaps, the way one notices a clock ticking wrong. Then the gaps grew. Then, one night in August, after a boy from the Rifle Brigade died with no name at all — his tags lost, his mates all dead too, nothing left of him but a face and a voice that called for a sister named Ada who was not there — she had found herself writing *Unknown, Rifle Brigade, called for Ada* in the back of the exercise book, and understood that she had crossed a line she could not see the far side of, and that she did not wish to find it.

The officers had their reasons. She tried, sometimes, to hold them fairly in mind. The war ate names the way the mud ate bodies; the machinery of record-keeping was vast and strained and staffed by men who themselves were stretched past breaking. Some omissions were accident. Some were chaos. But not all. Not the ones where a company had been fed into a salient that the maps said was friendly ground and the machine guns said otherwise, and the returns came back light, and someone senior decided that a light return reflected badly on the order that had made it light. She had heard enough at the edge of conversations, enough fragments over tea in the sisters' mess, to know that the erasure was sometimes not a wound in the system but a function of it.

*Albert Edward Hollis,* she wrote once more at the bottom of the entry, tracing the letters slowly, as if the act itself were a kind of prayer — and perhaps it was; she was not certain anymore what separated the two — *remembered.*

She blew out the candle. In the dark the hymnal closed around its secret like two hands folding shut.

* * *

There was a game the nurses played in the quiet hours, when the ward slept and the stove ticked and the wind worried the canvas annexes. They called it *the small lights*. Iris had invented it, or claimed to; Iris claimed everything, cheerfully, from the invention of card games to the discovery of the best corner in the mess tent. You named one small thing that had gone right that day, one point of light, and you were not allowed to name anything large. Not *the bombardment lifted* or *my leave came through.* Only small things.

"The bread wasn't green," Tam offered, on the night after Hollis died.

"That's not small, that's miraculous," said Iris, who had hair the color of struck matches and a laugh that got them all shushed at least once a week.

"A letter," Freya said, when her turn came. She felt the lie of omission sit lightly, almost comfortably, and disliked herself for how comfortable it sat. "A letter arrived."

"From home?"

"From — a friend." The word was inadequate and she knew it, but adequacy was not available to her on that subject. "An old friend."

Iris looked at her a moment longer than the game required, with those quick grey eyes that missed very little, and Freya understood that she had given something away — not the substance, but the shape of the thing, the heat of it. She covered it by dealing the next hand.

But it was true, and it was not a small thing, and she had lied about it twice over, and she lay awake afterward in her cot with the hymnal beneath the mattress pressing its rectangular silence against her back, thinking about the letters.

They had begun during her training, two years ago — careful, witty, unexpectedly tender letters from a Captain Aldous Vane, whom she had met exactly once, at a lecture in Cambridge on field sanitation, where he had stood at the back making dry marginal remarks to whoever would listen and she had been the only one who listened. He wrote about the war as though it were a badly run hospital and he its exasperated consultant. He wrote about books. He asked questions no one else asked — what the ward sounded like at three in the morning; whether the men dreamed in dialect; whether she, personally, believed the dispatches. That last question had arrived in March, and she had read it a dozen times before answering, and her answer had taken her three evenings to write and was, in the end, one line: *No. I have started keeping my own count.*

His reply had come back fast, as if he had been waiting for it: *Keep it well. Names are the only currency that doesn't inflate.*

And then, in September of 1916 — Passchendaele was not yet a word worn smooth by horror; it was still only a ridge with a village on it — a letter had reached her with black edging, official, brief. *Captain A. Vane, killed in action.* There had been no personal note. Officers' effects were returned to next of kin; she was not next of kin; she had no standing to inquire. She had grieved the way one grieves a correspondence: privately, disproportionately, and with the persistent irrational feeling that a mistake had been made somewhere in the machinery.

She kept his last letter folded inside the Lantern. It seemed the correct archive for it.

* * *

On the morning after Hollis's death, Freya did what she always did with the newly dead: she helped prepare them, and she checked the tags, and she noted the grave registration numbers when they came, and she entered everything in the ward book in her neat, compliant hand — *Pte. A. Hollis, 7th Norf., DoW* — and then, at the first opportunity, she slipped away and opened the hymnal and made sure the true account matched.

It was becoming a liturgy. She recognized it as such with a kind of clinical detachment, the way she might diagnose a fever in another woman: the hidden book was her chapel now, the ten minutes with the candle her offices of matins and vespers. She did not know whether God attended. She suspected that whatever attended was older than God and less forgiving.

Matron Cobb found her coming out of the supply alcove.

There was nothing remarkable in this; the alcove was on the way to everything, and Matron moved through her station like weather through a valley, everywhere at once, noticing everything. But Freya's heart performed a small ugly maneuver nonetheless, and she was aware that her hand was closed around the hymnal as though it were a wounded bird.

"Sister Ashgrove." Matron Cobb was sixty if she was a day, built like a dock crane, with a voice that could stop a hemorrhage at twenty paces. Her eyes went to the hymnal, rested there for one second — one second too long, or perhaps exactly long enough, Freya could never afterward decide — and moved on. "Private Hollis's effects are ready?"

"Yes, Matron. Tagged and listed."

"Good." She paused. The stove ticked. Somewhere beyond the canvas a lorry changed gear. "You were fond of him?"

"He was a good patient."

"That's not what I asked."

Freya met her eyes. "Yes, Matron. I was fond of him."

Something moved behind Matron Cobb's face — not softness exactly, but the place where softness is kept, the way ammunition is kept, in a dugout behind sandbags. "Four names on the board this morning," she said. "We had eleven deaths in nine days. I can count, Sister. I was counting before you were born." She said it without heat, without accusation, in the tone of a woman laying a single card face-down on a table and watching to see what her opponent does with it.

"Yes, Matron."

"Hm." She adjusted her veil with two brisk tugs. "The dead don't need us to make trouble for ourselves. But somebody ought to be able to tell their mothers the truth of it, when the truth is wanted. Mind it isn't your neck you lose over it." And she walked on, and Freya stood in the corridor holding a hymnal and understanding that she had just been seen — not the whole of it, perhaps, but the outline, the way a sentry sees a figure at the edge of a field and does not yet fire.

* * *

That evening the ambulances came down from the salient in a chain, mud to the axles, and the ward filled, and the night became the night it always became — stretchers and scissors and the sweet stench of gas gangrene, Tam running, Iris's voice steady as a metronome over the worst of it, Morphia. Morphia. Hold him. Hold him. — until near dawn the tide receded and left the ward strewn with the breathing and the not-breathing.

One of the not-breathing was a private of the West Ridings, nineteen years old, brought in with his abdomen opened by a fragment and no chance from the first minute, though he fought for six hours anyway with the dumb astonishing courage of the very young. Toward the end, when the morphine had drawn a curtain between him and the pain, he gripped Freya's hand and pulled her close and said, in the accent of some mill town she couldn't place, "Tell our kid I got the pigeon. Tell him I won." And then, drifting, urgent, clutching at her sleeve: "My name. Say my name, Sister. They'll get it wrong. They always get it wrong."

"They won't," she said. "Tell me."

"Joseph Amos Fearnley. Joseph Amos. Not Joe. Me mum calls me Joseph Amos when I'm in trouble." A ghost of a grin. "Say it."

"Joseph Amos Fearnley," she said clearly, and watched the words land in him like a lamp being set down carefully in a dark room.

"That's it," he whispered. "That's me. Now I'm — somebody—" and the sentence never finished, and the grip loosened, and the monitor of his breathing that every nurse carries invisibly in her chest went flat and silent.

Freya closed his eyes. Around her the ward went on — the moaning, the boiling urn, Tam's footsteps, the guns outside maintaining their low insomniac argument — and she stood for a moment in the middle of it all feeling the strange double exposure that had become her life: the visible world, ordered and official, in which Joseph Amos Fearnley would enter history as J. Fearnley or as nothing at all; and the invisible world she tended by candlelight, in which every syllable of him was kept, and kept, and kept.

At dawn, when the relief came and the stretcher-bearers carried him out under a blanket, she went to the alcove. Her hands were steady. They were always steady now; that was another thing she had stopped being proud of.

*Joseph Amos Fearnley. Private, 2nd West Riding. Died 15 October 1917, abdominal wounds, 4 a.m. Won a bet about a pigeon. His mother calls him Joseph Amos when he is in trouble. Remembered.*

She was cutting the candle when she heard it — not a sound so much as a change in the quality of the silence, the sense of the building attending to something. Through the slit in the biscuit tin she saw the shadow of a figure pass the gap under the supply-room door and pause there. Boots. Regulation tread, but unhurried, the tread of a man who has traveled a long way and is deciding something.

Then knuckles on the wood, twice, softly, and a voice — a stranger's voice, low, pitched for one listener only:

"Sister Ashgrove? My name is Elias Wren. I've brought something from Captain Aldous Vane." A pause, the length of a held breath. "You'll want to hear the rest, Sister, because the dispatches say he's been dead a year — and I'm telling you they're wrong."

The candle flame stood perfectly still between them, and Freya, with the Lantern open on her knee and forty-one names' worth of grief already gathering in the dark like weather, did not move either.
