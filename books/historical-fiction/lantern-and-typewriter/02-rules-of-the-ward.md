## 2. Rules of the Ward

Matron Cobb had three rules, and she recited them every morning at seven sharp, standing at the head of the ward with her hands folded like a woman about to say grace.

"Rule one: the ward comes first. Rule two: you do not promise a soldier what the army cannot give him. Rule three: you do not lie to me."

She said them in the same flat voice she used for everything — dressings, deaths, the weather. She was a broad woman built like a doorframe, with grey hair pinned so tightly it seemed structural, and eyes that had catalogued every kind of human misery and filed it under *work to be done*. The men called her the Cobb behind her back, as in a wall, and she pretended not to know, which was itself a kindness.

Freya had learned all three rules within a week of arriving at the dressing station outside Ypres, and had broken rule three within a month.

Not openly. Never openly. Matron Cobb would have smelled an open lie the way a hound smells rain. Freya's lie was small and silent and lived inside a hymnal, and every morning when Matron recited her rules, Freya stood at attention with her hands at her sides and felt the hymnal's absence against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

*You do not lie to me.*

The ward came first. That much was easy. The ward was everything — the long double rows of stretcher frames, the smell of carbolic layered over the sweeter rot of gangrene that no amount of disinfectant ever fully drowned, the hiss of the stove, the groans that became silence and silences that were worse than groans. The ward had its own weather, its own tides. You learned to read it the way sailors read the sky: a stillness in the corner where a boy was going quiet too fast meant fever turning septic; a man who asked for his mother in a voice pitched for a nursery needed holding more than morphine; the ones who joked were either healing or dying and it took skill to know which.

"You're staring again," Iris said, brushing past with a basin of water gone pink as weak tea. "Staring doesn't set bones, Ashgrove."

Iris Marchmont moved through the ward the way a needle moves through cloth — quick, precise, leaving everything held together behind her. She was twenty-three, two years older than Freya, with a face that might have been pretty if she'd ever let it rest, and hands that were already an old nurse's hands at an age when most girls were still learning which end of a thermometer went where. Her father was a vicar; her brother a captain in the Suffolks; her loyalty to the army was not sentiment but architecture, load-bearing, the thing the whole house stood on.

"The army is a machine," she'd told Freya their first week, wringing out bandages with those tireless hands. "Machines work because every part does exactly what it's told. The moment parts start improvising, people die. More than have to, I mean." She'd looked up then, and there had been something almost pleading in it. "There's enough death here without us adding to it by getting clever."

Freya had nodded and said yes, of course, and meant none of it, and hated herself a little for both.

* * *

Tam found her at the hymnal.

Not deliberately — Tam never did anything deliberately except eat, and even that he approached with the air of a man surprised daily by food's continued existence. He was seventeen, the station's youngest orderly, all elbows and earnestness, with a cowlick that defeated every attempt to flatten it and a habit of narrating his own work under his breath. *"Right then, Private Wilkes, off we go, mind your arm, that's the way, lovely."* He talked to the wounded like they could answer, and sometimes, Freya suspected, they did — in the small ways that mattered.

He caught her in the supply tent on a grey Thursday afternoon, when the shelling had eased to a grumble in the east like an indigestive god, and she thought she'd shut the flap properly and hadn't.

She had the hymnal open on the packing crate. Inside, where *Abide With Me* should have been, there was a page sewn from flour-sack linen, and on the page, in her smallest hand, thirty-one names. Thirty-one men who had come through this station and gone into the ground or onto a train without their names ever appearing in any dispatch, any casualty list, any record anywhere that their mothers could find. The ledger she called the Lantern, though she'd never said that word aloud to anyone, because saying it aloud would make it real, and real things got people shot.

Tam stood in the doorway with a stack of folded blankets, his mouth open.

For three full seconds neither of them moved.

"It's a hymnal," Freya said, which was true, and also the stupidest sentence ever spoken by a guilty woman.

"Yeah," said Tam. His eyes went from her face to the book and back. "Only — only hymnals usually have the hymns in them. Miss."

The blankets slipped. He caught them, fumbled, caught them again. And then he did something that undid her completely: he stepped inside the tent, pulled the flap shut behind him, and stood with his back against it like a sentry.

"I didn't see nothing," he said. "Whatever it is. I didn't see it."

"Tam—"

"I'm good at not seeing things." He lifted his chin, and for once there was nothing boyish in his face at all. "Me dad ran a pub in Stepney. Coppers come in regular. You learn what seeing is, and what it isn't. Seeing is a choice, miss. I'm choosing."

He turned and left before she could find words, and she sat down hard on the crate, her heart going like a drum corps, and understood that the ledger had just stopped being hers alone. Not in fact — Tam had seen pages and names and nothing else, and would ask nothing, because asking made you part of things and Tam was very clear about what he chose to see. But in weight. A secret shared, even accidentally, even wordlessly, is a secret doubled, and Freya carried it back into the ward feeling the extra ounce of it in every step.

That night she improved the hiding place. She cut the hymnal's spine lining loose with a sterilized scalpel, fitted the linen pages flat against the boards beneath the false cover, and glued it down with the flour paste Tam brought her without being asked and without comment. From the outside it was a hymnal, worn and water-stained, one among dozens the chaplain kept in a crate by the operating tent. From the inside it was a lantern, and thirty-one names burned in it now, and she slept badly and better than she deserved.

* * *

Sunday, Matron Cobb found her at the stove.

Not to reprimand. Matron never reprimanded when she could instead arrange circumstances so that reprimanding became unnecessary — it was one of her quieter talents, and one of the reasons the ward loved her without ever quite daring to say so. She simply appeared beside Freya with two chipped mugs of the chicory that passed for coffee, handed her one, and watched the rain come down over the duckboards for a while.

"My brother," she said at last, "was a fisherman. Off Hull. Terrible weather, fishermen. Half the boats lost some years." She sipped. "He used to say the sea has rules, same as a ward. Keep to your station. Trust the ship. And never, never turn your back on the water." She glanced sideways. "You've been keeping to your station very well lately, Nurse Ashgrove. Almost too well. Like a woman minding where she stands."

Freya's mug stopped halfway to her mouth.

"I don't know what you mean, Matron."

"No," Matron Cobb agreed placidly. "I expect you don't." She drained her chicory, set the mug on the stove ledge, and patted Freya's shoulder once — a blow that would have felled a smaller woman — before walking away down the ward, checking drips and dressings, calling each man by name, every name correct, every name known.

And that was the thing about Matron Cobb, Freya thought, watching her go. Every name. She knew them all — the wounded, the dying, the dead. She wrote them herself in the station's own ledger, in her own square hand, and if the dispatches that went back to England thinned those names out like broth, well, that was above her station, wasn't it. Wasn't it.

Wasn't it.

* * *

It was Iris who nearly ended everything, eleven days later, and it happened because Iris was kind, which was the cruelest joke of it.

A convoy had come in at dusk — forty men off a train that had taken twelve hours to move nine miles, and the ward had run until two in the morning, and when it finally emptied of screaming, Freya had fallen asleep sitting upright in the supply tent with the hymnal open on her knees, one hand resting on the linen page like a child asleep on a forbidden toy.

"Wake up." A hand on her shoulder, gentle. "Freya. You'll catch your death."

She surfaced into lamplight and Iris's face, close, tired, concerned. And Iris's eyes went down — naturally, innocently, the way eyes go to whatever is in front of them — to the open book.

"What's that? Not the chaplain's, surely. He counts them like spoons."

"It's mine," Freya heard herself say, and her voice came out level, which astonished her. "From home. My mother's. I like to have it near."

"May I?" Iris reached.

Every possible future passed through Freya in a single white instant: the linen page, the thirty-one names, Iris reading them, Iris understanding, Iris walking to the adjutant's tent because Iris believed in machines and rules and doing exactly what you were told, because that was how you saved people, wasn't it, that was the whole point—

"I'd rather you didn't," Freya said. "It's — the binding's broken. It falls apart if you open it wide. I keep meaning to mend it."

Something flickered across Iris's face. Not suspicion — something before suspicion, something more like hurt, quickly banked. Because in eight months of sharing a hut and a stove and the worst nights of the war, they had never lied to each other. Small omissions, perhaps. Kindnesses. Never lies.

"All right," Iris said quietly. "Keep it shut, then."

She gathered the sleeping kit she'd come for and left, and her footsteps went down the boardwalk slow, slower than usual, and did not speed up, and Freya sat alone in the lamplight with her heart hammering and the taste of the lie still in her mouth like coin metal.

*You do not lie to me,* Matron had said. She hadn't said anything about lying to Iris. But it amounted to the same thing, didn't it. The found family — Cobb and Tam and Iris, the stove and the duckboards and the three rules recited at seven sharp — was the only warm thing in five hundred miles of mud, and Freya had just put her hand through its window.

She opened the ledger and looked at the names. Private Ellis Hargreaves, 8th Yorkshires, died of wounds, no record. Corporal Daniel Okafor, King's African Rifles attached, died en route, no record. On down the list, thirty-one lights burning in a book disguised as praise for God, because God, presumably, was looking elsewhere.

"I'm sorry," she whispered — to Iris, to the names, to whoever collected apologies for this kind of thing. Then she closed the hymnal, pressed it shut until the paste held, and blew out the lamp.

Outside, the guns had started again in the east, patient and rhythmic, like a heart that would go on beating whether anyone remembered its name or not.

Three days later a runner came up the duckboard path through the drizzle, a stranger in a civilian coat with mud to the knees and a canvas satchel clutched to his chest, and asked at the gate — politely, terribly politely — for Nurse Ashgrove.
