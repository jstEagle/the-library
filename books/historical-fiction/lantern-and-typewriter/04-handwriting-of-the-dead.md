## 4. Handwriting of the Dead

The letters came to me first in the spring of 1915, when I was still a probationer at St. Aldhelm's, still afraid of the matron's footsteps in the corridor and of blood that would not stop. They arrived in brown paper packets, tied with string, addressed in a hand I would have known anywhere — even now, even with the mud of Flanders between us, even dead.

Captain Aldous Vane wrote the way other men built cathedrals: slowly, deliberately, each letter placed as though it might be judged. His capitals were tall and narrow, like men standing at attention. His lowercase *g* had a tail that curled beneath the line like something drowning and refusing to drown. He crossed his *t*s with a single decisive stroke, never twice, and he dotted his *i*s precisely at their centers — a small vanity, I thought then, a small order imposed on a disordered world.

I knew his hand before I ever saw it on paper, because I had seen it in the ward ledger at Netley, where he'd been convalescing from a shoulder wound the winter I trained there. He was forty-one then, grey at the temples, with a voice like a door opening into a warm room. He read to the blind boys on Thursdays. When the ward clerk fell ill, he kept the records himself, and I used to watch him write from across the aisle — watch the pen move without hesitation, watch the names go down whole and unbroken, every man accounted for.

*Every man accounted for.* That is what I loved in him, though I did not name it love until much later, and by then it was too late for naming.

He began writing to me after I was posted. Not love letters — nothing so simple, nothing so compromising. Letters about the war as he understood it, which meant letters about men: Private Dunn who sang in his sleep; Sergeant Malloway who kept a photograph of two different wives and could not decide which lie was kinder; a boy named Finch who had never seen the sea and described the mud at Ypres as being like the inside of a bell. Vane wrote me the war's inventory. And always, always, the names. He believed — he told me once, in the third letter, in a sentence I copied out and kept beneath my pillow like a relic — *that a name written down honestly is a small lamp lit against the dark, and the dark is very old and very patient, but it cannot put out every lamp.*

I wrote back. I told him about the ward, about Matron Cobb's rules, about Iris's terrible singing, about the boy who died holding my hand and calling me by his sister's name. I did not know then that I was practicing, letter by letter, for a ledger I would not begin for another year.

Then Passchendaele came, and the letters stopped.

* * *

"Where did you get these?"

My voice sounded strange to me in the dispensary — too high, too thin, like wire under tension. Elias Wren stood on the far side of the counter with his cap in his hands, turning it slowly, and he watched me with the particular patience of a man who has asked this question of himself many times and never answered it.

"You've read them," he said. It was not a question either.

"They're addressed to me." I held the topmost letter up between two fingers. The paper was soft with handling, foxed along one edge where damp had found it. My own name stood at the head in that cathedral hand — *Nurse F. Ashgrove* — the *F* tall and narrow, standing at attention. "This is his handwriting. Captain Aldous Vane has been dead eleven months. So you will tell me where you got these, or you will leave, and I will fetch the provost sergeant."

It was an empty threat and we both knew it. What would I say? *This stranger has brought me letters written by a dead man, letters that were mine already, and I want them back?* The colonel's staff would have questions I could not answer without answering everything else.

Wren set his cap on the counter. He was perhaps thirty, with a face that looked borrowed from several faces — nothing you could swear to in a courtroom. His eyes were the exception: pale grey, steady, and tired in a way that sleep does not touch.

"I can't tell you," he said.

"You can't—"

"I can't tell you how they came to me." He said it carefully, the way a man walks across ice he has tested before. "Not because I don't trust you, Sister. Because the telling would put you in a place you can't step back from. And I've come a long way to ask you for something, and I need you to come to it freely."

"Ask me for—" I stopped. The letters sat between us on the counter, six of them, the ones I had sent into the world and received back again like birds shot down mid-flight. "These are mine. I wrote the replies. Whoever gave them to you stole them from him, or from his effects, or—"

"He kept them," Wren said quietly. "All of them. In oilcloth, wrapped twice. He kept them the way you'd keep—" He paused, searching. "The way some men keep a Bible."

The room went very still around me. The carbolic smell of the dispensary sharpened. Somewhere down the corridor a trolley squealed its way toward the wards, and I heard Tam's laugh, bright and boyish, cut short by Matron Cobb's voice — the ordinary sounds of the station going on while something extraordinary opened under my feet like a trench.

"He kept them," I repeated.

"Yes."

"Then he's alive."

Wren didn't answer. But he didn't look away, either, and a man can deny a great deal with his mouth while his eyes are confessing everything. I gripped the edge of the counter. The wood was cold through my sleeves.

"The dispatch said killed in action," I said. "Third Battle of Ypres. October the ninth. I had it from the casualty list myself — I checked it three times, because I couldn't—" I stopped again, because the next words were *because I couldn't believe it*, and saying them aloud would hand this stranger something I wasn't ready to hand anyone.

"Couldn't believe it?" Wren asked gently.

"Couldn't confirm it." I lifted my chin. "There was no body. There were no personal effects returned. Only a name on a list. I know how lists get made, Mr. Wren. I write the other kind every day."

Something moved behind his eyes then — recognition, or relief, or both. "That's why he chose you," he said, half to himself.

* * *

I did not sleep that night. I sat on my cot with the letters spread over the blanket like a hand of cards dealt by a careless god, and I read them all again, and this time I read them as evidence.

*June 1916:* the letter about Finch and the sea. *August 1916:* an account of a ration party gone wrong, four lines long, ending abruptly — *I will tell you the rest when I can say it properly.* *November 1916:* the letter with the sentence about lamps. *January 1917:* a brief note, almost cheerful, reporting his promotion and complaining about the quality of army ink. *"They issue us ink that fades,"* he wrote. *"As if the army itself hopes the record will fade with it. Write your letters in pencil, Nurse Ashgrove. Pencil can be argued with; ink insists."*

I had laughed when I read that the first time. Now I read it with the hair rising on my arms.

And then the last letter, dated the twenty-sixth of September, 1917 — two weeks before Passchendaele took him off the face of the earth. It was shorter than the others. The hand was the same, but pressed harder, the pen biting through the paper in places, the tall capitals leaning slightly as if marching.

*"Something is wrong here,"* he had written. *"I have been keeping a private tally of the advance figures and they do not agree with what goes back to GHQ. Men who die are not dying where the reports say they died, or they are not dying at all in the reports — do you understand me? Whole platoons going into the ground and coming out of the paperwork as stragglers, as deserters, as clerical errors. I am told to correct my returns. I am correcting nothing. If anything happens to me, remember what I told you about lamps. Light them where you stand. That is all any of us can do, and it is more than they think."*

Two weeks later he was dead. Officially. Neatly. A name on a list — the very thing he had spent a war teaching me not to trust.

I sat with that letter until the candle burned down to a pool, and somewhere in the small hours a realization came to me with the gentleness of a bayonet: the dispatch hadn't erased Aldous Vane. It had *filed* him. Filed him the way one files a document that must never be read again.

* * *

In the morning I found Wren waiting by the gate, hands in his pockets, watching the mist burn off the flooded ground beyond the wire. Up close in daylight he looked worse — a healing scar at his hairline, a tremor in his left hand he kept folding into his pocket when he noticed it.

"You didn't sleep," he observed.

"Neither did you."

"I slept in a barn with goats. It's a different economy." He turned to face me. "You've made a decision. I can see it."

"Have you." 

"You've got a nurse's walk when she's decided something. Shoulders square. Chin set. You've been deciding things since dawn." He almost smiled. "I only need one hour of your time, Sister. One hour, somewhere we can't be overheard, and I'll show you what he's building. Then you can send me away if you like, and I'll take the letters with me, and you'll never see me again."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then refuse." He shrugged. "But you won't. You checked that casualty list three times. A woman who checks three times is already halfway to wherever he is."

I should have walked away. Every rule Matron Cobb had ever spoken, every instinct of self-preservation the last two years had hammered into me, said *walk away, report him, burn the letters, keep your small safe life.* And underneath those voices was another one — older, quieter — the voice of Private Ellison in my ward asking me to remember him, and the blank space in the dispatches where his name should have been.

"One hour," I said.

"That's all I need."

"But you'll answer one question first." I held up the last letter, the one with the pen bitten through the page. "You said you couldn't tell me how these came to you. Fine. Keep that. But answer me this instead, and answer it straight, because I'll know if you don't."

"Ask."

"Is he alive *and hiding* — or alive and trapped? Because those are two different men, Mr. Wren, and they need two different kinds of help."

For a long moment Wren said nothing. The mist lifted off the flooded fields behind him; somewhere a lark started up, absurd and indifferent, singing over ground that held the dead like a purse holds coins. When he finally spoke, his voice had dropped, and he glanced once at the gate sentry before leaning close.

"He's alive, he's hiding, and he's writing," Wren said. "And what he's writing is going to hang a general — if we can find someone who knows how to keep a tally of the dead."

He straightened, pulled his cap low, and nodded toward the road leading away from the station, toward the canal and the ruined villages beyond it.

"One hour," he said. "Bring the hymnal you hide it in. He'll want to see your hand."
