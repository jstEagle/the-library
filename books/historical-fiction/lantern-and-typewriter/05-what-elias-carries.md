## 5. What Elias Carries

Elias Wren had carried many things across the lines of other people's trust — ration chits, forged leave passes, a dead man's watch once, wrapped in oilcloth against the rain. He had learned early in this war that everything a man carried was either contraband or burden, and usually both. But nothing he had ever hauled through mud and checkpoint and suspicion weighed as much as what he carried into the dressing station at Poperinghe that grey morning: five letters, half a list of names, and a sentence he had rehearsed for eleven miles until it wore smooth as river stone.

*The Captain is alive.*

He said it to himself again as the sentry waved him through with the bored indifference of a man who had stopped seeing faces years ago. *The Captain is alive.* Four words. He'd carried longer sentences through worse places. And yet his palms were sweating inside his gloves, because he knew what those four words would do when they landed. Words like that didn't inform. They detonated.

He had watched her yesterday — Sister Ashgrove, the one they called Freya when Matron wasn't listening. Watched her read his card, watched her face do something complicated and quick, like weather crossing a field. She had recognized the hand. He'd seen the recognition land before she'd even finished the first line, seen her thumb press flat against the paper as if to hold it still. Then she had folded the card away and gone back to her work with a steadiness that told him two things: she was frightened, and she was not the kind of woman who let fright make her decisions.

Good. He needed someone brave. He needed someone careful. Most of all, he needed someone whose grief was older than her fear.

The problem was that he needed to tell her the truth, and Elias Wren had spent three years getting good at telling anything but.

* * *

She found him at dusk behind the laundry line, where the sheets hung like sails becalmed and the smell of carbolic fought a losing war against the smell of the latrines. Not a romantic place. That was deliberate; he'd suggested it because nobody stood behind a laundry line unless they wanted to be invisible.

"You came," he said.

"Don't flatter yourself." Her voice was low, clipped, the voice of a woman managing a ward on four hours' sleep. "I came because you have letters written in the hand of a dead man, and I intend to know how."

"Suppose I told you I couldn't say."

"Then we'd stand here until the light goes and you'd walk away having wasted both our evenings." She crossed her arms. Under the apron she was thin — thinner than she'd been in the photograph Vane kept folded in his breast pocket, the one Elias had seen a hundred times without understanding why a captain carried a nurse's picture like a relic. "I nursed men from Passchendaele, Mr. Wren. I've held the hands of boys who died calling for mothers who'd already been told lies about how they fell. I don't have patience left over for games."

"I'm not playing one." He glanced down the row of sheets. A shadow moved beyond them — Tam, the young orderly, hauling a basket — and waited until the footsteps faded. "But there are things I can't tell you yet, because every person who knows them becomes a link in a chain, and chains are what get men shot."

"Chains also hold things together."

"Yes," Elias said. "That's rather the point."

She didn't smile. But something in her posture shifted — a fraction less wall, a fraction more door. He took the chance.

"The letters," he said. "I didn't steal them. I didn't find them. Aldous Vane gave them to me."

The wind moved the sheets between them. Freya Ashgrove did not move at all.

"That's not possible," she said finally, and her voice had gone quiet in the way voices go quiet over graves. "I read the casualty list. I copied it myself. Captain Aldous Vane, killed in action, thirty-first of October, 1917. I—" She stopped. Elias watched her decide whether to finish the sentence, watched her lose. "I received letters from him. Through my training hospital, before. Eleven of them. The last one arrived six weeks after they told me he was dead."

"I know."

"How could you possibly—"

"Because he wrote to me about you." Elias reached inside his coat slowly, telegraphing every movement the way you did with horses and frightened people. "Not by name, at first. 'A sister at Étaples who writes the only honest sentences I've read since enlistment.' Later, your name. He kept your letters in a tin box. When the shelling got bad, he'd read them aloud to the men who couldn't sleep. Said your handwriting looked like order. Said if England sounded like you, the war might be worth the noise."

"Stop." It came out raw. "Stop talking."

"He's alive, Sister."

She turned away from him then, both hands coming up to her mouth, and Elias Wren, who had faced searchlights and checkpoints and a military policeman's revolver pressed under his jaw outside Hazebrouck, stood behind a laundry line and felt more exposed than he had ever felt in uniform or out of it. Grief was a country he could cross but never enter. He waited.

When she turned back her eyes were wet and furious, which Elias had learned was the most dangerous combination a person could wear.

"If this is a trick — if someone is using his letters to draw out—"

"Draw out whom? A nurse who keeps her head down and her patients alive?" He shook his head. "Sister, no one spends a dead captain's ink to catch a woman who changes dressings. What would they even want from you?"

And there it was — the flicker. Quick as a match struck in wind. Her eyes went somewhere else for half a second, somewhere guarded, and Elias filed it away the way he filed everything: *she's hiding something too.* Everyone in this war was carrying contraband. Hers just hadn't shown itself yet.

"I want proof," she said. "Not words. Proof."

"That," said Elias, "I can give you. Some of it. Tonight, if you'll listen."

"I've been listening since you handed me that card."

"No. You've been interrogating." He almost smiled. "There's a difference. Interrogation takes pieces. Listening takes all of them, even the ugly ones."

* * *

They walked — not together, that would draw eyes, but twenty paces apart along the duckboard path toward the canal, where the poplars had been shattered to splinters and the water lay the color of pewter. At the lock keeper's ruined cottage, its roof half-gone, its chimney standing absurdly upright like a man refusing to lie down, they stopped. Elias checked the road both ways out of habit, then sat on a fallen beam and took out the bundle.

Oilcloth, tied with waxed cord. Inside: the five letters, and beneath them a sheet of paper covered in cramped pencil — names, ranks, regiments, dates.

"What is this?"

"A fragment," Elias said. "Of a longer document. Read it."

She read. He watched her lips move slightly over the names — a habit of careful readers, he thought, people who read aloud inside themselves so nothing slips past. Halfway down the page her finger stopped.

"These men." Her voice had changed texture entirely. "Private Daniel Okafor. Rifleman Thomas Merrick. Lance-Corporal— these men came through my station. Some of them. I remember Okafor, he—" She looked up sharply. "These are marked deserters. Court-martial absentees. This list says—"

"This list says they died in the advance on the thirty-first of October. In the first forty minutes. On open ground, against machine-gun positions their own brigade had mapped and reported a week earlier." Elias heard his own voice go flat, the way it did when he recited this, the way a man's voice goes flat when feeling would otherwise kill him. "Forty-one men so far that we can name. The official record calls them deserters because dead men can't defend their honor, and deserters don't appear in dispatches, and names that don't appear in dispatches don't appear anywhere at all."

Freya sat down slowly on the beam beside him, the list in both hands, as though it were something living and fragile.

"Why?" she asked. "Why falsify it? Men die in bad advances every week. God knows. Why lie about *these*?"

"Because of the order itself." Elias looked out at the canal. "Brigade ordered the advance at dawn knowing — documented, in writing, known — that the guns hadn't cut the wire and the machine positions were intact. The attack was meant to relieve pressure elsewhere. A feint. Except feints are supposed to be small, and somebody inflated the numbers to make it look like a real push, and then when it failed, and it failed in nineteen minutes, the same staff officers who wrote the order rewrote the aftermath. Dead men became deserters. Deserters become statistics. Statistics become nobody."

"And Vane saw it."

"Vane wrote part of it." Elias turned to look at her properly. "That's the thing you need to understand about your captain, Sister. He isn't a hero who stumbled onto a crime. He was a staff lieutenant seconded to brigade intelligence. His own hand drafted the casualty summaries. Clean copies. He believed the figures he was given — until the night a signals clerk showed him the original wire traffic, and Vane understood that the numbers he'd typed up fair had been invented before the advance ever happened. Casualties projected, approved, and printed. They knew the cost to the last man before they spent it. And when the bill came due higher than projected, they adjusted the dead to fit the estimate."

Freya had gone very pale. "Projected casualties."

"Like ordering coal," Elias said. "That's what broke him. Not the dying — he'd seen the dying. It was the arithmetic. That a man at a desk could compute how many sons the war required and then, when the sons came in wrong, correct the sons."

The wind moved off the canal, cold enough to make the reeds rattle like dry bones. Freya folded the list along its existing creases, precisely, and Elias noticed that her hands, though steady, pressed each fold as if sealing something.

"Where is he?" she asked.

"I can't say."

"You can't, or you won't?"

"At present, Sister, they're the same thing." He held up a hand before she could sharpen. "He was wounded in the withdrawal after the advance — not badly enough for a stretcher, badly enough that walking was a decision he made every hour. He went into the flooded country east of the Yser with a Belgian farmer's family who owed him. He's been moving between cellars and ruins since. Half his hearing went with a shell burst; his eyes are going too, some old hemorrhage the army will never treat because officially he doesn't exist. And in those cellars, by candlelight, half-blind, he has been doing the only thing left to him. Writing it down. Names. Times. Grid references. The text of the original orders. Statements from two signals clerks and a gunner officer who'll sign nothing but told him everything."

"And the pamphlet—" she began, and stopped.

Elias raised an eyebrow.

Her jaw tightened. "You were saying."

"I wasn't. But now I'm curious what you know about pamphlets."

"Nothing. Rumors. Paper moves through hospitals the way infection does." She stood, brushing off her skirt with sharp little strikes. "You said fragments. You said you needed someone. Say it plainly, Mr. Wren. I'm tired and I've a night ward waiting."

So he said it plainly. It was, he reflected later, the plainest speech of his life, and it cost him more than any lie he'd ever told.

"I'm a courier. That's all I am — a pair of legs and a forgettable face. Vane gathers; others verify; I carry. But verification is the hinge, and the man who was doing it — a chaplain at Reninghelst — was transferred suddenly last month, and we think someone noticed him asking questions. We need someone with access to admissions books, burial returns, stretcher-bearer logs. Someone who sees the men before the paperwork erases them. Someone whose name appears nowhere near intelligence work." He met her eyes. "Someone who can take a list of names and prove where each man actually fell, and what was actually written afterward. Can you do that?"

The question hung between them. Behind her the sun was going down over the flooded flats, and the whole western sky turned the color of the canal — pewter, then copper, then a red that looked like the end of something.

"You're asking me to spy," she said.

"I'm asking you to remember. There's a difference, though I grant it gets narrower every year of this war." He stood too. "I'm asking you to check forty-one names against what you've seen with your own hands. If the record is true, you lose an evening. If the record is false, I'll take my letters and my list and never trouble you again. That's the bargain. Verify or dismiss. Nothing else is asked tonight."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow depends on tonight."

She looked down at the list in her hands. Elias watched her thumb move over the names the way it had moved over his card yesterday — pressing them flat, holding them still. He thought: *she knows some of them already. She's known for months, in whatever secret ledger she keeps behind her eyes, that the paperwork and the bodies don't agree.* He didn't know how right he was. He wouldn't know for weeks. But he recognized a fellow carrier when he saw one.

"If I do this," Freya said quietly, "and the names are true — then what? What happens to the men who wrote the order?"

"That's above my pay grade, Sister. I carry. Others decide." He paused. "But I'll tell you what Vane says, when the pain's bad and the candles low and he talks like a man settling accounts. He says the order can't be unpunished, because punishment requires a court, and courts require the truth, and the truth requires the names. So it starts with the names. Everything starts there. Forty-one men who were erased need to exist again — on paper, in ink, in front of witnesses — before anything else can happen. Justice, revenge, reform, ruin — all of it stands on the names."

"And if the names are never spoken?"

"Then it happens again," Elias said simply. "Next spring. Next offensive. Different brigade, same arithmetic. That's what he believes. That's why he's killing himself by candlelight in a cellar instead of lying quiet somewhere waiting for the war to end. Because he's seen what happens when the dead are permitted to be wrong about their own deaths."

Silence. Somewhere down the canal a duck startled up and beat away low across the water, and the sound was enormous in the quiet.

Freya held the list out to him.

Elias didn't take it.

"Keep it," he said. "Tonight only. Burn it in the morning if the names come back clean. But I think—" He hesitated, and for once the courier's smoothness failed him, and what came out was younger and plainer than anything he'd planned. "I think you already know some of these men didn't desert. I think you've known since you wrote their burial cards. I think that's why you came to the laundry line instead of reporting my card to Matron. We're both carrying something, Sister. Mine happens to weigh forty-one names."

She looked at him for a long moment. The last light caught her face and made it unreadable, a coin turning.

"St. Sixtus chapel," she said at last. "Ruined, north of the road. Tomorrow, after evening sick parade. Come alone. If anyone follows you, I'll report you and every word of this, and I'll sleep soundly after."

"Fair terms."

"They're the only terms I offer." She tucked the list into her sleeve — her sleeve, Elias noted, not her pocket, where a searching orderly might find it, but against the skin of her forearm where a nurse's hands would hide it naturally a hundred times a day. A professional's instinct. Or a smuggler's. "Mr. Wren. The letters. Did he — in the tin box. Were mine among them?"

"All eleven," Elias said. "In order. With your last one on top. He'd worn soft the corner where he held it."

Freya Ashgrove closed her eyes briefly, opened them, and walked away down the towpath without another word, her figure shrinking into the dusk until the dark took her, twenty paces, forty, gone.

Elias stood alone by the ruined cottage a while longer, listening to the water and the far mutter of guns like weather beyond the hills. He should feel lighter — the package delivered, the contact made, the chain extended one link further toward whatever end the chain was strung toward. Instead he felt the particular dread of a man who has just handed a lit lantern to someone standing in a powder store, and told her, honestly, that she ought to carry it carefully.

He pulled his collar up and started back along the road toward Poperinghe, rehearsing nothing this time, because tomorrow there would be no speech. Tomorrow there would only be a ruined chapel, a list of forty-one names, and whatever Sister Freya Ashgrove brought with her — proof, or fire.

He only hoped, as the mud sucked at his boots and the first star came out over the flooded flats, that the two were not the same thing.
