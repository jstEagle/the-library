## 6. The Hymnal Question

The ward smelled of carbolic and rain that had nowhere to go. It came through the canvas roof in a fine grey mist, beading on the stovepipe, hissing when the wind shifted, and Matron Cobb had declared — with the weary certainty of a woman who had fought mud at three stations before this one — that they would all drown standing up before the war gave them the courtesy of drowning any other way.

Freya moved down the row of cots with her basin and her cloth, and she did not think about the hymnal.

That was the discipline of it now. Not thinking about it. She had learned, in the weeks since Private Ellery had died asking her to remember him, that the ledger lived in her mind the way a splinter lives under skin: you could press around it, work over it, almost forget it, and then some small motion of the day would find the edge of it and the ache would bloom fresh. *Lance-Corporal A. Whitcombe. Private J. Oduya. Private T. Ellery.* Names the dispatches had swallowed. Names she wrote at night by the light of a shuttered lamp, her blanket over her head like a child reading contraband stories, while Tam snored in the next room and the rain kept its patient time against the tent.

She kept it inside the hymnal because the hymnal was the one book no one ever opened.

It was an old thing, bound in brown cloth gone soft as chamois, *Hymns Ancient and Modern* stamped in faded gilt on the cover. It had come to the station in a crate of comforts from some parish in Kent, and there were forty copies of it in the chapel chest, and no one — not the chaplain, who said his offices from memory; not the men, who were past singing; not Iris, whose faith was of the practical, unbookish sort — had touched one since the spring. Into its hollowed spine Freya had fitted the ledger: sixty leaves of thin paper, sewn herself with thread pulled from a pillowcase, each name entered in a hand so small it might have been the penmanship of a mouse. *Sanctus, sanctus,* sang the printed page. And beneath the board where no hymn could reach, the true litany.

She was rinsing her cloth when Iris came through the flap with her sleeves rolled and her jaw set in the particular way that meant tidying.

"Matron says the kit inspection's tomorrow," Iris announced. "Quartermaster's coming round for the linen count, and if he finds one bandage unaccounted for we'll all be writing explanations till Christmas. So we're doing our own first." She looked at Freya the way she looked at a wound she'd already decided how to dress. "Yours is a disgrace, Ashgrove. I've seen field latrines with better order."

"It's ordered," Freya said. "It's ordered in a system only I understand."

"Well, it can be ordered in a system the rest of us understand too, or it can be ordered by me while you're on shift. Which is it?"

There was no winning this. There never was with Iris, who had been a schoolmistress in Leeds before the war and carried the authority of chalk dust in her blood. Freya dried her hands. "I'll do it tonight."

"You'll do it now," Iris said pleasantly, "because you'll be on nights tonight, and because I know you, Freya Ashgrove, and 'tonight' means 'never.' Come on. Two pairs of hands. It'll take an hour and you'll thank me."

* * *

The trouble with letting someone into your space was that your space held your secrets, and secrets did not survive proximity.

Freya's kit occupied the foot of her bunk and half the shelf above it: spare uniform rolled army-fashion, mackintosh folded into its own skirt, the tin that held needles and buttons and a photograph of her mother outside a house in Norfolk with too many roses. The medical satchel. The bundle of letters tied in butcher's string — Vane's letters, though she thought of them as such only in the dark, and even then reluctantly, since Elias Wren had stood at the gate three days ago holding more of the same handwriting and saying nothing at all.

Iris worked like weather. She went through everything. She unfolded the mackintosh and refolded it tighter. She shook out the boots. She held up the photograph of the rose-covered house and said, "Your mum?" and Freya said, "Yes," and something in her chest pinched, because the lie she was keeping — the great one, the splinter-deep one — made every smaller truth feel provisional, as if all of it might be repossessed.

Then Iris reached for the hymnal.

It sat on the shelf between the letters and a jar of glycerine, and it looked, Freya had always believed, perfectly innocent — worn, dull, a book among books. But she saw it now as Iris must see it: out of place. A hymnal on a private shelf instead of in the chapel chest. A hymnal with a spine that sat a fraction wrong, a fraction thick, the way a coat sits wrong over a hidden letter.

"Don't—" The word was out before Freya could shape it into anything innocent. Iris glanced up. Rain ticked on the canvas. Somewhere down the ward a man called softly for water and no one went.

"Don't what?" Iris said.

"It's — falling apart." Freya crossed the two steps between them and took the book, and her heart was going so hard she felt certain Iris could hear it, could see it working under her collar like a trapped bird. "The spine's gone. Look." She turned it over, showing the frayed cloth, showing nothing, showing everything except the seam she had cut and restitched along the inner hinge. "If you open it flat it'll shed pages everywhere, and then I'll have a hymnal in pieces and Matron will want to know why I'm hoarding broken books."

Iris considered her. She had grey eyes, Iris, the pale flinty grey of a North Sea sky, and she used them sparingly but thoroughly, and just now she was using them on Freya in a way that made the tent feel smaller.

"You're jumpy lately," Iris said.

"I'm tired lately. Everyone's tired. We buried four men Tuesday."

"That's not what I mean and you know it." Iris took the hymnal back — gently, which was worse than roughly; roughness she could have argued with — and weighed it in her hand. "You go quiet at odd hours. You were out past lights twice last week. And now you're guarding a songbook like it's the crown jewels." She smiled, but it didn't reach the grey eyes. "I'm not accusing you of anything, Frey. I'm asking if you're all right."

Every door in Freya's mind stood open, and behind every door the same room: the ledger, the names, the lamp shuttered to a coin of light. She could tell the truth. It rose in her like floodwater — *I keep a record, Iris, I write down the men they erase, I have forty-one reasons already and the number grows* — and it rose so strongly that for one terrible second she nearly spoke it, nearly let the whole secret spill across the bunk and the shelf and their six years of friendship, because a secret shared is a secret halved, and she was so tired of carrying it whole.

But Iris's faith in the army ran deeper than her faith in God. Iris believed the dispatches because believing them was what kept the world shaped right. Tell her, and Freya would not gain an ally; she would gain a problem wearing a friend's face, and the friendship would die somewhere in the telling.

"I'm all right," Freya said. And then, because the silence demanded more, because Iris was still holding the book: "I write in it. That's all. Letters I don't send. To — to no one. Things you can't say in a postcard home without the censor reading them and your mother weeping into her tea." She heard her own voice steady itself, appallingly smooth, and hated how easily it came. "It's silly. It's private. That's all it is."

It was not all it was. It was the first lie she had ever told this woman, and it slid out of her dressed in truth's clothing — she *did* write things unsayable, they simply weren't prayers — and Freya understood, in the moment the words landed, that she had crossed a line she had not known was drawn until her foot was over it. You could not step back. There was no un-saying. From here forward, every honest word she spoke to Iris would travel over that buried false one, and part of her would always be listening for the ground to give.

Iris turned the hymnal over once more. Then she set it back on the shelf, spine aligned with the jar of glycerine, and stepped away, and something in her stepping-away was careful in a way it hadn't been an hour ago.

"Well," she said briskly, "your boots need re-lacing the eyelets are shot, and if you'd roll that mackintosh properly instead of stuffing it we'd both get on faster." She picked up the mackintosh and began again, folding, pressing, talking — about the quartermaster, about Tam's habit of hoarding sugar cubes like a squirrel, about the concert party coming to the casualty clearing station and whether anyone could bear to sit through an hour of soprano after a day of amputations. Ordinary talk. Warm talk. Talk pitched, perhaps unconsciously, to rebuild the bridge Freya had just quietly sawed at.

Freya helped. She laced the boots. She laughed at the squirrel remark. And all the while the hymnal sat on the shelf between them, six inches of hollowed wood and paper, radiating its small cold presence like a stone pulled from a fire — still hot long after the flames are gone.

* * *

Night shift. The ward at three in the morning was a country unto itself: lamplight turned low to blue coins, the breathing of thirty men in various stages of repair, the morphine clock ticking in someone's memory every four hours. Freya walked the rows with her lamp shielded, and the men surfaced to her like fish rising — *Sister? Sister, is it morning? — No, love, not yet — Sister, my mate, did my mate come through?* — and she answered each one, and each answer cost her something now, because she knew what the morning dispatches would say and what they wouldn't.

At the far end, in the cot by the stove, a Canadian gunner with a hip wound was sleeping badly, muttering a name over and over — *Danny, Danny* — and Freya stopped by him longer than she needed to. When she got off shift she would open the hymnal. She would add him to no list, because he was alive and listed; but she found herself composing, in the mouse-small hand of her mind, the entry she would write if the morning went badly: *Gunner R. Callaghan, 4th CFA, wounded Polygon Wood, asked after his brother Danny through the night.* Because that was the disease of the ledger now. It had stopped being an act of defiance and become an act of faith, a reflex, a way of praying — and she caught herself cataloguing the living the way she catalogued the dead, and wondered what she was turning into.

Toward dawn the mist came in again through the canvas seams and lay along the floor in knee-high drifts, so the cots seemed to float. Freya stood at the window-flap looking out at the grey, and allowed herself, for ninety seconds exactly, to think about Elias Wren.

He had left no address. He had left, folded into her apron pocket while she was dressing a wound, a single sheet of paper with eleven names written on it and one line beneath: *These are said to have deserted at St. Julien. I believe otherwise. Can you check them against anything?* Nothing else. No signature, no mark, no instruction. Just the names, sitting in her pocket like eleven small detonators, through the entire ordeal with Iris and the hymnal, through the entire night of the floating ward.

She had not yet checked them. She had not dared. Eleven names was a small thing to hold; what they might turn out to be was not. But dawn was coming, and with it the end of shift, and the hymnal waited on its shelf with its hollow heart, and the paper in her pocket had begun, very quietly, to burn.

Behind her, down the row of cots, Gunner Callaghan murmured *Danny* once more and slept on. Freya turned back to her rounds, and counted her steps, and did not look at the shelf, and did not believe for one moment that Iris hadn't noticed exactly where her eyes refused to go.
