## 7. Names That Were Never There

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Freya spread the two lists side by side on the floor of the linen store.

She had chosen the store because no one came here after ten o'clock — Tam's rounds ended at the ward doors, and Matron Cobb believed, with religious certainty, that a nurse found awake past her shift was a nurse too tired to hold a scalpel steady at dawn. The shelves of folded bandages rose around Freya like pale cliffs. She sat among them with her back against a crate of carbolic tins, the hymnal open across her knees, and Elias Wren's paper — a single foolscap sheet he had copied out in his small, careful hand before vanishing into the fog beyond the gate — weighted flat beneath a tin of iodine crystals.

On the left: the Lantern. Forty pages now, each name entered in her own hand, dated, annotated in the shorthand she had invented for herself so that even if the ledger were found it would read like nothing more than a prayer book gone strange. *Pte. R. Calloway, 11th Norfolks, died 14:20, wound to abdomen, asked for his mother.* On the right: Elias's list, which was not really his list at all but a transcription of something Captain Aldous Vane had compiled in whatever flooded cellar or ruined barn he haunted while the world mourned him.

The task was simple and terrible: find where the lists agreed.

Freya wet her thumb, turned a page of the hymnal, and began.

* * *

For the first hour it was almost peaceful work. There is a kind of quiet that settles over close attention, and she had known it since girlhood, bent over her father's parish registers, matching baptisms to burials. Name against name. Date against date. The candle hissed. Somewhere beyond the sandbags a flare went up and drifted down over the flats, turning the seams of the canvas walls briefly gold, then grey again.

She found the first match at half past eleven. Private Thomas Merrick, Royal Fusiliers, who had died on her table on the third of October with a shell fragment through the chest and whose name she had written by lamplight while his blood was still drying under her fingernails. Merrick appeared on Vane's list too — but not as dead.

He appeared as *absent without leave.*

Freya sat very still for a long moment. Then she checked the entry twice more, ran her finger along the line, read the notation beside it: *AWOL since 2/10. Struck from rolls. No pension entitlement.*

But she had held Merrick's hand while he died. He had been carried in from the forward posts, still wearing the mud of the line, and the stretcher-bearers had said — she remembered it now with the peculiar clarity of things that seem unimportant until they are everything — the bearers had said he'd come in from *ahead*, from beyond their own wire, crawling toward them through the crater field after the advance broke. He hadn't deserted. He'd been cut off. And someone, somewhere behind a desk, had chosen the word that would follow him into eternity rather than the word that would cost them anything.

Her pencil hovered. Then, in the margin beside Merrick's name, she wrote the smallest possible annotation: *died in action, witnessed.*

One.

* * *

By two o'clock there were nine.

They came slowly at first, then with a horrible momentum, as though the page itself understood what she was looking for and began offering it up. A lance-corporal from the Durhams marked AWOL who had died of gas on her ward with his boots still on. Two privates of the West Kents recorded as deserters whose bodies she had helped carry from the aid post to the burial trench behind the chapel — she had stood at that grave; she had heard the chaplain mumble the service; there had been rifles fired over the earth, which was a strange thing to do for deserters, and she had thought nothing of it at the time because she had thought nothing of so much, then.

That was the thing that made her hands go cold, colder than the store room warranted, colder than October. Not the falsifications themselves — she had already learned, in the dressing stations, that paperwork was a kind of weather that happened far above the heads of dying men and touched none of them. It was her own complicity in the forgetting. Every one of these names had passed through her hands and out of them, and she had grieved for an evening and moved on, and the machinery had closed over each of them like water.

She had started the Lantern because names deserved to outlive dispatches. She had never imagined the ledger would become evidence.

At some point she stopped annotating and started counting.

* * *

Toward dawn she did the arithmetic three times, because the first answer could not be right, and the second answer was identical to the first, and the third time her pencil point snapped against the page.

Forty-one.

Forty-one men, across four months of the advance and its long grey aftermath, recorded in official casualty returns as absent without leave or struck from the rolls outright — and present in the Lantern as dead, most of them dead in her own ward, under her own hands, with dates and wounds and last words she could recite like liturgy.

Forty-one men who would be buried in unmarked graves or none at all, whose mothers would receive the other letter — the cold one, the shameful one, the one that said your son abandoned his comrades — whose pensions would be refused, whose villages would lower their voices about them for a generation.

Not clerical error. She tested that explanation the way you test ice, putting weight on it carefully, wanting it to hold. But errors scatter; they don't pattern. These forty-one shared a shape. They had all died in the same week of the advance — the week the order had sent waves of men across open ground toward machine-gun positions that every surviving officer swore had been reported and known. They had all been killed moving *toward* the enemy or cut off *beyond* the wire trying to reach cover. And they had all been transformed, on paper, from casualties of a catastrophic order into authors of their own disgrace.

You did not commit that transformation once, or carelessly. Forty-one times, in sequence, required intent.

Freya set down the broken pencil. Her fingers were stiff with cold and graphite. Around her the bandage-cliffs stood white and silent, and she understood suddenly why hospitals kept their stores locked — not to protect the linen, but because a woman sitting alone in the dark with a list of the erased looked, from certain angles, exactly like treason.

*Purpose*, she told herself, testing the word. It should have felt warm. It felt like a blade being pressed into her hand, handle first, by someone she couldn't see.

* * *

She slept two hours and woke with the numbers unchanged.

In the ward that morning the ordinary miracles proceeded: Iris arguing cheerfully with a sergeant-major about the proper temperature of a foot bath; Tam hauling a tea urn nearly his own size and grinning at his own heroism; Matron Cobb moving down the rows like a lighthouse beam, touching each man's blanket, seeing everything, saying little. Freya worked among them with steady hands and a skull full of forty-one names, and twice she caught herself staring at a patient's chart as though the falsification might be visible there, inked between the pulse readings.

At noon Elias Wren appeared at the gate, as promised, in his civilian coat that fit him like an apology, carrying a basket marked *Red Cross comforts* that contained no comforts whatsoever.

They walked. That was the arrangement — walking looked purposeless, and purposelessness was camouflage. They took the duckboard path along the canal where the poplars had been shot to lace, and Freya waited until the nearest sentry was a smudge behind them before she spoke.

"Forty-one," she said.

Elias didn't break stride, but something in his shoulders changed, a settling, like a man accepting weight he'd expected. "You're sure."

"I'm sure I counted them three times and my pencil broke." She kept her eyes on the water, grey-green and sluggish, carrying its scum of ash and leaves. "Merrick. The West Kents. The boy from the Durhams who cried for his brother — do you know how many times I've told myself I imagined that boy? He's on both lists, Elias. He died holding my wrist and your Captain Vane's army wrote *deserter* next to his name."

"Vane isn't my captain."

"He's everyone's captain now. That's what happens when you die well — you belong to whoever needs you." She stopped walking. A barge loaded with shells slid past, towed by two weary mules on the towpath, and neither of them watched it go. "I need you to say plainly what this means. Because I've spent the night telling myself stories. That it's confusion. That the records got mixed in a bombardment. Say the true thing so I can stop pretending the other ones are possible."

Elias looked at the canal for a long moment. Up close, in daylight, he seemed younger than he had at the gate, and wearier, the way men look when they have carried something heavy for a very long way and have begun to suspect no one will take it from them.

"It means," he said, "that after the advance failed, somebody had to explain forty-one hundred casualties in a week on ground the maps called impossible. And it was easier to explain a few hundred cowards than one bad order. So the dead who couldn't be accounted for — the cut-off men, the missing, the ones whose bodies lay out in the crater field — became deserters. In batches. With signatures." His voice stayed flat, but his jaw worked once. "Vane saw the original order. He saw the rewrite. He tried to protest through channels and the channels explained to him what happens to majors who embarrass generals during a offensive. Then the shelling nearly finished what the channels started, and he decided that if the army wouldn't record the truth, the truth would have to be recorded anyway."

"Recorded where? By whom? You can't print sedition in a war zone. Paper is controlled. Presses are controlled. *Typeface* is controlled—" She heard herself stop.

Elias turned and looked at her then, fully, and there was in his face the careful blankness of a man watching a door open that he had only hoped existed.

"You've thought about presses," he said.

"I've thought about *paperwork*," Freya said. "All night. It's all paperwork, Elias. The order, the rewrite, the forty-one lies — every crime committed out here is committed twice, once in blood and once in triplicate, and the second copy always wins." Her voice had dropped low and come out steadier than she expected. "So the question isn't whether the truth can be shouted. It's whether it can be *printed*. Where. By whom. How many copies. And how it gets past every checkpoint between here and the people who'll weep when they read it."

The mules plodded on behind the barge. Somewhere eastward the guns kept up their distant, patient argument.

"That question," Elias said quietly, "has an address."

She stared at him. "An address."

"In town. There's a place — I won't say more here, and I'll want more than your curiosity before I take you anywhere near it. Curiosity burns off by morning. But if you mean what you just said — if you're asking the questions you just asked —" He hesitated, and she saw that underneath the courier's caution he was afraid, genuinely, and that the fear was not for himself. "There are people working on it. Setting type, Vane says. Making the record real. They'll need someone who knows the names. Someone who can verify. Someone the army doesn't watch, because it doesn't occur to them a nurse matters."

Freya looked back along the path, toward the tents and wards and the flag limping on its pole, toward Iris and Tam and Matron Cobb and the whole fragile household she belonged to. Then down at the canal, where her reflection wavered, broken and remade and broken again by the water.

Forty-one names sat behind her ribs like stones in a wall.

"When?" she said.
