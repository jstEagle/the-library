## 8. The Workshop on Rue Basse

Elias Wren had learned, in three years of carrying other men's secrets across Flanders, that the dangerous hours were never the ones spent in the open. A man could walk a road at midnight with a satchel of forged chits and feel safer than he did in a lit room with a friendly face. The open road asked nothing of you. It was the closed rooms — the warm kitchens, the trusting eyes — where everything could be lost.

So when he stopped Freya Ashgrove at the corner of the canal and told her, for the third time, that she could still turn back, he was not offering her an escape. He was testing the weight of what she carried.

She had dressed as a Belgian mill worker: dark shawl, wooden clogs borrowed from Tam's cousin in Poperinghe, her hair scraped back and dusted with flour so that even up close she looked like a woman walking home from a shift she'd rather forget. It was good work. Better than good. She had done it without complaint, without trembling hands, and she had asked only one question all evening.

"Will there be a light?"

He'd looked at her then. Most people, told they were being taken to a clandestine print shop, imagined darkness. She'd imagined the opposite.

"There'll be a light," he said now, turning down the lane along the water. "There's always a light."

The town was Ypres's poorer cousin — a straggle of brick and plaster south of the salient, half its windows boarded, its church tower missing its top like a broken tooth. Shells came here sometimes, lazily, the way weather comes to a coast. The inhabitants had developed the particular stoop of people who no longer straightened up between warnings. Elias moved through these streets the way he moved through all streets: unhurried, unremarkable, a thin man in a cloth cap whose face nobody remembered five minutes after he passed. It was his single talent, invisibility, and it had kept him alive through work that killed faster men.

Freya walked beside him and did not match it. Not because she was careless — she watched doorways, counted footsteps behind them, did everything right — but because she *looked* at things. He'd noticed it the first night, going through the letters with her. Other people read words; she read the spaces around them, the hesitations, the ink pressed too hard where a hand had paused over something terrible. A woman who looked at things that hard, he thought, would eventually be seen looking. And that frightened him more than any patrol.

"Left here," he murmured.

The Rue Basse ran downhill toward the old tannery district, unpaved, its gutters choked with leaves gone black from two autumns of gas alerts. No lamps. The houses leaned together like conspirators. Halfway down, where a collapsed wall had been stacked into a low barricade of rubble, Elias took her elbow and steered her through a gap barely wide enough for the satchel on his shoulder.

"Mind the second step," he said. "It lies."

They descended a stair cut into the side of the building — stone steps worn hollow in their centers by a century of feet — to a door that looked like nothing: green paint blistered, a brass plate long since pried off, a knocker that hung from one screw. Above them, faintly, the war went on. A distant stutter of guns, felt in the chest more than heard, like a heartbeat belonging to some enormous sleeping animal.

Elias knocked. Four beats, pause, two beats. Then he waited, counting under his breath, because Céline Dossin did not answer quickly and answered fast for no one.

A slot slid open at eye height. Lamplight spilled through it, gold and sudden, and behind the light a voice in French, flat with suspicion: "*Qui?*"

"The printer's apprentice," Elias said, "come about the wedding invitations."

There was a silence long enough to be a verdict. Then bolts — two of them, heavy, drawn back with the deliberation of a woman who had bolted doors through worse wars than this one — and the door opened inward on warmth and the smell of ink and hot metal.

"Come in before you freeze the whole house," said Céline Dossin.

* * *

The workshop occupied what had once been the cellar and ground floor of a wine merchant's premises. Elias watched Freya take it in — he couldn't help watching; she was the reason he'd brought her, and her face was part of the cargo — and saw the exact moment she understood what she was standing inside of.

It began as confusion. Her eyes went first to the press itself, an iron flatbed older than the century, squatting in the center of the room like a patient ox, its rollers gleaming with fresh grease. Then to the type cases ranged along the walls, hundreds of shallow wooden drawers, each one a grid of small compartments, each compartment holding a few hundred letters of lead — the whole alphabet multiplied until language became something you could weigh by the pound. Then to the composing stones, the paper stock wrapped in oilcloth against damp, the drying lines strung overhead where sheets hung like strange laundry, ghost-gray in the lamplight.

And then to the table beneath those lines, where three finished pages lay pinned out flat, and the moment arrived.

He saw her go very still.

She crossed the room without asking permission — Céline raised an eyebrow but let her — and stood over the pages the way one stands over a body one has been told is someone beloved. Her lips moved. Reading. And Elias knew which page she'd reached, because he knew every word of it by heart: the paragraph beginning *On the morning of the ninth of October, 1917, the 4th Battalion advanced across open ground...*

"They're real," she said quietly. It wasn't a question, but it wanted answering anyway.

"They're real," Elias said.

"I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I thought it would be a pamphlet. Something run off in a hurry. Eight pages, bad paper, everyone knows how it ends." Her finger hovered above the text without touching it, the way one hovers above a flame. "This is set properly. Justified margins. This is a *book*."

"It will be forty-eight pages when it's done," said Céline Dossin, coming forward with a lamp of her own, so that the two flames made Freya's shadow double on the wall. "Sixteen-page signatures. Two hundred copies, if the paper holds out, and the paper will not hold out, so perhaps one hundred and sixty." She said it the way another woman might discuss the price of bread — with grievance, but without drama. "My late husband bought this press in Liège in 1901. He set almanacs and funeral notices and, in the good years, a small newspaper. He would have hated what we print here." A pause; the ghost of something not quite a smile. "Or he would have stayed up three nights setting it himself. One or the other."

"You've been printing it here?" Freya turned slowly, taking in the room again — and now, Elias noted, seeing the details that mattered. The blackout curtains doubled at the windows. The trapdoor at the back, presumably down to the coal cellar, presumably an escape. The cot in the corner, the kettle on a spirit stove, the signs that someone lived inside the work. "How long?"

"Since August."

"And no one's found you."

Céline set her lamp on the composing stone and looked at Freya with the frank appraisal of a woman who had buried a husband and a son within eighteen months of each other and had consequently run out of patience with euphemism.

"No one has found me yet," she said. "That is a different sentence, mademoiselle. Say it correctly or don't say it at all."

Freya held the look. Didn't flinch. Elias found himself breathing again.

* * *

Later — after tea that tasted of chicory and coal smoke, after Céline had interrogated Freya in rapid French and slower English, after the ledger itself had come out of Freya's coat and lain open on the table between them like a patient on an operating table — Elias stepped out into the stairwell to listen to the street.

This was ritual. Ten minutes of dark and cold, ears doing the work eyes couldn't. The guns had settled into their night rhythm, ten miles east, a conversation conducted entirely in coughs. A dog somewhere near the canal, barking at nothing, or at something. A bicycle bell, far off, passing away. Nothing else. In this town, nothing else was peace.

When he came back down, the two women were bent over the Lantern together, and the scene stopped him in the doorway.

Céline Dossin held a composing stick in one hand and Freya's ledger in the other, and she was reading the names aloud — softly, in her accented English, each name given its full weight, the way one reads names in a churchyard — while Freya pointed to entries and explained. *Private Daniel Okafor, listed as wounded, died of wounds, no record of burial. Sergeant Halloran, listed as desertion, see my entry, I dressed his head wound myself.* And Céline would nod and say, "Then we set him in roman, not italic. The deserters are printed in italic in the official record. We undo the italics."

Elias stood in the doorway and felt something move in his chest that he hadn't permitted himself in three years.

Because this was the thing. This was the whole thing, and he had almost forgotten it. There had been so much fear — the couriers, the codes, the close calls, Vane's ruined face and ruined faith — that he had begun to think of the work as only fear wearing a purpose as a disguise. But here it was, its actual body: two women in a lamplit cellar, deciding in what typeface the dead would be forgiven.

History had an address. He had known it intellectually since the first crate of Vane's papers had reached him, but knowing it and standing in it were different trades. History lived at number seven, Rue Basse, down a stair whose second step lied, behind a green door with one screw holding its knocker. History smelled of ink and chicory. History was a widow with ink stains to the second knuckle and a nurse with flour still in her hair, arguing gently about whether a name should have its regimental number included or omitted, because the number might identify the family to reprisal and the omission might leave the family unsure it was truly their son.

"Both," Freya was saying. "Number, but set small. Let the families find them. Let anyone else have to squint."

Céline considered this, then nodded once, sharply, as if closing a transaction. "Small. Good. My son's number was in the dispatch that erased him. They used it to make him a statistic." She touched the type case nearest her — the eight-point, worn silver at its edges from her thumb. "We give the numbers back smaller than they gave us the sons."

No one spoke for a moment. Overhead, faintly, a shell fell somewhere north, and the jars on the shelf rattled their lids, and neither woman looked up. They had both, Elias realized, already absorbed the war into the rhythm of their work, the way a printer absorbs the clatter of his own press.

Freya closed the ledger carefully and looked up, and her eyes found Elias's across the room, and what was in them frightened him — not because it was reckless, but because it was the same thing he'd seen in Vane's eyes two years ago, before Passchendaele, before everything: the look of a person who has found the work they will do for the rest of their life, however short the rest of it turns out to be.

"When does the next signature go to press?" she asked Céline.

"Thursday. If the paper arrives."

"What do you need?"

"Hands," Céline said. "Setting type is slow, and my eyes are fifty-one years old, and every hour I spend reading your handwriting is an hour the press stands idle. Can you read a case?"

"I can learn."

"Can you keep silent in a raid?"

"Yes."

"Can you lie?"

Freya's jaw tightened. Elias saw it — the cost of that question, landing exactly where Céline had aimed it. The nurse had a found family upstairs in the dressing station; Elias had told him as much on the walk, warning her what this work demanded. Lying to strangers was easy. Lying to people who loved you was the tuition fee.

"Yes," Freya said.

"Then come Thursday," said Céline Dossin. "Bring your own apron. And mademoiselle—" she tapped the ledger once, lightly, "—this does not travel again. Not here, not anywhere. From tonight, what it contains lives in your memory and in our cases. Paper burns. Lead remembers."

* * *

They climbed the stair together just past two, and Elias pulled the green door shut and shot both bolts from outside, and for a moment they stood in the dark of the alley listening to the ordinary sounds of a town pretending to sleep.

"She tests everyone," Elias said. "Don't mistake Thursday for acceptance. She'll test you again. Harder."

"I know."

"And Freya—" He hesitated. He had delivered warnings all night, and this one mattered most, and he gave it anyway because she deserved it straight. "What she said, about the ledger staying here. She means it. And she's right. But understand what it means. Once those names are in her cases, in her metal, they stop being yours. They become the shop's. And the shop can be raided, and the shop can burn, and the people in it can hang. You'll carry that. Every name. Are you able to carry it?"

Freya was quiet long enough that he thought she hadn't heard, or had decided not to answer. Then, in the dark, he heard her exhale — slow, deliberate, the breath of someone setting a fracture.

"In the ward," she said, "we had a man last spring, a gunner, both legs gone. He asked me to write to his mother and tell her he'd been quick. That was a lie I told with love, and it was easy, and it changed nothing. His name went into a dispatch, and the dispatch was wrong, and nobody will ever fix it, because fixing isn't something dispatches do." Her voice didn't shake. That was somehow the worst part. "I'm tired of lying with love, Mr. Wren. I'd like to try telling the truth with it, once, before the war takes the chance away."

Somewhere down the lane, the dog started up again — three sharp barks, a pause, silence. Elias listened. Nothing followed. He touched Freya's elbow and turned them toward the canal, toward the long walk home, toward the ward and the hymnal and the life she'd have to lie to now.

But at the mouth of the alley he glanced back once, down the slope of the Rue Basse, to where the green door stood bolted behind them, and beneath the street's darkness he could feel — or imagined he could feel, which amounted to the same thing — the warmth rising up through the stone. A light below, burning on.

Two nights later, the paper delivery from Tournai failed to arrive, and Céline sent word that Thursday was postponed.

Three days after that, a colonel of the General Staff arrived at the dressing station with a requisition order and a pair of very patient eyes.
