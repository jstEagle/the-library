## 9. Céline's Types

The workshop smelled of ink and lamp oil and the cold breath of the canal that ran behind Rue Basse like a grey vein through the city. Céline Dossin had lived above it for eleven years, first as a printer's wife, then as a printer's widow, and now — though she would never have said so aloud, not even to Elias Wren with his careful eyes and his careful silences — as a printer herself.

She woke before dawn, as she always did, and went down the narrow stair with a candle cupped in one hand. The press stood where Auguste had left it, iron and patient, its rollers dark with old ink. She touched the frame the way other women touched saints' feet in church: briefly, humbly, asking nothing except to be permitted to continue.

On the wall above the type cases hung a photograph of a young man in a Belgian uniform, his cap slightly too large, his smile caught half-made, as if the photographer had called out something absurd at the last moment. Luc Dossin. Nineteen years old in the photograph. Dead at twenty-two, though the word *dead* was never permitted him by the men who kept the ledgers. The official record said *missing*. Then it said nothing at all. Then, when she wrote to ask, it said he had deserted.

Deserted.

Céline set her candle on the composing stone and looked at her son's face until the grief settled into its usual shape — not a wound anymore but a tool, worn smooth from use, fitted to her hand.

"Good morning, my heart," she said. "We work today."

* * *

She had learned typesetting at her husband's elbow the way other girls learned lace-making, and she had forgotten none of it in the four years since the Germans came up the road and Auguste went out with the civic guard and did not come back. Her fingers knew the case without looking: the upper boxes for capitals, the lower for lowercase, the small compartments of punctuation arranged like a village map she had memorized in childhood. She could set eight hundred ems an hour when her hands were steady. On bad nights — the nights when Luc came into her dreams wearing his too-large cap — she managed three hundred, and some of those had to be redistributed in the morning because grief made letters lean like drunks.

Elias Wren had come to her in October with a bundle of pages wrapped in oilcloth and a request that was really a confession. He told her about the advance — the one the dispatches called a *consolidation of positions*, the one that had fed two battalions across open ground into machine-gun fire that every junior officer on the ridge had predicted in writing. He told her about the reports rewritten afterward, the dead recast as deserters and stragglers, the arithmetic of shame performed so that no senior name need be attached to the error.

And he gave her the list.

Forty-one names. Men recorded as having fled their posts who had in truth died going forward — most of them forward, always forward, the poor obedient meat of them walking into the fire because they were told to and because they were brave and because there was nowhere else in the world to walk.

Luc's name was ninth on the list.

Céline had read it standing at her own door while Elias waited in the street like a man expecting to be struck. She read it once, folded the paper along its crease, and said, "You will need paper. Good paper, or the damp will take the ink. Come inside."

That was all. That was everything.

Since then, twice a week, sometimes three times, Elias came after dark with copy in his coat, and she set it, and locked the shop, and printed what the shop was built to print — funeral notices, wine labels, the parish bulletins whose innocent typography hid a second life beneath the forme. By day the press produced the ordinary paperwork of an occupied-adjacent town mourning its sons. By night it produced the truth, page by page, in editions small enough to carry in a bread basket.

It was not courage. She had thought about this long and carefully, lying awake in the room where Luc's cot still stood against the wall. Courage was Auguste going out the door with his old rifle. What she had was something colder and more stubborn — the refusal to let the lie be the last thing written. Every letter she set was a small stone laid against a door that powerful men wanted shut. She could not hold the door alone. But stones accumulated.

She printed as penance — because she had survived, because she had once believed the official telegram, because for three weeks after the *missing* notice she had prayed for a son who was already mud and chalk outside Passchendaele, and prayer aimed at a lie felt now like a second betrayal. And she printed as prayer, because there was no other kind left to her, and because setting Luc's name in metal — L-U-C, in twelve-point, in the same case she used for wedding announcements — was the only resurrection the century had offered her.

* * *

The girl came on a Thursday, in Elias's company, moving through the back streets with the careful walk of someone who has learned that night belongs to patrols.

Céline watched them come up Rue Basse from the shadow of the doorway — the tall Englishman with his courier's slouch, and beside him a woman in a nurse's cape, hooded against the rain that had started an hour past. Young. Younger than Luc would have been now, or near enough. There was a set to her shoulders that Céline recognized; she had seen it on women who came to the print shop to order memorial cards, that carriage of a person carrying something heavy and deciding, moment by moment, not to put it down.

"In here," Céline said, opening the door before they could knock. "Quickly. The rain is honest but the neighbors are curious."

Inside, she barred the door behind them and turned up the lamp just enough to see by and no more. The girl pushed back her hood. Grey eyes, red-rimmed with fatigue, taking in the press, the cases, the hanging sheets drying on lines strung across the ceiling like strange laundry — and stopping, inevitably, at the photograph on the wall.

"You're Dossin," the girl said quietly. "Luc Dossin's mother."

Not *the printer's*. Not Elias's introduction, whatever it had been. *His mother.* As if the list itself had introduced them.

"Yes," Céline said. "And you are the one who keeps the names."

She saw the flinch — quick, controlled, but real. So Elias had told her that much. Céline poured chicory coffee into three cups that did not match and set them on the stone where she composed, because the flat surfaces in this room were all working surfaces and she saw no reason to pretend otherwise.

"Elias says you can verify," she said. "Ranks, companies, spellings. That you wrote them down yourself, at the front, in your own hand."

"Some of them."

"Some is more than anyone else has." Céline sat, and did not offer the girl a seat, and noticed that the girl did not look for one. Good. "Then let us understand each other before anything else happens. I am not a patriot. I am not a rebel. I am a mother with a press. I do this because the army wrote *deserter* on my son and I intend that somewhere, in metal, in ink, in a form that outlives everyone in this room, the word *died* will stand in its place — died going forward, in obedience, in the rain, at twenty-two years of age. If you have come for glory, go home. If you have come because you cannot sleep otherwise —" she slid the coffee toward her — "then sit down and drink that, and we will see."

* * *

The test, Céline decided later, had assembled itself out of instinct rather than plan. Perhaps that was how Auguste would have done it; he had always said you learned more about a person from their hands than their face.

She took the girl — Ashgrove, Freya Ashgrove, a name like something off a seed packet — to the case wall. Forty drawers of type, each compartment a small grave of leaden letters, each letter cast in the same foundry that had served the town since before the war.

"Do you know what this is?" she asked.

"A type case."

"I know that. Do you know what it *is*?"

The girl looked at her. Rain dripped off her cape onto the flagstones, counting seconds. "Letters," she said finally. "Every sentence that will ever come out of that press is already sitting in these drawers. You just have to find it and put it in order."

Céline felt something move in her chest that was not quite pain. "Yes," she said. "Auguste used to say the truth is never composed, only distributed. Now. Show me."

"Show you?"

"You say you keep a ledger of names. Names are letters. Set me one. Set me the name of the first man you ever wrote down — the one you remember best. Compositor's stick. Don't touch the case yet." She held out the steel stick, handle first. "Just show me your hands know why we're here."

Freya Ashgrove took the stick the way a nurse takes an instrument — correctly, respectfully, without ceremony. But when she faced the case her hand hesitated over the drawers, hovering, and Céline watched her lips move silently, spelling, and saw her reach first for the wrong box — capitals instead of lowercase, the error of every beginner — and correct herself.

"P-r-i-v-a-t-e," the girl murmured. "J-james. No — J-o-s-h-u-a. Joshua Pell. Private Joshua Pell, Norfolk Regiment." Her fingers walked the lower case, finding letters with growing surety, and she set them in the stick upside-down and backward, as the craft demands, so that the name existed in mirror-writing, a secret even from the person setting it. "Pell. P-e-l-l. Died the fourth of October. He asked me to remember him. His name wasn't in the dispatches."

Her voice did not break. It simply stopped carrying sound for a moment, the way a clock stops between ticks.

Céline took the stick from her and held it to the lamp. The letters sat snug in their channel, evenly justified, spaced with a beginner's caution rather than a craftsman's confidence — but true. Backwards, unreadable, and true.

"He asked you to remember him," Céline said.

"He asked me to tell someone he'd been brave. Or just — that he'd been there."

"And did you? Tell someone?"

"Not yet." The grey eyes came up, steady and unflinching, and Céline understood that this was the real answer, the one beneath the answer. "I wrote him down. I've written forty-one of them down. Telling someone seemed dangerous. Writing seemed survivable. I'm beginning to understand those may be the same act."

"They are exactly the same act," Céline said, "once the ink dries." She unlocked the distribution frame and began, with two brisk movements, to return the type — p to its box, e to its box, the little leaden body of Joshua Pell's name sliding home into the dark. "This is the rule of my shop. Whatever you set, you distribute. Nothing stays composed overnight; if the police come, they find loose type and a legitimate trade. You take apart every name you build. It feels like killing them again, the first dozen times. After that it feels like keeping them safe."

"And after that?"

"After that," Céline said, closing the drawer, "you understand that this is a chapel and we are its sextons, and the sweeping is the prayer." She looked at the girl a long moment. "You may touch the case."

Something passed over Freya Ashgrove's face — relief, and fear, and underneath both, the particular hunger of a person who has been alone with a secret for a very long time and has just been handed a room full of people to share its weight.

* * *

They worked until nearly two.

Céline taught her the ritual of it: the setting stick braced against the thumb, the measure held firm, letters lifted with the tip of the tweezers rather than the fingers because skin oil fouls the ink. She taught her the words — *sorts*, for the individual letters; *hellbox* for the bin where broken type went, which made the girl smile despite everything, the first smile Céline had seen from her; *forme*, for the completed page locked in its chase, tight as a held breath.

Elias sat by the stove and pretended to read, but Céline noticed he watched them both, and she noticed something else: that when Freya laughed — once, softly, when she dropped an entire line of *Passchendaele* into the hellbox and had to fish it out letter by letter like a surgeon retrieving shrapnel — Elias Wren looked at the girl the way men look at things they have decided not to want, which is to say constantly, and badly, and from a great defensive distance.

Well. That was not Céline's business. Her business was lead and paper and the forty-one.

Near midnight she pulled from the drying lines a proof sheet — a trial impression, ink still smelling sharp — and handed it to Freya without comment. The girl read it standing under the lamp. It was the opening page of the account Elias had been feeding her in sections: the order for the advance, the ground it was sent across, the machine-gun positions known and marked on maps that survived in duplicate. Below it, in columns, the beginning of the roll — names, ranks, regiments, dates. Nine names set so far. Thirty-two to come.

"Number nine," Céline said.

Freya found it. Luc Dossin, 3rd Line Infantry, killed 26 October 1917. The letters were clean, upright, certain. Twelve-point. The size reserved in this shop for weddings.

"The same size as the announcements of marriages," Céline said. "I chose it deliberately. A marriage and a death should be given equal honor in type; it is only the living who rank them." She took the sheet back gently, because proofs were precious and paper was counted like ammunition now. "When the edition is done, I will pull one page with his name and burn it in the stove, and scatter nothing, and keep nothing, because he does not need the paper. He needs strangers in England and Flanders reading his name and thinking: he died going forward. That is his monument. You are helping build it. Do you feel the weight of that?"

"Yes," Freya said. And then, with the bluntness of the exhausted: "I also feel afraid. All the time. Since I met Elias I have lied to the people I love most, and I'm good at hiding things, and being good at hiding things turns out to be the loneliest skill a person can have."

Céline set down the proof sheet and studied her — this English girl with rain still in her hair and forty-one ghosts folded into a hymnal somewhere, standing in a stranger's print shop at midnight asking, without quite asking, whether there was room for her here.

"When Auguste was alive," Céline said, "an apprentice worked for us, a boy from Liège, clumsy, forever dropping his compositing sticks. Auguste told him: *you drop things because you carry them alone. Two hands, boy, and a friend to lift the heavy formes.*" She nodded toward the press, the cases, the drying lines hung with truth. "There are five of us now. Elias. Tam — the boy who runs for us, God preserve his eager heart. Myself. You. And the press, which counts as a person in this room; it has moods enough. That is your found family, such as it is: scattered, hunted, and bound together by lead. It is not much. It is more than most people die with."

Freya looked around the workshop — at the photograph of the boy in the too-large cap, at the forme locked and waiting on the bed of the press, at Elias pretending not to listen by the stove — and something in her shoulders changed. Céline had seen that change before, on women in mourning who decide, mid-grief, to live anyway. The heaviness stayed. But it became weight carried in common.

"Show me how to lock up the chase," Freya said. "If I'm to be a sexton, I should learn to sweep properly."

* * *

They finished at half past one. Céline locked the shop, banked the stove, and walked them to the door, where the rain had thinned to a drifting mist that turned the lamplight of Rue Basse into soft halos, each lamp a small defiance of the dark.

"One thing more," she said, holding the door so the light would not spill far. "You hide your ledger in a hymnal, Elias tells me. At your station."

Freya went very still. "He tells you everything?"

"He tells me what touches the shop. Your hymnal touches the shop." Céline smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile; grief had filed edges off her tenderness years ago, leaving only the useful parts. "Then hear an old woman's observation, freely given. Hymns are sung by everyone. Everyone includes officers. A book that everyone opens is the worst hiding place in the world and the best, depending entirely on whether the people around you love you or suspect you. Which do you have?"

"Both," Freya said quietly. "That's the problem."

"Then solve it before the paper stock arrives Friday. We print the next signature Saturday night, and I will need your eyes on the roll — thirty-two names, checked twice." Céline reached out and, on impulse, touched the girl's cheek, brief as a blessing. "You set a fine line of type, Nurse Ashgrove. Joshua Pell would approve of your spacing."

Freya blinked hard, once, and said, "Thank you," in the voice of someone accepting far more than thanks.

She and Elias went out into the mist, two shadows folding themselves along the canal wall, and Céline barred the door behind them and climbed the stairs to the room with Luc's cot against the wall.

She slept better than she had in months. In the morning she would understand why: for four years she had been one woman holding a door alone, and now there were hands on it besides hers — a courier, an eager boy, a nurse with rain in her hair and a hymnal full of the dead. Stones accumulating. The door growing heavier against the men who wanted it shut.

She did not yet know that among the people who loved Freya Ashgrove, suspicion was already awake and walking the ward at night with a candle, looking for what did not belong.

But the paper stock would arrive Friday. And Saturday night, whatever came, the press would speak.
