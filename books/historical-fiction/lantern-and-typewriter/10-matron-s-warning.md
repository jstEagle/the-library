## 10. Matron's Warning

The lamp above Matron Cobb's desk had a cracked glass chimney, and through the fracture it threw a thin bright line across her ledgers — the official ones, bound in grey board with brass corners, stamped WAR OFFICE PROPERTY — so that every page she turned seemed slit by light. Freya stood at attention before the desk and tried not to look at that line of light too long. She had learned, these past weeks, that things which looked like wounds could be openings, and things which looked like openings could be traps.

"You sent for me, Matron."

"I did." Matron Cobb did not look up. Her pen moved in small, economical strokes, entering figures into columns that would never be questioned because no one outside this room would ever read them. Freya watched her write and thought, not for the first time, that Matron's hand was the opposite of everything the war demanded: unhurried, exact, as though numbers were patients who deserved her full attention even in death.

Especially in death.

"Sit," said Matron, without looking up.

There was only one chair, a hard wooden thing with a wicker seat gone shiny from years of tired women. Freya sat. The dressing station was quiet around them — the deep quiet of two o'clock in the morning, after the convoy lorries had come and gone, after the last morphine dose, after Tam had finally been persuaded to stop sweeping a floor that was already clean and go to his bunk. Somewhere down the ward a man breathed in the particular rattling rhythm that meant the pleura was filling again, and Freya catalogued the sound automatically, the way she now catalogued everything: names, sounds, faces, all filed away in a book sewn into the spine of a hymnal three feet from where she sat, inside her kit bag, against the wall.

Matron finished her column. She blotted the page, squared the blotter, laid down her pen parallel to the ledger's edge with a precision that made Freya think of instruments laid out for surgery. Then she looked up.

Edith Cobb's face was famous among the nurses for being unreadable, but Freya had spent enough nights beside her at the table to know its weather. There were lines around her mouth that deepened when she was amused and shallowed when she was afraid. Tonight they were shallow. Tonight her eyes were doing something Freya had rarely seen them do: they were sorry.

"You were out of quarters on Tuesday night," Matron said. "And Thursday. And again tonight until half past eleven."

Freya's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. "The laundry tent needed—"

"Don't." It wasn't sharp. That was the terrible thing about it. Matron said *don't* the way she said *hold still* before a debridement — gently, firmly, meaning that any movement would only make it worse. "Iris told me you'd gone to the laundry. I checked the laundry. Sister Prewitt keeps a log of overtime hours, and your name is not in it for any night this month." She folded her hands. "So we will not insult each other with laundry."

The lamp hissed softly. Down the ward, the wet-breathing man coughed, and someone — Tam, probably, unable to sleep either — murmured to him.

"May I ask what I'm accused of, Matron?"

"Accused." Matron almost smiled. The lines around her mouth flickered and went still again. "You've been reading too many of those novels you keep under your mattress, Ashgrove. Nobody has accused you of anything. Yet." She rose, and Freya realized with a lurch how tall she was when standing — how she filled a doorway the way she filled a ward, not with bulk but with gravity. She came around the desk and stood looking down at Freya, arms folded, the lamplight cutting its thin bright line across her apron like a scar.

"When I took my training," Matron said, "at St. Bart's, in 'ninety-four, there was a sister there named Agnes Ferris. Best nurse I ever saw. Better than me, God knows, though don't repeat that. She could read a pulse from across a room. She could tell a man was hemorrhaging internally before the surgeons believed her. When the typhoid came through the children's wards she didn't sleep four hours a night for five months, and every child she lost, she wrote the name in a little book she kept in her pocket. Not the official record — the hospital kept that, cold and correct. A little book of her own." Matron paused. "Do you know what happened to Agnes Ferris?"

"No, Matron."

"She was dismissed. No reference. The matron found the book — found that she'd been recording deaths the hospital listed as recoveries, during an outbreak the governors wanted hushed because it touched their own families — and Agnes refused to burn it. She stood in the matron's office with that little book in her hands and she said, *these children existed*." Matron's voice did not rise. It never rose. But something in it gathered, the way pressure gathers behind a bandage. "She said it as if that settled the matter. As if existing were a thing the world honored." She unfolded her arms. "The world does not honor it, Ashgrove. The world files it. And the filing clerks do not forgive the people who unfile."

Freya's hands, in her lap, had gone very still. She was aware of her heartbeat in her throat, in her wrists, in the roots of her teeth. *She knows.* The thought arrived whole and freezing, like water down a collar. *She doesn't know what or where, but she knows there is a something.*

"I don't understand what you're telling me, Matron."

"Yes, you do." Matron crouched then — actually crouched, bringing her face level with Freya's, an intimacy so far outside regulation that it frightened Freya more than anger would have. Up close her eyes were grey shot through with amber, and tired in a way that no sleep could touch. "But I'll say it plainly, because plainness is a mercy and I have few mercies left to give. In twenty-three years of nursing I have learned there are two kinds of us. There are nurses who keep rules. They are good women, most of them. Necessary women. The army runs on them, and when the war ends they'll get medals and pensions and they will deserve both, and they will sleep well, and they should. And there are nurses who keep people." She let that sit between them. "You are the second kind. You have been since the day you arrived — since before that, I'd wager, back to whatever it was that put that look in you. The second kind is rarer and the second kind is better and the second kind burns, Ashgrove. Every time. Sooner or later, one way or another, they burn. Agnes Ferris burned. And I have spent twenty-three years asking myself whether watching it happen was the price of loving her or the sin of failing to stop her, and I still don't know the answer, and tonight I am asking myself about you."

"Matron—"

"I haven't finished." She straightened, joints cracking softly, and returned to her side of the desk, and somehow the distance restored between them made the words land harder rather than softer. "Here is what I know. I know that Private Ellery, the boy who died on the fourth, asked for you specifically in his last hour, and that you stayed past relief, and that whatever he gave you to carry, you are carrying. I know that a civilian courier with mud on his boots has called at the gate twice this month asking for you by name, and that Sergeant Halloran logged him as a Red Cross supply man, which he is not, because Red Cross men do not walk like soldiers trying not to walk like soldiers. I know that Nurse Byrne has been watching you with a face like a storm deciding where to land, and that she has not yet reported anything, which tells me she loves you, and love is the only reason a woman like Iris Byrne withholds a report." She ticked each item off with two fingers against the desk's edge, unhurried, inexorable. "And I know that your hymnal has gained weight."

The room tilted, very slightly. Freya heard herself say, with a steadiness she did not feel and marveled at, "It's the same hymnal I was issued."

"It is. *Hymns Ancient and Modern*, standard issue, and I have carried one through two campaigns, and I know what one weighs in the hand the way I know what a loaded syrette weighs." Matron picked up her pen and began, obscenely calmly, to true up a column of figures. "I am not going to open it. Understand me. I could. Your kit is subject to inspection and always has been, and if I chose to lift that book off your shelf right now and fan its pages, whatever is stitched inside would fall into my lap, and then I would be obliged — *obliged*, Ashgrove, the word does real work in sentences like this one — to act. And I do not wish to act. Because I remember what the dispatches said after the advance in October, and I remember counting my dead that week, and I remember that the number in my own tally book was forty-one higher than the number that appeared anywhere official, and I have been a keeper of rules long enough to know exactly what that difference smells like."

Forty-one.

The number struck Freya like a physical blow — the same number, the impossible, convergent number, that lay written in Elias Wren's careful list of men marked deserters who had died advancing. She kept her face still through sheer animal discipline, but her mind was roaring: *she counted them, she counted them herself, she has known for months, she has been sitting on this knowledge in this cracked-lamp room while I smuggled pages past her door.*

"Matron," she said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, roughened. "What are you asking me to do?"

"I'm asking you to hear a warning, since you seem constitutionally incapable of hearing advice." Matron set down the pen again. "Whoever you're helping, and whatever truth you're stitching into that poor hymnal, hear this: the men whose names you keep are not merely embarrassed by the record. They are *endangered* by it. There is a difference between an institution protecting itself and men protecting themselves, and the second kind will kill, and call it duty afterward. I have seen what happens to witnesses in this war. The Germans never managed to kill as many of our officers' consciences as our own paperwork did." She leaned forward. "If you must do this thing — and you will, I can see that you will, you have the Ferris look, God help you — then do it with your eyes open. Know whom you trust. Know whom you're spending. And know that when the fire comes, it will not come for you first. It will come for whoever stands closest to you and holds less. Think hard about who that is, in this station, tonight."

Freya thought of Tam sweeping the clean floor. Of Iris's storm-face. Of Céline Dossin's ink-stained fingers setting type by night in Rue Basse. Of Elias, walking like a soldier trying not to.

"That's not fair, Matron."

"No," Matron agreed. "Nothing here is. Fairness was evacuated in the first retreat and hasn't found its way back." She opened a drawer and took out — of all things — a tin of barley sugar, the kind sold in Poperinghe, and pushed it across the desk toward Freya with one finger. An offering. An absolution neither of them could name aloud. "Take one. You've gone thin."

Freya took one. Her fingers were not quite steady and Matron saw it and pretended, graciously, magnificently, not to.

"For the record," Freya said, standing, because her legs wanted to stand, "there is nothing in my kit that shouldn't be."

"For the record," Matron replied, returning to her ledger, "I believe you. Which should terrify you more than disbelief, because it means I've decided to be complicit, and complicit old women are the most dangerous creatures in any army." She wrote a figure. Blotted it. Did not look up. "Goodnight, Nurse Ashgrove. Mind yourself on the stairs; the third one's loose. I've been reporting it for six weeks and they still haven't mended it. Some things, you learn, never get fixed by reporting them."

Freya walked to the door. Her hand was on the latch when Matron spoke again, quietly, without turning, the pen still moving in its small economical strokes.

"Ashgrove. The boy Ellery. What was his name for the record — his whole name? The one they'll carve somewhere, if anyone carves anything."

"Thomas Henry Ellery," Freya said. "Of Kettering. He had a sister who plays the piano."

Matron's pen paused — just for a moment, just long enough — and then resumed.

"Kettering," she said. "My mother was from Northamptonshire. Good county. Write that down somewhere safe."

* * *

Freya did not go straight to her bunk. She walked the long way, through the corridor that smelled of carbolic and wet wool, past the ward where Tam had finally surrendered to sleep sitting up in a chair beside the wet-breathing man, his chin on his chest, one hand still resting near the patient's wrist out of pure habit. She stopped and pulled the blanket higher over the boy orderly's shoulders, and he stirred and murmured something that might have been *sister* and might have been *mum*, and her heart broke a little, cleanly, the way Matron had predicted it eventually would.

Then she went to the sluice room, locked the door, and stood with her hands braced on the cold porcelain edge until her breathing steadied.

*Two kinds of nurses.* She turned Matron's words over like shrapnel, looking for the edges. Keepers of rules and keepers of people. It flattered her to be placed in the second category and she distrusted flattery, especially when it arrived wrapped in prophecy of fire. But it wasn't flattery, she realized. It was grief. Matron had loved a woman named Agnes Ferris once, and had watched her destroyed by the machinery of record-keeping, and had spent twenty-three years deciding whether love required intervention or witness, and had never decided, and tonight had chosen — not intervention, not witness, but the third thing, the unbearable third thing: complicity. *I've decided to be complicit.* No officer in the chain of command had offered Freya anything so costly.

And the forty-one. Always the forty-one. The number followed her now from Elias's list to Matron's tally book like a debt collecting interest.

She unlocked the sluice room and went back down the corridor, and that was when she saw the strip of paper lying on the flagstones beneath the ward door — a folded slip, the kind Elias used, the kind that traveled through three anonymous hands and arrived smelling faintly of pipe tobacco and candle wax. She glanced up the corridor. Empty. Down the corridor. Only the lamp at the end, burning its steady, indifferent flame.

She picked it up and read it by that lamp, holding it close, angling the paper so the words faced her alone.

*Change of route. He wants to answer your letter himself. Tuesday, the usual hour, the usual place. He says: tell her the ink is still good.*

Freya read it twice. Then a third time, because the last sentence refused to behave like logistics. *Tell her the ink is still good.* A code, surely — courier's cant, some phrase agreed along the chain. But it was also, unmistakably, Aldous Vane's phrasing; she could hear the dry, precise irony of him in it, the man who had signed his letters from a training camp in Essex with jokes about the quality of the army's pens. A man officially dead for a year, sending her messages through the dark like signals from a ship everyone believed sunk.

She folded the slip small and slid it into her sleeve, and stood a moment in the lamplight with her heart hammering out a rhythm that had nothing to do with fear.

Tuesday. Two days.

Behind her, down the dark corridor, from the direction of Matron's office, she could no longer see whether the light under the door still burned.
