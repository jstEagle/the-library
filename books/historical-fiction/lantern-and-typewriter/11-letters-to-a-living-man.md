## 11. Letters to a Living Man

The first letter took four days to write.

Freya began it on the night after Elias Wren left the dressing station, sitting on her cot with the hymnal open on her knees as though she were reading scripture by candlelight. The ledger lay beneath the false spine of Psalms, and she did not take it out. She didn't need to. Every name in it lived behind her eyes now, forty-one of them and counting, marching in step whenever she closed her eyes at night: *Whitcombe. Airey. Osei. The boy from the Royal Fusiliers whose name she had taken down with a pencil stub because his own hand could no longer hold one.*

She wrote instead to the man who was not dead.

*Dear Captain Vane.* She crossed it out. Too formal for a man who had once written to her about the smell of rain on the downs at home, who had signed himself *Aldous* in his last letter and drawn a small crooked lighthouse beside his name, a private joke between them that she had never explained to anyone and never would.

*Aldous.*

She crossed that out too, because writing it made her throat close, and she needed her throat working if she was going to say anything worth saying. She settled at last for no salutation at all — just began, the way you begin a letter to someone you have argued with in your head so long that the greeting feels redundant.

*I don't know how this will reach you, or whether it will reach you at all. Elias Wren says your couriers are careful. I am choosing to believe him, which is either faith or foolishness, and I find I no longer care to distinguish between the two.*

*You are alive. I have written and rewritten that sentence a dozen times and it refuses to behave like an ordinary sentence. It sits on the page like something exploded. They told us Passchendaele took you. There was a notice. Matron read it aloud. I remember thinking the war had finally learned my address.*

Then she stopped, pen hovering, and looked around the tent. Beyond the canvas wall, the ward breathed its night sounds — the rattle of a man's chest in bay three, Tam's soft footsteps on his rounds, the wind worrying the guy-ropes like a large animal nosing at the pegs. Somewhere out there in the dark was a flooded farmhouse, and inside it a man the army had buried on paper, keeping accounts of his own.

She wrote until the candle guttered:

*Here is what I can give you, since it is all I have. I keep names. The ones the dispatches drop. I started without deciding to — a dying private asked me to remember him, and I did, and then the next one asked, and I did that too, and now there is a book of them hidden in a hymnal and I suppose that makes me a kind of heretic, because I have begun to think the book matters more than the psalms.*

*Elias gave me a list. Forty-one men, recorded as deserters, dead in the October advance. I have checked every name against my own entries. They match. Every man they called a coward was killed carrying out an order no sane officer should have given, and then erased twice — once by shellfire, and once by ink.*

*I want to help you finish whatever you are building. Tell me what you need. Verification, testimony, copies, anything. But understand what I am telling you when I say I will do it: I am not brave. I am simply past the point where pretending is possible.*

She hesitated over the closing. Then, before she could lose her nerve, she added a single line more:

*The crooked lighthouse. I still have your last letter. I kept it in my kit through two moves and one bombardment. Draw me another one, if you can still hold a pen. Let me know the hand hasn't changed.*

*— F.A.*

She folded the pages into a strip narrow enough to hide along the seam of a bandage roll — Elias had shown her how — and waited.

* * *

Elias came back on the fourth evening, mud to the knees, smelling of horse and woodsmoke, and collected the bandage roll without ceremony, as though he were collecting laundry. He was becoming a fixture of the station's edges: the men knew him as a dispatch rider, Iris knew him as a nuisance, Tam knew him as someone who paid in chocolate for small errands. Only Freya and Matron Cobb — or so Freya suspected — understood what actually traveled in his saddlebags.

"How far?" she asked, while he cinched the roll into a waterproof pouch.

"Three hands. A farmer outside Poperinge, a brewer's cart, and a woman who runs a laundry near the canal." He said it flatly, recitationally, but his eyes flicked up at the end. "Each one thinks they're passing love letters. In a sense they're right."

"Does the laundry woman know what she carries?"

"She knows not to ask." He swung onto his bicycle. "Replies come the same chain, sometimes faster. Don't watch the gate too obviously when they arrive. Watched gates get remembered." And he pedaled off into the blue dark of the evening, wheels hissing on the duckboards, gone.

For nine days, nothing.

Nine days in which the war continued its ordinary industry — convoys arriving at midnight, stretchers stacked like cordwood, Iris laughing at something Tam said over the tea urns, Matron Cobb moving down the ward with her lamp held high, casting everyone's shadow long against the canvas. Nine days in which Freya changed dressings and counted pulses and smiled at wounded boys and felt, underneath all of it, the specific ache of a letter unanswered — an ache she recognized with some embarrassment from girlhood, from waiting by windows, except that now the window was a front line and the boy at the other end of it was officially a corpse.

On the tenth day, a corporal from the mail detail handed her a package wrapped in brown paper and string, postmarked nowhere useful, containing two spools of surgical thread and a note: *From the quartermaster, per your requisition.*

Inside the spools, wound beneath the thread, were strips of paper in a hand she knew better than her own.

* * *

She read them that night, alone, by the shielded light of her candle, unrolling each strip carefully and laying them across her blanket in order like evidence at an inquiry. Which, she supposed, is exactly what they were.

*F.,*

*So the lighthouse found you after all. I confess I did not expect it to. When they posted my death notice I imagined a few people would be relieved and most would be indifferent, and I built my plans accordingly. You have inconvenienced both categories.*

*Yes. I am alive, in the way that a house is standing after a fire — the walls are up, if you don't lean on them. My eyes are half what they were; a shell at Langemarck took the brightness out of the left one entirely. I write large now and crooked, and you will forgive the state of this page because I cannot see the edge of it. But the hand is mine. I tested it myself, holding the pen the way you taught me to hold a grudge — firmly, and slightly to the left.*

He had drawn, at the bottom corner of the third strip, a lighthouse. Crooked. Leaning like a tired sentry. Its beam was a single scratched line reaching toward the torn edge of the paper.

Freya pressed her thumb over it for a long moment before she could make herself read on.

*You know already what I know, or you soon will, so let me be plain and spare us both the dance. On the ninth of October we were ordered forward across the open ground east of the village, against machine positions that had been registered and reported for eleven days. The report exists. I wrote part of it. The order went out anyway. Four hundred and six men went over. Forty-one came back under their own power, and the rest are in the ground or in no-man's-land, and by morning the operation report described the advance as "carried out with insufficient artillery preparation owing to circumstances beyond the control of this headquarters," and by the following week the missing were being posted as deserters, because a deserter dishonors only himself, whereas a massacre dishonors the signature at the bottom of the order.*

*I was carried out with the wounded. Nobody looks closely at a face ruined by shell-blast. By the time the swelling went down, I was Aldous Vane to no one, and I decided that was the most useful thing I had ever been.*

*Since then I have been assembling what I could not assemble while wearing a uniform: the order itself, in draft and fair copy; the signals log; the ammunition returns showing the guns that were supposed to cover us never fired; and now, God help me, your ledger. Because a document proves that a thing happened. Names prove who it happened to. Céline Dossin is setting the type as I dictate this — she lost a son at the same advance, though he is not on any list I could find, which is precisely the point of everything.*

*Now I will tell you why I am really writing, because Elias will tell you I am a coward about letters and he is right.*

*If anything happens to me — and the odds shorten daily; the colonel whose signature is at the bottom of the order has people looking, and I have been careless lately, half-blind men are careless — then the record must not die with me. It cannot sit in a farmhouse waiting for a better historian. Someone has to carry it the last mile: verify the names, guard the copy, see it printed entire, and put it into enough hands that burning one workshop burns nothing that matters.*

*I chose you before I ever knew the Lantern existed. I chose you because of your letters during training — you wrote about patients the way other people write about weather, as though they were facts of the world that deserved exact recording. Exactness is the whole weapon, F. They have lies, ranks, and printing presses of their own. We have spelling. If our spellings are true, we win slowly, and slow winning is the only kind available to us.*

*Finish it, if I fall. Not for me. I am not worth finishing things for. Do it for Private Whitcombe, whom I saw go down at thirty yards from the wire with his hands up, and who appears in the official record as absent without leave.*

*Draw nothing for me. Just keep the light.*

*A.V.*

*P.S. — The left eye sees shapes at dusk. At dusk I can almost pretend I am mending. Tell your Elias to come at the new moon regardless of what I say. He knows why.*

Freya sat for a long time with the strips spread across her blanket, the candle burning low, the ward breathing beyond the canvas. Then she rolled the strips tight, wound them back beneath the surgical thread, returned the spools to their paper, and placed the parcel in the bottom of her kit, beneath the hymnal, beneath everything.

She did not sleep. Around three in the morning she heard Tam's rounds pause outside her tent, hesitate, and move on, and she realized she had been crying without noise, the way nurses learn to, and she was glad of the training even as it shamed her.

* * *

Her reply took only one night to write, because there was less to decide now. The decision had already been made somewhere below thought, the way a hand pulls back from a hot iron.

*Aldous,*

*Consider it finished-in-advance. If you fall, the record goes the last mile if I have to sew it into my own uniform and walk it to London on my feet. This is not bravery. Bravery implies alternatives.*

*Some things you should have, since you are apparently determined to be honest with me and it seems churlish to hoard:*

*One. I lied to Iris yesterday. Small lie — where I'd been, whom I'd seen. She looked at me afterward the way one looks at a clock that has begun running backwards, and I have not been able to meet her eyes since. You should know that the cost has already started. I intend to keep paying it. I am merely noting, as a good recorder, that the account is open.*

*Two. Matron Cobb watched me seal your first letter. She said nothing. Matron saying nothing is louder than most people shouting. Either she suspects and is choosing blindness — a kindness — or she knows and is choosing silence, which frightens me more, and comforts me more, and I cannot yet say in what proportion.*

*Three. The new moon is the twenty-ninth. I will tell Elias nothing of your postscript, since you clearly meant me to manage him, and I will manage him.*

*Four. Here is your answer to the question you didn't quite ask: yes, I remember the downs, and the rain, and the smell of the gorse after it. I remember your handwriting before I remembered your face. When your death notice came I burned nothing and threw away nothing, which I now understand was instinct doing the work of hope.*

*Keep the light yourself, damn you. You are not permitted to be a martyr. Martyrs are easy to ignore; inconvenient living men with documents are much harder.*

*The lighthouse leans. Good. Straight towers fall in storms. Lean into it.*

*— F.*

She sealed it inside a bandage roll and gave it to Elias two days later at the gate, and this time she did not watch him go. She stood with her back to the road and listened to the bicycle fade, and felt the strangest sensation — that the most important correspondence of her life was traveling out through the mud in the same saddlebag as the regimental laundry lists, and that history, whatever it was, had no dignity whatsoever at street level. It was thread and paper and a woman who ran a laundry and didn't ask questions.

* * *

The reply, when it came, was short. Just one strip of paper, wound inside a spool of black silk that arrived with a box of dressings marked for the operating tent.

*F.,*

*Received. Understood. Agreed on all points, including the martyrdom ban, which I accept under protest.*

*Progress here: the first signature of the pamphlet is nearly set. Céline works like a woman saying rosaries. Your forty-one are now set in metal, which means they exist in a form that requires fire or theft to destroy. I find I sleep better knowing that. I recommend the feeling to you.*

*One caution, and then I will stop lecturing, having noticed that lecturing is what old captains do instead of weeping. The colonel — Hargreave — has requested a routine inspection of casualty administration at stations in your sector. Routine, they always say. He is hunting arithmetic. Numbers that don't reconcile are how they will find us, long before any informer does. Whatever you carry, make sure it reconciles.*

*And F. — thank you for the letter about the gorse. I read it four times. At dusk. With the good eye.*

*Keep the light.*

*A.V.*

She read it twice, committed it to the fire of her candle flame, and sat watching the ash curl.

A routine inspection. Casualty administration. Her sector.

Outside, the camp was settling into its night noises, but beneath them Freya caught another sound — voices low and quick by the officers' tents, and lamplight where lamplight shouldn't be, moving. She told herself it was nothing. She was very good, these days, at telling herself things.

She slid the hymnal deeper under her kit, pulled her blanket to her chin, and lay listening to the ward breathe, rehearsing in the dark the numbers of her ledger until every one of them reconciled — and did not hear Iris return late from the supply tent, nor see her pause at the gap in the canvas, nor notice how long her friend stood there, watching her by the light of the storm lantern, before walking quietly on.
