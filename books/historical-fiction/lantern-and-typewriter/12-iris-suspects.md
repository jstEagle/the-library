## 12. Iris Suspects

Iris Pemberton had been raised to believe that a ward ran on rules the way a clock ran on springs: invisibly, inevitably, and with ruin for anyone who pried at the casing. Her father had been a railway stationmaster in Derbyshire, a man who could tell you to the minute when the 6:40 would arrive and grew visibly pained when it did not. She had inherited that faith — in timetables, in uniforms, in the quiet majesty of things done properly. It was why she had volunteered in the first place. Not out of any grand passion for healing; she'd been honest enough with herself, by now, to admit that blood still turned her stomach on bad days. She had come because a hospital was an ordered thing even at the edge of a war, because there was comfort in a clean bandage applied at the correct interval, in a chart filled in with a steady hand, in knowing one's place and keeping it.

Which was why Freya Ashgrove was beginning to worry her more than the shelling ever had.

It started small. Small things always did. A lamp burning behind the curtain of the nurses' cubicle at two in the morning — no, not burning; glowing, low, hooded, the way one shielded a flame from a blackout inspection. Iris had noticed it first in October, coming off a night shift with her feet aching and her thoughts fixed on nothing more ambitious than cocoa and sleep. She had paused at the partition, meaning only to ask if Freya wanted tea, and had seen the shadow of her friend bent over something on her knees, writing. Writing, at two in the morning, by lamplight, when every sane soul in the dressing station was dead asleep or wishing they were.

Freya had looked up before Iris could speak — she always seemed to know when she was watched — and had smiled that warm, tired smile of hers, and said, "Letters home. Can't sleep."

And Iris, fool that she was, or perhaps loyal beyond the point of wisdom, had believed her.

She believed her the second time, too, though the second time there were no letters visible at all — only Freya's hands moving quickly to close something as Iris's footsteps sounded on the duckboards, something small and dark that slid into the pocket of her apron with a speed that spoke of practice. Practice was the word that lodged under Iris's skin like a splinter. One did not become practiced at hiding letters from one's dearest friend unless one had hidden them many times.

The third time was last Tuesday, and it was not a light in a cubicle but an absence. Iris had woken at half past midnight — the habit of a year of night shifts, her body rising to its duties whether rostered or not — and had gone down the corridor for water, and had passed Freya's cot and found it empty, blankets folded back with military neatness, boots gone.

Boots. At midnight. In November.

Iris had stood in the dark corridor with her cup of water going cold in her hand and felt something shift inside her chest, some load-bearing wall developing its first hairline crack. Because there were only three reasons a nurse left her quarters at midnight in boots, and two of them were forgivable — a dying patient calling for her, a summons from Matron — and neither of those explained why Freya hadn't woken her, hadn't left word, hadn't done any of the hundred ordinary decent things that friendship ran on.

The third reason was the one Iris refused, for six days, to name.

* * *

She watched instead. She was not proud of it. Watching sat ill with everything she believed about herself; it felt like the behavior of a spy in a cheap novel, all shadows and held breath. But she watched, and what she found was worse than anything she had imagined, because it was so much less dramatic than treason and so much more damning than simple vice.

Freya was careful — Iris granted her that, and it stung, the care of it. The absences came perhaps once a week, never twice in a row, always on nights when the ward was quiet and the roster thin. She told Tam she was checking stores. She told Iris, when Iris asked directly where she'd been on Wednesday, that she'd taken a turn about the yard — *couldn't sleep, needed air* — and her voice didn't falter, didn't color, didn't do any of the things voices did in the novels when a lie crossed the lips. That steadiness frightened Iris more than stammering would have. Stammering meant a conscience at war with itself. Steadiness meant the war had already been won, quietly, and Iris had not been invited to witness it.

And then there was the hymnal.

Iris found it by accident, which was how such things always happened, God arranging His discoveries to spare the finder the sin of searching. They shared the kit shelf, their dressers side by side, and on Thursday morning, reaching past Freya's things for the tin of carbolic, Iris's elbow caught the hymnal — the little khaki-covered *Hymns Ancient and Modern* every nurse carried, issued like a rifle and treated with rather less reverence — and knocked it to the floor. It fell open. Books fell open at their most-read places; her father used to say a Bible showed its heart that way.

This hymnal fell open at the back, and the back was wrong.

There was a thickness to it, a swelling near the spine, as though the book had swallowed something. And the pages at the rear — Iris saw this in the single second before she righted the book and set it back on the shelf, heart hammering as if she herself had been caught — the pages at the rear did not move like pages. They moved as one. Bound together. Sewn in.

She stood in the ward corridor with the carbolic tin in her hands and did not open the hymnal again. That mattered to her afterward, in the long accounting she conducted in sleepless hours: she had not opened it. Whatever else she was guilty of, she had not opened it. She had set the book back exactly as it lay, spine toward the wall, and walked away, and spent the rest of the morning pouring antiseptic into wounds while her mind ran in circles like a dog on a chain.

A hymnal was a private thing. A woman's hymnal was nearly as private as her correspondence. To open it would be theft, plain and simple, whatever it held.

But Freya sewed things into hymnals. And left her cot at midnight in boots. And lied with a steady voice.

Iris's father had another saying, one he deployed when a signal arm stuck or a lamp went dark on the up line: *Small faults grow into wrecks.* She heard his voice saying it now, in his stationmaster's cadence, patient and inexorable, and she hated him a little for being right.

* * *

On Sunday she almost spoke to Freya directly. They had the afternoon off together — rare enough to feel like a gift — and walked into Poperinghe to buy thread and, if they were honest, to buy an hour of ordinary life: a table at Skindles' with weak coffee, a newspaper three days old, the luxury of discussing nothing. Freya was good company that afternoon, better than she'd been in weeks, laughing at Iris's impression of Sister Hartley's walk, warm as the girl Iris had met in training at Camberwell, the one who used to sneak buns up to the ward cats and cry over Dickens.

Twice Iris opened her mouth. Twice she closed it. What was the question, after all? *Where do you go at midnight? What is sewn into your hymnal? Why do you lie to me?* Any of them asked aloud would burn the bridge they were standing on, and Iris could not yet see what waited on the far bank. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps a love letter, an illicit romance, a soldier she was ashamed of — Iris could forgive that, could almost bless it, God knew the war starved people of tenderness until they did desperate things for the taste of it. Perhaps that was all it was, and to accuse a friend of treason when she was merely guilty of love would be a wound Iris could never take back.

But a romance did not require a ledger-shaped bulge in a hymnal. A romance did not require practiced lies. A romance did not make a woman write by hooded lamplight with her shoulders hunched like a conspirator's.

And there was one more thing, the thing Iris kept pushing to the bottom of the pile like an unwashed dressing. Elias Wren. The stranger who had come through the gate in October with his box of "personal effects" and his soft, watchful manner, and who had — Iris was nearly certain — spoken to Freya alone in the yard for the better part of an hour. Who had returned once since, ostensibly to deliver a second parcel, though the parcel had been small enough to hold in one hand. Who had looked at Iris herself with a frank assessment she'd found faintly insulting, as though she were a door he was deciding whether to bother locking.

Freya had changed after Wren's first visit. Iris could not have sworn in a court-martial to what the change consisted of — that was the maddening thing — but it was real. Something had entered Freya through that man, some purpose or infection, and had been growing in her ever since, silent as gas gangrene, and now it was walking her friend out of her cot at midnight in boots.

Sunday evening, back at the station, Iris made her decision. Not lightly, and not cleanly; she made it the way one makes all the decisions that matter, with the stomach clenched and the mind still arguing. She would not go to command. Whatever Freya was doing, handing her to the colonel's provosts without understanding it first would be a betrayal no friendship survived, and if Iris was wrong — oh, if she was wrong — she would have fed a lamb to the wolves for the sake of her own unease. No. There was one person whose judgment encompassed both duty and mercy, who had run this station through three years of shells and influenza and the endless meat-train of the wounded without ever, in Iris's observation, making a wrong call on a soul.

Matron Cobb would know what to do. Matron Cobb always knew what to do.

* * *

She caught her at half past ten that night, in the dispensary, where Matron was counting morphine tablets against the ledger with the grim thoroughness she brought to everything that could be stolen, hoarded, or lied about. The lamp between them threw Matron's shadow enormous against the shelves of bottles, a giantess made of glass and tincture.

"Matron. Have you a moment?"

Cobb did not look up from her count. "If it's about the boiler again, the answer is still that we boil water on the stove like Florence Nightingale and thank God for it."

"It isn't the boiler."

Something in Iris's tone must have carried, because Matron set down her counter, closed the drug book, and looked up. She had a face built for authority — broad, weathered, the eyebrows of a disbelieving archangel — but her eyes were older and kinder than the rest of her, and Iris had seen them go gentle over dying boys a hundred times. Now they simply waited.

Iris had rehearsed this on the walk across the yard. She had planned to be oblique, tactful, to raise the matter of "irregularities" and let Matron draw conclusions. Standing there under the lamp, she found the plan crumbled like wet sand. What came out instead was the truth, blunt as a dropped instrument.

"I think something's wrong with Nurse Ashgrove. And I don't mean medically."

For a long moment Matron said nothing. The paraffin heater ticked in the corner. Somewhere outside a sentry coughed.

"Sit down, Nurse Pemberton," Matron said at last, indicating the stool by the scales.

Iris sat. And then, because she had begun and could not stop, she gave it all up like a confession: the lamplit cubicle in October, the object pocketed in November, the empty cot and the missing boots, the hymnal that fell open wrong and moved wrong at the back, the lies — she hated saying the word, hated hearing it in her own voice — the steady, practiced lies, and finally, reluctantly, Elias Wren, and the change that had come over Freya after his visit, like weather.

When she finished, the silence was so complete she could hear the flame of the heater breathing.

"You've known all this," Matron said, "and said nothing. For how long?"

"Six weeks. Nearly seven." Iris's cheeks burned. "I know how it looks that I waited—"

"How it looks," Matron said, "is that you loved your friend too much to inform on her and too much to ignore her. Which is the most uncomfortable way to love someone there is." She rose, went to the window, and parted the blackout curtain a finger's width, looking out at the dark yard as though the answer might be marching up the drive. "Tell me, Nurse. In all your watching. Did you see anything that suggested she was doing harm? To a patient, to the station, to any of us?"

"No. That's just it — I didn't see anything at all. I saw her *leave*, and I saw her *come back*, and whatever she carries, she carries it out and brings it back and—" Iris stopped, struck by the shape of it. "She brings it back. If she were stealing, or meeting a man, she wouldn't need to bring anything back. Whatever it is, she keeps it here. On our shelf. Beside my things." Her voice cracked slightly on the last words, and she despised herself for it.

Matron turned from the window. For a moment her face did something complicated — Iris would remember it later, that flicker, and wonder at it — a movement around the eyes like pain briefly surfacing in deep water, and then gone.

"And what is it you want from me?" Matron asked. "You've not reported her to command, though by your own account you've had seven weeks of evidence and the regulations are clear about your obligation. You came to me instead. Why?"

Because you're fair, Iris thought. Because you keep people as well as rules. But what she said was: "Because if I'm wrong, command won't care that I'm wrong. They'll take her away and ask questions forever, and even if they believe her in the end, they'll never forget her, and neither will she. And if I'm *right*—" She faltered. "If I'm right, I don't know what she's carrying, but I know she's afraid of something, and I want — I need — to understand it before I decide which master I serve. Hers or the army's."

"That's not loyalty to two masters," Matron said quietly. "That's loyalty tearing itself in half. I know the feeling intimately."

"Then what do I do?"

Matron came back to the table and stood looking down at her, and for once the authority drained out of her face and left only the woman, sixty years old and tired, carrying things of her own that Iris had never thought to wonder about.

"You do nothing yet," she said. "You say nothing to command, and you say nothing to Ashgrove — not yet. Watch, if watching eases you, but gently; she'll sense a huntress quicker than you think, and a frightened woman does careless things. Let me carry this awhile. I've carried heavier."

"But if she's doing something that endangers the station—"

"Then I will deal with it, Nurse, and I will deal with it myself, and the military police will hear of it from no one under my roof." The authority snapped back into her voice like a tourniquet drawn tight. "That is not sentiment. It is tactics. This station holds together because my people trust me, and I will not hand them to the colonel's hounds on the strength of a hymnal that fell open. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, Matron."

"Good." Matron picked up her drug book again, then paused, and her voice gentled by a degree. "Pemberton. One more thing. You came to me instead of to the colonel. Remember that choice. Whatever Freya Ashgrove turns out to be — fool, saint, criminal, all three — you chose tonight to give her the benefit of the doubt, and that choice says more about you than anything she's doing says about her. Don't lose sight of it. These years try to make us all informants. Resist where you can."

Iris left the dispensary with her head spinning and her heart no lighter — heavier, if anything, because Matron had not dismissed her fears, and had not confirmed them either, and had taken them up like a bundle she clearly recognized the weight of.

She was halfway along the dark corridor to her cot when she heard it: footsteps ahead, soft on the duckboards, and the creak of the side door that opened on the yard. She pressed herself into the alcove by the linen cupboard, pulse jumping, and through the gap beneath the stair she saw Freya pass — dressed, booted, a canvas satchel flat under her coat — pause at the door with her hand on the latch, glancing back up the corridor with that uncanny alertness, then slip out into the night. The door clicked shut. Through the frost-furred window Iris watched her cross the moonlit yard, hug the wall past the coal store, and vanish through the gap in the hedge that everyone pretended not to know existed.

Not toward the latrines. Not toward the officers' mess. Toward the road. Toward town.

Toward Rue Basse, Iris would learn soon enough, though tonight she knew only that her friend had lied to her face over coffee that very afternoon — *no plans tonight, an early bed* — and was now out in the freezing dark carrying a satchel, and that Matron Cobb, when told, would have to choose too.

Iris stood in the cold alcove a long time, watching the empty yard, feeling the crack in her loyalties widen by a hair's breadth with each tick of the corridor clock.

Tomorrow, she promised herself. Tomorrow she would tell Matron what she had seen tonight.

She did not know — could not yet know — that when tomorrow came, the ward would wake to find a staff colonel's motorcar already standing in the yard, mud-spattered from the Ypres road, and a summons waiting for Matron Cobb with the dawn.
