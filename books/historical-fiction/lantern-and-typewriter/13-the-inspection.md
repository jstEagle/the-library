## 13. The Inspection

The telegram came at half past four in the morning, and it was the word *figures* that woke Freya fully.

She had been sleeping in the chair by Bed Nine, where Private Hollis had lasted two days on morphine and stubbornness and had finally gone wherever the morphine could not follow. The ward smelled of carbolic and wet wool and the particular sweetness that no amount of disinfectant ever quite conquered. Rain ticked against the canvas roof in a rhythm she had long since stopped hearing — until Sister Brice shook her shoulder and pressed the flimsy yellow slip into her hand.

*STAFF COLONEL HARCOURT-VEY. INSPECTION 0900. ALL RECORDS TO BE AVAILABLE FOR EXAMINATION. DISCREPANCIES IN RETURNED CASUALTY FIGURES UNDER INVESTIGATION.*

*Discrepancies.* The word sat in Freya's palm like a cold coin. Somewhere up the chain, someone had noticed that the numbers going out did not match the numbers coming in — or rather, that they matched too neatly, too conveniently, forty-one men folded out of existence like letters tucked into an envelope no one was meant to open.

Matron Cobb read the telegram standing at the ward door, her hands folded into her apron, her face giving away nothing at all. She had a face built for giving away nothing; twenty years of nursing had carved it smooth as river stone. But her eyes went once, briefly, to Freya — one second, perhaps two — and then back to the paper.

"Very well," Matron said, to no one in particular. "Sister Brice, the wards will be spotless by eight. Tam, the stores inventory. Iris, the drug cupboard counts. Nurse Ashgrove—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—the records room is yours. I want every return, every ledger, every list squared away and accounted for."

"Yes, Matron," Freya said, and heard how steady her voice came out, and marveled at it from a small distance, as though it belonged to another woman.

*Every ledger,* Matron had said. *Accounted for.*

The Lantern was not accounted for anywhere. The Lantern lived inside the hymnal on the shelf above Freya's bunk, between *Hymns Ancient and Modern* and a water-stained copy of Tennyson, and if a staff colonel's fingers opened that spine he would find not psalms but names — three years of them, hundreds of them, written in her small careful hand: men who died whose deaths were never reported, men who were reported dead who were not, men recorded as deserters who had died face-down in the mud of an advance their own generals had ordered and then disowned.

Freya made her bed. Folded her blanket into its hospital corners. And then, with the unhurried movements of a woman doing nothing more remarkable than tidying, she took down the hymnal, carried it to the records room, and began to think about coffins.

* * *

The records room was a lean-to shed behind the operating theatre, smelling of paper and mouse and the ghost of ether that seeped through the plank wall. Shelves ran floor to ceiling: admission books, discharge books, death registers, ration returns, the endless bureaucratic sediment of a war that generated paperwork faster than it generated victory. Freya stood in the doorway with the hymnal against her chest and let her eyes move over the shelves the way Céline Dossin's eyes moved over a case of type — looking for the piece that would fit, the letter that would carry the sentence without anyone noticing it carried anything at all.

Coffin inventories.

They kept them because the Graves Registration officers required them: tall foolscap sheets, ruled in columns, recording number, name where known, unit where known, disposition of remains. Nobody read coffin inventories. They were the most morbid and therefore the most invisible documents in the entire station. A colonel inspecting casualty figures would go for the death register first, the admission books second, the dispatch copies third. He would walk past the coffin inventories the way a man walks past gravestones — acknowledging them with his hat, never reading the inscriptions.

Freya pulled a bundle of the sheets, still fastened with the official brass clip, and laid them on the table. Then she opened the hymnal to Revelation, where the pages had been carefully split along the spine with a razor weeks ago, and slid the Lantern out.

It was smaller than her hand span, sewn herself from butcher's paper and the good thread Tam stole from the sewing stores without ever asking what it was for. Its cover was plain grey. Its contents were everything.

She turned the pages slowly. Here was Private Hollis of Bed Nine, entered last night in pencil before the morphine took him past speech: *Hollis, P., 2nd R. Fusiliers, d. 0430, wound abd., mother Mrs. E. Hollis, 14 Tanner's Row, Leeds.* He had asked her — the way they all asked, some with words, some only with their eyes climbing the ceiling — *will they know? Will someone know I was here?*

"They'll know," she had told him, which was the only sacrament she had left to give.

And somewhere in London or Leeds, when the official telegram failed to arrive — because Hollis's death would be swallowed in the great soft mouth of a casualty return rounded down, misfiled, adjusted — his mother would wait. That was what the erasures actually did. Not statistics. Waiting. Women at windows. The war's cruelest invention was not gas or shrapnel but the letter that never came.

Footsteps outside. Freya's hands moved before her mind finished the thought — the old surgeon's trick, hands faster than fear. The Lantern went flat against the coffin inventory, aligned with its top edge, and the brass clip closed over it, and the whole bundle went back onto the shelf between the amputation returns and the laundry manifests, grey paper among grey paper, one more sheet in the station's long confession of dead men.

Iris put her head round the door. Her cap was slightly crooked, which meant she'd been running, which meant she'd been awake all night with the convalescents and was running on tea and duty.

"You've heard, then." Iris's voice was low. "The colonel. Tam says there's a staff car already at Poperinghe, asking directions." She hesitated. "Freya — you've nothing they'd want? Nothing irregular?"

The lie assembled itself in Freya's mouth with terrible fluency, and she hated how easily it came now, how the muscles of deception had been building themselves quietly for months like calluses.

"The returns are straight," she said. "Whatever the figures say at headquarters, ours match our graves. You can count the crosses yourself."

Iris looked at her a moment longer than the answer required. There was something in her friend's face — not suspicion exactly, not yet, but the beginning of arithmetic. Iris was the sort of person who counted things. It was why she was so good with drug charts and so difficult to lie to.

"I know," Iris said at last. "I know you're straight. It's just — someone isn't, somewhere, and when the staff come hunting they don't much care whose door they kick in." She straightened her cap. "Better hide your private papers, anyway. Letters and such. They open everything."

When she had gone, Freya stood with her hand pressed flat against the shelf, feeling through the wood the faint rectangular pressure of the hidden book, and thought: *she warned me. She doesn't even know she warned me.*

* * *

Staff Colonel Harcourt-Vey arrived at nine o'clock precisely, which told Freya he was a man who had never once been late and never once forgiven lateness in others. He was tall in the way that staff officers were tall — not physically so much as sartorially, his uniform cut closer than any field officer's, his boots polished to the black shine of furniture rather than mud. He had a face like a closed ledger himself: neat features, thin lips, pale eyes that performed their inspections of people the way they performed inspections of rooms, cataloguing and dismissing in the same motion.

He was accompanied by a captain carrying a leather dispatch case and by Matron Cobb, who walked half a step behind him with the expression of a woman conducting a tour of a property she did not own and did not intend to defend.

"Nurse." The colonel stopped in front of Freya in the ward, having apparently selected her at random, or perhaps precisely because she stood nearest the records room door. "You keep the returns here?"

"The records room is mine, sir, under Matron's direction."

"Mm." His pale eyes moved across her face, taking inventory: the shadows under her eyes, the ink stain on her left cuff, the steadiness or otherwise of her hands. "There has been a discrepancy identified in casualty reporting within this sector. Figures transmitted to GHQ do not reconcile with figures held at division. Someone is either incompetent or dishonest. Do you understand the seriousness of that, Nurse?"

"Falsified casualty reports are theft from the families, sir," Freya said. "Yes."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Approval, possibly, or the recognition of an unexpected instrument — a blade found in a drawer where one expected spoons.

"Then you will understand," he said, "that I intend to look at everything. Every book. Every page. And that obstruction of this inspection carries consequences that do not distinguish between the guilty and the merely careless." He turned. "The records room, please."

Freya led him down the duckboard path with the rain starting again around them, fine and needling, and she was aware of every separate sensation with horrible vividness: the smell of the wet poplars, the squelch of his boots, the weight of the hymnal-shaped absence in her chest. Terror, she discovered, was not a fog. It was a lens. It brought everything into focus so sharply it hurt — the exact grain of the records-room door, the precise angle of morning light through the one cracked window, the captain's thumb resting on the brass clasp of his dispatch case. She would remember this hour for the rest of her life, detail by detail, and some part of her mind noted this fact calmly, as if filing it for later use.

Inside, the colonel worked the way she had imagined he would: fast, systematic, contemptuous. He went through the death register with a wetted thumb, checking entries against a typed list the captain produced from the case. He pulled admission books at random and fanned the pages. He asked questions in a flat, rapid voice — *when was this man admitted, who signed this return, where is the duplicate of this form* — and Freya answered each one, and heard her own voice remain level, and understood that the levelness cost her something physical each time, like blood.

"Your admissions for October exceed your reported deaths by eleven," the colonel said at one point, tapping a page. "Explain."

"Evacuations, sir. Eleven men evacuated to base hospitals whose subsequent status we wouldn't hold locally. Their deaths, if they occurred, would be recorded at the receiving station."

"And if those stations also failed to record them?"

"Then the discrepancy would be theirs, sir. Ours reconciles." She said it clearly, and watched him look at her again — that longer look, the reassessment. Somewhere beneath her ribs her heart was performing a drumroll, but her face, thanks to three years of delivering bad news to dying men, remained a well-lit, orderly room.

It was while he was working through the middle shelves that his hand moved toward the coffin inventories.

Freya saw it happen in slow motion. His fingers rose, hovered, drifted — drawn perhaps by the brass clips, the tidy bundles, the sheer untouched orderliness of them, the way a thief's eye goes to the drawer nobody locks because nobody cares. The captain had stepped to the window. Matron Cobb stood in the doorway behind them, and Freya felt the older woman's attention settle on her back like a hand.

"Sir," Freya said, "if I may — the figures you're reconciling are casualty returns. Those are grave registrations, disposition of remains. They postdate the reporting chain entirely. If your discrepancy originates upstream, those won't bear on it."

A silence. The colonel's hand stayed where it was, suspended over the bundle that contained, beneath its third sheet, a grey paper book full of ghosts.

"You're instructing me on methodology, Nurse?"

"No, sir. I'm trying to save your time. There are four hundred beds and the wards don't inspect themselves." She gestured toward the door, toward the light, offering him the thing inspectors always wanted eventually: an audience. "If you'd like to see the returns in sequence — admissions against discharges against deaths, month by month — I can lay it out for you properly. On the table. With the duplicates alongside."

Another silence, longer. Rain on the roof. Somewhere out in the yard, Tam was singing under his breath while he hauled coal, something music-hall and filthy, blessedly oblivious.

The colonel withdrew his hand.

"Very thorough," he said, in the tone of a man declining a dish while memorizing the menu. "You will lay it out. Everything on the table. I will return within the hour."

* * *

He returned within the hour, and Freya was ready.

She had spent fifty-five minutes constructing a battlefield. The great table in the center of the records room now bore the entire reporting history of the station arranged in a fan: admissions, discharges, evacuations, deaths, duplicates cross-referenced with ribbon, each month tabbed with strips of linen tape on which she had written dates in her neat hand. It was, she knew, genuinely impressive work — the kind of display that satisfies an inspector because it gives him something to admire instead of something to doubt. She had learned this from Matron: when you cannot hide a thing, overwhelm. Give the searching eye a feast, and it will not go hungry into the corners.

She had also done one other thing.

While the colonel inspected the wards, Freya had taken the Lantern from the coffin-inventory bundle — too risky to leave it under his eye twice — and made a decision that had frightened her more than the inspection itself. She could not hide the book anywhere he might conceivably look: not her bunk, not the hymnal shelf, not the drug cupboard, not the linen store. He would search everywhere a hiding place existed, because she would have searched everywhere a hiding place existed, and she was looking at a man very much like herself.

So she had hidden it where no hiding place existed at all.

The coffin inventory bundle now contained, clipped at its heart, a single additional sheet — one of the Lantern's own loose pages, removed and substituted, grey butcher's paper among grey foolscap, covered edge to edge in columns of names and dates and next-of-kin addresses in her small writing. To any glance it was simply another inventory sheet, densely filled, professionally maintained. Names, numbers, dispositions. *Hollis, P., 2nd R. Fusiliers.* A page of the true record sitting in plain sight inside the station's most ignored document, one brass clip away from a colonel's fingertips.

The rest of the Lantern — the body of it — was at this moment sewn into the lining of a coffin inventory cover itself, stitched flat between the pasteboard backing and the grey cloth, in stitches so fine they read as factory reinforcement. She had done the sewing at six that morning with Tam's stolen thread, her hands steady, her mind reciting the forty-one names like a rosary. If the colonel took the bundle apart sheet by sheet, he would find the page. If he took the covers apart, he would find the book. If he did neither — if his eye slid off coffin inventories the way every eye in the British Army slid off coffin inventories — then the truth of forty-one murdered reputations would survive the morning folded inside the furniture of death itself.

He worked for ninety minutes. He checked her fan of returns against his typed list, found the sums true, found the duplicates present, found the nurse unshakable. Twice he paused over the coffin inventories; twice Freya spoke — once about Graves Registration procedure, once about a supply delay affecting burial parties — and both times his attention lifted away like a bird off a scarecrow's shoulder. The last time, he drew out the top sheet entirely, scanned it, and handed it to Freya to refile.

Her fingers did not tremble. She would think about that later, with something close to awe: her fingers did not tremble, and she refiled the sheet, and beneath it the Lantern's own page lay flush and anonymous, a traitor in uniform, indistinguishable, safe.

"Your returns reconcile," Colonel Harcourt-Vey said at last, closing the death register with a snap. "Which means the discrepancy lies elsewhere in the chain. Which means," and here his pale eyes rested on her with a new and more dangerous quality, the look of a man marking a card for future play, "that someone above this station is either lying or losing documents. You have a methodical mind, Nurse Ashgrove. It would be wasted at a dressing station."

"Perhaps, sir. But the beds don't make themselves."

Almost — almost — the corner of his mouth moved. He gathered his captain and his dispatch case, paused in the doorway with rain silvering the air behind him, and delivered his parting shot with the mildness of a man discussing weather:

"There will be further inspections. Unannounced. Men who falsify the record rarely stop at one falsification, and leaks have a way of running downhill before they run up." He settled his cap. "Keep your accounts straight, Nurse. All of them."

*All of them.* The words followed her like a draft through the shed after the staff car ground away down the lane.

For a long moment Freya did not move. Then Matron Cobb appeared in the doorway, took one look at her — at whatever was visible in her face, which Freya suspected was everything and nothing — and said only: "Tea. My quarters. Now."

In Matron's narrow room, with the stove ticking and the rain proper now against the glass, Freya sat with a tin mug in her hands and told her everything. The inspection. The coffin inventories. The page in plain sight and the book sewn into the cover. When she finished, Matron was silent for a long time, turning her own mug slowly on its saucer.

"You hid it inside the one document no soldier will ever volunteer to read," Matron said finally. "Among the dead, because the living can't be bothered to count them." She looked up, and her river-stone face was, for once, not smooth at all. "That was either brilliant or insane, and I genuinely cannot tell you which."

"Neither can I, Matron."

"Harcourt-Vey suspects a leak. He hasn't found yours, but he'll come back, and next time he'll bring authority to take things apart board by board." Matron set down her mug. "You cannot keep that book here, Freya. Whatever you're doing — whoever's helping you — it leaves this station soon, or it buries us all with it."

Freya stared into her tea. Outside, far off down the road toward Poperinghe, she could hear the staff car's engine fading, and beneath it, faintly, absurdly, Tam still singing.

That evening, when the ward lights were lowered and the rain had settled into its nightly patience, Elias Wren came to the gate out of the dark with rain beaded on his coat, and the look on his face told her before he spoke that the news was worse than inspections.

"You need to come with me," he said quietly. "There's been trouble. It's Tam they're asking about."
