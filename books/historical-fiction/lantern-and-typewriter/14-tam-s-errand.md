## 14. Tam's Errand

Tam Osgood had three ambitions in this war, and he kept them folded in his breast pocket the way other men kept photographs of sweethearts.

The first was to matter. Not to be decorated — he'd given up on decorations when he'd been rejected twice for the infantry on account of his lungs, which whistled like a kettle whenever he ran — but simply to matter, in some small and verifiable way, to somebody who wasn't dying. The second was to see a train leave from the far side of Ypres without flinching at the whistle. He was working on that one daily, and losing. The third was a secret so tender he didn't even fold it properly: he wanted Nurse Ashgrove to say his name right. Not *Tam*, not *boy*, not *orderly* — but Thomas. Nobody used Thomas except his mother, and she was in Norwich writing letters that arrived smelling of lavender and worry, and it seemed to him that if Freya Ashgrove ever said *Thomas* — just once, in her low voice that made even the word *morphine* sound like a kindness — then he would have mattered, wouldn't he? To her. In some small and verifiable way.

He was seventeen years and four months old, though he'd told the recruiting sergeant eighteen and told everyone else nineteen, and he had been at Number Sixteen Dressing Station for seven months, during which time he had learned to carry a stretcher without jostling, to hold a basin steady while a man was sick into it, to wash blood out of his own tunic in cold water so it wouldn't set, and to sleep through anything except silence, which woke him every time.

It was the silence that woke him on the morning of the errand.

* * *

Not true silence. Nothing in Flanders was truly silent. But a particular quality of hush, the hush of a ward between rounds, when the lamps were trimmed low and the only sounds were breathing — the easy kind and the hard kind — and somewhere down the corridor Sister Cobb's boots ticking like a clock that disapproved of everything.

Tam lay in his bunk in the orderly's annex and listened to the hush and felt, as he often did at that hour, the strange double life of his own chest: relief that the guns were quiet, dread of what the quiet meant they were all saving up for.

Then footsteps. Soft ones, deliberate ones, coming along the duckboards toward the annex door.

He knew them before the knock came. He'd spent enough nights pretending to be asleep while nurses passed in the corridor to have catalogued every pair of feet at Sixteen. Matron walked like a verdict. Iris like a girl skipping stones. And Freya Ashgrove walked like someone carrying water across ice — careful, level, committed, no wasted motion.

The knock came anyway. She always knocked. That was one of the ten thousand things about her that Tam had noticed and filed away and never said aloud, because saying things aloud about Nurse Ashgrove was how you got looked at by Iris in a certain way, and being looked at by Iris in a certain way made his ears go hot for an hour.

"Tam. You awake?"

"Always," he said, which was true, and swung his feet to the cold floor.

She came in as far as the doorway and stopped there, which was also correct — a nurse did not enter the orderlies' sleeping quarters, and Freya Ashgrove was scrupulous about the rules, scrupulous in a way Tam had privately decided was a kind of armor. She held a parcel wrapped in brown paper and tied with plain string, roughly the size of a hymnal or a large book, and under the dim blue shading of the lamp her face was doing something complicated that Tam couldn't read but could feel, the way you can feel weather change behind a wall.

"I need a favor," she said.

His heart performed a maneuver he felt all the way down in his stomach. "Yes," he said, before hearing what it was, because he'd promised himself months ago that if she ever asked him anything directly he would say yes first and find out later what he'd agreed to.

"You haven't heard it yet."

"Don't need to."

That earned him half a smile — the tired kind, the kind that cost something — and she stepped inside and lowered her voice further, though there was nobody awake within forty yards except men too full of morphia to hear God himself.

"There's a package that needs to go into Poperinge. To a shop. A stationer's — De Vos, on the main square. You know it?"

Tam knew it. Everyone knew it; it was where you bought writing paper and cheap chocolate and the postcards of ruined cloth halls that the men sent home to prove they were still alive among ruins. "I know it."

"The ambulance run goes in tomorrow morning. Matron's approved a stores trip and I've asked if you can ride along to help load. It's all proper — signed chit and everything." She held up a paper slip as proof, and it was a real chit, Tam saw, properly stamped, which mattered because everything at Sixteen lived or died by stamps. "But there's this as well." She turned the parcel over in her hands once, twice, as if checking its weight against something invisible. "It needs to reach De Vos before noon. You hand it to the man behind the counter — old fellow, grey beard, spectacles. Tell him it's the second consignment of carbon paper he ordered. That's all. He'll understand."

"Carbon paper," Tam repeated.

"That's all you need to know," she said gently, and something in the gentleness made the hairs on Tam's arms stand up, because he had learned by now — seven months of dressing stations will teach you — that people only get gentle when the thing underneath is sharp. "It's letters. Correspondence for a friend of mine. Nothing more."

"Letters," said Tam.

"Private correspondence." Her eyes held his and they were steady and they were asking him something larger than the question she'd asked, and Tam, who could not have articulated what it was, understood it perfectly anyway. She was asking him to be trusted. She was asking whether he was the sort of person who could be handed a wrapped book and a sentence about carbon paper and carry both without turning them over in the dark.

"I can do that," he said.

"Thank you, Thomas."

There it was. His whole name, spoken plainly, in that low voice, in the blue hour before dawn, and Tam felt it go through him like warm water through a cold pipe, and he thought: *now I matter,* and he thought: *whatever this is, I'd carry it through a barrage.*

What he thought next — much later, much too late, in the way of all thoughts that arrive after their usefulness — was that Nurse Ashgrove's hands had trembled, very slightly, around the parcel. He hadn't seen hands tremble like that since Passchendaele, and the men whose hands trembled like that were the ones who'd seen something they couldn't put down again.

* * *

The ambulance run to Poperinge went out at half past eight under a sky the color of dishwater, and Tam rode in the back among crates of dressings and a drum of carbolic, bouncing on a folding seat, rehearsing his line. *Second consignment of carbon paper.* Four words. He turned them over like coins, testing each for false metal.

Beside him sat Corporal Denny, the driver, a Lancashire man with a face like a closed fist and a running commentary on everything they passed, which was mostly mud, mud in various stages of ambition, and the occasional shattered tree standing up out of it like a man buried to his shoulders.

"Carbon paper, eh?" said Denny, when Tam, filling a silence, mentioned the errand.

"For the office," Tam said. "Stationery."

"Stationery." Denny rolled the word around like a pebble. "We're seventeen miles from the biggest artillery concentration in the history of the world, and we're running errands for stationery."

"Somebody has to keep the forms straight."

"Somebody has to," Denny agreed, and changed gear, and said nothing else, but he glanced back at Tam once in the mirror, briefly, the way a man glances at a dog he suspects is carrying something in its mouth it oughtn't to have.

Poperinge rose out of the flatland like a promise — whole roofs, unbroken windows, a square with a church tower that still had its top on. Tam loved the town the way soldiers love all such towns, with a hunger that had nothing to do with beauty: here were cafés where the beer was cold, and a cinema that smelled of sawdust and cigarettes, and girls who looked at you, and for one afternoon the war became a rumor instead of an employer.

They unloaded stores at the supply depot, and Denny went off to argue with a quartermaster about a requisition, and Tam stood in the main square with the parcel under his arm feeling suddenly, enormously conspicuous. The brown paper was ordinary. The string was ordinary. His tunic was ordinary. He was, by any measure, the most ordinary object in the square, and yet he walked the hundred yards to De Vos's shop feeling like a marching band.

The bell over the door clanked. Inside it was cool and dim and smelled of paper and glue and pipe smoke, shelves rising to a ceiling lost in shadow, and behind the counter stood exactly the man described: old, grey-bearded, spectacled, with ink stains on his fingers like bruises.

"Yes?" said the old man, not looking up.

"I've got—" Tam's mouth went dry. Four words. He'd had four words. "The carbon paper. Second— the second consignment. Of carbon paper. That you ordered."

Something happened to the old man's face. Nothing moved in it, and yet everything rearranged, the way a room changes when a lamp is carried into a different corner. He set down his pen. He took the parcel from Tam's hands with a care that seemed excessive for stationery, weighed it once, and nodded.

"Tell your mistress we're short-handed these days," he said. "Compositors are hard to come by. But the work gets done."

"Right," said Tam, who understood none of it. "I'll tell her."

"And tell her—" The old man paused. Through the shop window Tam could see the square, the church, two military policemen leaning against a lamppost with the loose patience of cats outside a mousehole. "Tell her the type is holding well. No cracked sorts. We're proud of the fount."

"I'll tell her that too," said Tam, and got out of the shop as fast as politeness allowed, because the old man's voice had dropped into a register Tam recognized from the ward — the register men used when they were saying true things in front of strangers and needed the strangers to think they were talking about nothing.

In the square, the military policemen had stopped leaning. They were watching the door of De Vos's shop now, casually, the way men watch a door when a third man has gone around the back of the street to watch the other end of it.

Tam walked away at a pace he hoped read as bored.

* * *

The ride back was quiet. Denny drove with his fist-face forward and his thoughts elsewhere, and Tam sat among the empty crates and let the town fall away behind him and tried to assemble the morning into something that made sense.

A parcel of private correspondence, delivered as carbon paper. An old man who spoke of compositors and types and founts — printing words, Tam realized slowly, words out of a printer's shop, words his father would have known, his father who had worked eleven years at the *Norwich Argus* before the army took his eyes. *The type is holding well. No cracked sorts.* That wasn't stationery talk. That was a message dressed as stationery talk, going through Tam like a wire through insulation.

Which meant the parcel was—

He stopped the thought at the edge, the way you stop at a trench parapet. Because whatever the parcel was, Freya Ashgrove had carried it herself to his bunk at five in the morning, and Freya Ashgrove was the steadiest person at Number Sixteen, and if she was frightened, then the parcel was the least of what was frightening, and Tam found he did not want to walk any closer to the edge than that. Not today. Today he had done a thing. Today he had mattered. Nurse Ashgrove had said *Thomas*.

He held onto that the way a drowning man holds a plank, and it bore him all the way back to the dressing station, and it might have borne him longer, except that as the ambulance swung through the gate he saw the ward through the windows and the ward was wrong.

Not busy-wrong. Busy was normal; busy was Tuesday. Wrong was stillness. Nurses standing in knots instead of moving. Matron Cobb at the center of one knot with her spine like a pikestaff, and beside her a staff officer Tam didn't know, a colonel with red tabs and a face like a ledger balanced on a knife's edge, and behind the colonel, two men in the uniform of the Military Police, standing the way stone lions stand, except that stone lions don't look at everyone who passes.

Denny braked. "Visitors," he said. "Bad ones. Red tabs means they've come from Corps, and MPs means they've come to take something away." He looked back at Tam in the mirror, and this time the glance lasted. "You want to know something funny, lad? There was a bloke at the depot this morning asking questions. Polite bloke, staff cap, notebook. Asking which units ran stores into Pop, which drivers, which loaders." A beat. "I said I couldn't remember. Funny thing, memory."

Tam's mouth had gone dry again, and this time it stayed dry.

He climbed down with the others and crossed the yard, and as he passed the open door of the receiving ward he heard the tail of the colonel's voice, clipped and pleasant, the most dangerous voice there is:

"—irregularities in the casualty returns, Matron. Small ones. The sort of smallness that interests me precisely *because* it is small. Someone is counting something they shouldn't be. And when I find out who—" Here the colonel smiled, and Tam, passing in the doorway, saw the smile and thought of the old man's words: *the work gets done.* Two sentences about the same secret, one hopeful and one hungry, and between them, in the middle, carrying parcels and believing in carbon paper, stood Thomas Osgood, aged seventeen and four months, who had volunteered to matter.

Matron Cobb's eyes crossed the yard and landed on him — landed, held, measured — and Tam had the sudden vertiginous certainty that she knew about the parcel, about the morning, about the whole hidden architecture of the last weeks, that she had perhaps always known, and that her look was not accusation but arithmetic: *one boy, one errand, one more thread for the colonel to pull.*

"Orderly," she said, loud enough for the colonel to hear, in her flattest ward voice. "There's beds to strip in C. Stop dawdling."

"Yes, Matron," said Tam, and fled into the smell of carbolic and the rustle of sheets, and began stripping beds with hands that would not quite stay steady, and through the window of Ward C, past the row of heads on the pillows, he could see the colonel's car still sitting in the yard, engine off, patient as a spider, waiting.

And it occurred to him, folding a blanket with great care, that whatever he had carried into Poperinge that morning had not been letters.

And that he would carry it again if asked.

And that one of those three facts was going to kill him.
