## 15. Forty-One Reasons

The Rue Basse smelled of ink and coal smoke and, faintly, of the river that ran somewhere below the cobbles — a smell Freya had come to think of as the smell of the truth being made.

It was past midnight. The workshop's shutters were drawn and bolted, a blanket nailed over the one window that faced the street, and still Céline Dossin worked with the lamp turned so low its flame seemed embarrassed to be seen. She sat before the type case like a woman at prayer, fingers moving over the little lead letters with a tenderness Freya had only ever seen in wards, in the last minutes, when there was nothing left to do for a man but touch him.

"Read," Céline said. It was not quite an order. Her accent turned the word into two syllables, a small bell struck twice. *Ree-ad.* "You read, I set. We go faster this way, and my eyes—" she tapped the corner of one, "—they are fifty-one years old and they have cried too much already."

Freya unfolded the list on her knee. Elias had copied it out in his cramped, careful hand from Vane's papers: forty-one names, forty-one regiments, forty-one addresses to which the official telegrams of condolence had been sent — each one a lie by omission, each one telling a mother or a wife or a father that their boy had fallen *with honour in the field*, when what he had actually done was walk into machine-gun fire on the orders of a man who then wrote him down as a deserter.

Forty-one families who would never know. Unless they knew.

"Private Thomas Ellery," Freya read. "Mrs. Ada Ellery, 14 Chandler's Row, Bethnal Green, London."

Céline's hands moved. The letters came up from their compartments one by one, upside down and backwards, mirror-writing that would become truth when the press came down. *E-L-L-E-R-Y.* She set them into the composing stick with a click, click, click, like a clock counting something other than time.

"You know these men?" Céline asked. She did not look up. Her hands knew their work.

"Some." Freya looked at the name. Thomas Ellery. "Ellery — yes. He came through our station in October. A shrapnel wound in the shoulder, nothing terrible. He joked about it. He said the Hun had tried to take his coat off without asking permission."

"And after?"

"After he went back up." Freya heard her own voice go flat, the way voices went flat in the ward when you could not afford the feeling underneath. "He was in the advance. The one that—"

"The one that never happened." Céline's mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely grief. "Yes. I understand. In Belgium we are experts in things that never happened."

* * *

They worked through the night in that rhythm: read, set, read, set. And somewhere in the third hour, Freya stopped reading names as words and began to see them as faces.

It happened with the fourth name, or maybe the fifth. "Lance-Corporal Daniel Osei, Mrs. Grace Osei, 3 Fournier Street, Spitalfields." She had known an Osei. Of course she had known an Osei — half the London battalions were stitched together out of streets she could picture, and she had held enough of those soldiers' hands to know their mothers' voices without ever hearing them. This Osei had sung while Iris changed his dressing, some music-hall thing about a girl named Daisy, getting every word wrong, and Iris had laughed until she'd had to sit down on the footstool, and Matron Cobb had appeared in the doorway wearing her expression of disapproval worn thin as paper over something warmer, and had let him finish the verse before telling him that singing patients were still patients and would keep their arm still.

Was it the same man? She couldn't be sure. There were a thousand Oseis. But her heart insisted, and her heart had learned in three years of war that insistence was all it had.

"Céline," she said. "Do you mind if I tell you about them? As we go?"

Céline looked up for the first time in an hour. The lamplight caught the silver in her dark hair and the ink stains on her fingers, four of them black to the second knuckle.

"My son," she said, "is on your list."

Freya had known this. Elias had told her, carefully, the way you hand someone something breakable. But hearing it from Céline herself, in this room full of her dead son's memorial made of metal letters, was different.

"Émile Dossin," Freya said. "Rifleman. His mother is Madame Céline Dossin, Rue Basse—"

"Number nine." Céline touched her own chest briefly, a gesture that might have been a salute in another life. "You see? I am on the list also. Forty-one families. I am one address among forty-one." She picked up the composing stick. "So yes. Tell me about them. All of them. Émile's friends never write to me. They cannot. Perhaps they think it hurts me. Idiots. What hurts me is the silence."

* * *

So Freya told her.

She told her about Private Ellery and his stolen coat. She told her about a sergeant named Bramley whose address was a farm outside a village called Thurlby in Lincolnshire, and how Bramley, waiting for evacuation, had described the entire layout of that farm down to the duck pond, and how Tam — young Tam, who hung on every word like scripture — had asked if there were ducks *now*, in winter, and Bramley had said ducks don't care about wars, lad, ducks are pacifists, and Tam had laughed so hard his own stretcher-bearer shoulders shook.

Click. Click. Click. *B-R-A-M-L-E-Y.*

She told her about Corporal Whitcombe of Cardiff, who had written poetry in pencil on the backs of cigarette packets and hidden them in his boot because he said the officers would confiscate anything that rhymed. She'd found the packets when they cut the boots off him. He'd been beyond speech by then, but his eyes had tracked hers across the ceiling, and she had read one of the poems aloud to him — badly, stumbling, because her voice had wanted to break — and she would swear until she died that at the last line his breathing eased, as if the poem arriving back to him through another person's mouth was a kind of homecoming.

Céline set *W-H-I-T-C-O-M-B-E* and then stopped, both hands flat on the case.

"This one," she said. "This one I will set twice. Once for the list. Once for myself." She pulled a fresh galley and began again, letter by letter, slower now, ceremonial. "A poet. They call him a deserter."

"They call thirty-eight of them deserters," Freya said. The number sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. "Thirty-nine, counting your son. Two they recorded as killed by enemy action, which at least is true in the way that everything is true — they were killed, and it was the enemy's guns, but it was our order that walked them into the range of those guns."

"And the order itself?"

"Rewritten. Dated differently. Worded so that the advance reads as authorized reconnaissance that exceeded its instructions." Freya had read Vane's copies of the documents so many times she could recite the phrases. *Acting without orders.* *Failure to withdraw when directed.* Phrases written by living men about dead ones, phrases designed to make widows ashamed of their husbands, mothers ashamed of their sons. "The dead can't contradict it. That's the whole architecture of it. You build the lie on top of the grave, and no one digs."

"No one," Céline said, "except us."

* * *

Toward dawn — though dawn in the workshop was only a grey line under the shutters, a change in the quality of the silence outside — Freya reached the middle of the list and her voice began to fail her.

Not from tears. She had learned long ago to keep tears for later, banked like a fire overnight. It failed her instead from accumulation, from weight, the way a shelf fails — not any single book but the sum of them. Forty-one names read aloud into a room the size of a chapel. Forty-one addresses spoken into the dark where a Belgian widow turned each one into lead.

She thought: *somewhere in Bethnal Green there is a woman named Ada Ellery who sets a place at the table for a son the army has told her died running away.* She thought: *she will die believing it, unless.* Unless this. Unless pages. Unless a press in a cellar behind a shuttered window on a street that smelled of the river.

"Elias says the pamphlet goes to the families first," Freya said, when she could speak steadily again. "Before anyone else. Before the newspapers, before Parliament, before any of it. Each address gets a copy."

"With what written on it?" Céline asked. "Just the facts? Names, dates, the order, the falsification?" She shook her head before Freya could answer. "No. Facts are not enough. Facts are for committees. For a mother you must say: *your son did not run.* Four words. Everything else is scaffolding around those four words."

Freya looked at the list in her lap. Forty-one men. Forty-one sentences that amounted to the same four words.

"Your son," she said quietly. "Tell me about Émile. Not how he died. How he lived."

Céline was silent for a long moment. Then she took off her spectacles, polished them on her apron with great thoroughness, put them back on, and began setting type again, and spoke while her hands worked, which Freya understood was the only way she could speak of it at all.

"He played the violin. Badly. His father bought it from a pawnbroker in Antwerp and Émile loved it more than he respected it — he treated it roughly, carried it by the neck, and it sang anyway. When the Germans came through in 'fourteen we left in an afternoon. He carried the violin. I said, take bread instead, and he said, *Maman, bread keeps you alive, the violin tells you why.*" Her hands paused on the case. "He was seventeen. They took him anyway, the year after, because Belgium was desperate. And in his letters — I have eleven letters, I count them every night like a rosary — in his letters he wrote that when he played for the English soldiers, they gave him things. Chocolate. Buttons. One of them gave him a photograph of a girl in Margate. He said music was better currency than money because everyone was rich in it for a moment."

Freya's throat closed. She thought of the ward. She thought of the singing patient, the joking private, the poet in the boot. She thought: *this is what the ledger has been for, all along. Not names. This. The violin, the ducks, the girl in Margate.*

"That's why the dispatches matter," she said. "That's why the *official* version matters. Because a dispatch is the only biography most of them get. One line. *Died of wounds.* *Missing, presumed dead.* And for these forty-one: *deserted.* One sentence, and it's wrong, and it's forever."

"So we make a longer sentence," Céline said. She lifted the composed line from the stick and slotted it into the forme with a finality that sounded like a door closing. "We make a whole book if we must."

* * *

They finished the last name as the grey light strengthened: Private Alfred Nunn, Mrs. Edith Nunn, 22 Back Lane, a village called Snettisham on the Norfolk coast. Freya had never met an Alfred Nunn. She felt the lack of him like a missing tooth — forty faces assembled now in her mind and the forty-first standing just out of sight, refusing to resolve.

"I don't know him," she admitted.

"Then invent him," Céline said. "No —" she raised an ink-black finger, "— not invent. *Imagine* him. Imagination is not lying. Imagination is what we owe the ones we never met. Somewhere in Norfolk there is a woman who knows exactly how he laughed, and we will send her page, and she will supply what we cannot. That is why the addresses, Freya. The army writes to the addresses to deliver lies. We write to the addresses to deliver the correction. The family completes the man. We only carry the message."

Freya folded the list along its worn creases. Her thumb had smudged the first entry — ELLERY, in Elias's pencil, blurred now by her handling — and she thought: *good. Let my fingerprints be on it. Let every one of us leave fingerprints.*

Outside, far off toward the salient, the guns had started their morning argument, distant and rhythmic, like a heart heard through a stethoscope. Inside, Céline locked the type case, slid the galleys into their wooden tray, and covered the whole forme with a cloth, the way you cover bread while it rises.

"Tomorrow we print the first signature," she said. "Pages one through sixteen. If the press behaves. If the paper arrives. If the sky does not fall." She looked at Freya across the lamp, and her eyes were tired and absolutely unextinguished. "If the sky falls, we print anyway. Paper burns, but the forme — the forme survives fire, mostly. Lead remembers."

*Lead remembers.* Freya carried the phrase out with her into the blue cold of the morning, along the wet cobbles, past the shuttered houses, thinking of hymnals and hems and hiding places, thinking: *I will be the paper if I must. Fold me, sew me, burn me — the words will survive in someone.*

She was halfway back to the station, the list pressed flat against her chest inside her coat, when she saw the figure waiting near the gate in the early light — upright, immaculate even at this hour, staff cap squared with a precision that made the morning seem untidy around him.

Colonel Hargreaves. Standing at her gate as if he had been there for some time.

As if he had been watching the road.
