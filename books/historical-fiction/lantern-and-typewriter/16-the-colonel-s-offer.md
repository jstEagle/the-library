## 16. The Colonel's Offer

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a lance-corporal who would not meet Freya's eyes.

"Sister Ashgrove. Colonel Hargreaves requests your presence at headquarters. Fourteen hundred hours."

*Requests.* The word had the texture of a drawn bolt. In the eleven months since she'd come to the dressing station at Brandhoek, no one had *requested* her presence anywhere; they had ordered her to wards, to triage, to the long nights of mud and magnesium light, and she had gone because going was the work. A request from a staff colonel — a man with a red tab and clean boots and an office that smelled of paper instead of gangrene — was not a courtesy. It was a hook, baited and lowered into dark water.

She said nothing of it that morning. She changed a chest dressing for a Gunner called Pell who wept without knowing he wept. She wrote a letter for a boy from Cardiff whose hands were bandaged into mittens, taking down his clumsy loving sentences about a girl named Bronwen and a dog named Biter, and she did not open the hymnal in her kit, did not let her fingers drift toward it even once, though the ledger inside seemed to warm against her hip like a live coal all through the morning. Iris moved around her in the ward with her usual brisk competence and an unusual silence, and Freya caught herself cataloguing the silence — was it suspicion, or only tiredness? — and hated herself for cataloguing anything about a woman who had once held her hair back while she vomited after thirty-six hours of gas cases.

At half past one she pinned her veil, checked her cuffs, and walked the duckboard path toward headquarters under a sky the colour of dirty wool.

* * *

Colonel Hargreaves's office had been a schoolmaster's study once, in some Belgian village life that now seemed archaeological. The bookshelves still stood along one wall, their spines replaced by ledgers and file boxes; a map of the salient covered most of another wall, studded with pins whose coloured heads made a rash across Flanders. On the desk: a telephone, a decanter, two glasses. Two glasses. That detail settled into Freya's stomach like a swallowed stone.

"Sister Ashgrove." He rose — he actually rose — and shook her hand. His grip was dry and precise, a man who handled documents carefully. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please."

He poured sherry without asking whether she wanted it. Up close he was fiftyish and grey and unremarkable, the kind of face designed by God or the War Office to be forgotten, except for his eyes, which were pale as skimmed milk and entirely awake.

"You've been at Brandhoek how long?"

"Eleven months, sir."

"Before that, Étaples. Before that, training at — " he glanced at a card on his desk, though Freya would have bet her last shilling he'd memorised it days ago — "the London hospital where you trained. Good record. Excellent record, in fact. Matron Cobb speaks highly of you."

"Matron is kind."

"Matron Cobb," Hargreaves said, "is many things. Kind is not the word I'd have chosen first." He smiled to show this was humour. It did not reach the milk-pale eyes. "You'll forgive me if I'm direct. My time here is short, and I suspect yours is precious too."

He opened a file. Freya kept her face still, her hands folded in her lap, and thought: *this is what it is to be interrogated, then.* Not the lamp and the threats of the films. Just sherry and soft questions and a man reading your life back to you in the flat voice of an accountant.

"Last month there was an inspection here," he said. "Casualty figures. Admissions, evacuations, deaths. There has been — " a small pause, delicate as forceps — "a discrepancy. Nothing large. A few figures that don't reconcile between station returns and corps-level summaries. Names, in some cases, that appear in one place and not another."

"How strange," Freya said.

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "Isn't it." He turned a page without looking at it. "The thing about arithmetic, Sister, is that it is a moral science. Numbers are either true or they are false, and when they are false, someone has made them so. Either out of carelessness — which is forgivable, in wartime — or out of intention. Which is not."

"And which do you suspect, sir?"

"I?" He set down the file. "I suspect clerical error. I hope for clerical error. I pray nightly for clerical error, Sister, because the alternative is that someone in this theatre has been keeping records that contradict the official ones, and if such a person existed, they would not merely be mistaken. They would be dangerous. To the army. To morale. To the families at home, who receive news through proper channels precisely so that grief can be managed rather than — " he searched for the word, found it — "*weaponised* against them."

Freya looked at her sherry. Amber, trembling faintly with her pulse. She willed the tremor still.

"You asked to see me, sir," she said. "I doubt it was to discuss arithmetic."

"No." He leaned back. Outside, somewhere beyond the sandbags, a battery opened up — distant, rhythmic, like a heart heard through a wall. Neither of them turned toward the sound. Men in this country had learned to hear guns the way fish heard water. "I asked to see you because I have here a recommendation. A transfer. Home, Sister. England. A base hospital in Hampshire, if you like Hampshire — comfortable, modern, a train line to London. And attached to it, a commendation. Mentioned in dispatches. For your service at Brandhoek during the heavy weeks of October." He recited it lightly, a man offering a box of chocolates. "There are sisters out here who'd cut off a finger for that paragraph in the Gazette. Some of them have done considerably worse things for less."

"Why me?"

"Because you're good. Because you've earned it. Because — " and here the pleasantness dropped away, all at once, like a cloth pulled off a cage — "because sometimes the wisest thing an intelligent person can do is recognise a door opening before it becomes a window closing."

The room went very quiet. Even the guns seemed to hold their breath.

"I don't understand, sir."

"Don't you?" He studied her with something that was almost gentleness, and that gentleness frightened her more than anger would have. "Sister Ashgrove. I inspected this station three weeks ago. You showed me a coffin inventory with commendable efficiency. Your columns were immaculate. Your totals reconciled beautifully. Do you know what struck me most about that inventory?"

"The numbers, sir?"

"The *handwriting*," he said. "A hand trained for ward reports doesn't usually produce such fine copperplate. Yours is beautiful. Distinctive. The sort of hand a man remembers." He lifted his glass but didn't drink. "And a week later, reviewing returns from the sector, I find a casualty summary — unofficial margins, pencilled annotations — in a hand I'd swear I'd seen before, holding a coffin list. Of course, pencil fades. Pencil can be erased. Pencil can appear and disappear depending on what a man wishes he had seen."

The fire in the grate ticked. Freya became aware of her own breathing and forced it slow, shallow, bored. Somewhere beneath the fear, cold and clear as well water, a single thought surfaced: *he doesn't have it. He suspects, but he doesn't have it.*

"If someone were keeping private records, sir," she said, "of men the dispatches missed — wouldn't that be less sedition than charity? Remembering the dead isn't treason."

"Remembering the dead is not," Hargreaves agreed. "Publishing them is. Circulating them is. Handing ammunition to every pacifist, every German propagandist, every grieving mother with a grievance and a newspaper — that is not charity, Sister, that is *war against your own side*, conducted in the only quarter where we cannot afford to lose: belief. You may think a name in a ledger is a small light. Light travels. That is the whole trouble with it."

He knew. Or he believed he knew, which amounted to nearly the same thing, and yet he sat here pouring sherry instead of sending for the military police. Freya turned the geometry of it over and felt the shape of the trap assemble itself around her: the transfer, the commendation, the door held open with one hand while the other rested on the window frame. A test dressed as a gift. If she took Hampshire, she went home silent, bought and grateful, and whatever notes existed in whoever's margins would die of neglect. If she refused too hotly, too knowingly, she confirmed the milk-pale eyes' suspicion and gave him the thread to pull until the whole garment unravelled — the hymnal, the workshop, Elias, Céline, Tam, Iris, everyone.

Unless she refused the way a simple nurse would refuse. Confused. Slightly wounded. Loyal to nothing but her patients and her matron.

"Sir," she said slowly, "may I speak plainly?"

"I insist upon it."

"I think you've been told something about me that isn't true, or is true of someone else. I keep my ward reports. I write letters home for men whose hands won't write. I do it in copperplate because my grandmother beat penmanship into me with a ruler, God rest her. If my handwriting has turned up somewhere it shouldn't, then either someone is imitating it or someone is looking very hard for guilt and finding handwriting instead." She met the pale eyes. "As for Hampshire — I'm honoured, truly. But there are forty beds in that ward and half of them can't hold a spoon, and Sister Quill is alone over Christmas if I go. Mentioned in dispatches is a fine thing. So is sleeping at night."

Silence. The guns resumed, patient as tide.

Hargreaves set down his untouched glass. When he spoke again, the pleasantness was gone entirely, and what remained was worse: professional regret, the tone of a coroner.

"Do you know what happens to a discrepancy, Sister, once it stops being a discrepancy and becomes a pattern? It acquires investigators. Investigators acquire authority. Authority acquires warrants — searches, seizures, arrests. And arrests are indiscriminate instruments. They take the guilty and the adjacent. Orderlies. Matrons. Sisters who happen to share a billet with persons of interest." He let that sit. "I am offering you a way for none of that to be necessary. Doors, Sister. I am offering doors."

"And windows," Freya said quietly, "if I don't walk through."

"I admire precision." He almost smiled. "Yes. Windows. Though I'd ask you to consider — before you romanticise defenestration — that people who fall from them rarely fall alone."

She stood. He did not stop her. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and heard herself say the thing she had not planned to say, the true thing, the coal that had been burning under her ribs since a dying private had gripped her wrist and begged her to remember him:

"My brother died at Loos, sir. Officially. The dispatch said *killed in action, instantaneously, painless*. Three years later a man who'd lain beside him wrote to say it had taken nine hours and that no one came, because the stretcher-bearers had been told the position was already taken. Someone wrote *instantaneous* so that a mother could sleep. I understand why. I've written gentle lies myself, for dying men, a hundred times." She turned. "But I was taught that a lie told kindly is still owed a debt. And debts come due. If someone out here is keeping honest accounts, sir — then I hope, for all our sakes, they're never found. But I won't pretend to hope they never existed."

She left before he could answer. In the corridor her knees behaved until the third doorway, and then she stopped beside a noticeboard of sanitary regulations and pressed her forehead against the cool wood and breathed until the shaking stopped.

* * *

She told no one. That was the discipline of it now — the new, corrosive discipline of a life split into compartments. There was the ward-self, who changed dressings and laughed at Tam's terrible jokes. There was the ledger-self, who lived in a hymnal between "Abide With Me" and "Just As I Am." And there was this newest self, born an hour ago in a colonel's office: the self who lied fluently to power and discovered, with horror and a strange exhilaration, that she was good at it.

But the found family had eyes. Matron Cobb intercepted her at the dispensary that evening, not blocking the door — Matron never blocked anything, she simply occupied thresholds until you understood them — and looked at Freya over her spectacles for a long moment.

"Well?" Cobb said.

"Well what, Matron?"

"You've the look of a woman who's been handed something expensive and put it in her pocket without counting the change." Cobb weighed a bottle of carbolic in her hand, set it back. "Headquarters sent for you. The whole station knows. The whole station also knows that Staff officers don't send for ward sisters to discuss dressings."

"He offered me a transfer home. Commendation. I declined."

Something flickered behind Matron's eyes — not surprise; Freya realised with a jolt that surprise would have been easier. This was confirmation. Of what?

"Hm," said Cobb. "And did he tell you what declining costs, these days?"

"He dressed it up as concern for orderlies and matrons."

"Naturally. That's how they price things now — not in your blood, in ours." She took up the carbolic again, businesslike, dismissal in every line of her — and then paused, and said, without turning, in a voice stripped of everything but iron: "Sister Ashgrove. When the bill comes due — and bills always come due — you will not pay it out of my nurses. Whatever you're carrying, whatever you've decided, you will remember that there are girls in this station who joined up to save lives, not to spend theirs buying yours time. Am I understood?"

"Perfectly, Matron."

"Good." And Cobb swept off down the corridor, skirts hissing like sand through an hourglass, leaving Freya to stand in the carbolic-smelling dark and wonder — not for the first time — whether the warning had been a threat or a shield.

* * *

Elias Wren came at midnight, as he always came, slipping through the gap in the latrine fence where the wire had been cut and re-rigged to look intact. She met him by the wrecked water tower, breath smoking, and told him all of it in a low rapid voice — the summons, the sherry, the coffin inventory, the handwriting, the offer, the refusal, Matron's words.

He listened without moving. That was the unnerving thing about Elias: he received catastrophe the way stone received rain.

"Hargreaves," he said at last. "It's worse than Céline thought, then. He's not fishing for a leak in the figures. He's fishing for *you*."

"For the ledger."

"For whoever holds it. Same thing, from where he stands." Elias pulled his collar up against the wind. "He can't move openly yet — he needs proof, and proof means seizure, and seizure means explaining to his own superiors how an unofficial account of the advance survived a year under his nose. So he offers doors. He'll offer again, and softer, and then harder. And when soft and hard both fail — "

"He raids."

"He raids." Elias was quiet a moment. In the distance a flare went up over the salient, hung white and trembling, and died. "There's a man you need to meet," he said finally. "The one who writes those letters you know so well. He's been asking for you — not your lists, not your verification. *You*. He says if Hargreaves has begun making offers, there's no more time for caution, and he'd sooner trust a woman who refuses a commendation than any man who's never been tested." A ghost of a smile. "He's not an easy meeting, Sister. He's not the man from the letters anymore. But he's asked, and he doesn't ask."

Freya looked north, toward the drowned country where Passchendaele lay under its crust of frozen mud, where a dead captain waited in a flooded farmhouse with the truth folded up inside his ruined body like a letter in a burned envelope.

"When?" she asked.

"Thursday night," said Elias. "Wear nothing you can't run in."

That night she did not sleep. She lay in her cot listening to the wind worry the tent ropes, and beneath her pillow, wrapped in its oilcloth, the hymnal lay with all its small lights banked inside it — forty-one names, and more, and her own name waiting at the end of the page like a signature on a warrant. She thought of Hampshire, of green fields and trains to London, of a life with no windows to fall from. Then she thought of Private Ellis, who had died begging to be remembered, and of a mother in Cardiff who would read *instantaneous* and believe it, and she got up in the dark, took out the ledger, and by the light of a shaded candle added one more entry:

*Offered everything to stay silent. Refused. Let it be written that I was asked, and what I answered.*

Outside, far off toward Rue Basse, a dog began to bark — and would not stop.
