## 17. Vane, Alive and Ruined

Elias came for her on a Thursday, in the hour between the last light going out of the sky and the lamps being lit, when the ward was quiet enough that a nurse might slip away and be missed only by the dead.

He stood at the edge of the vegetable garden behind the dressing station, hands in the pockets of a coat that had been good once, in another country, another war. He did not smoke. He never smoked, which she had come to understand was not virtue but vigilance — a man who could not afford the flare of a match.

"You're sure," he said. It was not a question so much as a door held open, one last time.

"No," Freya said. "But I'm coming."

He almost smiled. "That's the first honest answer anyone's given me all year."

They went out through the gap in the hedge where Tam kept his smuggled bicycle hidden, past the paddock where two mules dozed standing up, and onto the pitted lane that ran east toward the flooded lowlands beyond Vlamertinghe. Elias had borrowed — or acquired; with Elias it was never entirely clear which — an ambulance cart, its red crosses freshly painted over a layer of older paint that showed through like scars. They were, officially, transporting medical stores to an advance post. Unofficially, they were carrying nothing but a nurse, a ledger sewn into the lining of her greatcoat, and whatever was left of Captain Aldous Vane.

The road deteriorated as they went. Duckboards gave way to mud, mud to water lying flat and pewter-colored under a sky the color of old linen. The shelling had stopped here months ago, not from mercy but because there was nothing left to shell. The land had been murdered past the point of interest. Willows stood stripped and white as bone. A farmhouse roofline appeared and disappeared in the mist, then reappeared closer, and Freya saw that it was not one farmhouse but the skeleton of one, half-sunk, its upper story tilted like a ship settling.

"Here?" she said.

"He chose it," Elias said. "Nobody looks for a living man in drowned ground."

* * *

She had built him, over the year, out of paper.

It was a thing she knew she'd done and could not stop doing. The Aldous Vane of her letters — the training years, before Passchendaele, when he had written to her weekly with the patient, wry voice of a man describing war to someone he refused to frighten — had become, in her mind's dark hours, something more than a captain of infantry. He had become a figure. The man whose handwriting matched letters carried by a stranger who would not say how he got them. The man who had seen the order given and the reports rewritten and had chosen, impossibly, to live long enough to prove it. In the ledger's margins, in the spaces between names, she had drawn him tall and clear-eyed, unbowed, a lantern of a man.

Elias helped her down from the cart at the lip of what had been the farmyard. Water came halfway up their boots immediately. He led her along a submerged plank path she could feel rather than see, toward the dark doorway where the house still stood.

"He'll be difficult," Elias said quietly. "I want you prepared for that."

"I've been prepared for difficult since 1914."

"This is different." He stopped at the threshold. Inside, she could smell woodsmoke and stagnant water and something else — the particular sourness of a room inhabited too long by one sick man. "Everyone wants him to be what the dispatches said he wasn't. Brave, or dead, or both. He isn't either. Go in knowing that."

* * *

The interior had been one large room once; now it was three, divided by fallen beams and hanging plaster, open to the sky in places where the roof had gone. Rain dripped somewhere with the regularity of a clock. A fire burned in a hearth choked with rubble, small and carefully fed, and beside it, in a chair made from a packing crate and a blanket, sat a man reading by candlelight.

He did not look up when they entered. "You brought her, then," he said. His voice was dry, unhurried, sanded down. "Despite my instructions."

"Your instructions were eleven months old," Elias said.

"Eleven months is plenty of time for a man to change his mind." And then Aldous Vane raised his head, and Freya's constructed hero died quietly in the space of a single breath, and she was glad of it, though she didn't yet know why.

He was thin — thinner than illness alone makes a man, thin the way men get when eating has become a chore performed to keep the body running while the mind does other work. His hair had gone grey at the temples though she knew he was not yet forty. But it was his eyes. The left eye was clouded, milk-pale, turned slightly inward, blind or nearly so. The right was sharp enough for both, and it fixed on her with an intensity that felt less like recognition than appraisal — the look of a man checking goods against an invoice.

"Nurse Ashgrove," he said. "The one who keeps the book."

"The Lantern," she said. "Yes."

"Don't call it that in front of me." He closed the book on his knee — she saw it was a farmer's account ledger, columns of figures in faded ink — and gestured with two fingers at the room around them. "Look where names have gotten me. Half-blind in a bog, living on tinned beef and rainwater, waiting for the day someone follows the wrong man home. Call it a list. Call it evidence. Don't make it a lantern. Lanterns are for churches and lighthouses and other buildings that don't burn down."

Freya stood very still, letting the disappointment move through her and noting, with a kind of clinical interest, that beneath the disappointment was something else. Not pity. Something harder and more useful.

"You wrote me letters," she said. "For two years. You described the mud at Armentières so precisely I could taste it. You told me about your sergeant's dog. You told me—" she stopped, because the memory arrived unbidden: *if anything in this war is worth keeping, it will be kept by people like you, not people like me.* "You told me you believed records mattered."

"I did." He said it without flinching. "Then I watched a brigade walk into machine-gun fire on a morning so foggy the men couldn't see the wire, on orders everyone on the staff knew were madness, and I watched forty-one of them die in nine minutes, and I watched the same afternoon a colonel rewrite the action into a victory with 'regrettable losses,' and I watched those losses entered as desertion, because deserters don't get pensions and don't get inquiries and don't get mothers writing letters asking what happened to their boys." He leaned forward, and the firelight moved across the ruined eye. "Records matter, Nurse. That's exactly why they lie with them. Now sit down. Your feet are in the water and you'll lose the toes."

There was a second crate. She sat. Elias remained standing by the doorway, arms folded, watching both of them with the expression of a man who has heard this argument before and knows neither combatant will win it.

"You've read what I sent," Vane said.

"Some of it. Elias rationed you like sugar."

"Elias is sensible." The good eye flicked to the younger man. "Tell her."

"She wouldn't be here if she listened to me," Elias said.

Vane made a sound that might, in a healthier throat, have been a laugh. Then he reached into the blanket across his knees and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it with fingers that trembled until they touched paper, and stopped trembling altogether.

"Read this," he said.

* * *

It was a copy of the brigade order for the eleventh of October, 1917. Not the version printed in the war diary — she recognized the official phrasing, the careful geography, the objective given as "the ridge line" as if the ridge were a place a man could stand — but a draft. Penciled. Annotated. Dated four hours *before* the attack, with marginal notes in a hand she now knew intimately: *ground observed from OP at dawn — full field of fire, no cover, no smoke plan. Protested to Bde Major. Overruled. "Timing set by Corps." Nine minutes, gentlemen. We are sending them across a shooting gallery and calling it a flank movement.*

Below that, in different ink, added later: casualty returns, original and revised. The original listed the missing by company, by name. The revised — the one that went up the chain — listed thirty-eight men as "stragglers, disciplinary action pending," and three as killed. Forty-one men, erased into criminals.

Freya read it twice. Her hands were steady. Later she would shake; later, in the cart on the road home, she would press her palms flat against her knees until they ached. But in that ruined room, with the rain ticking through the broken roof and the blind man watching her with his one working eye, she was perfectly calm, the way she was calm during a hemorrhage, when the blood is everywhere and the only thing in the world is the next correct action.

"My name is on the protest," Vane said. "Which means I'm either a traitor or a witness, depending on who wins the argument. The army has already decided I'm a traitor. They've decided it so thoroughly they killed me off in the dispatches a year ago — very tidy. Gave my mother a lovely telegram." His voice cracked on the word *mother*, just barely, and he covered it by turning the page. "So. You cross-checked my list against yours. Elias says you found matches."

"Forty-one," Freya said. "Every name. Every one of them came through my station or a station like mine, and every one of them was recorded dead in my ledger and recorded deserter in theirs." She paused. "Private Daniel Osei, West Ridings. He asked me to write to his sister in Cardiff. I wrote. She answered. She's still answering, Mr. Vane. She thinks her brother ran."

Something moved through his face then — not softening, exactly, but the momentary collapse of a wall. He looked down at the oilcloth packet as if it had grown heavy.

"That's why it has to be exact," he said, quieter. "Every date. Every company. Every spelling. If we publish a pamphlet with one error in it, they'll use that error to bury the other forty thousand words. I'm half-blind and I misread my own notes some nights. I need eyes that have already been keeping the record." He looked up. "Yours."

"And what happens to the eyes afterward?" Freya said. "Because I've noticed, Captain, that everything connected to this gets burned or buried or shot. You. The printing. The couriers. What happens to the nurse?"

"Probably nothing good," he said. "I won't lie to you. Lying to nurses is how the army got into this."

"Charming."

"It's honest, which is rarer." He set the papers down on the crate between them, deliberately, the way a man lays a card face-up. "Here is what I can offer. Work that matters. Evidence that will outlive every officer currently drawing a general's pay. And the certainty that when this war ends — however it ends — there will exist somewhere a true accounting of what was done to those forty-one men, in their own names, spelled correctly." He spread his ruined hands. "That's the entire inventory. No glory. No safety. No happy ending guaranteed or even likely. Most days I'd settle for surviving to see the type set."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, water shifted against the walls of the drowned barn, a slow breathing sound.

Freya looked at him — really looked, past the myth she had built and past the wreckage sitting in front of her — and understood, finally, what Elias had tried to tell her at the door. There was no hero here. There was a man who had been dismantled, beam by beam, the way this farmhouse had been dismantled, and who had crawled into his own ruins and kept working anyway. It was not inspiring. It was terrible. And it was, she realized, the most trustworthy thing she had ever seen, because a man this broken had nothing left to perform.

The myth shrinks, she thought. The mission grows to fill the space.

"All right," she said.

Vane blinked. "All right, what?"

"All right, I'll verify your lists. All right, I'll take the pages to the workshop on Rue Basse. All right, I'll spell Osei correctly, and the other forty, and the hundreds more in my ledger that aren't on your list yet." She stood, water draining from her skirts back into the farmyard muck. "But I have conditions."

"Conditions." He said it the way Matron Cobb said *insubordination.*

"One. When it's finished — truly finished, printed, distributed, undeniable — my name goes in it. As compiler. As witness. Whatever you like. But the women who kept these men alive long enough to die properly named deserve to be counted too."

"Granted."

"Two. You stop deciding things alone. Eleven-month-old instructions, Elias said. You nearly got yourself starved to death in a bog out of pride. From tonight, this is a network, not a hermitage. If you fall, the work doesn't fall with you."

His good eye narrowed, and for a moment she saw the officer he must have been — the one sergeants obeyed and majors resented. Then it passed, and what remained was tiredness, and beneath the tiredness, faintly, something that looked almost like relief.

"Granted," he said again. "God help me. Two insubordinates in one room."

"Three," Freya said, and now her voice did waver, just once, because she had saved this for last and it cost more than the others. "You write to your mother. Or let Elias do it. Tell her you're alive. Not where — not how — just that. She thinks her son is dead, Captain. I know what that does to a family. I carry forty-one of them in a book."

For a long moment Aldous Vane did not answer. The rain ticked. The fire settled. And then he bowed his head over the oilcloth packet, over the penciled protest with his name on it, and his shoulders shook — once, twice — and when he spoke, his voice had gone rough as gravel.

"She stopped writing in March," he said. "I told myself it meant she'd accepted it. It's easier to be dead than to explain what I am." He looked up, and the clouded eye and the clear eye both faced her, and he nodded slowly. "Third condition granted. Now go, before the road dries. And Nurse—"

She stopped at the doorway, Elias already a shadow ahead of her in the mist.

"When you set the type for Osei," Vane said, "spell it with an S at the end. Welsh spelling. His sister will be looking for it."

* * *

They did not speak for the first mile. The cart's wheels sucked and slapped through the floodwater, and the mist came down thicker, folding the ruined farmhouse away behind them until Freya could almost believe she had dreamed it.

It was Elias who finally broke the silence, without taking his eyes off the road.

"Well?"

"He's not what I imagined," Freya said.

"No one ever is."

"No." She turned the packet over in her lap, feeling the weight of the penciled truth inside it, the weight of a name spelled with an S at the end. "He's worse than I imagined. And the work is better." She looked up at the grey sky, at the road ahead running dark toward Ypres and the ward and Iris and Matron and all the warm, ordered world she was now walking away from one page at a time. "Elias — how soon can we start setting type?"

Elias glanced sideways at her, and in the failing light his expression was hard to read, though his voice was not.

"Sooner than you think," he said. "Céline's already cast the first line. She's waiting on one thing." He paused, and the cart jolted through a hole in the road. "She's waiting to know the nurse is real. By tomorrow night, Freya, you're going to meet the woman printing your ledger — and pray God the colonel's spies haven't met her first."
