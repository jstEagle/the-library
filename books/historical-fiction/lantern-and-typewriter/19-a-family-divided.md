## 19. A Family Divided

The storm came inland off the channel that evening, and with it came Iris.

Freya heard her before she saw her — the particular stride, boots striking the duckboards of the ward corridor like a metronome wound too tight. In eighteen months of shared night shifts and stolen cigarettes behind the laundry line, Freya had catalogued Iris Marchetti's footsteps the way a sailor catalogues weather. The light step meant good news. The dragging step meant a bad loss in theatre. This step — this hard, deliberate percussion — meant something had been decided.

She was folding bandages at the supply trolley when Iris filled the doorway of the linen store, and she knew, before a word was spoken, that the hour she had dreaded since the first name went into the Lantern had arrived.

"Shut the door," Iris said.

"Iris—"

"Shut the *door*, Freya."

Freya shut it. The store room was narrow, lit by one shielded lamp, walls of shelving stacked with rolled linen that breathed the smell of carbolic and cold starch. There was no window. There was no witness. Iris had chosen the ground carefully, and that alone told Freya how long this had been building.

Iris held out her hand. Flat. Open. A gesture she might have used asking for scissors.

"Give it to me."

"Give you what?"

"Don't." Iris's voice cracked on the word and she steadied it, jaw working. "Don't do that. Don't give me the ward voice. I've watched you lie to Matron about your kit. I've watched you slip out past curfew three nights running last week — Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday — and I've watched you come back with ink on your fingers and mud on your hem that doesn't match anything within two miles of this station. I've watched you for weeks, Freya. Weeks. And every time I told myself there was an explanation, because you're my friend and friends don't invent explanations for each other." She drew a breath. "So give me what's in the hymnal."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere beyond the door, a stretcher party passed, and both women stood rigid until the sound faded.

"The hymnal is a hymnal," Freya said quietly.

"Then open it. Here. Now." Iris's dark eyes were wet, and that was the worst part — not anger, never quite anger, but the fury of someone who has loved a thing and watched it turn strange. "If it's just psalms, I'll stand here while you read all hundred and fifty of them and I'll apologise on my knees. Open it."

Freya did not move. She thought, absurdly, of the first week of training at Netley, when Iris had held her hair back over a basin after a bad anatomy practical, and said, *you and me against the whole bloody profession*. She thought of Matron Cobb's voice: *there are two kinds of nurses.* She thought of forty-one names, forty-one addresses, Céline's bleeding fingers on the type case, and Elias's face in the lamplight saying *you are already in it, Sister, whether you chose to be or no.*

"I can't," she said.

"Why?" Iris's voice rose and she strangled it down to a whisper. "Why can't you? That's what I keep coming back to. Whatever it is — letters, a man, some bloody scheme of Wren's — why can't you tell *me*?"

"Because telling you makes you guilty."

"I'm already guilty!" The whisper broke loose entirely. "You think ignorance is armour? You think if they come through that gate asking questions, they'll look at my clean hands and say, well, nothing to see here? We share a billet, Frey. We share a *kit shelf*. If you go down, I go down beside you, and the only difference between us will be that you knew what for and I didn't."

*That isn't true,* Freya wanted to say. *They'll hang the keeper and question the bystander.* But she had learned enough these past months to know the army did not distinguish so finely. Guilt travelled sideways like gas.

"Then walk away from me," Freya said. "Ask for a transfer. You've got the marks for the surgical team, you'd get it inside a fortnight—"

Iris stared at her as though she'd slapped her.

"A transfer."

"It would protect you."

"It would—" Iris laughed, a short, terrible sound with nothing in it. "It would protect *me*. God. Do you hear yourself? You're standing in a linen cupboard offering me a lifeboat and won't even show me the water." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment, and when she lowered them she had rebuilt herself, brick by brick, into the nurse everyone trusted — steady Iris, cheerful Iris, who sang to the shell-shocked boys at three in the morning. Only her hands gave her away, knotted together white at the knuckles.

"I know what you're doing," she said. "Not the details. I don't need the details. I know it's about names and papers and that man Vane, because Wren talks in his sleep and Tam talks when he's nervous and you talk in your handwriting, which is worse than either." She stepped closer. "And I know that whatever it is, it involves moving things out of this station under people's coats, and I know that Colonel Hessler's staff came sniffing round the casualty ledgers twice this month, and I know — *Freya, listen to me* — I know what happens to a dressing station when headquarters decides it's dirty."

Freya held still. "Say it plainly."

"They'll close us." Iris's voice dropped low and level now, which was more frightening than the shouting. "They'll post half of us east without notice and send the other half under escort for questioning. They'll do it at dawn, mid-convoy, with patients on our tables — and those patients, Frey, those boys who can't walk to a latrine without two orderlies, will be loaded onto lorries and shipped to a casualty clearing station that's already overflowing, because some staff colonel decided our beds were needed for men whose paperwork is in better order. You've seen the CCS at Brandhoek in a bad week. You've *seen* it."

"I have."

"And you still—"

"I still." Freya heard her own voice come out flat and strange, and realised with a distant surprise that she was angry too — had been angry for months, banked down under discipline, and Iris had finally struck the flint. "Yes, Iris. I know exactly what happens at Brandhoek. I sat up all night with a boy from the Royal Fusiliers whose chart said *gunshot wound, thigh* and whose leg came off at mid-thigh because the clearing station had run out of the proper splints. Paperwork in perfect order. He died at dawn with a form signed in triplicate. So don't you dare tell me the system protects our patients. The system wrote Private Daniel Okafor out of existence six days after I closed his eyes, and when his mother writes asking where her son is buried, some clerk will tell her *no such soldier appears upon our rolls*, and she will spend the rest of her life thinking he ran."

The words hung in the carbolic air. Iris flinched as if each name were a thrown stone.

"That isn't what I'm saying," she said, softer. "God knows I'm not defending their ledgers. I've copied enough of them to want to burn the lot. I'm talking about *here*. About Tam, who'd follow you into a fire because you once fixed his bootlace. About Matron, who's sixty years old and should have gone home to her sister in Whitby a year ago. About the thirty-one boys on our wards tonight. Whatever you think you're setting right, the bill doesn't come to you alone. It comes to all of us. It comes to people who never got a vote."

"You have a vote now," Freya said bitterly. "Half of us do. That's more than the dead ever got."

"Oh, that's fine." Iris threw up her hands. "That's very fine. Quoting suffrage at me in a linen store. Next you'll be quoting scripture, except you've cut the pages out, haven't you? What's left of the Psalms when you've hollowed them for your little—"

She stopped herself. Too late. The words were out, and Freya felt them land somewhere beneath the ribs, precisely where Iris had aimed them.

For a long moment neither spoke. The lamp guttered; Freya trimmed the wick with fingers that did not tremble, and took a small, cold pride in that.

"You went through my kit," she said at last. Quietly. "While I slept."

Iris's colour flared and died. "I tidied it. You'd left it open—"

"You went through my kit looking for it, and you didn't find it, because it wasn't there, and instead of asking me — *asking me*, Iris, one question over tea, we've shared a billet for fourteen months — you spent three weeks watching me like I'm a spy. Like I'm one of *them*."

"What was I supposed to think?" Iris's composure was going, going. "What was I supposed to think, seeing you creep out at midnight to God-knows-where with a man nobody's ever heard of, carrying things sewn into — into *uniforms*, Tam let that much slip before I stopped him—"

The floor tilted, very slightly, beneath Freya's feet.

"Sewn into what?" she asked. Her voice sounded calm. Some detached observer inside her noted the calm and admired it.

Iris heard it too. She heard it, and understood, one beat too late, exactly what she had done. Freya watched it move across her face like cloud-shadow — the dawning horror, the grasping for retreat.

"Nothing," Iris said. "Tam doesn't know anything. He was frightened, he was babbling, I didn't—"

"*Sewn into what*, Iris."

"I don't know! Uniforms — hems — I don't know, I stopped listening, I made him swear—" She pressed her fist to her mouth. "Frey. *Frey.* I never meant—"

But the arithmetic had already finished itself in Freya's head, swift and merciless, the way triage arithmetic finishes itself: Tam carried. Tam talked. Tam talked to Iris, who was loyal to the army down to the marrow — and Iris, whatever else she was, whatever she chose in the next ten minutes, had now *heard*. Which meant the secret lived in a third head, a fourth, spreading outward like blood through a dressing, and every additional heart that held it was another point of failure, another mouth the colonel's provosts could pry open with fear or promise.

Fourteen months of friendship, and in four minutes of honesty they had managed to endanger everything.

"This is why," Freya said. Her voice was almost gentle now, which she knew would wound Iris worse than shouting. "This exact thing. Not because I don't trust you. Because secrets are like wounds — every pair of hands that lifts the dressing lets in the air. I kept it from you to keep you safe, and you found it anyway, and now you're not safe, and neither am I, and neither is a nineteen-year-old orderly who volunteers for errands because he wants to matter."

"Then stop." Iris caught her sleeve, urgent, pleading. "Stop now, before it goes further. Hand it over — burn it, bury it, I don't care, I'll help you, I'll stand watch — and we go back to being what we were. Sisters. You and me against the profession. Remember?"

The old words, offered back like a coin. And Freya felt, genuinely, terribly, how much she wanted to take them. To set down the weight. To be only a nurse again, tired and useful and held inside the warm animal huddle of her found family, with no ledger hidden in any book and no widow waiting by a press on the Rue Basse.

But behind Iris's shoulder, in the dark of the shelving, she could see them all: *Ashgrove F., remembered.* Private Cobb, erased in '15. Forty-one families waiting for a truth that existed nowhere but in her handwriting and Céline's type. And a press that had already begun to speak.

"If I stop," Freya said, "they stay dead twice."

"Frey—"

"They stay dead twice, Iris. Once on the field and once in the record. And the men who ordered it go on writing reports, and promoting each other, and sending the next thousand over the same ground, because no one — *no one* — wrote down what actually happened. Someone has to write it down. That's all I am. A woman writing it down." She freed her sleeve, gently. "You can report me. I mean that. If you believe it's right — truly right, not just frightened-right — then go to Matron, or go further, and I won't hate you for it. That's the promise I'll make you. Whatever you choose, I won't hate you."

Iris looked at her for a long time. The lamp-light moved on her face and showed Freya every year of the war they'd aged together, and something else besides — the moment of decision turning over behind her eyes like a coin that hadn't yet landed.

"And if I don't report you?" Iris said hoarsely. "If I keep my mouth shut and my head down and pretend I never opened your kit? What am I then?"

Freya thought of Céline Dossin, typesetting her son's name among the saved. Thought of Matron's two kinds of nurses. Thought of the answer that cost the most, and gave it anyway, because Iris deserved nothing cheaper.

"Then you're one of us," she said. "And you carry the same risk. And I will never ask you to forgive me for that."

Iris laughed again — broken, half-sob. "You sanctimonious— you absolute—" She wiped her face roughly with the heel of her hand, smearing carbolic across her cheek. "I hate you. Tonight I hate you completely."

"I know."

"I'm taking the late ward," Iris said. "Don't follow me. Don't talk to me. Don't *look* at me till I've slept on whatever this is." She wrenched the door open, paused on the threshold with her back to Freya, shoulders shaking once, twice, and said, without turning: "Daniel Okafor. Write his mother properly, whoever she is. Promise me that much, at least."

"She's first in the next printing," Freya said. "I set her line myself."

Iris nodded, a small jerking motion, and was gone. Her footsteps went down the corridor — fast, uneven, nothing like her catalogue of steps — and the store-room door swung slowly shut behind her.

* * *

Freya did not sleep. At two in the morning she stood at the ward's high window, watching rain come off the channel in grey curtains, and made her cold inventory of damage. Iris knew of the smuggling, if not its shape. Tam had talked once under fear and might talk again. The colonel had requested paper-stock records from Ypres town. Every thread pulled tighter, and here she stood holding the spindle.

Elias would have to know. All of it — tonight, before the knowledge hardened into something that could be used. She was reaching for her cape when the curtain at the far end of the ward twitched aside and Tam appeared, torch cupped, rain streaming off his oilskin, and she saw at once by the way he stood — too straight, too controlled, the stillness of a boy holding himself together with both hands — that the damage had outrun her inventory entirely.

"Sister," he whispered. "You'd best come. They've taken the Rue Basse road, and there's lorries on it. Four of them. No lights."
