## 20. The First Page Printed

The press had a voice, and Céline Dossin had learned it the way one learns the voice of a sick child — by listening through the wall at night, by knowing which cry meant hunger and which meant something worse.

Tonight the Heidelberg spoke to her in its low iron monotone, the flywheel turning with the patience of a thing that had outlived two owners and a war. *Thump. Whisper. Thump.* The platen kissed the forme of type, and the paper took the ink, and somewhere in that small marriage of pressure and grease a sentence became permanent. Céline stood at the feed board with her sleeves rolled above her elbows and her forearms black to the wrist, and she fed the machine sheet after sheet as though she were spooning medicine into a mouth that would never stop asking.

Behind her, the workshop on Rue Basse smelled of kerosene, damp brick, and the particular sweetness of old lead. The gas ring under the glue pot hissed. Rain came off the canal in gusts and ticked against the high windows like fingers testing whether anyone was home.

No one was supposed to be home. That was the point of the hour. Printing was done between midnight and four because the military police kept office hours, because the neighbors on Rue Basse were either asleep or paid to be deaf, and because Céline had decided long ago — back when the first galleys were still a fantasy in Elias Wren's careful hands — that if they ever did come for her, she wanted them to catch her mid-work. Let them see what it cost. Let them carry away the image of an old woman with ink to her elbows rather than the image of an old woman hiding.

"Sheet's crooked," said Freya Ashgrove from the delivery tray.

Céline did not turn around. "Then take it out and burn it."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You said it."

There was a rustle, the sound of paper being drawn free, and then a pause that Céline could hear as clearly as speech. She knew that pause. It was the pause of someone reading the same line twice.

She let the girl have her moment. There was time enough for everything else; there always was, until suddenly there wasn't. She pulled another sheet from the stack — good stock, smuggled from a paper mill outside Tournai by a cousin who owed her more than money — squared it against the guides, and fed it home. *Thump. Whisper.*

They had been at this forme since Tuesday. Eight pages, set by hand, locked into the chase with furniture and quoins that Céline had tightened so many times her thumbs had gone soft-skinned and sore. The type itself was a mongrel — body sizes that didn't match, bought piecemeal over three years from bankrupt printers in Ghent and Brussels, some sorts worn nearly round at the shoulders so that every *e* looked tired. Her late husband, Auguste, would have wept at the case. Auguste had believed a compositor's duty began with clean sorts and even spacing, that disorder in the type was disorder in the soul.

Auguste had been dead eleven years now. Their son, Luc, dead fourteen months, though no document in Belgium or Flanders said so. In the documents, Private Luc Dossin had deserted his regiment on the eighteenth of October, 1916, and been struck from the rolls, and that was the end of him. No grave number. No letter. A clerk's pen stroke where a boy used to be.

Céline fed another sheet. *Thump. Whisper.* On this one, if the impression held true, was printed the account of the advance ordered across open ground against positions the staff had known were held — known, because Vane had written it down before he wrote it down nowhere else — and beneath it, the first nine names of men recorded as deserters who had in fact died carrying that order into machine-gun fire. Nine names. Forty-one in all, once the pamphlet was done. She knew all forty-one the way she knew the stations of the cross. Luc was the seventh.

*Take and eat,* she thought, pulling the fresh sheet and laying it face-up on the drying racks with the tenderness she might have given bread. *This is my son's body, which the clerks refused.*

She was not a religious woman anymore. Religion required believing that someone was keeping count.

* * *

At half past two, Elias Wren came up from the cellar where he'd been oiling the treadle spare and checking the escape route — the loose grate behind the coal bin that opened onto the drainage culvert, which opened onto nothing useful but at least opened onto dark. He carried three cups and a bottle of something Belgian and terrible, and he set them on the stone shelf beside the glue pot without ceremony.

"Break," he said.

"We're not finished," Céline said, feeding another sheet.

"The rack's filling. If you want the ink to dry before dawn instead of smearing onto the next forme, you'll stop and drink your coffee." He glanced at the press. "How many?"

"Two hundred and six. Of two hundred and fifty."

Elias did the arithmetic the way he did everything — silently, and with his jaw tightening slightly, as though the numbers were arguments he was losing. Two hundred and six sheets meant forty-four chances left for the press to throw a roller, for a quoin to slip, for the police lorry whose engine she'd heard idling near the fish market at midnight to make an unscheduled turn onto their street.

Freya came around the drying racks with a sheet held carefully by its edges, like a photograph still wet. She was twenty-six years old and had the posture of a woman twice that, spine straightened by things seen at dressing stations, and tonight her eyes were doing something Céline recognized: going bright and unfocused, the way eyes go when they are looking at a person who is not in the room.

"This one," Freya said. "This one's straight. Look — the rule under the title runs edge to edge, not a hair off."

Céline took it. She looked. She was obliged to admit the girl was right, and admitting it gave her a satisfaction entirely disproportionate to the fact, because the truth was that Céline had been waiting for this sheet for three years and had told herself she wouldn't feel anything when it came, and here she was feeling everything, and here was the proof that she had raised the forme well enough that a stranger could hold it and call it beautiful.

It was the first signature — printer's word, first gathering of pages — complete. Title page, testimony, the opening of Vane's account, the first nine names in a column set solid down the inner margin like a spine. The paper was cream-colored and warm from the press. The ink smelled of linseed and lampblack and, to Céline, faintly of the year 1910, of Auguste's shop on the Rue du Marché, of Luc at nine years old standing on a stool to reach the composing stick, setting his own name in reverse because he liked how it looked backward.

*Luc. Cul.* He'd laughed at that. *Cul*, bottom, and a nine-year-old boy finds nothing funnier than the word *bottom* set in twelve-point Baskerville.

"Well?" Elias said.

"It will do," Céline said, which was the highest praise her mouth permitted, and both of them knew it, and Elias almost smiled, and Freya did smile, and for a moment the three of them stood in the lamplight around a single sheet of paper as though around a font at a christening.

* * *

They drank the terrible coffee Elias made — chicory cut with real coffee cut with chicory again, war in a cup — and Céline sat on her stool by the stone sink and allowed herself, for exactly as long as the cup lasted, to imagine the morning after the war.

Not victory. She had stopped believing in victory somewhere in the winter of 1915, when the lists started coming and the lists stopped making sense. What she imagined was smaller and better: a Tuesday. Ordinary daylight. She would open the shutters of the shop and sweep the step, and a woman she didn't know would come down Rue Basse with a marketing basket and stop at the door and look at her, really look at her, and say, "You're the one who printed it. You're the one who put my brother's name back." And Céline would say yes, come in, mind the type cases, and the woman would stand among the lead and the ink and the smell of it, and she would not thank her — thanks would be too light a coin — but she would simply stand there, and Céline would know that somewhere in Belgium a family was setting a place at the table again for a son who had died a soldier and not a coward, and that the priest would say the right words at last, and the stone in the churchyard would carry the right date and the right manner of dying, and the lie would finally, finally be too heavy for the men who told it to lift.

That was the whole of her ambition. Not justice — the inquiry, if there ever was one, would bury it, she'd seen inquiries before — but restoration. A name returned is a small resurrection. She had settled for small resurrections the way other women settled for small pensions.

"You're crying," Freya said quietly.

"No," said Céline. "I'm lubricating." She wiped her face with the heel of an inky hand and thereby gave herself a black stripe from cheekbone to jaw, and Freya laughed — a short, surprised sound, the laugh of a girl who hadn't laughed in weeks — and Elias looked up sharply at the sound as if it were a noise the building shouldn't be capable of making.

"Forty-one names," Freya said. Her thumb moved along the column on the sheet, not touching the wet ink, hovering just above it. "Nine here. When I read them I can see them. Corporal Hendriks had a way of whistling through his teeth when he changed a dressing, like it hurt him worse than it hurt you. Private Ostermann — the second Ostermann, there were two — he asked me to write to his mother in Liège and I couldn't spell the village and he drew me a map on the back of a cigarette packet." She stopped. Her thumb stopped. "I never sent that letter. I couldn't find the village on any army map, and by the time I found a civilian atlas he'd gone, and the dispatches said he'd deserted, and what does a mother do with a letter like that anyway? *Dear Madame, your son was brave, also officially a coward, please advise.*"

The room went quiet except for the flywheel ticking as it slowed.

"So we print the village," Céline said.

Freya looked at her.

"In the pamphlet. Under his name. *Ostermann, J., of — whatever the village is called.* Let the atlas argue with the army. Paper remembers longer than clerks." Céline drained her cup and stood, joints announcing themselves one by one, knees, hips, the small treacheries of sixty-one years. "And you'll draw the map yourself, in the margin, if you remember it. A map is a kind of signature."

"He'd have liked that," Freya said. "He was always drawing. Horses, mostly. Bad horses."

"Then a bad horse in the margin too, if you can manage it. We have room. We have," and here Céline gestured at the workshop entire, the racks of drying sheets, the cases of type, the formes wrapped in damp cloth awaiting their turn, "we have nothing but room."

* * *

They went back to work, because joy in that room was rationed like everything else, and because the press didn't care about grief or christenings — the press cared about feed, pressure, timing. Céline resumed her post. *Thump. Whisper.* Two hundred and ten. Two hundred and fifteen. Elias took over the delivery tray, squaring sheets onto the racks, and Freya fetched the next batch of stock from the cellar, and for an hour the only words exchanged were technical ones — *tighten the near-side quoin, the third line's gone gray, mind the frisket* — and these words, Céline reflected, were the most honest words spoken in Belgium that night, because technical words cannot lie. A forme is either locked or it isn't. Ink either covers or it doesn't. The war ran on the opposite principle entirely.

It was Freya who heard it first. She had the ears of her profession — nurses hear the change in a ward's breathing before any monitor could — and she lifted her head from the stock cabinet with her finger to her lips.

Engines. More than one. Down toward the canal end of the street, moving slowly.

Elias killed the lamp over the press. The darkness dropped on them complete, and in it Céline heard the sounds sharpen: rain, the tick of cooling metal, her own pulse, and beneath it all the lorries, big engines laboring in low gear, the sound of vehicles carrying weight.

"They're turning," Elias breathed. "Rue des Sarts — they're coming up Rue des Sarts—"

"That road goes nowhere but the church and us," Céline said. Her voice came out flat and calm, which surprised her; apparently sixty-one years of widowhood had trained her for exactly this, for bad news arriving at inconvenient hours. "Kill the stove. Cover the forme. Freya — the rack by the window, throw the tarpaulin, and then everyone downstairs, now, and no lamps until I say."

The machines obeyed her. In the dark, hands did what hands had rehearsed: the gas ring hissed out, the canvas came down over two hundred sheets of drying truth, the chase was slid under the sacking behind the type cabinets. Céline stood a moment alone in the blackness with her palm flat on the cold iron of the Heidelberg, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping child you might have to wake and run with.

"Not yet," she whispered to it. To the machine, to the forme, to Luc's name sitting in its bed of lead seven lines down, patient as bone. "You've waited fourteen months. Wait till Thursday. Give me till Thursday."

Outside, the engines labored closer. Then, agonizingly, they changed pitch — accelerating, passing, fading — toward the church, past it, gone down the far road toward the garrison at the canal bridge, hauling whatever weight they carried to somebody else's door.

Céline let out a breath she'd been holding since 1916.

But she noticed that neither of the young people laughed, or sighed, or made the joke the moment invited. Freya stood in the dark with the tarpaulin rope still in her fist. And Elias said, very quietly, in the tone of a man reporting a pulse:

"That's the third patrol this week. And the first one that turned up our street."

Nobody answered. The press ticked as it cooled. On the racks behind their canvas shroud, the first signature of the true account dried slowly in the dark — nine names, one map yet to come, a bad horse in the margin — fragile as a newborn and already, Céline understood, marked for the same world as everything else born in wartime: hunted from the hour of its birth, and worth every hour it lived.

"Thursday," she said aloud, to the dark, to the machine, to the two young people breathing in it. "We print faster."
