## 21. Snow Over Passchendaele

The snow began at dusk on the fourth of December, falling the way snow falls in Flanders — not gently but grudgingly, as if the sky had to be persuaded to give them anything clean.

Freya stood at the dressing station doorway and watched it come down over the duckboards, over the wire, over the flooded shell-holes that had spent all autumn pretending to be lakes. The snow didn't cover the war. It whitened it. It made the whole ruined plain look like a page someone had tried to blot out and failed, every crater a grey shadow beneath the white, every shattered tree a stroke of ink the eraser had missed.

Behind her, the ward breathed its familiar breath — the rasp of wounded lungs, the clink of a kidney dish set down too hard by an exhausted hand, Matron Cobb's voice moving through it all like a metronome: *Turn him. Change that. Not yet.* The ordinary music of the place. She had learned to hear it as music, which frightened her a little. It meant she had been here long enough for horror to become rhythm.

"Freya." Iris touched her elbow as she passed, arms full of soiled dressings. "Your two in bay four are settled. And you've been standing in that doorway so long you've grown icicles."

"I'm off at nine," Freya said.

"You're off *now*," Iris corrected, with the particular tyranny of a friend who has arranged something without asking. "Matron gave you the evening. Tam will take your rounds. Go on, before she changes her mind."

"Given me the evening for what?"

Iris's smile was sly and kind at once. "There's a man in the mess tent who's been reading the same page of a newspaper for two hours. I assumed he was waiting for someone."

* * *

Elias Wren did not look up when she came in. That was his way — he never announced himself, never made a claim on a room. He sat at the trestle table under the hissing storm lantern with a newspaper open before him and his hands flat on either side of it, as though he were pinning the day down lest it escape. His greatcoat was dusted white. There was snow melting in the brim of his cap.

"The shipping news is fascinating this time of year," Freya said.

He looked up then, and she saw the tiredness in him before he could arrange his face over it. "Iris told me you'd be off at nine. She lied about the hour."

"She lied to get me here. I expect she lied to you too. We're a station of liars; it keeps morale up."

He almost smiled. He folded the newspaper with unnecessary care, squaring the edges, and she understood it was not tidiness but nerves — Elias Wren handled paper the way other men handled loaded things. "There's a courier due at midnight," he said. "From the direction of the coast road. I have an hour of nothing to do and nowhere warm to do it in except here."

"Then sit," Freya said. "And let me make you tea that isn't made from the dregs of the dregs."

* * *

The tea was terrible and they both pretended otherwise. They sat across the table from one another while the storm lantern hissed and the wind worried at the tent ropes, and for a while they spoke of nothing — of the snow, of Tam's endless schemes to trade cigarettes for jam, of Céline Dossin's fury when a line of type came out reversed. Small talk. But Freya had learned that small talk between people carrying large secrets was its own language, a code of normality they maintained for each other like a lamp kept lit in a window.

"There's a lull," Elias said at last. He said it carefully, the way a man tests ice. "Three days now, the guns quiet on our stretch. The staff say the weather's bought us a pause."

"The staff say a lot of things."

"Yes," he agreed mildly. "They do."

She studied him across the table. In the months since the night he had first appeared at the gate with his impossible letters, she had catalogued him the way she catalogued wounds — methodically, because method was how she survived knowing things. The scar through his left eyebrow from a war she knew only in outline. The way he sat angled toward doors. The way he never finished his tea while it was hot, as if he expected to be called away mid-cup, because he always had been.

"Elias," she said. "When this ends — the printing, the pamphlet, all of it. When it's done or when we're caught. Have you ever thought about what comes after?"

The question landed in the silence between them like a dropped instrument.

"No," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the honest one." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table. "Men in my line don't keep futures. A future is a thing you can lose. I find it simpler not to own one."

"That's not simple. That's just poor."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered, as though laughter were also contraband. "Matron Cobb says something similar. About me. Less kindly."

"Matron Cobb says everything less kindly. It's how she shows affection."

* * *

The snow thickened outside. Through the canvas wall they could hear it ticking against the fabric, and beyond that, faint and far, the front making its winter sounds — not the grinding summer roar but occasional single thumps, a flare going up somewhere, drifting down as a red star behind the clouds.

"Passchendaele's under there," Elias said quietly, nodding toward the north wall of the tent, toward everything. "Under the snow. All autumn I carried papers through that ground. You couldn't tell trench from grave most days. Now it's white and it looks — " He stopped. Started again. "It looks innocent. Like a field where nothing happened."

Freya thought of her ledger. Forty-one names now, cross-checked against Elias's lists, each one a man recorded officially as a deserter who had in truth died walking into machine-gun fire because an order said walk. The Lantern lay sewn into the lining of her kit bag not ten feet away, inside the hymnal, beneath the psalms.

"When I was training," she said, "in London, my instructor told us that nursing was mostly housekeeping at the edge of death. Keep the ward clean, keep the records accurate, keep your hands steady. She said if we did those three things we could be proud." She wrapped both hands around her cooling cup. "She never mentioned what to do when the records themselves are the wound."

"No one trains for that."

"No. You learn it the way you learn anything out here." She looked up at him. "Badly, and fast, and alone."

"You're not alone."

It was such a small sentence. Three words, delivered flatly, staring down into his cup. And it went through her with more force than anything he had said in all the months she had known him — more than the revelation that Aldous Vane lived, more than the lists, more than the first sight of Céline's press standing in its lamplit workshop like an altar.

Because she understood what it cost him to say it. A man who doesn't keep futures, offering her one word of company as though it were ration chocolate — precious, hoarded, given anyway.

* * *

They walked afterward. It was madness — the coast road after dark, snow falling, couriers possibly about — but the lull had made everyone slightly drunk with quiet, and there was a stretch of lane behind the station where the poplars still stood, three of them, black and ragged against the white, and no one went there because there was nothing there to requisition.

Their boots made the only prints. The world was muffled, blue-grey, hushed. Somewhere a church bell that belonged to a church that no longer existed rang the hour anyway, struck by some faithful hand in some cellar, and the sound came across the fields round and slow.

"Aldous used to write to me about bells," Freya said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "Before Passchendaele. He said the sound carried further than any order ever did, and meant more."

"He'd know. He took enough orders."

"He wrote me forty-one letters. I counted, once, after — " She stopped. After he died. The words still snagged in her like shrapnel, even now, even knowing. "After they told me. I counted them because counting was all I could do. Then a stranger arrives carrying letters in the same hand and tells me the man isn't dead at all, and I thought — do you know what I thought? I thought: *of course*. Of course the war would kill him and then un-kill him, just to teach me that nothing written down can be trusted. Not dispatches. Not deaths. Not even my own grief."

Elias stopped walking. Snow gathered on his shoulders; he seemed not to notice.

"And now?" he asked.

"Now I think the opposite." She watched her breath cloud and vanish. "Now I think writing things down is the only weapon anyone has left. The falsified reports are just ink. So are the true ones. It's a question of whose ink survives." She glanced at him sideways. "That's why Céline sets type until her fingers bleed. Why Vane hides in a flooded farmhouse with his evidence. Why I keep the Lantern. We're not fighting men anymore, Elias. We're fighting paper with paper."

"And paper burns," Elias said softly. "Both kinds."

"Yes." She held his eyes. "So we'd best be quick, and we'd best be brave."

* * *

They reached the third poplar and stood beneath it. Its branches creaked under their white burden. From here they could see, far off, the flare-line of the front pulsing behind the snowclouds like a heart beating under a bandage.

"If we finish it," Freya said — and her voice came out lower than she intended, almost shy — "if the pamphlet gets printed, distributed, if the names reach the families — what then? For you. For me. Do you ever imagine it? Even a little?"

Elias was silent a long moment. Snow ticked onto the ground around them.

"I imagine a room," he said finally, as though confessing to a crime. "A small one. Above a shop, maybe, or behind one. A stove that works. No canvas anywhere. And a table with nothing on it that anyone wants." He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "That's the whole dream. An empty table. Men like me aren't meant to want more, and wanting more is probably how we get killed, so I try to put it back in its box."

"What's in the box besides the room?"

"Don't."

"Is there a chair in the room?"

"Freya."

"Two chairs?" She heard herself smiling and couldn't stop it, and found she didn't entirely want to. "It's a very specific fantasy for a man who claims not to keep futures. An empty table. A working stove. I think there's bread in this fantasy. Fresh bread. Am I right?"

He looked at her then — really looked, the way he rarely permitted himself — and the whole careful architecture of him wavered, just for a moment, like a flame in a draught. "You're right," he said quietly. "There's bread. And someone reading aloud, badly, from whatever's printed that week, while the other one pretends to listen." He exhaled, and the breath smoked between them. "There. Now you have it. Now it exists outside the box, and I'll spend the rest of this war being afraid of losing it, which is exactly the stupidity I swore off years ago."

"I'm sorry."

"You're not."

"No," she admitted. "I'm not."

* * *

They did not touch. That was the discipline of it, the strange courtliness that had grown up between them without either declaring it — two people who carried secrets learned not to leave fingerprints on one another. But they stood closer than they needed to, shoulder nearly to shoulder beneath the ruined tree, watching the snow come down over the field where so many of her names lay buried, and the closeness was itself a kind of speech.

"Elias," she said. "If it goes wrong — the raid, the arrests, whichever comes first — promise me you'll take the master copy east. Don't come back for anyone. Not me. Not Céline. The pages matter more than the people carrying them, and we both know it, so say it out loud once so I don't have to wonder later whether you hesitated."

"I won't say it, because it's a lie."

"It's not a lie, it's strategy—"

"It's a lie," he repeated, and his voice had an edge she had never heard from him, quiet and absolute. "I've spent six years watching men treat each other as expendable in the name of documents and objectives, and I will tell you what I've learned: the document is worth nothing unless the people are worth something first. The pamphlet matters because Céline's son mattered. Because your forty-one mattered. If we save the paper by spending the people, we've built our monument out of the same lies the colonel builds his." He shook his head slowly. "I'll carry the pages. But I'm not trading anyone for them. Refuse to."

The bell rang again across the fields, slower, as if the ringer were growing tired.

Freya found she could not answer. Something in her throat had closed — not grief exactly, and not joy, but the strange compound the war distilled from both, the feeling she had never found a name for and suspected no language contained. She looked at Elias Wren, snow settling in his hair, future leaking out of him despite himself, and thought: *this is what belonging costs. This exact ache.*

"Midnight," he said at last, regretfully. "My courier."

"Yes."

They turned back along their own footprints. Behind them the snow was already filling them in, patient and thorough, erasing the evidence that anyone had stood beneath the last poplar and spoken of chairs and bread. By morning the lane would be blank. By spring, perhaps, the whole field would be ploughed and green and lying.

* * *

But the war does not honour lulls.

She was halfway through the ward at half past eleven — checking bay four, Iris's two sleeping men, the ledger's weight a familiar warmth against her hip — when Tam came in from the gate, stamping snow off his boots, his young face bright with importance.

"Nurse Ashgrove! There's a fellow at the gate asking for you — well, not for you, for the *courier*, only the courier's not due till—" He caught himself, glancing at the patients, and lowered his voice to a stage whisper that half the ward could hear. "Only he's wet through and he keeps saying it's *urgent*, urgent about the paper stock, and he won't give his name to anyone but—"

"Tam." Her blood had gone cold and slow. "What paper stock?"

"The man wouldn't say. Only—" Tam frowned, digging in his pocket, and produced a scrap of paper sodden with snowmelt, unfolding it with clumsy care. "He gave me this for you. Said you'd know."

She took it. The handwriting was Elias's — hurried, pressed hard, the pen driven rather than guided:

*Courier intercepted at the crossroads. Not mine. Watch the boy. Burn what you can't carry. — E.W.*

Tam was still talking — something about the man at the gate, about how he'd promised to wait, about whether he should fetch hot tea — and Freya heard none of it. She was looking past his shoulder, through the frost-furred canvas of the ward entrance, at the snow coming down soft and silent and white over everything, hiding everything, the way it hid the graves, the way it hid the truth, the way it would hide, by dawn, whatever came up the coast road next.

"Tam," she said, very quietly. "How long ago did that man leave the gate?"

"Ten minutes, maybe. Why?"

"Where was he headed?"

Tam blinked at her urgency, hurt and puzzled. "Toward the depot. I offered to walk him partway — he said no need, he knew the track by the culvert. Only—" He hesitated, and in the hesitation Freya felt the whole fragile winter night tilt beneath her. "Only he asked an odd question first. Asked if I often ran errands after dark. Said a lad my size could earn good money carrying things nobody asks about."

Outside, far off toward the crossroads, a whistle blew — one note, thin and wrong, cut short.
