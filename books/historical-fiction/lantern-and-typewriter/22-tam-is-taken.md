## 22. Tam Is Taken

The lorries came to Poperinghe on a Tuesday that smelled of rain and coal smoke, and Tam Merrick was carrying eleven pages of the truth sewn into the lining of his greatcoat, folded flat against his left ribs like a second heartbeat.

He had volunteered for this. That was the thing he kept returning to, standing in the queue at the transport office with his papers sweating in his glove — he had *volunteered*. Nobody had pressed a pistol to his neck. Nurse Ashgrove had only asked, in her quiet way, whether he was going into town anyway, and whether he'd mind taking a parcel to the cobbler on the Rue de la Chapelle, nothing perishable, nothing urgent, just something that needed to travel with a person rather than a post sack. He had said yes before she finished asking, because being asked at all had felt like being trusted, and trust was rarer in his life than sugar.

Eleven pages. He didn't know what they said. He knew they were important because Elias Wren had checked them twice against a list and then looked at Tam with those grey eyes that never seemed to blink all the way, and said: *Don't open it. Don't read it. Don't let anyone see you carry it. If you're stopped, you're delivering buttons for the laundry contract.*

Buttons. He could remember buttons.

The rain thickened as the lorry jolted along the pave toward the town. Tam sat wedged between two men from the Army Service Corps who were arguing about a football match played two years ago, and he let their voices wash over him and tried to think about anything except the weight against his ribs. It wasn't heavy. That was the astonishing thing. Eleven pages of paper weighed less than a biscuit. Less than a bullet, certainly, and men carried bullets by the hundred without their hearts hammering like this.

It wasn't the weight. It was what the weight meant.

He had seen the list once — not read it, just seen it — the night Céline Dossin's press was running and Freya had spread her ledger open under the lamp, forty-one names in her small upright hand, and Céline touching each one as she set its type. Forty-one men recorded as deserters who had died advancing. Tam had understood then, in the wordless way he understood most things, that paper could be a weapon or a wound depending on who held it, and that he was now a man who carried ammunition through occupied streets.

*Buttons,* he told himself. *You are a boy taking buttons to a cobbler.*

At the drop point — the cobbler's shop with its cracked window and its smell of leather and glue — a woman with flour on her forearms took the parcel from inside his coat without looking at him, murmured *God keep you, small one* in French, and gave him a heel of bread in return. The whole exchange lasted nine seconds. Tam walked out into the rain feeling ten feet tall.

That was when the military police stepped out of the doorway opposite.

There were three of them, red caps bright even under the grey sky, and a sergeant with a notebook already open, as though Tam were an appointment they'd been keeping. The tallest one put a hand flat on Tam's chest — not rough, worse than rough, *casual*, the way a man stops a delivery boy — and said, "Where you posted, son?"

"Number Sixty-Two Casualty Clearing," Tam said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Orderly."

"Papers."

Tam produced them. The sergeant read them slowly, wetting his thumb to turn the page, while the tall one drifted behind Tam with the unhurried menace of a cat circling something small. Rain dripped off Tam's cap brim. Somewhere down the street a church bell counted the half hour.

"You come from the clearing station alone?"

"Yes, sir."

"Alone?" the sergeant repeated, softer, which was somehow louder. "Walking into Poperinghe on your own on a wet Tuesday. No errand?"

"Laundry," Tam said. "Button contract. I delivered—" He stopped. He had been about to say *I delivered the parcel* and there was no parcel anymore, and the absence of it hung in the air between them like smoke.

The sergeant smiled without any warmth reaching his eyes. "Delivered what?"

"Nothing. A message for the— for my matron. About the linen stores." The lie came out crooked and he heard it go crooked and knew, with a sick lurch, that they heard it too.

"Right," said the sergeant, closing the notebook. "You'll come along and help us with our inquiries about linen stores. All in order, son. You're not arrested." He patted Tam's shoulder almost kindly. "You're just helping."

They walked him not to the guardroom but to a house with sandbags at the windows and a sign stencilled over the door: FIELD SECURITY SECTION. Tam had passed it a dozen times without seeing it. Now he saw it the way a man sees a grave he'll soon occupy.

* * *

The room they put him in was small and clean and that was the worst part. He had expected filth, shouting, a lantern swinging on a hook. Instead there was a table, two chairs, a map of the salient pinned to the wall with pins clustered around Ypres like flies on meat, and a teapot. A captain named Hollis — round-faced, mild, spectacles polished bright — poured tea for both of them as if they were friends calling on one another of an afternoon.

"Sugar?" he asked.

"No thank you, sir."

"Good lad. Sugar's scarce." Captain Hollis sat, folded his hands, and regarded Tam with an expression of such gentle patience that Tam felt his skin crawl. "Now. Thomas Merrick. Seventeen years old — is that right? Seventeen and already doing a man's work. Your mother must be proud."

"She died," Tam said. "Two years back."

Hollis's face did something that might have been sympathy in a better man. "Mine too. Mothers." He sighed, sipped. "Tommy. May I call you Tommy? Here's my trouble. We've been having trouble with paper. Pamphlets, leaflets, seditious muck printed somewhere in this sector and circulated among leave trains — have you heard anything of it?"

"No, sir."

"No. Of course not." Hollis opened a folder on the table. Inside, Tam saw with a distant, swimming horror, was a page — a single page, water-spotted, typeset in Céline's tight columns. He couldn't read it upside down but he knew its texture the way he knew the texture of bread versus stone. They had one. Someone had been searched. Someone had talked, or someone had lost it, and the loose thread was here in this clean little room tied to his own wrist.

"We've been asking where these are made," Hollis went on pleasantly. "Paper's traceable, you know. Everything's traceable if you're patient. And today we stop a seventeen-year-old orderly walking into town with no parcel, no message, and a story about button contracts that changes shape twice in thirty seconds." He turned the folder around so the page faced Tam. "Do you know anything about this?"

"No, sir."

"It's a list of names, Tommy. Men described here as murdered by their own command. Treasonous filth. Whoever prints it wants our boys mutinying in the trenches. Now — a good soldier tells me everything he knows, and I protect him. A foolish boy protects whoever used him, and I have to treat him as part of the network." Hollis leaned back, folding his hands across his stomach. "Which are you?"

"I don't know anything," Tam said. And it was true, God help him, it was true — he didn't know what was printed, he didn't know the addresses, he barely knew the names — and his innocence rose in him like floodwater, and with the floodwater came fear, because he suddenly saw how fear would look from outside. He saw his own face as they would see it: pale, sweating, eyes jumping to the folder and away. Guilt has a face and innocence often borrows it.

Hollis watched him for a long moment. The clock on the mantel ticked. Outside, the rain kept on, indifferent as artillery.

"You're frightened," Hollis observed.

"Yes, sir."

"That's sensible. Frightened boys tell the truth eventually. Everyone does." He stood, gathered the folder, and paused at the door. "We'll hold you overnight. Give you time to think. There's a cot in the next room. If you want to talk in the night, knock on the wall — Sergeant Pryce sleeps light." At the door he turned, mild as milk. "One more thing, Tommy. Who asked you to go into town today?"

And Tam, seventeen years old, exhausted, terrified, standing in a clean room with a traitor's teacup still warm in his hand, heard himself say:

"Nurse Ashgrove sent me to the linen stores, sir. But I never went. I got sidetracked."

The lie came out smooth this time, and he hated it, and he was grateful for it, and he did not know which of those feelings would survive the night.

Hollis nodded slowly. "Nurse Ashgrove," he repeated, writing nothing down, remembering everything. "Get some sleep, son."

* * *

Freya learned of it at dusk, from Iris, of all people — Iris coming into the ward with her jaw set hard and her voice pitched low beneath the clatter of the evening feed.

"They've taken Tam. Security section, Poperinghe. One of the ASC drivers saw them walk him out of the Rue de la Chapelle." Iris's hands were fists at her sides. "Matron's been on the telephone for an hour. They're saying he's assisting inquiries."

Freya felt the ward tilt beneath her, the way a deck tilts. Eleven pages. She had sewn them herself into his coat lining that morning with her own needle, her own thread, humming some idiot tune, and he had grinned up at her and asked if he'd done well, and she had said *you've done perfectly, Tam* — and now he was sitting in a whitewashed room being offered sugar by a man whose profession was unspooling boys like thread.

"He doesn't know anything," she said. "Iris, he doesn't know anything. He carried pages he couldn't read to a place he couldn't find again—"

"Then why do they want him?"

Because they always want someone. Because a net is made of knots and every knot is a person. She said none of it. She said, "I'm going to Matron."

But Matron Cobb was already coming to her. Freya saw her coming down the ward between the cot rows, lamp-light sliding over her starched front, her face carrying that terrible calm that meant the calm was load-bearing. She took Freya's elbow — not gently, not roughly, with the grip of a woman steering a cart out of a rut — and drew her into the dispensary and shut the door.

"Sit down, Nurse Ashgrove."

"Matron, if they question him—"

"They will question him. And he will weep, and stammer, and tell them exactly nothing, because he knows exactly nothing, and then they will ask him the one question he can answer." Matron's eyes were flint. "*Who sent you.*"

The dispensary was very quiet. Bottles of iodine and cascara lined the shelves like a jury. Freya heard her own pulse in her ears.

"He covered," Freya said. "Whatever he said — he wouldn't give anyone. Not Tam. He'd sooner—"

"He's seventeen," Matron said. "Seventeen-year-old boys cover for people right up until the moment they don't, and then they cover for themselves instead, and no one can blame them for it. So we will not wait to find out which hour tonight that moment comes." She straightened her cuffs with two sharp tugs. "Elias Wren. Where is he?"

"The workshop, presumably—"

"Then get word to him *tonight*. Not tomorrow. Tonight." Matron Cobb looked at her then, and for one unguarded instant Freya saw beneath the flint: a woman who had spent twenty years counting other people's costs. "You wanted to keep records, girl. Records have teeth. This is the first bite. Pray God it's the shallowest."

She swept out. Freya stood among the bottles a moment longer, then pulled her cape from its hook and went out into the rain to find a courier who could reach Rue Basse before midnight — and found Elias Wren already waiting under the eaves of the dispensary door, hat dripping, face grey in the dark.

"I know," he said, before she spoke. "Céline heard within the hour. Freya—" For the first time since she'd known him, the man looked rattled; the grey eyes moved and came back unsettled. "The colonel has wanted a name for weeks. If Tam gives him yours—"

"He won't."

"If he does," Elias said quietly, "it won't be Tam who ends you. It'll be a file, and a transfer order, and a cell in Rouen, and none of us will ever be able to prove what happened to you. That's how it works. Paper, Freya. It always comes back to paper."

Somewhere beyond the wire, far off toward the salient, the guns began their nightly argument — a low rolling thunder, patient, certain of winning eventually. Freya pulled her cape close and thought of Tam in a clean room with a traitor's teapot, thinking of buttons, trying to be brave in the way that seventeen-year-olds are brave: badly, completely, and at the worst possible price.

"Then we move faster than their paper," she said. "Tonight."

Elias was already turning toward the dark road, and behind them both, in the lit ward window, Iris stood watching them go with an expression Freya did not turn around long enough to read.
