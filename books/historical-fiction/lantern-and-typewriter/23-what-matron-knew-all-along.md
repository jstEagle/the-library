## 23. What Matron Knew All Along

The ward smelled of carbolic and wet wool the night Tam was taken, and Freya learned that grief, in a dressing station, had a sound: the particular hush that fell when a bed was stripped too soon.

She had watched them carry his blankets out folded into a square. She had watched Iris stand in the doorway of the empty cot with her arms crossed tight, as if holding herself together with her own hands. And she had watched Matron Cobb do nothing at all — no pacing, no prayer, no wringing of hands — only the slow, deliberate lighting of the lamp over the duty desk, as though the answer to everything were simply to make light where there was dark.

It was that stillness that frightened Freya most.

* * *

Two days passed. Two days of Freya carrying her guilt like a stone sewn into her apron pocket, feeling its weight every time she lifted a basin, changed a dressing, smiled at a boy who asked whether the shelling had stopped. Tam sat somewhere in a guardroom at the railhead answering questions he could not answer because he knew nothing, and his knowing nothing was the only wall between the military police and all of them — and she had put him behind it. She had let Elias use him. She had signed off on the errand with a nod because she had been tired and hurried and flattered by Tam's eagerness to matter.

On the second evening, after the last of the convoy cases had been settled for the night, Matron Cobb said, without looking up from the ledger she was keeping — the official one, the one full of names that were permitted to exist:

"Nurse Ashgrove. My office. Now."

Freya's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

The matron's office was a converted linen cupboard at the end of the ward, six feet by eight, smelling of starch and camphor. A desk, a chair, a stool, one window boarded against shellfire with a slit of glass at eye level through which the ruined poplars of the salient were visible, black against a bruised sky. There was room for exactly two people to stand in it without touching, and Matron Cobb arranged it so that they did not touch, which somehow made it worse.

"Sit," said the matron, and Freya sat on the stool, knees together, hands in her lap, a girl of twenty-six rehearsing lies and finding the shelf where she kept them suddenly bare.

Matron Cobb closed the door. Then she reached beneath the desk — Freya's heart slammed once, hard, against her ribs — and set something down between them.

Not the Lantern. Not the hymnal.

A photograph. Old, creased twice, gone soft at the corners from handling. A young man in the uniform of the Norfolk Regiment, standing stiffly beside a bicycle, squinting into sun that existed only in another year, another country entirely.

Freya looked at it. Looked up. "Matron—"

"His name was William Cobb," said Edith Cobb, in a voice as level as a spirit gauge. "My younger brother. He went out in the spring of 1915 with the Norfolks, and in May of that year his battalion was ordered to hold a section of line near Ypres that every man above the rank of major privately agreed could not be held." She turned the photograph so that it faced Freya squarely, the way one presents evidence. "He held it anyway. He died holding it. And when the dispatch came home to my mother in Norwich, it said that Private W. Cobb had been killed *while absenting himself from his post*."

The words seemed to fall into the little room and keep falling, past the floorboards, past the earth, past everything.

"They wrote deserter on my brother," Matron Cobb said. "They wrote it in ink, in an official hand, and it went into a book, and the book went into a file, and the file went into a drawer in some ministry, and my mother received a letter expressing regret for the loss of a coward. She died believing it. I have read that dispatch perhaps four hundred times in my head, Nurse Ashgrove, and I have never once found in it the sentence that would have been true: *Private Cobb remained at his post when every other post had fallen, and he fired until his rifle burst, and then he threw stones.*"

Freya could not speak. Her throat had closed around something hot.

"How do I know what actually happened?" the matron continued, softly now, terribly softly. "Because a sergeant who survived crawled back that night with his jaw hanging off his face, and I dressed him three months later in a hospital at Étaples, and before the ether took him he told me. Told me everything. The order, the ground, the guns that were known to be there. He told me, and then he died of his wounds, and I was left holding the truth the way you hold a burn — knowing that if I opened my hand everyone would see it and nothing would be changed by their seeing."

She looked at Freya then, fully, and Freya understood that she was seeing not a matron but a woman of fifty-one years who had been standing at attention inside her own life since May of 1915.

"So," said Matron Cobb. "The hymnal."

* * *

"I don't—" Freya began, and heard her own voice come out thin and guilty and transparent as gauze.

"Sister Ashgrove." The matron's mouth twitched, almost a smile, and it was worse than any anger. "I have run this station for nineteen months. I know how many hymnals we possess, because I counted them when we arrived, because counting things is how a matron survives. We possessed eleven. Since November we have possessed eleven hymnals and one book that weighs slightly more than a hymnal and opens, if you are careless enough to drop it, onto pages ruled in a hand I taught you to make legible when you first came here crying over your first gas case."

"You've known since November." It was not a question. It was Freya's whole hidden year collapsing into a single sentence.

"I have known since the night you hid it while I stood at your shoulder watching you do it in the mirror of the supply cupboard door." The matron folded her hands on the desk, atop her brother's photograph. "You were very clumsy. You looked like a woman hiding a love letter. I have seen women hide love letters; I knew exactly what I was looking at, except that whatever you were hiding made you look less like a girl in love than a woman at a graveside."

"Why didn't you say anything?" The question tore out of Freya before she could weigh it. "All this time — why?"

"Because saying something would have required me to decide what to do about it, and I did not wish to decide." For a moment the composure cracked — just a hairline, just around the eyes — and Freya saw underneath it the enormous fatigue of a woman who has been alone with a secret so long the secret has grown roots into her. "Do you understand what it means, what I am telling you? Every morning since November I have chosen, again, not to open my hand. I have falsified the kit inventory to account for the weight. I have rearranged the duty rosters so that you were never searched. When Sergeant Halloran wanted to turn out the nurses' lockers during the colonel's inspection, I told him the coffins in the mortuary shed needed a recount, and sent him out into the rain to count boxes that contained nothing but a ledger and forty-one dead men's names."

"The coffin inventory," Freya whispered. "That was you."

"That was me," said Matron Cobb. "And here is the thing you must hear, and hear completely, because after tonight there will be no taking it back." She leaned forward across the small desk, and the lamplight caught the grey at her temples, the deep lines bracketing her mouth, the eyes that had watched ten thousand boys go out of the world. "I did not cover for you because I approve. I covered for you because I have spent thirty years keeping rules, and the rules killed my brother twice — once with machine guns and once with ink — and I am done pretending the second death is the lesser one. But understand the price. If this comes out, it does not come out as your sin alone. It comes out as mine. They will not send me home with a commendation, Sister. They will send me to a court-martial, and they will take my name off the register, and they will write in ink what kind of nurse Edith Cobb was. I have accepted that. What I have not yet decided is whether you have."

"I have," Freya said, and was surprised to find her voice steady. "I decided it the night Private Merrick died asking me to remember him, and his name wasn't in the dispatches, and I wrote it in a hymnal by candlelight like a criminal. I've been deciding it every day since. It doesn't get easier. It gets heavier. But I've decided."

The matron studied her for a long moment. Somewhere beyond the boarded window a gun spoke, far off, unhurried, the war clearing its throat.

"Do you know," Matron Cobb said at last, "that is the first time anyone has answered me honestly in this office in nineteen months? Do you know what people say to me? They say *yes matron, no matron, very good matron*. Nineteen months. And you sit on that stool shaking like a leaf and tell me the truth straight into my face." She sat back. Something in her shoulders loosened — a degree, no more, but Freya saw it the way one sees a lock begin to turn. "Your Captain Vane would be proud of you. Yes — I know about Vane. I know about the stranger who comes and goes under the name Wren. I know about the widow on the Rue Basse, or at least I know that you disappear on your nights off in a direction that is not the canteen and come back smelling of printer's ink and Belgian coffee. I am old, not blind."

Freya stared at her. "Then you've known *everything*—"

"I have known everything except whether I was willing to lose you," said the matron simply. "That was the question. That was always the question. And tonight—" her voice caught, briefly, on the word *tonight*, "—tonight they took Tam Ollerenshaw, who is seventeen years old and still has jam sometimes at the corner of his mouth, and carried him off to a guardroom to ask him questions about a war he cannot possibly explain, and I sat at that desk and lit the lamp and thought: *Edith, you have been deciding for two years, and a child is paying for your indecision.*"

She rose. In the tiny office she seemed suddenly taller than her five feet four inches, the way certain people become larger at the moment they stop apologizing for themselves.

"So we will stop deciding and start doing," she said. "Here is what will happen. Tomorrow morning I am going to telephone the adjutant at the railhead and inform him — in the voice I reserve for inspecting generals and bishops — that Orderly Ollerenshaw is a minor, that he is on my strength, that he has a documented head injury from the dressing-station fire in March which makes prolonged questioning medically inadvisable, and that I require his return for observation within twenty-four hours or I shall escalate the matter to the DMS's office, where I happen to know the deputy director owes me his life from Gallipoli. That will not save Tam by itself. It buys us a day. Perhaps two."

"And after the day?" Freya asked. "After the two days?"

"After the two days," said Matron Cobb, "your Mr. Wren had better produce something better than a boy who knows nothing — because a boy who knows nothing, questioned long enough, becomes a boy who confirms everything. Fear fills in blanks, Sister. The police provide the answers and the frightened supply the signatures." She paused, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into a register Freya had never heard from her — low, private, almost tender. "Which brings me to the last thing. The reason I truly called you in here tonight, above and beyond confession."

From her uniform pocket she drew a small envelope, unmarked, softened at the edges.

"In 1915," she said, "after the sergeant told me what really happened to my brother, I wrote the truth down. Everything he'd said. The position, the order, the names of the officers who gave it and rewrote it afterward. I sealed it and I kept it, and I told myself that someday — after the war, when it was safe, when someone in authority might listen — I would send it to someone who could use it. I have carried it for two and a half years, Nurse Ashgrove, through Étaples and Boulogne and this charnel house you and I call a workplace. I have carried it the way you carry yours in a hymnal." She laid the envelope on the desk beside the photograph of William Cobb and his bicycle, side by side, the two testaments. "Tonight I am giving it to you. Add it to the Lantern. His name goes in — Private William Cobb, Norfolk Regiment, killed holding his post, recorded as a deserter. Forty-two reasons now."

Freya took the envelope with both hands, the way one takes bread, or a child, or a flame.

"Matron," she managed, "I don't know what to say."

"Don't say anything. Say it in type, on the Rue Basse, where it belongs." Edith Cobb straightened her cuffs — the old gesture, the armor going back on — but her eyes stayed warm, and Freya saw that something had shifted between them forever, some load-bearing wall rebuilt on new foundations. "You are not alone anymore, Sister. You haven't been for some time. Neither have I. That is the whole of what I wished to tell you, and I find that having told it, I am hungry, which is absurd at this hour, so you will come to the kitchen and eat burnt toast with me, and we will say nothing further of consequence, and tomorrow we will fight."

They stood in the doorway together a moment, the two of them, before crossing the dark ward. Through the slit of boarded window the guns muttered eastward. Below, in the kitchen, Iris was already boiling water for tea, humming something tuneless and brave, and Tam's empty cot waited at the end of the row like a held breath.

It was Iris who met them at the kitchen door, her face white in the lamplight, a telegram trembling in her hand.

"Matron," she said. "It's Tam. They're moving him — tomorrow, oh-six-hundred, to headquarters at Cassel. For formal questioning. It's on the wire." Her eyes went from the matron to Freya and back, wide and frightened and searching. "There isn't time. Is there? Tell me there's time."

Matron Cobb took the telegram and read it once, slowly, and Freya watched the new resolve in her face harden into something that would not break.

"There is time," said the matron, "for exactly one telephone call, one lie, and one miracle. Wake Mr. Wren."
