## 24. Extraction of a Boy

They kept Tam at the provost post behind the railhead, in a hut that had once stored mules' harness and still smelled of leather and dung and old fear.

Elias learned this the way he learned most things now — by standing too long in a queue of men, listening, buying a corporal two cups of chicory coffee and letting the man talk. The boy had been taken on the Ypres road with pages sewn into the lining of his greatcoat, and he'd been held for three days without charge, which meant someone above the corporal wanted to know what he knew before anyone official was told he existed.

"Doesn't say much, your lad," the corporal said, licking coffee from his thumb. "Cries a bit at night. Keeps saying he only carried letters for his matron."

Elias felt the words go through him like cold water through cloth. *Letters.* Thank God for Tam's innocence; thank God he'd been told nothing, so there was nothing to confess except a lie that happened to be true-shaped. But a boy who cried at night would eventually cry whatever they wanted him to cry. Three days was already two days too long.

He walked back toward the dressing station through the grey drizzle, past the duckboards and the stacked stretchers and the eternal smell — mud, iodine, chloride of lime, the sweetish rot underneath everything that no one mentioned anymore. He rehearsed what he would say to Freya, discarded it, rehearsed again. There was no good way to tell her. There was only the telling.

* * *

Freya heard the lorry before she saw it — the wrong engine note, heavier than an ambulance — and by the time it ground to a stop outside the ward tent she had already counted the steps between her and Matron Cobb's office, already calculated how many seconds of warning she could buy.

The officer who climbed down wore the red tabs of staff headquarters under his rain cape, and Freya's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. Not the colonel himself — a major, younger, carrying a folder — but the folder was enough.

"Nurse Ashgrove?" He didn't wait for confirmation. "The orderly Thomas Merrick has been detained for questioning regarding the possession of seditious material. You're listed as his supervising nurse. I'm instructed to ask whether you can account for his movements over the past fortnight."

Every eye in the ward was pretending not to listen. Iris stood at the far table, her hands flat on a folded bandage, not moving. Matron Cobb appeared in the doorway of her office with the unhurried authority of a woman who had outlasted four commanding officers and intended to outlast this one too.

"You may account for his movements to me," Matron said, "in my office, Major, where we keep the duty rosters. Nurse Ashgrove is needed here. We have eleven men prepped for evacuation and one doctor."

It was beautifully done. The major hesitated — rank against seniority, paper against the immovable fact of a matron who owned her ward like a captain owns a ship — and then followed her inside, and the door closed, and Freya went back to her patients with hands that did not shake until she let them.

That night, in the lee of the laundry boiler where the steam hid breath and faces both, Elias told her everything: the hut, the corporal, the crying.

"They'll move him to Roulers within the week," Elias said. "After that he goes into the system, and the system doesn't give boys back because they're innocent. It gives them back when they're broken, or it doesn't give them back at all."

"Then we get him out before the week ends." Freya said it simply, the way you'd say *then we change the dressing*, as if extraction were a procedure with clean steps. But he saw what it cost her — the way her jaw set, the way her eyes went to the dark window where the ward lamps burned like small stubborn stars.

"It's not that simple. They want a name from him. Someone who gave him the papers. If we pull him out suddenly—"

"If we leave him," she said, "they'll make him give a name anyway. Any name. Yours, mine, Céline's. A frightened boy will hand them the whole network to make the questioning stop." She turned to face him fully. "So we don't leave him. And we don't pull him out either. We make them *want* to let him go."

Elias looked at her — this nurse who a year ago had hidden names in a hymnal and flinched at her own daring — and understood that the ledger had done something to her. It had taught her that courage was not a quality you had or lacked but a thing you performed daily, like washing hands, like changing dressings, until it became simply what your hands knew how to do.

"How?" he asked.

She told him. And the terrible thing, the thing he would think about later with a kind of awe, was how quickly it came to her — as though some part of her had been planning for betrayal since the day she first wrote a dead private's name by lamplight.

"We give them an informant," she said. "A real one. Verifiable. Just not ours."

* * *

His name was Sergeant Deel, and he was not a bad man, which made the work easier and worse in equal measure.

Deel was a pay-corps sergeant stationed at the railhead, a widower of forty with a taste for cards and a debt at the estaminet behind the goods yard that had grown past what a sergeant's pay could quietly absorb. More importantly, six weeks earlier, drunk and resentful after a losing hand, he had written a letter to the provost marshal alleging — vaguely, drunkenly — that "certain parties at the forward dressing stations were circulating printed matter of a treasonous character." The letter had gone into a file and the file had gone into the colonel's growing stack, and Deel had sobered up terrified that his letter might someday be traced back to him.

Which meant, when Elias approached him — not accusing, never accusing, just a friendly fellow from the mail service who happened to know about debts and letters — Sergeant Deel was the most cooperative man in Flanders.

"He'll confirm anything we put in his mouth," Elias reported to Céline's back room, where the press sat silent under sacking and the lamp was hooded to show nothing through the shutters. "And better — he'll confirm it eagerly, because cooperating makes him valuable instead of culpable. All we have to do is build him a story the colonel will believe."

"The story being?" Céline asked. Her hands were stained black to the second knuckle; she never seemed able to wash all the ink away anymore, and Freya had stopped thinking of it as ink and started thinking of it as her son's name ground into her skin.

"That the pamphlets come through the estaminet," Freya said. "That Deel has seen couriers passing bundles there — civilians, Belgian smugglers, nothing to do with our people. That Tam was duped into carrying a package by one of these smugglers, whom Deel can describe. Vaguely. Every vague detail checks out because there's nothing to check."

"And the smuggler?" Elias asked. "When the colonel looks for him?"

"By then Tam's released, the trail's gone cold, and the colonel has something better than a frightened boy — he has a *network*. Smugglers, a drop point, a chain running back toward the coast. He'll chase that ghost for weeks." Freya paused. "I hate it. I'm building the man a fiction to hunt so he doesn't find the truth. But it buys us time to finish the edition and move the plates."

Céline said nothing for a long moment. Then: "In 1914 the Germans took our town and posted notices saying anyone who lied to them would be shot, and half the town lied to them every day, cheerfully, about grain and sons and rifles buried in gardens. Lying to power is a civic duty, girl. Print the sergeant's confession."

* * *

The confession was Elias's masterwork, and he hated every hour of making it.

Not a written document — too formal, too easy to question. Instead, three conversations, spaced across two days, each one letting Deel "remember" a little more, each one carefully witnessed. A drink with a military policeman who owed Elias a favor. A chance encounter on the platform with the colonel's own adjutant, during which Deel — coached, sweating, desperate to be useful — described the estaminet drop in trembling detail: the corner table, the man in a stevedore's coat with a Limburg accent, the brown-paper bundles passed under the bar.

"He's even got dates," Elias told Freya afterward, in the cold gap between the laundry tents where they'd taken to meeting because nowhere else was safe. "Dates when Tam was actually on the road. That's the beauty of it — we built the fiction around the true timeline, so any check against the duty rosters confirms him."

Freya stared at the frost forming on the canvas beside her cheek. "You've spent three days constructing a lie with the discipline of a surgeon closing a wound."

"I spent ten years before the war writing advertising copy for a firm in Rotterdam," Elias said. "Lying in bulk. This is the first time it's been for something."

But there was a shadow in it he didn't say aloud, because saying it would make it more true: the story was good. Too good. Men had hanged on thinner fictions, and the colonel was not a stupid man — he was a careful one, and careful men eventually noticed when the truth arrived too conveniently dressed.

* * *

The colonel received Sergeant Deel on a Tuesday morning, in an office that smelled of stove-iron and pencil shavings, and listened to him for forty minutes without once interrupting, which was his habit and his weapon.

Deel sweated. Deel remembered. Deel produced, when invited, the description of the Limburg man, the corner table, the bundles, the dates. He named the estaminet keeper as a witness who could corroborate "if approached gently, sir, he's frightened of the Germans across the line and frightened of us too, it's the whole coast that way, sir."

The colonel dismissed him with thanks and a promise that his cooperation would be "remembered," and sat alone for a long while after, turning his fountain pen end over end.

It fit. That was what bothered him. For three weeks he had been assembling a picture from fragments — casualty figures that wouldn't reconcile, a leak in the dispatch chain, a boy caught with seditious pages who claimed with tearful sincerity that he'd been given a sealed package by a stranger and told it was church newsletters for the chaplain. And now a witness walked in whose account slotted into every gap like a key cut for the lock.

Careful men distrust keys that fit perfectly. The colonel had learned that in India, in '09, breaking a gun-running ring whose informant had been too precise, too available, too eager — and had been, in the end, the ring itself, feeding them a rival gang.

But. He looked at the file. The boy was nothing — a mule-brained orderly, seventeen at most, whose terror was genuine and whose ignorance appeared equally genuine. If there was a network, the boy was its errand runner, and errand runners were replaceable and valueless. The value lay up the chain, and the chain, according to Deel, ran through the estaminet toward the coast.

Very well. Let them think he'd swallowed it.

He wrote two orders that morning. The first directed the release of Orderly T. Merrick, "no further evidence of complicity having emerged," with a note that the boy be returned to his unit and watched. The second — quieter, unnumbered, dictated to no clerk — authorized a discreet survey of all commercial paper stocks purchased in the district since October, and a list of every printing press, bindery, and stationer within twenty miles of Poperinghe.

Let them have their little victory. A net was not judged by the fish it missed. It was judged by how fine the mesh was woven.

* * *

Tam came back to the dressing station on a Thursday afternoon, walking beside a transport driver, thin and grey-eyed and trying very hard to look like a boy returning from ordinary leave.

Freya saw him from the triage table and made herself keep suturing — two more stitches, knot, scissors, *now* — before she crossed the mud to meet him, because a matron's ward was always watching and joy was as suspicious as grief.

"You're back," she said. Her voice came out steady. Inside, something enormous and wordless was pressing against her ribs.

"Yes, Sister." His salute wobbled. Up close she could see what three days in a provost hut had done: the rawness around his eyes, the way his hands wouldn't quite close, the new carefulness in how he held his body, as though he'd learned that bodies could be taken apart by questions.

"Merrick." Matron Cobb appeared beside them, brisk as weather. "You look half-starved. Report to the cookhouse, eat everything they give you, and then report to me for duty. Your kit's been kept in my office. Some things go missing otherwise."

"Yes, Matron." Tam's chin trembled. "Thank you, Matron. Thank you, Sister. I didn't — they kept asking who gave me the parcel, and I kept saying the truth, that a man at the crossroads gave it to me, and I thought—" He stopped, swallowed, mastered himself the way they'd taught him, badly. "I thought I was going to be shot. I'm sorry I cried, Sister."

"You have nothing to be sorry for," Freya said, and put her hand briefly on his shoulder, one nurse to her orderly, in front of everyone, meaning all of it. "Go and eat."

He went. And Freya stood in the churned mud with the ward noise rising around her — moans and orders and the kettle and the wind in the guy-ropes — and felt the victory land exactly as hollow as she'd known it would. They had gotten the boy back. They had fed the colonel a ghost. And none of it had changed the essential arithmetic, which was that somewhere a careful man was writing lists, and their names were moving slowly up those lists, and the edition was only half printed.

* * *

That evening the found family gathered in Céline's back room — Elias, Freya, Céline, and Matron Cobb, who had begun attending these councils with the calm of a general inspecting a front she privately expected to collapse. Iris was not there. Iris was on night duty, and the distance between her and Freya had grown wide enough now to have its own weather.

"Tam's safe," Elias said. "Deel's story holds. The estaminet keeper, when the MPs came asking, confirmed everything — because Deel got to him first and paid him with the same coin we paid Deel. Fear and gratitude. The whole fiction is load-bearing now."

"Then we celebrate," Céline said dryly, "by printing faster. Six signatures remain. At current pace, twelve days."

"The colonel released Tam too easily," Matron said.

Everyone looked at her.

"I've served under his type for thirty years," she went on, unpicking a thread on her cuff. "He didn't release that boy because he believed the story. He released him because a boy in a cell is worth nothing and a boy on the loose is worth everything — bait, or a thread to follow home. Mark me. From tonight, someone is watching Tam. Someone may be watching all of us. The noose hasn't loosened, friends. It's been measured."

Silence. The press clock ticked under its sacking. Somewhere out in the dark a train went through the rail junction, hauling guns north, and the windows buzzed faintly with its passing.

Freya thought of Tam eating in the cookhouse, gulping food with tears in his eyes, alive and free and watched. She thought of the forty-one addresses pinned inside Céline's cupboard, waiting for their names to reach them. Twelve days. Perhaps fewer.

"Then we finish it before the mesh closes," she said. "All of us. Whatever it costs."

Matron met her eyes across the lamplight, and for once said nothing at all — which, from Matron Cobb, was the loudest agreement Freya had ever heard.

None of them knew yet that the colonel's quiet second order had already left his desk that afternoon — already crossed to the paper merchant on Rue Basse, whose ledger, opened under polite pressure, contained one entry that mattered: sixty sheets of good stock, sold in November, to a widow named Dossin.
