## 25. The List Grows Teeth

The first copy came to her folded inside a soldier's pay book.

He was a private from the 9th Norfolks, brought in at dusk with a shrapnel wound through the forearm, and when Tam cut away his tunic and handed her the effects in their canvas bag, the pay book sat among them like any other — grey cardboard, swollen with damp. It was only when she opened it to check his number that she felt the second thing inside it, a paper folded to the size of a stamp, and unfolded it against her palm.

Eight pages. Small type. No title but a line at the top: *For the families of the forty-one.*

Her breath went out of her slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door closes somewhere far off.

She looked up. The ward stretched away under its low canvas ceiling, rows of cots, the blue gloaming of evening lamps, Sister Hargreaves making her round with the medicine tray. Nothing had changed. Everything had changed. Somewhere between Céline Dossin's press on Rue Basse and this boy's pay book, the pamphlet had grown legs. It had walked. It had traveled in pockets and haversacks and kit bags, passed hand to hand in the dark of leave trains, and now it lay open in her palm smelling of ink and coal smoke.

"Anything wrong, Nurse?" Tam asked, tying off the last of the bandage.

"No," she said. "Nothing."

It was the second lie she had told him, and it came easier than the first. That frightened her more than the pamphlet did.

* * *

She read all eight pages that night by the shielded light of her candle, the ledger open beside her, checking name against name. The type was crooked in places — set in haste, some letters standing proud of the line like teeth in a crowded jaw — but every one of the forty-one was there. Rank, regiment, date, the place where they fell: the open ground below the ridge, the positions the machine guns had held all along. And beneath them, in smaller type, the sentence Vane had written with his ruined eyes bent close to the page:

*These men did not desert. These men were ordered forward and were killed, and their deaths were entered as desertion to conceal the order that killed them.*

Below that, no signature. Only a printer's mark she recognized — a small lantern, cut into wood or lead, crude and unmistakable.

Freya sat for a long while with the pages in her lap, listening to the wind work at the tent ropes. She had imagined this moment, or something like it, back in the days when imagination still seemed safe — the truth loose in the world, moving without her, doing what paper does. She had thought it would feel like victory.

It felt like watching a lit match pass from hand to hand in a timber yard.

Because the pamphlet could not be stopped now, and neither could what came after it. Every copy was a small fire. Every man who read it and said nothing was still marked by having read it. And somewhere above them — in headquarters, in the colonel's office with its maps and its tidy columns of figures — someone had already noticed that the numbers no longer added up quietly.

She folded the pages small again, smaller than they'd been, and slid them into the hollow spine of the hymnal where the Lantern lived, and blew out her candle, and lay in the dark listening to Iris breathe across the aisle, slow and even and trusting, and thought: *it's begun, and we cannot call it back.*

* * *

By the end of the week, the murmurs reached the ward itself.

They arrived the way weather arrives — not all at once but as a change in pressure, a restlessness in the men that had no clinical name. Freya noticed it first in the long convalescent rows, the walking wounded who would soon go back or go home. They talked less about the war and more around it. Conversations stopped when officers passed. Men looked at each other across the aisles of cots and seemed to arrive at understandings without speaking.

On Thursday evening she caught a fragment. Two privates near the stove, heads together over a shared cigarette, and as she passed with the bedpan trolley she heard the younger one say, low: "—my cousin swears he saw it on the leave boat. Printed proper. Forty-one names, he said, and the order named besides—"

The older man hushed him with a look, quick and sharp, glancing down the ward — past Freya as though she were furniture — toward the duty sister's desk.

Freya kept walking. Her hands were steady on the trolley handles. Inside her chest her heart was going like a press run.

That night in the sisters' mess, the talk was of other things — a concert party due at the end of the month, the price of eggs in Poperinghe — but beneath it ran the same current. Matron Cobb sat at the head of the table mending a cuff, saying little, her needle going in and out, in and out. Once, when a young doctor made a joke about the censors and the letters they blackened, Matron's needle paused for half a stitch before continuing. Freya watched her and understood that Matron heard everything, weighed everything, and gave back nothing. It was her way. The ward ran on what Matron chose not to say.

Iris, across the table, caught Freya's eye and smiled, and Freya smiled back, and the smile cost her more than the lie to Tam had. Because Iris believed things were what they appeared to be. Because Iris's whole self was built on the idea that if you did your duty cleanly and loved the people beside you cleanly, the world would hold. And the world was holding less and less.

"You're quiet tonight," Iris said later, as they washed their hands at the basin, side by side, sleeves rolled, steam rising.

"Tired."

"You're always tired." Iris wrung out the flannel, wrung it again as though it had personally wronged her. "You know you can tell me anything, Freya. Whatever it is. I hope you know that."

"I know it," Freya said, and meant it, and hated that meaning it changed nothing.

* * *

The officers grew watchful the way cats grow watchful — no announcement, just a stillness that hadn't been there before.

It began with small things. A staff captain appeared at morning sick parade, unannounced, taking notes. The mail was late twice in one week, and when it came, several envelopes had been opened and resealed with a clumsiness that insulted everyone's intelligence. The adjutant began walking the wards at odd hours, hands clasped behind his back, pausing at bedsides to ask men questions that sounded friendly and weren't: *Had they received any printed matter lately? Any papers passed to them on the trains? Had anyone approached them with stories about the autumn advance?*

Most men said no. Most men meant it. Some men said no and meant otherwise, and Freya could see it in their faces — the careful blankness of soldiers who had learned which questions were traps.

Then, on the second Sunday of the month, the chaplain's service was moved. Instead of the usual open-air gathering behind the wards, the men were marched to it in formation and marched back the same way, and the sermon — Freya stood at the edge of it, hymnal in hand, feeling the ledger's weight inside it like a swallowed stone — the sermon was on the subject of rumor. Of the wickedness of tales. Of how the enemy sowed discord with lies dressed as truth, and how a good soldier's duty was to trust his officers and keep his mouth shut and his heart clean.

"A tale is not true because it names names," the chaplain said, his voice carrying flat across the parade ground. "A lie can carry a hundred names. Truth needs none. Remember whose voice you listen to."

Freya looked at the faces around her. Young, sunburnt, bandaged, attentive. Some of them nodding. One or two — the ones she suspected had seen the pamphlet — looking steadily at the middle distance with expressions she recognized from mirrors.

Afterward, as the men dispersed, an officer detached himself from the group by the gate and crossed to her. She knew him slightly: Captain Mowbray, intelligence section attached to the corps staff, a neat man with a neat mustache and eyes that never quite finished a sentence.

"Nurse Ashgrove." He touched his cap. "Matron speaks highly of your record-keeping."

"She's generous, sir."

"Not generous. Precise." He smiled. His teeth were very even. "We've had occasion to consult your admission registers. Exemplary work. It's remarkable, isn't it, how much depends on accurate records. Names, dates, numbers." He let the words sit a moment, like stones dropped in still water. "One hates to think of inaccurate records circulating. Confusing grieving families. Undermining confidence." Another smile. "You'd report anything irregular, I'm sure. Anything that came into your hands improperly."

"If anything did, sir."

"If anything did." He touched his cap again. "Good afternoon, Nurse Ashgrove."

She watched him walk away, neat as a ledger line, and understood that the word *irregular* had been chosen the way a surgeon chooses instruments.

That night she took the Lantern from the hymnal and read it entire, all the names from the beginning — Private Ellis who died asking her to remember him, and the ones after him, months of them, the small lights — and she found her hand was shaking, not with fear exactly, but with a fierce, cold clarity that felt like fear's older sister. *They are looking,* she wrote at the bottom of no page, only thought it, thinking being safer than writing now. *Let them look. The names are already out of my hands. They are in pay books and coat linings and the memories of cousins on leave boats. You cannot court-martial a rumor. You cannot shoot a number that won't add up.*

But she knew, even as she thought it, that they would try.

* * *

The leave train came through on Tuesday nights, and Elias came with the news on Wednesday.

He arrived at the dressing station as a wounded man — that was the arrangement, a sling and a plausible limp and a stretcher-bearer's nod — and she treated his arm, which needed nothing, while he spoke in a voice pitched under the sound of the generator.

"It's moving faster than we hoped." His eyes stayed on her hands, on the bandage she was winding unnecessarily tight. "Céline counted the distribution. Three hundred copies in ten days, and those are only the ones we know of. Leave trains to Boulogne and Étaples. The base camps. Two hospitals on the coast — a copy turned up in a chapel pew, if you please, tucked under a prayer book." Almost a smile. "Somebody has a sense of theater."

"And the army?"

"The army has noticed." The smile went. "Paper requisitions are being traced. Céline's supplier in the city was questioned on Saturday — he sold her stock under another name, but the quantity will show if they follow it back. There are men asking questions at the rail junctions. Not military police — worse. Plain clothes. They stand at the carriage doors watching hands, watching faces." He flexed his fingers inside the sling. "Vane says the next fortnight decides it. Either the edition gets wide enough that suppressing it becomes the scandal — too many copies, too many eyes, burning it makes them guilty — or they find the workshop first."

"And if they find it?"

Elias was quiet a moment. Through the canvas wall came the sounds of the ward settling for the night, a man coughing, the squeak of a trolley wheel, ordinary sounds, the sounds of a place that believed itself safe.

"Céline won't leave," he said finally. "I've told her. She says her son is in the type. You can't argue with a woman like that. So if they come—" He didn't finish it. He didn't need to.

Freya tied off the bandage, cut it, tucked the end. "Tell her," she said, "that if it comes to it, she's not to burn anything until the pages are out. Promise me you'll tell her. We didn't set that type so it could die in its own house."

Elias looked at her then, really looked, and whatever he saw made him nod slowly. "I'll tell her."

"There's something else," she said, keeping her voice level, her hands busy coiling the bandage roll. "An officer came to me. Intelligence. Mowbray. He asked about my records. About *inaccurate records circulating.* He wanted to see whether I'd flinch."

"Did you?"

"I'm a nurse. We don't flinch; we proceed." But her hands had gone still, and he saw it, and she saw him see it, and there was no use pretending anymore, not between the two of them, not after the flooded farmhouse and the letters and the winter they'd shared the weight of. "Elias. They're not searching blindly anymore. They're narrowing. Mowbray mentioned the admission registers — mine specifically. If they pull every station's books and compare them against the casualty returns, the discrepancies will point here. To this ward. To whoever kept records too carefully."

"That's a long way from a name."

"It's a shorter way than it was last week."

He was silent. Outside, the generator throbbed; a flare went up somewhere east of the ridgeline, hung, died. When he spoke again his voice was lower. "Vane wants to accelerate. Print the balance of the edition inside the week instead of the fortnight, distribute hard, and scatter the plates. He says speed is armor now."

"He may be right."

"He also may be signing all our death warrants faster." Elias rubbed his eyes with his free hand, and for a moment he looked very tired, tired the way she felt, tired down in the marrow where sleep doesn't reach. "Go carefully, Freya. Watch Iris. Watch everyone. The closer this comes to the surface, the more people have to decide what they are. And you can't choose for them."

"No," she said. "You can only decide what you are yourself."

She removed his sling herself, gently, and sent him back into the dark as a man with nothing wrong with him, and stood a moment in the doorway afterward watching the night swallow the road, thinking of three hundred copies moving through the dark like embers on the wind, thinking: *we lit this, and now the wind has taken it, and no one — no one — knows where it will land.*

* * *

She got her answer to that on Friday.

A sergeant of the Lincolns, forty years old, both hands bandaged to the wrists from a fuse accident, was wheeled in at midmorning, and by afternoon he was sitting up in the convalescent row, and by evening he was talking. Not to her — to the boy in the next cot, a nineteen-year-old gunner with a scalp wound — in the low, easy voice men used when they thought the nurses were beyond hearing or beyond mattering.

"—not just talk, lad. Paper. Properly printed. My mate had it in his greatcoat lining, sewn in, like contraband tobacco. Names of our own blokes. Fellows from the battalion." A pause; the rustle of a man shifting painfully. "My missus writes me the wives are passing copies round the village back home. Round the *village.* You understand? It's not a trench thing anymore. It's everywhere."

"They'll hang whoever printed it," the gunner said — not horrified; almost hopeful, in the way the young are hopeful about fire.

"Maybe. Maybe they'll hang a lot of folk first." The sergeant's voice dropped further, and Freya, changing a dressing two beds down, had to lean into her work to catch it. "But I'll tell you what I told my mate. Somebody finally put it in writing. Somebody said the dead didn't run. After all this time. Somebody said it in ink." A rough sound that might have been a laugh. "Ink's a strange thing, lad. You can burn a man, but ink — ink gets about."

Freya straightened, pressed the dressing flat, and moved on down the row, and her face was the calm professional mask it had been trained to be, and underneath it something was singing.

At the duty desk, Sister Hargreaves was frowning at the day book. "Odd," she murmured, half to herself. "Captain Mowbray's been round twice today. Asked for the admissions book both times." She glanced up at Freya, mild, incurious. "He seems very interested in numbers lately."

"Yes," Freya said. "So I've heard."

She signed the book where she was required to sign, and walked the ward's length under the evening lamps, past the cots and the men and the murmurs moving among them like water finding its level, and at the end of the aisle she paused by the window, where the dark had come down over the ridges and the guns had begun their distant, patient counting.

Three hundred copies. Wives in villages. Ink getting about.

And somewhere behind her, boots on the duckboards, unhurried, precise — the sound of the colonel's world closing its arithmetic around a single discrepancy, narrowing, narrowing, toward a hymnal on a shelf in a nurse's kit.

She did not turn around. There was nothing yet to see. But she stood at the window a moment longer than she needed to, committing the ward to memory — the lamps, the breathing, the whole warm body of the place that had become her family — the way you memorize a house before the flood comes.
