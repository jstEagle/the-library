## 26. Iris Chooses

Iris found the note on a Tuesday.

She would remember that afterward — the smallness of the day itself, the grey drizzle coming down over the ward in sheets, the smell of carbolic and wet wool, Tam whistling off-key while he restocked the splint basket. An ordinary Tuesday at the dressing station, except for the note, which she did not find so much as it found her: folded into the pocket of her spare apron where no note had any business being, written in a hand she almost recognized, addressed to no one and therefore, unmistakably, to Freya.

*Rue Basse. Thursday after midnight. The type is set. Come if you can. — E.*

She read it twice. The first time her eyes simply slid across the words without taking hold of them, the way a hand slides off wet stone. The second time they took, and took hard.

*Rue Basse.* She knew the street. Everyone in the sector knew it — the crooked lane behind the estaminet in Poperinghe where the Belgian widows sold lace and coffee substitute and, if you asked the right way in the wrong tone, other things. It was not a place nurses went alone at midnight. It was not a place anyone went at all, really, unless there was something to buy or something to hide.

And *E*. Elias Wren, with his careful face and his courier's walk, who came and went through the station like weather, whom Matron tolerated and Tam adored and Freya — Freya watched the door for, in those small unguarded seconds before she remembered herself and became again the composed Sister Ashgrove whom everyone trusted and no one quite knew.

Iris stood in the linen store with the note in her hand and felt the floor tilt very slightly under her, the way it had tilted the first time she'd seen a man's leg come off above the knee and learned that the world was held together by nothing but habit.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she had been circling for weeks the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

Freya was doing something.

It was in the nights first — the absences, excused with rounds and stores inventories that never appeared in the ledger book. Then in the smaller things: the way Freya's eyes went to the window when lorries passed; the hymnal she carried everywhere, even to supper, even though Iris had known her two years and had never once seen her sing; the new stillness in her when casualty figures were read aloud, a stillness like a woman counting something no one else could see.

Iris had told herself stories about it. That Freya was writing letters to a man. That Freya was ill and hiding it, as the best of them always hid things. That Freya was grieving someone from before the war and the grief had finally got its teeth in. All of them kinder than the truth, whatever the truth was, and all of them lies, and she had known they were lies, because she had been a nurse long enough to know what lying looked like from the inside — how it settled into a person's hands first, then their voice.

She had gone to Matron about it three weeks ago. She remembered standing in that little office that smelled of ink and camphor, saying, *I think Freya's mixed up in something, Matron. I don't know what. I think someone should watch her,* and hearing how it sounded as it left her mouth — like a betrayal dressed as concern, which was exactly what it was, though she'd have cut out her tongue before admitting it — and watching Matron Cobb look at her over the spectacles for a long, long moment.

*Iris,* Matron had said, in that voice like a flat iron pressed down on wrinkled linen, *there are two kinds of nurses in this war. Those who keep rules and those who keep people. Think carefully, before you tell me anything else, which one you're asking me to be.*

And Iris had thought — had thought all the way back down the corridor and all the weeks since — and had arrived, tonight, holding this note, at the place where thinking stops being possible and choosing begins.

* * *

She worked the evening shift as though nothing had happened. That was the discipline of the ward: grief, terror, revelation — all of it folded away into the body's muscle memory, hands moving through bandages and morphia doses and the endless arithmetic of pulse and temperature while the mind screamed quietly somewhere behind the eyes. She changed dressings on a gunner from Hull whose shoulder was a ruin. She held a boy of nineteen through the shaking hours after his foot came off. She wrote letters home for men who couldn't write, putting down *I am well and thinking of you all* in her round schoolroom hand, and thought: I have spent this whole war learning to write sentences that are kind and false. What is one more?

Freya came on duty at eight, hair pinned, sleeves rolled, that particular set of her shoulders that meant she hadn't slept. Their eyes met across the ward — across forty beds and the lamplight and the low moan of the man in bed twelve — and Freya smiled at her, quick and warm and real, and Iris smiled back and felt the note press against her ribs where she had transferred it to her uniform pocket like contraband, like a bullet she was carrying for someone else.

*Come if you can,* the note said. But the note wasn't for her. The note was proof.

That was the word she kept snagging on. *Proof.* She had enough now. A note naming a place and an hour; months of absences; a hymnal that never opened; a stranger named Wren; Tam running errands with a shine in his eyes like a boy carrying love-letters, poor lamb, not knowing what he carried; and underneath it all, since the inspection, since Colonel Hargreaves had walked the ward with his pale eyes ticking over faces like an auditor over accounts — underneath it all the knowledge that there was a leak in the casualty figures, that command was hunting it, that hunting was what Hargreaves did the way hounds did it, patiently and with pleasure.

She could take the note to Matron. No — she had tried Matron once and been turned away with a warning wrapped in wisdom. She could take it to the adjutant. To Hargreaves himself, even, directly, and be the good soldier she had been raised to be, the daughter of a churchwarden, the girl who had joined up because rules were the only thing holding the world together over the abyss. One envelope. One sentence: *I believe Nurse Ashgrove is passing information to persons outside the station.* By Friday Freya would be in custody, or worse, transferred beyond reach, and whatever machinery she had built would be seized and dismantled, and the station would go on, and Iris would go on, and everything would be clean.

Clean as a dissecting table, said a voice in her head that sounded uncomfortably like her own.

She stood at the ward window near midnight, watching the rain stitch silver threads down the black glass, and made herself look at it straight, the way she made herself look at wounds.

What did she actually know? Nothing solid. Suspicion, pattern, instinct. And what did she actually fear? Not that Freya was a spy — the idea was laughable; Freya Ashgrove couldn't lie about the state of the linen cupboard without flushing pink. What she feared was simpler and much worse: that whatever Freya was doing would get people killed. Patients. Tam. Matron. Herself. The whole fragile web of them, the family none of them had chosen and all of them needed more than blood, torn apart because Freya had decided some truth mattered more than the people breathing around her.

But — and here her mind balked, and circled, and came round again — what if it wasn't like that? What if the truth and the people were the same thing? She thought of Private Ellery, last autumn, the one whose name had never come through on the rolls, whose mother had written three times asking why the War Office said he was missing when Iris herself had held his hand as he died. She had signed the ward book. She had seen the entry vanish from the daily return like water into sand. She had told herself clerical error and believed it, mostly, the way you believe a roof holds until the night it doesn't.

Forty-one men, Céline Dossin's typesetting iron had burned into the rumour of the ward, recorded as deserters, dead in an advance no one would name. Iris didn't know where she'd heard the number. Rumour moved through a dressing station faster than blood through a body. Forty-one mothers writing letters that got no answer.

Her father's voice, in memory, Sunday-sermon steady: *Iris, the eighth commandment has no fine print. Thou shalt not bear false witness — not for kings, not for causes, not for love.*

And beneath it, older, from her mother's kitchen, flour on her hands: *Rules keep houses standing, love. People keep them homes.*

* * *

Wednesday passed like a held breath. Iris watched Freya across the ward with new eyes and hated herself for it — watched her count out morphia, comfort the Hull gunner, laugh at something Tam said, and every ordinary gesture now looked like concealment, the way a familiar room looks wrong when you've moved one picture frame.

Thursday came. Rain again, colder, with a wind off the flats that rattled the shutters. At eleven Iris finished her shift, walked the corridor to the nurses' quarters, and sat on her cot in the dark without lighting the lamp, the note in her lap.

The adjutant's office was fifty yards away. The guard post was closer. She had all night. She had, in fact, only to stand up.

Instead she found herself doing sums she didn't want to do. If she reported, what exactly did she report? A scrap of paper in another woman's pocket, which proved only that Freya received notes — receiving notes was not yet a crime, though Hargreaves would make it one fast enough. To make the report bite she would have to give him everything: the absences, the hymnal, the pattern of her suspicion, months of watching her closest friend the way she'd watch a septic wound. She would have to become, permanently and publicly, the woman who informed on Freya Ashgrove. And if she was wrong — if whatever waited on Rue Basse was half what the rumours whispered, a printing press, a true account, names — then she wouldn't be keeping rules. She'd be feeding forty-one erased men to the fire a second time.

And if she was right — if Freya was reckless, if the thing did blow up, if Tam or Matron or some patient paid for it — then silence would be hers to carry for the rest of her life, and she knew what carrying looked like. She saw it every day on the faces of men who had gone over the top beside brothers they couldn't save.

Either choice was a wound. There was no clean option. That was the discovery, the real one, arriving late and unwelcome: she had spent two years believing the ward was the place where choices were hard, when in fact the ward was simple. Bleed, bind, ease. Out here in the dark, morality was triage too, and sometimes every patient died.

Somewhere down the corridor a door closed. Boots went past toward the latrines. The wind worried the shutter like a dog at a bone.

Iris thought of Freya's face at supper, laughing at Tam. Thought of her hands, which had closed the eyes of four hundred men and never once trembled until recently. Thought of the hymnal, and understood suddenly — the understanding arriving whole, like cold water — that she had seen the shape of the thing weeks ago and refused to read it. A hymnal carried everywhere and never opened. A woman who had stopped singing. You didn't hide a love letter in a hymnbook. You hid a testament.

*Those who keep people,* Matron had said. And Matron — Matron had covered for her, Iris realized now, or at least had not stopped her, had sent her away with a parable instead of an inquiry. Which meant either Matron was careless, and Matron Cobb had never been careless in her life, or Matron already knew, and had chosen — was choosing, every single day, in every signature on every falsified-looking form she countersigned without question — to keep people.

The clock in the corridor struck twelve.

Iris rose. She did not go to the adjutant. She went to the stove at the end of the corridor, the little coke stove that kept the water hot for night tea, and it was banked but alive, a red seam glowing through the vent, and she knelt in front of it like a sinner at a rail.

She unfolded the note one last time and read it by the stove-light, giving it its due, giving *him* his due, whoever E was and whatever he was building on Rue Basse. *The type is set. Come if you can.* Come if you can — as though it were a wedding invitation and not treason. As though midnight were a friend's door.

Then she fed it to the stove.

The paper caught slowly, reluctantly, the way damp paper does — edges curling brown, then black, the handwriting blooming orange from within so that for one moment *Rue Basse* glowed like a lamp lit in a dark window — and then it was ash, curling, weightless, gone. Iris watched until the last fleck dimmed, and then she stayed kneeling a moment longer with her palms open to the small heat, feeling nothing like relief. Complicity, she thought. That's what this is. Not innocence, not ignorance. I am in it now. Whatever burns on Rue Basse, I warmed my hands at it.

She told herself it was love that had decided. Perhaps it was. Perhaps it was also the forty-one. Perhaps it was the memory of Private Ellery's mother's third letter, unanswered, and the shame that had lived in Iris ever since like a splinter too deep to draw. Love and truth had been enemies her whole tidy life, and here at the end of it — at thirty-one, at a coke stove at midnight in Flanders — she discovered they had been married all along, and she was only just meeting the household.

She banked the fire, went to her cot, and lay awake listening to the rain until the grey came up.

* * *

Friday morning, Freya found her folding bandages and touched her elbow — light, sisterly, the touch of two years of shared shifts and shared horrors — and said, "You look done in, Iris. Late night?"

"Couldn't sleep," Iris said. "Wind."

It was the first lie she had ever told Freya Ashgrove, and it went out of her smooth as silk, and she understood with terrible clarity that this was how it began for everyone — for Freya, for Matron, for all of them — one small merciful falsehood, and then the next, and then a whole architecture of them, load-bearing, until the day the structure came down or held.

Freya squeezed her arm and moved on down the ward, and Iris bent over her bandages and let her eyes burn briefly, privately, into the linen.

She had burned the note. She had said nothing. She was one of them now, God help her — keeper of people, carrier of secrets, complicit in whatever dawn was coming.

And it was coming. She felt it the way animals feel weather: in the way the officers' mess lights burned later each night, in the sound of Hargreaves's staff car grinding through the gate mud on Saturday morning, in the colonel himself pausing at the ward door, pale eyes moving over the nurses one by one — over Iris a heartbeat longer than the rest — before driving on toward town.

Toward Poperinghe. Toward the estaminet. Toward, perhaps, a certain crooked lane behind it where a widow kept a workshop that smelled of ink.

Iris stood in the doorway with a basin of bloody water in her hands and watched the car shrink down the road, and for the first time since the stove, she prayed — not for forgiveness, and not for courage, but simply: *be finished. Be finished before he finds you.*
