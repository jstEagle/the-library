## 27. The Colonel Closes In

Colonel Ambrose Sterne had learned long ago that the truth of an army lived not in its dispatches but in its paper — in the ledgers behind the ledgers, the requisitions behind the requisitions, the small honest arithmetic that no one bothered to lie about because no one thought it mattered.

It mattered now.

He sat in his billet at Hazebrouck with three sheets spread before him on a table cleared of everything else, as though they were maps of an offensive rather than scraps of printed sedition. Lamplight fell yellow across them. Outside, rain worked patiently at the shutters, and somewhere down the lane a mule complained at the dark.

The pamphlet had reached him by way of a chaplain's assistant who had found a copy folded inside a hymnal left on a leave train out of Poperinghe — which was itself instructive, Sterne thought. Even God's own books were being used to smuggle the thing now. The title page read *A True Account of the Advance of 9 October, Set Down for the Families of the Fallen*. No author. No printer's imprint, which was illegal under any number of statutes and also, he noted with something adjacent to respect, professionally careful.

He had read it four times. The first time as an officer assessing damage. The second time as a man. He did not permit himself a third reading as a man.

The account named the order — the advance across the open ground north of the Stroombeek against positions known to be held by machine guns, ordered at dusk when reconnaissance had already reported otherwise. It named the casualties: not the two hundred and six admitted in the dispatches, but four hundred and seven. It named forty-one men subsequently recorded as deserters, men whose bodies had been recovered from the field and buried without ceremony while their families received letters describing them as absent without leave. And it bore, at its close, a signature Sterne had hoped never to see again outside of a file marked *deceased*:

*Aldous J. Vane, Captain, late 7th Northamptons.*

"Dead men," Sterne said quietly to the lamp, "ought to have the decency to stay so."

But he was too old a hand at this to let outrage do his thinking. Outrage was what the pamphlet wanted from him; outrage made officers clumsy. What he required was method. The author could hide. The typesetter could hide. But paper could not. Paper had a birthplace, a price, a weight, a watermark, and a trail of invoices behind it like a bride's train.

He turned to the third sheet — a plain white stock, slightly warm in tone, heavier than army pulp. He tilted it toward the light. There. Faintly, like a secret told once and regretted: a watermark, a stylised lily crossed with a press-iron. Belgian manufacture. Not French, not British Army stationery stolen from some clerk's stores. Belgian.

That narrowed the world considerably.

* * *

In the morning he summoned Lieutenant Hask, his intelligence officer — a young man with a scholar's stoop and a bloodhound's patience, whom Sterne had chosen precisely because he looked like nothing so much as a harmless donkey seconded from Cambridge.

"Hask. How is your French?"

"Adequate, sir."

"Your Flemish?"

"Nonexistent, sir."

"Then you will learn enough of it in three days to buy paper." Sterne laid the sheet before him. "This stock. I want to know where it is sold, who buys it, and in what quantities. Start with the paper merchants in Poperinghe and work outward — Bailleul, Armentières, and every shop in Ypres that still has a roof. Printers, stationers, tobacconists, anyone who sells by the quire. Do not ask after pamphlets. Ask after paper. Men guard their opinions; they will chatter about stock all day."

Hask touched the watermark with one finger. "Belgian, sir?"

"Belgian. Which means our printer is local, or buying locally, which in either case means he is near, because nobody hauls paper twenty miles through shell fire for a hobby." Sterne leaned back. "And Hask — the widow Dossin question."

The lieutenant blinked. "Sir?"

"There is a woman on the Rue Basse in Poperinghe — Céline Dossin, widow, keeps what claims to be a jobbing print shop. Funeral notices, ration cards, menus for the officers' clubs. Her son served with a Belgian unit and does not appear in any roll I can find." Sterne watched Hask's face absorb it. "I am not telling you she is guilty. I am telling you she exists, and that coincidence is a luxury I stopped believing in at Loos. Put her third on your list, not first. If she is innocent, I want her cleared by evidence, not spared by sentiment. If she is guilty, I want her unaware of how closely we are looking until the morning we stop looking and start acting."

"Yes, sir." Hask hesitated. "And if we find the press, sir — the pamphlet names officers. Names above me. Possibly above you."

Sterne regarded him for a long moment. Above him. Yes. Major-General Sir Edmund Ravelle, whose advance order it had been, whose dispatch had converted four hundred and seven dead into two hundred and six, and whose patronage reached, as patronage did, all the way to men who signed things in London without reading them. Sterne had dined at Ravelle's table twice. Ravelle had laughed at his jokes both times, which Sterne had privately considered the most frightening thing about him.

"The army," Sterne said, "is not a court of law, Hask. It is an instrument, and an instrument cannot function while a piece of paper goes round telling every private soldier that his officers lie to his mother. Whatever we find on the Rue Basse, the charge will be sedition — undermining discipline in time of war, distribution of false and subversive statements calculated to prejudice His Majesty's forces. Sedition is clean. Sedition needs no inquiry, holds no witnesses against us, and lets a firing squad do what a tribunal would only muddy." He gathered the sheets together with two fingers. "We are not investigating whether the pamphlet is true. We are investigating where it sleeps at night."

Something flickered across Hask's face — quickly mastered, but not quickly enough. Sterne catalogued it and said nothing. Every instrument wobbled at first use.

"Three days, sir?"

"Three days. And Hask — if any nurse, orderly, or matron of the casualty clearing stations asks you what you're about, you are conducting a routine audit of stationery accounts. Which will even be true, in its way. I find the best lies are bookkeeping."

* * *

The bookkeeping came back faster than Sterne expected, and better than he dared hope.

On the second evening Hask stood before him with a notebook gone soft at the edges from rain and recited, in the flat voice of a man who had spent two days being lied to in two languages: "Paper merchant in Poperinghe, name of Verhaeghe, on the market square. Sells a stock called *lis et fer* — lily and iron, the watermark matches. Sells it to perhaps a dozen customers. Most take a quire or two a month. One customer takes a ream."

"One ream."

"Every fortnight since September. Collected, not delivered, by a boy — Belgian, fifteen or sixteen, comes on a bicycle. Verhaeghe says the boy pays cash and never haggles, which in his words makes him 'either an angel or a criminal.'" Hask consulted the notebook. "Verhaeghe would not say where the boy went. But my sergeant followed him Tuesday on foot as far as the canal bridge, where the boy made him plainly, and lost him in the fish market. However—" Hask paused, allowing himself, for the first time, the ghost of satisfaction. "However, sir. Before the fish market, the boy stopped. At the Rue Basse."

Sterne sat very still.

"He went in the side door of the Dossin print shop carrying nothing, and came out eleven minutes later carrying nothing, and rode away whistling."

Eleven minutes. Long enough to deliver a message, short enough to carry no paper. Sterne felt the shape of the thing assemble itself in his mind with the cold pleasure he otherwise took only from a well-drawn map: the widow printing, the boy couriering, the network threaded through the hospitals — because it must run through the hospitals, mustn't it, that was where the names came from. Someone inside the wards was feeding the machine. A sister, an orderly, someone with access to admission books and burial returns and the little dishonesties of the daily state.

He thought of the casualty clearing station at Dozinghem and its brisk, unremarkable Matron Cobb, whose paperwork was famously immaculate. Immaculate paperwork was itself a kind of clue. Honest records were messy; only concealment was tidy.

"And the quantities," Sterne said aloud. "One ream a fortnight. Five hundred sheets. At eight pages to the signature, say thirty-two pages to a full copy — that is a potential edition of some thousands, depending on the format. They are not printing a curiosity, Hask. They are printing an argument, and they intend to win it by weight."

"Then we should move quickly, sir."

"We will move precisely." Sterne rose and went to the window. Rain had stopped; the sky over Poperinghe was the colour of dishwater, and the road below ran silver with it. "Speed is what amateurs mistake for decisiveness. If we raid tomorrow on suspicion alone, we find a print shop, a widow, and perhaps a pamphlet or two — and the widow weeps, the newspapers hear of a Belgian woman persecuted, and the real edition is already on a train to Boulogne in somebody's kitbag. No. I want the whole animal. I want the distribution list. I want to know which ward, which nurse, which orderly, which matron. I want the names of every hand between that press and that leave train, because when this breaks — and it will break publicly, whatever Ravelle prefers — I intend to be the man who can produce a complete list of the conspirators. That list is worth more to me than the press."

"And the press, sir?"

"The press burns beautifully either way. Presses are replaceable. Networks are not." He turned back. "Draft my request to Division tonight. Authority to conduct a search and seizure of premises at the Rue Basse, Poperinghe, on grounds of seditious publication. Cite the pamphlet by exhibit number, cite the watermark trace, cite the courier. Keep Vane's name out of the written request."

Hask looked up sharply. "Out entirely, sir?"

"Entirely. If I write *the late Captain Vane* in an official document, then some staff officer must eventually explain why the late Captain Vane is alive to commit sedition, and that explanation will travel upward to men who would rather it didn't exist. Documents die in drawers, Hask. That is their great virtue. The raid happens on the strength of the paper trail alone — watermarks, couriers, quantities. Clean, provable, sufficient." He permitted himself a thin smile. "Let the dead man remain a clerical anomaly. Anomalies keep."

* * *

The request went up on the third day and came back endorsed within forty-eight hours, which was fast — fast enough to tell Sterne that someone above him had been waiting for exactly this permission and relieved to be asked. The endorsement carried a marginal note in a hand he recognised: *Proceed with discretion. No publicity prior to action.* Ravelle's adjutant's writing, which meant Ravelle's wish, which meant Ravelle had finally understood that the pamphlet was not going to exhaust itself like a bad rumour.

Discretion. Very good. Sterne had planned on discretion regardless.

He built the raid the way he built everything — backwards, from the moment of entry. Two military policemen in civilian clothes posted at either end of the Rue Basse from midnight. A party of six to the shop itself, entering at dawn, when the type would be set from the night's work and the previous day's output still drying — printers were creatures of rhythm, and rhythm was predictable, and predictability was death. A second party to the house behind. A third to watch the canal path, in case the boy came pedalling into the middle of it. Warrant drawn under the Defence of the Realm Act, articles so broad they could cover a search for anything from a printing press to a loaf of contraband bread.

And one more thing, which he wrote into the orders himself and underlined twice: *No papers to be destroyed or removed by any person during the operation. All documents to be preserved intact for examination.* Because a press proved printing; only the files proved the network. Somewhere in that shop or that house there would be a list — addresses, routes, names. There always was. Conspirators were sentimental about their own correspondence; it was the one vanity common to saints and traitors alike.

He reviewed the orders by lamplight on the fourth evening, initialled each page, and set the date: dawn, the sixth.

Then he sat for a while with the pamphlet open on his knee, reading it a fifth time despite himself, and let himself consider — briefly, in the privacy of his own skull, where no court-martial could follow — the possibility that every word of it was accurate. Four hundred and seven men sent across open ground at dusk against reported machine guns. Forty-one of them resurrected afterward as deserters to balance the arithmetic. He had seen the ground in October. He had seen the state of the wire and guessed at the guns behind it, and he had said nothing then either, because saying things was not what instruments did.

The difference between himself and the woman on the Rue Basse — and he was increasingly certain there was a woman at the centre of it, the lists were too tender, too full of mothers' addresses, to have been compiled by any soldier — the difference was that she had decided the truth mattered more than the army, and he had decided the opposite, and one of them was going to be proved wrong within the week.

He closed the pamphlet. Sentiment, he told himself, was just indiscipline wearing a black armband.

At nine o'clock a runner brought him a final signal from Hask, pencilled in haste:

*RUE BASSE. LIGHTS IN SHOP UNTIL LATE THREE NIGHTS RUNNING. BOY COURIER SEEN TWICE TODAY, RIDING HARD. THEY KNOW SOMETHING OR THEY ARE RUSHING AN EDITION. RECOMMEND ADVANCE OF DATE.*

Sterne read it twice. So. The animals had heard weather coming; they always did, in the end, and it changed nothing essential — only the hour.

He took out his pencil, struck through *the sixth*, and wrote *the fourth* beside it.

Dawn, the day after tomorrow. Let them rush their edition. An edition finished in panic would be an edition half-carried, half-hidden, half-lost — and every page they failed to move in time would be evidence, neatly stacked, waiting for him with the ink barely dry.

He rang for the duty officer and gave the new date in a voice as calm as a man ordering breakfast, and outside the rain began again on the shutters, patient as ever, falling equally on the Rue Basse and on everyone who meant to burn it down.
