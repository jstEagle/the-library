## 28. Nights of Setting Type

### Céline

The third night began the way the second had ended: with Céline Dossin's fingers bleeding into the type.

She did not notice at first. One worked by touch in the dark hours, and the case before her was a country she had walked blind since girlhood — her father's shop in Liège, his hands over hers on the composing stick, *lowercase to the left, mon enfant, always*, as though typesetting were a catechism. Now the stick was cold in her grip and the candle burned low and the letters came to her like old friends arriving one by one out of fog: the e's worn smooth from use, the k's still sharp because no one in this language ever needed enough of them.

It was only when a name smeared red beneath her thumb that she understood.

*Verhaegen, Michel.*

Her son's name, set in twelve-point, waiting to be inked, waiting to be read by strangers who would never know that the boy behind it had once hidden frogs in her flour bin and sworn the war would be over before he was tall enough to be conscripted. He had been wrong about the war and right about himself; he was never tall enough. The shell that took him had left nothing for the registry but a number, and the number had been struck through by some clerk in some office, and so Michel Verhaegen had ceased officially ever to have existed at all.

Céline looked at the blood on the letter and did not wipe it away.

"Ink," she said aloud, to the empty room, "is ink."

Then she wiped it away after all, because the colonel's men would examine every page they seized, and blood was evidence, and evidence was the one thing Rue Basse could no longer afford. She bound her fingertips with strips of bedsheet torn that morning, wrapped each finger like a tiny wounded soldier, and went back to the case.

Above her, through the floorboards, she could hear them: the English nurse reading the casualty lists in her flat, careful voice, and the courier boy copying addresses, and Elias Wren walking the perimeter of the building for the hundredth time, his boots making a soft patient rhythm overhead like a heart that refused to hurry. They were all of them running now. Even their restlessness ran.

Two thousand copies. That was the edition. Vane's testimony, the forty-one names with their regiments and their dates and the single sentence that explained everything — *reported deserter, killed in action, never reported dead* — repeated until it became not an accusation but a drumbeat. Two thousand copies, and the press could manage six hundred a night if the forme held, if the paper stock didn't run out, if the gendarmes stayed drunk on their own patience one more night.

Three nights. Perhaps four. Everyone in the building did the same arithmetic and no one said it aloud, because saying it aloud made the number smaller.

Céline set the next line, and the next, and somewhere past three o'clock she stopped being a woman at a case and became simply a pair of hands serving a purpose older than the war: the purpose of putting marks on paper so that the dead could not be unmade. Her father would have understood. Her father had printed forbidden pamphlets against the Kaiser's grandfather and died respectable and poor, and had told her once, wiping ink from her cheek with a rag gone grey with a lifetime of the trade:

*A press is a lamp, Céline. Everything else in this world can be argued with. Ink cannot.*

She worked until her wrapped fingers darkened through the cloth, and when the candle guttered she lit another without looking up, and when the sky beyond the shutters went from black to the colour of wet slate she allowed herself ten minutes in the chair by the stove, where she slept sitting upright, dreaming of frogs in a flour bin.

### Freya

Freya Ashgrove had learned to sleep in dressing stations, which meant she had learned to sleep anywhere, but sleep would not come to her on the cot Céline kept behind the paper racks. Instead she lay listening to the house breathe — the tick of cooling metal from the press room, Tam's snoring from the loft, the creak of Elias's rounds below the window — and she thought about names.

Forty-one families. She had read every address aloud twice now while Céline set the lines, and something had happened between the first reading and the second that Freya could not undo. The first time, they had been entries: *Private A. Merrick, 7th Battalion, mother at 14 Corve Street, Shrewsbury.* The second time, they were kitchens. She could see the kitchens. She could see the women standing in them receiving telegrams that called their sons deserters — *we regret to inform you* — and then receiving nothing further, ever, because the army had finished with them, having used them first as ammunition and afterward as cautionary tales.

Her own ledger lay beneath her, sewn now into the ticking of the cot itself, because Iris had gone through her kit once and might again. Every name she'd recorded at the station was copied into the pamphlet's final signature. The Lantern had become a page. The page would become two thousand pages. It was the closest thing to resurrection she knew how to perform.

On the third night she gave up on sleep and went down to help.

"You should rest," Céline said, not looking up.

"So should you."

"I am resting." Céline lifted her bandaged hand. "You see? Both feet up."

They divided the work the way tired people do, wordlessly: Freya read the copy aloud in a low even voice while Céline composed, because a reader freed the compositor's eyes, and Freya's voice, trained across a year of shouting over artillery to wounded men, could carry sense into a half-lit cellar without waking the boy upstairs. Name, regiment, date, sentence. Name, regiment, date, sentence. It was liturgy. It was the only church either of them attended anymore.

Halfway down the third column Freya faltered. Not on a hard name — on an easy one. *Ashworth.* A boy from the advance who had died holding her hand and asking whether God took messages without a chaplain.

"Continue," Céline said gently. "Grief later. Type now."

"Grief later," Freya agreed, and continued, and hated herself a little for how easily the agreement came, and understood that this too was devotion — the willingness to be a machine for a few hours so that a lamp could keep burning.

Near dawn Elias came down, and Freya saw at once that the news in his face was bad, and worse than bad: it was *specific*. He stood in the doorway turning his cap in his hands, and he said, "Paper merchant on the Vieux Marché sold our weight to someone Tuesday. Someone asking after Belgian printers who buy more stock than a shop front justifies."

"The colonel?" Freya asked.

"Or one of the officers the pamphlet names. Either way." He set the cap down. "We print faster or we don't print at all. I've sent word for Tam to bring the second crate from the cellar tonight instead of Thursday."

"Tam shouldn't—"

"Tam volunteered." Elias's voice was quiet. "He wants to matter. God forgive us, we let him."

Freya thought of the boy's face whenever he was trusted with anything heavier than a bedpan — the shining earnestness of him, the way he straightened his spine as though importance were a uniform he was finally being issued. She thought: *he is carrying our deaths around in a crate and calling it an errand.*

"Then we work," she said, and pulled the candle closer, and read the names until her throat was raw.

### Elias

Elias Wren did not believe in omens. He believed in ledgers, timetables, and the observable habits of military police, and by all three measures Rue Basse had perhaps seventy-two hours left as a functioning conspiracy.

He spent the fourth night doing what he did best, which was nothing visible. He walked. He counted lorries. He noted that the gendarmerie post near the canal had doubled its sentries and that neither of them drank from the flask anymore, which meant discipline, which meant orders, which meant soon. He mapped in his head the three routes out of the quarter — canal path, coal yard, the butcher's alley behind Sainte-Waudru — and assigned each a man and a contingency, and then sat on the loading step of the workshop and felt the peculiar loneliness of the planner: everyone else got to carry something. He only carried the map of everything going wrong.

The door opened behind him. Céline, wrapped in a shawl, pressing a cup of chicory into his hands.

"You'll wear a groove in my step," she said.

"It's a good groove."

They sat in silence a moment. Down the street a dog barked twice and stopped, and both of them listened to the stopping longer than to the barking.

"My husband used to sit exactly there," Céline said. "Smoking, before you all arrived and taught me disapproval of smoking. He said a printer should know his street better than his own face." She turned the shawl-collar up against the damp. "He died in the first month. Walking to this shop. A shell does not care what a man knows about his street."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be accurate." She nodded toward the interior, toward the sound of Freya's low voice reciting the roll of the erased. "How many remain?"

"Eleven signatures. If the forme holds and nothing goes wrong, we finish tomorrow night."

"And if something goes wrong?"

Elias drank the chicory. It was terrible. It was the best thing anyone had given him in months. "Then the pages that exist go out sewn into whatever fabric will hold them, and the rest becomes ash, and ash cannot testify."

Céline considered this with the composure of a woman who had already buried one war's worth of everything.

"No," she said. "Ash testifies fine. Ask any widow." She rose, gathered his empty cup, and paused at the door. "Wren. When they come — and they will come — save the type. Plates can be recast. My father's type has survived three governments." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Let the bastards burn the paper. We will print again."

Inside, the voice went on. *Name, regiment, date, sentence.* Elias sat on the good groove in the step and let the roll call wash over him like rain, and permitted himself, for exactly one minute, to believe they would finish.

### Tam

Upstairs in the loft, Tam Ollerenshaw was not sleeping either, though for entirely different reasons. He was reading.

He wasn't meant to have a copy — Elias had been very clear about circulation — but a proof sheet had come off the press crooked and been destined for the scrap box, and Tam had rescued it the way he rescued things: instinctively, guiltily, with the conviction that broken things deserved custody. Now he sat cross-legged in the straw with the candle shielded behind his kit bag, reading the same paragraph for the sixth time.

*...that on the morning of the 9th, the order to advance having been given against positions known to be manned, the casualties numbered not the sixty-one officially reported but upwards of four hundred, and that the discrepancy was resolved not by correction but by designation, the dead being entered as absent, and the absent as deserters...*

Tam read it and reread it and could not make his mind sit still inside it. Sixty-one. Four hundred. He had carried stretchers. He knew what four hundred looked like; he had helped with barely a tenth of that number once and hadn't slept properly for a week. Somebody had written down sixty-one. Somebody had dipped a pen and drawn a line under four hundred men and written *sixty-one*, the way you'd correct an invoice.

And underneath the horror, unwelcome as a stone in a boot, sat another feeling, and Tam was ashamed of it and could not evict it: pride. His name was nowhere in the pamphlet. But he had carried its paper. He had hauled crates through checkpoints with the pages pressed under sacks of lint, whistling past sergeants, heart hammering, and every mile of it had mattered — he could see now, holding the proof, exactly how much it had mattered. For the first time since he'd lied about his age to get into uniform, Tam Ollerenshaw was not a boy fetching things. He was part of a sentence. Four hundred men had been erased, and he, Tam, seventeen years old and terrified of the dark, was one of the small stubborn lights refusing to let the erasure stand.

He folded the proof sheet carefully and hid it inside the false bottom of his kit, which was not a real false bottom, merely a flap he'd invented and prayed no one inspected.

Below him, the press clattered. He could feel it through the floorboards, a great patient animal chewing through the night. Tomorrow he'd take the tram out to the depot for the last crate of stock — Elias had said tomorrow, and tomorrow Tam would say yes before Elias finished asking, because that was who Tam was, apparently, now. A boy who said yes.

He pinched out the candle and lay back in the straw, listening to the press work below, and slept at last, deeply, the sleep of the useful, while four streets away in a billet behind the ramparts a staff officer wrote a requisition for transport and marked it: *priority, dawn, discretion.*

### Céline, Again

The fifth night, the forme cracked.

It happened at eleven o'clock, in the middle of the thirty-first signature, a split in the wooden furniture that let the whole locked block of type shift a hair's breadth to the left, and Céline knew it before she saw it — knew it in her wrists, in the sudden wrongness of the impression, the way a nurse knows a pulse has changed. She cried out — one short syllable in Flemish that Freya correctly translated as a curse upon all carpenters living and dead — and they pulled the forme apart and found the damage, and found also that there was no spare furniture of the right length, because of course there wasn't; the war had taken spare everything from the whole continent.

"We wedge it," Céline said. "We wedge it and we print slow and we pray it holds for six hundred."

"With what?"

Céline looked around the workshop with a printer's eye, inventorying the world as raw material. Her gaze settled on the rack of old poster boards, and then on the broom, and then, finally, on the frame of the window shutter she had replaced in peacetime and never fitted properly because her husband had been the joiner and she had been the one who handed him things.

"The shutter," she said. "Bring me the shutter."

They sawed it by candlelight, Tam holding the wood and Céline guiding the saw with a steadiness that made Freya think of surgeons, and the pine smelled impossibly of ordinary life — of Sundays and paint pots and a man long dead measuring twice — and none of them spoke while the blade bit, because it felt like a funeral for something, though none of them could have said what.

By midnight the forme was wedged and locked and true. By one, the press was running. By three, Céline's bandages had soaked through again and Freya had rewrapped them without either woman breaking the rhythm of the work — read, set, lock, pull, stack; read, set, lock, pull, stack — and Tam ferried stacks to the drying racks and came back for more with his face lit from within, and Elias stood watch at the shuttered window counting the hours aloud in his head and telling no one.

At some point near dawn, Freya stopped reading and simply sang — very quietly, a hospital hymn, the one they sang when a man was going and everyone pretended it was about heaven rather than about leaving — and Céline set type to the tune of it, her bleeding fingers moving in time, and the press stamped out its six hundredth sheet as the light came grey through the slats.

Eleven signatures remained. Then nine. Then, as the sun cleared the rooftops and made the dust in the air golden and ordinary, seven.

Céline Dossin looked at her hands, ruined and steady, and at the racks of drying pages — Michel's name on every one, multiplied into a congregation — and felt something she had not felt in three years. Not hope. Hope was for people with futures. It was nearer to satisfaction, the craftsman's cold clean joy of a job nearing completion, and she held onto it the way you hold a coal in cupped hands.

Outside, far off toward the ramparts, an engine started.

Just one engine. Lorries idled all over Ypres all day; there was nothing in the sound, nothing at all.

But Elias crossed the room in four strides and put his ear to the shutter, and Freya saw his shoulders change, and she knew — the way she'd known pulses, the way Céline had known the forme was cracked — before he turned around and said, quietly, in the voice of a man cancelling sleep forever:

"They're mustering. We have one night, maybe less. Wake the boy. Nobody sleeps tomorrow."
