## 29. Vane Comes Out of Hiding

The farmhouse had a name once — Ferme Sainte-Odile, stencilled in fading white on the byre door — but the war had eaten it down to its bones and given it back to the water. The flooded pasture beyond the broken windows lay flat as hammered pewter, and in it stood three dead trees, black and patient, like men waiting for an order that would never come.

Aldous Vane sat among them in his own fashion: propped upright on a chair Céline Dossin had sent out from the city, wrapped in two army blankets that smelled of camphor and mildew, his ruined left eye turned toward the window because he could no longer turn it away.

He was listening to Freya read.

She had come out at dusk with Elias, across the duckboards and through the willow scrub, carrying the first finished signature of the pamphlet inside her coat, against her heart, where she carried everything now. She read aloud because Vane's right eye could no longer manage small print, and because, she had learned, the words were easier for him when they arrived in a living voice — as though the names needed breath before they could be believed.

"—Private Daniel Okafor, 2nd Battalion, listed as missing, believed deserted, 14 October. His mother keeps a shop in Cardiff. We have written to her. Private Thomas Merrick—"

"Stop." Vane raised a hand. The hand trembled; he hated it and hid it inside the blanket when he remembered to. "Read the last line again. The one about the order."

Freya found it. "'At nineteen-forty hours, the battalion advanced across open ground toward the Pilckem ridge, in full view of established machine-gun emplacements, under orders issued by—'"

"That one." His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "That is the sentence they will kill me for. Not the names. Names are sad; sadness is forgivable. That sentence is arithmetic, and arithmetic convicts."

Elias Wren stood by the cold hearth with his arms folded, a man built of angles and patience. "Then we don't put your name on it."

"You've said that every week since October."

"And you've refused every week since October. I'm saying it again so my conscience is clean."

Vane smiled — a terrible, crooked thing, half of his face refusing the invitation. "Your conscience is spotless, Wren. It's the only part of you that never gets wet." He turned his good eye to Freya. "Read me the rest. All of it. I want to hear whether it stands up without me."

She read until the light failed and Tam — who had insisted on coming, who was learning the routes now, eager as a young dog — lit the storm lamp behind a screen of sacking. She read the forty-one names, and then the second list, the one still growing, and then the paragraphs Céline had set in type so carefully that not a single letter leaned wrong. Outside, the water in the pasture caught the last grey light and held it, and somewhere far off the guns spoke their dull evening syllables, and none of it stopped.

When she finished, the room was quiet enough to hear the lamp burning.

"Well?" Elias asked.

Vane was silent a long time. Then: "It stands up without me." He said it like a man reporting a death. "That's the trouble, Wren. It doesn't need me anymore. A document that doesn't need its author can be dismissed by him — disowned, denied. They'll say I was mad, or bought, or never wrote a word of it. But if I stand beside it—" he pushed the blankets aside, and Freya saw how thin his wrists had become, bird-bones under grey skin, "—if I stand beside it in daylight, with my name and my rank and what's left of this face, then dismissing it means dismissing a witness they themselves declared dead. And a dead man's testimony is very hard to call treasonous. You can't shoot me twice. Legally speaking."

"They'll manage," Elias said dryly.

"Yes," Vane agreed cheerfully. "They'll probably manage beautifully. But they'll have to do it in front of witnesses. That's all I'm asking for, Wren. Witnesses."

* * *

They argued until midnight. Freya kept score without wanting to.

Elias argued strategy: a signature could be forged; a face at the press could be followed; one man's courage could burn down everything forty people had sewn into their hems. He argued with the flat patience of a man who has buried arguments before and expects to bury this one too.

Céline Dossin, who had come out that night to deliver the second signature, argued grief. She stood in the doorway of the farmhouse kitchen with rain still dripping off her shawl, a small woman with ink permanently mapped into the creases of her knuckles, and she said, "My son is page four. Page four does not need a captain standing over it. It needs to reach his aunt in Liège before spring. If you come to the Rue Basse, you bring the colonel to the Rue Basse, and then there is no spring for any of us." Her voice did not rise. It never rose. It simply went through you like a compositor's rule.

And Vane answered them both the same way, again and again, with variations, the way a man taps a wall listening for hollow places.

"A record hidden is a rumor. A rumor dies with the people who hold it. I have spent fourteen months in this ditch watching my own obituary get rewritten — first hero, then casualty, then cautionary tale. Do you know what they wrote about Passchendaele, in the dispatches? *Local difficulties overcome.* Forty-one men became local difficulties." He laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "I am done being overcome quietly."

It was Freya who ended it, though she didn't plan to. She only said what she had been carrying since the ward, since the Lantern's first page, since a dying private named Wilfred Acheson had gripped her wrist and asked her to say his name back to him so he'd know it hadn't already been erased.

"You told me once, in a letter, that memory was the only country left worth defending." She looked at the fire, not at him. "You can't defend it from a ditch, Captain. I know. I tried. I kept my ledger hidden for a year and it changed nothing except me. The names didn't get braver because I was careful with them. They just got heavier."

The silence after that was the kind that means a decision has already happened and everyone is simply waiting for the man concerned to catch up.

Vane reached for the lamp and lifted it, holding the flame close to his own face, the way he might have once held a match to a map. Half of it was ruin — the scarred eye, the shrapnel-ruddied cheek, the beard grown wild over a jaw that had gone hollow. The other half was still, unmistakably, the officer whose letters had made a nursing student in Kent believe that the war could be answered with precision and honor.

"Courage isn't what I thought it was in 1914," he said. "I thought it was going over the top. Loud. Measurable. Decoratable." He set the lamp down. "It turns out it's much smaller than that. It's letting yourself be seen precisely when being seen will cost the most. Everything else — the charges, the advances, the medals — that's just fear wearing uniform." He looked around the room, at each of them in turn. "Tomorrow night I come to the Rue Basse. I sign the testimony in my own hand, in front of whoever is there, and I walk out the front door afterward like a man with nothing to hide, because hiding is the one thing I refuse to do ever again."

"Céline will never allow it," Elias said.

"Céline," said Vane, "printed her son's name on page four knowing exactly what it would cost her. She'll allow it. Grief always recognizes courage; it just wishes it had come sooner."

* * *

He did not sleep that night, and neither did Freya.

She found him near dawn in the byre, sitting on an upturned crate among the smell of old straw and rusted iron, watching the flooded field turn from pewter to pearl. He heard her footsteps on the cobbles and didn't startle — a year underground had tuned him like an instrument.

"Nurse Ashgrove." He pronounced it carefully, formally, the way he had in his letters, and she realized with a jolt that the formality was tenderness. "Or should I say Vesper?"

"Not yet," she said quickly.

"No." He almost smiled. "Not yet. But soon, I think. A name kept in the dark too long goes rancid. Ask me how I know." He patted the crate beside him. She sat. The cold came up through the wood into her bones.

For a while neither of them spoke. The dead trees stood in the water, and the sky did what Flanders skies do, which is make even dawn feel like a concession rather than a promise.

"I owe you a confession," Vane said finally. "In my letters, during your training — the ones Elias showed you. I wrote to you as though I were whole. As though the war were a problem that precise men could solve with accurate reports and moral clarity." His good eye stayed on the water. "I wasn't whole even then. I'd already seen the machinery. What I couldn't do was say it plainly, because saying it plainly meant becoming what I am now — a man in a ditch with one eye, writing pamphlets nobody official will ever admit to reading." He turned to her then. "So I wrote beautifully instead. Beauty was my desertion."

"It wasn't," Freya said. "Those letters are why I started the Lantern. Whatever you intended, Captain, they landed in a dressing station and became defiance. Words don't get to choose what they grow into."

He absorbed that slowly, the way damaged men absorb everything, one grain at a time. "Then we're even," he said. "Your ledger is why I'm walking into that workshop tomorrow instead of staying here until the water takes the house and then takes me. I read your entries, you know. The ones Wren copied out for me. Forty-one names, cross-checked against lists I bled for." His voice roughened. "Do you understand what you did? I had evidence. Evidence is paper. Paper can be burned. But you — you had *witness*. Every name in your book, you saw the man die. That can't be burned. It can only be carried." He shook his head. "I spent fourteen months thinking I was building this thing alone. I was building it alone. You were building the other half."

"The halves meet tomorrow," she said.

"At a typesetter's bench, God help us." He laughed — really laughed this time, briefly, and for a moment he was the man from the letters, and the ditch fell away. Then it settled back over him like the blanket. "Ashgrove. If tomorrow goes badly—"

"Don't."

"—if tomorrow goes badly," he continued, inexorable, "you finish it. Not Wren — he'll be running routes. Not Céline — she's paid enough. You. You're the only one who knows all the names by heart, and I've watched you recite them in the dark like a rosary. Promise me the record gets out even if everyone carrying it falls."

Freya looked out at the water, at the trees standing their eternal guard, and understood that she was looking at her own future in miniature: upright, exposed, unyielding, drowned ground holding its shape anyway.

"I promise," she said. "But it won't go badly."

"Why not?"

"Because," she said, "for the first time since I opened that ledger, every single person involved has chosen this. Iris chose it by burning a note. Matron chose it years ago. Tam chose it by volunteering. Céline chose it the day she set her son's name. Nobody left is doing this out of accident or obedience." She met his eye — the good one, and then, deliberately, the ruined one too, because he deserved to be seen whole. "That's stronger than any plan, Captain. Plans fail. Chosen things don't break the same way."

Vane was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, softly, "Write that down. It belongs in the book."

* * *

The next evening they moved him in an ambulance.

Not a stolen one — Tam had fixed that, with the guileless efficiency of youth, by telling the transport sergeant that a stretcher case needed transferring to the casualty clearing station and that he'd personally see to it. The sergeant, drowning in paperwork, waved them through. Dramatic irony rode in the passenger seat beside Tam the whole way, and he never noticed it, humming, checking the straps twice because he was thorough about the things that mattered to him.

Inside the ambulance, under a tarpaulin, between two genuine stretchers bearing genuinely wounded men who had been paid in cigarettes and sympathy to sleep through the journey, Aldous Vane lay very still and rehearsed.

Freya rode with him. Through the canvas above her head she could see the lights of Ypres sliding past, the broken skyline of the Cloth Hall against a bruised sunset, and beneath her hands she could feel the tarpaulin rising and falling with his breath — quick at first, then slower, slower, until he had mastered himself the way he must once have mastered a company before an advance.

"Say it again," he murmured. "The order of proceedings."

"Elias meets us at the Rue Basse. Céline unlocks at nine. You sign the testimony — all six pages, witnessed by myself and Céline. Then you stand at the door, in the open, under the lamp, for anyone passing to see. Ten minutes. No more. Then the ambulance returns you."

"And if the ten minutes become eleven?"

"Then I drag you out by your collar," Freya said, "and I am surprisingly strong for someone who lifts wounded men all day."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "You know what's strange? I'm not afraid." A pause. "No — that's a lie, I'm afraid constantly, I've been afraid for fourteen months, fear is the wallpaper of my existence now. But underneath it, underneath all of it, there's a floor. And the floor says: whatever happens after tonight, the record exists. It's printed. It's in thirty uniforms heading for four countries. They cannot unprint it. For a year I thought I had failed — that surviving Passchendaele was the failure, that I should have died with my men and let the story stay clean. Tonight I stop thinking that." His hand found hers in the dark and gripped it, bird-bones and iron. "Surviving was the assignment, Ashgrove. Witnessing is the mission. I finally have my orders."

The ambulance slowed, turned, stopped. Footsteps. A knock — two, then one, then two: Elias's rhythm. The doors opened onto lamplight and rain-smell and the narrow gullet of the Rue Basse, and Céline Dossin stood in the workshop doorway with her sleeves rolled and her ink-stained hands at her sides, looking at the ruined captain the way a compositor looks at a line of type that has finally come out true.

"You're late," she said. "The case has been waiting an hour."

"So has the truth," Vane replied. "But it walks slowly these days."

Inside, the workshop smelled of ink and oil and candle wax, and the press waited like an altar. On the bench lay the final signature — the testimony pages, blank where the signature belonged. Six pages. Sixteen months of horror, set in twelve-point Baskerville by a widow's bleeding fingers.

Vane stood before them without help. He declined the chair. He took the pen Céline offered, dipped it, and paused — not from doubt, Freya saw, but the way a man pauses at a grave to make sure he has said everything.

"Aldous Edmund Vane," he said aloud, spelling it as he wrote, each letter a footfall. "Captain, 2nd Battalion. Officially killed in action, October 1917. Presently alive, presently accountable, and presently stating:" — the pen moved through the flourish of the final e — "*that every word herein is true, and that I will answer for it, in any court, before any tribunal, or before God, whom I expect to be more reasonable than the Army Council.*"

The scratch of the nib was the loudest sound in Flanders.

Then Freya stepped forward, and beneath his name she wrote her own — not Ashgrove, not yet Vesper, but *Freya Mary Ashgrove, witness* — and Céline pressed her thumbprint beside it in ink, and Elias signed last, fast and slanting, like a man leaving a room he intends to return to.

Four signatures. The lamp above the door burned steady. Somewhere out in the city, a church bell counted the hour, and Aldous Vane walked to the open doorway and stood beneath it, visible to the whole street, straight-backed as a flagpole, letting himself be seen.

Ten minutes. Freya counted them against her heartbeat.

At minute eight, a boy came running down the Rue Basse with rain streaming off his cap, and Freya knew before he spoke — she knew by the particular quality of panic in a messenger's legs — that the world outside the lamplight had already begun to move.

"Soldiers," the boy gasped, clutching his sides. "At the end of the street. Asking questions. Lots of them."
