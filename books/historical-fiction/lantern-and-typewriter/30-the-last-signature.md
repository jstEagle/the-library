## 30. The Last Signature

The farmhouse smelled of lamp oil and wet plaster, and by the time we finished, it smelled of ink.

Céline had brought the press's proof copy wrapped in oilcloth beneath her coat, and now it lay open on the trestle table like a patient prepared for surgery — the full signature of the pamphlet, forty-eight pages set and pulled over three sleepless nights, every line squared, every name spelled true. Elias stood at the shuttered window with his ear turned to the road. Tam sat on an upturned crate with his hands pressed flat against his knees, the way he did when he was trying very hard to be a soldier instead of a boy. Matron Cobb had not been invited. She had come anyway, and no one had thought to be surprised.

Aldous Vane sat at the table's head in a chair Céline had dragged from her kitchen, and he looked, I thought, like a man being fitted for his own monument.

He had dressed for it. That was the thing that undid me first. He wore his captain's tunic — faded almost to the color of ditchwater, the buttons gone dull, one shoulder seam let out badly to accommodate what the shrapnel at Passchendaele had left of him — but he had brushed it, and he had shaved, clumsily, a nick healing at his jaw, and someone — Elias, I suspected — had pinned his ribbon back on straight. He had spent a year refusing to exist, and tonight he had chosen, deliberately, to put his own name back on like a coat.

"Read it to me once more," he said. His voice had a rasp in it now, gravel settled into the throat. "All of it. Not the argument. The names."

So I read them.

I had read these names so many times they lived behind my eyelids — Private Thomas Merrick, who had asked me to remember him while my hands were still red to the wrist; Corporal Aloysius Byrne; Lance-Sergeant Peter Halloran, who had carried a wounded man four hundred yards and been told afterward, in the dispatches, that he had deserted his post. Forty-one men written into the advance order and out of the record, forty-one families given lies instead of bodies, forty-one names I had copied into the Lantern by lamplight while the ward breathed around me and Iris slept two beds away.

But this time I read them aloud in front of the men they had been taken from — or rather, in front of the man who had watched them die and then watched the paper erase them — and it was different. Reading them alone had been keeping a vigil. Reading them here was testimony.

Vane listened with his eyes closed. Half-blind, he saw better that way; he had told me once that his ruined eye made its own weather, grey shapes moving across everything, and shutting it gave him peace. When I reached the last name — Private Daniel Dossin — Céline made a small sound, not quite a word, and Vane opened his good eye and looked at her across the table.

"He went over the top at my left shoulder," Vane said. "Nineteen years old. He'd shown me a photograph of you that morning. He said, *my mother prints things, sir, she'll know what to do with all this.*" Vane's mouth moved without quite becoming a smile. "He was right. It took him three years to be right, but he was right."

Céline pressed her knuckles to her lips. She had heard her son described before, I knew — she had interviewed half the survivors of that advance herself, patiently, over months, gathering their fragments the way Freya Ashgrove gathered names. But hearing it from the officer who had stood beside him, in the room where the record was finally being corrected — that was a different sacrament. She bowed her head, and I saw her lips move, and though I could not hear it I knew it was the same prayer she whispered over every sheet she pulled from the press.

"The argument," Vane said when I finished. "Read the argument."

I read his words back to him: *On the morning of the fourth of October, 1917, orders were given to advance across open ground against known machine-gun positions. These positions had been reported by reconnaissance and were known to the issuing staff. The advance was broken within twenty minutes. Casualties exceeded nine hundred. In subsequent dispatches, the action was represented as a successful probing operation, and the dead — specifically forty-one men of the third wave — were entered as deserters, missing through cowardice, or casualties of unrelated actions. This document is compiled from the testimony of surviving witnesses, from casualty ledgers kept independently of official channels, and from the sworn account of the undersigned.*

"And the last line," Vane said.

"*I, Aldous James Vane, Captain, witnessed the order, witnessed its consequence, and attest that every statement herein is true.*"

He nodded slowly, as if checking the weight of something.

Then he held out his hand. "Pen."

* * *

Elias gave him the pen. There was a formality to it that none of us had planned but all of us understood — even Tam sat up straighter, even Matron folded her hands. Outside, the wind worried at the boarded window and somewhere far off the guns kept up their low, indifferent conversation, the sound that had become, after three years, less like thunder than like breathing.

Vane took the pen in his right hand. His hand shook. Not from fear — I had learned to read his tremors by then, had catalogued them the way I catalogued everything, because cataloguing was how I kept the world from drowning me — but from the damage, the old wound that had unmade his grip and his sight and half his balance, the wound the army had used to declare him dead rather than admit what had actually happened at Passchendaele.

He steadied the pen against the page with his left hand, the way a man braces a rifle, and wrote his name.

It took him a long time. The signature came out lurching, the letters leaning into each other like tired soldiers, but it was unmistakably his hand — the same hand that had written me letters during my training year, letters I had read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth, letters from a man I had mourned at a memorial service with a hymn and everything. Seeing that handwriting now, alive and deliberate on the page, still produced in me the same small vertigo it had produced the day Elias Wren had walked through the gate with those personal effects: the world tipping a degree off true, and never quite righting itself.

"There," Vane said, when it was done. He sat back. For a moment he looked exhausted in a way that had nothing to do with his body. Then he looked around the table — at Céline, at Elias, at Tam, at Matron, at me — and something moved across his face that I had never seen there before, not at the flooded farmhouse where I'd found him embittered and half-feral, not in any of his letters. It took me a moment to recognize it, because I had seen it so rarely in this war that it seemed almost foreign.

Relief.

"A year ago I thought if I ever got this down on paper I'd feel avenged," he said. "I don't. It doesn't avenge anything. Nine hundred men are still dead and the men who killed them will drink port tonight." He touched the edge of the page with one finger, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "But the paper is true. Whatever happens to it, whatever happens to us — this paper is true, and I am the one who made it true, and no one can take that out of the world now without showing their hand." He looked up at me. "That's worth more than vengeance. I only wish I'd known it sooner."

"You knew it," Céline said quietly. "You just couldn't afford to feel it until it was done."

Vane huffed — almost a laugh. "Widows are permitted to be wise. It's in regulations." Then, to me: "Sister Ashgrove. Your ledger."

My stomach tightened. "Sir?"

"Your ledger. The Lantern." He said the word carefully, the way you handle something breakable. "Elias has told me what you've built. Every entry, cross-checked against the dispatches, dated, sourced. Forty-one names and more besides — names from other actions, other omissions, going back how long?"

"Two years," I said. "Since Ypres. Since Private Merrick."

"Then bring it."

Every instinct I owned rose up at once. Two years of hiding that book — inside a hymnal, inside a coffin inventory, inside the lining of my kit bag, inside the plain sight of a woman nobody thought to suspect — two years of lying to Iris, of flinching at inspections, of learning the exact weight a hymnal should be so mine would never betray me. And now he wanted it out, here, in a farmhouse that might as well have had a target painted on its roof.

Matron Cobb caught my eye across the table. She didn't nod. She didn't need to. Her look said plainly: *you already know what kind of nurse you are. Stop pretending otherwise.*

I fetched the Lantern from the false bottom of my kit bag where it had ridden since the inspection, and I laid it on the table beside the printed signature of the pamphlet, and for a long moment nobody spoke. My ledger was a cheap notebook, water-stained, its cover warped from damp and from the heat of too many nights too close to a stove. Next to Céline's typeset pages it looked like what it was: a private grief made public by stubbornness, a lantern made of a matchbox.

But Vane looked at it the way other men looked at colors.

"This," he said, "is the primary source. My testimony is one voice. Yours is forty-one voices plus however many more —"

"A hundred and six," I said. "In total. Including the ones from before the advance."

"A hundred and six." He repeated it softly, weighing it. "A hundred and six men the dispatches erased, and one woman who refused to." He pushed the pamphlet toward me. "Then finish it. Both documents. They go out together or not at all — the testimony gives your ledger standing, and your ledger gives my testimony teeth. But there's a space left. On the colophon page. We argued about it for two nights — Céline wanted no signatures beyond mine, for safety. Elias wanted pseudonyms for everyone. I overruled them both, and I'm about to overrule myself." His good eye fixed on me. "There is room for one witness. One name, after mine, attesting that the record was compiled honestly and completely. If anything happens to me between here and publication — and we both know the odds — the document needs a second voice the inquiry can't dismiss. Someone who can produce the originals." He tapped the Lantern. "Someone who has been keeping them since before I started looking."

The room had gone very quiet. Even the wind seemed to hold off.

"They'll hang you for this," I said. "Signing it. Coming out of hiding for it. You know that. Colonel Hargreaves has been hunting this network for a month, and the moment your name appears on that page he stops chasing ghosts and starts shooting at a target."

"Yes," Vane said simply. "That's rather the point. A testimony signed by a dead man can be dismissed as forgery. A testimony signed by a living officer — an officer they declared dead, an officer whose existence embarrasses them — that they have to answer. I've been hiding for a year, Sister, and hiding has kept me breathing, but it hasn't kept me *useful*, and I have discovered, late, that usefulness is the only currency I have left." He smiled, crooked and tired. "Besides. I'm already dead on paper. There's a certain freedom in it. Let them shoot a ghost."

"It isn't funny."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. It's arithmetic." He turned the pamphlet to face me and slid the pen across the table. "Witness. Please."

I looked at the colophon page. At the bottom, beneath Vane's lurching signature, a single line had been set in type:

*Compiled and verified by —*

And beneath it, blank space. Céline had left the space empty rather than set a placeholder, because, she said, a lie in type spreads to the whole page.

My hand went to the pen. And stopped.

Because I understood, suddenly and completely, what I was about to do. Not the danger — I had understood the danger since chapter upon chapter of nights, since the first lie to Iris, since the coffin inventory, since the colonel's offer of transfer home that I had refused while my heart hammered like a fist on a door. What I understood now was older than the danger. It was that a name is not a label. It is a claim. For two years I had written other people's names into my ledger and hidden my own, and I had told myself that was humility, that it was prudence, that the work mattered and the worker didn't. But a ledger with no witness is only gossip. A light that hides itself lights nothing.

Private Thomas Merrick had died asking to be remembered, and I had remembered him, and remembering had cost me Iris's easy laughter and my own safety and nearly Tam's life, and I would pay all of it again, and the paying had a name, and the name was mine.

"Freya Marion Ashgrove," I said aloud — my whole name, spoken into that room for the first time as what I was, not what I pretended to be.

Then I wrote it.

My hand did not shake. I noticed that. After everything — after the inspections and the near-discoveries and the burning workshop we all knew was coming like weather on the horizon — my hand was steady, and the letters came out clean and upright, and they looked, next to Vane's ruined scrawl, like a nurse's handwriting, which is exactly what it was.

Freya M. Ashgrove. Witness.

"There," I said, and my voice cracked on the single syllable and I let it.

* * *

Afterward there was tea, because Céline insisted, because in her country and her faith and her grief there was no event so solemn that it did not also require tea. We drank it standing, in a farmhouse lit by one shielded lamp, five people and a boy holding cups of chicory substitute and pretending it was celebration.

Tam drifted over to me while the others talked logistics in low voices — routes, couriers, the leave trains, the plan we all pretended was a plan rather than a prayer. He was fidgeting with his cup, turning it, and I recognized the fidget. Tam fidgeted when he wanted to ask something and was afraid the asking would make him smaller.

"Out with it," I said gently.

"Sister — Miss Ashgrove —" He'd stumbled over the name, and I realized he'd read the colophon. Of course he had; he'd helped carry the pages. "Is it really you? Your name, in the book? Like Captain Vane's?"

"It's really me."

His face did something complicated — awe and fear and pride fighting for the same ground. "Only — I've been carrying things for weeks, and I never knew what they were, properly, and now I know, and—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "My mum doesn't know where I am half the time. If anything happened to me she wouldn't even get a proper letter. That's what they do, isn't it? What the book's about. They take the letter away and give you nothing."

"That's what the book is about," I said.

"Then I want my name in it too."

I looked at him — seventeen, ears still too big for his face, hands that had held dying men with a tenderness the army had never taught him — and I felt the full weight of what he was asking and what it would mean to grant it. "Tam—"

"Not to print," he said quickly. "I know I'm not — I haven't done anything like you lot. Just — in yours. In the ledger. So it's written down somewhere that I helped. That I chose to." He swallowed. "Nobody's ever going to write down anything I chose. They'll write down my number and my unit and where I got posted. But this—" he nodded at the Lantern, sitting closed on the table, ordinary as a hymnal, extraordinary as scripture — "this is the real army, isn't it? This is the one that counts."

So I opened the Lantern one last time, to the final page, where my own name now stood at the bottom in fresh ink, and beneath it I wrote, in the same steady hand:

*Tamuel R. Pryce, orderly. Volunteer courier. Chose.*

He read it twice. Then he blinked hard, saluted — absurdly, movingly, the way boys salute when they mean it with their whole chests — and went back to his crate, sitting a little taller than before.

* * *

We worked past midnight. Céline and Elias collated the remaining signatures of the edition, squaring each stack with a bone folder and wrapping them in oilcloth sized for hems and bandage rolls. Matron sat apart with her needle and thread, sewing a test fold into the lining of her own cloak with the unhurried precision of a woman who had been mending the war's damage for three years and intended to keep mending past the end of it. Vane dozed in his chair and woke at every sound, and between waking he talked to me in a low voice about what should happen after — the inquiry he imagined, the families he hoped would learn the truth, the world he would probably not see — and I let him talk, because a man signing his death warrant deserves someone to listen to the life he's signing away.

It was Elias, at the window, who went still.

Not the ordinary stillness of listening. A different kind — the total arrest of a man whose body has understood something before his mind consents to. He tilted his head. Held up one hand. And in the silence he demanded, we all heard it: engines. More than one. Heavy lorries, low-geared, taking the Ypres road at a crawl, the way vehicles move when they are navigating by map in the dark and do not care who hears them coming.

"What time is it?" Matron asked. Her voice was perfectly calm, which frightened me more than shouting would have.

Elias checked his watch by feel. "Half three."

"They're early," Céline said. "Hargreaves said — the pattern, the raids — always at dawn—"

"Dawn's at six," Elias said. "They're two hours out if they stop to cordon the crossroads. Ninety minutes if they don't." He was already moving, snuffing the lamp, plunging us into a darkness stitched through with the grey glow from the shutter seams. "Vane can't travel fast. Céline, the plates — can they be burned clean?"

Céline was silent for the length of three heartbeats. When she answered, her voice was steady and terrible. "Everything can be burned clean."

"Then wake the fire before they arrive," Elias said. "Freya — the ledger, on your body, sewn if you have to. Tam—"

"I see them first," Tam said. He was already at the door, cap jammed on, and there was no boyishness left in his voice at all. "The lane behind the orchard — I can be at the crossroads before the lorries and watch which way they turn. If they turn toward Rue Basse, I ring the chapel bell twice. If they come here—"

"If they come here," Elias said, "you run, Tam. You don't warn us. You run east and you keep running, because the pages matter more than any of us, and that includes me, and it includes—" he paused, just barely — "it includes the Captain."

From the darkness where Vane sat, a dry voice: "It includes the Captain."

Tam slipped out into the night. Through the gap in the shutters I watched him cross the yard, small and quick and carrying our fate in his legs, and I pressed my hand flat against my kit bag, against the Lantern riding against my ribs where it had always belonged, one hundred and eight names and one of them finally mine.

Far down the dark road, faint but unmistakable, the first engine note grew louder.

Dawn was still two hours away. The dawn was coming anyway.
