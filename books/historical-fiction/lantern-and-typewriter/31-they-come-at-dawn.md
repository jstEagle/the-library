## 31. They Come at Dawn

**Tam**

Tam Osgood woke before the birds did, which was how he knew something was wrong.

He had learned the hours of Rue Basse by their sounds — the baker's cart at half past four, its iron-rimmed wheels complaining on the cobbles like an old man rising from a chair; then the gulls; then, much later, the milk girl whistling something off-key that was probably meant to be a hymn. He slept in the loft above Céline's workshop now, three nights out of four, because Elias said a courier should sleep near his cargo, and Tam had never in his life been needed for anything enough to sleep near it.

But this morning there were no wheels, no gulls, no whistle.

There was only a sound he knew from France, a sound he had hoped he'd left behind when they'd sent him back across the Channel with his bandaged hand and his discharge papers — the low diesel mutter of army lorries under way without lights.

He rolled to the loft window, breath fogging the cold glass, and looked down the long slope of the street toward the canal bridge.

Three of them. Maybe four. Headlamps hooded, canvas tilted over the backs, and riding the running boards the unmistakable shapes of men with rifles slung across their chests. Military police. Not two constables come about paper stock — a column.

The cold went through him all at once, as if the window had opened instead of him.

They were early. That was the whole of it, the whole terrible arithmetic of it. The raid was meant to be days away yet — Elias had said so, Céline had said so, everyone who knew anything had agreed the colonel would need another week for warrants, another week to be sure. And here they came in the blue-black hour with no lights, moving fast, which meant someone had talked or someone had guessed, and either way every minute Tam stood at the window freezing was a minute the presses kept turning below him.

He could hear them. God help him, he could hear the press still going, that steady patient clatter like a loom, Céline working through the night as she had worked through every night this week, her fingers wrapped in rag strips because the skin had split along the seams.

Tam took the ladder too fast and nearly broke his ankle on the last rung. The workshop smelled of ink and lamp oil and wet wool, warm as a kitchen, and for one stupid second he wanted nothing more than to stay inside it forever.

"Céline." His voice cracked; he hated it, fourteen years old and his voice cracking at the end of the world. "Céline, wake up. They're coming. Lorries, down by the bridge."

She was already upright. She slept, he'd learned, the way cats do — entirely, but on the surface of sleep, ready to surface whole. She crossed the floor barefoot, put her ear to the door, and listened the way other people prayed.

"How many?"

"Three. Four. I couldn't—"

"Four." She said it flatly, doing sums he couldn't see. "Then not just police. Police come in pairs." She turned and her face in the lamplight was calm in a way that frightened him worse than the lorries did. "Go out the yard. Over the wall, past the tannery, take the lane behind the church. You know where the nurses' station is."

"You're coming with me—"

"I am staying with my type." She said it the way she said everything, as if it were a fact about weather. "And you are carrying what's finished. Third shelf, the biscuit tin, the false bottom. Take it and run and don't look back at this house, Tam Osgood, whatever you hear from it. Promise me."

"I can't just—"

"*Promise me.*"

Down the street, faint but distinct now, came the grind of brakes on the canal bridge.

He promised.

* * *

**Freya**

Freya had fallen asleep at the desk again, cheek on the open ledger, and woke with an impression of type pressed faintly into her cheek like a brand.

The ward was quiet in the way she loved best — the deep-hour quiet, Matron's lamp trimmed low, the breathing of thirty sleeping men rising and falling together like a tide. Iris was on nights with her tonight, stationed at the far end, and Freya could see the small gold glow of her reading a letter by candle-shield, and the sight of it ached. Two weeks since the confrontation in the linen store. Two weeks of careful civility, of passing each other instruments without meeting eyes, of a friendship held together with sutures that neither of them trusted.

She was rubbing the sleep from her cheek when Tam came through the doors.

That alone would have woken the dead. Tam belonged to the day shift, to errands and grins and burnt toast; Tam had no business existing at half past four in the morning, let alone existing at a dead run, hatless, coat torn at the shoulder where he'd gone over a wall, a biscuit tin crushed against his chest like a football.

"They're coming," he gasped, before the doors had even swung shut behind him. "Miss Ashgrove — the workshop — lorries, four of them, they were on the bridge, I heard them stop—"

The world went very simple and very bright, the way it did when the shells started.

Freya was moving before thought caught up. Tin into her hands — heavier than it should be, sewn pages, hundreds of them, Céline's work, Vane's testimony, forty-one names and four hundred more. "Iris." Her voice carried down the ward without waking the men; she'd learned to shout in whispers in her first month at Ypres. "Iris. Wake Matron. Get the men in the side wards moved — say it's a gas alarm drill, say anything, but get the corridors clear."

Iris was already off her stool, candle pinched dead between finger and thumb, and for one suspended moment their eyes met across the ward and Freya saw the question land in her — *this is it, this is what you've been doing, this is the thing that will kill us* — and saw the question lose. Iris turned and ran for Matron's room without one word wasted, and Freya could have wept for gratitude if weeping were on the schedule.

It wasn't. Nothing was on the schedule anymore except minutes.

"Tam." He was swaying, chest heaving, a scrape bleeding along his jaw. She took his chin in her hand and made him look at her. "Where's Céline? Where's Elias?"

"Still there. She wouldn't leave. She told me—" His voice broke. "She told me not to look back."

Outside, far off toward the town, a dog began to bark, and then another, and Freya thought: *they stopped at the bridge. They haven't moved yet. Someone is knocking at a door, asking questions, being thorough, and thoroughness is the only gift we will ever receive from military bureaucracy.*

Maybe ten minutes. Perhaps fifteen.

She looked down at the tin in her hands, and then around her at the ward — the sleeping men, the folded uniforms stacked for the morning laundry, the bandage bundles waiting for the supply wagon at six.

And the plan that had lived for a week in the margins of her mind, half-formed, unspeakable, stepped forward all at once and became the only plan.

* * *

**Elias**

Elias Wren had spent two years learning to read the night, and the night was telling him plainly: go now.

He'd been coming back along the canal path from the courier drop — the last delivery, the final bundle of pamphlets handed to a railway porter with a socialist streak and a widow's brother among the erased — when he'd seen the lorries cross the far bridge. He'd gone flat in the reeds without deciding to, body older and wiser than mind, and watched them halt at the head of Rue Basse while an officer jumped down and conferred with a man holding a lantern, and the lantern-light showed him the officer's collar and Elias's stomach turned over: not a captain. A full colonel. They'd brought the colonel himself, at dawn, in person, which meant the order had come from above the colonel, which meant there would be no warnings, no polite searches, no chance to swallow evidence.

This was not an inspection. This was an execution of paperwork.

He counted the men dismounting — twelve, fifteen, twenty — and did the arithmetic he'd done a hundred times in France and never once found kind. Twenty men could surround the block in ninety seconds. The front door of the workshop was oak but it was also oak from 1880, and Céline Dossin had never once, in all the months he'd known her, locked it. *A print shop is a public trust,* she said. *You lock a door against thieves. I keep mine open for readers.*

He moved along the canal path, low, using the towpath wall, and came into the town from behind, through the tannery yard where the stink covered everything including him. The back of Rue Basse was already filling with soldiers spreading out in pairs, and he crouched in the shadow of a coal store and watched them take their positions with the unhurried competence of men who had done this to other houses in other towns and would do it again.

There was a light burning in Céline's window. He could see the shadow of the press through the frosted glass, still moving, still working, and something in his chest tore along an old seam.

*Get out,* he willed at the window, at the silhouette bent over the case. *Through the yard, over the wall, the way the boy went. Leave the type, leave the plates, leave it all — you can set it again, you can set it in hell itself if you must, but only if you're alive to hold the stick.*

The silhouette did not move. Of course it didn't. It dipped and rose, dipped and rose — setting line after line, finishing the page, because Céline Dossin did not run from funerals; she attended them.

Somewhere near the head of the street, boots rang on cobblestones in unison, and a voice Elias knew — the colonel's voice, dry as a filed report — gave a single quiet command.

The front door of the workshop was not forced. It was simply opened, because it had never been locked, and the light of twenty lanterns poured into the little shop like water into a hull.

Elias closed his eyes. When he opened them he was running.

**Matron Cobb**

Beatrice Cobb had been a nurse for thirty-one years and a widow for nine, and she had learned that most disasters announce themselves politely if you know how to listen.

So when Iris hammered on her door at half past four stammering something about lorries and drills and Miss Ashgrove wanting the corridors cleared, Beatrice did not ask questions. She put on her shoes, took her keys, and walked into the ward like weather.

What she found was controlled chaos of a very high order — Freya directing, Tam hauling screens, Iris moving sleeping men with a tenderness that would have made Beatrice proud on any other morning — and at the center of it, Freya kneeling at the laundry baskets with a needle already threaded, sewing pages into the hem of a stretcher-bearer's spare tunic with stitches small enough to shame a seamstress.

"Well," said Beatrice Cobb.

Freya didn't look up. Her hands didn't even pause. "Matron. I'm sorry. There isn't time to explain and you'd only—"

"I've known since the hymnal," Beatrice said. "March. The binding was wrong. Do hurry."

For the first time since Tam had burst through those doors, Freya Ashgrove looked up, and her face was seventeen years old and drowning. "You *knew*?"

"I keep people, dear. I told you there were two kinds." Beatrice took the basket from beside her and knelt, taking the next uniform, threading her own needle with fingers that hadn't shaken since Passchendaele. "My brother's name was in a book like yours once. Nobody kept it. Sew faster and lecture later."

They worked. God forgive her, but they worked beautifully — Iris sewing without having been told, without asking, her jaw set like a woman signing something; Tam running pages from the tin to the needles; the whole ward converted in eight minutes into a distribution house disguised as a laundry. Pages into hems. Pages rolled into the cardboard cores of bandage spools. Pages folded flat into the false bottoms of kit bags scheduled on the morning wagon to the coast.

And beneath the rhythm of it, from the direction of the town, thin as thread: bells. Not church bells. The alarm bell at the gendarmerie, rung once, twice — and then stopping, as if someone had decided against it.

Beatrice's needle did not pause. But she heard Freya hear it, and saw the girl's eyes go to the dark windows, and knew that somewhere down those black streets a Belgian widow was alone with her son's name and twenty men with lanterns.

"Finish the basket," Beatrice said quietly. "All of it. Whatever happens at Rue Basse, these pages leave this building by six o'clock or we have burned her house for nothing."

**Céline**

They gave her the courtesy of a colonel, which she supposed was meant to frighten her more than a sergeant would have.

Céline Dossin sat at her workbench with her hands folded over her apron — hands wrapped in dirty rags, ruined, beautiful, the hands that had set eleven thousand lines of truth since October — and watched the officers move through her shop lifting things with white cotton gloves as though her typecase were a relic and they archaeologists.

The press stood silent. She had stopped it herself, five minutes before they came through the door, wiped it down, and covered it, because whatever else happened the machine would be clean. Jan had built models of engines with the same care. She thought of him the way she always thought of him now — not as a boy dying in a field she'd never seen, but as a boy at a bench, concentrating, tongue between his teeth.

"Madame Dossin." The colonel stood before her workbench. Up close his exhaustion showed through the polish, and she catalogued it with a craftsman's interest: a tired man, a used-up man, sent to do the burning personally because no one below him could be trusted to burn correctly. "You are printing seditious material. Falsified military records. You understand what this means."

"I understand what it *meant*," Céline said. "It meant my son existed. That is all it ever meant. You may arrest me for it. Many men have arrested me for less true things."

"We have warrants covering the premises, the equipment, and your person." His eyes went to the covered press, and she saw the calculation there — plates, type, paper stock, evidence. "Where are the printed copies?"

"In fires," said Céline, "and in hems, and in the pockets of men whose names you erased, and in places you will never find because they are not places, Colonel. They are people." She unfolded her hands. Under the rags her fingers were trembling, and she let them. "You are looking for paper. Paper was never the thing. Paper was only how it traveled."

Something moved behind his eyes — the flicker of a man hearing, despite himself, the shape of his own defeat described accurately. Then it hardened.

"Search the cellar," he said to the sergeants. "Take the press apart. If there are plates, I want them."

And Céline Dossin, widow, printer, mother of an erased man, looked at the lamp beside her workbench — the lamp with the reservoir she had filled higher than usual, the way she had filled it higher every night this week, the way any printer fills a lamp who knows what oil is for — and made the arithmetic she had been making all along.

Her son's memorial was typeset and locked in the chase on the bed of that press. Forty-one names, and his among them, in her own hand-set letters, the finest work of her life.

They were not going to take it apart with gloved hands and carry it away in an evidence crate to be photographed and mocked and lost.

"There is one copy I have not hidden," she said, rising, and her voice came out steady, almost gentle. "Would the Colonel care to see it? It is on the press. It is the finest impression we ever pulled. I think a soldier should see it. I think you especially."

The colonel hesitated — pride, curiosity, the eternal vanity of rank — and nodded once.

Céline walked to the press. Her ruined hands were steady on the lamp.

* * *

**Freya**

From the ward's high windows, facing east across the roofs, you could see the smoke before anyone could see fire.

Freya saw it first — a smudge against the paling sky, wrong-colored, too black for chimneys at this hour — and stopped stitching mid-pull, and Iris followed her gaze, and Tam, and then Matron, and for a moment the whole room stood still as a photograph, thirty sleeping patients and four conspirators and one column of smoke rising over Rue Basse.

"Oh," said Iris softly. It wasn't horror. It was closer to grief, or to prayer. "Oh, she wouldn't have—"

"She would," said Freya. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "She absolutely would."

The tin at her feet was empty now. Every page sewn, rolled, folded, hidden — in hems and bandages and kit bags, in the lining of Tam's coat, in the false spine of Matron's own ledger of ward stores, in Iris's glove box, in Freya's bodice against her heart where the Lantern had lived so long it had worn a groove. The names were leaving the building at six o'clock on a supply wagon, on a leave train, in the bodies of everyone in this room.

And behind them, over the roofs, the smoke thickened, and somewhere beneath it a press and a typecase and forty-one names in lead were becoming ash, and somewhere in that ash or beside it was a woman with ruined hands whom Freya would never see again, and possibly a man named Elias Wren who had run toward the lanterns when every sane instinct said away.

Freya picked up the needle.

"Sew," she said, and her voice did not shake, and later she would wonder at that, would wonder where the shaking had gone and whether she'd ever get it back. "We finish the basket. We finish everything. Whatever she burned it for, we make it count."

They bent to the work as the sky outside went from black to grey to the color of smoke, and none of them looked up again until the first lorry ground to a halt outside the gates of the dressing station, and a voice at the front desk, dry as a filed report, asked for Nurse Ashgrove by name.
