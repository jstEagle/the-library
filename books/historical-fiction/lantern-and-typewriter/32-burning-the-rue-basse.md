## 32. Burning the Rue Basse

Céline Dossin had set type by lamplight for eleven nights, and on the twelfth she smelled the lorries before anyone saw them.

It was not the engines — those she had heard all winter grinding along the Menin road, hauling shells and wounded men in equal measure. It was the smell beneath the engines: cold iron, boot leather, that particular military soap the gendarmes used, as if cleanliness itself had been conscripted. The wind came off the canal carrying it up Rue Basse like a message, and her hands stopped over the case before her mind understood why.

She stood very still in the doorway of the workshop, one hand resting on the frame of the old Heidelberg press, and listened.

Down the street, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then the first one stopped, abruptly, the way dogs stop when someone kicks them.

"Emile," she said quietly, to the boy who was washing ink from the rollers with turpentine, his sleeves rolled to the elbow, his young face slack with exhaustion. "Put down the rag."

He looked up. Emile was nineteen and Belgian and had been with her since the second week of the work, when he had appeared at the door asking for nothing except to be near the machine that was making the truth. His father had been a compositor in Liège; he had inherited hands that knew metal. Now those hands went still above the roller, and his eyes went to hers with the question boys ask when they already know the answer.

"How long?" he said.

"Minutes. Perhaps less." She crossed the room quickly now, all hesitation gone, because there is a kind of clarity that arrives only when everything you love is about to be taken from you. "Wake whoever is sleeping upstairs. Get the finished signatures out through the cellar door. Everything printed goes — everything. Leave the rest."

"The plates—"

"Leave them," Céline said, and heard her own voice come out flat and final, the voice of a woman who had buried a husband in 1914 and a son in 1916 and had learned that some things cannot be saved, only honored.

* * *

They came down the stairs two at a time — Tam first, the English orderly, who had been sleeping in the loft for three nights running rather than risk the walk back to the dressing station in the dark; then the Flemish girl Anka, whose brother's name was also on the list; then Nurse Ashgrove, Freya, who had arrived an hour ago with a satchel of pages sewn into the lining of her greatcoat and had sat down at the stone without taking off her coat, setting type beside Céline in silence, their shoulders almost touching, until neither of them could see straight anymore.

Céline watched Freya take in the room — the smell of turpentine sharpening, the barking dogs, the way Céline herself was already moving toward the back wall where the oil lamp hung — and saw the understanding arrive on the nurse's face like weather.

"No," Freya said. Not loudly. But the word had weight.

"Yes," Céline answered, and kept moving.

"They can't have the plates," Elias Wren said from the doorway, where he had materialized the way he always did, silent as smoke. He was breathing hard; he had been watching the road. "Two lorries, maybe three. Gendarmerie and staff officers both — I saw the red tabs. They'll be here in ten minutes, twelve at most. If they take the plates, they take the evidence, and worse—" he looked around the room, at each of them, "—they take the proof of who we are. Every man and woman in this room becomes a name on a charge sheet instead of a rumor."

"So we burn it," Tam said. His voice cracked in the middle of the sentence. He was holding a bundle of finished signatures against his chest like a child holds bread. "We just — we burn it? The whole shop? Madame Dossin, your press, your — this is your *house*, madame—"

"It is my workshop," Céline said. "My house burned in August 1914, with my husband's photographs in it and my mother's chairs and every letter Luc ever wrote me before he was tall enough to enlist. I learned then what I am about to teach you now, boy: things that burn were only ever borrowed."

She took the lamp from its hook.

* * *

There is an order to destroying a print shop, and Céline knew it the way she knew the order of setting a page: headline first, body after. The danger was never the paper — paper burns fast and leaves nothing. The danger was the metal. Type can be read. Plates can be photographed. A single forme left intact in the stone bed of the press would hang every one of them, and would give the colonel what he wanted most: the pamphlet itself, whole enough to prove sedition and thin enough to hide from the public forever in an evidence locker.

So they worked, and they worked fast, and Céline directed it all with a terrible calm she did not feel.

"Freya — the case. Dump the cases into the bucket, all of them, mix the sorts. Mixed type is alphabet soup; sorted type is testimony." Freya was already moving, tipping the wooden trays, forty-one names' worth of letters rattling together into anonymous confusion. Céline heard her doing it and thought: *this woman has spent months putting names in order, and now she must unorder them, and she will do it, because she understands.*

"Elias — the formes. Out of the press, into the stove. All of them. Melt them or break them, but no line of mine leaves this room readable."

"Anka — the paper stock. The ream wrappers have the mill mark, the watermark, the lot numbers. That's how the colonel found us. Every scrap of that paper goes in the fire too."

"And Emile—"

But Emile was already at the press, laying his hands flat on the iron frame the way a man lays his hands on a dying animal, feeling its warmth, its mass, its stubbornness. The Heidelberg had belonged to a printer in Ghent who had smuggled it out piece by piece in coffins during the first year of the war. Céline had bought it with the last of the money her husband had left hidden in a flour tin. For two years it had spoken for the dead.

"Madame," Emile said, and his voice was very small. "Must it be the press?"

Céline crossed the room and put her hand over his on the cold iron.

"Luc's name is in that forme in the stove," she said. "His name, and forty others, and six hundred more besides, and the account of the morning they were sent to die so that a general could keep his reputation. If the officers take that metal, they melt it down themselves and pour it into something useful — belt buckles, perhaps, or medals." She felt him flinch under her hand. "I will not let my son become a medal."

* * *

The fire, when it started, started well.

Céline had laid the groundwork weeks ago without knowing why — old instinct, the instinct of women who live in occupied countries and keep their houses ready for grief. There was paraffin in the cellar for the lamps. There was the pile of spoiled makeready, sheets ruined by bad impression, stacked knee-high beside the stove. There was turpentine, and there was ink, and ink, she had learned as a girl in her father's shop, burns like a grudge.

Elias poured the paraffin. Freya struck the match.

Later, people would ask Céline who lit it, and she would say the truth: the English nurse lit it, and Céline held her hand steady while she did, because a flame needs a witness or it is only an accident.

The fire took the paper first. The spoiled makeready caught with a soft rushing sound, almost gentle, and the flame ran up the stack faster than a man could walk, and then the room filled with light — real light, orange and alive, the first warmth any of them had felt in months — and Céline stood in the middle of her own workshop and watched it go.

The formes in the stove were melting. She could hear it, a faint ticking and slumping, lead surrendering to heat, letters losing their shapes. *L* becoming nothing. *U* becoming nothing. *C*. Somewhere in that liquid silver was the word she had set more times than any other word in two years, the name she had chosen as the first line of the first page because the pamphlet should open with the truest thing anyone had ever written about the war:

**LUC DOSSIN, PRIVATE, 3RD LINE REGIMENT. KILLED 4 OCTOBER 1916. HIS NAME DOES NOT APPEAR IN THE DISPATCH OF HIS COMMANDING OFFICER. THIS IS THAT DISPATCH. THIS IS THE DIFFERENCE.**

She had set it four hundred times. She had kissed her thumb and pressed it to the type once, early on, before Emile caught her at it and she made him swear silence. Ink from a printer's thumb on a printer's type — the forensics of it, the intimacy. Her touch was in every copy that had gone out on the leave trains. Her son had traveled into England and Scotland and the trenches of five divisions inside other men's coats, carried in her fingerprints, and now the fingerprints were going too, curling off the metal in the stove, rising as smoke through the broken roof tiles into the snow.

"Madame." It was Freya, at her elbow, her face lit from below by the fire, soot already striping her cheek. "The back wall — the memorial—"

"I know," Céline said.

On the back wall of the workshop, above the stone where she composed, Céline had hung a small framed photograph: Luc in his uniform, taken three days before he enlisted, trying to look older than eighteen and succeeding only in looking frightened. Beneath it, in a narrow frame, the first proof sheet of the first page — the one with her thumbprint smudged in the margin. She had hung them there the day the shop opened, and she had told no one why, and everyone had known anyway.

The fire was walking toward the wall. Paper walls, plaster and lath, old wood. It would reach the photograph in minutes.

"You don't have to watch," Freya said gently.

"Child," Céline said, "I have watched worse. I watched them load him onto a cart with the other ones, wrapped in canvas, in the rain, and I was not allowed to touch him because of the regulations. I stood behind a rope." She did not take her eyes from the wall. "This is not worse. This is the opposite of worse. Do you know what this is?"

Freya said nothing.

"This is a funeral," Céline said. "A proper one. With fire, the way we did it in the old country before the priests taxed it. My son has been lying in an unmarked grave in a foreign field for two years while a general wrote letters saying he never existed. Tonight the last place his name lived is going up in flames in front of witnesses who loved him, and tomorrow his name walks out of here in the pockets of people who will carry it to the ends of the earth." She found she was smiling, and the smile hurt her face like a crack in ice. "Show me another mother in Belgium who gets to attend her child's resurrection. Go on. Show me one."

The fire reached the wall. The plaster whitened, then split. The frame around the proof sheet blackened, held, and fell forward into the heat, and Céline watched the paper catch — watched her son's printed name flare white at its edges, gold at its heart, and rise.

"There you are, mon cœur," she said softly, in French, in the language she had sung to him. "There you are."

* * *

They got out through the cellar with nine minutes to spare, though none of them counted; counting was Elias's job and he did it silently, herding them down the steps into the cold brick dark while the workshop roared above them like a church organ learning anger.

Tam carried the signatures. Anka carried the address lists — forty-one families, copied in Freya's careful hand, folded flat against her body. Freya carried the Lantern itself, the ledger, which had never once been in the workshop until tonight, because Céline had forbidden it — *never let the original and the copies sleep under one roof* — and Freya had obeyed her, and obeying had saved it, because the ledger was sewn now into the lining of her coat and would not burn unless Freya burned.

At the mouth of the alley behind the tannery, Céline stopped and turned around.

Rue Basse glowed. The workshop windows threw light across the cobbles in long trembling bars, and the smoke went up straight into the windless dark, a black column taller than the church spire, and somewhere in it — she knew exactly where, she could have pointed to the spot — the Heidelberg press was beginning to scream as its iron warped. It sounded, she thought, almost human. It sounded like a thing objecting.

"Madame Dossin." Tam's voice, shaking, beside her. The boy was gray-faced in the firelight, the bundle of pages clutched to his chest, his breath coming in white puffs. "Your shop. Your whole — everything. And you're standing there like—" He couldn't finish. He was crying, she realized. Nineteen years old and crying for her, for a Belgian widow he'd known five months, and she loved him for it the way she loved all of them: helplessly, completely, in advance of grief.

"TAM." She took his chin in her ink-stained hand and made him look at her. "Look at me. Look at what I am holding." She nodded at the bundle in his arms. "What is that?"

"The — the signatures. The finished pamphlet."

"How many?"

"Forty. Maybe fifty complete sets."

"And where are they going?"

He swallowed. "Into the uniforms. Into the hems, the bandages. Tomorrow. On the leave train. On everyone willing to carry."

"And my shop?" She gestured at the burning building with a sweep of her arm, grand as a stage actress, and heard her own voice ring out over the fire. "My shop is a *building*. Buildings are where you keep things you might lose. That—" she tapped the bundle once, hard, "—is what you keep when you cannot afford to lose anything. I have traded a house for a hundred couriers. Show me the officer who wouldn't make that trade. Show me the general."

Above them, something in the roof gave way with a crash, and sparks went up in a spiral, hundreds of them, drifting east on the wind like a slow bright snowfall falling upward. Anka made a sound low in her throat. Elias put a hand on Céline's shoulder, and she let it stay there, because even stone leans sometimes.

"We have to move," Elias said. "When the lorries arrive and find it burning, they'll sweep the streets. Every house, every café. We split up, we go to ground, we reconvene at the station at dawn—"

"Dawn is too late for the train," Freya said. Her voice was steady but her hands, Céline noticed, were pressed flat against her coat, over the ledger, as if holding a wound closed. "The leave train leaves at six. The pages have to be distributed tonight."

"Then God help us, we distribute them tonight," Elias said. "Everyone takes their share. Everyone walks separately. Nobody looks at anybody."

"One moment." Céline raised her hand, and to her mild astonishment, they all stopped — the English nurse, the smuggler, the boy, the girl — five people standing in a freezing alley with the light of her life burning behind them, waiting on a widow's word.

She looked at each of them in turn. She wanted to say something worthy of the moment, something a monument would engrave. What came out instead was plain, and true, and better.

"My husband used to say a printer has only one prayer," she said. "He said we say it every time we pull the lever and the page comes out clean. *Let it be read.*" Her voice caught, and she let it. "Two years I pulled that lever. Two years, and my arms ache, and my eyes are ruined, and my son's name went out into the world four hundred times and came back to me four hundred times in the letters of strangers — mothers, wives, girls he'd danced with in Liège — writing to say *we read it, madame, we know him now, he existed.*" She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, leaving a black stripe through the tears, and laughed — actually laughed, short and rough as a cough. "So burn the shop. Burn the press. Burn the plates and the paper and the roof. Let the colonel stand in my ashes tomorrow with his empty hands and his empty report."

She picked up her share of the pages — twenty sets, bound in brown paper, light as bread — and tucked them inside her coat, against her heart.

"*Let it be read,*" she said again. "Now go. All of you. Before the soldiers get here and find five people warm from somebody else's fire."

They scattered into the dark like sparks off the roof, each one carrying a piece of the light.

Céline Dossin went last, alone, walking north along the canal with the glow dying behind her and the snow beginning again, and she did not look back, because a proper funeral does not need its mourners staring. In her coat, her son's name rode next to her heart. Ahead of her, somewhere past the tannery and the bridge and the sleeping town, a leave train waited in the dark with its boiler warming, and by morning the names would be sewn into hems from Ypres to Southampton, and no army on earth could unpick them all.

Behind her, the chimney of the Rue Basse settled into rubble with a sigh, sending up one last column of sparks.

"Proper funeral," she told the snow. "The best I could give you, mon cœur."

Then she walked on toward the station, an old woman with a heavy coat, unremarkable, unstoppable, carrying the morning news.
