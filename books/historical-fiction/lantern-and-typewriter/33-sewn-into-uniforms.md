## 33. Sewn Into Uniforms

The ash was still warm when we started counting what we had saved.

We did it in the cellar of Céline's sister-in-law's house, three streets back from the Rue Basse, where the walls sweated and a single bulb hung from a nail like a dying moon. Céline had carried out two crates under her apron while the roof burned; Tam had run the third through the garden in his arms like an infant; I had taken nothing but the Lantern, which rode against my ribs where it always rode now, sewn into the lining of my waistcoat by Matron Cobb herself with stitches so fine you would swear they were factory work.

Two hundred and six signatures survived. A full edition would have been four hundred. Céline stood over the crates with her hands still smelling of smoke and said, "Half is not failure. Half is a seed." She said it in French first, then again in her careful English, because she wanted to be certain we all understood. We understood.

The question was how to move it.

A pamphlet could be seized. A parcel could be opened. A satchel could be dropped, searched, traced back up its own thread until the whole network lay on a table under electric light, each of us named in turn. Elias paced the width of the cellar — four steps, four steps, four steps — and laid out every route he knew, and every one ended in a checkpoint or a clerk with orders.

It was Matron who solved it, and she solved it the way she solved everything: by looking at what was already in the room.

"We have forty-one nurses," she said. "Sixteen orderlies. Ambulance drivers coming and going. Leave trains running twice a week to the coast, and from there, boats to England carrying hundreds of men home with their kit bags and their greatcoats and their pockets full of letters they're not supposed to have written." She looked around the cellar, and her voice went quiet in the way that made even colonels stand straighter. "No one searches a nurse's hem. No one has ever searched a nurse's hem. They search our bags and find lint and morphine and hymnals, and they apologize for the trouble."

Elias stopped pacing.

"You want to sew it into the uniforms."

"I want to sew it into everything," Matron said. "Hems. Collars. Bandage rolls that will be unwrapped in dressing stations from here to Amiens. Kit bag linings. The false bottoms of biscuit tins. Every page that leaves this house leaves inside something the army itself is carrying, because the army never looks at what it trusts."

Céline began to laugh — low, cracked laughter, the first I'd heard from her since the fire, since before the fire. "The record travels as laundry," she said. "Good. Good. Let them iron it flat."

* * *

We worked for three days without stopping, except to change dressings, because the war did not pause for our conspiracy and men were still arriving from the line with their wounds and their terror and their names.

The sewing fell mostly to Iris, and I will write this plainly because history should know it: Iris Halloway, who had confronted me in the linen store with her voice shaking — *whatever you're doing will get people killed* — sat cross-legged on the floor of the cellar with a needle in her teeth and taught the others how to make a lockstitch invisible against army wool. She unpicked hems with a razor blade, folded a single page into quarters no larger than a communion wafer, tucked it into the fold, and drew the thread closed behind it so cleanly that I ran my thumb along the seam afterward and found nothing but cloth.

She didn't speak to me much those first days. But when she handed me my own greatcoat back — rehemmed, heavier by one page — she held it a moment longer than she needed to.

"Left side," she said. "Over the heart. It seemed right."

That was all. That was everything.

Tam became our quartermaster, because Tam had a genius for making lists look innocent. He wrote out the inventory of the ward stores in his careful schoolboy hand — bandages, gauze, splints, safety pins — and beneath each item, in a code of his own devising that used the pin column as a tally, he tracked how many pages had gone out and in whose uniform. He called the code "the knitting pattern," and if anyone ever questioned him he would produce the list and explain the pins with such earnest boredom that the questioner's eyes glazed over before he finished.

"He's going to be a criminal mastermind someday," Elias said, watching Tam work.

"He's going to be a schoolmaster," I said, "and terrify generations of children with his handwriting."

But I watched the boy too, and what I felt watching him was older than humor. Tam was nineteen. He had volunteered for errands since the day I met him because he wanted, more than anything in the world, to matter. And now he mattered. Now every stitch he tallied was a name walking out of a burning building and into the world. I saw it change him — saw the eagerness settle into something steadier. He stopped volunteering. He simply acted, quietly, as though he had always known how.

* * *

The leave train left on Thursday.

I remember the platform because it was raining, that fine grey drizzle that turned everyone into ghosts of themselves, and because Private Dunmore — the man whose name had started all of this, the dying private who had gripped my wrist in the dressing station and asked me to remember him — was on it, walking on a leg that would hold, bound for Blighty and his mother's kitchen in Sheffield.

Iris had sewn a page into his tunic hem three nights before. Page eleven. His own name was on it, third from the top, spelled correctly, dated correctly, recorded as killed in the advance of the ninth and officially listed nowhere at all.

He didn't know it was there. We had decided that — decided that no soldier would carry what he couldn't unknow if he were searched and caught. Plausible ignorance was armor. But as the whistle blew and he gathered his kit, Dunmore paused beside me on the platform, and the rain ran down his face, and he said, "You wrote to my mum. The sister told me. Said somebody wrote to tell her I wasn't forgotten."

"Someone remembered you," I said. "That's all."

He nodded, and his jaw worked, and then he said the thing that I have carried longer than any of the rest of it. "My name won't be on anything official, Sister. I know how it works. So wherever you put it — whatever you did with it — thank you." He tapped his chest, twice, over the heart, without knowing why, without knowing that under the wet wool and the stitching, folded into quarters, his name was riding home against his skin.

The train pulled out. I stood on the platform until the last carriage vanished into the rain, and I thought: there goes the Lantern. There go forty-one names and a captain's testimony and a Belgian widow's grief, traveling third class to the coast in a dead man's greatcoat.

By Sunday, Iris had sewn thirty-eight pages into outgoing uniforms. By Wednesday, ninety. Orderlies took them on ambulance runs wrapped in bandages destined for casualty clearing stations. Two drivers carried them in their coat linings all the way to Étaples. A chaplain — Father Brennan, who asked no questions and blessed everything freely — agreed to carry a bundle in his mass kit, reasoning that God had hidden plenty of contraband in churches over the centuries and would not object to some going the other way.

Matron kept a rule: no page traveled alone with anyone unwilling. She assembled us once in the cellar and said, "If any of you wants out, say so now, and you'll hear nothing more about it, ever." No one spoke. She waited a long moment, then nodded once, and that nod was the only ceremony any of it ever got.

* * *

Not everything went smoothly. I want to be honest about that too.

A corporal named Whitcombe was pulled off a leave train at Hazebrouck for a routine search, and though the page in his kit bag lining was never found, the delay cost us two days of frozen nerve while we waited to learn whether the net was closing. Nurse Adele Fournier, one of the Belgian volunteers, was stopped at a checkpoint outside Poperinghe, and the sergeant made her unpack her entire bag onto the wet road — and she stood there in the mud while he fingered through her spare collars, one of which held page sixty-three folded into the shoulder seam, and she smiled at him and offered him a boiled sweet, and he let her repack and wave her through.

She came back grey-faced and shaking, and Céline held her head between her hands and said, "You see? They cannot imagine it. Their imagination is the hole in every net. They think a woman carries needles to mend her own things."

"They think we carry nothing worth reading," I said.

"They think we are furniture," Céline said. "So let the furniture testify."

There were other moments, smaller ones, that I set down here because the big histories forget them. The night Tam sewed a page into the lining of his own cap and wore it through a surprise inspection, standing at attention while a lieutenant walked the line of orderlies, close enough that the boy swore later he could feel the paper warming against his scalp. The morning Iris unpicked and resewed a page three times because the fold showed through the wool of a nurse's cape in raking light, muttering to herself in the tone she usually reserved for incompetent surgeons. The evening Matron Cobb sat down among us, took up a needle, and hemmed twelve greatcoats in silence, her hands moving with the speed of a woman who had been mending other people's clothes since before any of us were born, and when Tam asked her where she'd learned to stitch like that she said, "In a mining village, love, where a torn coat meant a cold child and nobody could pay a tailor," and went back to sewing names into the empire's wool as though it were the most natural work in the world.

Perhaps it was. Perhaps remembrance is just mending done on time.

* * *

On the sixth night, Elias brought word that changed the temperature of the room.

He came in wet to the knees, having walked the long way round to shake any tail, and he stood dripping by the crates while Céline poured him chicory coffee that tasted like burnt earth and he drank it anyway.

"They're asking questions at the rail junctions," he said. "Not about paper. About people. Who's been traveling, who's been absent from duty at odd hours. The colonel hasn't filed his report on the Rue Basse yet — my contact says the raid found nothing he can use, and a report that says *we burned our own evidence and found ash* doesn't win a man promotion. So he's still hunting. He knows now there's a network. He can't prove it, so he's going to squeeze until someone cracks."

"Squeeze how?" Matron asked.

"Transfers. Reassignments. Calling people in for quiet chats with no witnesses." He looked at me. "And requests for personnel files. Yours included, Freya. Mine. Céline's late husband's. He's building a map of us out of paperwork."

The cellar went very quiet. Somewhere above us a clock ticked, and the rain worked at the little window, and I thought about every page already traveling — a hundred and fifty-some names folded into wool and linen, scattered along rail lines and boat routes, unwrappable, unburnable now, multiplying beyond anyone's power to recall.

"He's lost the paper," I said slowly. "Even if he takes every one of us tomorrow, the pages keep moving. Nurses rotate home. Men demobilize. Every uniform that crosses the Channel carries—"

"Carries the record inside the army's own luggage," Elias finished. "Yes. That's why he's squeezing. Not to stop the pages — he can't, not anymore — but to stop us from saying it out loud. To make sure that when the truth surfaces, no one alive can be tied to it. He doesn't need to burn the pamphlet, Freya. He needs to burn the people who could vouch for it."

"Then we'd best make sure," Matron said, threading a new needle without looking up, "that there are too many of us to burn."

* * *

We sent out the last of the surviving signatures over the following week. One hundred and eighty-six pages, distributed across twenty-nine uniforms, nine kit bags, fourteen bandage rolls, a biscuit tin, a mass kit, and — at Tam's solemn insistence — the lining of a teddy bear being posted home to a sergeant's daughter, on the grounds that no officer alive would open a child's toy.

One hundred and eighty-six seeds. Céline counted them out the final night, touched each crate empty as a cradle, and said nothing at all, which from Céline was a eulogy and a christening both.

I stood in the doorway of the cellar afterward with Elias, listening to the rain, feeling the Lantern press against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

"It's out of our hands now," he said. "Whatever happens next happens on trains, in ports, in kitchens in Sheffield and Cardiff and Glasgow. Mothers are going to open seams they never knew existed and find their sons' names written down by a stranger who cared."

"That was always the point," I said. "Dunmore taught me that. Nobody remembers the dispatches. People remember who bothered."

Elias was quiet a moment. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone careful. "There's one more thing. My contact at the junction office says the colonel has requisitioned a file — a thick one, marked with the station's designation. Personnel, movement logs, supply records. Everything." He hesitated. "Freya — the requisition slip had your ward number on it. Your ward. He's not hunting a network anymore. He's hunting *us*, by name, and he's starting with the people who signed the station ledger."

Above us, the clock struck midnight, and somewhere down the dark street a lorry changed gear — early, too early, engines don't run at that hour without orders — and I put my hand flat against my ribs, over the Lantern, and listened.
