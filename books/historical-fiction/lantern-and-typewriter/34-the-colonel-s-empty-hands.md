## 34. The Colonel's Empty Hands

Colonel Ambrose Havelock-Strand stood at the mouth of the ruined workshop and let the smoke taste settle on his tongue. It was bitter, resinous, faintly sweet — the smell of burning paper, of burning glue, of burning wood. Beneath it, something else: charred metal, hot iron cooling in the grey morning air. The press.

He had come expecting to seize a press.

He had told himself all the way down the Rue Basse, standing stiff-backed in the lorry while Ypres receded behind him and the dawn came up bruised and colourless over the flats, that this would be a simple matter. A printer's shop run by a Belgian widow with more sentiment than sense. A handful of seditious pamphlets, half-set type, perhaps a list of names written by some disgraced officer too cowardly to sign his treason in the open. He would have the woman in custody by eight o'clock. He would have the plates by nine. By noon he would be drafting a dispatch that would close the matter cleanly, quietly, before it could grow legs and walk into the newspapers or — far worse — into Parliament, where men with nothing better to do than mourn their constituents had lately developed an appetite for questions about casualty figures.

Instead there was ash.

He stepped carefully over the threshold, if it could be called that any longer. The door had burned to its frame; what remained was a black archway, like the entrance to a kiln. Inside, the workshop was a cathedral of ruin. The roof beams had collapsed inward, forming a lattice of char against the pale sky, and through the gaps the early light fell in long dusty shafts onto a floor of grey ash and blackened debris. Water from the fire brigade's belated efforts pooled in the corners, steaming faintly, carrying flakes of burnt paper on its surface like grey lilies.

Major Renwick crunched across the floor beside him, notebook open, pencil moving. "Press is destroyed, sir. Cast-iron frame cracked clean through — look here. Whoever fired it knew what they were doing. Poured something over it first. Oil, by the smell. Maybe paraffin." He crouched, prodding at a warped mass of metal with his boot. "Type's melted. Lead doesn't take much."

"And the stock?" Havelock-Strand asked. His voice came out flat and controlled, which pleased him. Control was the whole of command, his father had told him once. Control of yourself first, then of others, then — if you were fortunate and ruthless enough — of the record itself.

Renwick walked the perimeter, counting under his breath. "A ream or two, half-burnt, near the back wall. Nothing printed. If there were finished copies, sir, they're gone. Either burned with the rest or—" He stopped himself.

"Or carried out," Havelock-Strand finished for him.

"Yes, sir."

The colonel turned slowly in the centre of the ruin, and the ash rose around his boots in small sighs, and he understood — with a clarity that arrived not as a thought but as a physical sensation, cold water poured down the inside of his chest — that he had lost.

Not lost the raid. The raid had gone precisely as such raids went when the enemy was given six hours' warning. What he had lost was larger, and it could not be recovered by any exercise of rank, because rank operated on documents, and the documents here had been converted into smoke.

He looked up through the broken roof at the sky, white and featureless as an unprinted page.

* * *

They found the hymnbook at half past seven.

A lance-corporal sifting the rubble near what had been the composing bench called out, and Renwick brought it over wrapped in a handkerchief, handling it like evidence of a crime rather than what it plainly was. It was a small volume, cloth-bound, its cover scorched brown at one corner but otherwise intact. *Hymns Ancient and Modern.* The spine was cracked from use. Someone had sung out of this book, many times, in this room where names were being set in lead.

"Inside cover, sir," Renwick said, and opened it.

There, in a careful copperplate hand quite unlike the widow's, someone had inscribed a presentation: *To Céline Dossin, that her hours may be lightened. — E.W.*

Elias Wren. So the courier had a name after all, and had been careless enough to leave it in ink. Havelock-Strand felt a brief, animal satisfaction at that, and then, immediately, a colder feeling beneath it, because he understood what the inscription meant. It meant the book had been left deliberately. Books survived fires when they were carried out beforehand and brought back afterward. This one had been placed in the ash like a coin in a dead man's mouth — payment, or a message, or simply the one thing the arsonists could afford to surrender.

He took the hymnbook in both hands and leafed through it. Between the pages of "Abide With Me" he found a single sheet of paper, folded twice, dry despite everything, which told him it had been set there within the hour.

He read it standing in the ruins, while his men picked through the ashes behind him, and his face did not change, because faces never changed in front of subordinates, that was the first discipline and the last.

The note was unsigned. It said:

*You are looking for forty-one names. You will not find them here. You will find them at home, at Christmas, in the mouths of mothers. You wrote them yourselves, Colonel, in your own reports. We have only read them aloud.*

It was signed with a small drawing — a lantern, sketched in a few strokes of the pen, its flame a tiny star.

He folded the note along its existing creases and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where a man might carry a love letter or a death sentence. Around him the soldiers went on sifting. The widow Dossin was nowhere to be found, nor the printer's apprentice she'd employed, nor anyone who could be made to stand in front of a tribunal and be called a traitor. Neighbours along the Rue Basse opened their doors to Renwick's questioning with the bland, impenetrable helpfulness of people who had seen armies come and go for three years and had learned that the war ended eventually but the neighbours remained.

"No one saw anything, sir," Renwick reported at last. "Fire started around four. By the time the brigade arrived it was fully involved. Widow Dossin — no one knows. Gone to relatives in Ghent, one neighbour says. Another says she never had relatives in Ghent."

"She has relatives wherever the pamphlets are," Havelock-Strand said. He looked down the street. Ordinary shutters. Ordinary morning smells — bread, coal smoke, horse dung. A city swallowing the event whole and digesting it without effort. "Have the house watched regardless. And find me the paper merchant."

"The paper merchant, sir?"

"Every sheet in that press came from somewhere. Paper is requisitioned, rationed, sold against permits. Somebody in this town sold or traded a quantity of good printing stock to a Belgian widow, and merchants keep ledgers." He paused, hearing himself, and something moved at the corner of his mouth that was not quite a smile. "Yes. Ledgers. Everyone keeps them."

* * *

By ten o'clock he was back at headquarters, and the real work began, which was the work of writing.

His office smelled of cold tea and carbolic. On the desk lay the file he had spent five months assembling: the trace of misprinted casualty returns; the discrepancy between the regimental diary's account of the advance of the ninth of October and the figures submitted afterward; the memorandum from Division adjusting the numbers of killed and missing with a bureaucratic delicacy that amounted to forgery; and beneath all of it, the original order — the order that had sent two battalions across eleven hundred yards of open ground against machine-gun positions that every intelligence summary for a fortnight had described in detail. He had not issued that order. But he had countersigned the revised casualty return, in his capacity as staff officer to the corps commander, and a countersignature is a kind of authorship.

That was the fact he had been running from since the first anonymous paragraph had appeared in a leave-train newspaper in December — a paragraph so small most men never noticed it, listing names that official dispatches said did not exist. Names of men marked deserters. Men whose families had received the coward's telegram instead of the hero's. He had known, reading that paragraph, that someone out there held a true copy of the truth, and that the truth had his initials near the bottom of its second page.

Now the press that produced those paragraphs was cracked iron in a Belgian doorway, and the woman who ran it was smoke, and the officer whose testimony anchored the whole affair — Vane, officially dead these fourteen months, a ghost with a grudge — had not appeared at the fire, and still Havelock-Strand sat at his desk with the sensation of a man who has won a battle and lost the country behind him.

Because you cannot court-martial smoke. You cannot charge a lantern with sedition. Every copy of the pamphlet already existed outside his reach — folded into kit bags, riding the leave trains to Boulogne and Étaples, crossing the Channel in the pockets of men going home on embarkation leave. He had watched them go for weeks and told himself the circulation was contained, local, a rumour rather than a document. Now, with the source destroyed, the rumour became immortal. Rumours with sources die when the source is caught. Rumours without sources become scripture.

And there was worse. There was the arithmetic he could not escape, sitting alone with his tea going cold. Forty-one families had received falsified telegrams. If even one mother compared her son's death notice with another's — if even one solicitor or vicar or parish clerk began collecting the discrepancies the way a child collects shells — then the document he was about to write would someday be read alongside the true one, and his signature would sit on both, and history does not need much to hang a man. It needs only two papers that disagree.

He drew a fresh sheet toward him. Good heavy stock, headquarters watermark. He dipped his pen.

*Report on search of premises, Rue Basse, [town], 17th inst.*

He wrote the date. He wrote the time of arrival: oh-five-forty. He wrote that the premises, occupied by one C. Dossin, widow, had been found alight upon the arrival of the search party, cause unknown, possibly a heating accident in the night. He wrote that the press had been destroyed by fire prior to seizure. He wrote that no printed material of a seditious nature was discovered.

All of it true, in the way that a photograph is true — accurate in every particular, and arranged.

What he did not write: that the fire had begun within an hour of his own request for raid authority being granted, which meant someone in his own chain of communication — a signals clerk, an aide, a man in the mess — had passed the word. He did not write that he now understood the leak ran through his own headquarters like a vein through marble. He did not write that the hymnbook and the note sat in his breast pocket, addressed to him personally, telling him exactly what had been done with the forty-one names. He did not write that the search had found, among the ash, the melted stubs of type spelling fragments of words he recognized — *killed*, *advance*, *ninth* — proof that the pamphlet had named dates and orders in plain text.

And he could write none of it, because to write any of it was to put on the record that a true account existed, was circulating, and had escaped. His report must show a minor incident: an accidental fire, an empty room, nothing seized because nothing was there. Any other report invited inquiry, and inquiry was a door that swung both ways.

So he wrote the falsification with a steady hand, and as he wrote he heard, very distinctly, in the theatre of his mind, the voice of the nurse.

He had met her only once, formally, during the inspection — Ashgrove, Sister Freya Ashgrove, steady-eyed, correct, her station immaculate, her casualty ledgers in perfect order. He had offered her a transfer home and a commendation, and she had declined with a politeness so complete it had taken him two days to understand it had been a refusal. He had thought at the time: *another sentimental woman who mistakes nursing for strategy.* Now, sitting in the wreckage of his certainty, he reconsidered. She had declined the offer the way one declines an invitation to be bought. And somewhere between her dressing station and the Rue Basse there had passed, he was now certain, a current — names, lists, corroboration — and he could prove none of it, and pursuing her without proof would only advertise the thing he needed buried.

She had beaten him with the only weapons he could not confiscate: memory and charity. Every name she had recorded, every family she had comforted, every orderly and matron and fellow sister drawn into the work — they were not a network in the way his intelligence officers used the word. They were a congregation. And congregations do not break under interrogation; they disperse, and carry their texts with them.

He signed the report. *A. Havelock-Strand, Col.* The nib scratched, and the sound was very loud in the quiet office, and he thought: *I have just committed the same crime I was sent to punish.* Not the same in scale — he told himself it was not the same in scale — a smoothing of a record, a kindness to the service, a mercy to the living rather than a theft from the dead. But his pen moved the way their pens had moved, converting what happened into what could be borne, and he knew the difference between a mercy and a theft, and he knew which one this was, and he wrote his name anyway.

* * *

In the evening Renwick brought him the day's harvest: statements from four witnesses who had seen nothing, a charred fragment of a delivery invoice suggesting paper purchases totalling several hundredweight over five months — several hundredweight, enough for thousands of copies — and a list of the workshop's suspected associates that read like a census of everyone the colonel had failed to intimidate.

"There'll be a follow-up, sir," Renwick said. "Division will want results. Sedition in the lines — they'll want arrests."

"Division will want the appearance of results," Havelock-Strand corrected. "Arrests produce trials. Trials produce testimony. Testimony produces—" He stopped. *Newspapers. Questions. My name.* "—complications. The matter is closed. Accidental fire. Write it up accordingly."

Renwick hesitated. He was a decent man, the major, decent in the way of staff officers everywhere, which is to say decent until instructed otherwise. "And the pamphlets already in circulation, sir? The ones on the leave trains?"

"What pamphlets?"

The silence stretched. Renwick's pencil hovered. Then, understanding arriving in his face like dusk, he wrote: *No pamphlets recovered.*

"Good man," Havelock-Strand said.

After Renwick left, the colonel sat a long time in the lamplight. He took the hymnbook from his drawer — he had kept it, telling himself it was evidence — and turned to the flyleaf, to the dedication in careful copperplate. *That her hours may be lightened.* He thought about the phrase. He thought about the woman Dossin, whose son's name — he had checked the records that afternoon, unable to stop himself — appeared on no official casualty roll at all, having been erased with the efficiency of a rubber stamp, so that the woman had printed five months of pamphlets about a boy the army insisted had never died.

He thought about the note in his breast pocket. *We have only read them aloud.*

He opened the hymnal to "Abide With Me," because it was the page the note had rested between, and he read the verses standing up, though he could not have said why. *Swift to its close ebbs out life's little day; earth's joys grow dim, its glories pass away; change and decay in all around I see —* He closed the book sharply, as if it had bitten him.

Change and decay. Yes. He was fifty-four years old and he had spent the evening discovering that he was a man who falsified reports, and that he had always been one, and that the only difference between himself and the forgers he hunted was that they had done it for the dead and he had done it for the comfortable. That thought was intolerable, so he did what disciplined minds do with intolerable thoughts: he filed it. He locked it in the same drawer as the hymnbook and the note and the memory of Sister Ashgrove's level eyes, and he turned the key.

But filing is not erasing. He knew that better than any man alive.

* * *

Three days later, a letter reached him through ordinary channels, which was itself extraordinary, because nothing about this affair had come through ordinary channels yet. It was written on cheap paper in a small, upright, feminine hand. There was no signature. There was a sketch of a lantern in the corner, its flame a star.

It thanked him, courteously, for his report of the seventeenth, a copy of which — how? — had evidently reached its author. It observed that the report was a fine piece of work, accurate in every particular and arranged. It noted that a copy had been retained. It expressed confidence that the colonel would wish no further attention drawn to the matter of the Rue Basse, since any thorough inquiry into how a fire had been anticipated would necessarily begin with the signals traffic of his own headquarters, and end — the letter was so polite about this — wherever the evidence led.

Then, last, a single line:

*The names will be read aloud. You may choose the decade.*

He read it four times. Then he fed it to the flame of his desk lamp and watched it curl, and the lantern drawing glowed briefly orange and vanished, and he sat looking at the ash in the tray, thinking: *they have converted my own pen into their accomplice, and my own report into their shield, and there is no order I can give, no arrest I can make, no wire I can send that does not dig the hole deeper.*

Across town — or across the line, or aboard a leave train chugging toward the coast with its cargo of hem-sewn pages — the names were travelling, and he could no more recall them than recall the smoke off the Rue Basse.

He rang for his adjutant and, when the young man appeared, gave the only order remaining to him: that the file on the Rue Basse incident be closed, sealed, and lodged with the archives, marked *no further action.*

The adjutant noted it and withdrew. The colonel sat back in the lamplight, an empty-handed man in a full uniform, guardian of a record that now testified against its keeper, and waited — as the guilty wait, as the defeated wait, as men who have read their own obituary in an unsigned letter wait — for the decade in which the names would finally be read aloud.
