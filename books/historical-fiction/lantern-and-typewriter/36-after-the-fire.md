## 36. After the Fire

The smoke could still be smelled three days later, if the wind came off the flats from the south — a thin, bitter thread of char and hot metal that drifted through the ward at odd hours and made everyone go quiet for a moment without knowing why.

Freya learned to notice it the way she had once learned to notice the change in a wounded man's breathing before the collapse came. She would be folding a bandage or writing a temperature chart, and there it would be: Rue Basse, faint as a ghost's cologne. And her hands would stop, and she would stand in the middle of the ward with lint in her fingers while somewhere behind her eyes a press clattered and Céline Dossin laughed with ink on her chin and a boy named Jules looked out from a line of type no larger than a fingernail.

They had told the official story by then, of course. The colonel's men had told it for them, which was its own kind of mercy. A Belgian printer, suspected of hoarding contraband paper stock; a fire of undetermined origin; no casualties found. *No casualties found.* Freya had almost laughed when she heard the phrase repeated in the mess tent, because it was true in a way no one intended. There were no casualties found because there were no bodies left to find, only ash and a warped iron frame where the press had stood, and a hymnbook — someone said a hymnbook had been found on the doorstep, scorched at one corner, as if thrown clear.

She knew whose hymnbook it was not. But she liked to imagine the colonel holding it, turning its pages, finding nothing inside but psalms.

* * *

The orders came down within the week, as orders do when an army wants a place to forget itself.

Matron Cobb was reassigned first — a base hospital at Étaples, effective immediately, "for administrative reorganization," which was the army's way of saying *we would like you somewhere we can see you*. She read the telegram standing at the end of Ward C with her hands folded over her apron, her face doing what her face always did, which was nothing at all, while around her the ward went on being a ward: the coughing, the groaning, the smell of carbolic and wet wool, a boy from Lancashire calling out for his mother in a voice pitched high as a whistle.

"Étaples," she said to Freya afterward, in the supply room, among the shelves of gauze. "I haven't been posted anywhere since 1914. They've kept me here like a keel keeps a boat steady." She took down a tin of safety pins and began, methodically, to pack it. "Now they want me where I can't steady anything."

"They know," Freya said. It wasn't a question. Since the night of the fire she had stopped pretending there were things Matron didn't know.

Matron set the tin down and looked at her — that long, level look that had stripped generations of young nurses of their pretenses. "Of course they know. They don't know *what*, but they know something moved through this station and out the other side, and they can't catch it, and it infuriates them." She resumed packing. "You'll want to be careful how proud you feel about that. Pride is how they catch people. They wait until you're pleased with yourself and then they knock."

"I'm not proud."

"No," Matron agreed. "You're grieving. That's different, and it's more dangerous." She closed the supply cupboard and turned to face Freya fully, and for a moment her composure shifted the way ice shifts on a pond — not breaking, but showing the dark water underneath. "My brother's name is in your book. You never told me it would be. I found it myself, the night before the inspection, when I went looking for trouble in your kit and found it looking back at me."

"Thomas Cobb," Freya said quietly. "Second Battalion. Erased in September 1915."

"He used to sing off-key," Matron said. "In church, deliberately, so the rest of us would laugh and lose our places. Mother despaired of him." Her hands, Freya noticed, were perfectly steady on the shelf edge. "You will write to me from wherever you land. Not letters that say anything. Letters that say nothing, so I know you're alive."

"Yes, Matron."

"And Freya—" For the first time in all the years Freya had known her, Edith Cobb hesitated before speaking. "When this is over — if any of it ever is — I should like to hear the names read aloud. All of them. His among them. Not written. Read. A name on paper can be burned. A name in the air has to be caught."

Freya promised. It was the easiest promise she had ever made and she knew it might cost her everything, and she made it anyway, and Matron nodded once, briskly, as if accepting a signed chit for extra blankets, and walked back out into the ward to tell a boy from Lancashire that his mother was coming, whether or not it was true.

* * *

Iris left on a grey Tuesday, posted east — Salonika, of all places, half a continent away, where the war was fought in mountains and malaria instead of mud. She came to find Freya at the gate with her kit bag already over her shoulder, and the two of them stood in the cold morning light with the lorry idling behind her, and neither of them knew how to begin.

It had been Iris who had found the workshop's address. It had been Iris who had burned the note rather than carry it to anyone. Freya had never thanked her, because thanking her would have meant naming what she'd done, and naming it would have made Iris complicit in words, and somehow the silence between them had become the truest thing either of them owned.

"You could say it," Iris said finally. "Whatever you're going to say. Say it, and I'll say whatever I'm going to say, and then we'll have done with it."

"I nearly ruined us," Freya said. "All of us. You, and Tam, and Matron, and everyone who trusted me."

"Yes," Iris said. "You did." She adjusted the strap on her shoulder, a small soldierly motion she must have picked up without noticing. "And you were right. Both things are true, and I hate that both things are true, and I have decided to stop hating it on the boat."

Freya felt her throat tighten. "Iris—"

"I burned the note." Iris said it flatly, quickly, like pulling a splinter. "I want you to know that I know that you know, and that I am not going to ask you anything, ever, and that if you send me a letter that says nothing, I will understand it perfectly." She paused. "There's a page sewn into the lining of my kit bag. Under the mess tin. I found it after the fire and I didn't ask how it got there, and I'm not giving it back. If I'm going to Salonika, I'm taking forty-one reasons with me."

Freya stared at her. "How did you—"

"Céline put pages in everything that left that building. Every uniform, every kit roll, every parcel of bandages bound for anywhere." Iris's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "Did you think you were the only one smuggling truth out of Flanders? That woman has been distributing your pamphlet through the entire laundry system since October. Half the nurses on leave trains are carrying him without knowing it. She told me the day before the raid, while she was sewing my hem, very calmly, as if she were telling me the price of thread." She picked up her kit bag. "Her son's name is in my lining now. I intend to be worthy of it, which is more than I can say for most of what I've been worthy of these past four years."

She held out her hand. Freya took it, and then abandoned the hand for an embrace, because some partings refuse formality, and Iris let her, stiffly at first and then not stiffly at all, her chin hooked over Freya's shoulder, her breath unsteady against Freya's ear.

"Come east when it ends," Iris said. "Salonika is beautiful in the spring, everyone says so, though no one has ever seen it in spring because we're all too busy dying. Come and see it. We'll drink something terrible and read names until sunrise."

Then she was up into the lorry, and the tailboard banged, and Freya stood at the gate watching the transport shrink down the road toward the railhead until it was a smudge, and then not even a smudge, and the wind off the flats carried the smell of old smoke across the empty yard.

* * *

Tam was demobilized last — not discharged, exactly; the paperwork called it *transfer to home establishment on medical grounds*, which was the army's delicate way of saying that a seventeen-year-old orderly who had been detained for questioning and released under circumstances the colonel preferred not to examine was more useful far away than near.

He didn't want to go. That was the trouble with Tam; he had never wanted to go anywhere except further in.

"It's not fair," he said, sitting on an ammunition crate behind the kitchen while Freya packed his kit for him, folding his spare shirts the way she'd taught him to fold them a year ago, sleeves in, collar out. "Everyone else gets to stay and finish it, and I get sent home like a — like a parcel."

"You're not a parcel."

"I'm worse. Parcels don't know what's inside them." He kicked at the crate leg. "Sister, I carried those pages. I know exactly what I was carrying. They asked me for four days and I told them nothing, not because I'm brave, but because I genuinely didn't know anything worth telling, and now they're sending me home for being too stupid to inform on my friends, and everyone will say poor Tam, and I'll have to live in a village where nobody knows anything happened at all."

Freya sat down beside him on the crate. Behind them the kitchen chimney smoked gently, cooking something with cabbage in it, and the ordinariness of it was almost unbearable.

"Tam," she said. "Do you remember Private Hollis?"

His kicking stopped. Everyone remembered Hollis — the boy from the second chapter of all their lives, the one who had died asking to be remembered, whose name had gone into the Lantern's early pages in Freya's careful hand while the official dispatch recorded him as *died of wounds, name withheld pending notification*, and then apparently never notified anyone at all.

"He's in my book," Freya said. "Page nine. Alongside thirty-one others who were there that night, and forty-one more from the advance, and now hundreds more besides, because the book didn't stop growing just because the workshop burned. Do you know why it didn't stop?"

Tam shook his head.

"Because it stopped being mine. The night of the fire, Céline put pages into every stitch of cloth that left that building, and most of the people carrying them don't even know what they're carrying, and the ones who do — Matron, Iris, Elias, me — we're scattered now across half the map, and every one of us is a lantern with the shutters open." She took his hand, his big clumsy orderly's hand with the burn scar across the knuckles from the night he'd pulled a man out of a burning dugout. "You're not being sent away from it, Tam. You're being sent *into* England, which is where the families live. Forty-one addresses, and hundreds more coming. Someone has to knock on doors in villages where nobody knows anything happened at all, and say: your son was not a deserter. Your husband did not run. Here is his name, printed, witnessed, true."

Tam was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone thick. "There's a page in my kit bag. Under the foot wraps. I found it when I was packing and I thought maybe you'd put it there by mistake."

"There are no mistakes in Céline Dossin's sewing."

"What do I do with it?"

"Nothing," Freya said. "For now, nothing. Carry it. Let it learn the weight of your shoulders. And when the war ends — truly ends, not the armistice, the ending, which takes longer — you take it out, and you read it, and you decide what kind of man carries a page like that. I think you already know. I think you've known since Hollis."

He wiped his nose on his sleeve, sniffling hugely, seventeen years old and ancient. "Will you write to me? Nothing-letters?"

"Every month. I'll say nothing beautifully."

He laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and hugged her hard enough to crack a rib, and then he was gone down the road with his kit bag over his shoulder like every other boy the war had borrowed and returned slightly larger, walking toward a channel boat and a village and a life in which he alone would know what lay sewn beneath his foot wraps.

* * *

Elias left without ceremony, because Elias Wren did everything without ceremony; ceremony, he said once, was how the army taught people to mistake noise for meaning. But the evening before he went — back to the courier chain, back to the flooded farmhouse country, back to whatever remained of Vane's network now that Vane himself was a grave with no marker — he found Freya in the linen store, counting gauze by lamplight because counting was the only prayer she had left.

He stood in the doorway a while before she noticed him. He had gotten thin again. They both had. The fire had taken something from each of them that food wouldn't replace.

"The last signatures went out yesterday," he said. "Vane's testimony, your ledger's final entries, the whole of it. Twelve copies, sewn and folded and gone in twelve directions. Whatever happens now, happens without us in the middle of it."

"Good," Freya said. Then, honestly: "No. Not good. Empty."

"Both." He came in and leaned against the shelf beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was as close as either of them ever came to saying certain things aloud. "There's an inquiry coming. You know that. The pamphlet's been read in too many places for the War Office to ignore it entirely, and ignoring it entirely is their favorite thing to do. There will be a committee, and closed sessions, and a report that runs to four hundred pages and concludes that errors were made in the fog of war. And then it will be buried."

"Unless someone digs it up."

"Someone usually does." He glanced sideways at her. "Eventually. Years later. Someone stubborn."

Freya folded gauze. Outside, the wind was rising, and somewhere far south the ruins of Rue Basse breathed their smoke into the dark, thinner now, almost gone, the way grief thinned but never quite left.

"Elias," she said. "If they summon me — if the inquiry wants to know where the pages came from, who typed them, who carried them — what do I say?"

He considered this with the seriousness he gave everything. "Say nothing that burns anyone else. Say everything that burns the lie." He pushed off the shelf and stood, and at the door he paused. "Vane used to say that history isn't a record. It's a relay. Each of us runs with it until we can't, and then we pass it, and the passing matters more than the running." He tapped the doorframe twice, his old courier's habit, a message for whoever came next. "Run well, Nurse Ashgrove."

Then the lamp guttered, and the yard outside was empty, and Freya stood alone in the linen store of a dressing station that was emptying around her like a tide going out, holding gauze that no one needed counted.

She opened the supply cupboard, and from beneath a false bottom in the bandage box she lifted the Lantern — the true Lantern, the original, saved from the workshop by her own hands on the last night, its cover scorched at one corner, its pages swollen with names. She had thought, in the fire's aftermath, that she would feel finished. Instead she felt the way a runner feels at the exchange point: emptied, and waiting, and listening for footsteps coming up behind her.

Somewhere in London, men were already drafting questions. Somewhere in the villages of England and Belgium, families were already opening letters they had waited years to receive. Somewhere a report was being written that would try to bury all of it — and somewhere, someday, a pen named Vesper waited to be picked up.

Freya blew out the lamp, tucked the Lantern inside her coat, and walked out into the dark to answer the summons when it came.
