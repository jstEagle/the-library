## 37. The Trial That Wasn't

The inquiry was held in a room that smelled of floor wax and old paper, on the third floor of a government building in Whitehall whose name no one could remember and whose windows had been painted over during the Zeppelin raids and never cleared. The paint had gone the colour of weak tea. Light came in anyway, diffused and sourceless, so that the men seated at the long table appeared to float — seven colonels and a bishop, their faces lit from nowhere, like photographs left too long in a developing bath.

I sat at a smaller table opposite them with my hands folded, wearing the grey dress I had kept from my demobilisation because it made me look like what I was supposed to be: a former nurse of no particular consequence, a woman who had happened to be mentioned in passing by three witnesses and who had therefore been asked, politely, to attend.

No one called it a trial. The word used in the letter was *inquiry*. The word used in the corridor outside, by a clerk with ink-stained cuffs, was *matters arising*. The word I used to myself, sitting in the tealess quiet while the chairman arranged his papers with the deliberation of a man laying out surgical instruments, was *burial*.

"Mrs. Ashgrove." He got my name wrong. I did not correct him. "You served at Number Fourteen Casualty Clearing Station, near Ypres. In nineteen-seventeen."

"I did."

"You will be aware," he said, "that certain documents have been circulating. Unauthorised publications. A pamphlet." He said the word as though it were something he had found stuck to his boot. "You are not accused of anything, Mrs. Ashgrove. Let me make that clear at the outset. This is an inquiry, not a prosecution. We simply wish to establish whether you can assist us in identifying the source of certain claims regarding casualty figures for the action of October the twelfth."

The action of October the twelfth. Forty-one men sent across open ground against machine guns the staff knew were there, and then written off the ledger of the living under headings that varied — *missing*, *deserted*, *died of wounds sustained elsewhere* — but which all amounted to the same thing: they had never been there at all. The advance had been a mistake. The mistake had been made into a fiction. And the forty-one had been made into nothing.

"I may be able to help you," I said.

There was a small stirring among the seven faces, the kind of movement a jury makes when it senses the witness is about to say something inconvenient.

"Your records," the chairman continued, "your ward records, dressing registers, and so forth — these would have been retained by the station?"

"They were burned," I said. "In November of seventeen, when the station was shelled out. Standard procedure before evacuation." This was true, as far as it went. It went exactly as far as I allowed it to go. The Lantern itself had crossed the Channel sewn into the lining of Tam's greatcoat, and it now lived wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards of my sister's house in Norfolk, where I visited it the way other people visited graves.

"So you have no documents."

"I have my memory."

Someone at the far end of the table wrote something down. The bishop looked at his folded hands.

"And your memory," the chairman said, "would confirm or deny the claim — made in this pamphlet — that forty-one soldiers of the Ninth Brigade were killed in the action of October the twelfth and subsequently recorded under false designations?"

I had rehearsed this moment on the train down from Norfolk, watching the fens slide past, rehearsing it the way one rehearses a confession. I had imagined giving them everything: the names, the dates, the ledger, the workshop on the Rue Basse, Céline's bleeding fingers, Vane standing in the doorway of a burning building to sign his testimony with a hand that shook. I had imagined the room going silent, the bishop taking off his spectacles, history arriving at last at its own address.

What I had not imagined was how tired I would feel, how the grey light would flatten everything, how the chairman's voice would carry the particular patience of a man who has already decided the outcome and is merely walking the room through the motions of reaching it.

"My memory," I said, "confirms that forty-one men died in that action whom I personally treated, or whose deaths I personally witnessed, or whose names were given to me by comrades who survived. My memory confirms that none of those forty-one names appears in the official casualty returns for that date. Beyond that, I couldn't say. Memory is not evidence, Colonel. You taught me that yourself, in a sense. It was the army's own insistence on paperwork that taught me what happens to men who exist only in memory."

The silence that followed had texture. One of the colonels, a heavy man with a moustache like a hedge, shifted his weight and his chair creaked, and in the creak I heard the whole architecture of the thing: they were not here to find the truth. They were here to contain it.

"The pamphlet," said the heavy colonel, "makes accusations against officers who cannot defend themselves. Several are dead. One is a serving general."

"One is dead," I said, "because he insisted on signing his testimony." I did not say his name. I had promised myself I wouldn't, not first, not in this room. Let them say it. Let them be the ones to speak Aldous Vane aloud, if they dared.

"We are aware of Captain Vane's death," the chairman said quietly. "It was regrettable."

Regrettable. The word hung in the painted light. I thought of Elias's face when he told me — not the telling itself, which had come in fragments over two years, but the moment after, when we had stood in the street outside my lodgings and he had taken off his hat, which was the only mourning Elias Wren permitted himself. Regrettable. Yes. So was the weather at Passchendaele. So was the mud, the wire, the order, the rewrite, the lie.

"Mrs. Ashgrove," the bishop said, speaking for the first time, in a voice like a warm bath, "you must understand the position of this committee. The war is over. The nation is grieving. To reopen questions of this nature — to suggest that officers falsified returns, that families were misinformed — it would wound thousands of people who have already given everything. Sometimes the kindest thing is to let the dead rest."

I looked at him for a long moment. He bore it well; bishops are trained to bear looking at.

"With respect," I said, "the dead do not need rest. They need their names. It is the living who need rest, and the living will rest easier knowing the truth than wondering about it. I have received letters, sir. From families. Dozens of them. Would you like to hear what a mother writes when she learns that her son was not a deserter? She does not write that she wishes she'd never been told. She writes that she can sleep again. That she has taken the shame off the mantelpiece and put flowers there instead. You speak of wounding people. These people have been wounded for two years by silence. The pamphlet did not wound them. It cleaned the wound."

Nobody answered. The clerk's pen scratched. Somewhere below us a door slammed, and the sound travelled up through the building like a pulse.

We adjourned at noon for luncheon, though no one pretended to eat. In the corridor I found a young man waiting for me, a civilian in a suit too big for him, holding his hat in both hands the way Tam used to hold his, and for one absurd second I thought it was Tam, grown older and thinner, and my heart did something painful. But it was only a stranger — a brother of one of the forty-one. Corporal Edwin Marsh's brother. I knew the name before he gave it, because I had set it in type with my own hands and read it aloud to Céline while she distributed the letters into the composing stick, one small leaden soldier at a time.

"They said I couldn't come in," he said. "They said family weren't admitted. But I wanted — I wanted to shake your hand. If it was you. Are you the one who wrote it?"

"I'm not the one who wrote it," I said, which was technically true; the pamphlet bore no author, and the testimony in it belonged to a dead man and a Belgian widow and an entire household of nurses and orderlies who had carried pages out of a burning building stitched into their uniforms. "But I knew the men. I know your brother's name."

"He wasn't a coward," the young man said. His voice cracked on *coward*. "My mum's waited two years for someone official to say it. Two years of neighbours not meeting her eye."

"No," I said. "He wasn't a coward. He advanced on foot across four hundred yards of open ground against entrenched machine guns, because he was ordered to, and because he was brave, and because stopping was not in his nature. I never met him. But I held the hand of a man who lay beside him, and that man spoke of him until he died. Corporal Marsh was carrying water to the wounded when he was hit. He was carrying *water*."

The young man pressed his hat against his chest and turned away quickly, and I watched him go down the corridor with his shoulders shaking, and I understood then — fully, finally — what the inquiry could never understand. It did not matter what happened in the room upstairs. It had never mattered. The verdict had been delivered years ago, in kitchens and chapels and parlours across three countries, delivered in pencil on cheap notepaper, delivered to me care of a poste restante in Brussels, delivered in handwriting that shook or didn't shake, in English and Flemish and the broken French of mothers who had learned one foreign phrase and spent it all: *merci*. Thank you. As if I had done something. As if setting names in lead and sewing pages into hems were a gift and not merely a debt paid late.

* * *

The inquiry sat for nine days. I attended on four of them. On the fifth, the chairman announced that the committee had examined the documentary evidence — such as could be located — and interviewed the available witnesses, and had determined that the discrepancies in the casualty returns of October 1917 arose from "administrative confusion under conditions of extreme operational pressure," and that while individual errors were acknowledged, no evidence of deliberate falsification had been established to the standard required.

To the standard required. I copied the phrase into my notebook because it deserved preserving. No evidence had been established because the evidence had been burned, buried, scattered across the backs of leave trains in the pockets of nurses, and printed on paper stock whose supplier had been traced and questioned and had died, conveniently, of influenza in January. The one complete record of the truth lay under my sister's floorboards, and I was the only living person who could produce it, and I had looked seven floating colonels in the face and chosen — for now, for the moment, for reasons I am still turning over — not to hand them the match with which they would burn the last of it.

That choice cost me more than any lie I told in the war. Iris would have understood it; Iris, who burned the note with the workshop's address and said nothing, complicit out of love. Matron Cobb would have understood it too, in her way: rules versus people, and the people were forty-one families who had already received the truth through channels no committee could seal. The pamphlet had run to three thousand copies. Three thousand copies, and every one of them alive somewhere, passed hand to hand, read by lamplight, argued over in public houses, copied out in longhand by people who could not risk keeping the original. You cannot hold an inquiry against paper that has become gossip. You cannot court-martial a rumour. Céline had understood that better than any of us. *Print is not the weapon,* she told me once, her fingers bandaged to the first knuckle, ink in the cracks of her knuckles like veins of ore. *Print is the seed. You put it in the ground and you cannot call it back.*

On the final day, the committee thanked me for my attendance and informed me that no further action would be taken, and that I was advised — the word *advised* doing quiet, terrible work — against further public comment on the matter. The chairman shook my hand. His grip was dry and brief, a handshake like a receipt.

Outside, it was raining, the fine grey London rain that doesn't fall so much as hang. Elias was waiting across the street under a awning, hat brim dripping, looking exactly as he had looked the day he arrived at the gate of the clearing station with letters written by a dead man. Two years older. Both of us two years older, and thinner, and quieter, and carrying less.

"Well?" he said.

"'Administrative confusion,'" I said. "'Under conditions of extreme operational pressure.'"

Elias almost smiled. "Forty-one men, administratively confused into desertion."

"They've ruled there's nothing to prosecute. Which means there's nothing to appeal. Nothing to overturn. The lie stands, officially, forever — stamped and filed and closed." I heard my own voice go flat and hard and stopped myself. "And yet."

"And yet," Elias agreed. He took an envelope from inside his coat. It was soft with handling, postmarked twice, forwarded once. "Brussels. From Céline."

I opened it right there under the awning, rain ticking on the canvas above us. Inside were two things: a photograph of the Rue Basse workshop as it now was — a gutted shell, black beams, weeds in the doorway, a child's chalk drawing on the scorched wall that might have been a sun or might have been a press wheel — and a letter in French so direct it needed no translation of the heart:

*They came to question me in March. I told them I printed wedding invitations and prayer cards and that the fire was my fault, a lamp knocked over, an old woman's clumsiness. They believed the old woman. But Freya — I enclose the list. Since winter, thirty-seven families have written to me. Thirty-seven. Some found my name through the pamphlet's printer's mark, which was foolish of me and I am not sorry. They write to tell me their sons' names were spoken in churches, in schoolrooms, at gravesides. In Ypres last month a teacher read all forty-one to her class. Forty-one children stood up, one for each name, and then sat down again. She wrote to ask me if the list was complete. I am sending her the supplementary pages. Tell your English committee whatever you like, ma chérie. The verdict is in. It was delivered in thirty-seven envelopes, and more are coming, and I have begun keeping a new book.*

A new book. I read that line three times. Céline Dossin, sixty-one years old, printing again — prayer cards, perhaps, and wedding invitations, and between them, in small editions on cheap paper, the names, the corrections, the slow stubborn gospel of the erased.

"She's started again," I said.

Elias nodded slowly. "Tam heard from a chaplain on the leave lines. There's talk of a gathering. The anniversary of the advance. October the twelfth." He glanced up the street toward the government building, its painted windows, its waxed floors, its seven colonels and a bishop floating in sourceless light. "Not here. Not anywhere official. A chapel — what's left of one. Out past Poperinge. The one near the flooded farm."

The flooded farm. Vane's hiding place. The place where I had found the myth shrunk to a half-blind man in a chair, and where the mission had grown larger than the myth, and where — two years ago this autumn — I had last seen Aldous Vane alive, arguing that he should stand beside his own testimony while we begged him not to.

"There'll be people there," Elias said. "Families. Some of the others — Matron's written to me, she can get north through Belgium easy enough, she's still got her Red Cross papers. Iris is posted to Cologne but she says she can take leave. Tam—" he smiled properly then, the first time in weeks— "Tam has already bought his ticket. He didn't even wait to be asked. He says—" Elias paused, and his voice did the thing it always did when he quoted Tam, the tenderness he tried to keep out of it failing completely— "he says a boy carried those names once without knowing what they were, and now he'd like to carry them knowing."

I stood in the rain under the awning and thought about verdicts. A committee of seven had spoken that morning, and their words would be printed in a blue-bound report that would sit on shelves gathering the dust of indifference, and in fifty years some historian might pull it down and note, in a footnote, that questions had been raised and formally laid to rest. That would be their monument: a footnote.

But forty-one children had stood up in a classroom in Ypres, one for each name, and sat down again. Thirty-seven families slept. A widow kept a new book. And on the twelfth of October, in a roofless chapel within walking distance of the ground where the forty-one had fallen, the names would be read aloud — all of them, and not only the forty-one, but the hundreds more the Lantern had gathered, the ones from the dispatches and the ones from the margins, the ones Céline had set in type and Tam had carried and Iris had protected by burning and Matron had shielded by silence and Vane had signed for with the last steady motion of his ruined hand.

"Elias," I said, "do you think anyone will come?"

He looked at me as though I had asked whether the tide would come in.

"I think," he said, "that you had better bring the Lantern."

I had it with me. Of course I had it with me. I had not let it out of arm's reach since Whitehall's first morning — it rode in my valise wrapped in oilcloth, under the spare collar and the photograph of a ward full of ghosts, and it weighed nothing and everything, the way a debt weighs. I put my hand through the valise handle and felt the corner of the oilcloth packet against my palm, square and patient, forty-one reasons and several hundred more.

"Then we'd best book passage," I said, "before the committee advises me against that too."
