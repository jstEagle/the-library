## 38. Reading the Names Aloud

The chapel had lost its roof in 1915 and never regained it, so the congregation gathered under open sky, among walls that still stood like patient witnesses. Rain had come and gone for three days, but on the morning of the anniversary it held off, and a thin October sun lay across the broken flagstones like a cloth laid over a table set for guests.

Freya arrived early. She always did now; she had learned that the hour before a hard thing was not to be rushed through but inhabited, the way one inhabited the minute before a dressing change — hands steady, breath even, the work ahead named plainly. She walked the aisle between fallen stones, counting them out of old habit, and stopped at the place where the altar rail had been. Someone had set a lantern there. Not hers — hers was sewn into memory, page by page, uniform by uniform — but a lantern all the same, its glass smoked, its wick trimmed by some careful hand.

She knew whose hand before she turned around.

"You're early too," Tam said. He was twenty-three now and carried himself like a man who had decided to be tall. He wore civilian clothes that fit him badly, and he was holding, with both hands as though it might bolt, a bundle of papers wrapped in oilcloth.

"Someone has to light the lamp," Freya said.

"Matron's coming," Tam said. "I met her at the station. She's slower than she was, but she'd never admit it." He hesitated. "Iris wrote. She can't get leave from Berlin — she's nursing there now, did I say? — but she sent this." He produced an envelope, soft at the corners from much handling. "She asked me to read it if it seemed right."

"It will seem right," Freya said, "when it's time."

* * *

They came all morning, in ones and twos and then in knots of five and six, as though drawn along invisible threads back to the country none of them could quite leave behind. A man on crutches made of a rifle stock and a chair leg. Two sisters in black who turned out, when they introduced themselves, to be the widow and the mother of Private Edwin Marsh — number nine on the list. A former sergeant-major who stood at the door for a long while as though unsure whether he was permitted, until Matron Cobb, arriving on Tam's arm with her stick striking sparks from the stones, said in her parade-ground voice, "Sergeant-Major Doyle, you are late, and you are expected," and he came in like a boy summoned to supper.

Elias Wren came at noon, dusty from the road, carrying two more bundles of pages. He had been collecting them for a year now — reconstructed copies, fragments, whole signatures smuggled out in hems and bandage-rolls and hymnals across three countries. He had learned to ask questions the way a prospector pans a stream: patiently, over and over, trusting the small bright weight at the bottom.

"We have copies enough," he told Freya quietly, laying his bundles beside the others on a trestle Céline Dossin had sent up from Brussels with her compliments and her son's photograph pinned inside the wrapping paper. "Not perfect ones. Some pages exist in four hands, some in one. But the names — the names are all accounted for."

"All of them?" Freya asked. It was the question she had asked every day since the fire, and no answer had ever satisfied her, and this one would not either.

"Four hundred and six," Elias said. "Your forty-one, and everything after. Four hundred and six men written out of the record and written back into it by nurses and orderlies and a Belgian printer who bled onto her type cases doing it."

Freya looked at the bundles. Four hundred and six. She had carried thirty-one of them herself through the wire of her own silence, sewing them into uniforms in a dressing station that smelled of carbolic and fear, and she had believed, then, that she would die with them unspoken.

"Read them aloud," Vane had said, dying in her arms with the road noise behind him and his blood going cold faster than the evening. *That is all. Read them aloud someday, where someone can hear.* She had promised him with her forehead against his hand, which was the only way left to promise anything.

"Someday" had taken seven years. It had taken inquiries that buried themselves, families that wrote letters into a void, a pen name chosen in secret, a manuscript hidden in a floorboard. But someday had also taken this: a ruined chapel, a lit lantern, and four hundred and six names waiting like passengers at a platform.

* * *

Matron Cobb opened the proceedings, because there was no force on earth that could have prevented her.

She stood where the altar had been, leaning on her stick, and looked out at perhaps two hundred people seated on benches brought from the village, on folding chairs, on cushions, on folded greatcoats kept all these years because throwing them away seemed a kind of forgetting.

"I am not a clergyman," she began, "and I will not pretend to be one. I am a matron, retired, and I have spent my life learning two things. The first is that rules are for the living, and must bend when the living are breaking. The second is that people are for keeping — all of them, including the dead, who cannot keep themselves." Her voice cracked only once, on the word *brother*, and she rode through the crack the way she had once ridden through shelling, upright, unblinking. "My brother Thomas was killed on the ninth of May, 1915. You will not find him in any dispatch. Today you will find him here. That is what we have come to do, and we will do it properly, which means thoroughly, and it means no one leaves early."

There was a sound from the congregation that was almost laughter and almost weeping, which was, Freya thought, exactly the sound this gathering deserved.

* * *

They read in relays, because four hundred and six names spoken slowly would take hours, and hours were precisely what everyone had brought.

Tam went first. He stood straighter than Freya had ever seen him stand, even the day he'd been released from detention and walked out past the colonel's office without looking at it, which had been the proudest walk of his young life until possibly this one. He read the first forty-one himself — Freya's forty-one, the deserters who were not deserters, the men erased twice, once by shellfire and once by ink. He read each name, then each regiment, then the date, then the single line Elias had insisted upon: *He fell in the advance of [date], and he fell with his regiment.*

"He fell with his regiment." Six words. They were the entire pamphlet distilled; they were the sentence the colonel had burned a press to prevent; they were, Freya thought, watching the faces in the congregation, more than enough.

When Tam read the ninth name — *Edwin Marsh, Private, 8th Battalion, fell the third of October, 1917, he fell with his regiment* — the widow and the mother rose together, neither supporting the other, both standing absolutely straight, and the mother said, in a voice like a bell struck once, "That is correct. I was told he ran." And then she sat down, and her daughter-in-law took her hand, and nothing else needed saying in that family for a very long time.

* * *

The afternoon turned gold and then grey and then gold again, weather moving over the open roof like a slow procession. The readers changed every half hour. A chaplain who had once refused to bury a "deserter" with full rites read for his turn with tears running freely into his collar. Sergeant-Major Doyle read in a parade voice that shook only on the last syllable of each name, as though each one weighed something. A girl of nineteen whom nobody knew — she had found a copied page folded into a secondhand coat bought in Lille, she said, and had spent two years finding out what it meant — read a full hour, and no one asked how she had gotten there, because that was the nature of the thing now: it traveled, it recruited, it gathered strangers into itself the way a lantern gathers moths, gently and fatally and with a kind of beauty.

Céline Dossin read the names of the Belgians, in French and Flemish both, her voice worn smooth as her old type cases. When she reached her son's name she paused, touched the case of papers at her breast, and said, "Luc Dossin. He fell with his regiment. And I fell with him, a little, every day since, and today I rise." Then she sat down beside Freya and gripped her hand hard enough to leave marks, and Freya let the marks stay.

Elias read. Iris, in absentia, was read by Tam from her letter, which said, in part: *Tell them I chose, in the end. Tell them the note is ash and always will be. Tell them I am proud to have been wrong about my friend, and prouder still that she never once lied about the dead.*

Freya read last of the named readers, because Matron arranged it so and because no one argued with Matron. She stood where Vane had stood in the flooded farmhouse, hollow-eyed and half-blind and insisting on truth the way other men insisted on water, and she read for an hour without stopping, and the strange thing — the thing she would remember all her life — was how easy it became. Each name left her lighter. By the hundredth she felt nearly weightless, as though seven years of carried pages were being lifted from her spine one soldier at a time, each man taking his due and his departure together.

Near the end of her hour, an old woman in the front row began, softly, to answer each name with a single word: *"Here."* Not present in body — these men were under French mud, most of them, or scattered into the chemistry of the fields they died defending. But *here*, meaning: remembered, counted, restored to the roll. Others picked it up. By the time Freya finished, two hundred voices answered every name with *here*, and the sound went up through the missing roof into the open sky, and Freya understood that this was the cathedral the chapel had been trying to be all along: not walls, but voices.

* * *

It was dusk when they finished. Four hundred and six names, four hundred and six answers, and then a silence that no one hurried to break — the good kind of silence, the kind that follows a wound dressed properly.

Matron Cobb rose one final time. "Tea," she announced, "is served in the vestry, such as it is, and anyone who spills sugar on my tablecloth will wish the war had taken them instead." And the congregation broke into the ordinary business of being human again — laughter, jostling, the passing of sandwiches — because catharsis, Freya reflected, is not a lightning strike but a tide, and tides recede into small domestic movements, cups and coats and calls of goodbye.

Elias found her at the lantern as the crowd thinned. He looked older than his years and younger than his deeds, which she suspected was how she looked too.

"There's a letter," he said. "Forwarded through the courier chain — the old one, the one nobody officially closed. It's addressed to 'the Keeper of the Lantern.'" He held it out. "No return address. But the postmark is London, and the hand—" He stopped. His face did something complicated.

"What?" Freya said. "Elias. Whose hand?"

"That's the trouble," he said. "I'd swear it's Aldous Vane's. But Freya — Aldous Vane is dead. We watched him die. I carried you away from him myself."

She took the envelope. The handwriting on its face was unmistakable — the strong downstrokes, the slight forward lean of a man writing against time, a hand she had known first in letters during her training and last in a signature witnessed at a table in a doomed workshop. She turned the envelope over. On the back, in the same hand, four words:

*Some names survive their men.*

Inside the chapel the tea things clinked; outside, the first drops of evening rain struck the broken stones, and the lantern flame bent and held.

Freya broke the seal.
