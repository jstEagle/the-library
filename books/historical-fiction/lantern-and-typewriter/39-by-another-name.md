## 39. By Another Name

The manuscript had been finished for three years before I found the courage to send it anywhere.

It sat in a biscuit tin beneath the loose floorboard of my cottage in Cornwall, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, and every spring I told myself this would be the year. Every autumn I told myself next year. The tin held more than paper. It held forty-one names in my own careful hand — names I had copied from the Lantern on the last night of its existence, when the pages were being unpicked from uniform hems by candlelight and Céline Dossin's hands shook so badly she could not thread her own needle. It held Aldous Vane's testimony, transcribed from his dictation in that flooded farmhouse while rain came through the roof and pooled on the flagstones beside his boots. It held the colonel's order as it was first written and as it was afterward rewritten, both versions laid side by side like a confession and its alibi.

And it held a question I could not answer for a long time: whose name should stand on the cover?

I had tried every answer. My own name — impossible. The inquiry of 1919 had buried the pamphlet's claims with bureaucratic tenderness, but it had not buried the *people*. Men who signed their names to that record were dead or scattered. Elias was alive; I knew because a postcard came each Christmas, unsigned, postmarked somewhere different each year — Trieste one winter, Lisbon the next, once, impossibly, Reykjavik. A living witness could not put his name to it without making himself a target for every officer who had risen on the strength of those falsified reports. And there were such men. The war had ended; careers had not. Some of them sat now in Whitehall offices with maps of the next war already pinned to their walls.

Céline was dead. She died in 1926, in Ostend, in a small room above a bakery, and the sisters who nursed her said she asked at the end whether the book had been printed yet. I have never forgiven myself for the answer they gave her.

Tam was alive and married and teaching carpentry to boys in Birmingham, and he wanted nothing — nothing — ever again to do with paper that men in authority might want to read. I understood. He had been nineteen when they detained him, nineteen and trembling, and he had paid for our courage with a year of nightmares. Iris was in Singapore, matron of her own hospital, having made her peace with the army she had nearly betrayed and never quite forgiven herself for nearly betraying. Matron Cobb had retired to Whitby and grown, if anything, sharper with age. Her letters arrived quarterly, terse as telegrams: *Still alive. Still angry. Have you sent it yet?*

So the name could not be mine, and could not be theirs.

It was Matron who solved it, in a letter dated the fourteenth of March, 1931. I still have it. Her handwriting had deteriorated into an aggressive scrawl, as though the pen itself were being interrogated.

*You keep asking who will sign it. You are asking the wrong question. Ask instead what needs saying. A record is not an author. A record is a lamp set in a window, and no one asks the lamp its surname.*

*But since you insist on a name — use his. He earned it. He paid for it. And you owe him the only thing left to give: the light of day.*

*Sign it Vesper Vane.*

I sat with that letter for two hours by the window, watching gulls work the wind off the Atlantic, and I understood that she had given me something larger than a solution. She had given me a way to disappear inside the truth. Vesper — evening, the hour of lamps, the hour the Lantern was written, always after lights-out, by the glow of a shielded candle. Vane — the man who had seen the order for what it was and been dismantled by seeing it. His name would stand in print where his body could not. My hands had done the work; his name would carry it. And between the two of us there would be nothing — no author at all, only a record, only a lamp.

I wrote to three publishers. Two declined without explanation. The third — a small house in London run by a Quaker named Mr. Pym, who had lost two sons at Passchendaele and printed pacifist pamphlets out of a converted chapel — accepted within a fortnight. His letter said, in part:

*I do not know who you are and I will not ask. But I was at that battle, madam, and I buried boys whose regiments were later told they had deserted. If half of what your manuscript asserts is true, it must be printed, whatever it costs. If all of it is true, God help us, it must be printed twice.*

* * *

The printing took place over eleven days in October 1931, and I insisted on being there.

Mr. Pym had offered to handle everything by post. I told him I needed to see it done, and he did not ask why, which was one of the many reasons I trusted him. I traveled up from Cornwall with a single suitcase and stayed in a lodging house off Clerkenwell Road, and each morning I walked to the converted chapel and stood among the cases of type like a woman standing in a church she had been exiled from.

The compositor was a young man named Alfred with ink-stained fingers and a stutter that vanished entirely when he talked about letters. On the second day he paused over the galley containing the forty-one names and looked up at me.

"They're all different, aren't they," he said. "In here." He tapped his temple. "You can feel it. Most lists, you set 'em and they're just — stock. Rations, casualties, train times. This lot, each one's got a weight."

"They do," I said.

"How d'you know them all?"

"I was there when most of them died."

He did not ask anything else. He simply worked more slowly after that, letter by letter, the way one walks through a ward at night. By the fifth day I noticed he had begun setting the names first thing in the morning, when his hands were steadiest, before the ordinary work of the day had tired him. No one had instructed him to do this. Some knowledge passes from type to hand without passing through speech.

There was a moment, on the eighth day, that I have never managed to describe to anyone without my voice going strange. Alfred lifted the first complete signature — sixteen pages, folded — off the press, and before he trimmed or bound or did anything at all, he held it out flat on both palms, the way Tam had once held a wounded man's dog tags, and Mr. Pym came and stood beside me, and we looked at it together in silence.

Sixteen pages. In 1917, sixteen pages like these had come off Céline Dossin's press on the Rue Basse and gone out sewn into hems and folded into bandages, carried on leave trains by nurses and orderlies who knew they were committing sedition and carried them anyway. That press had burned rather than surrender its plates. Céline had watched her son's memorial go up in smoke and called it a proper funeral. And here, fourteen years later and four hundred miles from the fire, the same words rose again out of the same kind of machine, smelling of the same ink.

"Second printing," I said quietly, to no one in particular.

Mr. Pym heard. "No," he said. "First printing. The other one was the proof."

* * *

The title page read:

**THE LANTERN: BEING A TRUE RECORD OF FORTY-ONE SOLDIERS ERASED FROM THE DISPATCHES OF THE THIRD ARMY, WITH THE TESTIMONY OF CAPTAIN ALDOUS VANE AND THE ORDER THAT KILLED THEM**

*by Vesper Vane*

We argued about the subtitle. Mr. Pym thought it too long. I told him every word of it had been fought for, and he let it stand.

The pen name did its work perfectly, which is to say it did its work invisibly. Reviewers speculated. A columnist at the *Daily Mail* devoted half a column to guessing at the author's identity — a staff officer? a chaplain? a widow of one of the erased? The speculation was, I confess, useful; controversy sold books better than grief ever has. None of them came near the truth. No one guessed a nurse. We had been trained, all of us, to be furniture — competent, invisible, reliable as bedpans. The country had looked through us for four years. It seemed it could look through us still.

Vane's old regimental association wrote an outraged letter demanding to know by what right an anonymous party impugned the honor of officers whose descendants were living gentlemen. Mr. Pym framed it and hung it in the chapel lavatory, where, he assured me, it received a great deal of attentive readership.

But then the other letters began.

They came first in a trickle, then in a steady rain, addressed to the publisher, forwarded to me through a poste restante box in Penzance under the name Mrs. E. Marsh — a name I invented on a wet Tuesday and grew strangely fond of. The writers were mothers. Sisters. Sons born after the fathers died. One envelope contained a pressed poppy and nothing else. Another contained a photograph of a young man with a crooked smile, and on the back, in pencil: *Pte. J. Whitcombe — No. 14 on your list. Mum kept his kit bag 14 yrs. Nobody would tell her why he got no medal. Now she knows it wasn't him. Thank you. Thank you. Thank you.* Three times, underlined deeper each pass, until the pencil had nearly torn the card.

I answered every letter. I could not help myself. I sat at my kitchen table in Cornwall, night after night, writing back as Mrs. Marsh, telling women things I had told no one: how their sons and brothers had actually died, on what ground, facing what, and that the word *deserter* had been a lie stitched over their names by men who had never stood within a mile of a machine-gun position. I told them the truth cost nothing to give away but had cost everything to carry. Several of the correspondents figured out — women are not fools — that whoever answered their letters had been *there*. One wrote back: *Whoever you are, dear, you write about the mud like someone who has knelt in it. God bless you, whichever sex you are.*

I kept those letters in a second tin. The first tin held the record; the second held what the record had raised from the dead.

* * *

Elias came in the spring of 1932.

I heard the gate before I saw him — that particular uneven footfall, left heel striking harder than right, a souvenir of a wire entanglement neither of us ever discussed. I was in the garden, on my knees in the bean row, dirt to the wrists, and I did not get up. Some reunions are better conducted at ground level.

"You're older," he said, when he reached the fence.

"So are you."

"Yes. Well." He looked at the cottage, the low slate roof furred with lichen, the lamp visible even in daylight through the front window — I kept it burning, though I had never explained to anyone why, and he did not ask. "I read it," he said. "Cover to cover. Twice."

"And?"

"And I sat in my hotel room in Paddington and cried like a boy, if you want the honest account of it. Then I went down to the lobby and bought a second copy and posted it to a man in Brussels who was there on the Rue Basse the night of the raid. He lost two fingers to a bayonet that night and has spent fifteen years telling people he cut them off in a printing accident." Elias smiled, and the smile had all the old wry weariness in it, aged like everything else. "Now he won't have to lie anymore. You've no idea what that's worth to a man. To stop lying after long enough — it's like putting down a pack you forgot you were carrying."

"It's Vane's doing," I said. "Not mine."

"It's everyone's doing, and no one's, which is exactly as it should be. That's the trick of it, isn't it? The one decent trick this whole business ever taught me." He crouched down beside the bean row, joints cracking, and studied my hands in the dirt. "Matron told you to use his name. I heard her say it, actually — she wrote to me too, did you know that? Terrible woman. Wonderful woman. She said, and I quote, *the girl will hide forever unless someone shoves her into the light sideways.* So she shoved you sideways. With his name. Clever to the last."

"Elias." I pulled off a glove. "Why did you really come?"

He was quiet for a moment. Gulls went over, crying their thin cries. Out past the hedge the sea did what the sea does, endlessly, indifferent and faithful at once.

"There's a man," he said finally. "A journalist. Dutch. He's been collecting copies of the pamphlet — our pamphlet, Céline's — for years. The ones that survived, the ones sewn into uniforms, the ones that got home in bandage rolls. He's found eleven complete copies. He wants to do a piece, a proper one, continental papers, wide circulation. About the raid. About Céline. About the forty-one." He turned his hat in his hands, an old nervous habit I'd forgotten. "He wants to interview the people who were there. And Freya — there aren't many of us left who can say *I was there*, and fewer every year. Céline's dead. Vane's dead. Half the workshop's dead. He's coming to England next month, and I can give him a list of names, or I can burn the request and tell him the witnesses are all ghosts."

I looked at my hands. Nurse's hands once; gardener's hands now; author's hands, secretly, all along.

"If I talk to him," I said slowly, "I'm not a ghost anymore. Ghosts are safe. That was the entire architecture of the pen name — the whole point—"

"The whole point," Elias said, "was the truth getting out. It's out. It's *in print*, Freya, it sits on shelves. What isn't out is *you*. The woman who wrote the Lantern by candlelight. The woman who hid it in a coffin inventory while a colonel breathed down her neck. The woman who sewed pages into her own petticoat and walked them through a checkpoint singing hymns with her heart going like a drum. There is a person attached to that record, and she has spent fifteen years pretending she's a pseudonym." He stood, wincing, and looked down at me in the bean row with an expression I remembered from another life — the expression of a man delivering orders he hated giving. "You don't have to decide today. You never have to decide. But I'll tell you what Matron would say, because I know you're wondering. She'd say: *two kinds of people, girl — those who keep rules and those who keep people. You kept people. People are still keeping you.*"

He stayed for tea. He admired the beans. He left on the evening train, and from the gate he called back one last thing, not turning around, speaking to the lane rather than to me:

"He'll be in Penzance on the ninth of June. Room above the chandlery. In case a certain Mrs. Marsh happens to be walking past."

Then he was gone down the road, uneven footsteps fading, and I stood in the garden until the light went the color of brass and the first star came out over the water, and I went inside and lit the lamp.

* * *

I did not sleep that night. I sat at the desk beneath the framed final page of the Lantern — my own name at the bottom of it, in the place where witnesses sign, the entry I added the night Vane signed his testimony knowing it signed his death warrant — and I thought about names.

All my life, it seemed, names had been the whole of the argument. Names erased from dispatches. Names restored in a hidden ledger. Names burned rather than surrendered. A dead man's name given to a living woman so the truth could walk abroad wearing a borrowed coat. Forty-one families who had waited fourteen years to learn that the word *deserter* was a forgery. And underneath it all, my own name, folded away like a page sewn into a hem, carried through checkpoints, never declared, never lost.

Mrs. E. Marsh. Vesper Vane. Nurse Ashgrove, of whom the official record said almost nothing, which had always been precisely the point.

On the ninth of June, I told myself, I could remain a ghost. The book was out. The names were read. Mothers had stopped believing lies. Wasn't that enough? Wasn't that the victory, complete?

But I thought of Céline dying above a bakery, asking whether the book had been printed, and the sisters telling her no, and I understood that there was one question worse than exposure. It was the question the Dutch journalist would otherwise write, in wide circulation, across a continent: *who were they?* Not the forty-one — those names we had saved. But the ones who carried the lantern. Somewhere in the world there ought to be one living face attached to the flame. Otherwise the record would float free of human hands forever, and a truth no one will claim is a truth half-buried. I had spent fifteen years learning that lesson from the other direction: the colonel's report on the raid, describing ash and a hymnbook and no one responsible, had been believed precisely because no one would stand behind it. Anonymous falsehood and anonymous truth suffer the same disease. They can be denied.

I am tired of things that can be denied.

The lamp burned steadily on the desk. Outside, the sea kept its long appointment with the shore. On the ninth of June, the tide comes in at Penzance in the morning, and there is a chandlery on the harbor road, and a room above it where a Dutchman will be waiting to meet whoever dares to climb the stairs.

I took out a clean sheet of paper, and my hand — the steady one, the one that had written the Lantern by shielded candle while men died an arm's length away — did not shake at all.

I began: *Dear Sir. You do not know me, but you have been reading my work for some time...*
