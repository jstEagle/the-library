## 1. The Weight of Ordinary Paper

The trick to being forgettable, Sable Eriksen had learned by his third year on the roads, was to be slightly disappointing in every category at once. Not handsome enough to be remembered as handsome, not ugly enough to be remembered as ugly. A face like a receipt for something already purchased. He wore a coat the color of wet cardboard and a hat that had surrendered its shape sometime during the second winter of the war, and he practiced, in tavern mirrors and checkpoint queues, a way of standing that suggested he was waiting for someone more interesting than himself.

It worked. Nine years of carrying things across the Republic of Vessany — dispatches, salaries, sealed orders that made colonels go pale — and not one soldier, smuggler, or customs clerk could later describe him with any confidence. They remembered the weather. They remembered the mud. Once, memorably, a woman at a ferry crossing recalled in vivid detail a courier who did not exist and never had, while Sable stood beside her nodding along.

He was proud of this. He would have said, if pressed, that it was a kind of patriotism. The Republic was dying — everyone knew it now, the way you know a fire has reached the stairs — but it died a little slower because men like him moved quietly through it, stitching its last provinces together with paper. Sable did not think of himself as brave. Bravery required being noticed. He thought of himself as necessary, which was quieter and, he felt, more durable.

History happened to other people. That was the whole philosophy, really, and it had kept him alive through three offensives, two changes of command, and the winter the Kesselmoor road closed. He carried things; what was in them belonged to generals, ministers, men whose signatures were heavier than their bodies. His job began when the seal was waxed and ended when it was broken. Everything between was mud and mileage, and he was very good at both.

So when the summons came — not a summons, exactly, but a note slipped under his door at the Kestrel Rooms in Vessany City, unsigned, saying only *Marrow Lane, dusk* — he went without drama. Dusk on Marrow Lane smelled of coal smoke and the river's low tide, and the Ministry annex there had lost most of its windows to the spring bombing and replaced them with boards painted to look like sky. Sable had always found that detail more obscene than the ruins themselves.

His handler was waiting on the third floor, in a room that still had glass.

Her name was Deputy Corvane, though she had been three different names in the nine years Sable had known her, and he suspected she'd been four before that. She was a small, precise woman who filed her fingernails during briefings and could recite the border tariffs of six neighboring states from memory. She had never once shaken his hand. She had never once asked about his health, or his mother, dead eleven years, or anything else that might constitute a human transaction. This was why he trusted her. Trust, in Sable's experience, was just familiarity with someone's indifference.

"You're looking well," she said, which was how she always began, and which meant nothing, and which he had come to find almost fond.

"I'm looking unremarkable," he said. "That's better."

"Sit."

There were two chairs and a table, and on the table a leather satchel, plain, scuffed at the corners, identical to a hundred satchels Sable had carried before. Beside it lay a folded travel pass stamped with the double-headed crane of the Republic, and beneath that, weighted under an inkwell, a single sheet of instructions in Corvane's own cramped hand.

"Halven Bridge," she said.

Sable kept his face pleasant and empty. Halven Bridge was the border city where, if the newspapers were to be believed — and they printed only what the Ministry allowed, so they believed very little — the armistice delegation would convene within the month. The Republic's envoys on one side of a long table. The Coalition's on the other. And somewhere between them, the terms that would decide whether Vessany continued to exist as a line on maps or became a paragraph in other countries' histories.

"That's the war," he said carefully. "I don't go to the war."

"You're going to the end of the war. There's a difference." Corvane turned the satchel so the buckle faced him. "Codes. Cipher keys for the delegation's final communications — recognition protocols, verification sequences. Without them the envoys can't authenticate the terms. With them, everything signed at that table is binding and real." She slid the travel pass toward him. "You'll carry them personally. No relays, no drops. You in Halven Bridge by the twentieth, no exceptions, no delays worth mentioning."

Sable looked at the satchel. It weighed nothing. It always weighed nothing; that was the joke of the work. The most important cargo of his career and it would ride against his hip lighter than a loaf of bread.

"Why me?"

"Because you're nobody," Corvane said, without cruelty, the way she might have said *because it's raining*. "Every reliable courier we have left is known on the roads. Faces posted at crossings. Half of them are probably already dead; the other half are worth intercepting. You—" she looked at him with something that was almost, almost warmth, "—you are the only man in this Ministry whom I genuinely believe no one will look at twice. That is your entire value, Eriksen, and I advise you not to improve it."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good." She tapped the instruction sheet once. "Standard protocols. Seals stay intact. If you're searched, it's commercial correspondence, dye invoices, you're a factor's agent for Halloran & Sons out of Brech. The story holds up; I wrote it myself and I've used it four times. Don't improvise. Improvisation is how couriers become interesting."

"And after delivery?"

"After delivery you're done. Paid in full, papers cleared, and if the Republic survives you'll get a pension, and if it doesn't—" she shrugged, a small economical motion, "—then no one will ever have employed you at all, which may be the safest condition available to a man these days."

It should have been a relief. It *was* a relief, mostly. Nine years, and at the end of the road to Halven Bridge there was a door marked *finished*, and beyond it whatever ordinary life remained to a man whose greatest talent was vanishing in plain sight. Sable picked up the travel pass, folded it into his inner pocket where the dye invoices lived.

Then Corvane reached for the satchel to hand it to him, and her hands shook.

Not much. Not the way a drunk shakes or a frightened man shakes. It was a fine tremor, quickly mastered, the kind you'd miss if you hadn't spent nine years learning to read handlers the way other men read weather. But Sable had watched this woman sign death warrants over tea without spilling a drop, and now the satchel trembled between her fingers for the length of half a breath before she set it down on the table instead of passing it, and smoothed her skirt, and said, in a voice flattened clean of everything:

"The codes are correct. The seals are correct. What happens after Halven Bridge is above both our stations, and my advice to you is the same advice I give myself: carry it, don't carry it home in your head."

"Deputy," Sable said slowly, "is there something in this bag I should—"

"There is nothing in that bag," Corvane said, "but paper."

She turned to the window then, to the painted boards pretending to be sky, and Sable understood the audience was over. He took the satchel. Buckled it. It hung against his hip light as bread.

At the door he paused, because some instinct older than his caution made him pause. "You've never had shaking hands before," he said. "In nine years."

Corvane didn't turn around. "Everyone's hands shake eventually, Eriksen. It's the century." A beat. "Twentieth, by the way. The day after tomorrow at the latest. Roads east of the Marn are burning."

He went down the stairs past boarded windows and out into the coal-smelling dusk, and told himself, the whole walk back to the Kestrel Rooms, that it was nothing. Handlers got tired. Everyone's hands shook; it was the century. The satchel held codes, cipher keys, arithmetic dressed in wax. Whatever was decided at Halven Bridge would be decided by ministers and marshals at a long table in a city he'd never seen, by people whose names would appear in histories he would never read.

History happened to other people. He had built a life on that sentence, and the sentence had never once failed him.

* * *

He left on the nineteenth, two days early, because the eastern roads were burning and because leaving early was itself a form of unremarkability — no one remembers the man ahead of schedule. The morning was grey and cold for late spring, and the road north out of the city was thick with the wrong kind of traffic: not merchants, but families, carts stacked with furniture and children, the whole slow river of people who had finally stopped believing the war would pass them by. Sable moved against that current, alone, unremarkable, a factor's agent carrying dye invoices to Brech.

By midday he had put ten miles behind him and the city was a smudge behind, and the satchel rode his hip light as ever, and he had nearly succeeded in thinking about nothing at all — his finest skill — when the road narrowed through a burned orchard and a checkpoint rose out of the ash.

Two soldiers, a barrier, a folding table. Behind the table sat a woman with a census ledger open before her, a pen moving steadily, asking each refugee in turn the questions censuses ask: name, age, origin, occupation, number of dependents. Her voice carried in the flat quiet of the orchard, patient, unhurried, a voice that expected answers.

Sable joined the queue and began, methodically, to become no one: shoulders down, gaze mild, the factor's agent from Brech assembling himself layer by layer like a coat.

The line moved. The pen scratched. And when his turn came, the census taker looked up at him with clear grey eyes and said:

"Name?"

And then, before he could answer, before he could even arrange his face: "And tell me, sir — coming from the city — did you pass by way of the Kesselmoor road?"
