## 2. One Question Too Many

The checkpoint at Marn's Ford was nothing — a sawhorse barrier, a brazier burning greenish fuel oil, two soldiers and a clerk with a folding table. Sable had passed through forty checkpoints like it in eleven years of carrying things for the Republic of Vessany, and he had a method: be dull, be brief, be gone.

He rehearsed it on the last hundred meters of road. Name: Sable Eriksen, freight agent. Business: commercial correspondence, Vessany Consolidated Paper. Destination: Halven Bridge, to negotiate a supply contract before the border closed. All of it true in the way that mattered, which is to say none of it was false enough to trip a man up under repetition. The best cover story wasn't a lie at all. It was a life so small no one wanted it.

He'd learned that early. His mother had been memorable — a singer, red hair, opinions about the Provisional Council delivered loudly in market squares — and memorable people in Vessany tended to end up on lists, then in files, then nowhere. His father he barely remembered; the war took him in the second autumn, one line in a casualty gazette. Sable had taken the opposite lesson. He dressed in the gray-brown of a man who sold something boring. He kept his voice level and his eyes down. He gave soldiers exactly what they asked for and never a word more, because extra words were invitations, and invitations were how forgettable men became interesting ones.

Eleven years, and no soldier had ever remembered him an hour after he passed. He counted that his great achievement. History, he told himself, happened to other people. He just moved paper through it.

The soldier on the left looked seventeen and cold. The soldier on the right looked bored. The clerk at the table —

The clerk was not what he expected. Checkpoint clerks were usually old men with ink-stained cuffs or boys too clumsy for rifles. This was a woman in a census office coat, slate-blue, brass buttons gone dull, with a leather ledger open before her and a pen already moving. She had dark hair pinned back without ceremony and the kind of face you forgot until she looked at you directly, and then you didn't forget it, because her looking had weight to it, like a hand set flat on a table.

"Next," said the bored soldier.

Sable stepped up. "Eriksen. Freight agent." He put his papers on the table: transit permit, trade license, a letter of introduction from a paper merchant in Osterlin whose signature Sable could reproduce in his sleep because he had, in fact, written the letter himself. Everything a clerk needed to stop looking at him.

The woman read. Her name was stenciled inside her coat collar where it gaped slightly at the throat: BRANDT, I., CENSUS DIRECTORATE 4. Her pen ticked boxes. Name, origin, destination, purpose. Sable answered each question in four words or fewer. He watched the bored soldier drift back toward the brazier and felt the familiar loosening in his chest, the almost-done feeling, the moment when a man stops being a problem and becomes a line in a ledger.

Then Brandt asked a fifth question.

"What did you see on the Kesselmoor road?"

Sable's face did nothing. That was the training, if you could call eleven years of survival training: the face does nothing while the rest of you catches up. But something behind his ribs went tight and small, like a fist closing around a coal.

"I came up from Osterlin," he said. "I don't come by Kesselmoor."

"You came by the ferry crossing at Dult." She hadn't looked up. The pen was still moving, ticking some box he couldn't see. "There are two roads north from Dult. The low road runs through Kesselmoor parish. Which did you take?"

"The high road."

"When?"

"Does it matter? I'm carrying commercial correspondence, not—"

"It matters." Now she did look up, and her eyes were brown and very still, the stillness of water over something deep. "It matters because the low road has been closed since the third of the month and no one will tell me why, and I have been asking travelers for nine days, and every one of them tells me they took the high road."

"Then everyone took the high road."

"Yes," she said softly. "Isn't that remarkable."

The seventeen-year-old soldier had drifted back, curious. Brandt dismissed him with a flick of two fingers — not a request, Sable noted, but a gesture that assumed obedience, the way actual officers gestured — and turned back to her ledger. And Sable saw, as her sleeve shifted, the second book.

It was smaller than the census ledger, bound in cheap gray board, the kind of notebook a schoolboy might carry. It lived inside the big ledger like a letter inside an envelope, and when she moved her arm he saw perhaps half a second of its pages: columns, dense handwriting, dates.

Census takers did not keep private notebooks. Census takers recorded heads of household, livestock, grain stores, taxable hearths. What they did not record — what no official of any Directorate was permitted to record, per Directive 19, which every functionary in the Republic had memorized along with their oath — was testimony. Accounts of military activity. Names of the dead outside official channels. Anything that might, in the language of the Directive, *prejudice the historical record*.

Directive 19 had been issued in the spring, when the front began moving backward instead of forward. Everyone understood what it meant. The Republic was going to lose, and the men negotiating the peace intended to lose cleanly. No stains on the surrender papers. No inconvenient pages for the victors to wave about later. Whatever happened in places like Kesselmoor parish would happen and then would not have happened, and the men who signed the armistice would shake hands across a table with clean histories, and Sable Eriksen would carry whatever sealed thing they needed carried and ask nothing, because asking was the first step toward knowing and knowing was the first step toward ending up on a list.

"Your papers are in order, Herr Eriksen," Brandt said.

He reached for them.

She held them one beat longer. Not dramatically. Just long enough.

"The high road adds six hours," she said, conversational now, almost idle, as if remarking on weather. "Every traveler says the same. You all say the same words, do you know that? 'The high road.' Same inflection, most of you. As though you rehearsed it." She slid the papers across the table. "As though someone told you to say it."

"No one told me anything."

"Mm." She made a final mark in the ledger. "Pass through, then. Mind the mud past the mill."

Sable took his papers and walked. He kept his pace even — a hurrying man was an interesting man — and passed the barrier and the brazier and the bored soldier, and he did not look back, because men who looked back were remembering, and remembering was dangerous, and besides there was nothing behind him except a checkpoint and a woman doing her job.

Except it was not her job. That was the thing that sat in him like a swallowed stone as he walked the next kilometer through bare poplars with the river flashing silver between the trunks. Asking travelers what they'd seen on a closed road was not her job. Keeping a secret notebook was not her job. Dismissing soldiers like a general was not her job. She was doing something else entirely, out here in the mud at Marn's Ford with a folding table, and she was doing it alone, which meant either she was mad or she believed she had company somewhere — a network, a plan, a use for whatever a courier might have seen.

*I don't come by Kesselmoor.* He had said it smoothly. He had said it well. And it was true, which was the strange part — he genuinely hadn't come by Kesselmoor. But he knew why the low road was closed. He knew because two nights ago, in a taproom in Dult, a carter had drunk too much plum brandy and talked about smoke on the southern horizon and soldiers turning refugees back from the parish line, turning them back with rifles, and the carter had said a word — one word, half-swallowed — that Sable had chosen not to hear. He'd finished his beer, paid, and gone to bed. In the morning he'd taken the high road and thought no more about it, because thinking was not his trade.

Carrying was his trade. Carrying, and forgetting.

He stopped at a stream crossing to refill his flask, and found himself doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. Nine days she'd been asking. Every traveler saying *the high road*. A woman with a contraband notebook, collecting the shape of a silence — because that's what she was doing, he realized; she was mapping the hole. Where the silence was thickest, that's where the truth was buried. It was a census of the unsaid. It was, he thought with something uncomfortably close to admiration, the most honest document in Vessany, and it was hidden inside the most dishonest one.

And she had looked at him. That was the part he kept circling back to, walking the muddy kilometers north with his collar up against the drizzle. Not at his papers — at him. Everyone looked at papers. Papers were safe; papers were the whole system working as designed. But she had looked at *him*, had weighed him, had let him go with the papers and kept something else — some account of his face, some entry in that gray notebook that said *freight agent, evasive, knows more than he carries.* For eleven years he had been invisible, and in ninety seconds this woman had seen him, and being seen felt — 

He didn't have a word for it. It felt like both a threat and an invitation, and he hated that the invitation half was louder.

At dusk he reached the coaching inn at Verlaine Cross, three rooms and a leaky roof, and paid for a bed with exact change and ate his stew in the corner with his back to the wall, and told himself the day was over and the codes were dry in their oilskin wrap against his ribs and tomorrow he'd make Halven Bridge by nightfall and be done with history forever.

He was halfway through the stew when the door opened and the drizzle blew in, and with it a woman in a slate-blue census coat, travel-stained to the knee, a canvas satchel over one shoulder and a folded census ledger under her arm — and, tucked inside the ledger where only Sable could see it, the corner of a gray schoolboy's notebook.

Brandt scanned the room, found him instantly, and did not look away.

"Herr Eriksen," she said, shaking rain from her coat. "Fancy meeting a man who takes the high road."
