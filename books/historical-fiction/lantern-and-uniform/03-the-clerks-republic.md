## 3. The Clerks' Republic

The granary had been a granary the way a skull had been a face. Ilsa led Sable up the broken loading ramp at dusk, when the shelling to the east paused long enough for birds to reconsider the sky, and she watched him take it in: the blown-open south wall, the silos punctured like tins, and in the middle of all that ruin — lamplight. Dozens of lamps. Small ones, oil-stingy, arranged along trestle tables with the discipline of a field hospital.

"What is this place?" Sable said.

"A census," Ilsa said.

He gave her the look she'd been collecting since the checkpoint: the courier's flat nothing-face, the one that said *I carry things, I don't examine them.* She was getting better at reading what moved underneath it.

Inside, the smell was paper and lamp oil and cold grain dust, and under that, faintly, the sweet rot of the war itself. Clerks sat at the tables in coats too thin for October, writing. Not requisitions. Not ration chits. Names. Page after page of names in a dozen different hands, ruled into columns by people who had clearly once worked for ministries and could not stop working even though the ministries were ash.

A woman near the door looked up, saw Ilsa, and nodded her toward the far end of the floor, where an old man stood over a table built from two doors laid across barrels. He was very tall and had been taller; he folded himself around his work like a heron around a fish. His hands were enormous, blotched with ink to the second knuckle.

"Pell," Ilsa said. "I brought one."

Pell did not look up immediately. He finished the line he was writing — Sable would learn later that Pell never abandoned a line mid-entry, not for shells, not for blood — and then he straightened as much as his spine allowed and studied the courier from boots to collar with the unhurried attention of a man appraising a document of uncertain provenance.

"One what?" Pell said.

"I don't know yet. He was on the Kesselmoor road the week after."

Something changed in the old man's posture. Not excitement. Something more careful than that.

"Then he'd better sit down," Pell said, "and tell me what he saw. Or tell me he saw nothing, which is also information, if you understand how to read it."

"I'm a courier," Sable said. "I don't—"

"You're sitting down," Pell said, "or you're leaving. Those are the two documents you can sign here."

* * *

They sat him at a corner table with a lamp and a cup of barley tea that tasted of scorched iron. Around them the clerks went on writing. Sable kept glancing at them the way a man glances at a fire he hasn't decided whether to warm his hands at or run from.

Ilsa opened her notebook on the table between them — the contraband notebook, the census no ministry had authorized — and waited. She was good at waiting. It was half her profession.

"The road out of Kesselmoor," she said finally. "You came through three days after. What did you see?"

"Roads," Sable said. "Mud. A burned wagon."

"A burned wagon." She wrote it. The scratch of her pencil seemed louder than it should have been.

"There was a ditch," he said, after a while, "that wasn't a ditch."

She stopped writing. Across the room, Pell had gone still over his ledger without appearing to.

"Say it properly," Ilsa said quietly. "For the record. Ditches hold water. What held yours?"

He looked at the lamp. At the columns of names marching down every page in sight. At the woman three tables over who had written *Vaschenko, Marta, b. 1901, seamstress, Kesselmoor* and drawn a small careful line beneath it, the line they drew under the dead.

"Forty-one hours," Sable said. "That's how long the column took to pass where I lay. I counted because counting kept me quiet. Soldiers on both sides of the road. And the ditch full of—" His voice did something he didn't authorize. "Civilians. They'd been made to kneel at the culvert first. You could see the arrangement of it. Someone had organized the killing. There were stacks of their papers burned separately. Identity cards. Like they were erasing the names before the bodies."

Silence, except for pencils. Three clerks within earshot had stopped pretending not to listen, and now wrote faster.

Issa — Ilsa — slid her notebook across the table and turned it to a fresh page.

"Write it," she said. "Your own hand. Date, hour if you know it, what you counted. We don't transcribe testimony secondhand if we can help it. A record should be able to say: this man swore it, in this ink, on this night."

"Why?" Sable said, and there was something raw under the question. "Who reads it? Who's left to read it?"

Pell had come over without sound. Old men that size shouldn't move quietly, but he did.

"Regimes fall," Pell said. "I've filed paperwork under four of them. Each one believed, sincerely, that it was permanent architecture. Each one left behind exactly one thing that outlasted it." He tapped the ledger with one huge blotched finger. "Paper. Records outlive regimes, young man. Armies burn cities and the cities get rebuilt wrong, but a true entry — a date, a name, forty-one hours counted by a living witness — that survives translation, occupation, amnesty, everything. That's why we keep it here in a hole in the ground instead of a ministry. Ministries get captured. Holes get forgotten, and forgotten is a place you can hide a truth until it's needed."

"And when is it needed?"

"Sooner than you think," Pell said. "There's talk of an armistice at Halven Bridge. Talk means signatures. Signatures mean someone will write the official history of this war, and I promise you the official history has already been drafted, and I promise you Kesselmoor is not in it."

Sable's face did its nothing-thing. But Ilsa had spent a week learning the seams of that face, and she saw the flicker — quick, involuntary — of a man who knew exactly what was being carried toward Halven Bridge, sealed and diplomatic and none of her business.

"You think paper stops soldiers," Sable said. "It doesn't. I've carried orders that moved whole battalions. Nobody reads them twice."

"No," Pell agreed pleasantly. "But someday somebody reads them once, at the right moment, and then everything the paper describes becomes real — borders, pardons, graves, monuments. Paper is the only thing in this country with actual power anymore. The guns just enforce whatever the last signature said." He pulled out the chair opposite Sable and lowered himself into it with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions. "So. Courier. While your codes sleep in their little wax coffin — who do you imagine is going to read them?"

Sable didn't answer. He picked up the pencil instead.

Ilsa watched him write. He had a clerk's hand, neat and fast, and he wrote the way he traveled — economically, nothing wasted, but everything precise. *Ditch at the third culvert past the mill. Column passed 41 hours, continuous. Papers burned separately. Arrangement of kneeling suggests organization.* Then, at the bottom, after a pause long enough that she thought he'd refuse: his name. Sable Eriksen. Signed.

Anonymity surrendered on paper. She understood what it cost him. Everything about the man was built to be unrecorded.

"There," Pell said gently, taking the page, drying the ink with sand, entering it into the master ledger with a cross-reference number. "Now you exist somewhere besides your own memory. It's the most dangerous thing that will ever happen to you, and the only thing that matters."

* * *

They fed him stew at a folding table near the lamps, and Ilsa noticed he didn't leave afterward.

Nobody asked him to stay. That was the method, she realized, watching it work on him. The clerks simply made room. A stool appeared beside the girl who alphabetized. Someone handed him a stack of pages needing dates verified against railway timetables. Within an hour he was checking entries with the focused intensity of a man defusing ordnance, and the girl was showing him their shorthand system, and somebody's spare blanket had migrated onto the back of his chair without comment.

Found family, Ilsa thought. Built out of paper instead of blood. Half these people had lost everyone — she'd entered the dead of their families herself, in this very ledger. What held them together wasn't grief. It was the work. Grief got you through the night; the ledger got you through the war.

Sable worked until nearly midnight. Then he stood, stretched, and said, in his flattest courier voice, "I have a schedule. Halven Bridge won't wait."

"Nothing waits," Pell said. "Go safely. Come back anyway."

"I won't come back."

"Most of us said that," Pell said, and returned to his writing.

At the door, Ilsa caught up with him. The night outside was black and smelled of rain coming. He had his satchel strapped tight across his chest, and he stood holding the strap with both hands like a man holding a wound closed.

"Your seals," she said. Low, so the doorway couldn't hear. "Whatever's in them — you signed your name tonight. You know what that makes you, if those codes bury what you counted."

"It makes me a courier," he said. "I deliver. I don't decide."

"That's what the men at the culvert told themselves too. Somebody decides. Somebody always decides. They just prefer it be someone carrying instead of someone signing."

He looked at her then, and for a moment the nothing-face failed completely, and what was underneath was so young and so angry and so lonely that she almost stepped back.

"You have all this," he said, gesturing back at the lamplight, the pencils, the terrible gentle order of it. "You get to believe paper wins. I have a delivery window and handlers who check my route." He adjusted the strap. "Don't follow me again, Brandt."

"I'm a census taker," Ilsa said. "Following people is the entire job."

He walked down the ramp into the dark, and she stood watching until the rain arrived and erased him, and then she went back inside, because there were names waiting, and names did not wait.

Behind her, at the head table, Archivist Pell dipped his pen and began a new entry — the armistice at Halven Bridge, tentative date, parties unknown — underlining it once, the line they drew beneath things that were coming.
