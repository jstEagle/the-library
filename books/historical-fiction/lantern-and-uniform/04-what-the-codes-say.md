## 4. What the Codes Say

The shells came at dusk, the way they always did in Vessany that autumn — not because the gunners preferred evening, but because dusk was when the roads filled with people trying to get somewhere before dark, and a road full of people is a target you don't have to think about.

Sable heard them first as a sound like cloth tearing, far off, and then the tearing came closer, and then the world on either side of the Kesselmoor road began to come apart in orange pieces.

He didn't run. Running made you memorable. He walked — briskly, unhurried, a man with an appointment — until the third shell landed close enough to throw grit against his back, and then he ran like everyone else, because being dead was also a way of standing out.

There was a culvert where the road dipped through a drainage cut, half-collapsed, smelling of rust and stagnant water. Two other fugitives had already wedged themselves into it: a woman clutching a birdcage with no bird in it, and an old man who had been crying for so long it seemed less like grief than breathing. Sable folded himself into the corner farthest from both of them and put his satchel between his knees and his arms around it.

The bombardment walked up the road and back down again. Between salvos there was a silence with texture to it, a ringing quiet, and in that quiet the old man said, to no one, "They're shelling their own province," and the woman said, "They're shelling everything," and nobody argued.

Sable counted shells. It was something he did — counting gave the hands a job while the mind went elsewhere. He got to forty-one before he realized his mind hadn't gone elsewhere at all. It had gone inward, down into the satchel, to the thing wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with the grey wax of the Foreign Ministry, the thing he had carried for six days without once asking what it was.

*I just carry things.* That was the sentence he kept in his chest like a coin. He'd said it to Ilsa Brandt on the road, and she had looked at him with her census-taker's flat gray patience and asked, *And do you ever wonder what the things weigh?*

Forty-two. Forty-three.

Here was the truth about Sable Eriksen, the truth he arranged his whole life around: he was a forgettable man, and he had worked at it. Average height, average face, a voice trained to be pleasant and immediately set aside. He wore clothes that suggested a clerk, or a factor's assistant, or nothing much. At checkpoints the guards' eyes slid over him like water over glass. He had crossed eleven borders in nine years and been remembered by exactly one sentry, and that one had only remembered him because Sable had given the man his scarf during a frozen week in Dornholt, which had been a mistake, sentiment always was.

Forgetting was a craft. You built yourself out of unremarkable parts and you never gave anyone a handle to grip you by. And the chief rule of the craft, older than all the others, was this: **never ask what you carry.** Asking meant knowing. Knowing meant owning. A courier who knew what was in his case could be tortured for it, blackmailed with it, shot for it. Ignorance wasn't innocence; ignorance was armor.

Forty-seven. The shelling was moving east, away from them, toward whatever was east.

Except that armor had been developing cracks since the granary. Since Pell's lamplit table and the ledger with its careful columns of the dead. Since Ilsa saying, *Records outlive regimes,* and Pell nodding like a priest confirming doctrine. Since the clerks writing names in a bombed-out building as if writing were a form of rescue.

Because here was the crack, and Sable could feel it widening under his ribs: the clerks *asked*. They asked every day, all day. They asked what happened at Kesselmoor and wrote down the answer. Their whole republic of paper was built on the principle that ignorance was not armor but surrender.

The old man in the culvert had stopped crying. The woman rocked her empty cage.

Sable looked down at the satchel.

It weighed, he thought, about four pounds. He had lifted heavier parcels a hundred times without a thought. But tonight the weight had changed its nature. Tonight it sat against his legs like something with a pulse.

*Do you ever wonder what the things weigh?*

"Yes," he said aloud, before he could stop himself, and the woman looked at him, and he shook his head, and she looked away.

* * *

Protocol, if he followed it, was simple. He sheltered. He waited out the barrage. At first light he continued south toward Halven Bridge, delivered the seals intact to the armistice delegation, collected his chit, and dissolved back into the population of forgettable men. Whatever the codes bought — a border, a ceasefire, recognition, ransom — it would happen above his head, in rooms he'd never enter, and he'd walk away clean. Clean was the whole point. Clean was the salary.

But the shelling had knocked something loose in him, some bolt or rivet, and he found his hands moving before his judgment caught up: oilcloth unwrapped, the leather folio open, and there they were — three pages covered in cipher, and beneath the cipher, folded small, a plain-language memorandum. Because the men who wrote such things never trusted ciphers alone. Ciphers could die with the codebook. Paper survived.

He read it twice, because the first time his eyes refused to believe his own fluency.

**Memorandum annexed to protocol. In consideration of full recognition of the Provisional Government of General Marovay by the Allied Powers, the signatories agree that the incident at Kesselmoor, 14–16 March last, shall be designated closed, non-investigable, and excluded from all records, reparations schedules, and historical commissions arising from the present conflict. No party shall reference the incident in public proceedings. This annex to remain unpublished.**

Fourteen to sixteen March. Three days. An *incident*, in ministry language, which meant it had a number attached, and the number was large enough to need burying rather than explaining. He remembered the road now — Ilsa's voice on the second night, casual as weather: *You came up from the north? Through Kesselmoor? What did you see?* And how he had answered, honestly: *Nothing. Smoke. Empty houses.* And how she had looked at him then, not accusing, worse than accusing — *looking through* him, the way you look through glass at something behind it.

Empty houses. He had seen empty houses and thought *refugees fled, war is terrible, not my department.* He had seen a burned chapel with its door nailed shut from the outside and thought *looters, probably.* He had stepped over things in the ditch grass that he had told himself were sacks.

Three days. The village hadn't emptied itself in three days.

His stomach turned over, slow and enormous, like something rolling in deep water. He pressed the back of his hand to his mouth.

The vertigo came next. Not dizziness exactly — displacement. The sensation of having lived inside a story and suddenly seeing the room the story was written in. Nine years of clean deliveries. Chits signed, seals broken by other men, consequences filed in cabinets he never saw. He had imagined himself a neutral particle drifting through history's machinery. But particles aren't neutral. Every parcel goes somewhere. Every seal buys something. He had spent nine years being the hands of men who never dirtied their own gloves, and now, in a drainage culvert that smelled of rust and piss, with the sky still grumbling eastward, he understood that the thing he'd been carrying all along — every mission, all nine years — had weight after all.

Bodies. He'd been carrying bodies. Just never his own.

The woman with the cage was watching him. "Bad news?" she asked.

"It's work," he said. His voice came out steady, which amazed him.

"Work," she repeated, with the flat bitterness of someone whose work had burned with her house, and she turned back to her empty cage.

* * *

He should rewrap it. That was the discipline, that was the craft: rewrap, reseal impression in memory only, be the forgettable man at dawn. The seals were already broken — that couldn't be undone, and a courier caught with opened seals was a courier hanged, or worse, *questioned* — so the smart move, the only move, was to fold the memorandum back along its creases, restore the oilcloth, and carry the knowledge the way he carried everything: silently, invisibly, at arm's length.

He held the page over the folio and did not move.

Because there was a second thought underneath the first, and it had Pell's voice, and it would not go away: *A record that isn't kept is a record that doesn't exist. Regimes know this. Why do you think they write the histories first and fight the wars second?*

If he delivered the codes, the annex would be signed. Kesselmoor would become a word that had never been spoken. The ditch-grass shapes he had stepped over would be officially nobody, killed by officially no one, mourned by officially no record anywhere on earth except a secret ledger in a bombed granary guarded by seven exhausted clerks and a woman with contraband notebooks.

And he would walk away clean. Clean, with four pounds lighter in his arms and something else strapped to his back forever.

Far off, the guns stopped. The ringing quiet came down like snowfall. Somewhere east, smoke stood up straight in the windless evening, three columns of it, patient as chimneys.

Sable folded the memorandum. He wrapped the folio. He buckled the satchel.

Then he took out his knife, and from the seam of his coat lining he cut loose the spare thread, needle, and scrap of plain paper that every good courier carried for mending and maps, and he copied out the annex in his smallest hand, the hand he used for forged transit passes, pressing the letters flat and fast. When he finished he read his copy over once, folded it small, and sewed it into the lining beside the place the thread had been.

He was not sure yet what he would do with it. He only knew that for the first time in nine years he was carrying two things instead of one, and one of them was his own.

He settled back against the culvert wall to wait for light, and it was then he saw, across the drainage cut, half-sheltered under the far lip of the concrete, a figure he knew: gray coat, census taker's satchel, notebook already open on her knee, writing by the last of the light.

Ilsa Brandt had been following him for two days. And by the tilt of her head, the stillness of her pen — she had been watching him read.
