## 5. Road of Ash

I had buried three republics before this one, and I had learned the arithmetic of flight: whatever you cannot carry in one arm becomes someone else's burden or no one's memory at all.

The granary burned behind us at dusk. Not bombed — burned. Pell had ordered it himself, a bucket of lamp oil and a struck match, because the ledgers we could not take must not be taken by the men coming up the Kesselmoor road. He watched the flames take the rafters with the same expression he wore reading a well-made index: satisfaction, faintly sad.

"Paper wants to burn," Ilsa said beside him. "It's what paper does."

"Then we will be very busy people," Pell said, "keeping it from doing what it does."

He was seventy-one years old and walked with a stick he refused to call a cane — *a pointer*, he said, as if he might at any moment turn to a blackboard. The clerks moved around him the way water moves around a stone that has been in the river so long the river has forgotten any other shape. There were eleven of us when we left the granary: Pell, Ilsa, Sable Eriksen the courier who pretended not to be listening and listened to everything, and eight others whose names I will set down here because setting things down is the whole of my religion.

Marta Voss, who could copy a page faster than most men could read one.
The brothers Anselm and Rudi Kett, carters.
Old Fenya, who had been a registrar in Vessany City for forty years and still wore her ink-stained cuffs like medals.
Tomas Brandt, no relation to Ilsa, seventeen, who carried the second ledger against his chest like an infant.
And the rest — Dora, Havel, little Wren, and quiet Oskar who spoke perhaps ten words a day and copied in a hand so fine it looked printed.

Eleven. Remember the number. Numbers are how I pray.

* * *

We went east by night, along the drainage canal, because the road was for soldiers and the dead. The carts rolled soft on wet clay. Above us the sky did its work — red to the west where Halven Bridge would be deciding everything without us, black overhead, stars indifferent as clerks' initials.

Pell rode the first cart with the master ledger wrapped in oilcloth inside a grain sack inside a coffin lid we had sawn hollow. He had insisted on the coffin. "Let them dig for it," he said. "Let them be ashamed of what they find."

"You think they can be ashamed?" Sable asked. He was walking, hands in pockets, the courier's posture of a man belonging nowhere.

"I think shame is a seed," Pell said. "You plant it whether it sprouts or not. That is not our department."

Sable looked at him a long time in the dark. I have watched many people look at Pell that way — hungry and suspicious at once, the way you look at a door marked with light when you have been walking in fields all night. The courier had been with us six days and still said he was only traveling the same direction. Still checked the seals on his dispatch case every morning like a man touching a wound to see if it hurt yet.

"Pell," Ilsa said, "tell him about the census."

"There is nothing to tell," Sable said quickly.

"Everything is something," Pell said mildly, "or the archive would be very thin." But he let it go. He had a talent for letting things go at exactly the moment holding them would break them.

Behind us, artillery spoke in short syllables. Closer than last night. The new regime's columns were moving toward Halven Bridge to collect their surrender, and anything loose on the roads — refugees, records, witnesses — was being gathered up or ground down.

"Faster," Pell said, and the carts went faster.

* * *

At midnight we halted in the lee of a collapsed aqueduct, and Pell held what he called school. It was his habit. A candle stub, a knee for a desk, and whoever was awake got taught.

"Question," he said to Tomas. "A regime falls. Its archives are seized. What survives?"

"The copies," Tomas said.

"Where are the copies?"

"In strangers' houses. In other countries. In—" he patted his coat, embarrassed.

"In bread loaves," Fenya said, working a needle through a crust even now, sliding folded pages in where the dough had been scooped. "In hems. In the lining of a hat nobody would steal."

"Say it properly," Pell said.

Fenya smiled with her few teeth. "In people. The copies live in people."

"And when the state comes for the people?"

"Then there are more people," Ilsa said. "There are always more people."

Pell nodded, satisfied, and I saw Sable watching from the edge of the candlelight with an expression I recognized because I had worn it once, forty years ago, when old Archivist Lem had caught me stealing a file and instead of turning me in made me copy it out three times. The expression of a man being recruited by an argument he has not agreed to lose.

"You," Pell said suddenly, to Sable. "Come here. You carry sealed things for a living. Tell me — what is a seal?"

"A wax stamp," Sable said. "An official mark. It means don't open this."

"Wrong," Pell said, gently, the way he was gentle with wrong answers, which is to say completely without mercy. "A seal is a promise that the contents matter more than your curiosity. It is a machine for making obedience feel like virtue. Now — who benefits when a courier never asks what he carries?"

Sable's jaw worked. "The sender."

"And who suffers?"

Silence. The candle guttered. Somewhere east, a flare went up, white and obscene, and sank.

"That's the whole curriculum," Pell said, closing the lesson. "Everything else is penmanship."

* * *

Dawn came up gray and smelling of smoke that wasn't ours, and the shells found the canal road at first light.

Not aimed at us — aimed at the bridge head, at the rail junction, at the war's ordinary appetite — but a barrage is weather; it falls on everyone. The first round took the far bank. The second hit close enough to lift Marta off the cart seat and put her down bleeding from the ear, deaf on one side, shouting that she was fine, too loud, unable to hear herself. The horses screamed and Rudi held their heads and swore at them with a tenderness I have never heard him use with people.

"Off the road! Into the culvert!" Pell's voice cracked over the noise like a schoolmaster's over a playground fight, and absurdly, wonderfully, they obeyed — eight clerks and two carters and a courier scrambling into a stone drain built for rainwater, hauling the ledger chest between them.

That was when we saw the infantry.

A skirmish line, maybe thirty men, sweeping the canal bank ahead of the advance. Not an army. A net, dragging for whatever the barrage shook loose. They would reach the carts in minutes. And in the carts, under the false bottom of a grain sack, under oilcloth, under the lid of a coffin that had never been needed until now, lay four hundred pages bearing nine hundred names from Kesselmoor.

Pell stood up in the open.

Seventy-one years old, stick in hand, standing in shellfire like a man waiting for a late omnibus. He waved his arms. He shouted — I heard him distinctly, though the world was mostly thunder — *"Over here! Records office! We surrender! Take me!"*

They came for him. Of course they came for him. A lone old man waving is either a target or a prize, and soldiers do not stop to determine which.

What happened next happened fast, the way endings do. Pell walked toward them, away from the culvert, drawing them after him, and I understood — we all understood, too late — that he had arranged his own geography. He led them forty meters east, out of sightline of the carts, and when the lieutenant demanded papers Pell opened his coat with theatrical slowness and produced, instead, a single sheet, and began to read aloud.

I could not hear what he read. I know what it was. Page one. The first name. *Aldric Venn, farmer, aged 44, killed 3rd of March with his sons.* Pell had made us memorize page one months ago. "If everything burns," he said then, "the last copy is in your mouths. Recite."

They shot him at the fourth name.

I will write plainly what I saw, because plainness is the covenant: he fell forward onto his own sheet of paper, and the wind took the corner of it and fluttered it, and one of the soldiers stepped on it going past, and none of them knew they had just executed the entire memory of Kesselmoor and failed.

Then the skirmish line moved on toward the bridge, because the bridge mattered and an old dead man did not, and the war continued its great work of deciding what would count as having happened.

* * *

We dug no grave. There was no time and no depth of earth left unshelled. Fenya said words — not scripture, she'd lost her god somewhere around the second republic, but the registrar's liturgy: *name, date, place, witnessed.* She entered him in the margin of the ledger he died for, in his honor, in his own style of ink.

*Archivist Pell. Killed 11th October, canal road near Halven Bridge. Witnessed.*

Then Ilsa closed the book and looked up, and her face had changed. I had seen her angry and afraid and stubbornly cheerful. This was none of those. This was a woman accepting an office.

"He gave it to me," she said. "Before the shelling started. He said — if it's ever only down to one pair of hands, they should be hands that ask questions."

"Then it's yours," Marta said. "We're all yours. That's how it works now."

"It doesn't work like that anymore," Ilsa said. "One keeper dies, the record scatters — that's what he feared. It has to be more than one. It has to be everywhere, in everyone, so killing it means killing everyone who's heard a name." Her eyes went around the culvert, the mud-streaked faces, and stopped on Sable.

"No," Sable said.

"You haven't heard the request yet."

"I know the request. Everyone in this ditch is about to become a courier, and I'm telling you now, I deliver things. I don't *be* things. There's a difference."

"What's in the case?" Ilsa asked quietly.

His hand went to the dispatch case, reflexive. "That's mine. Sealed."

"A seal is a machine for making obedience feel like virtue," Fenya recited, mild as morning.

Sable looked at her, then at Pell's blood dried brown on the canal stones, then at the ledger under Ilsa's arm — four hundred pages that were, as of an hour ago, orphaned. Something moved in his face that I have seen only twice in a long life: the exact moment a man stops deceiving himself and finds, underneath, that he is not sorry.

"I have codes to deliver," he said at last. "To Halven Bridge. Tomorrow." A pause, and then, low, like a confession: "And I think I finally need to know what they say."

Ilsa handed him the ledger.

He took it the way you take a sleeping child from someone's arms — carefully, wrongly certain you'll drop it, changed forever by the weight.

East, the guns had stopped falling on the road and started falling on the city, and between the detonations, faintly, we could hear bells — Halven Bridge ringing itself hoarse, summoning delegates, summoning signatures, summoning the future to come and be decided.

"We have one day," Ilsa said.
