## 6. The Victor's Historian

Halven Bridge smelled of paint.

That was the first thing Ilsa noticed, stepping down from the grain cart with her legs cramped and the ledger's copies strapped flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat. The city had been shelled for three years — she'd seen it from the ridge at dusk, the broken teeth of its waterfront towers against a burning sky — and now here it was, scrubbed and whitewashed, scaffolding on every corner, men in the victors' gray-green already repainting the customs house in official colors. Three days until the armistice. They were painting over the war before it was even finished.

"Documents," said the soldier at the bridge gate.

She had documents. That had been Pell's genius: everyone in the clerks' republic carried papers, real ones, honest ones, because a lie you couldn't sustain under questioning was worse than no lie at all. Ilsa Brandt, census taker of the Interior Ministry, reassigned to administrative duties in the occupied zone, travel authorization countersigned by a dead man whose signature Pell had taught her to forge so well that even she sometimes believed he was still alive.

The soldier barely looked. He was watching a column of supply lorries cross the bridge behind her, their engines growling up from the south where Vessany still technically existed as anything more than a set of coordinates.

Inside the old quarter, the city performed normalcy with desperate energy. Market stalls. A woman selling apples that had traveled farther than most soldiers. Children chasing each other between sandbag emplacements as if the sandbags were playground equipment, which, Ilsa supposed, they now were. She bought two apples because buying things made you invisible, and paid with coins she counted out slowly, listening all the while.

Sable had gone ahead the night before, alone, toward his handlers and his sealed codes and whatever bargain he thought he could strike with men who owned him. She hadn't tried to stop him. You couldn't stop a courier from delivering; you could only make sure that what arrived wasn't the whole story. That part was hers.

* * *

She found the historian on the third day, and she found him because he wanted to be found.

Not literally — Colonel Edvard Vasch did not post his location in the windows of cafés — but the victors' historiography section had taken over the old tax bureau on Kesslerplatz, and a building full of clerks required supplies, and supplies required requisitions, and requisitions were posted publicly, which was how Ilsa learned that the man drafting the official narrative of the war took his coffee at seven-forty each morning at the Café Aurora on the square, alone, at a corner table with his back to the wall.

She watched him twice before she approached him.

He was not what she expected. She had imagined the victor's historian as something armored — a thick-necked staff officer, or a cold ideologue with a voice like a lecture. Instead Vasch was slight, gray-templed, fiftyish, with the stooped shoulders of a lifelong desk man and reading glasses pushed up on his forehead like a shopkeeper's. He wrote in longhand in a green notebook, unhurried, occasionally consulting a leather folder stamped with an eagle she did not recognize. He ate his pastry in small precise bites. He thanked the waiter by name.

*He looks like Pell,* she thought, and hated herself for it.

On the second morning she got close enough, drifting past his table with a market basket, to see the notebook upside down. Columned pages. Dates. Headings in a careful bureaucratic hand. And one line, halfway down, that stopped her breath even read backward:

*Kesselmoor — see annex, disposition pending.*

Disposition pending. Four hundred and eleven names in Pell's ledger, and the man writing the war's official memory had reduced them to four words and a filing decision.

* * *

She spent the evening in the cellar room the surviving clerks had rented above a cooperage — six of them now, out of the thirty who had walked out of the granary — copying pages by lamplight. Dov Hessel, youngest of them, hands still ink-stained from a print shop that no longer existed, argued while he worked.

"You can't blackmail an institution," he said. "You can blackmail a man."

"I'm not blackmailing an institution." Ilsa blotted a page of names — Aldric Fen, ferryman; Marta Osten, aged nine — and set it in the drying stack. "I'm correcting one."

"With what leverage? He's a colonel. You're a woman with a basket."

"With his own draft." She tapped the copy she'd made from memory that afternoon, sitting in the café's washroom with her heart slamming: the column headings, the dates, the disposition-pending line. "His narrative isn't finished. Which means it can still be finished wrong. He knows what happens to historians whose drafts embarrass the delegation — he's seen it happen to other men, in other wars, or he wouldn't be so careful. Careful men are reachable men."

"And if he just calls the guards?"

"Then we learn that too." She heard how it sounded and didn't soften it. "Pell used to say a record is a wager. You write it down believing someone will read it. This is the same bet, moved closer."

Dov looked at her for a long moment. Then he went back to copying, and after a page or two he said, quietly, "Aldric Fen, ferryman. Good handwriting today, Brandt."

It was as close to a blessing as the clerks' republic issued.

* * *

The third morning, she sat down at his table.

Not dramatically. She simply appeared across from him as he opened his notebook, the way a person sits when a café is crowded, and set her market basket on the floor, and folded her hands, and waited for him to look up over his glasses.

"Madam, this table—"

"Colonel Vasch." She kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice of a functionary discussing paperwork. "Kesselmoor. Disposition pending. I've come about the disposition."

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, controlled, but not nothing. He closed the notebook without hurry. "You have me confused with someone else."

"I have you confused with no one. Annex fourteen of your draft history, currently in your folder, currently unfinished. The village of Kesselmoor, the twelfth of Harvestmonth. Four hundred eleven civilians, though your own field records say four hundred twenty-seven, because eleven died of wounds afterward and five were never recovered from the millrace." She recited it the way Pell had taught them all to recite: not with heat, with precision. Heat was a luxury. Precision was armor. "Your government has agreed to recognize certain officials of the new Vessany regime in exchange for certain silences. One of those silences is spelled K-e-s-s-e-l-m-o-o-r, and you are holding the pen."

The café went on around them — cups, chatter, a spoon against porcelain — and Vasch let it go on for a moment, looking at her with the frank assessing gaze of a man reclassifying a document. When he spoke, his voice was quiet and entirely without fear, which frightened her more than shouting would have.

"Who are you?"

"A clerk."

"There are no clerks left in Vessany. There are ours, and theirs." He turned his coffee cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, aligning the handle with the table edge, and she understood suddenly that this was a man who aligned handles, who believed in order, who had perhaps once been the kind of archivist who loved paper for its own sake before the eagles got hold of him. "Whatever you think you have, madam, understand the arithmetic. If you expose this, the armistice fails, the war continues, thousands die, and Kesselmoor enters the record anyway — as an atrocity committed during hostilities, one among hundreds, forgotten within a decade. If you don't, there is peace, and the village is... a grief, privately held. I take no pleasure in it. But I have read enough archives to know which version of events survives longer."

"The false one," Ilsa said. "You're right. The false one always survives longer. It's lighter. Easier to carry." She leaned forward. "But it has to be written by someone. And that someone has a name, a face, a table he sits at every morning at seven-forty. Armies are weather, Colonel. You're a man. Men can be held responsible. That's the entire difference, and it's the only one that matters."

For the first time, he looked genuinely at her — past the basket and the forged papers and the census-taker's plain coat, at the thing underneath. "What do you want?"

"I want Kesselmoor in your annex. Names, dates, responsibility, attribution to the units involved. In the official record. Before signatures."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow morning, at this table, you'll find copies of your own draft — annotated, corrected, dated — circulating among the foreign delegations' attachés, who are presently very hungry for exactly this kind of discrepancy. We have them. We've had them since before you crossed the river."

It was perhaps sixty percent true. They had copies — Dov's fair hand, forty pages, hidden in bread loaves and a coffin bound for the garrison cemetery. Whether any attaché would ever see one, whether any attaché would care, whether the whole apparatus would simply grind them flat — none of that was certain, and Ilsa felt the uncertainty like cold water in her shoes. But Vasch couldn't know the percentages either, and she watched him do the same arithmetic she was doing, and saw the moment the numbers came back uncomfortable.

He aligned the cup again. Quarter-turn. Handle to the edge.

"You'll hear from me," he said, "through channels. Not here."

"I look forward to it."

* * *

She was three streets away when the trap closed — not for her, as it happened, which she would understand later, and which would frighten her more than being caught ever could.

Two military police stepped out ahead of her at the corner of the cooperage lane, and a third fell in behind, unhurried, closing the triangle the way they'd done it ten thousand times in ten thousand cities. Ilsa's hand went to the strap under her coat, to the forty pages riding warm against her ribs, and her mind began the cold inventory: the coffin at the freight yard, the loaves with Dov, the copies sewn into Sable's coat lining that he probably didn't know were there—

"Brandt?" said the sergeant in front. "Ilsa Brandt, census ministry?"

"Yes."

"Not you we want. Man answering your description was seen going into the tax bureau last night after hours. Dark coat. Courier's satchel." The sergeant consulted a slip of paper. "They pulled him out of the historiography section an hour ago. Papers on him. Sealed diplomatic codes."

Her blood went very still and then very fast. *Sable.* Sable, who had gone ahead alone, who had promised nothing except that he would deliver, who had apparently delivered himself straight into the building where the war's memory was being rewritten — carrying, of all things, the codes, the actual codes, into the hands of men who had already planned to kill him after delivery.

"Is he—" She made herself sound bored, bureaucratic, a colleague annoyed at a delay. "Eriksen? He was meant to meet me at the customs house. Is he detained?"

"He's detained," the sergeant said, in the tone of a man stating the weather, and jerked his head toward the bridge. "And you're coming too, madam. The colonel requests all persons connected with the courier be present for verification."

*The colonel,* she thought. Not a general. Not a tribunal.

Vasch.

Who had smiled, perhaps, as he gave the order — or hadn't, perhaps, which was worse — and sent his policemen through the painted streets of a city pretending it had never burned, to collect the one witness who had dared sit down at his table and tell him that men could be held responsible.

The ledger pressed against her ribs like a fist. Ilsa lifted her chin, adjusted her basket, and walked between the soldiers toward the bridge, doing the only arithmetic left to her:

How many hours until the signing — and whether a man with a pen could be turned into a man with a conscience before the ink dried.
