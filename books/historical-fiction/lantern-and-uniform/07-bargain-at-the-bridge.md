## 7. Bargain at the Bridge

They were waiting for him in the customs house.

Sable knew it before he reached the door — knew it by the way the lamplight sat wrong in the windows, too many shadows for one clerk on night duty, and by the smell of pipe smoke drifting through a building where no one smoked but men who had already decided something. He stood in the cold for a long moment, the courier's satchel heavy against his ribs, and let himself feel the old comfort of being nobody. Nobody got waited for. Nobody got cornered in customs houses at midnight.

But that man was three days dead. The Kesselmoor pages had killed him quietly somewhere on the road from the granary, and what walked up the steps now was someone else entirely.

He went in anyway.

The customs hall had been stripped to its bones — desks shoved against walls, a map of Vessany hanging crooked with pins marking nothing anyone believed anymore. Three men stood near the brazier. Two he recognized: Doss, his handler's handler, gray-faced and neat as an invoice, and beside him a younger man whose name Sable had never been given because names were currency and Sable had never been paid in them. The third wore the plain uniform of a line officer with the bearing of a much better one, and he was warming his hands as if this were a social call.

"Eriksen," Doss said. "You're late."

"Roads are bad." Sable set the satchel on the nearest desk. "Codes are intact."

"We know they're intact. We've known where you've been since Halven Ford." Doss said it without heat, which made it worse. "The census woman. The clerks. The granary." He paused, delicately. "The ledger."

The word dropped into the room like a coin down a well. Sable heard it hit bottom and kept falling after it.

"I don't know what you mean," he said, and hated how automatic it sounded — the courier's reflex, the forgettable man's armor. It had worked for eleven years. It sounded thin now even to him.

The line officer turned from the brazier. Up close he had a scholar's face gone soft around the jaw, spectacles fogged by the fire's warmth, and eyes that read people the way Pell had read documents — patiently, thoroughly, looking for the forgery.

"You do," the officer said mildly. "That's rather the point. If you didn't, we'd have shot you at the bridge checkpoint and been done with it." He took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve. "Colonel Vasch's office has a copy of your itinerary. Also a copy of pages four through nine of a certain document. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Someone copied that ledger before you ever reached this city. Someone is circulating it *tonight*."

*Ilsa.* The name rose in him like heat off stone. She'd done it while he slept, or during the copying marathon — she'd kept a second set back, or sent pages ahead with one of the clerks, the coffin-bearers, the bread-carriers. He felt a flash of something that wasn't quite anger. Pride, maybe. Or fear for her, arriving late.

"So you have it," Sable said. "Both things. The codes and the truth. What do you need me for?"

Doss laughed — short, humorless, the laugh of a man who had explained arithmetic to children all his life. "Because the codes are sealed to your hand. Because the delegation expects Eriksen, the forgettable man, the courier who has never once drawn attention in eleven years of service. Because if we deliver them ourselves and anything is wrong — anything — the armistice collapses and Vessany burns instead of surrendering." He stepped closer. "And because you're going to walk into that hall tomorrow and hand those codes over exactly as trained. And then—"

"And then I'm going to disappear," Sable said.

The silence confirmed it more cleanly than any confession. The young man shifted his weight. The colonel did not look away; he watched Sable with the detached interest of a man confirming a figure already entered in a column.

Sable looked at the satchel on the desk. Eleven years he'd carried things — codes, orders, deniable sums, other men's signatures on other men's futures — and told himself the carrying was neutral, that paper was just paper, that history happened to other people. He had learned better in a bombed granary full of clerks who wrote down names because no one else would. And now here was the last delivery of his career, the one that would buy the victors' silence about Kesselmoor, about four hundred people herded onto the frozen moor and shot until the ice ran red, about the thing the new regime would pay so handsomely to keep out of the record.

He could hand over the codes. Walk out the back. Maybe live a month, maybe a week, maybe — if he ran far enough — years, forgetting, the way he'd always been good at forgetting.

Or.

"Coffee," Sable said. "If we're negotiating, I'd rather not do it standing."

* * *

They gave him coffee because it amused them to. He drank it black from a chipped customs mug and laid it out plainly, the way Pell had taught him records should be kept: clean lines, no embellishment, every entry verifiable.

"The codes get delivered intact tomorrow. By me. Unopened, unexamined, the seals perfect. You get your armistice, your recognition, your tidy surrender." He set the mug down. "In exchange, the true record of Kesselmoor enters the armistice record. Not annexes. Not a private archive. Entered — read into the proceedings, minuted, signed alongside everything else."

Vasch's man — Sable had decided privately to think of him as the Historian, though he wasn't sure Vasch himself wasn't in the room under the plain uniform — actually smiled. "You're proposing blackmail. A courier. With what leverage?"

"With the fact that you can't be sure you have every copy." Sable held up a hand and counted. "One: copies exist outside this room — you said so yourself, pages four through nine are circulating tonight, and whoever holds them holds the rest. Two: the clerks who made them are scattered through this city, and they've spent a year learning how to hide paper from people who burn it. Three—" he met the Historian's fogged glasses "—if anything happens to me before delivery, my instructions go to the delegation directly. Sealed. In their hands within the hour. They'll open them wondering why a courier thought he needed insurance, and then they'll wonder what else needs insuring. Your armistice dies of curiosity."

"That's a bluff," the young man said.

"It's arithmetic," Sable said. "Check it."

He wasn't bluffing, exactly. There were instructions, written in the granary in Ilsa's careful clerk-hand, timed to reach two delegates if Sable failed to appear. But the real weight behind his voice came from somewhere else — the certainty, cold and total, that Ilsa Brandt had copies he would never find, hidden in ways he would never guess, carried by people he'd never meet. The network Pell had built was bigger than one granary. Bigger than one night.

The Historian studied him for a long time. Somewhere outside, a patrol went past, boots keeping time on wet cobbles.

"You understand," the Historian said at last, almost gently, "what happens to the record doesn't change what happened at Kesselmoor. Four hundred dead stay four hundred dead whether we minute it or not. You're trading your life for a footnote."

"No," Sable said. "I'm trading my life for the difference between a massacre and a secret. A massacre gets mourned. A secret gets repeated." He thought of Pell shielding the ledger with his body on the ash road, of the old man's last lesson delivered in blood: *records outlive regimes*. "You're buying silence with these codes. Silence expires. Records don't."

Another silence. Then Doss, quietly: "It's cheaper than the alternatives. If the pages surface mid-ceremony unsanctioned, the whole signing becomes theater. Sanctioned, minuted, buried inside forty pages of boundary language—" He shrugged. "It's manageable."

"You're agreeing?" the young man said.

"I'm agreeing that the trade costs us less than the leak," Doss corrected. "There's a difference."

There was, and Sable heard it perfectly. The trade costs less than the leak — and afterward, the courier becomes the cost. He had counted on this. Had built the whole bargain knowing that men like Doss did not forgive leverage; they absorbed it, filed it, and scheduled its disposal. He watched the calculation move behind the Historian's eyes and felt no triumph at all. Just clarity, cold and level as ice on the moor. He had stopped being cargo somewhere on the road. This — sitting in a stolen customs house, bargaining with his own death over a mug of bad coffee — was what it meant to be a player.

"Then we're agreed," Sable said. "Codes delivered. Ledger entered. And I walk out of the hall alive."

"Of course," the Historian said, and shook his hand. His grip was dry and scholarly and completely sincere, which was how Sable knew.

* * *

They put him in a guarded room above the customs house — "for your protection," Doss said, straight-faced — and Sable lay on a cot listening to the rain start against the roof tiles, doing the accounting he'd been avoiding.

Tomorrow he would deliver the codes. Tomorrow the ledger would enter the record, read into the minutes between clauses on river tolls and prisoner exchange, and some tired delegate would initial the page without reading it and thereby make four hundred deaths officially real forever. That part might work. Might.

Afterward, they would kill him. Not dramatically — he knew these men. An escort to the border, a quiet road, a ditch. Perhaps they'd blame partisans. The report would say Eriksen, regrettable loss, the courier who cracked under strain. Even his disappearance would be archived, tidied, made harmless.

He found, examining himself in the dark, that he wasn't afraid in the way he'd expected to be afraid. He was sad — for the reading room he'd never sit in, for the lamplit table and the clerks passing bread and ink down its length, for the family he'd only just admitted wanting. But underneath the sadness was something steadier, something that felt almost like rest: he knew exactly what he was worth now, and to whom, and why. For eleven years he'd been worth nothing to anyone except as a pair of forgettable hands. Tomorrow he'd be worth a massacre's silence, and the day after, nothing again — but in between, the record.

A floorboard creaked in the corridor. Boots, unhurried, passing his door. Pausing. Moving on.

Sable closed his eyes and began, silently, to rehearse tomorrow — the hall, the table, the seals breaking under his thumb, and then the moment when the delegates opened their briefing folders and found the pages Ilsa had seeded there, and his own voice, steady, beginning the names.

*Kesselmoor. The fourth of Frostmonth. Aldric Venn, ferryman. Marta Venn, his wife—*

Outside, the rain thickened toward snow. Somewhere across the city, in a cellar or a garret or a coffin shop, Ilsa was counting copies by candlelight, and neither of them would sleep.

By morning, one of them might be dead. Both of them had planned for that.

Only the record was supposed to live forever — and tomorrow, in front of the victors, Sable intended to make it so.
