## 8. Night of Copies

Sable counted the lamps first. Seven. In a city where the victors had cut the power grid like a throat, seven burning wicks was a confession, and every one of them sat on a table in the cellar of the Halven Bridge chandler's shop, ringed by clerks with ink-stained fingers and faces too tired to be afraid.

"Copy fast, copy clean," Ilsa said. "A smudged name is a dead man twice."

They worked in pairs. One read aloud, one wrote. The ledger — Pell's ledger, Kesselmoor and forty-one other entries besides — lay open under the brightest lamp, its pages soft as cloth from handling. Sable took the reading shift because his voice was the steadiest in the room and everyone knew it, though nobody said why. A courier learns to recite under interrogation. He'd never thought of that skill as a gift before tonight.

"Entry thirty," he read. "Kesselmoor, third week of the retreat. Four hundred and six names. Begin: Aldous Ferren, wheelwright—"

The pens moved.

He watched them while he read. Not the words — he'd stopped feeling the words somewhere around entry twenty — but the hands. A woman named Doret who had been a tax clerk in Vessany Proper for thirty years, writing each letter as if it were being graded. A boy of maybe sixteen, Tam, whose script shook until Ilsa stood behind him and put one hand on his shoulder, and then it didn't shake anymore. An old customs officer who copied numbers the way other men prayed.

This was what Pell had built, Sable thought. Not an archive. A congregation.

* * *

Ilsa measured the bread by lamplight in the shop above the cellar, where the chandler's widow — a broad, silent woman named Marta who asked no questions and was paid in nothing but the truth — kept her ovens banked for the night.

"A loaf holds four pages if you bake it around them, waxed first," Marta said. "Six if you don't mind the corners curling."

"I mind," Ilsa said. "Curling pages look like hidden pages."

Marta grunted, which Ilsa had learned was agreement.

They worked the dough together in the dark, flour to the elbows, and Ilsa slid the waxed sheets into the center of each loaf the way you'd slide a letter under a door — gently, so the paper wouldn't know it was crossing a border. Six loaves for six directions out of the city. Market carts, milk wagons, a funeral baker who owed Marta a debt from before the war. Each loaf would arrive at a different table in a different town, and someone at that table would cut the bread open and find four hundred names where the crumb should be, and they would have to decide what kind of person they wanted to be by breakfast.

That was the whole design, Ilsa thought. Not armies. Breakfasts.

She thought about Pell. He would have hated the risk and done it anyway, and then he would have written down the fact of the loaves themselves, because even the smuggling deserved a record. *Entry forty-two,* she imagined him dictating. *The night we baked the truth.* She smiled into the flour dust, and it wasn't entirely grief.

* * *

At two in the morning the knocking came — three slow, two fast — and a coffin came through the cellar door on the shoulders of two men from the undertakers' guild.

"It's real," one of them said. "Old fellow from the parish, died honest, God rest him. But there's room at his feet."

They lined the coffin's false bottom with the second complete copy, wrapped in oilcloth, and laid the old man's body above it with proper ceremony, because the guild did not do things halfway and neither did their conscience. Sable held the lamp while they worked and found his own hands steady in a way that frightened him more than shaking would have.

"You'll walk behind it tomorrow?" the younger undertaker asked him.

"I'll walk behind it."

"To the gate? Past the checkpoint?"

"Past the checkpoint." He looked at the coffin lid, plain pine, no ornament. "Nobody searches grief. That's the one thing this war hasn't figured out how to do."

The older undertaker spat thoughtfully into the straw. "Give it time."

* * *

By four the coats were going out. Ilsa stitched pages into linings with a curved needle and a surgeon's patience, sewing along the old seam lines so the bulge read as padding, as mending, as the ordinary poverty of people who patched their clothes instead of replacing them. Tam sewed beside her, slower, tongue between his teeth.

"My mother did this," he said suddenly. "During the famine. Coins. She sewed coins into my coat and told me not to run, because running makes lining tear."

"And did you run?"

"Once." He didn't look up. "She caught me at the bridge."

Ilsa set a stitch. "Then she knows how to wait at bridges," she said. "Go home when it's done, Tam. Sleep. Come back tomorrow looking stupid and harmless."

"That's your whole strategy, isn't it? Everyone stupid and harmless."

"It toppled bigger regimes than this one."

But when he'd gone she sat with the needle idle for a long moment, listening to the pens scratch below, and understood that she had just given away the only order she actually believed in: *be forgettable.* It was Sable's doctrine, the courier's creed, and here it was turning into a battle plan. Somewhere between the granary and this cellar, invisibility had stopped being cowardice and become armor. She wasn't sure when that happened. She suspected Sable wasn't either.

* * *

Near dawn, Sable took his break in the yard behind the chandler's shop, where the sky over the river was going the color of gun metal and the armistice flags on the far tower were already hanging out their folds for tomorrow. His handler's message sat in his pocket, burned into memory rather than paper: *deliver intact, then report to the quay warehouse alone.*

Alone. After delivery. He could hear the arithmetic in it.

Ilsa came out with two cups of chicory and handed him one without asking. They stood shoulder to shoulder, watching the flags.

"They'll kill you after," she said. Not a question. She'd been a census taker; she knew how states counted, and what they subtracted.

"Probably."

"And you're still doing it."

"I'm delivering both," he said. "Codes and ledger. That was the trade. What they do afterward is the only part left that's mine."

She turned the cup in her hands. "Pell used to say a record is a bet you place on strangers. You write it down and send it out and trust people you'll never meet to keep it alive." She looked at him sideways. "We've got copies in bread now, Sable. In coffins. In the linings of a sixteen-year-old's coat. Every one of those is a stranger saying yes."

"The bread might go stale. The coffin might get buried deep."

"So might we. The record doesn't care." She drained her chicory. "Come inside. You're on names again at five, and entry thirty is four hundred and six reasons long."

He followed her down, and the lamps were still burning, seven small disobediences, and the pens were still moving, and somewhere above them the first loaves were already rising in the dark around their secret pages like ordinary mornings deciding to carry something extraordinary.

At half past five, the knock came again — three slow, two fast — and Marta opened the door onto a wet gray colonel's aide with a requisition slip in his glove and the victors' seal already stamped on it.

The chandler's shop, the slip said, was required by the Office of the Armistice Historian. Effective immediately.
