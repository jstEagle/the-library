## 9. Read Aloud Before Signatures

The armistice hall had been a customs house once, back when Vessany still had things worth taxing. Now its marble floor was cracked into a mosaic of gray islands, and the victors had laid a long green table across the center like a bandage over a wound. Delegates sat on either side: the Republic's men in their threadbare black, the Coalition's in pressed gray, and between them, at the head, Colonel Vasch with his historian's pen already uncapped.

Sable came through the doors at nine o'clock sharp carrying a lacquered case he had carried for eleven days. Eleven days of pretending it was just paper. Eleven days of knowing better.

The guards took his name. He gave them one — not his own, but close enough to pass. That was part of the bargain too. His handlers stood near the windows in civilian coats that fooled no one, watching him the way men watch a horse they intend to shoot after the race. They thought they were the only ones who knew the plan. That was the beauty of it. Everyone in this hall believed they were the only ones who knew anything.

"Courier for the delegation," he said. "Sealed instruments of recognition."

A clerk in Coalition gray checked his papers against a list. Sable watched his own forged name travel down the column and stop where it was supposed to stop. The clerk stamped it. Somewhere behind him, in the gallery reserved for observers, Ilsa Brandt sat with her hands folded like a woman waiting for a train. She did not look at him. She had promised she wouldn't. Her notebook was in her sleeve, as always, contraband to the last.

He crossed the marble. At the green table, Vasch looked up from his draft — Sable could see the clean white pages, the narrative already written, Kesselmoor struck from it before the ink describing it had ever dried. Their eyes met for half a second. Vasch's face did nothing at all, which told Sable everything: *we have an arrangement, courier, and you will keep your end.*

"I have the codes," Sable said quietly. "And you have the record?"

"The ledger is entered into the annex," Vasch said, just as quietly. "Filed, sealed, unavailable for fifty years." Filed. Sealed. Buried with honors. It wasn't what they'd agreed — or rather, it was exactly what they'd agreed, because Sable had known they would cheat him from the first handshake. Men who buy silence do not pay retail.

So be it. He set the lacquered case on the table before the Republican delegates. "From the Foreign Ministry," he said. "To be opened before signatures."

They opened it. The codes gleamed inside like something surgical — the recognition terms, the borders, the price of the Republic's continued existence on paper. The old minister's hands trembled over them, and Sable thought of his handler's shaking hands eleven days ago, the first crack in the world, and how far past that crack he had walked since.

Then he turned and walked back toward the doors, because that was what a courier did. Delivered. Departed. Forgotten.

At the third row of seats, he let his hand trail along the backs of the briefing folders stacked for each delegate — a touch so casual it might have been a man steadying himself. In each folder, sewn flat against the cardboard by hands that had worked all night by lamplight, waited four pages copied from the true ledger. Names. Dates. Kesselmoor, the seventeenth of October, three hundred and forty-one souls, listed one by one in clerks' careful hands.

Ilsa's copies. Pell's covenant. The bread loaves and coat linings and the coffin that had crossed the city overnight, each carrying its small cargo of truth to strangers who might or might not be brave enough.

He reached the doors. The guards there nodded him through. Outside, morning fog lay on the river, and a tram rattled past carrying people who had no idea that history was being signed off two hundred meters behind him.

Sable stopped. Turned around. Walked back in.

* * *

The delegate from the northern Coalition states was a gray-whiskered man named Aldous Renk, and he was the first to open his folder, because he was the kind of man who read everything. Sable had counted on that. There is always one.

He saw the moment it happened. Renk's eyes went down the page, and his whiskers went very still, and his hand came up slowly to his mouth. Then he looked up — not at Sable, not at the Republicans, but at Vasch. And the silence that fell over the green table was the loudest sound Sable had ever heard.

"What is this?" Renk said.

Vasch did not look up. "An error in the printing. Please return those to—"

"It's dated," Renk said. "It's witnessed. It has names, Colonel." He read aloud, haltingly, as if the words were heavy: "*Kesselmoor. Seventeenth of October.*"

The Republican minister lifted his folder now too. Then another delegate. Pages came out of folders like moths from a coat. The hall filled with the dry whisper of paper, which was the only sound the dead had ever been able to make.

Sable stood in the aisle, and he understood that the moment would never come again, and so he did the thing he had rehearsed in the dark of the granary while the others slept.

He began to read aloud.

"Marta Voss, age thirty-four. Anselm Voss, age six."

His voice was not beautiful. It was a courier's voice, flat and forgettable by trade, and it carried anyway, cracking off the marble.

"Pietra Olem, age sixty-one. Dov Olem, age nine."

"Stop him," Vasch said, without heat, the way a man asks for coffee.

Two guards started down the aisle. Sable kept reading. He had perhaps forty names in memory — he had chosen them himself, the children first, because he wanted these men in their pressed gray to hear nothing but children until the very end.

"Halvi Brandt, age eight." He saw Ilsa flinch in the gallery; the name was her brother's, entered in the ledger among the Kesselmoor dead because the boy had died there too, and no one had ever written it down anywhere until Pell's clerks had. "Halvi Brandt, age eight. Recorded by witness, granary archive, entry four hundred and twelve."

The guards took his arms. He kept reading. They dragged him and he kept reading, shoes squealing on marble, voice bouncing off the high ceiling.

"Tomas Rell, age seven—"

A fist closed on the back of his collar and the rest of the name went out of him. But not out of the room. Because Aldous Renk was standing now, holding the pages, and in a voice built for parliaments he read the next name himself. And then the minister from the Republic — the trembling old man whose country Sable had sold — stood and read the one after that. A woman delegate from the western states took the next. The names moved around the green table like a relay, like fire passed hand to hand, while the guards hauled Sable backward through the doors and the sound followed him down the corridor, name after name after name, the dead of Kesselmoor entering the official record of the armistice of Halven Bridge one voice at a time, before the signatures dried.

The last thing he saw was Vasch, pen still uncapped, frozen over a draft that had just become worthless.

* * *

They held him in a cell under the customs house, which had a view of no river and no sky. He did not mind. For eleven days he had been a man carrying bodies; now the bodies were out in the world, in folders, in mouths, in minutes of proceedings that could not be unminuted.

His handlers came at dusk. The one with the steady hands this time crouched outside the bars and regarded him with something almost like professional respect.

"You understand there's a price."

"I priced it myself," Sable said. "That was the bargain."

"They'll want it quiet. No trial, no story. You'll go somewhere with no records." The man smiled thinly. "Which for you should be paradise."

"For me," Sable said, "it's just Tuesday."

But when they took him up the stairs an hour later, hooded, bound, past the green room where clerks were still transcribing testimony into the annex — *entry four hundred and thirteen,* someone dictated, *the courier's statement* — Sable found he was not afraid. He had spent his whole life believing history happened to other people. He had been wrong about that. History happened to whoever showed up with paper.

The truck idled outside. Beyond the hood he heard gulls, the river, a city beginning to believe in tomorrow.

And somewhere in it, he knew, a census taker with a notebook full of names was counting heads — including, if she had any faith left at all, one more.
