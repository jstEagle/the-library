## 10. Lantern and Uniform

The reading room had been a tax office once, under the old republic, and before that a chapel, and before that — if you believed the mason's marks in the cellar arches — a granary that had burned and been rebuilt so many times the walls were mostly memory held together with lime. Ilsa liked that about it. Buildings that kept being useful outlived everything built to be permanent.

Now it was the Office of Provincial Records, Substation Nine, and on paper it belonged to the new regime the way a glove belongs to a hand: fitted, obedient, empty. On paper, Ilsa Brandt was a minor functionary who catalogued deeds of sale and livestock transfers for a district that no longer produced much of either. She stamped things. She filed things. She wore the grey armband because not wearing it was a question, and questions were how people disappeared.

At night, when the inspectors went home to their rationed wine and their careful silences, she lit three lamps — never more, never fewer — and unlocked the cabinet at the back of the stacks.

Inside were forty-one fragments of the Kesselmoor ledger.

That number changed. It had been thirty-eight in spring, then forty-six after the widow Ostrava came down from the hills with pages sewn into her coat hem, then forty-one again after the raid on the Vell Street print shop took five copies and two couriers Ilsa refused to name even to herself anymore. The ledger was not a book now. It was a population. Each fragment lived in a different place — behind a loose brick in the tannery, inside the false bottom of a midwife's scale, folded into the lining of a child's school satchel whose owner did not know it carried anything heavier than lessons. Once a month, by rotation, each keeper brought their fragment here, or sent it with someone they trusted more than they trusted safety, and Ilsa copied it by hand onto fresh stock, verified it against her master concordance, and sent it home.

Her handwriting had gotten worse. Or better, depending on what you needed from it. The census hand she'd used on the road to Halven Bridge — neat, legible, made to be read — was useless now. What she wrote these nights was small, slanted, dense as seed. A page of the massacre record compressed could hide inside a page of grain tariffs. She'd learned that from Sable, or rather learned the shape of it from him, from the way he'd once explained his trade over a fire: *Nobody looks hard at ordinary paper. Ordinary paper is invisible.* He'd said it like a boast. She'd thought even then it sounded like a wound.

She dipped her pen. Outside, Halven Bridge settled into its curfew, the river running dark under the bridge itself, which still stood, which would always stand, which had become the regime's favorite symbol precisely because it had survived what the men who signed beneath it had not built and could not destroy. Propaganda posters showed it at dawn, clean-lined, triumphant. Nobody photographed it at night, when the lamps along its rails were lit one by one by an old lamplighter the state had forgotten to pension off, and the light fell on the water like something being returned.

Ilsa wrote names. That was most of the work, in the end. Dates and troop designations mattered; the colonel's signature mattered; but what the new regime could not abide, what Colonel Vasch himself — still in office, still drafting, his official history now in its third corrected edition — could not tolerate, was the sheer weight of names. Four hundred and eleven dead at Kesselmoor, and the ledger gave four hundred and six of them back their full names, ages, trades, the villages they'd come from. Five remained unnamed, marked only as *a woman, tall, found holding a boy of about seven* and four others like her. Ilsa had promised Pell she'd keep looking. Some promises you keep by refusing to finish them.

The lamp guttered. She trimmed the wick and thought, as she did every night at this hour, of Archivist Pell's last lesson, delivered with a shell falling somewhere behind him and his body already folded around the satchel: *A record is not a weapon. Weapons need hands. A record is a door, and doors only need to be found.* He'd died believing the clerks' republic would outlast every republic, including the one that killed him. He'd been right so far. So far was all any archive ever promised.

Footsteps on the stair.

Ilsa closed the concordance without hurrying — hurrying was a confession — and slid it beneath the tariff ledgers just as the door opened.

* * *

The man who came in was wrong for the room in every particular. Too thin for his coat, which was a prisoner's coat re-dyed a civilian brown that hadn't taken evenly. Hair grown long and going grey at the temples though he couldn't have been past forty. He stood just inside the door the way men stood who had spent months learning that rooms were places things happened to them, and he looked at the lamps first, then the shelves, and only last at her.

"Closed," Ilsa said. "Records office hours are nine to four."

"I know." His voice had gravel in it that hadn't been there on the road. "I'm looking for something that isn't on your index."

"That's most of what people look for."

He smiled — briefly, tiredly, the way a man tests whether his face still works. "It's a list," he said. "Of names. From a place called Kesselmoor."

Ilsa set down her pen very carefully, because her hands wanted to shake and shaking was also a confession. Under the armband, under the functionary's calm, something in her chest turned over like a page.

"Kesselmoor is not in our records," she said. "There was no incident at Kesselmoor. You should be careful. Saying otherwise is a question, and questions—"

"—are how people disappear. Yes." He looked at the floor. "I've been gone a while. Eighteen months. Prisoner exchange, two weeks ago, across the north bridge, in the snow. They gave me back my coat and my name and told me both were worthless." He raised his eyes. "Ilsa. It's me."

She had known since he said *closed* in that particular flat way, since the shape of him against the lamplight made her think of a man walking away from a checkpoint at dusk with his collar up. But knowing and hearing were different animals. Hearing it, her eyes stung, and she was furious at them.

"You look terrible," she said.

"They let me read in prison," Sable Eriksen said. "Did you know that? The new regime is very generous with reading material, so long as it's theirs. I read Vasch's history. All three editions. Do you know what changed between the second and third?"

"He found a way to make the armistice delegation look wiser."

"He cut the footnotes." Sable came closer, stopping at the edge of the lamplight as if it were a border checkpoint of its own. "Two hundred footnotes, gone. Sources, gone. I sat in a cell in Drenmark counting the differences, and I thought: somewhere out there, a census taker is writing down what he erased. And I thought it so many times it stopped being a hope and became a habit, like praying." He swallowed. "Are they safe? The fragments?"

"Forty-one."

He closed his eyes. For a moment he was just a man standing in a warm room after a long cold road, and Ilsa saw what eighteen months had cost written plainly across him, and she understood that this was the price he'd known he was paying in the signing hall when he opened his mouth and began to read. Triumphant sacrifice, she'd called it once, in the granary, and Pell had corrected her: *No such thing. There is only cost, and what you buy with it.*

"The exchange lists said you were dead," she said. "Twice."

"The first time was clerical error. The second time I paid a guard to make it true on paper." A ghost of the old courier's pride crossed his ruined face. "Ordinary paper, Ilsa. Invisible."

"And now?"

"Now I have no name worth forging and no mission worth carrying. They released me because I was cheaper than feeding me." He looked at the cabinet at the back of the stacks, and she watched him understand it without being told — the rotation, the keepers, the network of bread loaves and coat linings and school satchels that had grown from the night of copies into something Pell would have wept over. "You kept it alive."

"We kept it alive. You're late."

Something moved through his face — grief and relief arriving together, indistinguishable, the way they always do at the end of a war. "Is there work?" he asked. "I'm not asking for forgiveness or a bed. Though a bed wouldn't go amiss. I'm asking if there's work. I spent eighteen months learning that a man is what he carries."

Ilsa looked at him a long moment. Then she reached into the drawer where she kept the stationery allotted to Substation Nine — the regime's own stock, watermarked, official — and drew out a single clean sheet and laid it on the table before him.

"The five unnamed," she said. "The woman holding the boy. Three others from the east column. One the witnesses only ever called the schoolmaster. I've followed every road and every register for a year and a half." She slid the pen across the wood toward his hand. "But you were on the Kesselmoor road before any of us. Before the ledger. Before Pell." Her voice caught and steadied, the way the lamplight did. "Tell me what you saw. All of it. Every name you can reach."

Sable picked up the pen. His hand shook — she saw it, and pretended not to, and loved him for not hiding it — and he set the nib to the regime's own paper, in the regime's own office, under three lamps that were never more and never fewer, and began to write.

Outside, the old lamplighter made his way along the bridge, lighting the dark one lamp at a time, and the river carried the light away and kept it.
