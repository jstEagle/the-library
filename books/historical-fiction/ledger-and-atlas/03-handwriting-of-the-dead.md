## 3. Handwriting of the Dead

*Ada*

The lamp had burned low twice before she allowed herself to touch the letters again.

She had spent the first hour doing nothing but looking — the way her master had taught her to look, back when she was eleven and all wrists and impatience, before he set her hand on the paper and told her that a map was a lie agreed upon by everyone who drew it. *Look longer than you want to, Ada. The truth is in what you almost miss.* She could hear his voice still, gravelled and patient, though he had been dead since the first hard frost. She heard it now with a new and terrible clarity, because the handwriting on the table before her was his.

Not similar. Not an imitation good enough to fool a stranger. It was his — the particular forward lean of his capitals, the way his *r*s ended in a small deliberate hook like a bird's foot, the numeral four drawn open-topped because, he used to say, a closed four was a door slammed on the reader. She had inked beneath that hand for nine years. She had ground his pigments, cut his quills to his exact preference — left-biased, stiff-shouldered — and she knew the way his lines thickened when he wrote quickly and thinned when he was afraid. These letters were written fast. And they were written frightened.

She turned the topmost page toward the lamp. The date sat in its upper corner in Iversen's own numerals: the twelfth of November.

Master Iversen had drowned on the third of October.

Behind her, the stranger — Tomas Reval; the name meant nothing, the mud on him had meant everything — stood at the edge of the lamplight with the stillness of a man who has learned not to startle animals. He had not spoken in an hour. He let her work. That, too, told her something about him: most men would have talked, would have pressed, would have tried to steer her toward belief. He simply waited, hands loose at his sides, watching her the way one watches water for ice.

"Say it again," she said, not turning. "The dates. Slowly."

"You received word of his death—" Tomas began.

"The guild received word. A letter from the ferryman's daughter, countersigned by the parish clerk. Drowned at the shallows below Vranov, third of October. Buried the sixth." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "I read the burial record myself. I copied it into the workshop ledger in my own hand."

"And these were posted from Vranov on the twelfth of November," he said. "Nine days after your master was already in consecrated ground. I carried them myself. From a man with his voice and his hands and a scar across his thumb where a chisel slipped in '43 — you'll know the scar, you'll have seen it ten thousand times—"

"I've seen it," she said quietly. "Every day for nine years. He used to say it was the river's signature on him."

The lamp guttered. She trimmed the wick without thinking, her fingers steady in the way that only grief can make them, and laid her palm flat beside the letter as if she might warm the dead man's words back into life.

"Then someone is forging him," she said. "And whoever it is knows his hand better than anyone alive except me."

*Tomas*

He had rehearsed this moment across two hundred miles of frozen road, and every version of it had gone differently than this.

In his rehearsals, Ada Iversen had wept, or raged, or thrown him out into the dark within a quarter hour. In his rehearsals she had never stood so still, never asked questions in that dry, precise voice, never made him feel like the liar he half-feared he was. He had carried these letters through three provinces with soldiers' eyes on the roads, and the whole way he had told himself the hardest part was behind him once he put them in her hands. He had been wrong. The hardest part was standing at a lamplit table while a woman read a dead man's soul out of ink, knowing that everything she believed about the last months of her teacher's life was about to come apart in her fingers.

He watched her turn to the marginalia of the great atlas itself. It lay open on the long bench where it had lain, he suspected, for months — the masterwork, the thing Iversen had spoken of with something close to shyness whenever Tomas collected a packet and rode off with it strapped against his chest. *The Ledger and Atlas*, the old man called it, half joke and half testament. Every river gauge, every boundary stone, every toll schedule and customs rate for a hundred miles of frontier, rendered in copperplate and wash. A merchant's tool, everyone said. A monument to trade.

Tomas had known better since September. He had seen what Iversen slipped between the plates when he thought no one watched.

Ada bent over the atlas margin, comparing flourishes by eye, and he saw the moment it landed on her — the small involuntary motion of her shoulders, like a woman stepping into cold water. He knew that moment. He had lived inside it since the night the letters arrived.

"Here," she said. Her fingertip hovered above a notation in the atlas's lower margin, beside the survey plate of the Vranov shallows. A string of figures, a bearing, a depth sounding. Nothing remarkable. But above the figures ran a single line of Iversen's italic script — *the river keeps the older agreement* — and the *g* descended in that long, restless tail that no copyist could fake, because it wasn't taught anywhere. Iversen had invented it as a boy, he'd told Tomas once, to spite a schoolmaster who said his writing slanted like a drunkard's fence.

"It matches," Ada said. She sounded almost angry about it. "Stroke for stroke. Whoever forged the letters also forged the margins, or—" She stopped.

"Or neither is forged," Tomas said.

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere upstairs a beam ticked as the frost worked at the joists. Down by the river, faintly, the ice spoke too — a long groaning note, the sound it makes when it settles, which everyone in town ignored the way you ignore your own heartbeat.

Ada straightened and looked at him properly for the first time since she'd barred and unbarred the ink door. Her eyes were grey-green, the color of the river in April, and they went through him like a sounding line going through silt.

"Tell me exactly what he gave you," she said. "Not what you carried. What he *said*. Men who carry paper forget that the paper isn't the message."

*Ada*

He told her badly at first, in fragments, the way men tell things they have been carrying alone too long — and then, as the night deepened and she fed the stove and refused to let him stop, he told it whole.

He was Iversen's courier. Had been for two years. A surveyor's assistant by trade, a rider by necessity, the man who ferried the master's correspondence downriver to the archive brothers at the monastery and brought back old charts and older books. Twice a month, sometimes three. He knew the master's habits: the pipe after supper, the walk along the levee at dawn regardless of weather, the way he hummed when a plate was going well and went silent as a closed book when it was going badly. In late summer the humming stopped.

"He started locking the workshop," Tomas said. "He'd never locked it in my time. He started burning papers — not drafts, he burned drafts openly, but these were done at night, in the yard, in a bucket. And he started asking me strange questions. Whether I'd ever been stopped at the customs house. Whether the soldiers on the bridge asked to see inside parcels or just weighed them. Whether I trusted the guild."

"What did you tell him?"

"That the guild was the guild. That Alderman Voss had given him the commission and the commission was honor enough for any mapmaker alive." Tomas's mouth twisted. "Your face just changed, Miss Iversen. Don't think I didn't see it."

"My face did nothing of the kind," Ada said, but it had, and they both knew it. Nine years of devotion did not blind her; it had merely taught her which silences in her uncle's manner were loyalty and which were something colder. Master Iversen had taken the atlas commission from the guild with visible reluctance. She had told herself it was pride — an artist forced to work to a specification. Now the memory reassembled itself into something else entirely: a man accepting money from people he did not trust, because the alternative was letting someone else draw the maps.

Tomas reached into his coat — she flinched before she could stop herself, and he paused, hands open, until she nodded — and drew out a second bundle, smaller than the letters, wrapped in oilcloth tied with waxed cord. He unwrapped it on the bench with the care of a man handling a live coal.

Inside were six pages. Not letters. Plates — proof impressions, pulled from copper, each one a section of the river country between the Vranov shallows and the monastery bend. They were beautiful. They were also wrong.

Or rather, they were right in a way that made Ada's skin prickle. Each impression carried two sets of boundary markings: the border as every official map in the empire drew it, running along the channel's southern bank — and, in Iversen's fine red line, laid over the first like a vein over bone, another border altogether. One that followed the old channel. The channel the river had abandoned forty years ago, before the flood, before the surveys, before anyone thought it mattered.

"The river moved," she said slowly. "Everyone knows the river moved. The treaties were redrawn to follow the new channel. There are stones in the ground that say so."

"There are stones in the ground that say the empire owns up to the water," Tomas said. "But Iversen found the older agreements. The monastery has them — Brother Anselm showed him, that's how it began. The original accord, signed when the channel ran where Iversen's red line runs. By that accord, the ferry landing, the customs reach, the whole northern bank—" he touched the red line with one finger, gently, as if it might bite, "—belongs to the other side of the treaty. To the kingdom. Three governments, Miss Iversen, have built their border on a river that lied to them. Or that they pretended had lied. And your uncle found the proof, and put it into the atlas where no one looks, in a hand only a handful of people alive could read, waiting for someone to finish the work."

Ada looked down at the plates, and the workshop seemed to tilt around her. She thought of all the hours she had spent inking coastlines, resenting history for happening elsewhere, in capitals, to other people. She had wanted, with a childish ache she was ashamed of even now, to matter to the century. And all the time the century had been lying folded on her own bench, in red ink, under her nose.

"Why hide it?" she whispered. "Why not publish? Why not take it to the ministry, to the king, to anyone—"

"Because he tried the quiet ways first," Tomas said, "and quiet ways get men drowned in shallow water."

* * *

*Tomas*

After that, neither of them spoke for a long while, and the lamplight did its slow work on the room — softening the ink-stained benches, the racks of pens, the plaster cast of some coastline he couldn't name hanging on the wall like a saint's icon. He watched Ada Iversen lay the proof plates side by side, aligning their edges with unconscious precision, and understood suddenly why the old man had spoken of her the way he did. Not as an apprentice. As a successor. As a hand he trusted more than his own tired ones.

It was she who broke the silence, and she broke it with craft, not feeling. That was the thing about her that unsettled him most, he decided: she met catastrophe the way she must have met a misprinted plate — identifying the error, tracing it to its source, deciding what could be salvaged.

"If the letters are genuine," she said, "then he was alive on the twelfth of November. Six weeks after they buried someone in his grave."

"Or someone else's grave holds someone else," Tomas said. He had thought about this too much, on too many cold nights, and the thinking had worn a groove in him. "A body was pulled from the shallows. Bodies get pulled from that water every autumn. The parish clerk countersigned whatever the guild told him to countersign. And the guild—" 

"Is run by Alderman Voss." She said the name flatly, testing it, like pressing a thumb to a blade.

"I never said—"

"You didn't have to. You asked me tonight whether I trusted him, in every way but words." She began gathering the letters, stacking them square, and her hands finally trembled — only now, only when the analysis was done and there was nothing left between her and the meaning of it. "You're telling me my uncle was murdered, Mr. Reval. You're telling it to me with his handwriting. So say it."

He should have softened it. Some wiser man would have softened it. But he had crossed a frozen country carrying a dead friend's fear against his chest, and he was past the age of believing that mercy and vagueness were the same thing.

"Yes," he said. "And I think he knew it was coming. I think that's why the letters exist. Read the last one again. Not the words — the order of them. He wrote it like a man leaving instructions in a will."

She read it aloud, because reading aloud was how she verified, he realized — the same way her master had read soundings back to himself. Her voice stayed steady until the final lines:

*"...If you are reading this and I am not, then the atlas is unfinished and must not be finished wrongly. The girl will know. Ada will know what the margins mean; I taught her the hand for exactly this reason, though God forgive me, I hoped she'd never need it. Trust her before you trust me. Trust her before you trust anyone wearing a guild pin or a uniform. The truth of this river is heavier than any of us, but it belongs to her now. Tell her the coastline was never the lie. The lie is inland, where they think no one measures."*

Her voice failed on the last sentence. She set the page down with exaggerated gentleness, the way one sets down something that might otherwise be thrown.

"He taught me the hand," she said, almost to herself. "Two winters ago. Made me practice his italics for weeks — said every mapmaker ought to be able to write legibly in another's style, for corrections, for collaboration. I thought—" She stopped, and pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her eyes, and when she lowered it she was, if anything, more composed than before. "I thought it was a whim. He was full of whims."

"It wasn't a whim," Tomas said. "Nothing he did in the last year was a whim. He was building a lock, Miss Iversen, and he was cutting you as the key."

*Ada*

Dread was a familiar visitor; she had hosted it nightly since the news of the drowning, a dull guest who sat in the corner and asked nothing. This was different. This dread had a direction. It pointed somewhere — out the ink door, down the levee, into the guildhall where Alderman Voss kept his ledgers and his smile, and along the river to the places marked in red ink that three governments had agreed, loudly and officially and in stone, did not exist.

Purpose, she discovered, felt almost identical to fear. It just walked instead of crouching.

"We need to compare every margin," she said, and heard her own voice come out in the tone her uncle had called her surveyor's voice — the one that brooked no argument from rivers or men. "All of them, against the letters. If he hid the border in the plates, there will be more. Dates. Bearings. Perhaps a sequence — a trail, the way you'd chart a route. He wouldn't leave one mark. He was too careful. He'd leave a *system*."

"You understand what that means," Tomas said. "If there's a system, then whoever killed him doesn't know it's there. The atlas passed back to the guild when he died — Voss has had it in his keeping for months—"

"And returned it to me three weeks ago." The memory surfaced with a new chill. Voss himself, in furs, standing in this very doorway, saying the guild wished the family — *you, my dear, you're family in every way that counts* — to complete the masterwork unsullied. *Finish it as he would have*, he'd said. She had been moved. She had been grateful. She had spent three weeks lovingly inking a coastline for a man who may have had her uncle drowned, delivering the weapon back into his hands plate by plate.

Unless Voss knew. Unless the atlas had been searched and searched and found clean, because clean was exactly what Iversen had designed it to look like.

"Mr. Reval." She looked up. The lamp had burned low again and she let it; the dimness felt honest. "Tomorrow I begin a page-by-page examination, and you're going to help me, because your eyes are fresh and mine are compromised by love. We work by night. The workshop stays shut by day — I'm expected to grieve, and grieving gives me a reason to see no one. And when we find what he left—"

"If we find it."

"When," she said. "When we find it, we decide together who sees it. Not the guild. Not yet. Promise me that much."

He studied her across the dead man's handwriting, and she watched him weigh it, this stranger with river-mud still dried in the seams of his coat. Whatever he saw in her face seemed to satisfy some private audit, because he nodded slowly.

"You're more like him than you know," he said. "He used to say the same. *When*, not *if*. Said doubt was for people measuring, and conviction was for people who'd finished measuring."

Outside, the ice groaned again — longer this time, a note that rose and held and faded, and both of them turned their heads toward the window without meaning to. Far down the black river, near the bridge, a lantern moved where no lantern had business moving at this hour. A patrol, probably. Just a patrol.

Ada gathered the letters, the plates, the atlas, and locked them in the iron cabinet beneath the survey bench, turning the key her uncle had cut for her two winters ago and never explained.

"One more thing," Tomas said quietly, as the bolt slid home. "There's a name in the third letter. Marginal, tiny — I missed it a dozen times myself. Look at the bottom left. Tell me what you see."

She took the letter back out, held it to the guttering flame, and read the small slanted characters tucked against the margin like a watermark.

Then she blew out the lamp, because some names, once seen, are better read by heart than by light.

"*Voss*," she said into the dark.
