## 6. Three Checkpoints

The workshop smelled of linseed and cold iron when Ada lit the lamps that evening, and for a long while neither of them spoke. The atlas lay open on the great table between them — Master Iversen's atlas, her atlas now, though she could not yet bring herself to think of it so — its final coastline inked only two nights before Tomas Reval had come scratching at the ink door like something the river had coughed up. Outside, the wind had turned. She had felt it all day against the north-facing panes, a softer wind than the week before, a wind with water in it, and she hated it the way one hates a debt collector seen walking up the lane.

Tomas stood at the far end of the table with his coat still on, as if he expected at any moment to be sent back out into it. He had washed, and the mud was gone from his face, but the road had not gone out of him; it lived in the way he stood slightly forward on his feet, in the way his eyes went to the window every time a sledge runner shrieked on the street below.

"We should not light so many lamps," he said.

"No one can see in past the shutters."

"In my experience," Tomas said, "no one who says *no one can see* has ever been watched properly."

Ada trimmed the nearest lamp anyway, lowering the flame until the shadows climbed the walls and the great table became an island of amber light. It was, she thought, the way Iversen had liked to work — hemmed in by dark, the world reduced to what the hand could reach. She missed him with a violence that surprised her still, even now, even with his handwriting spread out before her proving that her grief had been built on a lie. Perhaps especially now. Grief had been simple. This was not.

"Show me again," she said.

Tomas drew three sheets from the oilcloth wallet he carried against his chest like a second heart. Not the letters this time — those were hidden elsewhere, sewn into the lining of her winter cloak, because she had insisted that no man should carry everything, that if the road took him the truth should not go with him. These were maps. Or rather: proofs. Pull after pull of the border country along the upper river, each bearing corrections in that unmistakable hand — the long descender on the *p*, the little hook Iversen gave to every terminal *r*, like a man turning to look back over his shoulder.

"The old line," Tomas said, laying the first proof down, "as the empire drew it forty years ago." His finger traced a boundary that swung wide east of the marshes, gathering in the ferry landing, the salt road, the chapel fields. "And here—" the second proof, "—the line as it stands in the printed atlases since the treaty. Note what is missing."

Ada bent close. She had studied these proofs for two nights and still the absence struck her fresh each time: the monastery. St. Anselm's-on-the-Marsh, drawn faithfully in every atlas she had ever cut a plate for, simply *not there* — the ground where it stood left blank, unmarked, as though the river had swallowed it and no one had thought to mourn.

"And the third," Tomas said quietly, "is what your master died knowing."

The third proof showed the true line — the line Iversen had surveyed himself, in the last autumn of his life, walking the marshes with a chain and a theodolite while the town believed him at home composing his masterpiece. On this sheet the border ran where the water actually ran, where the old markers actually stood, and it placed the monastery, the salt road, and half the ferry crossing inside territory the empire had ceded on paper but never abandoned in fact. Three governments, Tomas said, had agreed to let the ambiguity stand, because ambiguity fed them all. And a master cartographer of the guild, publishing the truth in an atlas that would sit on every minister's desk from here to the capital, was a lamp too bright for any of them to tolerate.

"He wasn't finished," Ada said. It came out of her like something pulled with forceps. "Everyone says he drowned before the atlas was finished. But it *was* finished. I finished it. I inked his coastline with my own hand."

"You finished what he meant you to finish," Tomas said. "He left the last plates for you deliberately. He told me so, the last time I saw him alive — which, I remind you, was nineteen days after this town buried an empty coffin." He smoothed the third proof flat with both palms, and his voice dropped. "He said: *the girl will know what to do with the silence.*"

*The girl.* Ada almost smiled. Almost. Then the word *died* arrived again in its full weight, and did not leave.

She turned away from the table and stood a moment at the cold hearth, where the ashes of yesterday's fire lay in their gray drift like a map of some featureless country. Fear had been living in her body for two days now, and she had learned its geography: it sat behind the sternum, it made the hands clumsy at fine work, it woke her before dawn listening for boots on the stair. She had imagined fear would feel like fire. It felt instead like damp — a slow seep under a door, everywhere and nowhere, impossible to strike a match against.

But Iversen had taught her what to do with silence. You did not fill it with noise. You surveyed it.

"All right," she said, and heard her own voice come out level, and was grateful to it. "If we are going to move this thing, we move it the way he would have moved a survey. Methodically. In order. Nothing carried twice." She came back to the table and pulled a clean sheet toward her — good rag paper, the kind she used for fair copies — and uncapped the iron-gall ink, and wrote across the top in her own neat capitals: **THE ROUTE**.

* * *

They worked as mapmakers work, which is to say they began by agreeing on what could be trusted and what could not.

"The customs house first," Tomas said. "It cannot be avoided. Every parcel leaving town by the south road passes through the examination room, and every traveler is asked their business and their destination. There is no ford above the bridge — the banks are clay cliffs this time of year — and the bridge itself has been garrisoned since the seals went up. So: the customs house, whether we like it or not."

"Then we do not lie our way through it," Ada said. "Lies require improvisation, and improvisation is where frightened people die. We tell the truth in such a way that the truth is boring." She drew the town, quick and sure, her pen moving with the fluency of twenty years' practice: the market square, the customs house squatting at the foot of the bridge road with its green door and its brass scale, the clerk's window glowing at all hours like a lantern in a bog. "What leaves this town honestly? What moves south in quantity, in parcels, that no examiner wants to open?"

Tomas considered. "Wedding goods," he said slowly. "There's a marriage at the Kessler farm, isn't there? Downriver. The whole street talks of nothing else."

"Linen," Ada said. "A trousseau. Embroidered household linen, packed by a woman, sealed with ribbon — and no clerk in this province will unpick a bride's embroidery with his own hands, not if there is a junior man to make do it." She was drawing already as she spoke, sketching the parcel, the false bottom she could build into a linen chest with the tools hanging on her own wall — the same tools that cut plate edges, that scored paper for folding, that had never once in her life been pointed at anything but accuracy. It occurred to her, with a small cold shock, that she was about to use her craft as a weapon, and that Iversen would have understood completely.

"The atlas pages go inside the chest," she said. "Flat, between layers of linen, wrapped in oilcloth. The letters stay with me — no. Wait." She stopped, pen lifted. "No. The letters go in the chest too. Everything together. One object, one examination, one risk."

"One risk," Tomas repeated, and shook his head. "That's the opposite of how a courier thinks. Never carry everything in one vessel. If the chest is taken, everything is lost."

"If the chest is taken and the letters are found on *you*, everything is lost *and* you are hanged for it," Ada said. "Whereas a bride's chest that turns out to hold papers — papers can be explained. Papers can be *misdelivered*. A clerk who finds documents may be bribed; a clerk who finds a courier may only report him." She met his eyes across the lamplight. "You have been running other people's words through danger for years, Herr Reval. I have spent those same years learning how objects travel. Trust me on the chest."

Something shifted in his face — reluctance giving way, grudgingly, to respect. "My master used to say you were the better technician," he murmured. "I begin to see he flattered neither of us."

She wrote: **First checkpoint — the customs house. Method: the Kessler wedding chest. Date: within four days.**

* * *

The second point took longer, because it required a name, and names were dangerous currency now.

"The monastery," Tomas said. "St. Anselm's. Your master stayed there during the survey — September, October. He trusted the brothers with his instruments, his notes, his person. If anyone holds a last letter, a deposition, anything in his hand beyond what I carry, it is Brother Anselm. And the archive there is stone, dry, and forgotten. Three governments agreed to pretend the monastery doesn't exist; that forgetfulness is the best vault in the province."

"It is also nine miles off the south road through marsh," Ada said. "Ground that looks solid and is not. Ground *I have mapped*." She pulled the regional sheet toward her and tapped the marsh margin, where her own younger hand had recorded soundings and seasonal flood lines in a brown ink gone soft with age. "The causeway floods with the thaw. After the ice breaks, the marsh road is a week of wading. Before it breaks, it is a hard morning's walk and a hard afternoon back. Which means the monastery is on the same clock as everything else."

"Everything is on the same clock," Tomas said grimly. "That is the whole shape of our problem. The river is our road and our enemy both. While it holds, we can cross. When it goes, this town becomes an island with soldiers on every bank, and whatever hasn't crossed by then belongs to whoever holds the bridges."

Ada looked at the window a moment. Beyond the shutter slats the sky was doing something it had not done all winter: raining. A thin, patient rain, the kind that comes before real warmth, ticking softly against the glass like a clerk counting out coins. The thaw was coming early. Everyone in town would be glad of it — the boatmen, the millers, the farmers with their drowned fields. She alone would stand in the street cursing the spring.

"Brother Anselm knew my uncle," she said. "Knew him well enough to write to him. There are letters in that drawer" — she nodded at the tall cedar drawer where Iversen's correspondence slept in ordered bundles — "from a hand that writes *Dearest Anselm* and means it. If we arrive carrying his regard, we will not be strangers. That is worth more than gold at a gate."

She wrote: **Second checkpoint — the monastery archive. Purpose: deposit the letters and the true survey under seal of the Church, where no customs officer may follow. Carrier: myself.** She saw Tomas stiffen and raised a hand before he could speak. "Not because I doubt your legs, Herr Reval. Because a woman visiting a monastery to pray for her dead uncle's soul draws no eye at all, while a foreign courier asking after archives draws every eye in the marsh. And because if the letters are to be deposited under seal, the depositor must be someone whose testimony will hold weight afterward. Mine will. Yours—" she gentled it as much as she could, "—yours is a courier's word against an alderman's. Forgive me. It should not matter. It will."

* * *

Which left the third point, and neither of them wanted to say it aloud, so Ada said it, because someone had to and because saying it might shrink it.

"The ferry landing."

"Yes."

"Frozen solid since Advent. The crossing has run on the ice all winter — sledges, foot traffic, the mail sledge twice a week. The moment the ice becomes unsafe, the garrison posts the landing, because a thawing river is exactly when people try to flee with things governments want." She drew the river's bend, the landing with its ice-stairs and its tarred sheds, the far shore a thin pencil line away and yet, in every way that mattered, another country. "So the crossing must happen in the narrow season. Not so early that the garrison is bored and watchful. Not so late that the ice is rotten. There will be perhaps three days — maybe four — when the ice groans and cracks and frightens everyone, and yet still bears a loaded sledge. The locals know those days. The soldiers don't."

"The ferryman does," Tomas said. "Old Joren. My master used him for years — quiet freight, quiet passengers. He owes Iversen more than money. I'll say no more than that until I've spoken to him, but I believe he'll run it."

"You *believe*."

"I believe," Tomas said, "that a man who has spent thirty years reading that river's moods will know the safe day better than any garrison captain. And I believe he loved your uncle. In my experience love is the only collateral that doesn't devalue."

Ada set down her pen. The sheet before her now held the whole plan in her small precise capitals, and looking at it she felt the strangest sensation — the fear behind her sternum changing state, the way water changes to ice or ice to water. Not gone. Never gone. But *structured*. A route with three points, dates, methods, contingencies, was a thing the mind could hold, and a thing the mind could hold was a thing the mind could survive. Iversen had taught her that too, though he had never said it plainly: that a map is not a picture of the world but an argument against panic. That the blank page is terror, and the ruled page is courage.

"Three checkpoints," she said. "Customs house, monastery, ferry landing. Four days to pack the chest. Then the marsh walk, then we wait on Joren's word for the ice. And through all of it, Herr Reval, we are two people who have known each other five days, committing crimes against three governments, in the name of a dead man's ink."

"When you put it that way," Tomas said, and there was a ghost of a smile in the tiredness of his face, "it sounds nearly sane."

"It sounds *surveyed*," Ada said. "Sane is a luxury. Surveyed will do."

She blew out two of the three lamps, and in the narrowed light they bent again over the proofs, checking and rechecking, two heads close together over the true border of the world — and neither of them noticed, then, the sound beneath the wind and the rain: a low, distant groan from the direction of the river, like something vast turning over in its sleep, beginning to dream of moving.

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere down the dark street, a soldier's boots rang once on stone and fell silent outside the workshop door — paused there a long moment, long enough for Ada's pen to stop mid-stroke and Tomas's hand to slide toward the knife in his boot — and then rang on, fading, past the shuttered windows and away.

Ada let out her breath slowly, and wrote the last line on her sheet:

**We move before the ice does.**
