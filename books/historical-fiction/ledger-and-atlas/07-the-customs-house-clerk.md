## 7. The Customs House Clerk

The customs house at Kesselbrücke had been a granary once, and it still smelled of rye — a ghost of sweetness beneath the newer smells of lamp oil, boot leather, and the particular staleness of rooms where men sit all day deciding the fates of strangers. Tomas felt the smell settle into his clothes as he climbed the steps, and he let it. Let it make him someone ordinary, a grain merchant's factor from downriver, tired of the road, wanting nothing more than his stamp and his bed.

The lie was easier to carry than the truth. The truth was sewn into a wedding parcel under Ada's arm, wrapped in blue paper and tied with white ribbon, and it weighed on her the way guilt weighs, though there was nothing guilty in it but paper and ink.

*Paper and ink,* Tomas thought, watching her climb behind him with her chin set just so, a woman delivering a gift to a cousin's wedding in Vellmar. *It has always been paper and ink that kills men.*

He had learned that lesson twice over now. Once at Iversen's elbow, when the old man had shown him how a border drawn with a steady hand could starve a village or enrich it, could make a farmer's field two fields belonging to two nations, could turn a river from one country's artery into another's wound. And once in a ditch outside Torun, when he had understood what the men who read such papers would do to keep them unread.

He pushed the thought down where he kept the other ones — the courier hanged at the bridge was still only rumor then, but rumors had a way of arriving before their subjects — and squared his shoulders, and became Herr Fassbinder of the Vellmar Grain Concern, and opened the door.

* * *

The hall inside was long and dim, lit by windows gone grey with rain and by three oil lamps that did more to warm themselves than the room. A queue of perhaps a dozen souls stood ahead of them: a drover with sheep in pens outside; two women with market baskets; a family whose father held their papers in a hat pressed to his chest as if the documents might fly. At the far end, behind a counter scarred by a century of cargo, sat the clerks — four of them, and above each a painted number.

Tomas took the measure of them the way Iversen had taught him to take the measure of maps: not for what they showed, but for what they concealed. Clerk One was young and impatient, stamping papers like a man punishing them. Clerk Two was old and asleep with his eyes open. Clerk Three was absent, his stool empty, a cold pipe beside an inkwell going dry.

And Clerk Four —

Clerk Four was a thin man of perhaps thirty-five, with ink stains up both wrists like failed tattoos, spectacles mended at the bridge with wire, and a face that had the particular patience of someone who has spent years being spoken at rather than spoken to. His number was Four, and beneath it, in smaller paint, someone long ago had written his name: **E. LUNZ**.

Tomas looked at Lunz's hands — precise, careful, a little lonely — and at the queue, and at the clock above the counter whose minute hand moved with the leisure of a man who knows no one is waiting for him at home.

*Clerk Four,* Tomas thought. *There you are.*

* * *

They waited an hour. This was part of it — Tomas had planned the hour the way he'd planned the route, because a man who rushes a queue draws eyes, and eyes were the one thing they could not afford. So they stood and shuffled and stamped their feet against the damp, and Tomas complained about the road in a voice pitched for the drover behind him, and Ada stood slightly apart, holding her parcel, wearing the expression of a woman who found queues vulgar but survivable.

She was good, he thought. Better than good. She had taken the role he'd sketched for her — the factor's sister-in-law, carrying a wedding gift ahead of him to Vellmar — and made it true in her posture, her gloves, the small impatient tap of her boot. He wondered briefly whether she had acted before, in some life he didn't know about, and decided she hadn't. She was simply the kind of person who, having decided to do a thing, did it completely.

The line shortened. The family went through with their hat-held papers; the old clerk waved them past without reading anything, which Tomas noted and did not entirely trust. The women with baskets followed. Then the drover, loudly disputing a tariff on sheep, was steered away to Clerk One, and the space before Clerk Four opened like a doorway.

Tomas stepped up to the counter and smiled.

"Herr Lunz," he said, reading the name off the wood with the air of a man greeting an acquaintance. "Long queue today."

Something flickered in the clerk's eyes — the small startled pleasure of a person addressed by name by a stranger. "It's always long," Lunz said. "Rain makes it longer. Papers?"

"Factor's pass." Tomas produced it from his breast pocket, unfolded with care rather than flourish. He had aged the document himself, two nights ago, with tea and a candle held too close — Iversen's training again; the old man had said a forger's first duty is respect for the original. "Fassbinder. Vellmar Grain Concern. I'm running ahead of my principal's shipment — he crosses with the barge goods next week."

Lunz took the pass and read it properly. That was the trouble with careful men; they read things properly. Tomas watched the clerk's eyes move line to line and kept his breathing even and thought, with a clarity that surprised him, of Iversen's hands on the last evening — the old man pressing a satchel into his arms, saying *you'll be the one who carries it through, boy, because you're nobody, and nobody is the most useful thing a man can be.*

"Purpose of travel?" Lunz asked.

"Wedding," Tomas said, and nodded back toward Ada, and toward the parcel in her arms. "My brother marries on Saturday. My sister-in-law brings the gift ahead — she wouldn't trust it to the barge. China," he added, lowering his voice as though confessing something faintly ridiculous. "Her mother's china. Forty pieces. If it breaks, I believe my brother's marriage breaks with it."

And there it was — the smallest crack of a smile at the corner of Lunz's mouth. Not much. A hairline fracture in the official face. Tomas pressed, gently, the way water presses at ice.

"You've a wedding yourself, have you?" he said. "Forgive me — I noticed the ring. New enough that you keep turning it."

Lunz's hand went, involuntarily, to the plain band on his finger. "Three weeks," he said. Then, catching himself: "That's not — it's not a customs matter."

"Nothing is, until someone makes it one." Tomas spread his hands. "I've been on the road eleven days, Herr Lunz. You're the first man who's asked me a question and seemed to want the answer. My congratulations to your wife. May you both outlive the empire."

The clerk stared at him. Then he laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after a winter. "Outlive the empire," he repeated, shaking his head. "My wife says I talk to my ledgers more than to her." He glanced down at the pass again, and this time his eyes moved faster, softer. "Vellmar Grain. Fine." He reached for the stamp.

Behind Tomas, very quietly, Ada shifted her weight from one foot to the other.

Tomas did not turn. Turning was death. But he heard it — the small sound of the parcel's ribbon tightening, or loosening, or being retied, and his heart performed a slow, cold revolution in his chest. She was adjusting it. She had to adjust it; he'd known that when they planned this, known that the atlas pages lay flat against the false bottom of the gift box, beneath a layer of actual china-wrapping straw, and that a parcel carried through rain for an hour grows soft at its knots. She was fixing the disguise while he fixed the man.

Two works happening at once, in a hall full of soldiers, over a counter with a stamp on it.

"Your sister-in-law," Lunz said, pen hovering over his ledger. "Name?"

"Iversen— " Tomas caught it half-formed, felt the name like a fishhook in his own throat, and turned it mid-air. "*Ivarsen.* Fru Ivarsen. My brother married into the Vellmar side; the name's hers from home."

Lunz wrote it. Tomas watched the letters appear — I-V-A-R-S-E-N — and thought: *so close, and so far.* Somewhere in the guildhall across town, Alderman Voss was at this moment probably telling soldiers to look for exactly that name, spelled correctly, attached to a mapmaker's daughter. But soldiers looked for names. They did not look for wedding china.

"Parcel goes on the scale," Lunz said, nodding past Tomas. "Anything for export needs weighing. Duty on manufactured goods, but personal gifts under ten pounds are—" he waved the pen, "—sentimental. Exempt."

"Sentimental," Tomas echoed gravely. "Yes. That describes my brother precisely."

Lunz laughed again — really laughed this time, brief and startled out of him, the laugh of a man surprised to find the muscle still worked. And Tomas felt the old, terrible warmth of it, the craft working, and hated himself a little for how easy it was to spend another human being's loneliness like coin.

Ada stepped up to the scale.

* * *

Later, Tomas would replay those ninety seconds the way one replays a fall — frame by frame, looking for the moment it could have gone differently, grateful for every frame in which it did not.

Ada set the parcel on the brass pan. It weighed — he saw her calculate it, saw her jaw tighten almost imperceptibly — nine pounds and some ounces, thanks to the straw and the two real teacups packed on top as ballast and decoy. Under the limit. Under, but not comfortably under.

Lunz leaned forward to record the weight, and as he leaned, his sleeve caught the edge of the counter, and the dry inkwell — the one beside the dead man's cold pipe — tipped.

It did not spill on the ledger. It spilled on the parcel.

A black bloom spread across the blue paper, quick and total, right over the knot of the white ribbon, right over the place where — if a man pulled that ribbon and lifted that lid and moved aside the straw — the redrawn border of three nations' ambitions lay flat in Iversen's unmistakable hand.

"Oh — oh, I'm sorry," Lunz stammered, already reaching for a rag. "Madam, I'm so sorry, the well's been dry, I didn't think—"

"It's nothing," Ada said.

Her voice was perfect. Calm, even faintly amused, the voice of a woman watching ink ruin wrapping paper rather than watching ink ruin everything. But Tomas saw her hand close on the parcel's edge, knuckles whitening, and he saw her mind working behind her eyes at the speed of drowning — because the stain covered the knot, and the knot was wrong, and any clerk with careful eyes who untied a stained ribbon would find it had been retied recently, hastily, by fingers that were not a bride's.

"Let me clean it," Lunz was saying. "We have spirits, we can—"

"Don't," Ada said, and then, softer, with a small helpless laugh that was the finest piece of acting Tomas had ever witnessed: "Don't touch it. Please. If my mother's ghost sees her china delivered in a cleaned-and-scrubbed parcel she will haunt us both. Ink dries. Weddings don't wait." She lifted the parcel off the scale and cradled it against her, stain and all, the way one cradles something loved and doomed. "Besides," she added, glancing at Lunz's left hand, "a newly married man should know better than to offer to handle another man's mother-in-law."

Lunz went red to the ears. "Quite," he managed. "Quite right." He scribbled the weight, stamped the pass — Tomas heard the thunk of it like a gavel sparing a life — and slid both across the counter. "Nine pounds, four ounces. Personal gift. Exempt. Next!"

But as they turned to go, as the doorway and the rain and freedom stood six steps away, Lunz spoke once more, quietly, not loudly enough for the guards by the door to hear:

"Fru Ivarsen."

Ada stopped. Tomas felt the whole hall tilt.

"The ink," Lunz said, looking down at his blotter, not at her. "It'll come out with milk. Warm milk, before it sets. My wife taught me that." A pause. "She teaches me something new every week. It's — it's a fine thing, marriage. Worth crossing borders for." He looked up then, and behind the wire-mended spectacles his eyes were kind and lonely and seeing far more than Tomas had given him credit for. "Safe travels. Both of you. Mind the ferry landing — they've doubled the watch there since Tuesday. Something about the thaw."

* * *

Outside, the rain had thinned to a drifting mist. Tomas walked beside Ada at funeral pace until the customs house was two streets behind them and a bend in the road hid it, and only then did he let out a breath that shook on its way out.

"Milk," he said weakly.

"He saw the knot," Ada said. Her voice was level, but her hands, when she adjusted the parcel, were not. "He saw the knot was retied and he told me how to remove the evidence of the stain instead of asking why there was one. He knew."

"Or he guessed and chose not to know. There's a difference, and it may save us yet."

"He said the ferry watch is doubled."

"Yes." Tomas looked back the way they'd come, at the customs house dissolving into mist, at the lit window where a lonely man was bending again over his ledgers. "He also gave us our lives and a piece of intelligence for the price of a joke about mothers-in-law. When this is over — if this is ever over — I intend to send him the finest wedding gift in the empire."

They walked on. Behind them, the river groaned somewhere under its ice, a low tectonic complaint that neither of them remarked upon, because remarking would make it real.

And ahead, where the street bent toward the monastery hill, a figure stood waiting in the mist — uniformed, motionless, familiar.

Tomas heard Ada's breath catch, and knew she had recognized him before he had: Lieutenant Brandt, arms folded, rain beading on his shoulders, watching them come with the expression of a man who had been standing there long enough to see exactly which door they'd exited, and carrying questions heavy enough to stop a heart.
