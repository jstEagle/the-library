## 8. An Old Flame in Uniform

The wedding-gift parcel weighed nothing and everything.

Ada carried it against her hip the way a woman carries an infant she has not yet admitted to wanting — one arm curved around it, her body turned slightly to shield it from the jostle of the customs queue. Inside, beneath a layer of good linen and a ribbon Tomas had bought that morning with coins he counted twice, lay the atlas: Master Iversen's life's work, forty-one plates of hand-inked geography, each one a small act of devotion and three of them, now, acts of treason. The border secret slept in the fold-lines like a seed in frozen ground. All she had to do was walk it past four clerks, two soldiers, and a seal stamped in imperial red, and do it looking bored.

Boredom, she was learning, was the hardest thing to counterfeit. Fear wanted to live in her hands; she made them hold the parcel loosely, carelessly, as though it were nothing but a christening gown bound for a cousin in Velmara.

"Keep your eyes on the middle distance," Tomas murmured at her shoulder. He had not touched her since they joined the queue — could not, without drawing attention — but his voice arrived close enough that she felt its warmth through the cold morning air. "Not up. Not down. The middle distance is where guilty people never look."

"You've done this before," she said. It was not a question.

"I've carried things before." A pause, thin as paper. "Never anything this heavy."

Ahead of them the line shuffled forward toward the customs house, a squat stone building that squatted at the mouth of the bridge road like a toad that had learned to collect taxes. Smoke rose from its chimney in a straight grey column. On either side of the road, soldiers of the Third Frontier Regiment stood in pairs, breath steaming, muskets shouldered more for display than use — though Ada noted that the pair nearest the river gate stood closer together than the others, and that their officer kept glancing at the ice as if he expected it to speak.

The spring thaw was early this year. Everyone said so. The rivermen said it, the monks on the hill said it, the ice itself said it in long groaning syllables that came up through the soles of her boots even here, fifty yards from the water. Three days, perhaps four, before the crossing closed. After that there would be no far shore until the ferries ran again in April, and by April the letters in Tomas's coat would be worth their weight in blood to any of the three governments that wanted them unspoken.

She adjusted the parcel. Under the linen, plate thirty-seven pressed its inked edge against her ribs — the coastline Master Iversen had never finished, finished now by her own hand, and threaded through its hachures with the true border that no atlas was ever meant to show.

*Uncle,* she thought, using the old name for him though he had been no kin at all, only kindness given a shape she could love. *If you are anywhere, be in my hands today.*

The queue moved. She moved with it.

---

Inside, the customs house smelled of wet wool, sealing wax, and the particular staleness of rooms where men sit all day deciding other people's fates. Lamplight fell yellow across a counter scarred by decades of knife-points and cargo hooks. Behind it stood four clerks at sloped desks, and above them, mounted on the wall like a saint's icon, the imperial double-eagle in gilt, one wing over the river, one wing over everything else.

Tomas stepped up to the first desk with the easy slouch of a man who had done nothing wrong his whole life. Ada hung back half a pace, the parcel on her hip, letting herself be furniture.

"Business?" the clerk asked. He was young, with a wispy attempt at a moustache and ink stains to the second knuckle — a man who copied documents rather than reading them, she judged, and who was probably lonely enough to welcome any interruption.

"Tobacconist's goods and a wedding gift," Tomas said. "For my sister. Marrying a chandler in Velmara." He produced papers with a flourish that was just careless enough to seem honest. "I'm delivering the gift myself. Family sentiment. You know how sisters are."

Ada did not look at him. She looked at the middle distance, where a fly was tracing slow circuits around the lamp.

The clerk glanced past Tomas at her, at the parcel, and something in his face softened — not suspicion softening but recognition, the ache of a man who saw in every bridal bundle a life he had not managed to begin. "Wedding gift," he repeated.

"Linen," Ada said, and heard her own voice come out steady, almost warm. "My mother's weave. She'd have come herself if the roads weren't what they are."

"The roads," the clerk agreed solemnly, as though the roads were a grief they shared. And then, because loneliness will always outrank duty in a man like him: "Is it true they're closing the crossing? My cousin wrote that the ferry—"

"Kolya!" barked the senior clerk from the far desk, without looking up. "Papers or turnings!"

Kolya flushed to his ears and bent over Tomas's documents, stamping with the wounded diligence of the publicly corrected. Ada watched the stamp rise and fall — *thump*, the eagle biting into wax — and thought: this is how empires actually run. Not on soldiers but on lonely men with ink-stained fingers, each one holding a door open or shut for reasons no general would ever understand.

The parcel stayed on her hip. Nobody asked about it. Wedding gifts, apparently, were beneath the empire's notice — and she felt a wild, unhinged gratitude toward every bureaucrat who had ever decided that women carrying linen were invisible.

Then the doors at the far end of the room opened, letting in a blade of cold light and a draft that made the lamps gutter, and the room changed temperature in some way that had nothing to do with weather.

Ada knew the stride before she saw the face.

---

Lieutenant Erich Brandt crossed the customs house floor the way he had once crossed her father's garden — certain of his welcome, measuring the ground without appearing to measure it. He had filled out since those days; the elegant thinness of the young man who had courted her with borrowed poetry was gone, replaced by the hard competence of a uniform worn long enough to become skin. His greatcoat was grey frontier-issue, his cap sat square, and at his belt, where a courting man might have worn a sword for ornament, he wore one with a scuffed scabbard that had clearly been drawn.

Three years since she had seen him. Three years since the letter she had written ending their engagement — kind, she had tried to make it kind, *you belong to the service and I belong to the coast, and neither of us should have to choose* — and he had answered with one line: *As you wish. Always.* She had told herself the brevity was pride. Standing here now, watching him stop six feet away with the parcel between them like a third person, she understood it had been something worse than pride. It had been a wound folded small enough to carry.

"Frau Iversen." He said her name the way a man says the name of a place he once lived and cannot return to. His eyes went from her face to Tomas — cataloguing the mud on his boots, the too-casual set of his shoulders — and back. "Traveling?"

"To Velmara. A family matter." Her heart was performing feats of engineering behind her ribs; none of it reached her voice. She had learned stillness from a man who drew coastlines for a living, and she spent all of that inheritance now. "Lieutenant Brandt. They've given you the river."

"They've given me the seals." His gaze flicked, briefly, to the parcel. One heartbeat. Two. "There are new orders posted this morning. All outbound freight to be inspected piece by piece. All parcels opened." He said it evenly, a soldier reciting regulations, but his voice dropped on the last words as if the regulation itself embarrassed him. "All parcels," he said again.

The room seemed to contract. At the near desk, Kolya had stopped stamping and was pretending very hard to study a manifest. Tomas, beside her, had gone very still in the way of a man calculating distances to doors.

"Then I'm glad we came when we did," Ada said.

"Yes." Brandt's jaw worked once. Whatever he was about to say next, she watched him decide against it, swallow it, and file it somewhere behind his eyes. "You'll want the Velmara road before noon. The thaw's come early — the ferry master says the ice is talking." He paused. "It talks before it breaks. That's the thing people forget. The river warns you, if you listen. Most people don't listen."

Was it a message? Everything was a message now; she had learned that much in ten days of Tomas Reval and dead men's handwriting. Or was it only Erich, being Erich — the boy who used to read the water level to her off the bridge pilings because he believed she found it interesting, when really he simply needed her to know that he noticed things?

"Thank you, Lieutenant," she said. "For the advice."

He straightened. For one moment the lieutenant's mask slipped and she saw underneath it the twenty-three-year-old who had once stood in her workshop doorway with rain in his hair and said, *Marry me and I'll ask for a coastal posting, I don't care about the career* — and how she had loved him for it, and how she had known even then that he would have resented the sacrifice within a year, and how both of those truths had been true at once, which is the cruelest way truth comes.

"Your escort can carry that," he said quietly, nodding at the parcel. "If your hands are tired."

"They aren't tired." She met his eyes and let him see — not the contents, never the contents, but the fact that she knew what he was asking, and that her answer was the same answer she had given him three years ago, written in a different hand. *You belong to the service. I belong to this.*

Something closed in his face, gently, like a book being shut by someone who intends to open it again later.

"Move along, then," Lieutenant Brandt said, loud enough for the room. "Papers in order. No irregularities here." He stepped aside, clearing the path to the river door, and as she passed him — close enough that she caught woodsmoke and boot-black and, beneath it, some trace of the soap he had used at nineteen — he added, in a voice pitched for her alone:

"We'll speak again, Frau Iversen. When it isn't so crowded."

---

Outside, the cold hit like clean water. Ada walked, did not run, down the steps and along the quay, Tomas a pace behind her, both of them wearing the faces of people with nowhere urgent to be. Only when the customs house was a hundred yards behind them and a fish-stall's awning gave them shadow did she allow herself to breathe out.

"He knew," Tomas said softly. "Or he guessed."

"He knows something." She kept her eyes on the river, silver-grey and swollen under its rotting lid of ice. "He didn't search us. He had the authority standing right there and he invented a reason not to."

"That's worse."

"It's worse or it's better." Her hands had begun to shake now that it was safe for them to shake; she pressed the parcel tighter to her hip and felt plate thirty-seven press back, patient as bone. "Erich Brandt doesn't do anything without a reason, and his reasons always come due. He let us pass because—" She stopped, because finishing the sentence required saying aloud the thing she had felt in the moment his sleeve brushed hers: *because part of him is still standing in my father's garden waiting for an answer.*

Tomas studied her profile. "You loved him."

"I was going to marry him." She said it flatly, a fact laid on a table. "That was before I understood that loving a man and trusting him are two different countries, and the border between them moves." She turned to look at Tomas then, and found his dark eyes already on her, and something passed between them that had no paperwork, no checkpoint, no seal. "He'll ask questions, Tomas. And when he asks, I won't lie to him. That's the danger. I'm a poor liar and he'll hear it in my voice the way he used to hear it when I told him I'd finished a drawing when I hadn't."

"Then we make sure that when he asks," Tomas said, "there's nothing left here to find."

Beyond the fish-stall, at the landing, the old ferryman stood at the end of his pier with his hands in his pockets, staring down at the ice as though listening to it. As they watched, he crouched, laid one palm flat against the frozen surface, and held it there a long moment — the gesture of a man taking a pulse.

Far out in midstream, the river spoke. A sound like a giant turning over in sleep, rolling under the ice from bank to bank, long and low and unmistakable.

The thaw had begun. Somewhere upstream, in the mountains, winter was losing its grip, and all that water was coming for them.

Ada pulled her shawl tighter and turned toward the town, toward the monastery hill where Brother Anselm waited, toward whatever Erich Brandt's promised questions would cost.

Three checkpoints, Tomas had said. One survived. Two remained — and the river was counting down faster than any of them had planned for.
