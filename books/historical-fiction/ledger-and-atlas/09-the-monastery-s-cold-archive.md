## 9. The Monastery's Cold Archive

**Ada**

The monastery appeared out of the snow like something remembered rather than seen — a grey shoulder of stone rising from the white valley, its bell tower leaning a degree eastward as if listening for the river it could no longer see. Tomas had led them off the road an hour past dark, following a track that was less a track than an argument between the hillside and the weather, and by the time the walls resolved out of the falling snow, Ada's feet had gone past pain into a numbness she distrusted.

"You're certain he'll take us in," she said. It was not quite a question.

"I'm certain he owed Iversen his life, and that Anselm pays his debts the way stone pays heat — slowly, but completely." Tomas stood at the small postern door set into the eastern wall and raised the iron ring knocker three times, paused, twice more. "I only hope he hasn't learned to stop answering."

The door opened a hand's width. Lamplight spilled through the gap and fell across their boots, their ruined trousers, the sledge-wrappings Tomas had carried bundled against his chest since the customs house. A face appeared in the light: old, seamed, with the kind of eyes that seemed to have been looking at something far away for so long they'd forgotten how to focus on what was near.

"Reval," the monk said. Not a greeting. An identification.

"Brother Anselm."

"I buried your name two years ago. It is inconvenient of you to dig it up." But the door swung wider, and the cold rushed in around them as if the monastery itself were inhaling. "Get inside before the snow gets inside after you."

* * *

**Tomas**

The warmth of the guest cell hit him like a blow. There was a brazier, a bench, a straw pallet, a jug of watered wine — luxuries arranged with the austere precision of a man who gave exactly what was needed and not a spoonful more. Anselm locked the door behind them with a key the size of a forearm and then stood regarding Ada with an expression Tomas couldn't read: not suspicion exactly, but the wary assessment of a man measuring whether a stranger was worth the cost of sheltering her.

"This is Ada Iversen," Tomas said. "The master's—"

"Niece. Apprentice. Daughter in every way that matters." Anselm said it flatly, and Tomas saw Ada flinch — not at the words but at the tenderness hiding beneath them. "He wrote to me of her. Forty letters, perhaps fifty. In twenty years, I do not think he wrote me a single one without mentioning what she had drawn, or inked, or corrected. You have your hands, girl. He said you had his hands."

"He never told me about you," Ada said quietly.

"No. He wouldn't have." Anselm lowered himself onto the bench with the care of a man whose joints had opinions. "There are friendships a man keeps in the cellar of his life, where the light doesn't reach. Your uncle kept me there because what passed between us would have been dangerous to him if it were known. And now—" the old eyes lifted to Tomas, and there was no distance in them at all now; now they were close as a blade, "—now you come through my gate with soldiers on the roads behind you and something wrapped in oilcloth against your chest. So. Tell me why I should not wake the prior and turn you both out into the drifts."

"Because Iversen is dead," Tomas said, "and the manner of his death is a lie, and you are the only man left alive who loved him enough to want the truth more than safety."

The brazier ticked. Somewhere above them, wind worried at the bell tower, and the old bronze gave out a sound too soft to be called ringing — a hum, like a struck throat.

Anselm closed his eyes. When he opened them again, he looked ten years older and somehow lighter, as though a weight had been set down.

"Show me what you carry," he said.

* * *

**Ada**

They went down. That was the thing Ada had not expected — that a monastery would have a cellar deeper than most prisons, that the archive of Saint Aldhelm's lay below the frost line in vaulted chambers where the air tasted of vellum and stone-cold and time. Anselm carried the lamp himself, refusing help, and the light swung shadows across shelves that ran back into darkness farther than Ada could follow with her eye.

"How many documents?" she asked, because her voice needed work and this was the only question her mind would hold still for.

"Eleven thousand charters. Four thousand letters. Deeds, dispensations, boundary settlements going back four hundred years." Anselm's breath smoked even down here. "Empires rise and redraw their lines, and every empire believes its maps are eternal. We keep the evidence that none of them ever were." He stopped before an iron-banded chest and set the lamp on a ledge. "Your uncle understood that better than any man I ever met. He used to say a map was just a lie with good posture."

Ada laughed — a broken, startled sound — because it was exactly the sort of thing Iversen said, exactly the cadence, and hearing it in a stranger's mouth in a frozen cellar under a mountain made her grief arrive all over again with its boots on.

"He said it about my coastline work," she managed. "Once. To my face."

"And did you improve it?"

"Yes."

"Then he did his work." Anselm unlocked the chest. From within, wrapped in linen gone yellow with age, he drew a packet of letters tied with plain cord, and held them a moment without offering them, as if weighing whether the dead man's privacy outweighed the living woman's need.

"He asked me to keep these," Anselm said. "Not to seal them. Keep them. He said, *If anything happens to me, Anselm, and it happens quickly — give them to whoever comes asking with my handwriting in their pocket.*" The old monk looked at Tomas. "You came asking with his handwriting in your pocket."

"I didn't know these existed."

"Of course you didn't. He trusted you with deliveries, not confidences. There's a difference, and men die of confusing the two." Anselm set the packet in Ada's hands. "Read."

* * *

**Tomas**

Tomas watched her read, and hated himself for watching, and could not stop.

By lamplight her face went through the stages he had already lived through alone, in a rented room in Velmora, with the same pages trembling in his own hands: first the frown of recognition — *that is his hand, no doubt of it, no forger alive could fake that g* — then the slowing, as the dates registered against the calendar, then the stillness that was worse than weeping, the absolute motionlessness of a person whose world has quietly come off its pins.

The last letter was dated nineteen days after the river took him.

Nineteen days after the official account — the slip on the ice-stairs, the drowning, the guild's tidy resolution — Iversen had sat somewhere warm and written in his careful, looping, unmistakable hand about the border survey he had been ordered to falsify, and about the men who had ordered it, and about what he intended to do about it.

"The Vosk corridor," Ada said at last. Her voice came out sanded flat. "The survey maps. The ones in the atlas — the ones I've been inking. The boundary along the northern reach." She looked up at Anselm, then at Tomas, and Tomas watched the cartographer in her take over from the grieving niece, because grief needs a task or it kills you, and drawing was hers. "Three governments signed the treaty line along the river's *old* channel. The channel moved eleven years ago. Everyone agreed to draw it where the treaty says, not where the water is. And the atlas—"

"The atlas shows where the water is," Tomas finished. "Every plate. Every marginal note. He hid the true course in the master copy, in measurements disguised as decoration, in soundings written into the flourishes. Whoever holds that atlas holds proof that the border everyone pretends is settled has been a fiction for a decade. Three governments will burn a province to keep that quiet."

"And the guild printed it anyway?" Ada's voice cracked upward. "Voss approved the plates. Voss *signed* them. He must have known what Iversen—"

"He knew," Anselm said.

It landed in the cellar like a dropped stone. Even the lamp flame seemed to steady itself.

Tomas turned slowly. "You know that. You know it the way a man knows a thing he has been carrying a long time."

"I know it," Anselm said, "because Elias told me. Eleven months ago. He came here in the autumn, unannounced, and stayed four days, and on the last night he sat where you are sitting, Reval, and he told me that the atlas was finished and that finishing it had been a death sentence, and that he knew who would sign it." The old monk's hands, resting on the chest's iron band, had gone white-knuckled. "He said, *Voss will approve every plate and smile doing it, because Voss has been selling our soundings to the chancellery in Karsk for six years, and the atlas completes his inventory.*"

"No." Ada said it very softly. "No. Voss trained him. Voss put him in the workshop when he was nineteen. Voss stood at his funeral and wept."

"Men can weep sincerely at funerals for men they have killed," Anselm said. "I have buried enough of them to know. Grief and guilt wear the same face, child, and only God can tell them apart, and He isn't telling."

* * *

**Ada**

She walked. She couldn't help it — she got up and walked the length of the shelf nearest her, trailing her fingers along the spines of dead documents, because if she stood still she was going to scream, and screaming in an archive felt like blasphemy even now, even with her whole understanding of the world on fire.

Voss. Alderman Voss, whose voice filled the guild hall like pipe-smoke. Voss, who had held her shoulders at the graveside and said, *Your uncle was the finest mapmaker this river ever produced, Ada, and I will see his atlas finished if it takes the guild's last coin.* Voss, who had offered her the master's desk, the master's title, the master's place at the long table.

Who had offered her the master's *place*. As though places were chairs, empty and waiting, rather than things a living man had been pushed out of.

"The letters say he was going to the capital," she said, stopping. "This one — dated the week he died — he writes that he'll present the atlas to the Royal Cartographic Society personally, that Voss expects him to deliver it to the chancellery instead, and that he means to *disappoint the alderman publicly and permanently*." Her finger trembled on the line and she steadied it with the other hand, the way she'd steady a ruling pen. "And then he drowns. On ice stairs he climbed every winter for thirty years."

"Accidents are the oldest tool in the murderer's kit," Anselm said, "because they require no explanation afterward. Only sympathy."

"And the inquiry?"

"Was conducted by men Voss chose." The monk rose, joints cracking like green wood, and came to stand beside her at the shelf. For a moment neither spoke. Down here, the silence had texture; it pressed on the ears. "I will tell you the worst part, Ada Iversen, and then you may hate me for it, and I will accept that. The worst part is that Elias knew. He wrote to me in October — *they will kill me before spring, Anselm, and I find I cannot make myself stop, because a man who knows where the truth is drawn and keeps silent is worth less than the blank paper he refuses to mark.* He chose it. He saw it coming and he chose it, and he left everything he knew in your hands because he believed yours were the only hands in that town that wouldn't sell."

Ada pressed her palm flat against the cold stone shelf and let it hurt. Let the cold bite in deep, all the way to the wrist, because the pain was simple and the rest of it was not.

"He let them kill him," she whispered.

"He let the truth survive him. It is not the same thing. Though I grant—" Anselm's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse, "—it feels the same, from where you stand."

* * *

**Tomas**

Later — how much later, Tomas couldn't have said; the cellar kept no hours — they sat among the documents with the lamp burning low and made their plans on the backs of a charter from 1712, because paper was paper and Anselm said the empire had never once honored its own archives anyway.

Two checkpoints remained. The ferry landing was the hard one; the customs house was behind them, survived by luck and a lonely clerk and a wedding-gift parcel that Ada had assembled with hands far steadier than her voice. Between here and the river lay the high road, the forest track, and — per the talk in the last village, per the smoke of watchfires visible from the ridge — a garrison that had grown in a fortnight from nothing to something with tents and pickets and mounted patrols.

"They're not hunting smugglers," Tomas said, tapping the charcoal line he'd drawn. "Smugglers run goods. They've sealed the crossings like a siege. They're hunting *paper*."

"Then the paper goes across the ice," Ada said, "or doesn't go at all."

Anselm stood apart, re-tying the remaining letters with the cord, and when he spoke he did not look up. "There is a copyist's press in the north scriptorium. Old, slow, mine. Before you leave tomorrow night, we will pull copies of every page you carry — two sets, hidden separately. If the originals drown or burn, the truth survives in duplicate. Elias would insist."

"Elias," Tomas repeated. The name sat strangely in his mouth; he had thought of the man for two years as *the master*, as *Iversen*, as a job and a debt and finally a ghost. Hearing him named plainly, familiarly, by someone who had drunk wine with him and argued with him and received his confidences — it made the loss heavier and more real, a body instead of a rumor.

"He called you reliable," Anselm said, catching the look. "In the October letter. *My courier Reval is the one honest leg my work still stands on.*" The monk's eyes came up, and they were not unkind, but they did not soften. "Which is why you will tell me now, while she is in the room and cannot be spared the hearing, what you have not yet told her. Why you vanished for two years. Why you crawled out of the snow carrying his handwriting like a relic. Why a man who served him faithfully was not at the funeral."

The cellar went very quiet. Ada had stopped moving. He could feel her attention on him the way you feel sun through glass — no heat, but undeniable.

Tomas looked at the charcoal map, at the river line he'd drawn with his own hand, and found he could not look anywhere else.

"Because I was the last one to see him alive," he said. "And because I ran. He told me what was coming, told me to carry the warning letters west and never come back, and I obeyed him — I told myself I obeyed him — and I was forty miles away, safe, dry, *alive*, when word came that he'd drowned. I could have stayed. I could have been there. I might have changed nothing, and I tell myself that every day, and every day it doesn't help." His voice had gone rough; he let it. "I didn't come back for the atlas, Ada. The atlas is the reason I give. I came back because a man who runs from a debt carries it forever, and I am tired of carrying it."

Silence again. The lamp fluttered once in air that hadn't moved.

When Ada spoke, her voice was low and even and colder than the cellar. "The letters say he sent you away *to save you*. That he wrote, *the boy will hate himself for leaving and that hatred will bring him back when I need him most.*" She laid the page flat on the charter, turned it toward the lamp, toward him. "He predicted you, Tomas. Down to the guilt. Down to the return." A pause, and something in her face shifted — not forgiveness, not yet, but the ground where forgiveness might someday be built. "So don't apologize to me. Finish what he started. That's the only apology he'll accept, wherever he is."

Above them, faint through eleven feet of stone, the monastery bell began to toll the midnight hour — and stopped at the second stroke, cut off mid-note, in a way bells were not cut off.

Anselm's head came up sharply.

"That," he said quietly, "is not my prior."
