## 10. Voss Comes Calling

The road down from the monastery was longer than the road up had been, though the distance was identical. Ada felt every stone of it through her boot soles, and every hour of it in the letter pressed flat against her ribs, sewn inside her bodice where Iversen's true last words lay against her heart like a second, colder heartbeat.

*Voss,* he had written. *If anything happens to me, look to Voss.*

She had read it four times by Brother Anselm's candle, four times on the cold stone bench while Tomas slept badly against the archive wall, and a fifth time at dawn before she'd let Anselm's needle close the seam over it. Each reading made the world rearrange itself slightly, the way a map does when you discover the surveyor lied — rivers bending where they should run straight, mountains rising out of plains that had been empty a moment before.

Voss. Alderman Voss. Head of the Cartographers' Guild. The man who had held her hand at Master Iversen's funeral and said, *We have lost the finest mind this river has ever produced, my dear, and you must let the guild carry his memory now.* She could still feel the dry papery weight of that hand. She could still hear the tenderness in it, or what she had taken for tenderness.

Tomas had wanted to come with her into town. She had refused him at the fork above the terraced fields, where the road split toward the monastery gate and the river path.

"You walk in beside me and they'll know everything," she said. "A stranger with mud to the knees and a courier's eyes? Voss will smell him from across the square."

"And you'll be alone with him."

"I've been alone with aldermen before." She had tried to make it sound light and failed. "I'm going home to my own workshop, Tomas. To my own lamp, my own ink, my own door. If I can't be safe there, then there is no safe anywhere, and we may as well burn the letters for warmth."

He hadn't laughed. He had held her elbow a moment too long — the gesture of a man who has learned that goodbyes are rehearsals — and then he had gone north along the hedgerows toward the tannery district, where a widow who owed Iversen debts of another kind would give him a bed and a face no one questioned.

Now Ada came down through the market gate as the bells rang the hour after noon, and the town received her the way towns do: indifferently, completely. Fishwives arguing over eels. A dray horse stamping at flies that were not yet alive but soon would be. Two militiamen at the customs house steps, bored, their carbines stacked against the wall like firewood waiting for a colder day. Nobody looked at her twice. She was Ada Iversen of the map shop, the master's niece-in-all-but-blood, and the town had decided long ago what she was, the way it had decided what the river was and what the border was, and no evidence on earth moved a town once it had made up its mind.

That thought carried her the last three streets, and it was still carrying her when she put her key in her own workshop door and found it unlocked.

She stood very still on the threshold. The lock was old but honest; she oiled it herself each quarter-day; it had never once failed to want its key. And now the door swung inward at her touch, silent, obliging, wrong.

Inside, someone had lit her lamps.

All three of them. Her work lamp with its green glass shade, the standing lamp by the plan chest, even the little brass student's lamp on the windowsill that she used only for examining watermarks — all burning at full wick, so that the workshop blazed with a hospitality she had not asked for and did not recognize. The smell of her own room reached her across the threshold: linseed oil, iron gall ink, the beeswax she rubbed into the drawing boards, and beneath it all something else, something that did not belong — pipe smoke, sweetish and expensive, the kind flavored with cherry bark that came out of the capital and cost more per ounce than her landlady charged for rent.

"Come in, Fräulein Iversen," said Alderman Voss, from the depths of her own chair. "And please shut the door behind you. There's a draft off the river today that plays havoc with an old man's chest."

* * *

He sat in her father's old armchair by the stove — the one she kept for visitors, the one almost nobody used — with his legs crossed and his hat on his knee and his walking cane hooked over the armrest like a sleeping animal. He was a spare man, Voss, built of angles and dryness, with a skull that seemed drawn rather than grown: strong jawline, high cheekbone, a brow ruled in like the margin of a chart. His hair was silver-white and precise. His hands, folded atop the hat, were the only soft things about him, and even those looked soft the way old vellum is soft — worn thin, but toughened by centuries of being handled.

Behind him, laid open across her own drafting table under her own magnifying lamp, was the atlas.

Ada's breath went out of her slowly, deliberately, the way she had taught herself to breathe when her hand trembled over a line she could not afford to spoil.

"You're in my workshop," she said. "Uninvited. With my door unlocked and my atlas open."

"I am protecting your workshop," Voss said mildly, "and your master's atlas, which is more mine than yours if we're strict about guild property, which I am not inclined to be today." He gestured with two fingers at the chair opposite him, the plain one, the apprentice's stool that Iversen had kept for forty years and that she had never been allowed to sit in while he lived. "Sit down, child. You look as though the monastery fed you nothing but theology."

She did not sit. She stood inside the closed door with her back against it and counted the exits — the door behind her, the window onto the yard, the coal chute — the way Tomas had taught her without ever meaning to, in the way he always knew where doors were in any room.

"How did you know I went to the monastery?"

"I didn't." He smiled, and it was a craftsman's smile, economical, no motion wasted. "I knew you went *somewhere*, because you left town before dawn in a week when strangers are stopped on every road and respectable women are not. I knew you would go to Anselm, because your master loved Anselm, and grief goes where love has already dug the grave." He turned a page of the atlas with one careful fingertip, squaring it precisely when he was done, a habit so exactly hers that her skin crawled. "Where is the young man, by the way? The one with the letters?"

"There is no young man."

"Mm." Voss examined a marginal flourish near the delta, tilting his head. "You know, Iversen drew these ornaments freehand, without ruling them first. Everyone told him it was vanity. A ruled ornament can be checked against its rule; a freehand one can only be checked against the man. That was the point, of course." He looked up at her over the lamp. "It is how we will know his forgeries, too."

"They are not forgeries."

"No?" He said it gently, terribly gently, the way a man says *no?* to a child insisting the moon followed the carriage. "Fräulein Iversen. Sit down."

She sat. Not because he commanded it but because her knees informed her that the argument was lost, and it is better to lose standing on your own terms. The apprentice's stool was lower than she remembered, or she was older than she remembered.

"Good girl." He rose, unhurried, joints crackling like green wood, and crossed to the plan chest against the far wall — her plan chest, her locked plan chest, and yet the drawers stood slightly ajar, all nine of them, like a mouth showing gaps. He slid one fully open and ran his fingers along the rolled sheets inside without pulling any out. "You will forgive an old man's curiosity. When word came to me that some vagabond was carrying pages in Iversen's hand dated after his death, I confess I did not sleep. I came here the same night. I have been here, off and on, since." He glanced back at her. "Your landlady makes excellent coffee, by the way. She worries about you. She says you've been strange lately."

"I've been grieving."

"Grieving." He tasted the word and set it down. "Yes. So have I, child. Do you imagine I did not love him? Thirty years in the same guild hall. We argued about projections until our voices gave out and then drank together until they came back." For a moment — she watched for it, watched the way she watched a coastline for false bays — something moved behind his eyes that might have been genuine, might have been memory, might simply have been the light off the lamps. "Which is why it wounds me, wounds me *professionally*, that some forger would make merchandise of his death. And worse, that he would enlist a grieving girl to carry his counterfeits past men with rifles."

"He isn't a forger. And neither am I a girl to be enlisted."

"No," Voss agreed, turning back to face her fully, and now his voice changed register, dropped into the warm low key that filled guild halls, that had talked budgets through three winters of scarcity. "No, you are not. You are the finest draughtsman this guild has produced since your uncle, and I include myself in that accounting without flinching. I have watched you since you were fourteen years old, inking hachures with a patience I have never possessed. I watched him teach you. I watched you bury him." He came closer and stood over her, and the cherry-bark smoke smell came with him, and she understood suddenly that the pipe was lit somewhere in this room, or had been, recently, often — that he had sat in this chair through whole nights, in her dead master's seat, watching her empty bed loft above the workshop like a lighthouse keeper watching for ships.

"What do you want, Alderman?"

"What I have always wanted." He smiled again. "The atlas completed. The guild honored. This town steady, and this province quiet, and the empire's spring coming in on schedule without a single border redrawn by rumor." He lowered himself onto the edge of her drafting table — *on* it, actually touching it, his coat hem brushing the margin of the great unfinished sheet — and folded his hands. "Here is what I know, so that we understand each other. I know that a courier named Reval was seen at the monastery. I know that the garrison at the customs house has orders concerning him. I know that letters exist which are either forgeries — in which case they are worthless and should be surrendered to the guild for proper examination — or they are genuine, in which case—" he paused, delicately, "—they are dangerous, and should also be surrendered to the guild, for proper examination. You observe that either way, Fräulein, the answer is the same."

"The answer is the same only if the guild examines honestly."

The silence that followed was very long. Outside, a cart passed, and the sound of its wheels came through the wall like something being dragged over ice.

Voss did not grow angry. That was the worst of it. He grew *patient*, the way winter is patient.

"Careful," he said softly. "That is sedition adjacent, child, and I am the only person in this town currently positioned to keep you from drowning in it." He straightened, brushed his coat, and became abruptly, dazzlingly generous — she saw the change come over him like weather. "So let us speak of better things. Let us speak of the future." He gestured around the workshop, at the lamps, the chests, the great sheet with its half-finished coast. "When Iversen died, the guild resolved — quietly, informally, but resolved — that the master's chair would stand vacant until the right successor appeared. Not his nephew in the capital, who draws pretty pictures of vineyards. Not old Brandt's cousin, who couldn't project a shadow. You, Ada. You. The guild will confirm it at midsummer. The title, the stipend, the archive key, the vote on the council — everything he had, and more than he had, because you will have what he never got: the atlas finished under your name, published, celebrated. *Iversen's Atlas of the River Provinces, completed by Ada Iversen.* Your name bound in gold beside his, forever. Every schoolchild in three provinces will learn the coastline through your hand."

He let it hang there. It hung beautifully. She hated how beautifully it hung — how the picture assembled itself in her despite her, the gold lettering, the guild hall, the chair by the south window where the light came in clean at noon, her name spoken in rooms she had only ever cleaned.

"All you must do," Voss said, and his voice came down to almost a whisper, intimate as a confessor's, "is give me the letters. All of them. Tonight, if you have them here — I believe you don't, but tonight regardless, through whatever channel you choose. And the pages the stranger carries, when you locate them. And then this unpleasantness dries up and blows away, like ink spilt on sand, and midsummer comes, and I stand up in the guild hall and say *the chair is filled*, and everyone applauds, and no one — no one, Ada — ever asks about a frozen river in February or a fall from a cliff that perhaps deserved a second question."

There it was. Laid on the table between them as neatly as a compass point: *a fall from a cliff that perhaps deserved a second question.*

He had confessed. Or nearly. Or in the language men like him spoke, in which confession and threat share a single grammar.

Ada made herself breathe. Made herself think of the letter against her ribs, of Iversen's cramped furious hand: *look to Voss.* She had imagined, reading it, that she would feel rage when she next saw the man. She felt instead a terrible clarity, cold and level as a horizon line, and understood that rage was a luxury the next hour could not afford.

"If I refuse?" she asked.

Voss sighed, genuinely, deeply, the sigh of a man disappointed in an instrument he had hoped better of. He took up his hat, settled it on his head, collected his cane.

"Then midsummer comes anyway," he said, "and the chair goes to the nephew from the capital. And the inquiry convenes — there will be an inquiry, child; there always is when forgers circulate treason — and the guild finds itself obliged to testify that its former apprentice harbored a stranger of unknown allegiance, corresponded with a monk of questionable politics, and trafficked in documents forging the hand of a beloved dead man." He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and looked back at her with something that in poorer light she might have mistaken for kindness. "Expulsion is the gentlest outcome. The garrison's lawyers are less sentimental than I am. Think of Lieutenant Brandt's position, too, when they ask him how well he knows you."

The door opened. Cold air and fish-market noise poured in and broke the spell of lamplight.

"Oh — one more thing." Voss tapped the doorframe twice with his cane, absently, a man concluding business. "The atlas stays here. Where it belongs. I've posted a man to watch the street — for its protection, you understand. Yours as well." The smile, one last time, brief as a blink. "Do sleep, Fräulein Iversen. You'll need your steadiness of hand. Whatever you decide to draw next."

Then he was gone, and his footsteps faded down the stair, unhurried, measured, the tread of a man whose town this remained.

* * *

Ada sat on the apprentice's stool for a long time after the door closed, listening to the town go on outside — carts, gulls, a child selling chestnuts — indifferent, complete, decided.

Then she rose, crossed to the window, and parted the curtain a finger's width. Across the street, in the doorway of the chandler's shop, a man in a laborer's smock was studying the opposite buildings with the fixed idle gaze of a man studying nothing. He shifted his weight. Under the smock: boots. Garrison boots.

They meant to keep the atlas where it lay. Which meant they believed the letters were the true prize — or that the atlas, finished in the wrong hand, corrected along its margins, would become one.

She turned back to the great sheet glowing under the lamp. Iversen's coastlines ran on without him, beautiful and obedient, tracing a border that three governments had agreed upon and one dead man had disputed with every stroke of his pen.

Ada took down her good pen, the one he had given her, and uncapped the ink.

If Voss wanted the atlas protected, then protected it would be — but not the way he meant, and not tonight, and not alone. There were hours yet before dark, and somewhere north of the tanneries a courier was waiting to hear what the alderman had offered, and whether she meant to take it.

She blew out two of the three lamps, leaving one burning for the watcher to see, and began, with a steadiness that surprised her, to pack.
