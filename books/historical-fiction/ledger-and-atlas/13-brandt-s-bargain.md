## 13. Brandt's Bargain

Lieutenant Elias Brandt had learned, in eleven years of uniformed service, that the hardest duty was never the one shouted at you across a parade ground. It was the quiet one — the order that arrived folded twice and sealed in plain wax, asking a man to watch a door he had once kissed a woman beneath.

He stood now in the shadow of the customs house colonnade, watching that very door across the square. Snow came down in fine, patient grains, the kind of snow that meant the thaw was still weeks away and lying about it. The river beyond the rooftops made no sound at all, which was its own kind of warning. A silent river in February was a river gathering its argument.

Ada Iversen had gone into the chandler's shop four minutes ago. He had counted. Counting was what he did instead of thinking, on days like this one.

He knew what she carried.

He had known since the checkpoint — since she and her mud-boned stranger had walked past him with a wedding-gift parcel between them and Ada's chin lifted at precisely the angle she used when she was afraid and refusing to be. Eleven years ago he had kissed that chin. He had memorized its angles the way other men memorized drill formations, and he had never entirely unlearned them, though God knew he had tried. He had tried in the garrison at Kessel, in the marsh forts, in the long gray years of escorting surveyors who drew lines on paper and called it peace.

*One blind eye,* his orders read. Not his orders — his commander's interpretation of orders, delivered over brandy in a room that smelled of wet wool: *The woman is not to be detained unless she forces the issue. The courier is another matter.* The empire's arithmetic was always the same: weigh the inconvenience against the embarrassment, subtract the dead mapmaker whose name three ministries wished would stay dead, and arrive at the sum that a lieutenant on a frozen frontier could plausibly be ordered to produce.

Brandt had signed the receipt for those orders with a hand that did not shake, which he considered the day's one dishonesty.

Now he crossed the square.

The snow took his bootprints and gave them back filled with white, as though the town itself preferred to forget him the moment he passed. He pushed open the chandler's door into a warmth thick with tallow and beeswax, and there she was at the counter, gloved to the wrist, bargaining down the price of candle-stubs with the serenity of a woman negotiating with empires all her life.

She saw him in the dark glass of the window before she saw him in the room. He watched her shoulders do the smallest thing — not a flinch; Ada Iversen did not flinch — more a settling, the way a ship takes a wave it has been expecting.

"Lieutenant." She turned. "You've found the only shop in town where everything smells worse than the customs house."

"Madam Iversen." He inclined his head, formal enough that the chandler behind his counter lost interest and went back to his ledgers. "I wonder if I might trouble you for a moment of your time. Privately."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I will buy candles until my quarters smell like a church, and ask again."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the way frost makes fern-shapes on glass without ever meaning to be beautiful. "There's a bench by the mill race," she said. "It's public, which suits me, and cold, which suits you."

* * *

The mill race ran black and slow under its lid of ice, and the bench beside it had been put there, Brandt suspected, by some optimist who had never spent a winter in this town. They sat anyway, because Ada chose to sit, and because sitting put two feet of frozen plank between them, which was perhaps her idea of diplomacy.

He let the silence run first. It was an old habit from interrogation school — let the subject fill the quiet, learn their tempo from it. But with Ada the silence filled itself with memory instead, and that was the trap he'd walked into: eleven winters of things unsaid, stacking up between them like cordwood.

"You watched us at the checkpoint," she said at last.

"I watch everyone at the checkpoint. It is, professionally speaking, the entire point of the checkpoint."

"You looked at the parcel."

"I looked at *you*." He kept his eyes on the black water sliding under the ice. "The parcel was incidental."

"And what did looking at me tell you, Lieutenant? In your professional capacity?"

"That you were terrified," he said. "And that you were going through with it regardless. Which told me everything else."

The river groaned then — a long, deep sound, like a door opening in the floor of the world, and both of them went still until it faded. Early. Too early for February. Everyone in town had been pretending not to notice for three days.

"The ice is talking," Ada said quietly.

"The ice always talks before it breaks. So do governments." He turned to look at her properly then, and the sight of her profile against the gray water struck him in the old place, below the ribs, where he kept the version of himself that had once believed he would marry her. "I'm going to say something, Ada, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide what I am. Because I know what you're deciding. You've been deciding since the moment you saw my uniform at the gate."

"Have I?"

"You looked at me like a locked door looks at a key it doesn't trust."

That got the almost-smile back, briefly. "You always did have a talent for simile. Your letters were wasted on the army."

"My letters were wasted on a woman who answered none of them."

"I answered the first six."

"You returned the first six."

"*Unopened*," she corrected, "which is different, and which we are not discussing, because I did not walk out here to reopen an account eleven years closed, and neither did you. Say what you came to say, Elias. The chandler is watching us through his window and the whole town will have us betrothed by supper."

The use of his Christian name went through him like warm water through ice — sudden, total, leaving channels where there had been solid ground. She knew it, too. She had always known exactly which levers were hers.

"Very well." He drew a breath and made himself say it plainly, because plainly was the only currency he had left to offer her. "I know what you carry. Not its contents — I don't need the contents. Iversen's name crossed my desk in October with a seal on it I wasn't cleared to break, and men above me stopped saying his name aloud shortly after. Whatever pages left his workshop after his death are pages three governments have agreed, informally and deniably, should cease to exist. The courier who carried them out of the hills was found last week at the bridge at Sorrel Ford. You may have heard."

"I heard," Ada said, and her voice did not change at all, which told him how much it cost her.

"They hanged him with a customs manifest in his pocket. That was the message. Not for him — he was past reading. For whoever came next." He looked at her. "For you, Ada. For your mud-boned stranger. The message is: this far, no farther, and the price is posted publicly."

"And you?" she asked. "Are you the next part of the message?"

Here it was, then. The moment he had rehearsed on the colonnade and ruined in every rehearsal, because in every rehearsal she answered differently, and he understood now that he could not control her answer any more than he could control the thaw.

"I am offering you what I have," he said. "Which is one blind eye."

"One," she repeated.

"One. My sergeant watches the ferry landing. My corporal watches the bridge road. I can unwatch one of them for a night — lose the report, misfile the rotation, take the blame of a lapse. That is within the margin of what a lieutenant can survive. Two blind eyes is treason, discovered or not, and treason gets you a cell in the capital and a trial where the verdict is written before the charges." He held up a hand before she could speak. "Before you argue — understand what I am telling you. One blind eye means one crossing. One sledge, one load, one person on the far side of the river. It does not mean two. It does not mean the atlas goes one way and the letters go another and you stand in the square afterward daring the world to touch you, which — forgive me — is precisely what you are planning, because I have known you since you were seventeen and you have never once in your life taken the exit offered."

The silence after that was very long, and the river filled it with small cracking sounds, like knuckles.

"You've thought about my plans," she said finally.

"I've thought about nothing else for nine days," he said, "and I resent every hour of it."

* * *

They walked. Neither proposed it; the bench simply became unbearable to both of them at the same moment, and they found themselves moving along the mill path with the careful distance of people who had once known each other's walking pace intimately and were relearning it like a language half-forgotten.

"You loved him," Ada said. It wasn't a question. "My uncle. You sat in our workshop when you were a cadet home on leave, and he showed you the projection of the sphere, and you loved him. Don't deny it — you had the same face you have right now."

"He was the first man who treated maps as if they were moral documents," Brandt said. "Everyone else treated them as furniture for borders. He used to say a coastline was a promise the land made, and the cartographer's job was to keep the land honest." He smiled without joy. "I was twenty and I thought he was exaggerating. Then I spent a decade drawing lines that moved villages around like chess pieces, and I understood he hadn't exaggerated at all. He'd understated it."

"Then help me keep the land honest."

"There it is," he said softly.

"Elias—"

"No. Hear the arithmetic, since you're so fond of demanding plain speech." He stopped walking and faced her, and the wind off the river pushed her hood back and she didn't fix it, and the snow began collecting in her dark hair, and he made himself continue because if he stopped he would say something unforgivable. "If I give you the blind eye and you cross with everything and vanish, I lose my commission, possibly my freedom, and I will deserve it, and I will not regret it — I want that stated for the record you're always building in that head of yours. But if you stay — if you stand up in front of the guild, or the tribunal, or whatever tribunal Voss is building out of his fear and his ledgers — then my blind eye protects nothing. You'll be standing in the one place in this province my eye cannot reach. The guild hall is not my jurisdiction. The inquiry is not my jurisdiction. And Voss—" he paused, choosing, "—Voss has friends whose names appear on documents I am not cleared to read. Do you understand? Mercy has a border too, and it ends at the guild hall door."

"Then your mercy asks me to run," Ada said. "To send the evidence away and hide behind it while Voss writes my uncle's death into the record as an accident and my name into it as a forger's accomplice. That isn't mercy, Elias. That's evacuation."

"It's survival."

"For whom?"

"For *you*." The word came out louder than he intended, cracking against the ice, and somewhere downstream a crow lifted off the alders in a clatter of wings. He lowered his voice. "For you. God help me, Ada, I have buried enough people I — I have attended enough inquiries. I know how they end. The record is a machine. It takes in truth and prints convenience. I have fed it myself. I know its appetite."

"Then someone must refuse to feed it." She looked at him with those eyes that had ended his first life, calm and terrible. "Someone must stand inside the machine and read the true letter aloud while the gears close on her. You think I haven't counted the cost? I have done nothing for nine days but count it. But my uncle taught me that a map is a promise, and someone redrew the promise, and someone killed him for knowing it, and if I run — if I let the atlas go down the river while I sit safe in the crowd — then I have kept the parchment honest and sold the man. I won't. I would rather hang beside the courier at Sorrel Ford than live to ninety on the far side of that choice."

He stared at her, and eleven years collapsed, and he understood suddenly that he had never loved the girl he remembered. He had loved a rumor of her. The woman standing in front of him, snow melting in her hair, offering her neck to the machine of history — *she* was someone he was meeting for the first time, and meeting her felt like losing something and finding it in the same breath.

"You always were insufferable when you were right," he said hoarsely.

"It's the atlas training. We hold the line even when the coast disagrees."

* * *

They reached the corner where the mill path met the square before either spoke again. The customs house rose across the way, its windows lit against the dusk, soldiers stamping at the door — and beyond it, higher up the hill, the guild hall with its bronze weathercock turning slowly in the wind, pointing nowhere useful, the way it had all winter.

Brandt stopped. This was the edge of his offer; he could feel it, the way a surveyor feels a boundary stone under snow.

"One blind eye," he said. "Take it or leave it. The ferry landing, the night the ice begins to go — my sergeant will be called to the bridge on a false report of movement, and for one hour the landing will be unwritten. Use it or don't. But hear me on the rest, Ada, because I will only say this once." He stepped closer, near enough that he could see the candlelight of the square reflected twice in her eyes. "If the inquiry comes for you — and it will come, soon, Voss is frightened and frightened men move fast — then you send word to me through the chandler. Not a letter. Buy candles. Tell him which ones. I'll know."

"Why would you do all this?" she asked quietly. "After everything. After the letters I sent back unopened. Why?"

Because he had spent eleven years drawing lines that moved people like chess pieces and wanted, once, before the end of his career, to draw one that protected somebody. Because the empire had taught him every use for loyalty except the honest one. Because the alternative was standing at her tribunal in full uniform, holding the papers that condemned her, and living the rest of his life as a well-kept ledger.

"Because one of us should have been brave eleven years ago," he said. "I've decided it's my turn."

He saluted — formally, absurdly, in the middle of a public street — and turned toward the customs house, and had gone perhaps ten paces when the boy found him. An apprentice runner from the guild hall, breathless, cheeks slapped red, waving a folded card sealed in the guild's green wax.

Lieutenant Brandt broke the seal, read the single line, and felt the temperature of the evening change.

*Alderman Voss requests the honor of the Lieutenant's attendance tomorrow noon, upon occasion of a public declaration concerning the authenticity of certain documents.*

Concerning certain documents. Publicly. At noon, when the whole town stood in the square.

Behind him, he knew without turning, Ada was still watching. He folded the card into his glove, and thought: *the machine is moving faster than either of us planned, and tomorrow it arrives in the square, and my one blind eye will be useless against what happens in daylight.*

Tomorrow, Voss would call the letters forgeries.

Tomorrow, Ada Iversen would stop being a smuggler and become a defendant — and Brandt would have to decide, before noon, whether one blind eye was a mercy at all, or merely the slowest form of betrayal.
