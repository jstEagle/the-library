## 14. Forgery and Testimony

The summons came at dawn, delivered not by a boy with a note but by two journeymen of the guild in their formal blue sashes, standing on Ada's step with the stiff politeness of men who would rather have been anywhere else. *The Guild of Cartographers and Surveyors requests the presence of Ada Iversen before the full assembly, nine o'clock, the Guildhall.* The word *requests* had been written in a clerk's careful hand, but the ink beneath it was Voss's — she knew his habit of pressing too hard, of leaving ghost-grooves on the reverse of every page he touched. Even his paper bullied.

She dressed as though for a funeral, because that was what it was. Her best grey wool, the collar Iversen's wife had embroidered decades ago with a border of interlocking rivers, her hair pinned back so severely it hurt a little at the temples. Pain was appropriate. She wanted to feel every minute of what was coming.

Tomas stood in the workshop doorway while she buttoned her cuffs, and she could see him composing arguments, discarding them, composing others. He had been awake all night; she had heard him pacing the floor above hers like weather trapped in a room.

"You don't have to go," he said finally. "We could be at the monastery by midday. Anselm would hide us until—"

"And say what to them? That I was afraid?" She took her coat from its hook — Iversen's old coat, too broad in the shoulders, smelling still faintly of linseed and the particular dust of dried inks. "He built me, Tomas. Every line I draw, I learned from him. If they mean to erase him, they will have to do it while I stand there watching."

"They mean to do worse than erase him."

"I know." She met his eyes. They were brown, ordinary, exhausted, and she had come to love them more than any coastline she had ever drawn. "That's why I'm going."

* * *

The Guildhall had been raised by the same generation that raised the customs house and the monastery's west tower — heavy stone, narrow windows, a roofline built for snow and siege. In summer its great hall smelled of beeswax and cold air; in March it smelled of wet wool and tallow, and beneath both, faintly but unmistakably, of fear. Ada felt that last scent the moment she crossed the threshold. Fear has a chemistry. It rises off crowds the way damp rises off a riverbank.

The assembly was nearly full. Masters in their chain-of-office, journeymen crammed along the side benches, apprentices permitted to stand at the back as witnesses to how institutions discipline their own. She found familiar faces and watched them find hers: Master Kessler, who had taught her projection geometry, looked quickly away. Old Frau Lindqvist, who kept the guild's seal and had once held Ada on her knee at a midsummer feast, stared at her with an expression Ada could not read until she understood it — pity. Pity was worse than hostility. Pity meant the verdict had already been reached and everyone present knew it except, formally, the accused.

Alderman Voss sat at the center of the high table, and he had dressed for the occasion the way he dressed for everything, expensively and without warmth, his grey beard trimmed close, his hands folded on the ledger before him like a man at prayer who no longer believed in the god he was addressing.

"Ada Iversen," he said, when the herald had called the session to order. "You are here to answer for the possession and propagation of documents falsely attributed to Master Henrik Iversen, deceased. Do you have those documents with you?"

"No," said Ada.

A murmur ran the length of the hall. Voss's eyes narrowed by a degree — a cartographer's adjustment, fine-tuning his position.

"Then you confirm they exist."

"I confirm nothing about your framing of them." She kept her voice level. She had rehearsed this on the walk over, and rehearsal held. "I possess letters written in Master Iversen's hand, dated after the day he is said to have drowned. I have compared them against eleven years of his marginalia, his field notes, his private correspondence with the monastery archive, which Brother Anselm will attest to under oath if this guild permits sworn testimony. The hand is his. Not similar. His. I inked beside that man for six years, Alderman. I know his letters the way I know my own pulse."

"Skill in imitation," Voss said mildly, "is the first qualification of a forger. And grief—" he let the word sit, let the hall absorb its soft cruelty "—grief makes us credulous. You loved him as a daughter loves a father. No one disputes your devotion. We dispute only what the forgers have made of it."

"The forgers," Ada repeated. "Name one."

"That is rather the question we hoped *you* would answer." He unfolded his hands. "You arrived in this town in the company of a stranger with no papers, no guild standing, no name anyone can verify beyond the alias 'Reval.' Within days of his arrival, you were concealing documents, evading the customs seals, moving through the countryside under cover of darkness. Lieutenant Brandt's report describes the parcel you carried through the checkpoint. A wedding gift, you claimed." Someone in the benches laughed — short, nervous, quickly stifled. "The empire is at a delicate hour, Miss Iversen. There are men across three borders who would pay dearly for maps of our river defenses, our crossing points, our garrisons. And here is a woman with access to the most complete atlas ever drawn of this frontier, suddenly traveling with an unverified foreigner and lying to officers of the crown."

"Lying to protect the truth about how my teacher died."

"He fell through the ice," Voss said. "It happens every winter on that river. Ask the river."

"I did ask the river." She let that land. She saw Kessler look up. "I asked it in the form of eleven pages of his handwriting, Alderman. Shall I read them aloud?"

The hall went very quiet. This was the hinge, and she felt it turn beneath her — the whole room balanced on whether she would be allowed to speak those pages into the record or whether Voss would close the door first.

"You shall not," Voss said, "because this assembly is not a stage for the theatrical reading of forged materials, the distribution of which is itself a crime under the emergency statutes now in force." He turned a page of his ledger with terrible patience. "But since you raise the question of authenticity, let us resolve it properly."

He nodded to the door. Two clerks entered carrying a document case between them, and Ada's stomach dropped before she even saw the wax seal, because she recognized the case. Guild property. The reference collection — the locked cabinet in the archive where the guild kept authenticated specimens of every master's hand, taken at induction, renewed at each promotion, precisely so that questions like this one could be settled forever.

Voss drew out a single sheet and held it up. "Master Iversen's hand specimen, taken when he attained mastership, witnessed by four members present in this hall today. I have submitted the letters you claim are genuine to comparison against this specimen and against the atlas itself." He paused, savoring. "Three independent examiners. Their finding: the letters are imitations, executed by someone with considerable training — training, one might observe, available to very few people in this town. The flourishes are exaggerated. The letterforms drift. A dying man's hand, perhaps, one examiner suggested charitably. Or an impostor's."

"That specimen," Ada said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated herself for it, steadied it, "is from twenty-two years ago. His hand changed. Everyone's does. Compare the letters to the marginalia in the *current* atlas — the atlas *you* have been guarding so tenderly since the day he died."

"The atlas," said Voss, "has been examined as well." Something moved behind his face then, something quick and satisfied, gone in an instant, but Ada caught it, and the catching chilled her more than anything else that morning. "And here we arrive at the gravest matter. Because the atlas itself shows signs of tampering. Marginal annotations, altered plates, insertions not in the master's verified hand. Insertions concerning—" he glanced down at his ledger as if consulting it, though Ada knew he knew every word by heart "—the boundary survey of the northern reach. Military matters. Matters which a loyal guild, in wartime conditions, is obligated to report."

The hall had stopped being a hall. It had become a machine, and Ada could hear its gears — the small coughs, the shifting of feet, the way no one met her eyes because meeting them required deciding something, and deciding had been made expensive.

"You're doing it," she said quietly. "You're doing exactly what he wrote that you would do."

"Master Iversen," Voss said, "drowned in January."

"He drowned in February," said Ada, "and he was alive in March, and I can prove it, and so can Brother Anselm, and so can the ferryman Halvard, and so, Alderman, can *you*, and every person in this hall knows why you need him dead twice."

The words rang off the stone and hung there. For one long breath she thought she had done it — thought she had cracked the surface the way the thaw cracks ice, one sound and then the whole sheet groaning loose. Kessler's head came up. Frau Lindqvist's hand pressed flat against the bench. Somewhere in the apprentice ranks a boy whispered, *he was alive in March?*

Voss did not flinch. That was the terrible thing. He simply folded his hands again and looked at her with something almost like sorrow, performed so well that half the hall must have believed it genuine.

"There it is," he said softly. "There is the treason. Not in the forgeries — those are merely tools. But there, in the mouth of the guild's own adopted daughter: an accusation against this body, against me personally, on the authority of papers any competent examiner has already condemned. Child," and the endearment was the knife's handle, "you have been used. Grieved and used. When this is over, when the man who wrote those letters is found — and he will be found — I hope you will understand that some of us tried to save you from him."

He turned to the assembly. "The evidence before us warrants action. Under the statutes of the guild and the emergency ordinances of the province: Ada Iversen is stripped of her journeyman standing. Her name is struck from the rolls. She is barred from guild premises, guild employment, and the use of guild instruments, upon penalty of prosecution. Any member found aiding her shares her sentence." A beat. "And her case, given the military character of the materials involved, is referred to the provincial inquiry convening at week's end."

The gavel came down. Once. Twice. On the third stroke Ada realized she had been holding her coat buttons so hard the thread had cut her finger, and there was a small bright bead of blood on the grey wool, and she thought absurdly: *ink.* Even now, everything came back to ink.

* * *

They turned her out through the side door, past the instrument cases in the corridor, past the great framed map of the town that she had walked beneath since she was nine years old, that had taught her the shape of home before home had any other shape. A journeyman held the door for her. His eyes were frightened and sorry and he did not say a word, and she loved him a little for the silence.

Outside, the morning was hard and clear, the kind of light that showed every flaw in a drawing. The river lay flat and white below the terraces, and even from the Guildhall steps she could hear it — not breaking yet, not breaking, but talking to itself in the deep, the long subterranean syllables of ice deciding what it would become.

Tomas was waiting at the corner by the chandler's shop, and the relief on his face when he saw her was so naked that she had to look away from it, because if he embraced her there in the street she would come apart entirely, and she had one thing left to do and it required all her pieces.

"Well?" he said.

"I'm nobody now." She said it experimentally, testing the weight. "Struck from the rolls. Referred to the provincial inquiry." She laughed — a small, astonished sound with no humor in it. "Do you know, I spent my whole life believing the guild was the world. That the rolls were like the bedrock under everything. You work, you rise, your name goes in the book, and the book is forever." She looked back once at the Guildhall, at its narrow windows, its roofline built for siege. "It isn't bedrock. It's just paper. Everything is just paper, Tomas. Which means everything can be rewritten."

"Ada." He took her arm, gently, steering her away from the square, away from the eyes beginning to gather. "The inquiry meets at week's end. Voss wants you before a tribunal he controls, with the letters declared forgeries in advance, in a town where the courier they hanged on the bridge is still swinging as a lesson in what testimony costs. You cannot testify there. They'll destroy you and bury the pages in the same motion."

"I know."

"Then say it. Say what we have to do."

She stopped walking. Below them, past the rooftops, the ferry landing jutted out onto the white river, and she could see, even at this distance, the new palisade the soldiers had thrown up across it, and the smoke of the garrison cookfire rising thin and straight in the cold air. Three checkpoints. One remained. One night of ice left, maybe two, before the river took even that away.

"Halvard owes Iversen a crossing," she said. "First light, whichever morning the ice still holds. You take the atlas pages and you don't stop for anything on the far shore. Not soldiers. Not me. Nothing."

"And you?"

She looked down at her finger, at the bead of blood already browning on the wool, and thought of Iversen's last letter hidden beneath the floorboards, and of the tribunal chamber waiting for her at week's end like a mouth opening.

"I keep the letters," Ada said. "Someone has to put the truth into the record. That was always the work. That was always the whole work — making things true *on paper*, where they last." She lifted her chin toward the Guildhall, toward the inquiry, toward the entire machinery of paper that had just expelled her and believed that was the end of her. "They struck my name from their rolls. Fine. I'll enter it into a different record. Theirs isn't the only book being kept."

Tomas searched her face for a long moment, the wind pulling at his collar, and whatever he saw there — grief, terror, and underneath both something that had finally stopped asking permission — made him nod slowly, the way men nod at a river they've decided to cross.

"Then tonight," he said, "we split the evidence. And tomorrow at first light—"

The church bell began to ring. Not the hour — the alarm peal, rapid and irregular, the bell rung by hand. Down in the square, people were running toward the bridge, and a boy's voice rose above the rooftops, ragged with shouting:

*"The ice! The ice is going — the ferryman's sledge is out on the ice —"*
