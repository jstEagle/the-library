## 15. The Ferryman's Debt

The ferry landing looked different in the dark.

Tomas had seen it first as a boy of nine, when his father put him on the ice-road with a sledge of tanned hides and told him not to stop walking until he smelled woodsmoke on the far bank. He had seen it again at nineteen, crossing with letters for Master Iversen sewn into the lining of his coat. He had seen it a hundred times since — in every season, in every weather — and it had always seemed to him a modest thing: a shelf of planks jutting from the reeds, a rope-walk worn smooth by forty years of boots, the great flat boat hauled up on rollers above the waterline each autumn like a beast put to pasture.

Now it was a fortress. Two sentries stood where the plank walk met the shore road, their breath rising white under a shuttered lantern. Behind them, a timber barricade had gone up sometime in the past two days — Tomas could see the pale, fresh-cut faces of the boards even at this distance, moonlit and raw as opened bone. A cookfire burned behind the barricade, and beyond it, half-hidden by the boathouse, a tent had been pitched where no tent had ever been. The garrison had arrived quietly, the way weather arrives, and now it sat on the river's throat like a hand closing.

He lay in the frozen reeds thirty yards off, Ada beside him, her shoulder almost touching his. Her breath came slow and deliberate, the way she breathed over an ink line that could not be redrawn. He had learned to read her breathing the way she read coastlines: the small accelerations, the long controlled exhale that meant *I am afraid, and I have decided what to do about it.*

"Two sentries visible," she whispered. "There'll be four more sleeping behind the barricade. Maybe a sergeant."

"You counted the firewood," he said. "You're counting everything now."

"It's how I keep my hands from shaking."

He almost laughed. Almost. Instead he pressed himself flatter against the ice-hard ground and studied the landing the way Iversen had taught him to study anything — patiently, without mercy, until it gave up its structure. *Everything has a grain,* the old man used to say, tapping a map or a man's character with equal confidence. *Find the grain and you can split it clean.*

The grain here, Tomas thought, was the boathouse. It sat apart from the barricade, closer to the water, and its door faced the river rather than the soldiers. And behind the boathouse — he shifted his eyes along the shoreline, following the dark seam where willow scrub met ice — there was a path. Overgrown, invisible to anyone who had not walked it, but there. It led down to a second, smaller landing: the old one, abandoned when the new plank walk was built twelve years ago, its pilings rotted, its boards sprung. No soldier would guard a landing that did not officially exist.

But a ferryman might still remember it.

* * *

Old Máté had ferried the crossing for longer than anyone in town could say with certainty. He had ferried it before the last war redrew the border a mile east; he had ferried it through the quarantine year when the whole valley held its breath; he had ferried smugglers and bishops and brides and once, famously, a circus bear. He was seventy if he was a day, lived alone in a house built half of boat-timber, and spoke to strangers only when absolutely necessary — which, in Tomas's experience, meant he spoke to strangers never.

Tomas knocked on his door three times, paused, then twice more. The old signal. His father had used it; his father's father too, back when the family ran hides and salt across the ice and Máté's own father ran the boat.

The door opened a hand's width. One eye, rheumy and sharp at once, regarded him through the gap.

"You're dead," Máté said.

"Not yet."

"The other courier. The one who used to come with the master's letters." The eye narrowed. "They said a courier swung from the bridge."

"They said true. But it wasn't me." Tomas kept his voice low and level, though something in his chest clenched at the words. He had not gone to see the body. He had made himself not go. "Máté, I need to talk to you inside. Please."

The door hesitated — and then, with the creak of a hinge that had outlasted three governments, opened.

Inside, the house smelled of pitch, tar, dried fish, and lamp oil, the smell of a life spent at the edge of water. Tools hung in their places with the discipline of a ship. On the wall above the cold stove hung a single framed item, and Tomas's heart turned over when he saw it: not a saint's picture, not a family portrait, but a map. An old survey chart of the river bend, drawn in a hand he knew better than his own — the confident downstrokes, the small precise flourishes at the ends of place-names, the little fish inked in the deep channel as a private joke between surveyor and reader.

Master Iversen had given maps away the way other men gave alms.

"He came to me in October," Máté said, without preamble, lowering himself onto a stool with the care of a man whose joints had opinions. "The master. Last time he crossed."

Tomas went very still. "October."

"You heard me." The old man poured something amber from a clay jug into two tin cups and pushed one across. "He sat where you're sitting. He drank what you're drinking. And he paid me for a crossing he never took."

"I don't understand."

"He paid me for a crossing he never took." Máté drank, watching Tomas over the rim. "Put coin in my hand — good coin, more than the fare — and said: *Máté, someday soon a person will come to you needing the water, and the soldiers will be standing on your landing, and everyone else will tell that person no.* That's what he said. Word for word. I've had two months to wonder at it."

Tomas's cup trembled against his knee. Two months. October. The master had been alive in October — alive, and crossing the river, or meaning to — weeks after the guild had recorded his death, after the funeral, after the *accident* on the slippery towpath that no one had witnessed. Alive, and already afraid. Already arranging his own evidence's escape like a man moving furniture ahead of a flood.

And I carried his letters, Tomas thought. I carried them past this very house. If I had asked one question. If I had stopped once.

"Máté," he said, and his voice cracked on the old man's name. "The person he spoke of. It's me. Or it's me and one other. We need the water."

"I know who needs the water." Máté set down his cup. "Half the town knows someone's been asking after the ferry. Soldiers came yesterday asking after it too. They asked me how thick the ice runs midstream." A dry sound that might have been laughter. "I told them the truth. Ice is ice. It does what it wants. They didn't care for the answer."

"What did they want, really?"

"They wanted to know if I'd run anyone across. I said my ferrying days are done, my boat's up on the rollers, my bones are seventy years old." He shrugged. "All true, as it happens. Except the part about being done."

Tomas leaned forward. "The ice breaks early this year. You've heard the river."

"I've heard it my whole life. It started groaning Tuesday night. When the river starts talking, it doesn't stop till it's free." The old man's face went grim. "You've got days. Maybe less. After the break-up, nothing crosses this water for two weeks — and by then every checkpoint on both banks will have orders with names in them."

"Then we cross at first light. Tomorrow."

Máté was quiet for a long moment. In the silence Tomas could hear the river itself, faint under the floorboards — a low, shifting complaint, the sound of a giant turning in its sleep. The thaw had come three weeks early. Everyone felt it in their teeth.

"The new landing's garrisoned," Máté said at last. "Six men, a sergeant named Kroll who likes his brandy, a lantern on the plank walk that they light at dusk and douse at dawn — lazy habit, that. Dousing takes them ten minutes, because the corporal insists on doing it properly and the sergeant argues about doing it at all." He smiled without warmth. "Ten minutes is a long time on a dark shore."

"And the old landing?"

Something flickered in the old man's eyes — surprise, maybe, or respect. "Nobody's used the old landing since the new one went in. Pilings are rotten. Boards are rotten. But the ice by the willows runs thicker than midstream — spring water feeds it shallow, freezes solid early, lets go late. A sledge can come off the ice there, if the driver knows where the seam is." He tapped the floorboard with his boot, right over the buried memory of the old path. "I know where the seam is."

"You'd take us yourself?" Tomas had expected directions, a favor called in, perhaps a nephew. Not this.

"I'd take you myself." Máté rose, slowly, and went to the corner where a shape lay under sailcloth. He pulled it back, and Tomas caught the gleam of fresh tar, new-caulked seams, a hull that had been tended all winter like a sick animal brought back to health. "Been readying her since January. Don't ask me why. Something in my bones said get her fit." He looked back at Tomas, and for a moment the mask of age dropped and Tomas saw the man who had rowed soldiers and saints across this river for half a century. "Your master paid me for a crossing in October. I'm delivering it late. That's not charity. That's bookkeeping."

Tomas stood. His legs were unsteady, and not entirely from the cold walk. "Máté — if they catch you—"

"If they catch me, I'm an old fool who misjudged the ice." The old man waved a hand. "At seventy, what do they take from me? A few winters I wasn't going to spend anyway." Then his gaze sharpened. "But listen to me, courier. There's a condition."

"Name it."

"The girl comes too. Both of you. First light, both banks of the same boat." He said it flatly, leaving no room. "I don't split cargo across two crossings. One trip, one debt, paid entire. Whoever stays behind stays by their own choosing — but I won't carry half a story across and leave the rest standing on the shore for Kroll's men to pick up."

Tomas looked at the floor. He thought of Ada's breathing in the reeds, slow and deliberate, the breathing of a woman who had already decided something and simply hadn't said it aloud. He thought of the letters sewn against her ribs, and the way she had looked at the tribunal summons nailed to the guildhall door — not with fear, he realized now, remembering, but with recognition. She had known for days what she meant to do. Perhaps she had known since the monastery, since Brother Anselm laid the master's true last letter on the cold table and Voss's name sat in the middle of it like a bullet.

"She may not come," Tomas said quietly.

"Then that's between her and the river," Máté said. "Not between me and you. First light. Old landing. Come by the willows, single file, and step where I step or don't step at all." He poured the rest of the jug back, stoppered it, and turned to the window, to the black line of the river. "Now get out of my house, courier, before the patrol passes. And tell the girl—" he paused, and his voice roughened, "—tell her the master drew my river truer than God made it. Tell her I'd know her work anywhere. Tell her I'm honored to carry it."

* * *

Tomas found Ada where he had left her — not in the reeds, but in the ruined smokehouse on the hill above the landing, a stone shell with a collapsed roof that gave sightlines to the water and cover from the road. She sat with the atlas pages spread across her knees in their oilcloth wrapping, not reading them, just holding them, the way a person holds something they are deciding whether to keep or give away.

She knew before he spoke. He saw it happen — saw the knowledge arrive in her face as he came through the doorway, saw her arrange herself around it.

"Well?" she said.

"First light. The old landing below the willows. Máté himself. The ice by the willows holds another day or two, no more." He knelt in front of her. "Ada. He won't make two trips. He says whoever crosses crosses together."

The lamplight from the distant barracks fire reached them faintly through the broken roof, enough to see her eyes, which were calm in the way that frightened him most. Calm the way deep water is calm.

"Tomas," she said gently, as if explaining a coastline to a child. "If I cross, the letters cross with me, and they die on some publisher's shelf in the capital — evidence in a drawer, a footnote in ten years. Voss keeps his seat. The guild writes its history. The master stays a man who slipped on a towpath." She shook her head slowly. "But if I stay — if I stand in front of the tribunal and read those letters aloud, in front of the whole town, in front of Brandt, in front of God and the guild ledger — then the record changes while the ink is still wet. Testimony entered into history, dated, witnessed, undeniable. They can burn archives, but they can't un-hear a room full of people."

"And they can hang witnesses."

"Yes," she said simply. "They can."

The river groaned beneath them, long and low, a sound like a door opening somewhere underground. Above the landing, the sentry's lantern swayed once and steadied. Somewhere behind the barricade a man laughed at something, the ordinary laugh of an ordinary night, unaware of what the morning intended to do with him.

Tomas took her hands — ink-stained, chapped, steady as drafting tools. "Come with me. Cross first. Testify from the far bank, from safety, send the letters back with a lawyer, with Brandt, with fifty copies—"

"With what army?" She smiled, and it broke his heart, that smile — tender and immovable at once. "You taught me what the master believed. Everything has a grain. History has a grain too, Tomas, and the grain runs through this town, through that tribunal room, through me. The truth has to be cut along the grain or it splinters." She squeezed his hands. "Take the atlas. Get it printed. Let the maps speak from the far shore while I speak from the dock. Two voices, two banks. That's how we finish what he started."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Then Tomas bowed his head against her hands, and she let him, and the river kept talking underneath everything, patient as fate, counting down the hours until first light — when one of them would step onto the ice, and one would turn to face the room where history waited to be written.
