## 17. The Last Ice Crossing

Tomas had not slept. He lay in the ferryman's loft above the boathouse with his boots on and the atlas pages bound against his chest beneath his coat, listening to the river speak in the dark.

It was not a silence, the frozen river at night. It was a language. Long groans rolled up from beneath the ice like the voices of drowned men remembering their names, and somewhere downstream a crack ran out with a sound like a musket shot, then another, then a long tearing whisper that faded into the black. The thaw had come early — everyone said so, the way people say a thing they hope saying will prevent — and every hour the ice grew thinner over water that wanted to move again after four months of stillness.

He thought of Ada in her workshop across the town, awake too, he was certain of it, sitting at the master's old desk with the letters spread before her like a hand of cards she had already decided how to play. *I will testify,* she had told him, in that voice she used when she would not be argued with, the voice of a woman reading coordinates aloud. *The pages go with you. The truth goes with me.* He had quarreled with her once about duty and lost, and he would not quarrel again. But lying in the dark with the river groaning beneath him, he understood for the first time what it cost her to stay, and what it would cost him to carry what she could not.

Below him, Old Marek was already moving. Tomas heard the creak of the stair, the scrape of the lantern hook, the small ceremonial sounds of a man preparing for work he did not expect to survive. He went down.

Marek stood in the boathouse doorway looking out at the ice, and in the light of the single lamp his face was all planes and shadow, weathered down to essentials, the way driftwood is. He had loaded the sledge in the night: the runners wrapped in felt against noise, the harness oiled, two poles lashed crosswise for balance. Beside the sledge sat the coil of rope — sixty feet of good hemp, the line he had used thirty years to haul the ferry across open water, now to be paid out behind him as he walked.

"You should sleep," Marek said, without turning.

"I couldn't."

"Neither could I." The old man's breath hung in the lamplight. "Forty-one years I've crossed this river. Summer water, winter ice. Iversen mapped my crossing twice — once when I was young enough to argue with him about where the channel ran, and once the winter before he died. He sat in my kitchen drinking bad brandy and drawing my landing better than I'd ever seen it drawn, and he asked me questions no soldier ever asks. Where does the current turn. Where does the ice fail first. What do the reeds know in March." Marek smiled at nothing. "A man who asks those questions is a man who intends to come back."

"He didn't come back."

"No." The word fell like a stone through ice. "And they told us it was the towpath, the mud, the accident. And we believed them because believing is easier than digging." He turned then and looked at Tomas, and his eyes in the lamplight were pale as river stones. "They hanged a boy from the bridge on Tuesday. A courier. Seventeen years old, maybe. They left him there all day so the town could look. That was not justice. That was a message written in the only ink soldiers have." He picked up the rope and coiled it over his shoulder. "So we write ours in hemp and runners, and we see whose message carries farther."

* * *

They went down to the ice in the last hour of darkness, when the stars were thinning and the east held only a suggestion of grey, the color of unprimed paper. The town lay behind them blind and shuttered; ahead, the river ran wide and white toward the far shore, perhaps half a mile of ice between here and the willow scrub on the other side, and beyond that the road north, and beyond that the capital, and beyond that printers who would set the truth in type where no alderman's fire could reach it.

Tomas tested the ice at the landing with the iron-tipped pole Marek handed him. It rang solid — a clear bell-note, the sound of ice thick enough to bear a horse. Ten feet out, it rang again. Twenty feet, the note deepened. At forty it went dull, and a hairline of darker white showed where the ice had begun to sweat from below.

"The channel runs there," Marek said quietly. "Where the reeds are. We keep to the near shelf until the last hundred yards, then we cross the channel fast, straight, no stopping. If it holds, it holds. If it doesn't—" He shrugged, a gesture that contained the whole arithmetic of the morning.

"If it doesn't, you turn back," Tomas said. "That was the bargain. You get me to the edge of the channel. The rest is mine."

Marek looked at him with something close to pity. "Boy, I've been making bargains with this river since before your father drew breath. The river keeps its own bargains. Come."

They moved out onto the ice, Tomas pulling the sledge, Marek behind with the rope paying out from a loop at his belt, anchoring them both to the shore cleat by a length that grew longer with every step. The felt-wrapped runners whispered. The eastern sky brightened by degrees, and the river's whiteness took on color — blue in the shadows, pearl where the wind had polished it, and out along the far bank a low band of gold where the sun would rise within the hour.

Halfway to the channel, Tomas allowed himself the dangerous luxury of hope.

It was going to work. The ice sang under the runners, solid, generous. The garrison fires on the far bank were embers dying in the dawn. Perhaps Brandt had kept his one blind eye turned. Perhaps the sentries slept. Perhaps the river, having taken its toll of groans all night, would grant this one passage, this one sledge, this one stubborn cargo of paper and ink —

The first shot came without warning, flat and enormous across the ice, and struck the surface twenty feet ahead of them in a burst of white chips.

"Down!" Marek roared, and Tomas dropped, dragging the sledge with him, sliding on his side across the slick surface as a second shot cracked overhead and a third went wide and skipped away across the ice like a thrown stone. On the far bank, figures were spilling from the tents — three, four, five — rifles coming up, an officer's voice carrying thin and furious across the water.

"They knew," Tomas gasped. "God damn them, they knew—"

"Someone talked or someone watched. Doesn't matter now." Marek was beside him, flat on the ice, rope in his fists, and his face had gone very calm, the calm of a man who has run out of futures and found it a relief. "Listen to me. The channel is eighty yards ahead. When I say go, you go, and you don't stop for anything — not shooting, not cracking, not me. You hear me? Not me."

"Marek—"

"Eighty yards of bad ice, and every second you stand on it the river gets a vote." The old man gripped Tomas's wrist, and his hand was iron. "Iversen trusted you with his life's work. Ada trusted you with hers. Don't make liars of them. Whatever happens on this ice in the next five minutes belongs to you alone, and what happens after belongs to everyone. Go when I say."

Across the river, the officer was shouting orders. Sentries were running along the bank, parallel to the channel, trying to find the angle. The gold band of sunrise broke the horizon and laid a long blade of light down the middle of the river, straight across the channel, as if the day itself were pointing the way.

"Now," Marek said.

* * *

Tomas ran.

The sledge leapt behind him, runners shrieking now that speed had stripped away caution, and the ice rose to meet his boots in great ringing strides. Fifty yards. Forty. The shots came faster — he heard them strike and skip around him, heard chips sting his cheek, heard Marek shouting something behind him that the wind tore apart before it arrived. Thirty yards. The ice changed color beneath him, the healthy white giving way to a grey-green translucence, and through it he could see the water moving — actually moving, black and muscular, the current turning under the skin of the world.

Twenty yards. The ice spoke.

Not cracked — *spoke*, a deep groaning syllable that ran the length of the channel, and then a sound like a giant knuckle pressing down on a tabletop, and the surface beneath Tomas flexed, visibly flexed, a slow wave traveling ahead of the sledge. He threw his weight forward. Fifteen yards. Ten. Behind him the world split open with a report like a cannon, and he did not look back, because Marek had told him not to, because looking back was the one thing that would kill them both—

Five yards. The far shelf rose under his boots, solid white, blessed land-ice, and he hauled the sledge up it with a strength he did not know he owned and collapsed against the willow scrub, chest heaving, alive, across, done—

Then he heard the rope.

It was still attached. Sixty feet of hemp running taut from his belt-loop back across the channel, and at the end of it, standing on a raft of tilting ice in the middle of the black moving water, was Old Marek.

The channel had opened between them. Not wide — ten feet, twelve, a seam of grinding floes — but the old man's island was drifting, slowly, ponderously, downstream, and the rope between them hummed like a plucked string.

"Cut it!" Marek shouted. His voice carried strangely well over the water, unhurried, almost gentle. "Cut the rope, boy! It'll drag you in!"

"I can pull you across—"

"You can pull me into the current, and then we're both gone, and the pages drown with us." The old man was wrapping the rope twice around his forearms, deliberately, methodically, the way he might tie off a boat. "Listen. Listen to me now. The ice is breaking up behind me — the whole near shelf is going. In a minute there'll be no crossing for anyone, and that's a gift, do you understand? A gift. No soldier follows you today. No soldier follows anyone. The river closes the road behind you."

On the far bank, the soldiers had stopped firing. Tomas could see them at the edge of the collapsing ice, shouting among themselves, and he understood: they could not cross either. The thaw had chosen its side.

"Marek, please—"

"Forty-one years," the old man said, and he was smiling, Tomas could see it even at this distance, a smile worn smooth as his face. "Every crossing I ever made, I made for pay or habit. This one I make for something worth the price. Iversen asked me once what the river remembered, and I told him the river forgets everything. I was wrong. It remembers this." He braced his boots against the tilting ice and hauled the rope taut — not to save himself, Tomas realized with a horror that would live in him forever, but to anchor the floe, to hold the channel open a moment longer, to be certain, absolutely certain, that no pursuing boot would touch the far shore while Tomas still breathed on it. "Tell Ada the debt is paid. Tell her the ferryman sends his regards to the atlas. And tell her—" the ice groaned, tilted, and the old man's voice rose clear and unbroken over the black water, "—tell her history happened here, this morning, to us. Write it down."

The floe went.

It did not capsize so much as dissolve — the current taking it in one long green swallow, the rope snapping taut, humming, singing, and then, mercifully, parting with a crack that sounded almost like the ice itself, and the river closed over the place where Old Marek had stood as though he had never been, leaving only the wake, widening, smoothing, gone.

Tomas knelt in the willow scrub with the severed rope in his hands and the sledge beside him and the sun coming up gold over the water, and he did not make a sound. There was no sound in him. There was only the cold, and the weight of the pages against his chest, and the terrible arithmetic of the morning: one life spent, one cargo saved, one river crossed that could never be crossed back.

Behind him, upstream, the ice was going all at once now — a thunderous, cascading roar as the whole winter's bridge broke apart and began its journey to the sea. The road was closed. The evidence was safe. The promise was kept.

He gathered the rope's broken end, wound it around his fist, and stood.

Then, from the direction of the town, faint across the water, he heard the bells begin to ring — not the alarm, he knew the alarm — but the inquiry bell, the guild tribunal, summoned early, summoned now. Ada was standing up before them this morning, alone, with letters in her hand and Voss waiting in the front row.

Tomas shouldered the sledge harness and turned north, toward the capital, toward the printers, walking fast against the sunrise with a dead man's rope in his pack and a living woman's name in his mouth — and prayed, with every step, that he would not be too late to matter.
