## 18. The Inquiry

The bell had stopped ringing before Ada reached the guildhall steps, and the silence it left behind was worse than the sound — a held breath, the whole town drawn in and waiting to let it out against her.

She climbed the stairs slowly, because she wanted to feel every one of them. Fourteen steps of grey river stone, worn hollow in the middle by a century of boots, and she had run up them a thousand times as an apprentice with ink on her fingers and no weight at all in her hands. Now each step took something from her and gave something back: her fear went down into the stone and came up as something harder, colder, more useful. By the time she reached the doors she was not afraid anymore. Or rather, she was afraid all the way through, but the fear had been pressed so flat and thin that it shone like mica, and she could see through it.

The letters were inside her coat, against her breastbone, wrapped in oilcloth. She could feel them the way a nursing mother feels the child asleep against her — a warmth that would not be ignored, that demanded everything.

Inside the great hall, the guild had arranged itself like a court because it believed itself to be one. The tribunal sat on the dais beneath the old charter map of the river valley, the one Master Iversen himself had drawn thirty years ago, its vellum gone amber, its rivers still blue, its borders — she saw it now, God help her, she could not stop seeing it now — its borders running exactly where he had said they must never be allowed to run again. Three aldermen of the guild in their chains of office. Alderman Voss at the center, silver-haired, magnanimous, his hands folded on the rail before him as though he had come not to judge but to grieve.

And behind the rope barrier, filling the benches to the walls, the town. The miller's wife. The boys who swept the customs house. Old Marta from the dye works, who had taught Ada to keep her nibs clean when she was nine years old. Faces she had mapped without ever knowing it, all these years, the way she mapped coastlines — by habit, by light, by love — and had never once thought to put them on paper.

They had come to watch her be destroyed. Most of them. Some had come hoping for something else; she could tell which ones by how they would not meet her eye.

* * *

The clerk read the charges in a voice like dry paper turning.

*That Ada Iversen, journeyman of this guild, did knowingly traffic in forged documents; did conspire with persons unknown against the lawful determinations of the guild; did impugn the memory and the findings of the inquiry into the death of Master Henrik Iversen; and did thereby bring scandal upon the guild and upon the peace of the town.*

Scandal upon the peace. Ada stood where she was told to stand, on a chalked square on the floorboards like a mark on a chart, and listened to the word *peace* and thought about the courier hanged at the bridge, his boots swinging, and about what kind of peace required a man's body hung over running water to maintain it.

"You may answer," Voss said gently.

He had rehearsed the gentleness. She recognized craft when she heard it — she was a craftsman too. Every cadence of his opening had been inked and re-inked, corrected, burnished. He spoke of her grief. He spoke of how the whole guild mourned Henrik Iversen, "a man whose genius we will not see again," and how grief made the mind hospitable to fictions. He spoke of the stranger, Tomas Reval — *a courier of no guild, no letters patent, no name that any registry can find* — who had appeared at her door with papers whose very dates condemned them, since the dead do not write.

"Master Iversen drowned on the fourth of November," Voss said, and his voice broke slightly, beautifully, on the word *drowned*. "These letters are dated the nineteenth of January. I do not accuse this young woman of forging them herself. I accuse her of nothing worse than wanting her uncle alive so badly that she mistook a forger's cleverness for his hand." He spread his palms. "We have all wanted the dead back. It is the oldest mercy in the world, and the most dangerous."

A murmur ran through the benches, warm toward her, almost tender. Ada felt it pass over her like weather and understood, with a cold clarity, that this was the trap working precisely as designed. He was not going to hang her. He was going to pity her. Pity would close the matter forever, seal the letters away as evidence of her madness, and Master Iversen would go into the guild's history as a good man who died in the water, and the border would stay where three governments wished it to lie, and no one in this room would ever know they had been present at the burial of the truth.

She waited until the murmur died.

"Alderman," she said. "May I ask the tribunal a question?"

Voss inclined his head, gracious as a host.

"The guild's charter," Ada said, "which hangs above your heads, states that no member shall falsify any line, border, bearing, or measurement, upon pain of expulsion and referral to the crown magistrate. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"And the guild's first charge to any member is fidelity to the record. Not to the guild's comfort. To the record."

Voss's folded hands did not move. But something behind his eyes moved, the way water moves under ice — you see only that the surface has stopped being still.

"That is correct," he said.

"Then I am ready to answer the charges."

* * *

She unwrapped the oilcloth with steady hands, and the sound it made — that soft crackle — carried further than a shout. The clerk objected; the second alderman, old Frau Hessler, who had once been Iversen's own apprentice before anyone living remembered, said in a voice like a hinge, "Let the girl speak," and the hall settled.

"I will not read all of it," Ada said. "It is long, and much of it is technical — bearings, soundings, the methods he used to verify what he found. Master Iversen was never capable of writing anything briefly." A ripple of laughter, sad and involuntary, because everyone in this hall had been scolded by those margins at least once. "But I will read enough."

She unfolded the last letter — the true last letter, the one Brother Anselm had kept hidden in the monastery archive behind the false shelf, the one naming Voss — and began.

*"To whoever holds my atlas after me: if you are reading this, then I did not live long enough to finish the work, and someone has decided for me whether the work should be finished at all."*

Her voice held. She had expected it to break and it did not, and later she would wonder at that — that grief, given the floor, turned out to have better manners than she did.

*"I write this on the nineteenth of January, in Brother Anselm's cell, having seen with my own eyes the survey party on the south bank taking new measurements along the old channel. They are not measuring for flood control. I have compared their stakes to my plates. They are moving the border, and the atlas I have spent eleven years drawing will be used to bless the theft unless the true line is published first."*

She looked up. The hall had gone absolutely quiet in the way rooms go quiet when several hundred people stop breathing at once.

*"There is a man in this guild who knows. He has known since the autumn. He sold me the fiction of my own safety while selling my maps to the survey office. I will not write his name here, because names can be burned and pages survive; I will say only that he sits high, that he signs his approvals in green ink, and that when I told him what I had found, he looked at me the way a man looks at a ledger entry that has begun to cost him money."*

Green ink. Ada watched Voss's right hand — resting on the rail, dignified, motionless — and watched the fingers curl inward, slowly, one knuckle at a time, like a fern closing at dusk. In the front row, the customs house clerk — the lonely one, the one Tomas had charmed with talk of his mother's pickles — lifted his head with the expression of a man remembering something he had filed away without knowing why.

*"If I die before this is done, it will be called an accident, because accidents are cheap. Do not believe the river killed me. The river is the only honest witness left in this valley, and I commend myself to it gladly. What I cannot forgive is being commended to it by another hand."*

Ada lowered the page.

"Henrik Iversen wrote these words nine weeks after the date this tribunal says he drowned," she said. "I did not forge them. I could not forge them. No forger alive could forge the *soundings* in them — the channel depths off the monastery point, measured to the finger-width, which match the master's private notebooks in the guild vault, which none of us may open without three signatures." She turned to Frau Hessler. "Open them. Today. Compare the numbers. That is all I ask. That is the whole of my defense: *compare the numbers.* We are cartographers. It is the one thing we know how to do."

* * *

For a moment, nothing. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and gave up.

Then Voss rose, and Ada saw him become magnificent, because he was magnificent, she had never denied it — he was the best speaker the guild had produced in forty years, and a drowning man will climb even a mast that is sinking with him.

"Ladies and gentlemen of the guild," he said, sorrowfully, spreading his arms, "what you have heard is a performance. This letter — if it exists — proves nothing but that some clever hand knew the master's habits. Green ink! Half the aldermen sign in green. And as for the vault—" he smiled, pitying, wide, generous— "she asks us to open the vault on the word of a stranger and a girl half-mad with grief. Shall we also open the treasury? Where does it end?"

He turned to her, and his voice dropped into intimacy, into kindness, and that was when Ada understood that he had decided to end it here, now, quickly, before the numbers could be compared.

"Child," he said, "no one doubts you loved him. Come down from there. Give the pages to the tribunal's keeping, go home, rest, and in a month, when the thaw has passed and tempers have cooled, we will speak of your apprenticeship again—"

"The numbers," Ada said.

"—and no harm done to anyone, no scandal, no soldiers in our streets—"

"*Compare the numbers.*"

"—because I promise you, Ada, I promise you as I promised your uncle, that I will protect you from all of this—"

"He didn't want protecting." Her voice cracked now, finally, and she let it crack, because it was true and truth should sound like a voice breaking. "He wanted *witnesses.* That's what an atlas is. It isn't paper. It isn't lines. It's ten thousand witnesses standing in a row saying: *this is where things truly were.* And you have spent eleven years standing in front of them."

She held up the letter.

"Alderman Voss. Before this guild, before this town, before the record — I name you. You knew about the survey. You approved the plates that hide the new line. You signed in green ink. And when Henrik Iversen brought it to you, you sent him to the river."

The words went out across the hall and she felt them land, felt them strike different people differently — outrage here, horror there, and in the back rows, in the faces of the miller's wife and old Marta and the sweep-boys, something else, something slower and heavier: recognition. The look of people who had always half-known that the world was managed this way and were watching, for the first time, someone say it out loud within earshot of the manager.

Voss said, very quietly, "Remove the document from her," and the guards started forward, and that was the moment the hall chose.

Frau Hessler rose from the dais. She was seventy years old if she was a day, and she came down those steps without asking anyone's leave and stood beside Ada on the chalked square, small and straight as a plumb line. Then the customs clerk was on his feet holding a ledger above his head — "I've got entries, Alderman, I've got *entries*, dated November, payments marked 'survey discretion'—" and then old Marta, and then the miller's wife, and then more voices than Ada could count or sort, the hall rising like water coming up in a well, and above it all Voss shouting for order, for procedure, for the charter, invoking the law the way a man invokes a saint whose intercession he no longer believes in.

Guards at the doors. Boots on the stairs. And cutting through everything, from the doorway, a voice Ada knew better than her own heartbeat — Brandt, in his lieutenant's grey, holding a leather satchel over his head like a man surrendering:

"The customs ledgers! All of them! Under seal, before anyone burns another thing!"

* * *

Later — hours later, when Voss had been taken out between four soldiers past a silent, staring town, when Frau Hessler had sealed the vault with wax and her own ring, when the clerk's ledgers lay open on the tribunal table showing the payments in green-inked columns like a confession written in advance — later, Ada stood alone at the front of the emptying hall beneath the amber charter map.

Her legs shook. She noticed it with distant interest, the way one notices weather in a country one has left.

They would convene formally tomorrow. There would be more proceedings, magistrates from the capital, months of it perhaps. She had been expelled by a tribunal that no longer existed and reinstated by one that hadn't yet formed, and neither fact meant much tonight. What mattered was quieter than all of it: that the letter had been read into the record, entered, witnessed, signed by the clerk, dated. Whatever happened to her now, the words belonged to history. They were in the row of ten thousand witnesses, standing shoulder to shoulder, saying: *this is where things truly were.*

She touched her breastbone, where the oilcloth had lain all morning, and found it still warm.

Outside, the bells began again — not the alarm, not the tribunal bell, but the plain evening peal, the ordinary bell that rang every night of her life, and it sounded different now, or she was different hearing it. Down the hill the river muttered against the ice in the dark, restless, early, breaking.

And far up the north road, somewhere beyond the checkpoints and the frozen ferry landing, a man was walking with her atlas in his pack, and she whispered to the dark as if the miles were paper and her voice could travel across them: *Hold on. Hold the line. I kept my half.*

Behind her, in the hall, Frau Hessler struck a taper and lit the lamps over the vault door — the first light in that room in nine weeks — and the flame stood straight and steady, like a witness refusing to sit down.
