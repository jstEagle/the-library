## 19. Voss Unmapped

Brandt had stood at attention through worse mornings than this one — through inspections in freezing rain, through the reading of casualty lists after the border skirmishes of '46 — but he had never stood anywhere so difficult as the back bench of the guild hall while Ada Iversen's voice carried over the assembled town.

She stood where the master cartographers had always stood to present their plates: beneath the great window with its stained-glass compass rose, her hands steady on the lectern even though he could see, from this distance and from years of knowing her hands, that they were not steady at all. The letter she read from was old paper, foxed at the edges, and every word of it landed in the hall like a stone dropped into still water.

*They will ask you what the river is. Tell them it is a line. Lines are drawn by men, and men can be bought.*

Voss sat on the dais with his alderman's chain bright against his black coat, and Brandt watched him the way he had been trained to watch a man across a negotiation table: not for what he said, but for what he could not stop his body saying. And Voss's body said a great deal. His fingers found the chain and worried it. His smile stayed fixed a half-second too long after each of Ada's sentences. When she spoke the name — *Alderman Voss paid for the crossing to be sealed, and Alderman Voss paid for Master Iversen to fall into the river* — the smile did not break, but something behind it did, some load-bearing wall inside the man giving way so quietly that only a soldier who had watched cities surrender would have noticed.

The hall erupted. That was expected. What was not expected was the way Voss rose into the uproar with his arms spread like a shepherd calming a frightened flock, and the way the flock — half of it, anyway — quieted.

"Forgery," Voss said, and his voice was warm as brandy. "Grief makes strange ink. We have all been taken in by a clever girl and a stranger with no name."

*A stranger with no name.* Brandt felt that phrase settle onto his shoulders like a pack strap. He knew Tomas Reval's name now. He knew a great many things he had chosen not to know for three weeks, and the weight of chosen ignorance was the heaviest thing he carried.

He thought of the night before the inquiry, when he had gone to Ada's workshop and offered her one blind eye — *one, Ada, not two; I can lose you on the road but I cannot lose my commission* — and she had looked at him with such terrible kindness and said, *Then keep your eye, Lieutenant. I'm not running. I'm testifying.* He had loved her at nineteen, clumsily, the way boys love, and he had lost her to a posting and his own ambition, and he had told himself for eleven years that the loss was clean. It had not been clean. It had been sitting in him all this time like shrapnel too near an organ to remove, and last night, watching her refuse his mercy because she wanted justice instead, the shrapnel had shifted.

He could not give her two blind eyes. But he had a ledger.

It was in his quarters, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of his trunk, under shirts he had stopped caring about. Three weeks ago, when the seals came down and the customs house began its quiet work of mislabeling cargo, Brandt had done what a good officer does: he had kept records. Every crate passed through the checkpoint marked as church candles or millstones or salt fish, every crate with the same chalk mark on its slats — a small *V*, drawn quickly, the tail of the letter doubled. He had copied the entries by lamplight into a private book, dates, weights, signatures, the names of the clerks who had signed off without opening anything, including one clerk whose loneliness Tomas Reval had played upon so skillfully that the man had wept afterward and signed a confession Brandt had witnessed himself.

It was not proof of murder. He understood that. It was proof of smuggling on a scale that no alderman commits for candle wax, and proof that the sealed crossings — the seals Brandt himself had enforced, the orders that had frozen the river town like a held breath — had been ordered by Voss and paid for out of funds that moved through customs accounts like water through a broken pipe.

Proof enough to hang a man's reputation. Perhaps, if the tribunal pressed, proof enough to hang more than that.

On the dais, Voss called for order, and got it, because men like Voss always got order eventually. "The guild will deliberate," he announced. "Frau Iversen will be sequestered until—"

"The guild has already deliberated," said old Mistress Kessler from the front row, her voice thin as ice on a puddle but carrying all the way to the rafters. "We deliberated forty years ago, Alderman, when your predecessor tried to buy the coastal survey. Some things do not change in a guild. Only the names."

Brandt slipped out through the side door while the shouting started again. He had a ledger to fetch, and he had perhaps an hour before Voss did what cornered men of property always do: he burned what he could not own.

* * *

Ada heard the boots first.

She was in the workshop, under guard — two soldiers at the door whom Brandt had posted with the instruction *protect her*, an instruction she suspected cost him something to give — when the sound came up the lane: not marching boots, not the even tread of patrol, but running, many feet, and beneath it a smell that did not belong in a river town in late winter.

Smoke. Not hearth smoke. Paper smoke, sweet and acrid, the smell of archives dying.

She was at the door before the guard could stop her, and down the lane she saw it: light flickering orange behind the guild hall's high windows, and men coming out of the building carrying armloads — ledgers, folios, the great rolled surveys from the map room — and feeding them into a bonfire already built in the courtyard, already roaring, as though it had been laid days ago. As though someone had known.

And at the center of it, directing the destruction with the calm of a man overseeing a harvest, stood Alderman Voss, his chain glinting in the firelight, feeding the flames with his own white-gloved hands.

"No," Ada said, and then louder, a word she had never used in her life, aimed at soldiers: "Stop them! Stop—"

But the guards at her door were Voss's arrangements, Voss's men, and they looked at the fire and then at each other and did nothing, because doing nothing was safer than choosing. She pushed past them. She ran. The cold air tore at her throat and the heat hit her face in waves as she reached the edge of the courtyard, close enough to feel the fire pull the air out of her lungs, close enough to see pages curling and going up — survey sheets, decades of measurement, the coastline of the delta as Iversen had walked it in his youth, all of it rising in sparks toward a sky the color of dirty wool.

"You're burning the record," she shouted. "You can burn the record, Voss, but you can't burn what I've read!"

He turned. In the firelight his face was no longer warm. It was the face from the true letter, the face Brother Anselm had described, and Ada understood suddenly that she had never actually seen this man before today; she had only seen the office he wore.

"Child," he said, almost gently, over the roar, "the record is whatever survives the winter. Ask your master how well he survived it."

She lunged for the stack nearest her — actual ledgers now, the guild's enrollment books, names of apprentices going back two centuries — and a soldier caught her arms, not roughly, but firmly, and she fought him and felt her sleeve tear and did not care, because the fire was eating names, it was eating the guild itself, the long memory Mistress Kessler had spoken of, and Voss watched her struggle with an expression she would carry the rest of her life: not triumph. Relief. A man putting down a weight he had carried too long.

That was when the whistles started.

* * *

Brandt came around the corner of the customs house at a dead run with six men behind him and the ledger in his satchel banging against his hip, and the scene resolved itself the way battlefields resolve: fire, crowd, the woman struggling in a soldier's grip, and Voss standing in the heart of it like the axle of a wheel.

"Hold!" he bellowed, in the parade-ground voice that stopped horses. "By authority of the garrison command! Drop everything — everything — and step away from the fire!"

Chaos, briefly. Soldiers pouring into the courtyard. Townspeople arriving with buckets that no one, now, wanted used. Voss turning with his arms spread again, the shepherd once more, beginning, "Lieutenant, this is guild property and guild jurisdiction—"

"Guild jurisdiction ends where arson begins." Brandt pulled the ledger from his satchel and held it up so the crowd — and the growing ring of faces at the courtyard gate — could see it. "Customs ledger, kept by my own hand since the day the crossings were sealed. Every shipment passed under seal with your chalk mark on it, Alderman. Every entry. Every clerk's signature. Including a sworn statement from Clerk Hennig, witnessed, dated, describing who instructed him not to open certain crates." He let the pause stretch. "Crates that went to your warehouse. Crates that arrived the week Master Iversen died."

The courtyard went very quiet, the kind of quiet that has a fire burning in the middle of it. Ada had stopped struggling. Her eyes found Brandt's over the shoulder of the soldier holding her, and what was in them was not forgiveness — he knew better than to expect that yet — but recognition. *So you chose,* her eyes said. *At last.*

Voss's composure held for three more seconds. Brandt counted them. Then the alderman's gaze flicked — just once, just slightly — toward the alley behind the guild hall, toward the stable where, Brandt would learn within the hour, a horse waited saddled and a strongbox waited packed, and that flicker told every trained eye in the courtyard everything it needed to know.

"Seize him," Brandt said quietly, and the quiet made it worse than any shout.

Two soldiers took Voss's arms. He did not resist, exactly. He straightened his coat, adjusted his chain as though it were being photographed for posterity, and said, "There are men in the capital whose names would make yours forget themselves, Lieutenant," and Brandt answered, "Then they can write to me," and the crowd — the crowd that had whispered for weeks, that had crossed itself at the mention of the drowned master, that had watched Ada expelled and branded — the crowd made a sound. Not a cheer. Something older than cheering. A low collective exhale, like a town setting down a stone it had carried so long it had forgotten the weight.

They dragged the bonfire apart. Too late for most of it; the snow around the pyre was black with ash and floating flakes of burnt vellum, and Ada knelt in it, picking up fragments with bare fingers, trying to read charred edges. Brandt crouched beside her.

"Some of it's in the archive copies," he said. "Brother Anselm keeps duplicates. Iversen arranged it years ago — he told me once a map isn't finished until it exists twice."

She looked up at him, ash on her cheek, her torn sleeve hanging. "You knew that? You knew about Anselm's copies?"

"I knew a lot of things," Brandt said. "I've decided to stop pretending otherwise. It's a recent decision."

Behind them, soldiers were marching Voss toward the customs house cells, past the very checkpoints he had bought and sealed, and the townspeople lined the route in silence, and somewhere in that silence Ada heard, impossibly, the river — a deep groan from the direction of the landing, the ice speaking in its sleep, the thaw moving northward up the valley day by day. The clock that had been audible in the nights was audible now in broad daylight, and it struck her with sudden force:

Tomas was on the far shore. Or he was dead on it. Either way, the ice that had carried him and the atlas was groaning, and she had spent her testimony, and the man who had betrayed everything stood seized ten feet from her, and none of it — not one piece of it — told her whether the sledge had made the crossing, whether the ferryman had lived, whether the pages were safe or at the bottom of the channel or scattered across the floes.

She stood up. Ash drifted from her skirt. She put her hand briefly on Brandt's arm — a touch that said *we are not finished, you and I, but not now* — and turned toward the river.

From the far bank, faint across the grey water, came the sound of a bell. The monastery bell, ringing changes — not the alarm, not the tolling for a death, but the pattern Brother Anselm rang for good news, the pattern Iversen had joked was the only music the Church ever stole from the mapmakers.

Ada closed her eyes and listened, and let herself believe it meant the far shore.
