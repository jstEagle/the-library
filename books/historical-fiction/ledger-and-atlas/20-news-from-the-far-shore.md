## 20. News from the Far Shore

The river ran free on a Thursday.

Ada Iversen heard it before she saw it — a long exhalation from the valley, as though the land itself had been holding its breath since midwinter and had finally let it go. She was at her bench with a burnisher in hand when the sound came up the hill and through the shutters of the workshop, low and grinding and then suddenly liquid, and she set down her tools and went to the window because there was nothing else in the world to do.

Below, between the black shoulders of the ice that still clung to the banks, the water moved. It moved the way it had always moved, brown-green and muscular, carrying sticks and slush and one overturned skiff past the ferry landing where the pilings stood naked for the first time in four months. The garrison's tents were gone. The soldiers had marched east two days after the tribunal, called away by orders no one explained and everyone understood. The town had watched them go with that particular silence towns keep when they are ashamed of something they will never say aloud.

Ada pressed her palm flat against the cold pane.

*He crossed,* she told herself, as she had told herself every morning for six weeks. *The ice held. The far shore took him.* But telling herself was not knowing, and knowing was not seeing, and the distance between those three things had become the geography she lived in now — a country with no atlas, unmapped, uninked, its borders redrawn nightly by rumor.

She turned back to the bench. Work was the only prayer she trusted.

* * *

The workshop had changed, or perhaps she had changed inside it, so that it seemed a different room. Master Iversen's chair still sat by the north window, but she no longer flinched at it. His magnifying loupe hung on its hook above the ink shelf, and some mornings she wore it at her eye for an hour at a stretch until the world swam into his kind of focus: grain and fiber and the honest architecture of things. She had cleaned his brushes. She had re-inked his labels in her own hand, then stopped, then scraped them clean again, unable to decide whose name belonged on jars of Prussian blue.

The guild had not come for the atlas. That was the strangest part, the part she turned over at night like a stone found in a field. After the tribunal — after Voss was taken on the guildhouse steps with singed paper still clinging to his cuffs like gray moths, after Brandt laid the customs ledger on the table and said, in a voice stripped of everything personal, *Enter it into the record* — the town had expected confiscation. She had expected it herself. She had slept three nights running with the remaining letters sewn into the lining of her coat, ready to run badly and be caught quickly.

But no one came. An empire embarrassed by its own border is an empire that prefers its embarrassments quiet, Brother Anselm had written to her from the monastery, in his careful, cramped script. They will let the truth stand only if standing is cheaper than burying it. Do not mistake this for mercy.

She did not mistake it. But she used it. In the weeks after the river broke, she had worked fourteen hours a day restoring what the inquiry had handled: plates bent at the corners, vellum foxed by damp, one coastal chart torn along the estuary where a clerk's seal had been applied and peeled away twice. Her hands knew the work even when her mind wandered, and her mind wandered always in the same direction — east, across the water, to whatever lay on the far shore. A printing house. A man with mud-colored eyes who argued with her about projection methods and kissed her once on the frozen landing with the garrison watching, and had not written a single word since.

Couriers were unreliable in spring; she knew this; roads washed out, bridges groaned under meltwater, every post rider in the province was backed up behind a hundred flooded crossings. Knowing did not help. Each morning she walked down to the landing before the shops opened, ostensibly to check the ice-scour marks on the pilings for a survey plate she was drafting, actually to look at the ferryman's boat, which someone had righted and bailed and tied off, and which no one yet dared to row.

Old Marta had died on the crossing. The town had buried him in the churchyard on the slope, beneath a stone Alderman Procházka — the new alderman, the old one being occupied elsewhere — had insisted upon, though the inscription had been fought over line by line. *FERRYMAN AND FRIEND OF THE GUILD* was what survived. Iversen would have hated the word *friend*, Ada thought. He would have wanted *colleague*. He would have drafted a petition about it, with marginalia.

She smiled at that, alone on the landing in the early light, and the smile hurt her face because it was the first in days.

* * *

The package came on a Tuesday, with the afternoon mail sledge, carried up from the landing by the baker's boy, who took the stairs two at a time and arrived at the workshop door breathless and important.

"From the capital," he announced, as though the capital itself had sent its regards. "There's a fee. Postage owing."

Ada paid him double and did not notice she had done it.

The parcel was wrapped in oilcloth, bound with tarred twine, and heavy — heavier than paper had any right to be. Her fingers went still on the knots. Somewhere below her ribs, her heart began a slow, deliberate work, like a press screw tightening. She had imagined this moment so many times, in so many forms, that now that it stood in front of her she felt strangely outside it, watching her own hands shake as if they belonged to an apprentice she was obliged to correct.

The twine parted. The oilcloth fell open.

The atlas pages.

All of them. Wrapped inside a second layer of linen, dry despite the journey, ordered exactly as Tomas had taken them that dawn six weeks ago — she could tell because she had packed them herself, kneeling on the floor of the monastery guest cell while Brother Anselm kept watch at the window, folding each sheet between boards of seasoned birch with her heart hammering so loudly she had been certain the whole archive could hear it. The birch boards were here too, scarred now, water-stained along one edge where the spray of the crossing had reached them. One corner bore a dark crescent she did not want to examine too closely and could not stop examining, because she knew whose blood it might be and whose it might not, and the not-knowing was a splinter she carried everywhere.

Beneath the pages, a note. Two notes, in fact — one folded open, one sealed. The open one was in a clerk's hand, formal and faintly smug:

*To the bearer: The undersigned firm has examined the materials submitted and confirms its intention to print the same complete and without alteration, per agreement dated the 9th of April. Corrections to follow at proof stage. Payment arrangements enclosed. — Hessler & Vogt, Printers to the University, Capital District.*

*Without alteration.* She read the phrase four times. Five. She made herself read it slowly, the way she would check a bearing twice before committing a coastline to ink, because hope was a cartography of its own and she had been fooled by wishful surveys before. But the phrase did not change. *Complete and without alteration.* The true border — Iversen's hidden border, the one three governments had killed a man to keep off the maps — would be set in type and pulled on presses and distributed to universities and ministries and schoolrooms where children would trace its line with their fingers and never know what it had cost.

Her knees found the bench stool without her permission. She sat down hard among her own tools and held the clerk's letter against her chest and, for a while, allowed herself to weep the way she had not wept at the funeral, nor at the tribunal, nor on any of the forty-two mornings she had walked down to the landing. This was not grief. It was something rarer and more disorienting — relief arriving late, after the body had already learned to live without it, so that it landed like weather from an uncharted quarter.

* * *

When she could see again, she opened the sealed note.

His hand. She knew it before the wax was fully broken — the slightly backward lean of his letters, the way his *r*s hooked like small question marks. She had spent six weeks studying handwriting; she could read a man's soul in his pen lifts now, and Tomas Reval wrote the way he spoke, leaning toward you even on paper.

*Ada —*

*Forgive the silence. The proofs took longer than promised, and then the road from the capital was a swamp to the horizon, and I have learned that a courier who drowns in April delivers nothing in May. I am well. The shoulder healed crooked but it heals, and Hessler says a crooked shoulder is no disqualification for a proofreader.*

*They are printing it. All of it. I sat in their office while a man with grey whiskers measured the border line with dividers and pronounced it "remarkably fine work" and I could not tell him whose work it was because Iversen's name is not yet safe in the capital either — but it will be. Vogt wants to publish under both your names. I argued for it. I won. You will find the argument reproduced nowhere in this letter because I cannot remember what I said; I only remember that I meant it.*

*The pages are yours to do with as the guild requires. Send them back if you must; I copied everything before I surrendered them, in my own hand, and Hessler has the copies, so the truth now exists in two handwritings and three cities and can be drowned in none of them.*

*I am not writing the rest of what I want to write because the rest should be said at your door and not at your mailbox, and because I intend to say it. The river runs both directions. Give me until the roads dry.*

*— T.R.*

P.S. *The ferryman's family has been found. There is a sister in Vrbov. I have begun a debt ledger in his name. It is the least I can do and I intend it to be much more than the least.*

Ada read the letter twice, then a third time aloud, quietly, just to hear the words in a voice — hers, his, it hardly mattered. Then she set it down on the bench beside the atlas pages and looked at the two of them together, paper and promise, evidence and vow, and understood that she was holding, in one armful, everything she had been fighting for since the night a mud-spattered stranger had knocked at her ink door with letters from a dead man.

The absence arrived on schedule. Relief always dragged its shadow.

Because the pages proved he had crossed, yes — but they also proved he had let them go. He had handed the atlas to strangers in a printing office and stayed behind to argue for her name, and then he had written *give me until the roads dry*, which was not a date, and she was a cartographer, and she knew better than anyone what a thing without a fixed coordinate could do to a person waiting for it. Roads dried differently every year. Some years, in the high passes, they did not dry at all.

She got up and lit the lamp, though it was barely dusk, and cleared the bench with sweeps of her forearm — blotter, burnisher, the cold cup of tea she had abandoned at noon. She laid the atlas pages out in sequence across the whole length of the room, weighted at the corners with the brass objects of her trade, until Master Iversen's life's work stretched from door to window like a shoreline finally, fully seen.

Then she stood over it and read it as an enemy would read it — as Voss would have read it, as the ministers in their capitals read everything — hunting for flaws, for gaps, for the single soft place where the whole coast might crumble.

She found none. Of course she found none. She had checked it forty times.

But she found something else instead, near the eastern margin of the seventh plate, in the narrow band of blank vellum where a cartographer signs and dates his work: a faint indentation, invisible to ink, legible only to touch and raking light. She tilted the lamp. Shadows pooled in the grooves.

Letters. Scratched dry-point into the surface, then effaced — but not deeply enough, not by a man in a hurry with soldiers at the ice edge.

She fetched Iversen's loupe and knelt and brought the lamp low, and the words rose out of the vellum like drowned things surfacing:

*For Ada, who will finish what I could not, and forgive what I could not explain. The truth is a country. Map it anyway. — E.I.*

She did not move for a long time. Outside, the free river ran on in the dark, talking to itself, carrying spring down toward the sea.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when the knock came — soft, doubled, the knock of someone who had once lived behind doors like these and remembered how sound carries in a workshop at night. Ada's head came up from the seventh plate, where she had been copying the dry-point inscription onto a slip of tissue, preserving it in her own hand the way one preserves a fossil in plaster.

She crossed the room. She put her eye to the shutter seam.

A figure stood in the yard, hatless, travel-worn, one shoulder sitting slightly higher than the other, healed crooked. Mud to the knees. Mud, in fact, everywhere mud could reasonably be and several places it could not, as if the roads had dried just enough to become impassable in a new way.

Her hands were on the latch before her mind finished the argument it had been preparing all evening about dignity and composure and the proper interval a woman ought to observe.

Tomas Reval stepped back into the lamplight spilling through the open door, and up close he was thinner and browner and smiling the way a man smiles when he has carried something a very long way and is, at last, allowed to set it down.

"The roads dried," he said.
