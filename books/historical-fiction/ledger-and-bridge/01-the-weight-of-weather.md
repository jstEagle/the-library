## 1. The Weight of Weather

The storm arrived the way bad news does — first a rumor on the wind, then a certainty, then a great deal of noise about it.

I felt it coming in my knees before I saw it in the sky, which is the sort of thing I'd have said aloud to Tomas and he'd have laughed and said my bones were older than I was. Now the joke lives only on my side of the conversation, which is where most things live these days.

By noon the barometer had fallen like a man off a cliff, and by two o'clock the sea at Gwennol Point had gone from grey to that particular bruised color that means business. I did what I always did when the weather turned ugly: I checked the oil stores, wound the clockwork rotation to its full tension, cleaned the lens until it could have shamed a bishop's spectacles, and put the kettle on. A keeper who panics drowns. A keeper who makes tea survives long enough to panic later, privately, with something warm in hand.

The light at Gwennol Point is not a grand one. It stands forty feet above the water on a finger of black rock that juts westward from the coast as if the land itself were pointing out to sea and saying *mind that*. Ships mind it. Most of them. The ones that don't are why the Trinity House men pay me eleven pounds a year and pretend that's generous.

They pay Tomas's name for it, technically. The correspondence says *T. Lockridge* in a clerk's careful hand, and I sign my replies *E. Lockridge, per T. Lockridge*, which is a small fiction I maintain the way one maintains a garden wall — not because anyone is looking, but because if you let one stone go, the rest follow. Nobody questions a widow keeping her dead husband's post alive. It's practically patriotic. Grief is a fine disguise; it explains everything and invites no further questions.

*How do you manage alone?* people ask, when people ask anything at all, which is rarely.

*I manage,* I say, and let them fill in the rest. They imagine mending and prayer and the kindness of neighbors. What actually fills the space is routine so tight you could bounce a coin off it. Wind the mechanism every four hours. Trim the wick. Polish the brass. Log the weather, the passing vessels, the oil consumed. Write to Penzance quarterly for supplies. Answer no question fully.

That last one isn't written down anywhere official, but it governs more of my day than the lamp does.

* * *

The storm hit properly at dusk, arriving out of the southwest with all the subtlety of a debt collector. By full dark the tower was leaning into the wind like a drunk holding a lamppost, and the spray was going over the lantern room entirely, salt cracking against the glass in fists. I had been up and down those ninety-three steps so many times I'd stopped counting, carrying oil, carrying coal, carrying myself.

There is a particular loneliness in tending a light during a storm that I have never managed to explain to anyone who hasn't done it. You are desperately needed and completely alone in the same breath. Every ship out there might be depending on your little flame, and not one of them knows your name, or would care if they did. You are essential and invisible simultaneously, and after four years of it, I've come to suspect that's exactly how I arranged my life, though I couldn't tell you when I made the decision. Some walls get built brick by brick while you're looking elsewhere.

At half past ten I sat down in the watch room to log the hour. The logbook wants entries in a fair hand: wind direction, force, visibility, vessels sighted. I gave it its due. Then, as I always do, I wrote the entry under initials rather than names — *E.L.* — because the form asks for the keeper's initials and I have learned that initials commit you to very little. An initial is a curtain drawn halfway. You can still see the shape of someone behind it, but not their face, and certainly not their history.

Tomas used to sign his whole self across the page, big loops and flourishes, *Emrys Lockridge*, taking up half the line as though the page owed him room. He never met a question he didn't answer with interest. Ask him the time and you'd get the time, the phase of the moon, an anecdote about his uncle, and an opinion on the government. I loved that about him and it terrified me in equal measure, because I am a woman built of omissions, and living with a man who was all additions taught me just how much of myself I could hide inside his noise.

He'd been dead fourteen months now. Drowned, officially. The sea took him off the point in March of '17, and the sea has never been asked a single question about it, which suits everyone including the sea.

I keep his coat on the peg by the door. Not sentiment — practicality. It holds the station keys better than any of mine do, the pockets worn to the exact shape of what they carry. That's the thing about a dead husband nobody tells you: he leaves behind a whole architecture of habits, and you either knock it down or move in. I moved in. I sleep in the lee side of the bed. I read the paper back to front, starting with shipping, because he did. I catch myself answering questions in his cadence sometimes — cheerful, expansive — and then correcting course mid-sentence toward something smaller and safer.

Tonight, with the wind screaming around the gallery rail like a soul with grievances, I talked to him. I always do, in weather.

"You'd have loved this one," I told the logbook, because the coat was downstairs and I was not. "Sou'wester, force ten if it's a breeze, and the Merrow out of Falmouth went past at nine showing more nerve than ballast." The logbook declined to reply. Fourteen months of that, and I've grown quite fond of the silence, the way you grow fond of a limp — proof of what happened, carried everywhere.

The truth about grief, the part they don't put on the sympathy cards, is that it's mostly administration. You grieve in the gaps between tasks. There's a wick to trim, and you grieve while your hands are busy. There's brass to polish, and your hands are busy again. It's only when the work runs out — three in the morning, say, storm-bound, nothing left to fill or wind or wipe — that the grief gets the whole floor to itself and takes up all the room.

Three in the morning came, as it does. The lamp turned overhead with its patient mechanical heartbeat, the lens throwing its beam out over the water in long white arms, and I sat in the watch room with cold tea and let it have the floor.

I thought about the register. I'd been thinking about it all evening, the way you worry a loose tooth.

Every station keeps a register — household particulars, staff of the light, next of kin, that sort of thing. Ours hadn't been touched since Tomas filled it out, in his enormous handwriting, answering everything fully because he didn't know any other way. And somewhere in it, in a margin or between lines, there are traces of me that predate my widowhood, traces of a woman who existed before I became E. Lockridge, initials only, keeper's wife and then keeper's widow, a person assembled entirely from other people's paperwork.

A census comes next year. I know this because the papers say so, and because Mrs. Pendarvis at Porth Aman mentioned it in her last letter, adding that she supposed I'd be wanting to be counted proper this time, being a widow and head of my own house now. She meant kindly. They always mean kindly. Kindness is how most traps are baited.

What will I write? *Widow. Keeper. Age:* — well. *Born where:* — ah.

You see the difficulty. My life is a ledger with certain columns ruled out, and I have spent twenty years making sure no clerk ever asked me to total the figures. Give a full answer once, and the answers chain together like boats in a tow. Where you were born leads to whose daughter you were leads to whose sister you are leads to — no. No further. I answer no question fully. Half an answer is a mooring; a whole answer is a confession.

The wind shifted around four, backing northwest, which the old pilots say is the storm turning over in its sleep. I climbed to the lantern room one more time, checked the mechanism's tension, wiped the salt haze from the seaward pane, and stood a moment watching the beam swing out and sweep back, out and back, patient as forgiveness.

From up there the world is reduced to essentials: water, rock, light, dark. Nothing asks you anything. The lamp doesn't care what name you keep. The sea, which knows everything about everybody, tells no tales — it just takes them, or doesn't, and moves on. Sometimes I think the only honest relationship I have left is with the weather. It demands everything and expects nothing back but competence.

"Another night kept," I said to the glass, to the dark, to Tomas, to whoever collects such reports. The beam swung out. Somewhere beyond it, ships were threading their way through the black with nothing but my small stubborn flame between them and the rocks, and none of them knew it, and that was fine. That was the arrangement. Essential and invisible. I'd chosen it. I reminded myself I'd chosen it.

* * *

Dawn came up the colour of dishwater, the storm hauling off eastward and leaving the point strewn with wrack and one bewildered gull. I logged the clearing weather, tallied the oil — eleven gallons burned, a dear night — and went down to survey the damage.

The store room door had swollen shut with damp, which meant the crowbar, which meant an hour of swearing I won't reproduce here. But it needed opening regardless, because the storm had cost us oil and I wanted to count what remained against the spring order. Tomas kept the spare tins racked along the back wall, behind the shelving, in the dry corner. I pulled the rack out to check for leaks, the way he'd taught me — *check behind, Emrys, the front row lies* — and the shelf bracket came away from the wall with a crack of rotten wood.

Behind it, set into the stone in a gap I had never known existed in four years of living in this building, was a cavity. And in the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with tarred string, a book.

It was about the size of a psalter, heavier than it looked, corners worn soft. I knelt there on the cold flags with the crowbar still in my hand and the gull screaming somewhere overhead, and I knew — before I untied the string, before I peeled back a single fold of cloth — that whatever it was, Tomas had hidden it from me. From me, who shared his bed, his table, his name. He'd answered every question anyone ever asked him with his whole heart, and the one thing that mattered, he'd buried in a wall.

My hands were steady. That surprised me. I set down the crowbar, picked up the book, and weighed it — a good weight, a ledger's weight, pages dense with use.

The wind had died to a murmur. The kettle would want putting on. Any sensible woman would count her oil and leave the wall to its secrets.

But I had been answering questions halfway for twenty years, and here, dropped into my lap by a rotting bracket and a storm, was a question that demanded the whole answer.

I untied the string.
