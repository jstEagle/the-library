## 2. One Question Too Many

The supply boat came on a Thursday, which was unusual, because the supply boat always came on a Tuesday and the sea had never once been consulted on the matter. I saw her first as a smudge against the grey — a small trawler nosing through the swell with the particular stubbornness of vessels that have been told where to go by people who are not on them.

I went down to the landing stage with my coat buttoned wrong and didn't bother correcting it. There is no one at Gwennol Point to notice whether a widow dresses herself properly, which is either freedom or neglect depending entirely on the hour.

"Morning," called the boatman, a man named Cadoc whose face had achieved the texture of a rope left out in all weathers. "Got your oil, got your flour, got a passenger."

"We don't take passengers."

"She's not for taking. She's for delivering. Government business." He said *government* the way other men said *weather* — as an affliction one endured rather than discussed.

The passenger stepped down onto the wet stones with more confidence than the stones deserved. She was perhaps thirty, wearing a good wool coat that had been chosen by someone with an eye for coats and then worn by someone with no eye at all for weather. Her boots were wrong for this coast. Her hat was worse. She carried a leather satchel the way a vicar carries a Bible — close, and with the assumption that its contents made her welcome.

"Mrs. Lockridge?" she said.

"There's no Mrs. Lockridge."

She looked at me. I looked back. Somewhere behind us a gull made a comment it thought was funny.

"I'm sorry — you are the keeper of Gwennol Point?"

"I am the keeper."

"And your name?"

"Lockridge." I let that sit. "The light answers to me. Names are largely decorative here."

Her mouth did something small and complicated — the beginning of a smile, suppressed on principle. "Bronwen Hale," she said. "Census office. I'm completing the returns for the coastal stations. It should take no more than an hour."

An hour. I had planned my day around oil and wick-trimming and the slow, satisfying work of de-rusting the ventilator cap. An hour of questions was not on any list I kept.

"The census was taken in June," I said. "I answered it. A man came. He wrote things down. He had a pencil he sharpened with a knife, which struck me as optimistic."

"Yes, ma'am. And there were — irregularities." She said it lightly, the way you'd say *there's a bit of wind*, but I felt it land somewhere under my ribs anyway. "Stations like yours get missed or muddled. The war, you understand. Half the clerks are in France and the other half are doing three jobs badly. My job is to un-muddle them."

"And that requires coming out to a rock in November?"

"That requires going wherever the paperwork disagrees with itself." She smiled properly now, and it was a good smile, warm and slightly crooked, the sort that gets people telling you things they meant to keep. I resolved immediately to tell her nothing whatsoever.

"Cadoc can bring you back on the tide," I said. "There's tea if you want it while you wait. There's nothing else."

* * *

I want it recorded that I gave her the good chair. The one near the stove, not the one with the broken slat that announces every shift of weight like a guilty conscience. She sat in it and unpacked her satchel with the neat, unhurried movements of someone who has done this two hundred times and still finds it interesting.

My kitchen at Gwennol Point is not a room designed for company. It contains a table, two chairs, a stove Tomas installed himself and which I have kept alive through sheer spite, and a wall of hooks from which hang everything I own that isn't currently on my body. Visitors always look at the hooks. They see a life pared down to its hooks and they draw conclusions, usually pitying ones. They are not wrong to pity. They are simply wrong about what deserves it.

"Now," Miss Hale said, producing a ledger of her own — a fat government thing with a canvas spine. "Gwennol Point, station register, cross-referenced with Trinity House appointments and the June return." She ran a finger down a column. "Tomas Lockridge appointed keeper, 1896. Died — I'm very sorry — March of this year. Successor recorded as E. Lockridge, keeper."

"Yes."

"You'll forgive me, but the register gives no first name for the successor. Just the initial. And here—" she turned the page toward me, gently, as if turning pages roughly might break something, "—here there's been an alteration. Someone has scratched out letters. You can feel the grooves."

I looked at the page because refusing to look would have been worse. There it was in the lamplight: my own hand, four years ago, gouging out the letters that had spelled a name I'd stopped using before I ever signed this book. Under the scratches, if you knew what to look for — and apparently this woman knew what to look for — the ghost of ink still read like a confession.

"Paper was short during the war," I said. "We don't waste pages out here."

"Mm," she said, in a tone that agreed completely and believed none of it. "And the June census. The enumerator recorded: one male keeper, deceased; one female keeper, present; household, one." She looked up. "No wife listed. No next of kin. In fact, ma'am, you're recorded almost nowhere at all except where the law insists on it. That's unusual. Most people leave traces everywhere. Marriage lines, children's names, mothers' maiden names. You've left — well. Scratches."

"The sea takes what it likes," I said. "So do I."

Another smile, briefer this time. She made a note. I watched her write and found myself absurdly interested in the shape of her handwriting — quick, slanted, confident, the writing of a person who trusts paper more than people. Dangerous trait, in my experience. Paper keeps things. Paper is how they find you.

"Tea?" I said, mostly to make her stop writing.

"Thank you, yes."

While the kettle worked I stood at the window with my back to her, which was cowardice dressed as hospitality. Below, the swell came in long and green-grey, and the light tower threw its shadow across the yard in the way it does at midday, a black finger pointing inland, toward everything I had spent eleven years not looking at.

"May I ask about the Lockridge before you?" she said to my shoulder blade.

The kettle chose that moment to begin its noise, which I was grateful for, because it bought me three full seconds in which to decide who I was going to be.

"The Lockridge before me," I repeated.

"Tomas Lockridge. Your predecessor. He was keeper here nearly twenty years, wasn't he? That's a long time on one rock." A pause, and then, casually, so casually it had to be deliberate: "People say he was quite a character. Down the coast, in Porth Aman, the older fishermen still talk about him. Boats arriving at odd hours. Odd cargo. One man swore Tomas once landed at midnight with a hold full of — well, he couldn't say what, only that it wasn't fish."

The kettle screamed. I took it off the heat with hands that belonged to someone calmer than I was.

"Fishermen," I said, pouring, "will swear to anything after the fourth pint. Tomas kept the light. He kept it well. Whatever else he was, the ships came home, and in wartime that is the whole of the moral ledger as far as I'm concerned."

"The moral ledger," she echoed softly. "That's a nice phrase."

"It's mine. You can't have it."

She laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her — and for one treacherous moment I liked her. This is the trouble with solitude: it makes you soft. Eleven years alone and along comes a woman with a crooked smile and a government satchel and some animal part of me sat up like a dog hearing its name, wanting to be talked to, wanting to answer questions fully for the first time since the funeral.

I put the tea in front of her and sat down opposite, and I made my mistake.

Because she asked — lightly, filling space, the way people do when they think they already know the answer — "Was he your father, then? Tomas? Or an uncle? The register doesn't say, and family connections matter for pension purposes—"

"My husband."

It was out before I could catch it. Three words, plain as a gull dropping a stone. *My husband.*

Miss Hale's pen stopped moving.

I heard the silence arrive, and I understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, exactly what I had just done. Because the June census said one keeper, no kin. Because the marriage registers of Porth Aman contained no Lockridge wedding. Because the woman named E. Lockridge had appeared at this station out of nowhere in 1914 with a dead man's surname and a story nobody had ever thought to check — until now, until a woman with a crooked smile and too much curiosity sat drinking my tea and writing down my scratches.

"A widow keeping her husband's post," Miss Hale said slowly. "That's — actually rather remarkable. They don't usually confirm women as principal keepers. Not formally."

"They needed a keeper and I was here."

"Were you here long before he died? As his wife?" Her eyes had gone bright in a way I recognized and hated: the brightness of a puzzle resolving. "Only I'd have expected — records, ma'am. Banns, or a certificate, something in the parish books. I do look at those. It's half my work, matching people to their paper."

"Parish books burn," I said. "Churches burn. So do chapels. There was a fire."

"In which parish?"

"Near enough."

She wrote something. I watched the pen move and felt each stroke like a stitch going into me. Then she closed her ledger, squared it on her knees, and looked up with that warm crooked smile again, and somehow the smile was the worst part, because it said: *you've given me something, Mrs. Whoever-You-Are, and you know it, and now we both have to live with it.*

"Well," she said. "Thank you for the tea. And for the — clarifications." She stood, buckled the satchel. "I'll be finishing the northern circuit this week. Porth Aman, Nant Fawr, up past the quarry villages. If I find anything else that needs clarifying, may I come back?"

The honest answer was no. The safe answer was no. What I heard myself say, because the dog in me was still sitting up hopeful and because a lie told flatly invites suspicion while a door left open invites complacency:

"The light's always burning. Anyone the sea brings is welcome to dry off by it."

She paused in the doorway with the wind pulling at her terrible hat, and she looked at me for a moment longer than courtesy required — a long, level, appraising look, the look of a woman filing something away in a place sturdier than a ledger.

"I believe you," she said. "That's the strange thing. I believe every word you say, Mrs. Lockridge. It's the words you don't say that I've come for."

Then she was gone, down to the boat, out onto the grey water, and I stood in my kitchen among my hooks and my two chairs and listened to the engine fade.

When she was well past the point I went to the cupboard where I keep the lamp oil — the real cupboard, not the one visitors see — and knelt down in front of it with my heart going hard.

Because Bronwen Hale was wrong about one thing. She thought she'd caught a widow in a small dishonesty, a scratched-out name, a missing line in a parish book. She thought the worst secret on this rock was a woman pretending to be somebody else.

She had no idea that Tomas — quiet, decent, light-keeping Tomas, the man whose name I wore like borrowed clothing — had left more than a light behind him. She had no idea what else he'd hidden in this station, and neither, until that moment kneeling at the cupboard with her questions still ringing in my ears, had I.

But I suddenly, terribly, needed to know.
