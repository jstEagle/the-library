## 3. What Tomas Left

The storm took three days to die and left behind a silence so complete I could hear the lamp's own heartbeat — that faint tick of cooling brass, like something in the tower had opinions it was finally free to share.

I spent the fourth morning doing what I did after every great blow: walking the gallery rail with a rag, counting cracked panes, apologizing to no one for the salt crust on everything I owned. The light itself had behaved beautifully, which was more than I could say for the sea, or for me, or for the lie I'd told Bronwen Hale that now sat in my chest like a swallowed fishhook.

*No Lockridge before you.* I could still hear my own voice saying it, smooth as oiled glass. Fourteen years of practice at answering questions halfway, and the one time it mattered, I'd answered them wrong.

But regret is a poor fuel. Lamp oil was the fuel that mattered, and we were low.

This is the unglamorous truth of keeping a light: half the work is fire and weather, and the other half is inventory. You count what you have, you write down what you need, and once a fortnight you row or flag or beg your way into a supply boat that brings coal, oil, bread, and news, in roughly descending order of usefulness. The oil came in forty-gallon drums, and the drums lived in the store room beneath the stairs, which is where I went that morning, lamp in hand, skirts pinned up, feeling every one of my thirty-nine years in my knees.

The store room smelled of paraffin and rope and cold stone. I tipped the last drum. It gave the hollow gurgle of a thing already dead.

"Of course," I said to it. There is a certain dignity in talking to objects; they never pretend they weren't listening.

That meant the reserve tins, and the reserve tins were on the high shelf behind the spare wicks, which meant the ladder, which meant stretching on my toes the way Tomas used to mock me for — *you stretch like a heron, Emrys, all neck and ambition* — and reaching back into the dark where the wall met the shelf, my fingers walking along brick that had been walked by my fingers ten thousand times before.

Except this time they stopped.

There was a seam where there had never been a seam. A hairline crack in the mortar, too straight to be honest, running vertical between two bricks about the width of my palm apart. I'd have sworn before God and the Admiralty both that I knew every inch of this wall. But walls change when nobody watches, and so do men.

I worked the loose brick out with the heel of my hand. Behind it: a cavity, dry, lined with an oilcloth folded over twice. And inside the oilcloth, wrapped in a wool sock that had once belonged to me and had been missing since 1912 —

— a ledger.

Not a keeper's ledger. Not the official register with its columns for wind and consumption and hours burned. This was a school exercise book, cheap paper gone soft at the edges, and on the cover, in my husband's small, patient handwriting: *T.L. Accounts.*

Tomas kept accounts the way other men prayed. Daily. Thoroughly. With a quiet conviction that somewhere a just clerk was taking notes.

I carried it up to the kitchen because my hands wanted warmth and my legs wanted a chair, though I noticed even then that neither of those wants was the real reason. The real reason was that I didn't want to read it standing in a cold hole in the dark like a thief. If I was going to find out what my husband had been hiding behind a brick for however many years, I intended to do it sitting down, with tea, like a widow of some standing.

The kettle took its time. So did I.

* * *

Here is what I must make plain, because it matters to everything that came after: I knew Tomas had secrets. A wife always knows there are secrets; what she doesn't know is their weight. Ours was a marriage built on the pleasant fiction that we were both simple people. He was a lightkeeper from a line of lightkeepers; I was a woman who'd come to Gwennol Point under a name that fit me like borrowed boots, and neither of us ever asked the other to take the boots off.

He tended the lamp. I tended him. The sea did what the sea does. For eleven years it was the steadiest arrangement I have ever known, and I include in that assessment several governments.

But there were nights — and I let myself remember them now, sitting with that exercise book closed on the table like a sleeping animal — nights when the fog came down thick and the light was useless, painting the world white-on-white, and Tomas would stand at the gallery rail staring into nothing, and say things like, *Some nights the best thing this lamp can do is not be seen from the water.* And I, mending a sock or pretending to read, would nod, because I understood not being seen better than most.

And there was the matter of the boat. We kept two boats, officially. On certain black nights, one of them went out and came back lighter in oil and heavier in nothing anyone would write down, and Tomas would come in smelling of salt and someone else's tobacco, and he'd eat his supper and say, *Quiet night,* and I'd say, *Was it,* and that would be the whole of our conversation on the subject for another month.

I told myself he was running contraband. Tobacco, brandy, silk — the ordinary sins of a hard coast. I found the idea almost charming. My solemn, ledger-keeping husband, a smuggler. It made him more human, somehow. Everyone needs a vice; his appeared to be profit and mine was curiosity, and we agreed to starve both.

That was the story I lived in. I want that on the record. Whatever I found under the brick, I found it innocent.

* * *

I opened the book with the tea going cold beside me, because if I waited for the tea I might never open it at all, and a woman who won't open a book has no business calling herself brave enough to keep a light.

The first pages were exactly what the cover promised. Accounts. Dates, sums, initials. *Paid M.R., Porth Aman, 4/6. Paid for boat hire, 12/-. Recd from D.W., 30/-.* A merchant's tidy columns. I turned pages with growing disappointment, nearly — nearly — ready to laugh at myself for the drama of it.

Then the columns changed.

It began, I think, in the spring of 1915, though the dates were coded — he'd shifted to a numbering I only untangled later, days counted from some private zero. The entries grew longer. Less arithmetic, more prose, written fast, the pen pressed hard:

*R. tonight. Young. Sick all the way across. Left him with the quarry man. He cried and called me sir, God help us.*

And below that, in the margin, smaller: *His mother writes. Says thank you. Says he was worth more than the uniform.*

I turned the page.

*A. and her boy. She wouldn't leave without the boy. Two fares but she had nothing, so nothing charged. Told her the sea owes her a favor. I intend to collect on her behalf someday.*

Page after page. Names — not full names, initials and false names and sometimes just descriptions, *the tall one from Cardiff, the Quaker lad, the twins* — and sums, and routes. Porth Aman. Nant Fawr. A quarry village inland whose name he'd shortened to a single letter. Payments to boatmen, to a woman who forged papers and was recorded only as *the Pen*, to farmers who hid men in hay barns until the water was right. Deserters. Resisters. Boys who'd signed up at seventeen out of poetry and discovered the poetry stopped at the trench line. Men who could not, would not, kill.

My husband had been ferrying them across the channel. Not brandy. Boys.

I sat very still. Outside, the recovered sea went about its business, slapping the rocks with the smugness of a thing that has just wrecked something.

Here is the strange part, and I've had years now to examine it: my first feeling was not fear. It was pride. A hot, disgraceful, immediate pride — *that's my husband, that quiet man, look what he was* — and I sat there glowing over a book of crimes like a mother over a school prize. Eleven years of marriage and I'd thought his virtue was steadiness. It had been mercy, wearing steadiness as a coat.

The fear arrived late, the way weather does off this coast. You see the cloud bank and think you've time yet. Then it's on you.

Because these were names. Real names, or near enough. Routes. Sums. A complete anatomy of a smuggling ring that had moved draft resisters past the law of a country at war — and the war was not over. The armistice talk was talk. Courts do not stop for rumors, and tribunals do not pardon the dead, and the dead cannot hang, but the living can, and every living soul in this book was attached to a family, and families have addresses.

I was holding a list of people who could be arrested. And I was the only person alive who knew where it had been hidden, which meant I was also the only person alive who could decide what happened next. That is a kind of power I had spent fourteen years arranging my entire life to avoid.

I read on anyway. Of course I read on. You do not stop reading a thing like that; you simply delay the moment of putting it down forever.

Near the back, the handwriting changed. Looser. Later. And there, in an entry dated — decoded — the autumn of 1917, I found a name that stopped my heart the way a hand stops a clock.

*I.V. came through tonight. Would not give his true name but I know it. Her brother. Told him nothing. He goes inland with the quarry man till the water's kinder. If she ever learns, may she forgive the silence — hers more than mine.*

Idris.

My brother Idris, whom the family had mourned twice over — once when he refused the draft and vanished, and once, officially, when a telegram had said *missing, presumed* — was alive. Had stood in my kitchen, or my boat, close enough to touch. Had been fed by my husband's hands and sent inland to a quarry village, and neither man had told me, each protecting me in the particular insulting way men protect women, as though grief were cargo that couldn't survive handling.

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and I let it, because there was no one to hear and the sea has heard worse.

He was alive. And reachable — the ledger said where, or near enough. *Q.*, the quarry village, the letter appearing again and again down the columns like a heartbeat. Idris was at the end of a trail my dead husband had drawn in his own careful hand.

And so was everyone else.

I looked at the ledger and understood, with the slow cold certainty of the tide coming in around a stranded boat, what I was actually holding. Bronwen Hale had stood in my kitchen asking questions with census forms for camouflage. Papers got requisitioned. Registers got read. If that woman had seen anything — if she reported anything — if anyone traced the supply routes or the sums or the single damning initial —

They wouldn't hang Tomas. They'd hang boatmen. Farmers. A woman who forged papers. And Idris, who had died once already and could not afford to do it properly.

Grief curdles fast when you add fear to it. I sat in my kitchen with my husband's secret in my hands and loved him and was furious at him in the same breath, which is, I'm told, what marriage is.

Outside, far off down the coast road, I heard it — engine noise where no supply boat should be, a motor launch running dark along the headland, slowing.

Someone was coming to Gwennol Point.

I put the ledger in the bread tin, put the bread tin under my arm, and went to see who had come looking for my husband's accounts.
