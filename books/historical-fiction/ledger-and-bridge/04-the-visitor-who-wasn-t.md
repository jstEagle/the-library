## 4. The Visitor Who Wasn't

The boat came on a Tuesday, which I resented on principle. Tuesdays were for cleaning the lens, and a woman who has scrubbed salt crust off forty-eight prisms does not appreciate interruption before the second hour of it.

I saw the launch first as a smudge against the grey, then as a shape with too much purpose in it to be the supply boat. Supply boats wallow. They arrive like apologies. This one cut the water the way a man walks toward you in a room where he knows everyone's name but yours.

I put down the chamois, wiped my hands, and gave myself exactly ten seconds to decide who I was going to be.

This is a thing widows learn that nobody writes into the pamphlets: identity is not something you have, it's something you perform, and the performance improves with rehearsal. For six years I had been Mrs. Tomas Lockridge of Gwennol Point — competent, incurious, slightly deaf in the left ear when questions came from official directions. Six years of practice. I had assumed the role was worn smooth by now, like a stone under a waterfall.

I was about to learn how thin the stone had gotten.

* * *

The officer climbed the rock stairs without asking permission, which told me half of what I needed to know. The other half arrived when he did: tall, naval, thirty-five or so, with the kind of face that had been handsome at twenty and had since been repurposed entirely for work. His greatcoat was regulation and immaculate. His boots were wet and he didn't care.

"Mrs. Lockridge." Not a question. He had my name already, which meant he'd read something, which meant someone had written something down. "Lieutenant Commander Vey. I'm following up on certain irregularities in the census returns from this district."

"Are there irregularities?" I asked, in the tone of a woman who found the word mildly interesting, like a report of weather in a country she'd never visit.

"There are discrepancies." He let his eyes travel past me into the lamp room doorway, cataloguing. "A household register at this station lists one occupant. The census schedule for the same address records two."

"The census girl counted me twice," I said. "Once at the lamp and once at the house. She was thorough." I smiled the smile I kept for inspectors and clergy, the one that offered warmth while conceding nothing. "You've come all this way over arithmetic?"

"I've come all this way because arithmetic in wartime is rarely only arithmetic." He said it pleasantly. That was the worst part. Men who threaten you angrily want something they'll name; men who threaten you pleasantly want something you're supposed to hand over gratefully. "Miss Hale's schedules have been requisitioned for review. Standard procedure where returns touch on station personnel and shipping movements."

Bronwen Hale's papers. Taken. Which meant her casual questions last week hadn't been casual after all, or worse — they had been, and the machinery had ground them up anyway, indifferent to intent. Either way, somewhere in an office in Porth Aman sat a sheet of paper bearing my handwriting, my answers, and the answer I had given without meaning to give. *Widow of the previous keeper. Yes. And before him?*

And before him nothing. Before him I had scratched out initials in the register like a woman erasing herself, which is precisely what I'd done.

"You'll want tea," I said. "It's a long climb back down otherwise."

* * *

I made the tea in the kitchen of the keeper's cottage, and while the kettle took its time — kettles are loyal that way — I performed the small domestic theater of a dull widow's morning. I moved the bread tin as though it mattered. I remarked on the storm of last month as though weather were the most consequential thing to happen on this coast, which, officially, it was.

He stood in the middle of my kitchen and looked at things.

Not rudely. Worse than rudely — *professionally*. He looked at the clock on the mantel, the heavy case clock Tomas had bought in Falmouth and wound every Sunday of his life until the Sunday he died. He looked at the shelf of ledgers — light logs, oil inventories, the station register — spines all uniform, all accounted for. He looked at me longest of all.

"You've kept this station alone," he said, "for six years."

"Tomas trained me well. The light doesn't much care whether the hand on the wick belongs to a man or a widow."

"No," he agreed. "It wouldn't." He accepted his tea and held it without drinking, which struck me as a man declining to owe anyone even the debt of a swallowed mouthful. "Your husband. You must miss him."

The question underneath the question. I heard it the way you hear the second bell beneath the first, out on the fog signal. *Who was he? What did he do? Who are you?*

"Six years is long enough that missing becomes furniture," I said. "You stop noticing it unless you move house."

Something flickered across his face — not sympathy; recognition, maybe, or its counterfeit. "My wife says something similar about my absence. She claims grief and marriage are the same discipline." He set the untouched cup down. "I won't take more of your morning, Mrs. Lockridge. The Board values its keepers. Steady hands, steady lights." A pause, measured to the quarter-second. "Steady everything."

He went down the stairs the way he'd come up, and I watched the launch until it was smudge again, and then I stood in my own kitchen feeling like a stranger in it.

Here is the cost no one warns you about. To survive the visit, I had become, wholly and convincingly, the dull widow — flat voice, flat eyes, grief-as-furniture — and the mask fit so well that for a full minute afterward I could not find myself behind it. I looked at my hands and wondered whose they were. Somewhere along six years of answering-no-question-fully, the woman doing the performing had gotten thinner and thinner, and the performance thicker, and I had begun to fear that one day there would be nothing left inside the costume at all. Just a well-run lighthouse with nobody home.

Tomas used to say I could out-stubborn the sea. He never said what the sea does to stubborn things eventually. It wears them smooth. It makes them into whatever shape the water prefers.

I went to the clock.

* * *

Hiding the ledger in the clock was Tomas's joke turned against us both. He had built a false back into the case years ago — "the one place in this house," he'd laughed, "that I wind more often than I wind myself" — and it had held his private papers through the war: the payments, the names, the routes across the channel that moved deserters and draft resisters from quarry villages to boats that carried them to safety and silence. Idris's name was in it. My brother's hand, signing receipts in a code we'd invented as children, alive and findable on every page.

I had found it four days ago looking for lamp oil, and I had not slept properly since.

Now I opened the case, lifted the ledger out of its nest among the weights, and sat with it on the floor while the pendulum swung above my head like a slow metronome counting down something I couldn't yet name.

Burning it would take eleven minutes. I knew because I'd thought about it with the precision of a woman planning her own surgery. Eleven minutes and the names would be ash and Idris would be only a rumor and I would go back to being furniture-grief and fog and the clean mechanical mercy of the lens turning. No one could hang from pages that didn't exist.

But neither could anyone be pardoned by them. Because here was the arithmetic Vey would have appreciated: those pages weren't just evidence of crimes. They were evidence of rescues — forty-one souls crossed, dated, receipted, each entry proof that when the courts came freezing everything at armistice, there were men living who owed their lives to a smuggling ring run out of a lighthouse. Surrendered to the right tribunal at the right hour, that ledger wasn't a gallows. It was a pardon waiting for signatures.

Surrendered to the wrong man at the wrong hour, it was forty-one arrests and one brother swinging.

Vey had come today because of a census discrepancy. He would come again because of something larger. Men like him always come again. The only question was what he'd find when he did — and whether I would still be standing in this kitchen performing dullness, or gone, moving, carrying the thing he wanted toward the only people who might use it to save lives instead of ending them.

Idris. The coastal road. Three checkpoints between here and the quarry village, and an amnesty-for-names offer loose in the district like a lit match in a hay barn.

I wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, sewed it into the lining of my winter coat with sailmaker's stitches — Tomas had taught me those too, another inheritance he'd never meant to leave me — and hung the coat behind the cottage door where it looked like nothing, like every coat on every hook in Wales.

Then I cleaned the lens. Because the light doesn't care whose hand is on the wick, and tonight, like every night, it would need one.

* * *

At dusk, with the lamp lit and the mechanism humming its old patient hum, I allowed myself one honest moment — standing at the rail, watching the water go pewter and then black, letting the performance drop for the length of a breath.

Somewhere south, a lieutenant commander was writing a report that would include the phrase *nothing further observed*. Somewhere in Porth Aman, Bronwen Hale's neat schedules sat in a requisition tray, my lie preserved in her careful hand. Somewhere in a quarry village, my brother was alive under a dead man's name, not knowing yet that I was coming.

And somewhere between all of those points, a decision was already making itself in me, the way weather makes itself — invisibly, out of pressures I could feel but not yet see.

I went back inside and checked the coat hanging behind the door. It looked like nothing.

That, I was beginning to understand, was going to have to change.
