## 5. Bronwen Returns

*Emrys*

The census taker came back on a Tuesday, which I took as a personal insult. Tuesdays were for cleaning the lens, and the lens does not tolerate interruption. It collects salt the way grief collects reasons, patiently, from every direction, and it wants the whole of your attention while you wipe it.

I heard the boat before I saw her — not the supply cutter but the little skiff the navy kept for errands nobody wanted to put in the log. She rowed herself, badly, and stood in the shallows hauling the bow up the shingle like a woman arguing with a mule.

"You're off-duty," I said through the door, before she'd finished knocking.

"I am." Bronwen Hale had a voice that admitted nothing and a face that admitted everything. "Which is precisely why you can't report me."

"Report you for what?"

"For what I'm about to do." She held up a paper parcel, wrapped in brown paper and string, the way one wraps a bribe or a confession or a fish. "May I come in? It's cold, and what I have to say is worse in the cold."

Every sensible instinct I owned told me to close the door. I had spent eleven years being a dull woman with a dead husband and an unremarkable lamp, and dull women do not invite government clerks inside to hear things that are worse in the cold.

I stepped aside.

She sat where I told her, at the kitchen table under the window, and she unwrapped the parcel with the care of someone who has already rehearsed this moment on the boat and found it went better there. Inside was a sheet of paper. Old paper. Soft at the folds.

My stomach knew it before my eyes did. That softness is particular — paper handled often, hidden often, read by lamplight in a room where someone thought they were alone.

"Where did you get that?" I asked, and heard my own voice go flat as slate.

"The station office, when I was transcribing the register." She turned it so I could read it upside down, then thought better of the cruelty and rotated it. "It was tucked behind the fuel requisition book. Tomas Lockridge's hand. I copied it because it didn't match anything else in the file, and copying things that don't match is half my job and all of my temperament."

The page was a column of figures. Dates. Amounts in guineas. Initials where names should be — initials I knew, initials that had slept beside me for years without ever once confessing to me what they did at night.

"You said the paperwork was requisitioned," I said.

"It was. Captain Vey came to the district office four days after I filed. He took everything touching Gwennol Point. Everything except—" she tapped the copy, "—this, which never entered his inventory, and the original, which I removed first because I suspected he would come and I did not suspect he would be thorough. I was right about the first thing and wrong about the second, and I've been trying to decide which mistake to make next ever since."

"And you brought it here." I looked at her. Census takers are trained to look at people; I have spent a decade training people not to look back at me. She won. "Why?"

"Because Captain Vey thinks the smuggling ring your husband ran is still running." She said it plainly, no drama in it, which made it worse than drama. "Because armistice talks start in ten days and any man who deserted between 1914 and now is either going to be pardoned in the general wash or hanged as an example, depending entirely on who writes the list. And because Vey believes whoever inherits Tomas's ledger can buy their way onto whichever side of that line they prefer. He wants the ledger, Mrs. Lockridge. He doesn't much care whether he finds it or invents it."

The fire ticked. The lens overhead turned its slow patient turn, throwing its blade of light across the ceiling every few seconds, and I watched Bronwen Hale sit in that blade without flinching, over and over, like a woman proving something.

"There is no ring," I said. "My husband moved fish."

"He moved fish," she agreed. "And occasionally the fish had legs and draft cards."

I should have denied it. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone: I knew I should have denied it, and instead I sat down across from her and poured two cups of tea, because my hands wanted something honest to do.

"What exactly did Vey tell you?"

"Nothing. That's rather the point. He told the district clerk that the census irregularities at Gwennol Point suggested 'an ongoing arrangement,' and he used the word *ongoing* twice, and men only repeat words when they're proud of them. Then he requisitioned the file and posted a notice offering amnesty to informants. Full pardon, protection of identity, in exchange for names." She met my eyes. "Mrs. Lockridge. Someone out there knows what Tomas did. Someone is deciding this week whether your husband's kindness is worth thirty pieces or a rope."

* * *

*Bronwen*

Here is what they don't tell you when they hire you to count the nation: counting is a kind of caring. You cannot write *occupation: none, age: 41, relation to head of household: [blank]* about a woman and remain indifferent to the blank. I had gone back to Porth Aman, filed my other returns, sat through Captain Vey's requisition with my face arranged into bureaucratic blankness, and all the while the blank beside *Tomas Lockridge, keeper* sat in my chest like a stone I'd swallowed.

So I stole a document. Technically I removed it before it could be stolen from me, which is the sort of distinction that keeps clerks employed and would keep me out of prison if anyone bothered to ask, though I doubted they would bother.

She poured tea. That was the moment I understood I'd already chosen sides — you don't pour tea for a suspect, you pour it for a person — and I hated how easy it had been.

"You understand what I'm proposing," Emrys said. She had the voice of a woman who reads aloud to herself; every sentence arrived pre-measured. "You're proposing that we — that is, that I —"

"I'm proposing nothing yet. I'm proposing that we find out what Vey actually has. Because there's a difference between suspicion and evidence, and right now he has a scratched-out register, a missing original, and a theory. Theories hang people only after the evidence arrives to keep them company."

"And the original?"

I hesitated. There is no graceful way to say *I hid a government document inside my spare corset stays*, so I said it without grace. "It's in Porth Aman. Somewhere Vey's men won't look, because they are men, and because they search rooms and not lives."

Something moved behind her eyes — not gratitude. Recalculation. She was re-sorting me from *threat* into *asset*, and I watched her do it, and let her, because being useful is safer than being liked.

"If the ledger exists," she said carefully, "and I'm not saying it does —"

"You searched her house for oil and found it. Don't perform for me, Mrs. Lockridge, I count people for a living; I know what performance looks like."

The light swept the ceiling again. When it passed, she was different — older, or simply unhidden, which amounts to the same thing.

"My brother's name is in it," she said. "Idris. Alive. Reachable." Her hands were steady around the cup, which told me more than trembling would have. "Vey's amnesty-for-names notice. If Idris comes forward, he hangs anyway — deserters who surrender themselves aren't pardoned, they're processed. But if someone else sells his name first—"

"Then Vey gets his example and the armistice gets its ceremony, and everyone applauds both."

"So I take the ledger to him. Before the courts freeze. Before Vey's informants sell him." She set the cup down. "Three checkpoints between here and the quarry villages, and a widow carrying seditious paper sewn into her coat."

"That's insane."

"Yes," she agreed, with the mild tone of a woman confirming the weather. "It's also Tuesday, and I've finished the lens."

I should have gone back to my skiff and my safe little life of enumerating other people's secrets. Instead I heard myself say: "Vey will expect flight. Widows who run are guilty widows. What he won't expect is a keeper exercising her lawful authority — you carry papers, don't you? Station credentials, movement passes?"

"I carry papers."

"Then use them. Be boring. Bore every sentry on that coast to tears. And—" I pulled my coat on, because the tide was turning and I had a document in Porth Aman that needed to stay lost, "—when you reach the third checkpoint, if it goes wrong, ask for me by name. I'm the only census clerk whose records Vey trusts, which means I'm the only witness whose word might stand against his."

She looked at me for a long moment, the light sweeping us both, and whatever she decided, she decided it the way she seemed to decide everything — completely, and without warning.

"Why?" she asked. "Truly. Why risk your position for a stranger's brother?"

I wrapped the copied page and pushed it back across the table toward her. Evidence belonged with its owner; I'd only ever meant to borrow it.

"Because I counted you as nobody," I said, "and it's the first lie I've ever written down that I couldn't live with."

She almost smiled. It was terrible, the almost of it — like watching someone remember a language.

"Then help me get to Idris," she said, "and write down the truth after."

Outside, the wind had backed around to the southwest, which on this coast means weather coming within the day. I rowed for Porth Aman with my arms burning and my mind made up, and I did not allow myself, until the point was well behind me, to consider what Captain Vey would do to the pair of us if we were caught.

The answer, I suspected, was: exactly what he'd do anyway. Only faster.
