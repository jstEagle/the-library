## 6. Checkpoint at Porth Aman

I had sewn a ledger into my coat once before. The first time was for Tomas, three days after he died, when I took the pages out of the clock and put them against my body because I could not bear to leave them in a house that suddenly belonged to the Crown's memory rather than mine. I had told myself then that it was grief. Grief is a very forgiving word. It forgives smuggling, forgery, and treason, provided you do all three quietly and in black.

The second time was last night, by lamplight, with a sailmaker's curved needle and thread waxed against my palm until it hissed. I did not tell myself anything this time. I only counted stitches.

Forty-one pages remained after Bronwen and I had spent an evening deciding what the world needed to know. Forty-one names, payments, tides, routes — the arithmetic of forty-three men pulled out of the war's teeth and set down on far shores where nobody shot at them. The rest of the ledger was ash in the grate before dawn, and I had swept the ashes into the sea like a woman disposing of tea leaves. There is a particular kind of steadiness required for that chore, and I discovered I possessed it. That frightened me more than the soldiers would.

The coat was my winter one, heavy wool the color of wet slate, the kind of garment a lighthouse widow has worn so long it has stopped being clothing and become weather. A rectangle of oilcloth-wrapped paper lay flat against my left side, between lining and shell, from hipbone to ribs. When I pressed my arm against it, I could feel nothing but a faint stiffness, like an old scar. Good. Scars explain themselves. Paper does not.

Bronwen had wanted to come with me as far as Porth Aman.

"If Vey traced my copy," she said, "he traced it to *you*, not to a road."

"Then you're safer standing exactly where he expects you to be, copying things." I had learned over two evenings of uneasy alliance that Bronwen Hale argued the way gulls fight over fish: loudly, persistently, and without any intention of drowning. But on this point she had gone quiet, which I found more alarming than her noise. Quiet meant she agreed the risk was real.

"You'll send word," she said. Not a question. A condition.

"I'll light the lamp every evening at sunset," I said. "If it burns steady, I'm alive. If you ever see it go dark—" I shrugged. "Come collect the clock. It tells excellent stories."

* * *

The coastal road to Porth Aman runs eleven miles along cliffs that the sea is slowly auditing, taking a yard here, a foot there, leaving invoices of fallen rock. I walked it in the grey hour after sunrise with a market basket on my arm — bread wrapped in cloth, a tin of lamp oil I did not need, a cabbage for verisimilitude. No one suspects a woman carrying a cabbage. Cabbages are the great alibi of the rural poor.

My keeper's papers rode in my breast pocket: the warrant of appointment signed under Tomas's name, the station log extracts, the supply requisitions countersigned at Ffair-hywel. Since his death I had continued signing his initials, because a dead man's authority travels farther than a living woman's, and because correcting the record was precisely what I could not afford to do. There is a bitter joke in there somewhere about how I have spent nine years being the wrong signature. I did not laugh.

By midmorning the wind had come around northwest and begun pushing rain ahead of it in long grey sheets. I could see the checkpoint before I saw the soldiers: a farm cart turned sideways across the road at the mouth of the village, a tarpaulin strung between two poles, a brazier smoking sullenly. Three men in khaki and one in the darker uniform of the military police, and beyond them the village itself huddled under its slate roofs as if trying to be mistaken for geology.

I had rehearsed. I want to be honest about that, because later accounts of courage tend to omit the rehearsal, as though bravery arrives fully dressed. For two nights I had stood in my kitchen playing both parts — the sentry and the widow — until the widow's answers came out smooth as poured cream. Where are you traveling? To Ffair-hywel, for supplies, and to settle my late husband's accounts with the chandler. Purpose? The same purpose as always, young man: keeping a light burning so His Majesty's ships don't founder on my rocks. Papers? Here. Everything here, everything dull, everything in order.

What I had not rehearsed was the poster.

It was pasted to the tarpaulin pole at eye level, rain-blurred already, but not blurred enough. A printed notice, War Office letterhead, and beneath it a list of names — deserters and absentees, wanted for questioning — and beside each name a sum. My eyes went down the list the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. None of the names were in the ledger. None of the forty-three. These were other men, other rings, or perhaps no ring at all, just the machinery of panic grinding through its gears.

But at the bottom, in fresh ink added by hand, was a sentence that stopped my breath:

*Full amnesty offered to any person providing information leading to the apprehension of persons assisting deserters. Rewards payable. Apply to Capt. E. Vey.*

Amnesty-for-names. Vey had turned fear into currency and posted the exchange rate on a telegraph pole for anyone with a grudge and a memory to read.

I thought of Bronwen's face when she'd said, *someone may already be selling.* I thought of the supply boatmen who came monthly to Gwennol Point, cheerful men who noticed everything because noticing was the only entertainment the coast afforded. I thought of the chandler at Ffair-hywel, of the quarry clerk, of any of two dozen people who had seen Tomas's visitors over the years and filed them away without knowing what they filed.

And underneath that, colder: I thought of Idris. My brother, living under another man's name in a quarry village, reachable by exactly the roads I was walking. If someone sold the route, they wouldn't sell the forty-three who were safely gone. They'd sell the ones still here. Still touchable. Still mine.

"Morning, missus." The sentry nearest the barrier was young, red-faced from the brazier, with the permanent squint of a boy who'd been issued a rifle and a job and not much else. "State your name and business, please."

"Mrs. Tomas Lockridge," I said. "Keeper of the Gwennol Point light. I'm bound for Ffair-hywel — stores, and my husband's outstanding account with Morgan the chandler. He died in March. Accounts don't die with you, more's the pity."

The complaint did its work; small grievances make women credible. The sentry glanced at the corporal under the tarpaulin, who came over wiping his hands on his thighs.

"Papers."

I gave him the breast pocket documents, not the left side of my coat. He read slowly, lips moving. I watched him arrive at the initials — T.L., countersigned, stamped, official — and watched officialdom do what officialdom does best, which is believe itself.

"Gwennol Point." He looked up. "That's the light past Carreg Ddu?"

"It's the light you can see from Carreg Ddu if the haze lifts, which it won't today."

Something almost like a smile crossed his face and was disciplined away. "You'll have seen traffic on the water, ma'am. Boats running dark, maybe. Anything unusual."

"I see weather," I said. "Weather is unusual enough on that coast. And once a French trawler that had lost its way and wanted directions more than anything. I gave them directions. They seemed grateful. The sea doesn't respect borders, Corporal, so neither do I bother drawing them on the water."

He wrote something in a notebook. I let him. A note that says *chatty widow, knows boats* is the best note a smuggler's widow can earn.

Then, folding my papers closed, he paused. "You'll be wanting to know, since you're traveling alone." He nodded at the poster. "There's a proclamation. Persons aiding deserters — the Crown's offering amnesty to informants. Full pardon." He said it the way boys recite lessons, no weight on any word. "So if you heard anything. Seen anything."

Here it was. The door, held open, with warmth and legality on the other side of it. All I had to do was say a name — any name, Idris's name, half-remembered, hedged — and walk through clean. I felt the temptation physically, like a hand at my back, and I understood in that moment why Vey had chosen this method instead of raids. He hadn't built a net. He'd built a mirror, and every frightened person on this coast would look into it and find their own survival staring back.

"I've kept a light for nine years," I said. "Do you know what I've learned about people who come asking questions at lights?" I met his eyes. "Mostly they're lost, and mostly they're cold, and if you turn them away they die on your rocks and wash up in your harbor come spring. I answer the lamp, Corporal. What men do ashore is between them and their consciences — and now, it seems, between them and Captain Vey's ledger. Which I'm sure is longer than mine."

The corporal snorted despite himself. The young sentry flushed and waved me through, and the cart was dragged aside, and I walked into Porth Aman with my cabbage and my cargo and my heart going like a gull in a storm.

* * *

I allowed myself half a loaf and tea at the tavern, because a woman traveling to settle accounts takes refreshment; it was in the rehearsal. The tavern's single room was warm and smelled of peat and spilled beer, and I chose the corner table with my back to the wall, which is habit, not paranoia. Habit. I keep telling myself the distinction matters.

Two soldiers sat by the fire, off duty, boots steaming. I did not listen to them. I want that recorded: I did not listen. But the room was small and their voices carried, and refusing to hear is a skill I have never mastered, possibly because it has kept me alive too often.

"—amnesty's real, right enough. Sergeant Davies says the captain's paid out twice already. Two men down the coast, names given, money and pardon both."

"Who'd they give up?"

"Fisher fella at Aberdaron, and some woman keeps a shop — sells rope. Says she saw boats loading at night back in 'sixteen. Captain Vey's building a list, he says. Names first, arrests after the courts sit again."

After the courts sit again. So the law was frozen but thawing, and Vey intended to have his evidence stacked before the ice went out. And he had already bought two informants on this stretch of coast. Two. How many degrees of separation between a ropemaker's shop in Aberdaron and a lighthouse at Gwennol Point? In a place where everyone buys rope, bread, oil, gossip?

I finished my tea. My hands were perfectly steady, which I noted with a strange detachment, as though reporting on someone else's physiology. Fear had passed through me and come out the far side as something harder and more useful. Not calm — I mistrust calm — but resolve, the way sea water freezes into ice you can actually walk on.

Because here was the arithmetic, and I am a keeper's wife; arithmetic is my trade. If someone was selling, the route was already dying, and every day I waited shrank Idris's odds. The ledger in my coat was no longer just testimony for a future tribunal. It was a fuse, lit at both ends. Deliver it fast, or watch it detonate over the heads of everyone whose name was written inside it — including the one name I had crossed the country to save.

I bought a second loaf, tucked the basket over my arm, and stepped out into the rain.

The road east climbed away from the village toward the quarry country, and I stood a moment at the edge of town looking at it — grey stone, grey sky, twelve hours' walk to a brother who didn't know I was coming and wouldn't thank me.

Then I saw the motorcar.

It stood outside the tavern's stable yard, mud to its axles, a driver hunched at the wheel — and pinned under the wiper arm, flapping in the wind, a folded newspaper with a census form's distinctive buff corner peeking out of it. Requisitioned paperwork. I knew that buff color now the way I knew the taste of my own blood.

The car was pointed east. Toward the quarries. Toward Nant Fawr.

Toward, unless God had a sense of humor I hadn't previously credited, exactly where I was going.
