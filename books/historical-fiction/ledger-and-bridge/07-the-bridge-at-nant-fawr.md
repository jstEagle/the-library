## 7. The Bridge at Nant Fawr

The trouble with lying well is that people believe you. The trouble with believing you is that they keep asking.

The second checkpoint sat where the coast road pinched down to a single lane above the Nant Fawr — a plank table, two soldiers, and a brazier that had clearly lost its argument with the wind hours ago. I saw it from half a mile off, which was half a mile more than I wanted. There was no way around. The river had come up with the rains and swallowed the low path along the shingle, so the bridge was the road, and the road was the checkpoint, and the checkpoint was a boy of perhaps nineteen with a rifle he held the way one holds a cat one has been assured is friendly.

I stopped the cart twenty feet out and arranged my face into its winter setting: patient, slightly deaf, uninteresting.

"Morning." He said it like a question he hoped someone else would answer.

"Morning," I agreed, and produced Tomas's papers from the oilcloth wallet inside my coat. Keeper's authority. Widow of the Gwennol Point light. The ink had gone brown at the fold where my thumb had worried it for thirty miles.

He read them upside down first, which told me a great deal about his morning. Then right side up. His companion, older and colder, came around to look at me instead of the paper, which was worse.

"Lamp oil," I said, nodding at the cans lashed under the canvas. "And flour, if the army hasn't eaten it all already."

The boy almost smiled. The older one didn't.

"Purpose of travel?"

"I'm expected at the quarry office at Nant Fawr village. Stores for the light. They'll want a signature; I'll want mine back."

It was true in every particular except the ledger sewn flat against my ribs under my coat, forty pages of names and payments and channel crossings that could hang half a parish. I have found that the best lie is a small truth wearing a heavy coat. You give them something to check if they're minded to check, and most men aren't. Most men want their brazier lit and their shift over.

But the boy wasn't finished. He'd turned the papers over twice, hunting for something, and when he didn't find it he did the thing I will remember longer than I'd like: he looked at me as though I were a page himself, and began to read.

"You'll be the Lockridge widow," he said. "Gwennol Point."

"Tomas Lockridge kept that light eleven years," I said. "I keep it now."

"My gran's from Porth Aman. She used to say old Lockridge could read weather like a psalm." A pause, the rifle shifting. "She said other things too."

The wind filled the pause with salt. Somewhere below us the Nant Fawr went on doing what flooded rivers do, which is everything loudly and nothing usefully.

"People say things about lighthouse men," I said. "Mostly that they're dull, and mostly because we ask them not to visit."

That got the smile out of him after all, and I hated myself for how easily I spent charm like loose coppers when my brother's life was stitched into my lining. But then the older soldier leaned over the boy's shoulder and said, quietly, "Ask her."

"Ask me what?"

The boy's ears went red. He took a folded sheet from inside his greatcoat — cheap paper, badly printed, the kind of thing that gets nailed to garrison doors — and unfolded it with the care of a man handling evidence rather than paper.

WANTED FOR QUESTIONING. And beneath, a name in heavy black type.

*Idris Vaughan.*

My brother looked back at me from the circular the way photographs always betray you: younger, cleaner, a man caught between blinks by someone who meant him harm. Twenty years since I'd seen that face in the flesh. Long enough that grief for him had gone soft and habitual, like a stone in the pocket you stop noticing. Seeing it printed, priced, and distributed — that made the stone sharp again.

"He's no relation of yours?" the boy asked. "Vaughan. It's a common enough name down here."

Here is what I know about fear: it arrives ahead of itself, the way weather does, and if you watch for the signs you can sometimes get the shutters closed before it breaks. My hands were in my lap, folded, keeper's hands, steady from twenty years of brass fittings and clockwork. Under the coat, against my ribs, forty pages lay quiet as a held breath.

"Half of Cardiganshire is some relation or other," I said. "If I claimed them all I'd never get any lamp oil home." I tilted my head at the circular, mild as milk. "What's he meant to have done?"

"Deserted," said the older man. "Before that, moved men who deserted. There are those being paid for information now." He let that sit between us the way you'd set down something heavy. "Amnesty for names. Full pardon for whoever comes forward first."

So Vey's offer had traveled faster than I had. Of course it had. Fear travels light and pays its own fare. Someone at Porth Aman might already be counting what Idris's name was worth, and here was I, carrying the whole arithmetic across a bridge.

"Well," I said, gathering the papers the boy still held. "I hope they catch him, if he's done all that. A woman alone on a point can't be too careful."

I heard the words leave my mouth and marveled at them. *I hope they catch him.* Spoken in my own voice, with my own mouth, while his name pressed warm against my skin through two layers of wool and linen. That is what survival costs, I thought later. Not blood. Sentences. You pay in sentences, and you keep paying, and one day you've said so many false things that your true ones start to sound borrowed.

The older man waved us through with two fingers. The boy touched his cap and called after me, "Mind the middle span, missus! The water's taken the near rail!"

* * *

He was right about the rail.

The bridge at Nant Fawr was old timber on stone piers, built for carts going at a walk and a prayer, and the flood had climbed it like a debt collector. Water ran brown and muscular over the lower planks, maybe six inches deep at the near end and deeper past the middle, dragging at the wheels, shoving at anything that would be shoved. The far rail on the downstream side was simply gone — torn away clean, leaving a row of pale stubs like teeth.

I should have waited. There was a shepherd's hut upstream; I could have sat out the worst of it, dried out, thought properly. But the checkpoint was behind me, and behind me also was a boy who had seen my face and an older man who had watched me see a name, and waiting meant thinking, and thinking meant hearing *amnesty for names* over and over in the voice of a stranger.

So I drove on.

The water took the cart at the third pier. Not dramatically — rivers don't do drama, they do arithmetic — just a slow lean, a lurch, the left wheel finding a gap where a plank had rotted or been torn loose, and suddenly the whole load swung toward the open rail like a door coming off its hinges. The horse screamed and hauled. I grabbed the brake and the canvas tie both, and the canvas tore, and the first oil can went over the stubs of the rail and into the brown water and was gone between one heartbeat and the next.

And with it, for one white instant, I thought: *the ledger.*

Because I had moved it that morning. At dawn, in the shepherd's hut outside Porth Aman, I had taken the coat apart at the seam with Tomas's own sail needle and resewn the pages — not flat against my ribs this time but higher, into the shoulder, wrapped in oilskin, because a seam at the rib is where a searching hand goes first and a shoulder is where pity doesn't. And when I'd knelt to fix the wheel-tie at the checkpoint, getting down heavily, widowishly, the coat had swung and the weight had shifted, and I had felt the corner of the oilskin packet work loose from its stitches.

Loose. Working looser. Over a flooded bridge with the rail torn away.

I don't remember deciding to move. I remember the cold — the flood taking me to the hip, the current hitting like a crowd, the horse still screaming somewhere above and behind — and I remember my hand closing on cloth. On the coat. On the packet sliding free of its last stitch, turning in the water, fat and pale as a drowned bird.

I got it. Two fingers through the oilskin loop, then the whole fist, then I was hauling myself hand over hand along the rail stubs with the river trying to read me the way the boy had read me, patiently, page by page, looking for the truth underneath. The current pulled the coat off my shoulders and I let it go — let Tomas's good grey coat go spinning downstream like a shed skin — and I crawled the last yards to the pier on planks slick as fish, with the ledger crushed to my chest and the flour floating away behind me in its burst sack, ghosting white on the brown water like something trying to signal.

Then the far bank. Then gravel and gorse and my own breath, loud and stupid in my ears.

I sat in the wet until I stopped shaking, which took longer than pride allows me to admit. The horse stood at the bridgehead where the current had shoved us ashore together, blowing hard, one eye rolling white at me as if to ask what precisely I thought I was doing. Fair question. I had no answer that would survive daylight.

The ledger was soaked at one corner. I unwrapped it there on the bank with fingers that wouldn't behave, and the water had gotten into the outer pages — not the names, thank God, not yet, but the margins softening, the ink of Tomas's careful columns beginning to bloom like frost ferns. Forty pages. Thirty-eight sound. Two going.

And there it was, plain as the river: I cannot outrun this. Every mile added risk. Every checkpoint added eyes. The coat was gone, the flour was gone, and next time the river might be Captain Vey, and Captain Vey would take more than a corner. Sooner or later the ledger would be taken from me or given by me, and the only choice left in the world was which — and what remained of it when the choosing came.

I thought of burning it there on the bank. Dry grass, dry gorse, one match. Half the names gone in a minute, Idris among them, safe in smoke. No tribunal, no gallows, no amnesty-for-names turning neighbor against neighbor. Just a widow, wet and poorer by a coat, carrying lamp oil home.

I struck no match. Because the other pages weren't only names to hang. They were names to save — men crossed, men landed, men breathing air tonight in America or Australia because Tomas had written their passages in a smuggler's neat little hand. Burn the ledger and the rescuing dies with the rescue. Surrender it whole and the same pages that prove the crime prove the mercy. It had come to that, finally: not whether the book survived, but which half of it I would let the world read.

I resewed the packet into the remaining seam with numb fingers, badly, using thread from the coat hem I'd fished off a gorse bush like a beggar. The sun was dropping. Nant Fawr village smoked gently in the hollow below — quarry rows, chapel roof, one street of lamps being lit one by one by a man with a pole, the most peaceful sight I had seen in days.

Somewhere in that village lived a man named Griffiths, dead these six years, whose name my brother wore like the coat I'd just fed to a river.

I started down the hill toward him, wringing, and the light caught the wet oilskin at my shoulder and flashed once, bright as a signal lamp.

* * *

Idris opened the door before I knocked.
