## 8. Idris

The quarry village called itself Cwm Ddu on the map, but the men who lived there called it nothing at all, because a place where everyone is hiding from something learns quickly not to name itself.

I came down into it at dusk, when the slate dust hung in the air like a thought nobody wanted to finish. The cottages were grey and hunched, the way buildings get when they're built by men who expect to leave and never do. A dog barked once and was silenced by a look from its owner that I felt even across the lane. Everyone here had a talent for silence. It was the local industry, second only to slate.

Idris was living under the name Owen Pritchard, which amused me in a grim sort of way, because our mother's brother had been an Owen Pritchard, a dull and honest customs man who would have fainted dead away to learn his good name was sheltering a deserter's brother. My brother had chosen his disguise the way some men choose a wife — for respectability, and to spite somebody.

I found him at the third cottage I tried, mending a lamp wick by the window. He looked up, and for one long moment neither of us moved. Twenty years since I'd seen him last. He'd gone grey at the temples and stooped through the shoulders, quarry work doing what the army hadn't managed to do — break him slowly instead of all at once.

"You shouldn't have come," he said.

"Hello to you too."

He set down the lamp and crossed the room and held me hard enough to crack ribs, and I let him, and for about four seconds we were children again on the cliffs above Aberdyfi, before everything. Then he stepped back and shut the door and dropped the bar across it, and the four seconds were over.

* * *

From inside Idris's skull — though he would never have phrased it so generously:

She stood there dripping quarry fog onto his floor with a coat that bulged wrong at the left side, and he knew before she said a word what she'd carried three military checkpoints to bring him.

"Burn it," he said.

"I haven't told you yet what—"

"You don't have to." He'd spent two years learning to read people the way you read weather off the water, back when reading them wrong meant a firing squad. "You've got the ledger sewn into your coat, Emrys, or I'm a bishop. And you've come to show it to someone official, or you'd have burned it yourself on the road and saved your feet."

Her chin went up. She always did have her mother's chin and their father's stubbornness, God rot them both. "Captain Vey has offered amnesty for names."

"Vey." He said the name like spitting out a stone. "The one who traced Bronwen Hale's papers. Yes, word travels even here. We're not deaf in Cwm Ddu, only quiet." He put the kettle on because his hands needed employment. That was the trouble with Emrys standing in his kitchen: she made him want things he'd buried, tea and talk and family, ordinary comforts like a man might have. "Amnesty for names. Do you hear yourself? You hand Vey a book full of names and he hands you back pardons stamped with the same office that shot boys at dawn. You believe paper?"

"I believe evidence," she said. "I believe that thirty-one men crossed that channel alive because of Tomas, and that a tribunal can't hang families if the record shows those families were saving lives, not taking them. Names are proof, Idris. Proof is protection."

"Proof is a list," he said, "and a list is a shopping catalogue for a man like Vey. You think he wants your ledger for the tribunal? He wants it for the network. Every name in that book is a house that opened its door, a boatman who looked the other way, a woman who forged a pass. Amnesty-for-names means amnesty for *you*, sister, and rope for everyone you name."

The kettle began its low complaint. Neither of them moved toward it.

* * *

Emrys:

We argued until the candle guttered, and then we argued by its light anyway, because neither of us knew how to stop. We never had. Our childhood arguments had been conducted over whose turn it was at the looking-glass; this one was conducted over whether our family should live or die, which represented, I suppose, a certain growth.

"Tomas kept that book for seven years," I said. "Seven years he wrote every crossing down, every payment, every name — not because he was careless, Idris. Because he believed the day would come when the record mattered. When someone would need to see that ordinary people did extraordinary decent things while the world went mad. He called it 'the account that must balance.' His words."

"And Tomas balanced his account by dying on that channel, didn't he?" Idris said it quietly, and it landed like a slap anyway. "One night too many crossings. One storm too few scruples. Is that the arithmetic you want for the rest of us?"

"That isn't fair."

"No," he agreed. "Nothing about this is fair. That's rather my whole argument."

I turned away from him, to the window, where the quarry terraces descended into dark like steps going nowhere. "Twenty years ago," I said, "when Mother sent you to the mines and me to keep house, you raged about it for a week. Who got to be safe, you asked. Why her and not me. And now you sit in a borrowed name in a borrowed life and tell me safety is worth any price, including burning the one thing that could buy everyone else theirs."

"That was different."

"It was exactly the same. You were twenty and furious that *she* got the safe child and *I* got the dangerous one, and now you're forty-odd and furious that I'm asking you to be the safe one again. Some things don't change, Idris. They just change which of us is holding the knife."

He was silent a long time. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, the last shift walked home, boots crunching slate, a sound like slow applause for no one.

"You always could gut a man with a sentence," he said finally. "Mother's gift."

"One of us had to take after her."

* * *

Idris watched her turn back from the window, and what frightened him was not her anger. Her anger he knew like his own hands. What frightened him was the thing underneath it, the thing she'd carried alone since Tomas died: she wasn't asking permission anymore. Somewhere between Gwennol Point and this kitchen she had stopped being a woman who hid behind a husband's name, and he recognized it because he'd seen it in deserters on the boat over — the moment a man decided that fear had taken enough from him.

"The armistice freezes the courts," he said. "That's the rumor, anyway. After that, Vey's authority is ice under spring flood. If you deliver that book before the signing—"

"If I deliver it *as testimony*," she corrected. "Not as surrender. There's a difference, and the difference is Bronwen Hale, who copied a page and can verify the count of crossings. Thirty-one men, Idris. Thirty-one mothers' sons breathing tonight because Tomas wrote neatly and I keep my promises."

"And if Vey reads it as a confession instead? If he hangs the lot of us and calls it justice?" He took the poker and stirred a fire that didn't need stirring. "You'll have traded our necks for your clean conscience."

"My conscience stopped being clean the day I answered no question fully for eleven years," she said. "This is how it gets dirty again. Properly dirty. On purpose."

He almost laughed. God help him, he almost laughed, because she sounded like their grandmother, who'd once told a bailiff to his face that the tithe could be collected from her cold hands, and then made him tea while he waited to see whether she meant it.

"Half," he said.

She frowned. "What?"

"You want to burn it, I want to burn it, and neither of us will yield, so." He set the poker down. "Burn half. Keep the pages that prove the rescues — dates, numbers of men landed safe. Burn the pages that map the network. No routes, no payments, no names of the ones still living under borrowed roofs. Give Vey enough to pardon the dead men's honor and not enough to hang the living."

Emrys stared at him, and he watched her do the arithmetic she was famous for in the family — the sum that always came out against sentiment and in favor of survival.

"That destroys the proof of the smugglers themselves," she said slowly. "Tomas. The boatmen. They'd be unnamed."

"They're mostly dead already, Emrys. Tomas is dead regardless. But Idris Jones—" and here his voice cracked, just slightly, on his own true name, which he had not spoken aloud in two years, "—Idris Jones is sitting right in front of you, drinking bad tea, and would very much like to go on doing so."

* * *

Emrys:

We sat up most of the night, the ledger open between us on the table like a patient on an operating slab, and we cut it with his quarry knife, page by page, and I will not pretend my hands were steady.

Some pages went into the fire easily. Tomas's route maps, with their tidy annotations — *tide favorable after moonset; pay the ferryman at Pwllheli, not before.* Others fought me. The page with young Rhys Maddox's name, sixteen years old, fled from a training camp with frostbitten feet, landed safe in Ireland. I read it twice and fed it to the flames anyway, because Idris was right: Rhys was alive somewhere under another man's roof, and a name in Captain Vey's hands would not warm him.

What remained was half a book. Half a truth. Dates and counts, landings and returns, the bare skeleton of decency without the flesh that named anyone. Enough, I prayed, for a tribunal to see the shape of what Tomas had done. Not enough to hang a single living soul for it.

Near dawn, Idris sewed the surviving pages back into my coat himself, his stitches clumsy and close together, a quarryman's stitches, built to hold under weight.

"You'll go through Vey's checkpoint," he said. It wasn't a question. Word had come up the valley overnight with the milk cart: trains stalled at Nant Fawr, soldiers thick on the roads, and a captain with a face like a closed ledger asking after a woman keeper from the coast.

"How did you know it was Vey?"

"Because you told me his name last night with the particular calm you save for things you've already decided to walk into." He tied off the thread and bit it. "You're going to surrender it to him yourself, aren't you. Before he takes it."

"Surrender implies I lost it," I said. "I prefer *deliver*. As testimony. With Bronwen Hale arriving to verify, if her nerve holds."

"And if it doesn't hold? If she doesn't come?"

I pulled my coat on and felt the pages settle against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Outside, the sky over the quarry rims had gone the colour of pewter, and far off, faint as memory, the valley's church bell began ringing — early, urgent, wrong somehow, the way bells ring when news outruns the newspapers.

"Then I'll verify it myself," I said, "under my own name. All of them. Emrys Lockridge, née Jones, keeper of the light at Gwennol Point, widow of a smuggler, sister of a deserter." I picked up my bag. "It's past time somebody counted us properly."

Idris walked me to the top of the lane, and there he stopped, because a man under another man's name cannot walk one step further toward his own life than the law of his fear allows.

But as I turned down the road, I heard him call out — my true name, my girlhood name, thrown across the quarry fog like a rope across water — and I caught it, and I carried it with me down toward the checkpoint, and the bell rang on.
