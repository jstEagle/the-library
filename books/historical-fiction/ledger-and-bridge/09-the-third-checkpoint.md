## 9. The Third Checkpoint

The train to Caerffyn had been sitting at Porth Aman junction for four hours, and in that time the stationmaster had aged a decade.

"Armistice," he kept saying, as though the word were a weather system he could neither predict nor survive. "Any hour now, they say. Any hour. And what's a man to do with a train full of passengers when history lands on him?"

What he did was nothing, which suited me poorly and suited my conscience not at all. I sat in the waiting room with my coat across my knees — Idris's stitching gone crooked along the left seam where he'd sewn the ledger in by lamplight, arguing with me the whole while — and I watched soldiers come and go at the checkpoint gate and tried to look like a woman whose only concern was a delayed connection.

I have spent thirty years practicing invisibility. It is a craft, like keeping a light: mostly tedium, occasionally terror, and always the knowledge that one bad night will kill you.

The trouble with the third checkpoint was that it wasn't a checkpoint so much as an ambush wearing a uniform. They'd moved it from the platform to the station road itself, with a table, a lantern, and two sentries whose youth made them cruel in the way only frightened boys can be. And behind the table, consulting a paper he clearly knew by heart, stood Captain Vey.

He'd shaved since Gwennol Point. He'd also stopped pretending to be merely diligent. Diligence doesn't post men at every road into a quarry valley; ambition does that.

* * *

I considered my options with the honesty of someone who has run out of them.

Option one: turn back. But Idris was behind me, not ahead, and turning back meant walking toward him with Vey's men already asking questions in the villages. Option two: bluff again. My keeper's papers had carried me through Porth Aman and past a boy at Nant Fawr who'd read Idris's name off a circular with his finger under it like a schoolboy at scripture. But Vey had traced Bronwen's copied page — she'd told me as much before we parted, her voice steady in the way of a woman who has decided to be brave later, after the shaking stops — which meant he had a name from the ledger in his hand and my initials scratched out of a census register in his file. Bluffing against that was not courage. It was arithmetic done badly.

Which left option three, the one Tomas would never have chosen and Idris had begged me not to choose and I had been walking toward for nine days without admitting it: walk up to the table myself.

There is a peculiar freedom in deciding to be caught. Every previous decision in my life had been shaped around avoidance — don't answer fully, don't sign anything, don't let them write your name down. Now I stood in a cold waiting room rehearsing surrender, and I found my hands were steady. Perhaps because for once the plan and the fear pointed the same direction.

Bronwen was supposed to meet me here. That was the gamble underneath the gamble. She had the copied page memorized — names, sums, dates — and more than that, she had something no tribunal could manufacture: the rescue count. Forty-one souls crossed by Tomas's routes over six years, and not one payment unaccounted for. A ledger proves smuggling. Only a witness proves rescue. If Bronwen didn't come —

Well. Then I'd find out whether I'd been brave or merely stupid, and there is rarely time to enjoy the difference.

* * *

They called my name before I reached the table.

Not "Mrs. Lockridge." Not "madam." My name — Emrys — spoken in Vey's clipped officer's English with the Welsh vowels handled carefully, the way a man handles evidence. He'd gotten it from somewhere. Bronwen's requisitioned paperwork, most likely, or the scratched-out initials in the station register, or God knew what other record of me existed in some office I'd spent three decades not thinking about.

"Mrs. Lockridge." He corrected himself with a small smile, offering the married name back like a courtesy. "You've been difficult to correspond with."

"I keep a light, Captain. Correspondence comes second."

"So I understand." He gestured to the chair opposite. Two sentries flanked us, and beyond them the stalled train breathed steam into the grey afternoon like something dying slowly. "You're traveling toward Caerffyn. Alone. On the day before — if the rumors hold — the courts' authority freezes with the armistice. You'll forgive me for finding your timing interesting."

"You'll forgive me for finding your presence here interesting. This is a railway station, not a front line."

"The armistice makes everything a front line, Mrs. Lockridge. Including paperwork." He set a single page on the table between us, face down, one hand flat upon it. I knew what was under his palm. I had written half those entries myself in Tomas's last year, when his hands shook too badly for figures — the payments, the boat fees, the safe houses, the names written in a code we'd invented together over a kitchen table while pretending to discuss lamp oil prices.

Idris's name was on that page. So was mine, if you knew how to read the initials.

"Here is what happens now," Vey said pleasantly. "In approximately twelve hours, assuming the politicians do what they are paid to do, every prosecution pending in this jurisdiction becomes a negotiation, and every negotiation becomes a favor, and favors go to whoever holds the best cards. I intend to hold the cards. You are going to help me, or you are going to be the example that encourages others to help me. There is a cell in Caerffyn with your approximate dimensions."

For a moment I let myself look at him properly — the neat collar, the patient hands, the terrible economy of a man who believed entirely in his own necessity. Somewhere behind his eyes was probably a boy who'd joined up for reasons as good as Tomas's. That was the cruelty of it: both of us certain we were keeping a light.

"You want names," I said.

"I want the ledger. Names optional, though they do improve the outcome."

"And what do the named families receive? You offered amnesty-for-informants at Porth Aman. I heard."

His smile thinned. Word travels fast along a coast.

"Amnesty," he said, "for cooperation."

"Then hear my cooperation." I stood, and every sentry in the station stood with me, and I confess the symmetry pleased some small spiteful part of me that has survived worse winters than this one. "The ledger is in my coat. I am giving it to you freely, Captain, before you take it, and the difference matters — to the law, if any of it survives till morning, and to me regardless. But you will not read it alone, and you will not act on it tonight, because the man who wrote half of it is dead and the other half of it is testimony, not confession, and testimony requires a witness who can verify what it saved."

"And this witness?"

"Is late," said a voice from the station door, "because your trains don't run and your roads are flooded, and if you've started without me, Captain, I shall be very cross."

* * *

Bronwen Hale came down the platform with her census satchel still slung across her body like a bandolier, hat askew, cheeks wind-scoured, radiating the specific authority of a civil servant arriving at a scene she intends to reorganize. Two soldiers moved to stop her. She produced a document from the satchel with the speed of long practice and did not slow her stride, and whatever was on that document made them step aside, and I made a private note to learn someday exactly what Bronwen had copied besides the page.

"Miss Hale," Vey said, and something in his voice shifted — the tone of a man discovering his own requisition had been requisitioned. "Your materials were transferred to—"

"They were stolen, Captain, by your office, without warrant, and I have filed the complaint in duplicate. One copy goes to Caerffyn. The other, if the armistice signs tonight, goes nowhere, because the office receiving it will cease to exist, which I suspect you know, which is why you're standing at a railway station holding a piece of paper like it's a sword." She set her satchel on his table, beside his face-down page, and looked at me — one long look, quick and level, asking without words: *are you sure?*

I was sure. It was the first thing in nine days I had been sure of.

"He knows about your copy," I said.

"He knows about a copy. He doesn't know I verified the count against the harbourmasters' tolls at three landings, because harbourmasters keep their own books, Captain, and they are far less careful about who reads them." She turned to Vey, and I watched her decide, visibly, to spend her career. "Forty-one people crossed on those routes between 1912 and 1918. Not deserters who abandoned their posts — men whose papers were refused, boys of sixteen marked fit by doctors who hadn't examined them, one Quaker who'd sooner have died than shoot and very nearly did. Every crossing is dated. Every payment is recorded. Nothing was taken for profit — the surplus went to the families left behind, and I can produce those receipts too, if you give me until morning."

Vey looked at the face-down page under his hand. Then at me. Then, briefly and unwillingly, at the stalled train and the darkening sky and the century about to turn over without his permission.

"If I open this ledger," he said slowly, "and it says what you claim—"

"It says what it says," I said. "That has always been its trouble."

He turned the page over. Under his palm, in Tomas's careful figures and my steadier ones, lay the winter of 1916, the Nant Fawr crossing, four names, and beside each name a sum and a route and a small notation in the margin that only I had ever understood — *safe*, written in Welsh, in Tomas's hand, meaning *they got through*.

Vey read for a long time. The steam sighed. Somewhere down the line, faint as a struck match, a church bell began — uncertain at first, then another joining it, then a third, stations apart but ringing the same hour, and the sentries glanced at each other and one of them grinned like a boy let out of school.

"That's not the hour," the younger sentry said. "Sir. That's not the hour at all."

The bells kept coming. Vey did not look up from the ledger, but his hand had gone still on the page, and I understood that whatever he did next, he would do it in a world that had just changed shape — where a smuggler's book might be read not as a list of crimes but as a count of the saved, where a widow's testimony might weigh more than a captain's warrant, where a woman could stand at a table in her dead husband's coat and say her own name out loud.

"Emrys," I said.

Vey looked up. "I beg your pardon?"

"My name. Since everyone seems finally to want it written correctly." I held his eyes and did not blink, and beside me Bronwen took out her pencil, opened the census book to the corrected page, and waited.

Down the platform the bells rang on, and the first cheers were starting somewhere in the town below us, thin and disbelieving, like a tide coming in.

"Now then, Captain," Bronwen said. "Shall we begin with the count?"
