## 10. Light Kept Burning

The armistice came to Gwennol Point the way most news did — late, damp, and slightly wrong in the details.

I heard it first from the gulls, if you can believe that. They went up off the rocks all at once on the morning of the eleventh, a white sheet of them, and below in the cove the supply boat was flying every scrap of bunting its skipper owned, which turned out to be a Welsh flag and what may once have been a tablecloth. Then the foghorn from Porth Aman started up, three long blasts, over and over, until I understood it wasn't warning anyone of anything. It was simply noise, made for joy, which is a use I had never once considered for it.

I stood at the gallery rail with my hands around the cold iron and let the sound wash through me. Eleven o'clock. Somewhere in France men were climbing out of ditches they had lived in longer than some of them had been alive. Somewhere in a tribunal room in Caernarfon, a clerk was dipping his pen to write *pardoned* beside my brother's name.

And somewhere behind me, in the lamp room, the light I had kept burning through four years of war and one winter that tried to kill me personally turned its slow circle, indifferent as ever, doing its work whether the world was ending or beginning.

There's a lesson in that, though I've never been quick with lessons.

* * *

The tribunal had taken nine days. Nine days during which I sat in a rented room above a chandler's shop in Porth Aman, wearing the same grey dress, drinking tea I didn't want, while men in uniform read aloud from half a ledger.

Half. My half. The pages I'd carried through two checkpoints and a flooded river and one conversation with Captain Vey that I still rehearsed in my dreams, usually badly. The other half — the names of the families who'd sheltered deserters, the routes, the payments — those pages had gone into the stove at Gwennol Point the night before I left, and I fed them to the fire page by page like a woman saying goodbye to guests she loved but could not afford to keep.

Vey had stood in my kitchen while the smoke went up the flue. He'd watched me do it. That was the bargain neither of us said aloud: here is everything you need to hang me, and nothing you can use to hang anyone else.

"You understand," he'd said, "that destruction of evidence carries its own consequences."

"I understand," I'd told him, "that you have exactly one witness who can testify those pages recorded rescues and not treason, and she's standing in your way."

Bronwen Hale had arrived an hour later with her copied page and her census forms and her infuriating, unblinking competence, and between the two of us we'd made Vey arithmetic he couldn't argue with: forty-one men moved across the channel alive, against however many courts-martial he might win by hanging a lighthouse widow. Armistice negotiations were freezing the courts' authority day by day. He was a man racing a tide with a bucket.

He took the ledger. He took my testimony, given under my own name — Emrys Lockridge, born Emrys Carrow, sister of Idris Carrow, keeper of Gwennol Point light in fact if never in the record. Saying it out loud in that room felt like pulling a splinter twenty years old out of my palm. It hurt more than I expected, and then it hurt less than anything had in years.

The tribunal spared Idris. Spared the named families too, or rather declined to name them, since I'd burned their names before anyone could ask. Vey, to his credit or his defeat, recommended clemency in writing. Idris walked out of a quarry office in Nant Fawr a free man under his own name for the first time in three years, and the first thing he did was walk eleven miles to the coast to tell me I'd been a fool.

That's brothers for you.

* * *

He stayed three days. We argued about the ledger again, out of habit, even though it was over — he arguing I should have burned all of it, I arguing he should have thanked me, both of us knowing we were really arguing about 1898, about who got sent away to be safe and who got kept home to be useful, about our mother's face and our father's silence and every old wound we'd spent two decades picking at like scabs.

On the last morning he stood at the foot of the tower with his collar up against the wind and looked at the light the way he used to look at the sea when we were children — like it owed him something.

"Tomas knew," he said. "All those years. He knew where I was and he never wrote."

"He wrote," I said. "In there." I nodded at the lamp room. "Forty-one names. You were the fourth entry. He kept you safe the only way he trusted — on paper, in code, in ink that couldn't cry."

Idris was quiet a long moment. The wind took his breath sideways.

"He married well," he said finally, which from Idris is a eulogy, an apology, and an embrace, all compressed for transport.

Then he went down the path toward the road, and I watched him go until the headland took him, and I went back inside and trimmed the wick, because grief doesn't end, it just changes shifts.

* * *

Bronwen came on the December boat.

She came up the cliff path with her case in one hand and a paper folder held carefully flat in the other, as though it were a wounded bird, and when she reached the top she didn't knock — just stood there getting her wind back, watching me watch her.

"Mrs. Lockridge," she said.

"Miss Hale."

"Actually," she said, "it's Mrs. Hale, though my husband has been dead six years and I keep the name because it's mine now, isn't it? A name you've carried long enough stops being borrowed." She looked at me with those census-taker's eyes that missed nothing and had, I realized, missed nothing from the very first visit. "But you know that."

I stepped aside and let her in.

We drank tea at my kitchen table, the same table where I'd hidden a ledger inside a clock while a naval captain watched me perform dullness like a music-hall act. Bronwen set the folder down between us and slid it across.

"The corrected register," she said. "The requisitioned forms came back after the tribunal closed. There was a column for corrections, amendments — household members omitted or misrecorded. It requires the householder's own hand." She tapped the blank line. "I'm not permitted to fill it in for you. That would rather defeat the purpose."

I looked at the line. *Name of keeper.* And beneath it, waiting, the space where a wife had never been written, where initials had been scratched out, where Tomas had kept me invisible the way you keep a lamp shuttered — protected, and dark.

My hand shook. Not from fear this time. From the sheer strangeness of being asked.

I wrote: *Emrys Carrow Lockridge. Keeper's wife. Keeper.*

Bronwen read it upside down, as census people do, and something crossed her face that wasn't professional at all.

"They'll want to appoint a new keeper eventually," she said. "Tomas's post died with the commission. But there's talk — after the tribunal, after Vey's report — that the Board might make an exception. A woman who kept a light alone through the war and testified before a naval tribunal without flinching." She sipped her tea. "I may have mentioned it to someone. In writing. Several times."

"Bronwen Hale. Are you meddling?"

"I am a census taker," she said with tremendous dignity. "I count things. I counted one lighthouse keeper where the records showed none, and I intend to see the arithmetic corrected."

* * *

They left at dusk, the boat and its bunting and its skipper who saluted me now, which I found ridiculous and did not correct. The armistice bells were still going faintly from Porth Aman, hours past any reasonable celebration, as though the whole coast had decided that four years of silence deserved at least one night of noise.

I climbed the tower. I wound the clockwork, cleaned the lens, lit the mantle, and stood a moment in the small hot heart of the light while it gathered itself and began to turn.

Below me the sea ran black and silver to the horizon, and beyond the horizon, forty-one men were alive who would not otherwise have been, and one of them was my brother, and none of them knew my name except the ones who'd earned the right to.

The beam swept out. Swept back. Found the water, lost it, found it again.

I thought about how long I had answered no question fully. How I'd worn Tomas's name like a coat buttoned to the chin, warm and suffocating both. How invisibility had kept me alive and called itself safety, and how safety, it turns out, is not the same thing as belonging, and I had confused them for twenty years at the cost of nearly everything.

The light turned. I let it shine on me — no shutter, no shadow, no dead husband's initials between me and the glass. Just a woman at a lamp, in her own name, at the top of her own tower, on the first night of whatever comes next.

Somewhere out in the dark, a boat was coming in on the ebb tide, running the point the way boats always did, trusting the light to be there.

For the first time since I'd come to Gwennol Point, I understood that the trust ran both ways — that the coast had been keeping me all these years as surely as I'd kept it — and that tomorrow, when the Board's letter came, I would sign my reply *Emrys Carrow Lockridge, Keeper*, and mean every word of it.

The light turned. I stayed to watch it.
