## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The coach from Thornbury took eleven hours, and Edmund Ashgrove spent nine of them apologizing—to the woman whose basket he'd knocked over at the second change, to the driver for asking directions twice, and once, inexplicably, to a goat tied outside the inn at Marlow's Bend, who had looked at him with an expression of mild reproach he couldn't bear to leave unanswered.

"You'll wear your hat brim down to nothing," the driver said as they climbed the last ridge, "nodding like that."

"I wasn't nodding."

"You were. At the milestone, at the sheep, at that fence post. You apologize to fences, boy?"

Edmund considered lying, then decided it was too much effort. "They look like they've been standing there a long time," he said. "It seems rude not to acknowledge it."

The driver gave him a long look over his shoulder, the kind grown men gave Edmund when they'd decided he was either very simple or very strange and hadn't yet settled which. Edmund had collected that look his whole life, the way other men collected coins. He turned to the window instead.

And there was Hallowmere.

He had forgotten—no, that wasn't true; forgetting would have been a mercy—he had remembered exactly how it looked in October: the valley cupped in brown hills like a watch held in two hands, the observatory dome rising above the rooftops pale as an eggshell, the smoke standing straight up from every chimney because even the weather in Hallowmere seemed to mind its manners. And below it all, threading through the town square, the sound. You could hear the master clock from three miles out on a still day—a deep, patient *tock* carried up the ridge like a heartbeat under the world's floorboards.

Except.

Edmund leaned forward. The coach jolted down the switchbacks, and he counted without meaning to, the way he counted everything.

The tick was wrong.

Not stopped—stopped would be simpler, stopped was a broken spring or a snapped cord, and broken things could be fixed by Tuesday. This was worse. The beat dragged. A half-second of hesitation before each strike, so slight that anyone else in the coach would have heard nothing at all, but to Edmund it was like watching a man walk with a stone in his boot: still moving, still upright, but spending something extra with every step, and sooner or later—

"Three miles out and you're already frowning at my town," the driver said. "What did Hallowmere ever do to you?"

"The clock's sick," Edmund said.

"It's been keeping time since before my grandfather was weaned."

"That's what worries me." He didn't explain. Old mechanisms were like old men; they didn't fail all at once, they failed politely, gradually, covering for themselves out of pride until one morning they simply didn't wake up. Something in that drag—the hesitation, the *catch*—said the great clock had been compensating for months. Perhaps years. And whatever it was compensating for was getting heavier.

The driver snorted and let the matter drop. Edmund watched the town grow larger and did not nod at anything, which cost him considerable effort.

* * *

The Ashgrove house stood on Clockmaker's Lane, third from the corner, identifiable from any distance by the chaos spilling out of it. Ivy's washing line had annexed the neighbor's hedge. Tom's work boots stood on the doorstep, one upright, one on its side, as if the left had fainted. And pinned to the door itself, on paper gone soft and gray with weather, the same notice that had hung there for six years:

**ASHGROVE & SONS — CLOCKS MENDED, WATCHES CLEANED, NO JOB TOO SMALL, PLEASE WIPE YOUR FEET.**

There were no sons. There was Ivy, who was twenty-two and could argue a hawk out of a tree, and there was Edmund, who had arrived at fourteen with a carpet bag, no surname he'd use aloud, and a talent for small gears—and who had been absorbed into the household so smoothly that within a year the neighbors assumed he'd always been there, like damp in a south-facing wall.

He raised his hand to knock. The door opened first.

"EDMUND!" said Marigold Ashgrove, and the word hit him like warm water.

She was flour to the elbows, gray escaping her bun in every direction, and she pulled him inside by the sleeve before he'd finished setting down his bag, which meant he entered the house sideways, the way he always did, the way the house seemed to require.

"You've gotten thin. You've gotten *tall*, when did that happen, you've gotten thin and tall both, sit down—no, don't sit down, the chair's got mending on it—Tom! TOM! He's here!"

"I can hear, love." Tom Ashgrove appeared from the workshop doorway wiping solder off his hands, and behind him came the smell Edmund had crossed half the country for: hot metal, oil, cedar shavings, the particular sweetness of lamp-black. Tom looked Edmund up and down with a craftsman's eye. "You've gotten tall. Ruins my theory that you'd stopped growing out of spite."

"Hello, Mr. Ashgrove."

"How many times—" Tom began.

"Hello, Tom."

"There it is." Tom clapped him on the shoulder, twice, hard, which was how Tom hugged. "The letter didn't say you'd come so quick."

"The letter said the master clock was failing." Edmund set his bag down carefully beside the fainting boot. "That's not a thing you travel slowly toward."

"And there's the whole of you in one sentence." Tom grinned at Marigold. "No hello, how are you, how was the road—straight to the clock. We'll make a proper bore of him yet."

But Edmund was already looking past them, down the hall, toward the back stairs that climbed toward the tower path. His fingers had started their quiet restless counting against his thigh—one-two-three-four—which they did whenever his body knew where it wanted to be before his manners caught up.

"Eat first," Marigold commanded, reading him perfectly, as she always had. "You'll diagnose nothing on an empty stomach except your own bad temper. Ivy's out—she'll be sorry she missed you, she talks about you constant—"

"She does not," said Ivy's voice from the kitchen door, "because I'm standing right here and I have a reputation."

Ivy Ashgrove came in with a basket of onions on her hip and dirt on her cheek and looked at Edmund the way she looked at everything: directly, without negotiation. Then she set down the onions, crossed the hall, and hugged him—quickly, fiercely, the way she did everything—before he had finished deciding whether to allow it.

"You're thinner," she announced. "Sit. Eat. Tell us nothing about gears until you've had bread, I mean it, I'll hold your hands if I have to."

"She will," Tom confirmed gravely. "She's done it before."

So Edmund sat, and ate, and told them about the road instead—the goat, the fence posts, the driver—and Marigold laughed until she had to sit down herself, and Tom corrected his account of the journey in three places on grounds of national importance, and Ivy stole the crusts off his plate with the shameless efficiency of a seagull, and Edmund sat in the middle of all of it with his shoulders slowly coming down from around his ears.

This was the thing about the Ashgroves that he could never explain to anyone who hadn't lived it: they were *loud*. Not angry-loud—alive-loud. Every feeling arrived in the room at full volume, announced, shared, argued with, fed. In the shop where Edmund had apprenticed before this, feelings were kept in a drawer like spare parts, brought out rarely and oiled afterward. Here, Marigold loved you across a table whether you'd consented or not. It had terrified fourteen-year-old Edmund completely. Six years on, it terrified him slightly less, which he supposed was what people meant by home.

"So," Tom said finally, leaning back. "You heard it already, didn't you. Coming up the ridge."

Edmund set down his cup. "The drag. Half a beat late on the escape, maybe a little more."

Tom nodded slowly, unsurprised. "Started in the summer. Barely. Then by September you could hear it from the square, and now Mrs. Hollis says her hens won't lay regular because the mornings come wrong." He rubbed his jaw. "I've had the case open four times. Weight train's sound. Escapement's sound—or sounds it. Whatever's dragging, it's hiding from me."

"Nothing hides from a clock," Edmund said. "It just waits somewhere you haven't knelt yet."

Marigold and Ivy exchanged a glance over his head—that glance, the one he pretended not to see, the one that said *listen to him, listen to the little clock-priest*—and Edmund felt the familiar warmth of being useful rise in him like heat off a stove. Useful rather than known. That had been the arrangement, unspoken, for six years, and it suited everyone. He mended things. He kept his head down. Nobody asked questions he'd have hated to answer.

"Go on, then," Tom said, standing, eyes bright. "Go kneel."

* * *

The tower stair wound up inside the observatory's north pier, two hundred and six steps, and Edmund counted every one of them because his feet refused not to. The air changed as he climbed—cooler, then cold, then that peculiar mineral stillness of places built to outlast the people in them. By the last turn he could hear the drag clearly, magnified: *tick... tock... tick... tock*, each stroke arriving like a guest who pauses at the threshold to gather courage.

The clockroom opened around him.

Most folk imagined a bell and some works. What it actually was: a room the size of a chapel, stone-floored, brass-and-iron forested, the great pendulum swinging slow and grave in its pit below, the weight lines running up into darkness like harp strings for a giant. The master clock. Beater of hours for the town, for the observatory's telescopes, for every pocket watch in the valley that wanted to claim honesty. Sixty years old and still, at a glance, magnificent.

At a second glance, dying.

Edmund took off his coat, rolled his sleeves, and knelt at the escapement housing the way he'd knelt at a thousand others. He listened first—always first—with his ear near the iron and his eyes shut, letting the mechanism tell him its troubles in its own language. Drag on the release. A whisper of grit, high up. And underneath it all, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall: a sound no escapement should make. A dry, tiny, rhythmic *whisper-whisper-whisper*, like paper breathing.

His eyes opened.

He worked through the afternoon and into the dusk, cleaning, testing, ruling things out one by one, and the light went gold and then gray through the slit windows while below him Hallowmere lit its lamps one by one, obedient to the very clock that was quietly failing them. Twice he found himself talking to it, low, the way you'd soothe a horse. Once he caught himself doing it and glanced guiltily at the empty doorway, though there was nobody in the world he needed to hide the habit from except, perhaps, himself.

Near dark, his fingertip found it.

High in the movement frame, behind the chime governor—a place no maintenance hand had cause to go, a place Tom would never have reached because Tom's hands, good honest hands, were too broad for the gap—there was a panel. Not original. The brass around its edge had been cut, neatly, deliberately, by someone who knew engraver's tools, and then closed again so finely that sixty years of dust had sealed the seam invisible.

Someone had hidden something inside the master clock. Long ago. On purpose.

Edmund crouched there in the gathering dark with his heart going oddly loud, and did not pry it loose.

Not tonight. Tonight the drag was explained well enough—the governor's pivot was worn, starved of oil, a Tuesday's honest repair—and tomorrow he would fix it, and the town's hens would lay regular, and nobody need know anything about panels or whispers or paper breathing in the dark.

He gathered his tools. On the way down the stairs he counted the steps again, and at step one hundred and forty he made himself stop, because a man who counted everything eventually counted something he wished he hadn't, and the panel up there had begun, very softly, to count him back.
