## 2. A Ledger of Everyone

The Town Hall of Hallowmere smelled of beeswax and old paper, which was, in Marigold Ashgrove's considered opinion, the two best smells in the world after fresh bread and rain on hot stone. She had said as much once to her husband Tom, who had replied that she was a woman of expensive tastes, since bread cost money and rain cost nothing but an umbrella you'd lose anyway.

She climbed the stairs to the clerk's office with her basket on her arm—eggs for Mrs. Pettifer's widow's pension, a jar of plum jam for the clerk himself, because Mr. Quill did so love his jam and it never hurt to keep a man sweet—and her boots rang pleasantly on boards worn smooth by sixty years of civic business.

"Marigold!" Mr. Quill rose from behind his desk like a heron startled off a mudflat. He was a tall, stooped man with ink permanently staining the first knuckle of each hand, as though he had dipped himself into his own work and forgotten to wipe. "You've brought the eggs. And—" he spotted the jar, and his face performed the small miracle of a man trying not to look delighted and failing entirely, "—and jam."

"Greengage. Last of the season."

"You are," said Mr. Quill, accepting it with both hands as though receiving a relic, "the finest woman in this valley, and I include my late mother in that assessment, though I do so reverently."

"Your mother would have agreed with me about the jam. She made better."

"She did." He sighed, set the jar carefully beside his inkwell where it could supervise him, and then clapped his hands together. "Well. Since you're here, and since it's the day—would you care to see it?"

"See what?"

"The ledger, of course! It's the Feast of the Founding. The great book comes out. Every name in Hallowmere, entered in its own hand or the clerk's, going back to—" he touched the enormous volume lying closed before him on a velvet cloth, the way other men might touch a sleeping child, "—going back to 1741."

"I know what the ledger is, Ambrose Quill. My name is in it. So is my husband's, and both my children's, and my mother's before me, and her mother's before her. I've signed it four times in my life, and each time my hand shook worse than the last, and I'm not even sure why."

"Reverence," said Mr. Quill simply. "It's reverence. A name written down is a name that can't be lost. That's the whole idea. That's why we do it every year—the Feast of the Founding, everyone comes, everyone confirms their entry, anyone new signs their page. It's how Hallowmere knows itself."

He untied the ribbon—it took him some time; there was a great deal of ribbon, tied in a bow that had clearly taken practice—and opened the cover, and Marigold felt the little shiver go up her arms that she always felt in churches and at funerals and when the master clock tolled the hour across the valley. Some things were bigger than they looked.

The pages were heavy and cream-colored, ruled in faint red lines, and the earliest entries marched down them in a spidery copperplate: names, dates of birth, trades. *Hannah Merrilees, born 1738, spinner.* *Josiah Bell, born 1741, bell-founder* — and there was the joke the whole town still smiled at, that the bell-founder had come before the bell, and had built the observatory tower to house a bell he hadn't cast yet, out of pure confidence.

"Here's your grandmother." Mr. Quill turned the pages with fingers that hovered rather than pressed, a man who had spent his life handling fragile things. "Prudence Ashgrove. See, her trade is given as 'keeper of hens,' which I believe is generous, since by all accounts the hens kept her."

"They did. She told me so herself. Said a hen has more sense than a magistrate and twice the honesty."

"And here—" page turn, "—your own wedding entry. Thomas Ashgrove, wheelwright and clockmaker's partner. Marigold Ashgrove, née Fenwick, baker." He tapped the line gently. "You wrote that one yourself. Lovely hand. Very firm downstrokes."

"I was thirty and certain of everything," Marigold said. "Give me time and I'll be firm again."

Mr. Quill laughed, then sobered in the way of clerks approaching delicate matters, which was to say he began shuffling papers that didn't need shuffling. "And here is Ivy's confirmation from last year. Apprentice seamstress — well, she writes 'seamstress and general menace' and I let it stand, because the alternative was arguing with Ivy Ashgrove, and I value my evenings."

"As does everyone."

"But Edmund—" and there it was, the pause, the tiny hesitation Marigold had been waiting for without knowing she'd been waiting, the way you wait for a clock to strike and flinch slightly at the first note even though you knew it was coming. "Edmund has never signed."

The office went very quiet. Somewhere below, in the square, the market was setting up: crates thumping, a cart horse blowing through its lips, someone calling prices for winter apples. Ordinary sounds. But inside the room, the silence had weight, like snow beginning.

"He's shy," Marigold said.

"He's twenty-three," said Mr. Quill kindly. "I've known shy men of seventy. Shy isn't the word I'd choose. Avoidant, perhaps. There's a difference. A shy man hides because the world frightens him. An avoidant man hides because something in the world would find him." He held up both ink-stained hands. "I say this with no accusation in it whatsoever. I say it because I am the keeper of this book, and keeping a book means noticing what isn't in it. That's half the job, truly. Anyone can read what's written. Only the clerk reads what's missing."

Marigold looked at the ledger. The pages lay open to the current year, and there were the entries in their neat rows: Tom, wheelwright and horologist. Herself, baker. Ivy, seamstress and general menace. And beneath them, a blank space in the ruling, waiting, patient as all blank spaces are.

She thought of Edmund coming down the stairs that morning, quiet as ever, hair already smelling of oil and brass, taking the heel of yesterday's loaf because he believed—genuinely believed, after nine years under her roof—that the heels were nobody's favorite and he was doing the family a service by eating them. Nine years, and he still hadn't learned that she saved the heels for him on purpose.

They had found him in the winter of 'ninety, hadn't they? Or rather, he had been delivered to them, the way mysterious parcels are delivered, by a woman Marigold had met exactly once and never seen again—a thin, exhausted woman with a northern accent who said the boy was seven years old and his mother couldn't keep him and the Ashgroves were good people, wasn't that enough? And Marigold, who had buried one son between Ivy and Edmund and knew the exact shape of the hole a child leaves, had looked at the boy standing on her doorstep with a canvas bag held against his chest like a shield, and had said, "Come in, love, it's freezing, and you'll have the heel of the loaf if you want it," and that had been that. No questions. Tom had asked none either; he had simply shown the boy the workshop the next morning and put a small file in his hand, and Edmund had held it the way other children hold a candle.

Nine years. And in all that time, no papers. No parish record. No christening certificate. They had told themselves it was the times, that records got lost, that the poor moved about like birds. They had told themselves a great many comfortable things, the way families do, the way whole towns do, and the telling had worked right up until the moment a kind man opened a large book and said, *he's never signed.*

"Ambrose," Marigold said slowly, "what happens if a person doesn't sign? Truly. Not the ceremony of it—I mean the law of it."

Mr. Quill's eyebrows went up. It was not the question she usually asked over jam.

"The law of it," he repeated. "Well. Formally, residence in Hallowmere is established by ledger entry. That's the founding charter — the town keeps account of its people, and the people are owed the town's protection in return. In practice, in practice, dear woman, we're a valley of three hundred souls and half of us owe the other half money, so nobody has ever once been troubled over an unconfirmed entry." He hesitated. "But the entry is the proof, if proof were ever needed. Of belonging. Of right to shelter, to work, to a berth on the transport barges if it came to that—" He stopped. His eyes flicked up to hers and away again, quickly, like a man touching a stove to see if it's hot.

"What transport barges?" said Marigold.

"No—nothing. Talk. Rumor. You know how the ridge road brings news in distorted shapes, like shadows on a wall." But he was tidying his desk now with sudden industry, squaring papers that had been perfectly squared, and Marigold had raised two children and buried one and run a bakery through two bad harvests, and she knew exactly what a man looked like when he knew more than he wanted to say.

She put her hand flat on the open ledger instead. The paper was cool and smooth under her palm, and older than her grandmother, and it struck her suddenly—not for the first time, but harder this time—that this book contained everyone she loved except possibly the person she loved most fiercely of all, and that she had never once checked.

"Show me his space," she said. "Where his entry would go."

Mr. Quill turned back a few pages, gently, and there it was: the row for her family, Tom, herself, Ivy, and the ruled blank beneath, year upon year upon year of it, going back and back through the annual confirmations like a staircase that led nowhere. Nine Feasts of the Founding. Nine blank spaces. She remembered those feasts—roast goose, the whole town in the hall, Ivy racing the other children to be first to sign, Edmund always finding some errand, some gear to fetch, some fire to watch. She had thought: he's modest. He thinks his signature doesn't matter among all these grand old names.

She had thought wrong, and the wrongness sat in her chest now like a swallowed stone.

"He watches the clock strike midnight on New Year's Eve," she heard herself say. "From the tower window. Every year. Tom goes up with him sometimes. I used to think it was the machinery he loved." She laughed, and it came out wrong, too bright. "It's not the machinery, is it, Ambrose. It's the striking. Twelve times, loud enough for the whole valley, and nobody asking anybody anything."

"Marigold." Mr. Quill came around the desk and stood beside her, looking down at the blank spaces with his long heron's face full of something that was not quite pity—pity would have been unbearable—but close kin to it. "Whatever it is he hasn't told you—and I don't know that there is anything, mind, I only keep the book—whatever it is, it changed nothing about the boy I've watched grow up carrying your loaves and mending the church winder for nothing and blushing when you call him 'love' in front of the fishmonger. The ledger records facts. It has never once recorded worth. Worth is recorded elsewhere." He tapped his own chest, awkwardly, then seemed embarrassed and pretended he had been adjusting his waistcoat.

"You're a good man, Ambrose Quill."

"I'm a sentimental man with a government salary, which is nearly the same thing." He straightened. "Now. Will you tell him? About the signing?"

"I don't know," Marigold said honestly. "Ivy says if you corner that boy he folds up like a telescope. Tom says give him rope and he'll tie himself to you willingly, eventually, which is how Tom feels about most creatures including cats." She picked up her basket. The eggs rattled softly, ordinary and warm. "I think I'll bake instead. Nobody confesses to a question. Everybody confesses to a kitchen."

"That," said Mr. Quill gravely, "is the wisest sentence spoken in this office since the charter was signed."

She paused at the door. "Ambrose. Those transport barges. When the talk gets less shadowy and more solid—you'll come to the bakery first? Before the notices go up?"

Mr. Quill looked at her for a long moment. Then at the ledger, its pages breathing open on the blank spaces of her son's unwritten years.

"On my oath," he said, "you'll hear it from me before any drum sounds on the ridge road."

Which was, Marigold would remember afterward, the exact phrase he chose, and the exact promise the world was about to make a liar of.

She walked home through the square with her basket on her arm, past the stalls and the shouting apple-sellers and the children chasing a hoop, and above the rooftops the master clock stood in its tower against the pale sky, patient and enormous, holding all the hours of Hallowmere in its brass heart. She had lived beneath it her whole life and had never once thought about what else a thing that size might be keeping.

At home she built the oven fire high, and kneaded dough until her arms ached, and set two extra loaves to prove—one for Edmund, who would eat the heel and pretend not to notice the rest, and one, though she couldn't have said why, for whoever might knock at the door needing feeding.

It was while the second rising was underway that she heard them: hooves on the ridge road, many hooves, and the creak of leather, and—cutting across the valley like a knife through cloth—a bugle call that was no tune the town had ever played.

Marigold wiped her hands on her apron and went to the door, and stood listening, flour to her elbows, as the sound grew louder.

* * *

The drums reached the square before the riders did.
