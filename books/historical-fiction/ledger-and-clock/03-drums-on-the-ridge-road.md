## 3. Drums on the Ridge Road

The first sign of trouble was not the drums themselves but the way the dogs went quiet about them.

Hallowmere's dogs were a quarrelsome parliament. They barked at carts, at church bells, at the milk cart's particular squeak of axle, at one another in rotating committees that lasted most of the night. Edmund had learned to sleep through all of it within his first week under the Ashgroves' roof, and now, three years on, their noise was as much a part of his mornings as the smell of Marigold's porridge and Tom's habit of humming flat through his shave.

So when he woke on the last morning of October to a silence with edges on it — no dogs, only wind off the ridge and the far-off tick of his own travel clock on the sill — he lay still for a moment and felt the wrongness of it settle into his chest like a swallowed stone.

Then came the drums.

Not near. Not yet. A dry rattle somewhere up the Ridge Road, the sound carried down into the valley by the same cold air that had killed the dogs' courage. Edmund sat up in the narrow bed he'd been given in the loft above the workshop, pulled back the curtain, and saw them: six riders in grey-and-scarlet coming down out of the pines at an easy trot, breath steaming from their horses, bayonets catching the early light like a row of held-out needles.

He watched until they passed below the observatory hill and turned toward the town square, and then he did what he had done every day for three years, which was decide not to think about anything that wasn't gears.

* * *

"You've seen them, then." Old Tom Ashgrove was already at the workbench when Edmund came down, spectacles pushed up into his white hair, a broken flywheel cradled in his hands like a sick bird. "Six of them. I counted twice, because the first time I thought my eyes were going the way of my knees."

"Could your knees be right?"

"They could be right and my eyes could be wrong, which is the usual arrangement between them." Tom set the flywheel down and looked at Edmund over the top of his spectacles with those mild, unhurried eyes that had never once, in three years, asked Edmund a question he couldn't bear to answer. "Well. Soldiers don't come to Hallowmere for the scenery. The scenery comes free with the observatory."

"The clock doesn't need anything," Edmund said quickly. "I checked the escapement Tuesday."

"I know you did. You check everything Tuesday." Tom smiled, but it was the smile of a man holding something heavy behind his back. "Go eat your porridge. Whatever's coming will come slower if we watch it fed."

Marigold had made enough porridge for nine, which was her answer to all uncertainty. She was a round, quick woman whose hands were never empty — today a spoon, a dishcloth, Ivy's collar, a jar of plum preserve being pressed upon Edmund with the force of legislation. Their daughter Ivy came clattering down the stairs two steps at a time, seventeen years old and built entirely out of momentum, already talking.

"—seen them from the window, grey coats, red facings, that's the Ninth Provisional, Papa, or possibly the Twelfth, the button shapes are different but honestly who can tell at that distance, and there's an officer with a *moustache*, Edmund, a moustache you could hang washing on—"

"You'd enlist for the moustache," Marigold said.

"I'd enlist for the horse. I'd desert for the moustache." Ivy stole a spoonful of Edmund's porridge with the ease of long practice and dropped into the chair beside him. Under the chatter she was watching the window too, and Edmund noticed — because noticing small things was the whole of his trade — that her knee was bouncing like a balance wheel run fast.

They ate. Outside, the drums stopped, which was somehow worse than the drumming.

* * *

By ten o'clock the square was full.

Edmund stood at the edge of it, half-hidden behind the cooper's cart, doing what he was best at: being furniture. He had gotten very good at it over the years. There was a technique to it, he'd found. Stand slightly too far back. Hold something — a tool, a parcel, a length of string — so that people's eyes slide off you onto the object. Breathe slowly. Never be the first face anyone sees in a room.

The six scouts had become forty soldiers by some arithmetic nobody had witnessed, and they had drawn up in the square before the clerk's office with the crisp, economical movements of men who had done this in eleven other towns and expected to do it in thirty more. Stacked rifles. Tethered horses steaming by the pump. And in front of it all, on the clerk's own steps, an officer.

He was not the moustache-hanger Ivy had promised. He was young — younger, Edmund thought uncomfortably, than Tom Ashgrove's eldest apprentice had been when he'd died of the fever — with a pale, clever face and a voice pitched precisely to reach the back of any crowd without ever seeming to shout.

"People of Hallowmere," the officer said, and read from a paper as though the valley were a sentence he was pronouncing. "By order of the War Office, this valley is requisitioned, effective immediately, as a winter garrison. Your homes, your workshops, and your public buildings are required for the provisioning and quartering of His Majesty's forces."

A sound went through the crowd — not a word, just a movement, like wind through wheat.

"You will not be turned out into the snow," the officer continued, with the tone of a man granting a great favor. "You will be *registered*. Every resident of this valley will be entered upon the transport ledger, and when the garrison is established, civilian transports will carry you to stations beyond the passes. Until then, you will remain in residence, conduct your trades, and present yourselves for registration as summoned." He folded the paper. "The observatory," he added, almost as an afterthought, "will serve as the requisition office. Registration begins tomorrow at eight o'clock."

And that was how, Edmund thought numbly, a building could change professions in the space of a sentence. For as long as Hallowmere had existed, the observatory had been the town's heart — the great master clock in its tower keeping time for every hearth and hayfield for fifty miles, the astronomers upstairs arguing about the moons of Jupiter, the whole valley setting its life by the noon bell. Now it was to be a counting house. A place where names went in and berths came out.

He found himself thinking, absurdly, of the clock itself. Of the escapement he'd been tending all autumn, the delicate pallet-and-tooth conversation between gravity and restraint that kept the great pendulum honest. An army moving into the tower would be like a thumb stuck into a gear train. Everything would still move. Nothing would keep true time.

Beside him, Mr. Hedley the cooper muttered, "Registered. Like hogs."

"Like passengers," corrected his wife, in the voice of a woman determined to make the word fit whether it would or not.

* * *

The officer's name, they learned within the hour, was Lieutenant Prosser, and he wasted no sentiment. By noon, soldiers had chalked numbers on the doors of houses along the square. By one, a requisition detail had marched up the observatory hill, and Edmund watched from the workshop window as they carried the astronomers' charts out into the yard and stacked them under oilcloth with the brisk carelessness of men handling someone else's inheritance.

"They're taking the tower?" Ivy stood beside him at the glass, and for once she wasn't chattering. Her jaw was set in a way that Edmund privately called her anvil look.

"They're borrowing it," Edmund said. "That's the word the lieutenant used. Borrowed." He tried a joke because it was easier than the other thing. "Though I notice nobody mentioned giving it back."

"They never do." Ivy turned from the window. "Edmund. The registration. Tomorrow."

"What about it?"

"Everyone has to be on the ledger. Everyone." She said it lightly, the way she said most things, but her eyes stayed on his face a beat too long, and Edmund understood that Ivy — sharp-eyed Ivy, who noticed buttons at four hundred yards — had noticed the thing he had spent three years making sure nobody noticed.

He was not on the ledger. He had never been on the ledger. In three years of civic rituals, of census days and parish days and the annual signing that Marigold always described as "the town writing its own name down so it doesn't lose itself," Edmund had managed to be elsewhere: in the workshop, on a delivery, ill once with a convincingly timed stomach complaint. Tom and Marigold had never pressed. That was the arrangement, unspoken, the shape of the kindness they'd shown the boy they'd taken in after the coach accident — the quiet apprentice, no family to speak of, no history to speak of either, if everyone agreed not to look too closely.

But armies did not agree not to look closely. Armies were nothing *but* looking closely, with ledgers.

"It'll be fine," Edmund said. "I'm registered as Ashgrove's apprentice. Apprentices get listed under their masters. It's — it's ordinary."

It was even mostly true. What he left out was that *ordinary* meant written down, and written down meant examined, and examined meant questions, and questions were the one door he had spent his whole life keeping shut.

Ivy didn't argue. She just reached over and squeezed his wrist, once, hard, and went downstairs to help her mother turn the pantry inventory into a list of things that could be hidden, which was apparently a genre of list Marigold had begun compiling before lunch.

* * *

That evening the summons came up the hill: a corporal with red ears and an apologetic manner, carrying a printed notice that the observatory would be occupied at dawn and that "all persons employed upon the premises" should present themselves for enumeration.

"All persons employed upon the premises," Tom repeated, reading it by lamplight. He said it twice more, tasting it. Then he took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve very thoroughly, put them back on, and looked at Edmund across the kitchen table with an expression Edmund had never seen on him before — not worry exactly, but the careful blankness of a man rearranging furniture in his mind.

"Edmund," Tom said. "Tomorrow, when they ask your name—"

"Ashgrove," Edmund said. Too fast. He heard it land wrong and made himself slow down. "I mean — I'll say I'm your apprentice. Which I am. It's the truth, isn't it? Mostly."

Tom was quiet for a moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs the house settled its bones against the cold, and beneath everything, faint and constant, the master clock tolled the hour — seven strokes rolling down the tower shaft and through the floors, the heartbeat of the building, of the town, of Edmund's entire working life.

"Mostly is a difficult quantity," Tom said gently, "for a soldier to count."

Marigold put her hand flat on the table, over the notice, as though she could press the words out of existence. "Eat your supper," she said, to both of them, in the tone she used for weather and wars alike. "Whatever comes at dawn, it comes to us better fed."

So they ate. And Edmund lay awake in the loft listening to the great clock count out the night — eleven, twelve, one — each stroke falling into the silence like a coin into a well, and thought about names.

His own especially. The one he didn't use. The one he barely remembered, that lived somewhere behind his eyes in a voice he could no longer quite hear, a mother's voice saying it over and over in some room he'd lost the shape of before he was five. Three years ago the Ashgroves had pulled him half-frozen from the wreck of the mail coach and asked him exactly one question — *can you mend clocks?* — and when he'd said yes, or something close enough, they'd simply made room for him, the way you make room for a new gear in a train: measure it, seat it, let it turn.

Now the army wanted everything measured. Every name entered, every person numbered, every discrepancy flagged in a fair, tired hand. Tomorrow at eight o'clock, the observatory where he had found the only belonging he'd ever known would fill with clerks and cross-examinations, and Edmund Ashgrove — which was to say, whoever he actually was — would stand in line with the household that had saved him, and lie to men whose profession was catching lies.

At two in the morning the clock struck twice, steady and unbothered, and Edmund finally slept.

He dreamed of polished boots climbing the tower stairs, and woke to find the sound was real.
