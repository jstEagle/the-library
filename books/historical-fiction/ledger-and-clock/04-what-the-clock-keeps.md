## 4. What the Clock Keeps

The chime mechanism had been sulking for three days, and Edmund had decided, somewhere around the second day, that it was sulking on purpose.

He sat cross-legged in the clockroom with his sleeves rolled to the elbow and a rag over one shoulder, surrounded by the disassembled innards of Hallowmere's master clock like a surgeon at an unusually ambitious operation. The great case loomed above him, oak gone dark with a century of candle smoke, its face pale as a moon behind glass. Below, through the tower windows, the town went about its evening—lamplighters working their way up Cooper's Row, the smell of someone's bread drifting up even this high. Down there, people trusted that bell. It had told them when to rise, when to eat, when to bury their dead for longer than anyone living could remember.

And it was off by four seconds at the hour strike. Four seconds. To anyone else, nothing. To Edmund, it sounded like a man clearing his throat before telling a lie.

"Third hammer spring," he murmured to the mechanism, because talking to clocks was a habit he'd never seen any reason to break. "You're tired. I know. We'll get you right."

The trouble, he'd determined, lay in the chime train—the secondary works that governed the striking of the hours, separate from the going train that kept the time itself. A clock could run perfectly and still strike wrongly; the two were cousins, not twins. The third hammer had been catching on something, hesitating a half-beat before falling, and a hesitation in a bell strike is as audible as a stutter in a prayer.

He'd traced the fault through gear after gear, each wheel polished and true, until his finger stopped at the mounting bracket where the chime train anchored to the case itself. The bracket was loose. Not worn-loose, not age-loose. *Deliberately* loose—one of its four bolts backed out a full turn, sitting proud of its hole like a tooth about to come free.

Edmund frowned at it. That wasn't decay. That was design.

Clockmakers hid things. He knew this the way he knew grain direction and the temper of steel—every apprentice learned that old cases carried secrets. Letters tucked behind dials. Coins dropped into bases for luck. Once, in Master Petch's shop down in the low country, he'd helped move a grandfather case and found a wedding ring soldered inside the plinth, and neither of them had ever learned whose.

But this bracket hadn't been loosened a hundred years ago. The brass threads were bright. Recent work. Within his own memory of the clock, even—and he'd been climbing into this case since he was nine years old, small enough then to fit between the weights and the frame while Tom Ashgrove held the lantern and narrated each wheel's history like scripture.

Which meant somebody had hidden something here recently, and Edmund had a fairly good idea who, and the idea made his hands go still on the bracket.

*No,* he told himself. *Don't. Some doors are closed for reasons.*

But his fingers were already turning the bolt.

It came free with a whisper of oiled thread. Then the second bolt. The bracket swung aside on a hidden hinge—hinged, properly hinged, this was craftsman's work and no mistake—and revealed a cavity in the case wall perhaps eight inches square, lined with cedar to keep the damp out. Someone had built this. Measured it. Cut the panel so the grain matched the surrounding wood so perfectly that Edmund, who prided himself on seeing such things, had looked straight past it a dozen times.

Inside lay a bundle of letters tied with ribbon gone soft as moth-wing, and beneath them, flat and square, a child's tin whistle.

Edmund did not move for a long moment. Above him the pendulum breathed its slow tick—tock, tick-tock, the heartbeat of the whole valley, close enough now that he felt it in his sternum more than heard it.

He took the bundle out. His hands, which did not tremble on escapements, trembled now.

The ribbon gave without resistance, as if it had been waiting to be untied. There were eleven letters, written on cheap paper gone amber at the folds, addressed in a hand that slanted hard to the left, letters crowded together as though the writer feared running out of room—or out of time.

The first was dated the year Edmund turned five.

*My dearest boy,*

*By the time you can read this you will be too big for me to hold, which is the way of things, and I am glad of it even as I grieve it. You are safe. That is the whole of what matters and I will write it again so you believe me: you are SAFE.*

*They came for the ledgers in March. All the debts of all the families called due at once, and the colony gates shut, and Mr. Hollis says the writ will not be lifted in our lifetime. So I have done the thing we planned and I pray God and your father forgive me for doing it alone.*

*A family is coming through on the north road Thursday. Good people. They lost a son of their own the winter before—not to debt, just to fever, just to bad luck—and the wife's eyes when she looked at you were the kindest thing I have seen since the gates closed.*

*Their name is Ashgrove.*

Edmund read it twice. The words refused to arrange themselves into sense, the way a gear train refuses to run when one tooth is cut wrong. He read it a third time slowly, forcing each sentence through his understanding like wire through a drawplate.

*Ashgrove.*

Tom Ashgrove, who taught him to file brass flat by holding Edmund's hand in his own rough one and saying *no, lad, let the tool do the loving.* Marigold, whose kitchen smelled of cinnamon every Sunday whether or not there was anything to celebrate, because Marigold held that some days deserved the smell regardless. Ivy, four years old when they came, who had announced within a week that the quiet new boy was hers now and had never once revised the claim.

His family. His actual, blood-and-bone family, if the letters meant what they said, was—

Were not. Were strangers who became otherwise. Were a grief-stricken couple on a north road who took a debtors' child into their house and their name and never, not once in fourteen years, let slip so much as a syllable that he was anything but theirs.

His mother's hand shook on certain lines. He could see it, the ink wavering where her pen had pressed too hard or too little.

*I will not tell you their names, the ones who hunt us, because a name known is a door opened. I will only say your father owed what he owed honestly, which is more than can be said for the men who bought the paper on it. Do not hate the colony. Hate is heavy cargo and you have far to travel.*

*You are mine. Whatever name you carry, whatever roof stands over you, you are mine, and I am yours, and distance is only a kind of clock—we measure it, but we need not live inside it.*

There was a knock at the tower door, and Edmund nearly put his heart through the floorboards.

"Ed? Supper's going cold and Mam's threatening to send up the porridge she makes when she's cross, which is a punishment the Geneva Convention would forbid." Ivy's voice, muffled through oak. "Also Tom wants to know if the bell's fixed because Mrs. Tallow at the bakery asked him and he said 'ask our Edmund' like a man showing off a prize pig."

"I'll be down shortly," Edmund called, and was relieved to hear his voice come out level.

Boots descended the stairs. Silence returned, except for the pendulum, ticking on, indifferent.

He looked at the letters. Eleven of them. He should stop. He should retie the ribbon, replace the bundle, tighten the bracket, and spend the rest of his life being the Ashgroves' son, which he already was in every way that mattered, wasn't it, wasn't it—

He opened the second letter.

*They have moved us to the stone quarters. My cough is nothing. Do not worry about the cough.*

The third: *I saw the mountains today from the washing line and thought, he is beyond those mountains, and smiled like a fool in front of everyone.*

The fifth mentioned a child given away—a girl this time, another mother's daughter, smuggled out in a laundry cart. The seventh was mostly about a bird that nested in the colony eaves. The ninth grew shorter. The tenth was four lines long.

The eleventh was not in her hand at all. It bore a colony seal and a clerk's stiff script:

*To the family of the debtor E. Vane—*

Edmund's eye snagged on the name and skidded past it, refusing it, the way a horse refuses a fence.

*—informing you that the above died of lung fever on the 14th of February, her account settled by labor, no further obligations outstanding.*

Her account settled by labor. As though she were a ledger line. As though eleven years of writing letters to a son she could not send them to could be summed up as *no further obligations outstanding.*

She'd never posted them. Of course she'd never posted them—there was no post from inside a debtors' colony, no road in or out that didn't pass a gatekeeper counting everything. She'd written them and hidden them somehow, gotten them out somehow, into this case, into the keeping of a clockmaker who must have been a friend or a conspirator or simply a decent man, and she'd trusted the clock to hold them until—

Until when? Until the boy came back to fix it?

Edmund sat very still in the great darkening room while the pendulum counted out the seconds of a life he had apparently misremembered from the ground up. Fourteen years. Fourteen years of Sundays and cinnamon and Tom's laugh and Ivy claiming him, and underneath it all, all along, a woman behind mountains writing *you are mine* into pages that would reach him only after she was dust.

The strange thing—the thing he would turn over for years afterward—was that he did not feel less like an Ashgrove. He had braced for vertigo, for the floor to open, for *they are not your people* to come roaring up out of the letters. Instead what he felt was larger and more confusing: the Ashgroves had chosen him before he could choose them, had loved him across a lie they themselves were keeping, had carried his mother's secret in their house like a coal banked in ash. Marigold's extra cinnamon. Tom's patience with a boy who flinched at men in uniform. Ivy's ferocious, unexamined certainty that he belonged to her.

They had known. All this time. And they had let him be a child about it, unhunted, unworried, because that was the gift they'd decided to give.

Or—they hadn't known, and the letters had been placed here by his mother's ally, and the Ashgroves' love needed no secret explanation at all. He genuinely could not tell. Both stories fit the facts. Both stories fit the cinnamon.

The lamp guttered. Outside, full dark had come down over Hallowmere, and the valley lights were scattered below like a spilled purse.

Edmund retied the ribbon—carefully, the knot his mother's fingers might have pulled—and folded the colony letter separately, because that one he wanted apart from her voice. He reached for the cedar-lined cavity.

Then he stopped.

Because the last item in the box was not a letter. Wedged flat against the back wall was a folded paper of a different stock entirely—official paper, printed, a form of some kind, dense with columns. He drew it out and angled it to the lamp.

A transport manifest. Dated this very season. Berth assignments, names, trades—and across the top, in fresh official ink, a heading that made the room feel suddenly colder than the night pressing at the tower windows:

*REQUISITION ROLL — WINTER GARRISON — HALLOWMERE VALLEY*

Someone had hidden a copy of the army's evacuation ledger inside the clock. Before the requisition had even been announced. Which meant whoever built that panel—whoever had kept his mother's letters safe for fourteen years—was still alive, still in Hallowmere, and already preparing for the soldiers Edmund hadn't yet seen arrive.

Below him, faint through the floor, the town bells of the lesser steeples rang the quarter hour, late as always, chasing the master clock they could never quite catch.

Edmund slid the manifest into his shirt, tightened the bracket bolts, and stood in the ticking dark with his mother's letters against his chest, listening to the stairs for footsteps.

* * *

Down in the kitchen, the Ashgrove household glowed like the inside of a lantern. Marigold had, in fact, made the cross-porridge, and Ivy was defending her bowl with the posture of a soldier, and Tom was telling a story about a customer's cuckoo clock that had begun chiming in Latin, allegedly.

"—and the fellow swears it says *tempus fugit* plain as Sunday, and I says to him, sir, that's not Latin, that's the bird dying of boredom—"

They laughed. Edmund laughed too, from the doorway, holding his mother under his coat against his ribs, and found that the laughter worked, mostly, the way a repaired escapement works—with one tooth slightly wrong that only he could hear.

Marigold looked up at him and her smile did something complicated and brief, there and smoothed away, and Edmund thought: *she knows something. Or she knows nothing. And either way she set a place for me.*

"Sit down, love, before it thickens," she said. "You look like you've been arguing with angels."

"Just the third hammer," Edmund said, and took his chair, and passed Ivy the honey, and told no one anything at all.

Under the floor, under the foundations, under the mountain itself, the old colony roads ran dark toward gates that had supposedly opened for no one in fourteen years. And in his shirt pocket, folded against the heartbeat he'd inherited from a woman named for no ledger anyone in this room had ever signed, the requisition roll waited to be read by lamplight—every berth, every name, every family in Hallowmere accounted for in advance, as though someone had already decided how this valley would end.

Except, Edmund noticed, turning the page by candle after the others slept, one line near the bottom of the final column.

One berth, marked reserved, with no name entered at all.
