## 5. Names, Taken Down

Sergeant Corliss had been taking down names for twenty-two years, and in that time he had learned three things about the practice.

The first was that a name, spoken aloud to a stranger with a pencil, was never quite the same name a person carried inside themselves. People gave you the version they wanted recorded. The miller gave you "Josiah Fenwick, miller" and left out the drinking. The schoolmistress gave you "Clara Voss, schoolmistress" and left out the sister in the asylum down the valley. Everyone edited themselves for the page. Corliss had stopped taking offense at this somewhere around his tenth year of service. He edited himself too.

The second thing he had learned was that the ledger never cared. Paper was patient and paper was hungry, and it did not distinguish between a man's true name and the one he'd invented on the road somewhere between a bad debt and a better hope. It wrote down whatever you fed it and waited, with the terrible neutrality of all official documents, for the day someone would come along and compare it against the truth.

The third thing was that he was tired. Profoundly, bone-deep tired, in the way that had stopped being about sleep years ago and become something more like weather.

He set up the requisition table in the observatory's ground-floor hall at seven o'clock in the morning, because Captain Vane's standing orders said seven o'clock and Corliss had long ago stopped imagining that standing orders bent for frost, for grief, or for the fact that no reasonable person wished to be cross-examined before breakfast. The hall was a fine room for it, he had to admit. High windows, stone flags, the smell of lamp oil and cold brass drifting down from the tower above, where — if the rumors were to be believed — the great master clock of Hallowmere sat in its sickness, ticking wrong for the first time in anyone's memory.

The townsfolk came in out of the gray morning in ones and twos, stamping snow from their boots, clutching their caps and their shawls and, in several cases, the small paper talismans of ordinary life: a rent receipt, a marriage line, a christening record gone soft at the folds. They arranged themselves along the wall in a line that was less a queue than a congregation, and Corliss felt the old familiar weight settle onto his shoulders. Not quite guilt. Not quite duty. Something between, the way dusk is between day and night.

"Name," he said to the first man, "and trade, and how long resident."

"Josiah Fenwick. Miller." A pause, the length of a swallowed word. "Thirty-one years."

Corliss ran his pencil down the ledger column and found the entry. *Fenwick, Josiah, miller, b. 1841, res. 1868.* It matched. It always matched, for the miller. He marked the line, nodded the man through, and watched him go with the particular relief of a man who has checked a lock and found it sound.

"Name and trade."

"Clara Voss. Schoolmistress."

"Resident since?"

"1874." She hesitated, and Corliss, without looking up, said mildly, "The ledger has you at '73, missus. The winter of the bridge collapse. You told the census man you'd come the autumn before, to open the school."

A silence. Then, quieter: "I'd forgot."

"Ledgers don't forget," Corliss said, and corrected the line in his own record to match the book, because the book was the book, and because Clara Voss's extra year in Hallowmere harmed no one and he saw no profit in making her tremble over it. But he noted the hesitation. He noted everything. That was the job. That was the curse of the job.

* * *

By ten o'clock the hall was full and warm with breath and lamplight, and the line had acquired the texture that lines acquire after an hour: shuffling, murmuring, a baby crying somewhere near the door with the tireless conviction of the very young. Corliss's junior clerk — a requisition clerk named Pell, all elbows and eagerness, seconded from the quartermaster's office — worked the second table, taking down the trades and household counts that would determine berth allocations. Pell wrote fast and asked nothing, which Corliss had already decided was the boy's one virtue and would very likely become his one danger.

Corliss worked slow. He asked everything.

It was not cruelty. He wanted that understood, though he doubted anyone in the line understood it, and he doubted further that understanding would have comforted them if they had. But there was a difference, he had learned, between a man who interrogated to trap and a man who interrogated to *see*, and the difference showed in the questions. The trapper asked about discrepancies. The seer asked about the person standing behind them.

"Name and trade."

"Marigold Ashgrove. Seamstress — well, and a bit of everything, sergeant, if we're being honest. Mending, mostly. Baking, when the mood takes me and the flour holds out."

Corliss found the entry. *Ashgrove, Marigold, née Brightwater, seamstress, res. 1861.* He looked up. She was a broad, comfortable woman with flour, in fact, still dusting one sleeve, and she had the kind of face that smiled first and thought after — but the smile just now was doing work, holding something steady behind it, the way a dam holds a river.

"The Ashgrove household," he said. "Old Tom the clockmaker. Yourself. A daughter — Ivy?"

"Ivy, yes. Nineteen going on a force of nature."

"And an apprentice." He turned the ledger page. Turned it back. "I don't find the apprentice."

The smile held, but Corliss watched the eyes behind it go careful, the way a hand goes careful near a hot stove. "Edmund. He came to us — oh, it must be eleven years ago now. Tom took him on to learn the trade. He lives above the shop with us."

"Eleven years," Corliss said, "and never entered in the town ledger."

"Was he meant to be? He's not — he's family, but he's an apprentice, not a ratepayer. We never thought —" She stopped. Started again, more slowly, choosing each word like a seamstress choosing thread. "He was a boy when he came. Quiet as a shadow. Tom signed for him at the guild office; I'd have thought that was paper enough."

"It's paper enough for the guild," Corliss agreed. "The army is another animal, I'm afraid. The transport lists are drawn from the town ledger, and the town ledger has no Edmund Ashgrove in it. Which means that when the berths are assigned, there is no berth with his name on it, because there is no name." He said it plainly, without relish, and watched the words land on her the way he'd watched similar words land on a hundred faces over the years — the small collapse, quickly rebuilt, of a person realizing that the paper world had a hole in it exactly the size of someone they loved.

"He's a boy," Marigold said. "He's been here since he was a boy. He mended our clock, he shovels Mrs. Tallow's walk every morning without being asked, he —" She caught herself. "Sergeant. What do we do?"

"Come back tomorrow," Corliss said. "Bring anything you have — the guild papers, a letter, a christening record if there is one. I'll enter him provisionally. Provisional entries can be contested later, but they can also be honored, and between those two possibilities, madam, a man in my position prefers to keep the door open rather than shut."

She looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw there made her nod once, slowly, the nod of a woman revising an opinion. "Thank you," she said. "You're not what I expected from an army man."

"No one ever says that to the ones it isn't true of," Corliss said, and was rewarded with a real smile before she moved along.

* * *

The Ashgrove apprentice himself came through the line just past noon.

Corliss had been warned by Pell — the boy had been up in the tower all morning, working at the master clock under the eye of a bored sentry, and Pell had developed a fascination with him that Corliss suspected had less to do with suspicion than with the simple fact that Pell was nineteen and the apprentice was perhaps twenty and both of them were young men in a town full of old ones. "Quiet one," Pell had said, leaning over. "Doesn't talk. Hands like a surgeon, though. Watched him lift a gear wheel out of that clock like it was a sleeping cat."

Now the young man stood before the table, cap in his hands, and Corliss performed the small professional act of looking at a person and seeing everything at once. Tall, but stooped, as though he'd spent his life fitting himself into spaces slightly smaller than he was. Hands nicked and stained with brass filings. Eyes that went to the ledger on the table and stayed there a half-second too long — the look of a man reading his own absence.

"Name and trade."

"Edmund. Edmund Ashgrove. Apprentice clockmaker."

"Ashgrove," Corliss repeated, and let the pencil rest. "The ledger has four Ashgroves. Thomas, clockmaker. Marigold, seamstress. Ivy, no trade listed — we'll want one for the transport rolls. It does not have a fifth."

"No, sir."

"You've lived in Hallowmere how long?"

"Eleven years. Since I was nine."

"Eleven years." Corliss said it without accusation, and the boy's shoulders came down perhaps an inch, which told Corliss he'd been braced for accusation the way some people are braced for weather they've lived through before. "Where were you before?"

A pause. Long enough to matter. "I don't remember much of it," Edmund said. "I was young. There was — I came up the valley road. The Ashgroves took me in."

"You came up the valley road," Corliss said, "alone, at nine years old."

"Yes, sir."

"With no papers."

"No, sir."

"With no name anyone wrote down."

The boy's jaw worked. Corliss had seen this exact silence in a hundred faces across twenty-two years, and he knew precisely what it contained: not guilt, exactly, and not a lie, but the whole gray country that lies between the truth and the page. He had stood on that ground himself, once, a long time ago, in another ledger in another town, and he had never entirely left it.

"Edmund," Corliss said, and lowered his voice so the line behind couldn't hear, "I'm going to tell you how this works, because no one has told you, and it's cruel to leave a man ignorant of the machine that's about to pass over him. Tomorrow the berths are assigned from this ledger. The ledger is the truth, as far as the army is concerned — not your memory, not Mrs. Ashgrove's kindness, not eleven years of shoveling walks. If your name is not in the book, you do not exist, and if you do not exist, you do not board the transport. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Edmund said. His voice was steady. Corliss gave him credit for that.

"There is a process. Provisional entry, sworn statement, supporting papers. It exists for exactly your situation — war orphans, mostly, in the last war, and God knows there were enough of those." He pulled a blank form toward him and began, in his small precise hand, to fill in the header. "I'll start one for you now. Bring me what you have. The guild indenture papers, if Tom Ashgrove kept them. A letter. Anything with ink on it older than last week."

Edmund watched the pencil move. "Sergeant," he said, "why are you helping me?"

It was a fair question, and Corliss considered several answers — *because it's my job*, which was true; *because the captain's orders allow it*, which was also true; *because I once stood where you're standing*, which was true and not for sale. He settled on the one that was all three at once.

"Because I take down names," he said, "and I've found in twenty-two years that the page is wrong more often than the person is. The page says what someone wrote. The person is what happened." He slid the half-filled form across the table. "Bring me what happened, Edmund Ashgrove. Or bring me something close enough to it, and I'll do what I can with the difference."

The boy took the form. His hands, for all their surgeon's steadiness, closed around that single sheet of paper as though it were the last dry plank off a wreck, and Corliss looked away, because some things a man should not have to be witnessed doing.

"Next," he called, and the line moved on.

* * *

Pell came by at the end of the day, when the hall had emptied and the lamps were being trimmed, and stood at Corliss's elbow with the particular posture of a young man about to say something he'd been rehearsing.

"The Ashgrove apprentice," Pell said.

"What of him?"

"You flagged him. Provisional entry." Pell turned his requisition book around and showed a column of figures. "Berth allocations came down from the captain's office this afternoon. Civilian berths on the last transport before the valley seals: forty-one. I've run the count three times." He tapped the page. "Households on the ledger, at full strength: forty-six."

Corliss looked at the number for a long moment. Forty-one berths. Forty-six households. He had done this arithmetic before, in other towns, in other years, and he knew exactly what the difference between those two numbers meant and who would be asked to pay it.

"And the provisional entries?" he asked.

"Not counted at all, unless the captain honors them. Which—" Pell hesitated.

"Which he has not said he will."

"No, sir."

Corliss closed his ledger. Outside the high windows the light was going blue over the ridge, and somewhere above them both, in the tower, the great sick clock gave its uneven tick — a half-beat late, a half-beat early, like a heart learning to fail.

"Leave the allocation figures on my table," he said. "And Pell — the Ashgrove household. Mark them down as five souls, not four. If there's going to be an arithmetic done over their heads, I want it done honestly, with every one of them in the sum."

Pell wrote it down. Then, at the door, he paused. "Sergeant? The apprentice. You think he'll get a berth?"

Corliss looked at the blank provisional form, still lying on the table where the boy had left it — no, not left it; *taken* it, and yet somehow its ghost remained, the way a held breath remains in a room.

"I think," Corliss said, "that tomorrow the Ashgroves are going to learn how many berths are left, and I think that household is going to do arithmetic of its own. And I have been in this army long enough to know that when a family starts doing arithmetic with love in the equation, sergeant's clerks would be well advised to stand back and watch what they decide."

He trimmed the last lamp, and above him the clock ticked on, wrong and stubborn, keeping its own counsel about the hour.
