## 6. Berths and Bargains

The news came to Marigold Ashgrove the way most terrible news does: carried by a neighbor who could not wait to be the one carrying it.

"Mrs. Ashgrove! Mrs. Ashgrove!" Hetty Plume came puffing up the lane with her shawl flying out behind her like a banner at a very small, very frightened parade. "They've posted it! At the requisition office — a list! The berths!"

Marigold had been kneading bread. She wiped her hands on her apron with the deliberate slowness of a woman buying herself three extra seconds before the world changed. "Berths," she repeated, as though she were tasting an unfamiliar vegetable. "On the transport."

"On the *last* transport, Mrs. Ashgrove. Before they seal the valley. And there's only—" Hetty caught her breath, which did nothing for the suspense, since her face had already delivered the worst of it. "There's only eleven left. Eleven, for all of Hallowmere that hasn't signed yet."

Eleven. Marigold stood in her kitchen doorway and looked down the lane toward the observatory square, where the great dome caught the morning light and the master clock tower rose above everything like a patient father over squabbling children. The clock was still wrong. Edmund had said so himself last night, in his quiet way, turning his cup in his hands: *It'll take me another week, perhaps more. The chime train's worse than I thought.*

A week. She wondered how many weeks the army intended to give them.

"Thank you, Hetty," she said. "Tell your mother I'll be along directly."

"They're saying families go first," Hetty added, hovering, hungry for confirmation of catastrophe. "Whole households, if they're registered proper. That's why the sergeant's been so particular about the ledger."

"Yes," Marigold said. "I expect he has."

She went back inside and looked at her bread dough, which had risen beautifully, which seemed suddenly like a joke told by someone with no sense of occasion. She punched it down anyway. There was comfort in it. There was always comfort in work, and she suspected there was going to be very little else for some time.

Then she untied her apron, hung it on its hook, put on her good coat, and went to tell her husband.

* * *

Old Tom was in his workshop behind the house, which is where Tom Ashgrove had been for forty years whenever the world required him and frequently when it didn't. He sat at his bench with his spectacles down his nose, repairing the innards of a mantel clock belonging to the Widow Crane, and he did not look up when Marigold came in, because Tom never looked up until he was finished with a thought, and Marigold knew better than anyone alive that you simply waited.

She waited. The workshop smelled of oil and cedar shavings and the particular warm dust of brass, a smell Marigold had been married into for thirty-one years and would know blindfolded in any country on earth. On the wall, six clocks ticked in imperfect agreement, the way the family itself ticked: near enough together, never quite in unison, and somehow going forward regardless.

"Eleven berths," Marigold said, when she judged the thought was finished.

Tom's file stopped moving. That was all. He set it down on the leather pad, took off his spectacles, folded them, and turned on his stool to look at her.

"The whole town?" he said.

"The whole town that hasn't signed."

He nodded slowly, the way he nodded at a mechanism whose fault was worse than the customer feared. "And we're seven."

"We're seven."

They looked at each other across the workshop, husband and wife of three decades, doing sums without speaking them aloud, because they had long ago learned that the Ashgrove arithmetic was always done twice — once in numbers and once in love — and only the second sum ever came out right.

Seven mouths. Five berths, if they were lucky and the registration held and Sergeant Corliss's pen behaved itself. Two names too many. Or rather — and here Marigold felt the old cold thing move in her chest — two names too many, unless somebody gave something up, and every person in this household was constitutionally incapable of giving anything up except on behalf of somebody else.

"There's Ivy," Tom said, counting on his fingers as though Marigold couldn't. "There's you and me. There's young Peter and the baby. And there's—"

He stopped.

Marigold watched him stop. She watched the exact place where her husband's counting ran up against Edmund, the way water runs up against a stone in a stream and parts around it, and she understood, with the terrible clarity available only to mothers, what was about to happen in her kitchen tonight.

"He's ours," Tom said. It wasn't a question. It was barely even a statement. It was more like a man setting a load down on a table and saying *there*, finished, done.

"He's ours," Marigold agreed. "But Tom, love—"

"Don't 'love' me yet." He picked up the Widow Crane's clock, looked into its small open face as though seeking counsel from it, found none, and set it down again. "Five berths, Marigold. I'm sixty-three years old. I've mended every clock in this valley twice over, and I've buried my father and my brother and I've seen a century turn once already, and I can tell you honestly I didn't care much for the company either time." He smiled, that dry, crooked Tom smile that had made her laugh at nineteen and still made her laugh now, mostly. "What use have I got for a new century? Let the boy have mine."

"You'll say this in front of everyone?"

"I'll say it in front of everyone."

"And when they argue?"

Tom Ashgrove looked at his wife with mild, immovable eyes, the eyes of a man who had once held a mainspring under tension for four hours without blinking because the spring was temperamental and so was he.

"They'll argue," he agreed. "That's what makes us a family instead of a committee."

* * *

Supper that evening was lamb and barley and the last of the bottled damsons, and Marigold cooked it the way she cooked everything in a crisis: too much, and with the good plates. If the world was going to end, let it end noticing that the Ashgroves had used the good plates.

Edmund came in late from the tower, smelling of oil and cold iron, his hands stained to the second knuckle. He washed at the basin without being asked, which he'd learned to do years ago, and took his place at the table, which he'd learned even earlier, and Marigold watched him do both things and felt her heart do its usual complicated folding.

Peter, nine years old and loud as a church bell, was holding forth about the soldiers. "Corporal Wills says the horses are coming down next week and they'll stable them in the *churchyard*, and Mr. Pellowe says over his dead body, and Corporal Wills said that could probably be arranged—"

"Peter." Marigold, mildly.

"—and Ivy says if they touch the yew tree she'll—"

"*Peter.*"

He subsided into his barley with the air of a boy conserving ammunition.

Ivy came in last, snow in her dark hair, cheeks bright from the cold, carrying that energy about her that she'd carried since she was a toddler, the energy of a girl permanently mid-rescue. She'd been at the requisition office all afternoon, Marigold knew, "helping" — which in Ivy's language meant standing between frightened neighbors and soldiers' clipboards like a small fierce wall. She dropped into her chair, seized the ladle, served herself, served the baby, served Edmund before serving herself properly again, and only then looked up and read the room.

"Well," she said. "Somebody's died or somebody's decided something. Which is it?"

Marigold looked at Tom. Tom looked at his plate. And then, being Tom, he pushed back his chair slightly, laid his napkin beside his knife and fork with the neatness of a man settling accounts, and spoke.

"Eleven berths left on the transport," he said. "Five will cover this household. Six of us at table." He paused, and Marigold saw him choose not to soften it, because Tom believed — wrongly, in her opinion, but devotedly — that his family deserved plain dealing. "So one of us stays. And it's going to be me."

The silence lasted three full ticks of the kitchen clock.

"That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard," said Ivy.

"Ivy," said Marigold.

"It *is*. Da, you're sixty-three, the winters up the pass alone would kill you by February—"

"I've wintered sixty-three of them already, girl. I'm experienced."

"—and you can't even carry the coal scuttle upstairs anymore without resting on the landing—"

"That landing rest is *strategic*."

"—and what exactly do you propose to *do* up there, mend clocks for the crows?" Ivy had gone from seated to upright without appearing to traverse the distance, hands flat on the table, leaning into her father like weather. "No. No, it should be me. Nobody needs me for anything, I'm not apprenticed, I'm not married, the cellars need organizing but Ivy-from-a-distance can't organize them anyway, and I'm twenty-two and strong as an ox and I don't even *like* barley—"

"Ivy." This was Edmund. Quietly. The first word he'd spoken since sitting down, and it landed in the room like a coin dropped in a well. "Nobody's deciding anything yet."

"Somebody's decided something," Ivy said. "Da decided. You can see it on him. He's got his ledger-face on."

"I haven't got a ledger-face," said Tom, who absolutely had a ledger-face.

And then Peter, bless him and blast him, said the thing Marigold had been bracing against since the bread dough that morning. "Well, it can't be Edmund," he announced, with the magnificent tactlessness of nine. "Edmund's not even *on* the list properly. Corliss flagged him. Everybody knows. So really there's five of us needing berths and Edmund needing a miracle, so actually, Da, if you stay, then Edmund could have *your* berth, and nobody has to fight about it at all—"

"*Peter.*"

"What? It's just *sums*!"

And that was the moment Marigold looked across the table at Edmund and saw him go still — not offended-still, not frightened-still, but the stillness of a clockwork part coming under tension, the stillness she had learned to read the way she read weather off the fells. His hands, those careful engraver's hands that could cut a letter half the height of a grain of rice, had stopped moving entirely.

"I couldn't take it," Edmund said. "Tom. Your berth. I couldn't."

"You can and you will," said Tom, "or we shall sit at this table until one of us dies of stubbornness, and I warn you fairly, boy, I'm older and I've had more practice."

"You've a trade here. A *life* here. The valley knows you—"

"The valley will be full of cavalry by Candlemas." Tom's voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it heavier. "Listen to me, Edmund. Thirty years ago a woman walked up my lane with a child asleep on her shoulder and a story that wouldn't have fooled a constable, and I let her walk away and kept the child. Never asked her name. Never wrote it down. Best decision I ever made, and I've made it again every morning since, waking up to find you at my bench stealing my files." He cleared his throat, gruffly, as though the emotion were a bit of shavings caught somewhere inconvenient. "You're not a favor we did, lad. You're the interest it paid. So you'll take the berth, and you'll write to us from wherever the century puts you, and that is the end of the arithmetic."

"But it isn't the end of it," Marigold said softly.

Everyone looked at her. Even the baby, who was advanced.

Because Marigold had been doing her own sum all through supper, quietly, beneath the lamb and the shouting, and hers had come out differently than Tom's. Hers had included a line item no one else had entered: the way Edmund had gone white at *Corliss flagged him*. The way his hands stilled under tension. The way he had avoided signing the town ledger for eleven years with a politeness so perfect it had always, in retrospect, looked less like modesty than like a locked drawer.

Her son — and he was her son, whatever paper said, whatever blood said, whatever the letters she sometimes suspected existed inside that clock case might say — was keeping a secret. Had always kept a secret. And a secret, in the winter of 1899, in a valley being counted head by head by men with clipboards, was worth more than a berth. Or cost more. Marigold genuinely could not tell which, and that frightened her more than the army did.

"Tomorrow," she said, rising to gather the plates, because in the Ashgrove household profound moments were traditionally interrupted by dishwater, "we will all go to the office together. All six of us. And we will stand in front of Sergeant Corliss, and we will see what the ledger says, and we will decide as a family — *as a family*, Peter, which means no more sums at table — what gets written where."

"And if it comes down to one name?" Ivy asked, still standing, still bristling.

Marigold looked at her daughter, at her husband, at the boy at the end of the table who was studying his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger.

"Then it comes down to one name," she said, "and God help whichever of us is holding the pen."

Later, when the dishes were done and the lamps were low and the household breathed in its separate sleeps, Marigold stood a while at the window looking up at the observatory hill, where one light still burned in the clock tower. Edmund's light. Working late again on a clock that might, if he failed, cost the army its schedule and Edmund his neck; working late on a clock whose case, she had begun to suspect, held more than gears.

She thought of a woman on a snowy lane thirty years ago, and of the weight of a sleeping child, and of how some debts are never entered in any ledger at all, and yet come due all the same.

"Whatever you're carrying up there," she murmured to the light in the tower, "you won't carry it alone. Not while I've breath to argue with you."

Below her, in the dark, the kitchen clock ticked on — wrong, like its great parent on the hill, but faithful, faithful, faithful — and tomorrow, Marigold knew, the whole family would stand before the ledger, and the arithmetic would begin in earnest.
