## 7. Letters Not Meant for Me

The house had gone quiet in the way houses do when they have finally stopped pretending anyone is still awake. Edmund sat at the workbench in the corner of his attic room with a single candle burning, the letters stacked before him like a small, terrible parcel he had been asked to deliver to himself.

He had not meant to read them tonight. He had told himself he would wait until the clock was mended, until there was time and privacy and some steadier version of himself to do the reading. But he had said that every night for three nights now, and the bundle had sat in his drawer like a second heartbeat, and tonight the wind was rattling the shutters and sleep was clearly out of the question.

Seven letters. Tied with ribbon gone the color of old tea. His mother's hand on each one — *For my son, when he is tall enough to ask* — and beneath it, dates from a year Edmund had never thought much about: 1886, 1887. He had been four years old. Five. Old enough, apparently, to be given away, and young enough not to remember giving.

He picked up the first letter. His fingers were steady. That surprised him; he had spent ten years training his hands to be steady over small and valuable things, and apparently they could not tell the difference between an escapement spring and a dead woman's handwriting.

---

*My darling boy,*

*They have moved us again, further up the valley where the fog sits all day like a debt collector. I will not describe the place because you will read this someday and I refuse to let you carry pictures of it. Suffice to say your mother has learned to mend forty things she never wished to know how to mend.*

*I want you to know that you were not lost. Nothing about you has ever been lost. You were placed, as carefully as I know how, which was more carefully than anything else I have ever done in a careless life.*

Edmund set the letter down.

The candle guttered in the draft from the window. Downstairs, faint through two floors of old timber, he could hear Tom Ashgrove's snore, a great comfortable sawing sound that Ivy swore could be heard from the churchyard in a west wind. Marigold slept beside him. Ivy slept in her narrow room with her boots on half the time, so she claimed, ready for whatever the night might require of her.

His family. Asleep above him and below him and down the hall, entirely unaware that the boy in the attic was currently being taken apart by paper.

He read on.

---

*You are with people called Ashgrove now. I chose them the way one chooses a lock — by asking around until someone mentioned a family that takes apprentices without questions. A clockmaker's household, they say. Warm. Loud. The wife sings while she cooks and cannot carry a tune in a bucket, but no one has the heart to tell her.*

*I liked that detail best. I hope it is still true when you read this. If you have never heard her sing badly, then something has gone wrong, and you should go and make her laugh until she does.*

Edmund laughed. It came out of him sideways, cracked down the middle, half sob and entirely involuntary, and he clapped a hand over his mouth as though the sound might wake the whole street.

Marigold did sing while she cooked. Badly. Gloriously. She sang hymns at twice their intended speed and folk songs at half, so that the words arrived in the wrong order, and every morning for ten years Edmund had eaten porridge to this soundtrack and thought, *this is what a home sounds like,* without once wondering why a woman who owed him nothing had decided to feed him anyway.

She had known. Not everything — perhaps not even much — but she had known there were questions worth not asking, and she had built a whole life around not asking them. Ten years of deliberate kindness, performed daily, disguised as ordinary life.

And he had repaid her by lying to her face about a chime mechanism.

---

The third letter was shorter. The handwriting had begun to slope, the letters crowding each other like passengers on a full coach.

*The colony's books are wrong, my love. I need you to understand that, whatever else you learn. They write down debts that were never agreed and payments that were never made, and the ledger swells while we shrink. Mr. Hollis says the law is the law. I have begun to think the law is simply a ledger kept by whoever holds the pen.*

Edmund read that sentence four times.

*A ledger kept by whoever holds the pen.* He looked across the room at his own tools, laid out in their felt roll — the gravers, the punches, the fine files. The instruments of a man whose entire trade was making marks that meant exactly what they said. Somewhere below, in the clerk's office at the observatory, sat the town ledger with its columns of names, and somewhere beyond the ridge sat an army requisition officer with a fresh pen.

He thought of Sergeant Corliss's tired eyes going over the rolls, flagging discrepancies. He thought of Captain Vane, whom he had not yet met but whose reputation had come down the hill ahead of him like weather.

*Whoever holds the pen.*

He held a pen. He was, God forgive him, rather good with one.

He pushed the thought away the way one pushes away a hot pan — quickly, and knowing the burn would find him later.

---

The fifth letter was dated nearly a year after the fourth, and the gap between them said more than the writing did. Her hand had grown thin. The ink was watery, stretched, as if she had been rationing it.

*My dearest Edmund,*

*The doctor here is a barber with opinions. I tell you this only so you will not wonder, later, whether I went suddenly. I am going slowly, which is worse for me and better for the story, since it gives me time to say what needs saying.*

*Your father died owing eleven pounds to men who invented the sum. I have paid it in installments of health ever since, and the balance remains stubbornly unpaid, because that is how such balances are designed. Do not hate them. Hate is a heavy thing to carry up a mountain, and you will have enough to carry.*

*What I want you to know, when you are tall enough to ask: the Ashgroves took you in for nothing. No fee, no arrangement, no promise of labor beyond what any apprentice owes. I offered everything I had, which was nothing, and Mrs. Ashgrove — I corresponded with her once, through a cousin of the midwife — wrote back four lines. I will give them to you exactly, because they were the last generous sentence spoken to me in this valley:*

*"Send him up. We've a spare bed and a short temper between us, and the bed's the bigger of the two."*

Edmund put the letter face-down on the bench and pressed both palms flat against his eyes.

*We've a spare bed and a short temper between us.*

That was Tom Ashgrove to the bone, though Edmund had always assumed the phrase belonged to Tom alone. Apparently it ran in the family — or rather, apparently it had adopted him along with the rest of him.

He thought of the day they had collected him from the carrier's cart, a five-year-old clutching a canvas bag and a wooden toy boat he still owned and would deny owning under oath. Ivy, aged seven, had inspected him critically and announced, "He's got a smudge." Then she had licked her thumb and removed it, and that, as far as Edmund knew, was the founding charter of their entire relationship: she cleaned him up and bossed him about, and he let her, and neither of them had ever seen reason to renegotiate terms.

None of them were his blood. He had suspected, in the vague way one suspects winter is coming — abstractly, and then all at once, with the frost already on the glass. But suspicion was a coat you could try on and take off. This was different. This was stitched into him now, seam by seam, letter by letter, and there was no taking it off.

His mother had died in a debtors' colony up-valley, in the fog, paying installments of health on a debt that had been invented. And the army was coming to hold pens over new ledgers of their own.

---

The last letter was barely a page. Dated March 1887 — six weeks, he calculated, before the Ashgroves' reply had brought him up the mountain.

*If these reach you, someone loved you enough to keep them safe, and I am glad of it, and jealous of it, and glad of it again, in that order.*

*Do not look for my grave. It is unmarked and I have arranged to be annoyed about this in advance. Instead, do this: keep good time. Whatever else you become, be a man whose word runs true as his clocks. It is the only inheritance I have to leave you and I leave it gladly, having tested it thoroughly myself and found it wanting in the parent but serviceable in the child.*

*All my love, past the end of the page and past the end of the pen,*

*Your mother,*

*Rosamund*

Rosamund.

He said it aloud, once, very quietly, testing it the way one tests a repaired gear — turning it slowly, listening for catches. Rosamund. He had carried the name around inside himself for twenty-two years without knowing it existed. It fit. That was the unbearable part. It fit like a key that had been cut for a lock he'd been standing next to his whole life.

He tied the ribbon back around the letters with hands that did not tremble — his mother's other inheritance, apparently, arriving right on schedule — and hid the bundle again behind the loose panel in the clock case, where he had found it, where it had waited thirty years for a tall-enough son.

Then he blew out the candle and lay down and did not sleep at all, which at least was honest work.

* * *

Breakfast smelled of porridge, burnt toast, and Marigold's singing — this morning it was a lament about a drowned fisherman rendered at the tempo of a jig, which gave the drowning an oddly festive quality.

"Sit, sit," Marigold said, sliding a bowl at him. "You look like something the cat discussed and rejected."

"I've been up late with the clock."

"You're always up late with the clock." She peered at him. "Is that a compliment to the clock or an accusation?"

"A progress report," Edmund said. "It's coming along. The chime train needed cleaning, and the third wheel's worn its pivot, but I've got the parts roughed out. Another few days, perhaps less."

Every word of it true. Every word of it arranged, like flowers in a vase, to conceal the fact that he had spent the night reading letters instead of filing brass, and that his hands knew it, and that the pivot rod sitting half-turned on his bench upstairs was evidence in a trial he hadn't been summoned to yet.

Tom Ashgrove looked up from his newspaper. "Vane's man came round yesterday evening. Requisition office wants an inventory of the tower works by week's end. Brass, bell metal, the lot." He folded the paper with a crackle. "Man seemed keen on the bell itself."

"The bell's older than the army," Ivy said, buttering toast with the violence of a woman settling an argument nobody had started yet. "Let them inventory it. Inventorying isn't taking."

"Inventories," said Tom mildly, "have a way of becoming takings. I've seen it with sheep."

Marigold refilled Edmund's bowl without asking, which she did whenever she thought he was too thin, which was always, which was a system that had kept him well fed for two decades through sheer administrative error. Their eyes met over the ladle. Something in her face — a softness, a question, a door left deliberately unlocked — suggested that mothers, real or assigned or self-appointed, can smell a sleepless night the way dogs smell rain.

"You'd tell me," she said lightly, too lightly, "if the clock were trouble beyond your skill?"

"It's not the clock that's the trouble," Edmund said, and heard, too late, how much truth he'd just handed her, and scrambled to bury it. "The garrison traffic shakes the foundations. Everything in that tower is loose in its seat."

"Hm," said Marigold, in the tone of a woman filing something away in a drawer of her own.

Ivy kicked him under the table — not hard, just administratively. Later, her expression said. We will discuss this later, and you will lie to me less competently than you lied to her.

Edmund ate his porridge. Outside, on the ridge road, he could hear the regular crunch of boots — the scouts doing their morning count of chimneys, counting smoke, counting heads, counting names against a ledger he had not signed.

Another few days, he had said. He had until week's end to produce an inventory of the tower, until the transports came to empty the valley, and until then he was a man of no record living on borrowed kindness, holding a pen, holding his mother's name, and lying at breakfast to the only family he had.

The clock upstairs ticked on without him, patient as a creditor.

He stood, thanked Marigold for the porridge, kissed the top of her head — which startled them both, as he rarely did it, and never without cause — and went to see what the day would cost him.

Behind him, he heard Ivy say quietly, "Right. What did you find in that clock?"

And he kept walking, because the answer was a bundle of letters, and the truth of them was not his alone to spend — not anymore, and possibly not ever.
