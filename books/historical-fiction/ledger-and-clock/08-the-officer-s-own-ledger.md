## 8. The Officer's Own Ledger

Captain Ambrose Vane had three rules for auditing a requisition, and he kept them the way other men kept scripture.

The first was that paper never lied, though it was frequently lied to. The second was that sentiment in a subordinate was not a virtue but a leak, and leaks were found faster by smell than by inspection. The third—the one he'd carved into his own thinking like a maker's mark—was that every ledger, however humble, told you more about the hand that kept it than about the goods it counted.

He thought of these rules as he rode down the ridge road into Hallowmere on a morning so crisp it seemed the valley itself had been requisitioned by winter. Frost silvered the orchards. Smoke rose from chimneys in tidy, obedient columns. Somewhere ahead, a bell was ringing the hour with a voice so round and certain that Vane found himself checking his pocket watch against it out of pure reflex—and finding, to his mild irritation, that his watch was eleven seconds slow.

"Good bell," said Sergeant Corliss, riding at his left.

"Mm." Vane closed the watch. "We'll see."

Corliss gave him the look—a small, tired sideways glance that Vane had catalogued months ago as *the sergeant disagrees but is too sensible to say so*. It was, in its way, one of the most honest instruments in the entire survey company. Vane trusted it more than most thermometers.

"You disapprove of something already, Sergeant?"

"No, sir."

"You have the face of a man disapproving of something."

"It's a cold morning, sir. Faces do what they like."

Vane almost smiled. Almost. He rationed smiles the way the army rationed boots—one pair per man per year, issued only when the old ones were demonstrably ruined. "Tell me again what the town clerk gave you."

"Full register, sir. Every resident, birth, trade, household. Kept for—" Corliss consulted a folded paper, though Vane suspected he knew it by heart, "—going on sixty years. Clerk's name is Pellowe. Nervous as a wet cat, but his figures balance."

"Nervous men who keep balanced figures are the most useful kind," Vane said. "It's the confident ones who hide things. Confidence requires effort, and effort leaves traces." He nodded toward the town spreading below them along the frozen river: slate roofs, a market square, and above it all, crowning the hill like a hat worn slightly too proudly, the observatory with its long white dome and its square stone tower. "And the clock? The famous one?"

"The master clock, sir. Keeps the town time. Railway, church, everything sets by it. There's a family of clockmakers keeps it running—Ashgroves. Old man, his wife, two daughters, and an apprentice."

"An apprentice." Vane filed the word away the way he filed all words, in a drawer labeled *later*. "Registered?"

"I—believe so, sir."

*Believe.* Another word for the drawer. Vane said nothing. A good auditor learned early that the most valuable findings were never announced; they were carried quietly, like coins sewn into a coat lining, until the moment came to spend them.

They clattered into Hallowmere just as the bell finished its last stroke. Eleven o'clock. His watch, corrected, agreed to within a second. Vane looked up at the tower with the frank appraisal he'd give any piece of equipment: sound stonework, louvres recently painted, dial clean, hands true. Somebody loved this machine. That was worth knowing too. Loved things were defended things, and defended things could be leveraged.

* * *

The requisition office had been installed in the town clerk's own front room, which struck Vane as either efficient or cruel depending on how much you believed clerks had feelings. Clerk Pellowe sat behind his own desk like a man hosting burglars for tea, offering biscuits with trembling courtesy while soldiers stacked crates of army forms against his wife's good china cabinet.

"Captain Vane." The clerk rose, knocked his knee on the desk, recovered, and produced a bow that began as civilian deference and ended somewhere near prayer. "We're honored. Entirely honored. May I—tea? We have tea."

"No tea." Vane set his gloves on the desk in a neat parallel, which had the effect, he'd noticed over the years, of making rooms feel audited even before he opened his mouth. "The rolls, Mr. Pellowe. All of them. And your working papers since the announcement."

"Yes. Yes, of course." Pellowe fetched a leather-bound volume the size of a paving slab and laid it down with both arms, wheezing. "The great register, Captain. Every soul in Hallowmere. My father kept it before me, and his before him. Births, trades, households—all entered in fair hand, all witnessed. You'll find us a lawful town. An *orderly* town."

"I don't doubt it." Vane opened the cover. The first page bore a date from 1841 and handwriting like copperplate embroidery. He turned pages slowly—not reading yet, merely listening, the way another man might run a finger along a fence to feel for loose boards.

Behind him, he heard Corliss take up his station by the window, and heard Pellowe hover, and hover, and finally ask, in a voice pitched between pride and terror, whether the Captain would care to hear the history of the register.

"No."

Silence. Then, smaller: "It's rather a good history."

"Pellowe." Vane did not look up. "In four days this valley belongs to the Northern Army, and every person in it will be on a transport list with a berth number. What I need from you is accuracy. What I do not need from you is charm. Do we understand each other?"

"Yes, Captain. Sorry, Captain. Only—it's just that people aren't numbers, sir, if you take my meaning, and some of them are frightened, and—"

"They should be frightened accurately, then." Vane turned a page. "Fear with correct figures attached can be relieved. Fear without figures becomes rumor, and rumor becomes a riot, and riots are expensive. I am here to see that everyone in this town is counted correctly. That is not cruelty, Mr. Pellowe. It is arithmetic performed kindly. The kindest thing anyone will do for this valley this winter."

Pellowe blinked at him as though trying to decide whether he'd been comforted or threatened. Vane considered this a reasonable confusion. Most of his conversations landed in both drawers at once.

For an hour he worked through the register in silence, and it was—there was no other word for it—beautiful work. Entries cross-referenced. Trades verified against tax rolls. Deaths ruled in neat red marginalia. Whoever maintained this book had done it with a devotion bordering on religion, and Vane felt the professional pleasure of a man finding a well-cut gear where he'd expected slop.

Which was precisely why the loose board bothered him so much.

Three households showed amendments in the past decade—additions, corrections—each initialed by Pellowe and each perfectly proper. But there was a fourth place where the ink changed character. Not falsified, exactly. Nothing so vulgar. Simply... smoothed. The way a mason smooths mortar over a joint he doesn't want inspected.

"Ashgrove," Vane said aloud, tasting it.

"The clockmakers, sir," Corliss supplied from the window. "Good people. The whole town swears by them."

"The whole town swearing by someone is usually evidence of nothing except that the whole town has been asked." Vane tapped the entry. "Household of Thomas Ashgrove, master clockmaker. Wife Marigold. Daughters Ivy and Rose. Apprentice—" he paused. "Apprentice Edmund Ashgrove. Entered... when?"

"Nine years back, sir, when the boy was took in. There's a note—apprentice indenture witnessed by the parish."

"Show me the indenture."

Pellowe went pale in a new and interesting shade. "I—that would be in the parish box, Captain, and the parish records went to the district office years ago, it's all been centralized, you see, after the reforms—"

"So the boy's presence in this town rests on a line in a book and a document nobody present has ever seen." Vane closed the register gently, which somehow made the gesture worse than slamming it. "Sergeant. When you conducted preliminary registration, did you personally verify this apprentice?"

Corliss was quiet a moment. Vane watched him decide to tell the truth, and noted—with the small cold satisfaction he allowed himself perhaps twice a year—that the sergeant chose honesty. "No, sir. Household vouched for him. Neighbors vouched for the household. I let it pass."

"You let it pass." Vane stood, buttoned his coat, and let the silence do a full turn of the room before he added, mildly: "Note it for follow-up. Not an accusation, Sergeant. A thread. I pull threads because they're cheap to pull and expensive to leave."

"Yes, sir." And there was that look again—the disagreeing face—but deeper now, and Vane filed that too, under a heading he didn't particularly enjoy writing even in his own head: *my sergeant has begun taking sides.*

Sentiment. A leak. He'd deal with it later, kindly, in the manner of a man tightening a valve before it became a geyser.

* * *

The observatory smelled of oil and cold stone and, faintly, of apples—someone had been storing the autumn crop in the cellar stairwell, and Vane approved; waste was a moral failing.

A girl met them in the entrance hall—young, freckled, sleeves rolled, a smear of brass polish across one cheekbone like war paint applied by an enthusiastic child.

"You'll be the army, then," she said.

"I'll be Captain Vane. This is Sergeant Corliss. And you'll be?"

"Ivy Ashgrove." She wiped her hand on her apron before offering it, then seemed to remember he was a captain and converted the handshake into something halfway to a curtsy, which ended up resembling neither and both. "You've come about the requisition notice."

"You've come to conclusions quickly, Miss Ashgrove."

"Rose read the notice out at breakfast. Twice, because Dad made her the second time in case she'd dramatized it." She led them down a corridor whose walls were hung with star charts going soft at the corners. "You'll want to see the clockroom. Everyone does. It's the best room in the county and I'll thank you not to melt it."

"We haven't said anything about melting anything."

"You're the army, you've got a railway spur coming up the valley, and the clock case alone is four hundredweight of seasoned brass." She pushed open a door. "Everyone always does the sums eventually."

The clockroom stopped even Vane at the threshold—and Vane was a man who prided himself on never being stopped by anything, including weather, superior officers, and once, memorably, a bull.

The master clock filled the far wall: a case of dark walnut rising fifteen feet, glazed panels revealing a movement of brass and steel so intricate it looked less built than grown, wheel answering wheel down into the depths like a fountain frozen mid-fall. Three dials drove the observatory, the church, and the railway. A system of weighted rods ran off into the building's bones, carrying the seconds outward the way arteries carry blood.

Beside it, on a ladder, stood a young man with a lamp and a tray of tools, so absorbed in the escapement that he hadn't heard the door. He was thin, careful-handed, maybe nineteen, with the particular stillness of people who spend their lives around machinery that punishes haste.

"That's Edmund," Ivy said. "Our apprentice. Don't startle him; he bites when startled. Well. Frowns."

"Miss Ashgrove," Vane said, "has anyone from the requisition office spoken to your family regarding the disposition of this instrument?"

"Disposition." She rolled the word around like a boiled sweet she suspected was soap. "That's an army word, that is. Words like *disposition* and *requisition* and *inventory* are how you all say *taking* without having to taste it."

"Miss Ashgrove—"

"He's nearly finished repairing it, you know." She crossed her arms. "Eleven seconds fast, it had been running, all winter, and Dad's eyes aren't what they were for the fine work. Edmund's had it apart down to the fourth wheel. Whatever you're planning to do with it—write it down, weigh it, argue about it—remember that what's keeping every train and funeral and christening in this valley on time is that boy's patience. Brass melts. Patience doesn't grow back."

Vane regarded her. Nineteen, perhaps twenty. No fear in her anywhere, which meant either courage or ignorance, and her eyes said courage, and courage in the unregistered was a complication.

Because he had already decided, of course. He'd decided on the ridge road, watching the smoke rise in obedient columns. The Northern Army needed brass fittings for the munitions works at Harrowgate—bushings, bearing stock, gear blanks—and here lay four hundredweight of exactly the right alloy doing nothing all day but telling farmers when to eat lunch. Sentiment said the clock was a heart. Arithmetic said the clock was inventory. Vane had survived twenty years of soldiering by preferring arithmetic, and he intended to go on surviving.

But he was also, despite himself, a connoisseur. He walked the length of the case slowly. The joinery was flawless. The glazing sat true. Even the dust had been arranged with intent.

"Your apprentice," he said. "How long has he been with you?"

"Nine years."

"Where was he before?"

"Here," said Ivy.

"Before here."

"With us." Her chin came up a fraction. "With the Ashgroves, Captain. Where else would he be?"

It was a well-made answer. Vane had spent a career listening to answers, and he knew a load-bearing wall when he leaned on one. Behind them, faintly, the young man on the ladder had gone very still—stillness of a different quality than concentration. Vane didn't turn. You didn't turn toward a nervous animal; you simply mentioned, in a level voice, that you knew the animal existed.

"Sergeant," he said pleasantly, "make a note. The Ashgrove apprentice is to attend the registration office tomorrow morning. Routine verification. Nine years in a town and no paperwork anyone can produce—I'm sure there's an innocent explanation. Innocent explanations are my favorite kind. They come with documents attached."

He heard the ladder shift. Heard the small, precise click of a tool being set down on a tray with exaggerated gentleness, the way one sets down anything one is afraid of dropping—or throwing.

"And the clock itself, Miss Ashgrove," Vane continued, producing his notebook, "is hereby entered on the seizure schedule as army property, pending valuation. Its brass will be assessed against garrison requirements. If the assessment finds a better use for it as a clock than as munitions fittings, I will be the first to say so."

"You won't," Ivy said. "Will you."

"No," Vane said, with the honesty he reserved for people too young to matter politically. "I almost certainly won't."

Something moved behind her freckles—not tears; fury, banked and bright—and she looked past him at the great dark case, at the pendulum swinging its slow golden arc inside, keeping faith with every second in the valley regardless of what the century intended to do about it.

"Well," she said at last. "Then I hope you're very proud of your sums."

"I am," said Vane. "It's the one thing I've never had cause to be ashamed of."

He clicked the notebook shut. But as they withdrew down the corridor of fading star charts, he permitted himself one backward glance through the clockroom door—at the young man on the ladder, who had resumed his work with hands that were almost steady, and at the walnut case itself, and at the small square shadow near the base of the movement where the wood grain ran wrong. Where a panel, perhaps, had been pried loose and seated again by someone skilled enough to make it look untouched.

Almost untouched.

Vane smiled his twice-yearly smile, all to himself, and wrote a single word in his notebook, underlined twice:

*Tonight.*
