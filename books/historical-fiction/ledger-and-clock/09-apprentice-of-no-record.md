## 9. Apprentice of No Record

The summons came at half past seven in the morning, delivered by a private so young his uniform still had the creases of its folding. He stood in the Ashgroves' doorway holding a paper as though it might bite him, and announced to Marigold — loudly, as if volume were a form of authority — that one "Edmund Ashgrove, apprentice" was required at the requisition office in the observatory's west wing at nine o'clock sharp.

"Required for what?" Marigold asked pleasantly, in the tone she used for shopkeepers who tried to sell her wilted greens.

The private consulted his paper as if it might contain an answer. It did not. "Registration review, ma'am."

"I see," said Marigold, and shut the door with the gentleness of a woman who wanted very much to slam it.

Edmund had been expecting this since Sergeant Corliss first looked up from the ledger three days ago and frowned at the gap where Edmund's name ought to have been. Expecting it hadn't helped. He spent the ninety minutes between breakfast and nine o'clock at the workbench, ostensibly truing a pallet arbor, actually doing nothing whatsoever with his hands except keeping them from shaking.

"You could eat something," Ivy said from the doorway. She'd come down twice already under various pretexts — once with bread, once with tea, once with a scarf she claimed he'd need, though the morning was mild.

"I'm not hungry."

"You're never hungry when you're frightened. You go quiet and your hands get busy." She came over and put her palm flat on his shoulder blade, between his shoulder and his spine, the way she used to when they were children and he'd fallen out of the pear tree. "Whatever they ask you, you tell the truth or you tell nothing. Never lie badly. Bad lies are how people end up in real trouble."

"When have I ever lied badly?"

"The time you said the clockroom cat ate your essay."

"That cat *did* eat my essay."

"It was a clockroom. There was no cat." She squeezed his shoulder once and let go. "Nine o'clock. Don't be early. Early men look guilty."

* * *

He arrived at eight minutes past nine, which felt like the correct amount of late for an innocent man.

The observatory's west wing had been transformed in a week into something between a parish office and a customs house. The star charts were gone from their rollers; trestle tables stood in rows; clerks scratched away at registers with the industrious rhythm of mice in a granary. The smell of ink and cold tea hung over everything. At the far end, behind a desk too fine for its surroundings — some professor's, once — sat Sergeant Corliss, reading.

Corliss looked up when Edmund approached, and there was none of the officer's polish about him. He had the face of a man who had been rained on for forty years and had stopped minding. His whiskers were grey at the edges and his eyes were tired in a way that wasn't unkind.

"Edmund Ashgrove," Corliss read aloud, though he didn't need to. "Apprentice clockmaker. Sit down."

There was one chair, placed facing the desk like a penitent's stool. Edmund sat.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"My ledger entry is missing."

Corliss raised his eyebrows slightly, as if Edmund had passed some small examination. "That's the shape of it. Your entry is missing, and entries don't go missing by accident. Paperwork doesn't lose itself. So either somebody failed to record you, or somebody declined to be recorded, or—" he turned the ledger around and laid it open on the desk, "—there's a fourth possibility we'll come to."

Edmund looked down at the great book. He knew it well enough by sight: every name in Hallowmere, every birth and trade, in the clerk's careful copperplate going back sixty years. The Ashgroves' page was dense and domestic — Thomas Ashgrove, clockmaker, married Marigold Fenwick; Ivy born such-and-such a year; Tom junior, dead of the fever at four days old, entered in the same loving hand as everything else. And then, eleven years ago, a gap where a line should have been, and after it, in different ink, added later and less officially:

*Edmund — apprentice — taken in.*

No surname of origin. No birthplace. No date of birth. Just *taken in*, like a stray dog, which was more or less how it had happened.

"Eleven years in this household," Corliss said, "and no one thought to enter you properly?"

"There wasn't a procedure for it," Edmund said. "Mr. Ashgrove asked me if I wanted work. I said yes. That seemed sufficient."

"To whom?"

"To everyone concerned."

Corliss leaned back. He had the habit, Edmund noticed, of looking at a person the way a man reads a slow sentence — patiently, all the way through. "Where were you before Hallowmere?"

"Here and there."

"Here and there." The sergeant wrote nothing down. "Son, I'm going to be plain with you, because I've got two hundred of these interviews to do this week and no patience left for cleverness. Captain Vane has ordered a full audit of the rolls before the transport leaves. Every resident accounted for. Every name matched to a face and a trade and a birth record. Persons whose names cannot be verified are classified as undocumented." He paused. "You know what happens to undocumented persons?"

"They're not permitted to travel."

"Worse than that." Corlliss's voice dropped, not dramatically — just the way a voice drops when a man says something true. "They're struck from the list entirely. Not denied passage. Erased. As far as the army will ever be concerned, they were never in this valley at all. No berth. No ration chit. No protection. When the garrison seals the passes, an undocumented person is nobody's problem, and nobody's problem in a sealed valley in January has a very short life expectancy."

The room's noise went on around them — scratching pens, a clerk calling a name, someone laughing somewhere about something — and all of it seemed to happen behind glass.

"And you think I'm undocumented," Edmund said.

"I think your entry is a pencil note in a margin," Corliss said. "I think no one can produce a birth record for you. I think old Tom Ashgrove answered my questions about you last Tuesday like a man defending a position rather than recalling facts, which is a thing I've learned to hear. And I think," and here he tapped the ledger once, gently, "that you know exactly what's in that gap, because you flinched when I opened the book."

Edmund said nothing. What was there to say that wouldn't be a bad lie?

Corliss waited him out. He was good at waiting; you could see it was a professional skill, worn smooth like a tool handle. Somewhere across the hall a clerk called "*Bramley!* Next!" and a woman's voice rose in protest about her husband's trade being misrecorded as *labourer* when he was plainly a cooper, and the small injustice of it was argued and corrected and the world moved on, and Edmund understood suddenly that he would never again be able to take for granted the ordinary miracle of having your trade written down correctly.

"I came up the valley road," he said at last. "Eleven years ago. In autumn. I was seven years old."

"With family?"

"No."

"Alone? A child of seven walks the valley road alone?"

"Not alone." Edmund heard his own voice from a strange distance, level and quiet, the voice he used for delicate mechanisms. "A woman brought me as far as the ridge. Then I walked down alone. She told me to say I was from the north country, that my parents were dead, that I was looking for work. She said—" he stopped, started again. "She said never sign anything."

The pen in Corliss's hand had gone still.

"She gave me money wrapped in cloth," Edmund went on, "and a letter for the schoolmistress in Millbrook, who kept me a fortnight and then sent me along to Hallowmere because the Ashgroves were known to take apprentices without asking questions." He looked up. "That's the truth of it, Sergeant. Half of it, anyway. The other half I only found myself recently, and it isn't mine to hand out to a requisition audit."

Something moved through Corliss's face — not surprise, exactly. Recognition, perhaps. Or the memory of recognition.

"A debtors' colony," he said quietly.

Edmund didn't answer, which was an answer.

Corliss closed the ledger. He did it carefully, aligning the cover, squaring it on the desk, as if the book were a sleeping animal he didn't wish to wake. For a long moment he looked at the wall behind Edmund, at nothing, at whatever lived inside a man who had spent thirty years carrying other people's paperwork.

"I had a brother," he said. "Not blood — a lodger, my mother took him in during the famine years, same as your Ashgroves took you. When the rolls were called in our district, they asked him for papers. He had none. They struck him off the transport list at Cork, and he stood on the quay and watched us sail, and I was twelve years old and waving at him, and I waved until I couldn't see the quay anymore." He picked up his pen. Set it down again. "I never found out what became of him. That's what 'undocumented' means, son. It means the record forgets you while you're still alive to watch it do it."

"I'm sorry," Edmund said, uselessly, and meant it completely.

"Don't be sorry. Be quick." Corliss leaned forward, and now his voice had an urgency in it that made Edmund sit straighter. "Listen to me, because I can't say this twice and I shouldn't say it once. Vane intends to strike every unverifiable name before final inspection. That's colonist families hiding in cellars, that's folk with lost papers, that's you. Final inspection is set for the twenty-ninth. After that the ledger goes to the transport office and it's out of any decent man's hands."

"The twenty-ninth," Edmund repeated. Six days.

"If a name is *in* the ledger — properly in, inked in, with trade and kinship and a record behind it — Vane can't strike it without an inquiry, and inquiries take time he hasn't allotted. But a name that isn't there..." Corliss shook his head. "It's not a question of mercy. Mercy requires a signature, and signatures require a reason, and Vane's reason is that brass and order matter and people don't."

"Why are you telling me this?" Edmund asked. "You could lose your post."

Corliss almost smiled. It was a small, weathered thing. "I've been losing pieces of myself to ledgers for thirty years, son. One post more or less won't be noticed." He straightened the ledger a final time. "Now. Officially, this interview concluded with the subject unable to verify his origins, pending further review. Unofficially — go home. Talk to whoever you need to talk to. And whatever you decide to do about that empty line in the book—"

He tapped the ledger once more.

"—decide it soon. The twenty-ninth comes whether we're ready or not. Clocks always do."

* * *

Edmund walked back through Hallowmere in a daze that he disguised, out of long habit, as purposefulness. The town had changed under occupation the way a room changes when a stranger sits in your chair — same furniture, wrong gravity. Soldiers stacked crates by the bakery. Two gunners laughed outside the draper's. A boy swept snow that refused to stop falling, sweeping on principle.

*Undocumented.* He turned the word over as he walked. All his life he had worn his lack of history like a comfortable coat — *here and there*, *before Hallowmere*, *the north country*. It had cost him nothing but the occasional awkward silence at market day, the questions from children he'd deflected with jokes. Now the coat had been taken away and examined under lamplight, and he'd learned it was made of paper, and paper burns, and men with stamps decide what paper counts.

And yet — and this was the strange part, the part he couldn't explain to Ivy or anyone — underneath the fear was something that felt almost like relief. Eleven years of half-truths, and a tired sergeant with rain-worn eyes had looked straight through them and said *I know what you are*, and the sky had not fallen. He had confessed the worst of himself, or nearly the worst, and instead of the door closing he had been handed a date and a warning, which was, he suspected, the closest thing to grace the army issued.

He reached the Ashgroves' workshop and stood outside it a moment, looking up at the windows, at the warm light, at the shadow of Ivy moving past the upstairs curtain with an armful of something — blankets, probably, bound for the cellars, because Ivy had decided weeks ago that strangers in trouble were simply relatives she hadn't met yet.

Six days. One empty line in a ledger. A captain who counted brass, and a sergeant who remembered a quay, and a mother whose letters lay hidden in a clock case, telling him things he still hadn't finished reading.

Edmund went inside, and found the whole family waiting for him in the kitchen, and understood from their faces that Corliss had not been the only one making quiet decisions today.
