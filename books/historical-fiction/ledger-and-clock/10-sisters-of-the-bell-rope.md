## 10. Sisters of the Bell Rope

Ivy Ashgrove had never in her nineteen years been accused of doing anything quietly, and she did not intend to begin with treason.

It was not treason, she told herself, hauling the third basket of turnips down the observatory's back stairs before the frost had even lifted off the flagstones. It was hospitality. The fact that Sergeant Corliss's men were walking the ridge road with their registration books, and that the people currently sleeping in her father's wine cellar—which contained no wine, only dust and a smell of old apples—would be struck from every list in Hallowmere if they were found, well. That was a matter of paperwork. Ivy had decided some years ago that paperwork was simply weather: you acknowledged it, you dressed for it, and you did not let it tell you where to go.

"Mind the step," she called back up the stairwell. "There's a loose one about halfway down. It bit Mrs. Fenwick last spring."

Behind her came the shuffle of small feet, the muffled clink of tin cups, and the voice of a woman named Ada Quill, who had arrived at the observatory door two nights ago with three children, one carpet bag, and the particular straight-backed dignity of someone who had once owned a dress shop and intended to own one again.

"We are not refugees," Mrs. Quill said, for perhaps the ninth time since her arrival, as though repetition could make it so. "We are guests."

"You're family," Ivy said, because it was quicker than arguing, and because it happened to be true, in the way that mattered.

* * *

The trouble had started, properly speaking, on Tuesday, when Ivy went into town for ribbon and came back with six strangers instead.

She had gone to Prewitt's shop for a spool of green silk—the good kind, the kind that didn't fray—and had found the shop shuttered at half past nine in the morning, which was unheard of, Prewitt being a man who opened early enough to catch the milk carts. She had knocked. A voice inside had hissed at her to go away, in an accent Ivy recognized from nowhere and everywhere: the flat, careful vowels of people who learned to speak by listening through walls.

Now, Ivy Ashgrove had been raised by parents whose chief moral instruction consisted of *feed whoever is at your table* and *the rest is commentary*. Her father Tom claimed this was scripture. Her mother Marigold claimed it was common sense. Either way, Ivy had absorbed it the way other children absorbed measles—thoroughly, and with lasting effects.

So she knocked again. And when the door cracked open onto the frightened eye of a girl about her own age—a colony-born girl, obviously, with the thin wrists and the watchful stillness that the debtors' settlements stamped into a person like a maker's mark—Ivy looked past her into the dim shop, counted four more faces, did the arithmetic of bread in the Ashgrove pantry against mouths in that room, and said:

"Right. You'll want coats. It's a mile uphill and the wind comes off the moor like a grudge."

The girl's eye widened. "You don't know us."

"No," Ivy agreed pleasantly, pushing the door wider, "but I know Prewitt's shutters, and I know what the army does to people without ledger entries, and I know my mother has too many potatoes. Come along. You can carry the ribbon."

That was how it began: six strangers, one spool of green silk, and a mile of frozen road during which Ivy learned that the girl's name was Sable Quill, that she had a mother and two brothers hiding in the tannery outbuildings, that the tannery would be searched by Friday, and that Sable had not eaten properly since the scouts first came over the ridge.

By supper on Tuesday there were eleven extra souls in the observatory cellars, and Ivy's mother had looked at her daughter across the kitchen table with an expression that was equal parts exasperation, pride, and surrender, and had said only, "Well. Fetch the big pot, then."

* * *

Edmund found her Thursday morning in the clockroom, up a ladder with a lantern, feeding the great going-train its weekly oil, and he watched her from below with the patient wariness of a cat observing weather.

"You look terrible," he said.

"Thank you. I've been awake since Tuesday."

"That explains Tuesday's expression too."

Ivy came down the ladder with the oil can hooked on two fingers. Edmund had changed lately—she'd noticed it the way you notice a clock running slightly fast; nothing you can point to, everything slightly off. There were shadows under his eyes that hadn't come from work. He kept touching his chest pocket, where something flat and paper-shaped lived now. She hadn't asked. In the Ashgrove house you asked after appetites, after sleep, after whether anyone needed anything carried anywhere—but the deep rooms of a person stayed locked until they opened their own doors. Mother said that. Father said it less prettily but meant the same thing.

"There are fourteen of them now," Ivy said. "In the cellar. The Quills, the Harrow twins, that old fellow Bram who claims he was a bell-founder before the debts took him—"

"Bram Oakes," Edmund said quietly. "He signed the clockroom visitor book in 'ninety-one. Before the colony closed its gates. He made bells." Something crossed his face then, quick as a shuttered window, and his hand went to his pocket again.

"Well, he's down there now telling anyone who'll listen that our tower bell is a quarter-tone flat," Ivy said, "and honestly he might be right, it has always sounded to me like a man complaining about soup." She set the oil can on the bench. "Edmund. I need things. Rope, mostly. And lamp oil. And somebody who can get into town without being stopped, because my face is known now—the sergeant's clerk smiled at me yesterday, which either means he fancies me or he's memorized me, and both are bad."

"Why rope?"

"Because there's a coal chute behind the east buttress that comes up in the scree garden, and if we're ever going to move people between here and the tannery, we'll want a line fixed for the descent. And because asking why is a luxury we've given up until spring."

Edmund was quiet a moment. Then he said, "I'll take the list into town tonight. Nobody stops me. I'm nobody."

The words landed strangely—heavier than they were shaped—and Ivy looked at him sharply, but he was already reaching for her pencil, already writing, and whatever lived behind his face had been put away again.

"Ivy," he said, not looking up. "What you're doing. If Vane finds out—"

"If Vane finds out, he'll find a cellar full of guests and a family famous for taking in strays, and the worst he can call it is eccentricity. The Ashgroves have been eccentric for three generations. We're practically a landmark."

"He can do worse than call it names."

"I know," Ivy said. And because he looked like he needed it, and because she suspected—without knowing why—that he of all people understood what it was to be taken in rather than born to a place, she added: "But nobody in this house got here by being safe. Mother walked twelve miles in a storm with a foundling in her coat. Father gave our winter coal money to a widow he'd met twice. I'm just the latest model."

Something eased in Edmund's shoulders then, barely, like a gear finding its tooth. He finished the list, folded it, and put it away next to the paper shape against his heart.

* * *

Friday night, the cellar held twenty-two.

They came in twos and threes through the coal chute and the scree garden and one memorable arrival via the drainage culvert that Bram Oakes navigated with the confidence of a man returning to a tavern he'd been thrown out of decades ago. Ivy met each of them at the bottom of the chute with a blanket and a question—name, trade, who else was still out there—and wrote the answers in pencil in the back of her old school copybook, which she had titled, on the first page, in letters of defiant neatness: *GUESTS.*

Mother had objected to the copybook at first—not to the enterprise, never to the enterprise, but to the ink. "Pencil," she'd said. "And keep it on your person, and if the worst happens, it goes in the stove and not a word spoken that isn't about turnips." Then she'd gone upstairs and returned with every spare quilt in the house and had begun, without further discussion, assigning sleeping corners by family group, and Ivy had understood that the objection had never really been about ink.

Father's contribution was quieter. He came down the first Friday with his carpenter's apron still on, surveyed the crowded dark, and said, "Bunks," as though the whole problem were furniture, which in Tom Ashgrove's philosophy it always was. By Sunday there were sleeping shelves along the north wall, sanded smooth so no splinter would find a child, and a partition screen for the Quill women, and a little table near the far wall where Bram Oakes sat in the evenings teaching the Harrow twins to read by lamplight, using a hymnal and, latterly, a treatise on bell-founding that he appeared to know by heart.

It was the reading lessons that undid Ivy a little. She would stand at the top of the cellar stairs with a tray of bread ends and dripping, and listen to Bram's gravel voice sounding out *b-e-l-l*, and the twins' voices repeating it like an incantation, and think: *this is what we are for.* Not the Ashgroves specifically—people generally. Somebody had to be the place where the rope came down. Might as well be a house that already smelled of bread.

* * *

Sunday evening, Edmund came down the cellar stairs carrying a crate of candles he had "found" somewhere, in the way he found things now—wordlessly, efficiently, with a certain grim competence that worried her more than open fear would have. He set them by the bunks. He checked the chute hatch. He stood a moment watching Bram teach, watching Mrs. Quill mend a stranger's coat with stitches fine enough for bridalwork, watching the Harrow twins fall asleep mid-lesson against their mother's shoulders.

"They'll search the tannery Monday," he said, low, beside her. "Corliss's men. I heard the duty roster."

"I know. Everyone's out. Last one through the chute an hour ago." She glanced at him. "How did you hear the roster?"

A pause. "Corliss talks to me. I fix clocks. People talk to the man who fixes the clock."

"Mm," said Ivy, who had noticed that her brother had lately acquired the habit of being exactly where information pooled, and filed it away with all the other small unexplained Edmonds accumulating that season. "Well. Whatever you're doing, do it carefully."

"Whatever *you're* doing," he returned, "do it fed. You haven't eaten since Thursday. I counted."

"You counted?"

"Somebody has to keep track of everyone," Edmund said, and there was such an odd weight in it—such a specific tenderness, as if the words had a second meaning addressed to himself—that Ivy, for once, had nothing witty to say back. She took the heel of bread he offered and ate it standing there in the lamplight among her borrowed family, while above them the master clock ticked through the evening hours toward midnight, keeping time for a town full of hidden names.

* * *

Monday brought the search, and Ivy spent it doing the hardest work of all: nothing.

She sat in the kitchen with Mother, shelling peas out of season and out of any honest hope of eating them, while boots moved through the tannery yard below the hill. Mrs. Quill sat with them, hands folded, wearing Marigold's second-best shawl and the expression of a duchess attending a dull service. Down in the cellar, twenty-two people were being silent in the way only people who have practiced silence can be—no coughing, no creaking, the babies dosed with honey water and rocked in turns by a rotation Ivy had scheduled to the minute.

The boots came up the drive eventually. Knocked. Corliss himself, tired-eyed, apologetic in the way of a man doing sums he doesn't like.

"Routine inspection of the outbuildings, ma'am. Cellar too, if it's all the same."

"It's all the same," Marigold said serenely, "except that my preserve shelf is none of the army's business, Sergeant, and I will thank you not to breathe on the damson."

He looked at the preserve shelf. It was magnificent—forty jars, gleaming, arranged by color, and every single one concealing a hollow beneath a false bottom that Father had built Saturday night with the serene craftsmanship of a man making a cradle. Corliss looked at the jars for a long moment. Then he looked at Mrs. Quill, whom any man with eyes could see was no Ashgrove, and at Ivy, whose hands were shaking around her peas, and something passed across the sergeant's face—some flicker of the arithmetic he must do every day, between the book in his hand and whatever remained in him that wasn't the book.

"Fine preserves," he said. "Damson keeps well. My mother used to say a household's judged by its cellar." And he tipped his cap, turned his men around, and left.

Ivy let out her breath after the gate clicked. Mrs. Quill reached over and steadied her pea bowl without appearing to move.

"You shook the peas," Mrs. Quill observed, "not the reverse. Sloppy."

"Yes, ma'am."

"But you counted us out of the search correctly, and the babies never woke, and your mother lied to a uniform with her chin level." Mrs. Quill picked up her mending. "When this is over, girl, you will come to me for finishing lessons. Any woman who can organize a conspiracy deserves to have her hems done properly."

Ivy laughed—her first real laugh in five days—and heard it echo down the cellar stairs, where it was answered, faintly, by twenty-two voices trying very hard not to laugh back.

* * *

That night, she climbed to the clockroom to bring Edmund his supper, because he had taken to sleeping up there among the gears like a spider who'd gotten ideas above himself. She found him at the case with the panel open—that strange hidden compartment he'd discovered weeks ago and never explained, which she had privately decided was either love letters or smuggling and had resolved to tease him about at Christmas—and he was sitting on the floor with his back against the great wheel, staring at nothing.

"Supper," she said. "Also, we're safe. Also, Mrs. Quill is adopting me. Also—" she set the plate down and folded her arms, "—you're going to tell me what's in your pocket eventually, and when you do, I'll probably wish you'd told me sooner, so consider telling me now and saving us both the suspense."

Edmund looked up at her. For a long moment he seemed to weigh it—all of it, whatever vast and terrible *it* was—and Ivy braced herself to finally hear it.

Then, from below, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of Bram Oakes's voice rising in an old founding-song, and the Harrow twins joining in on the chorus wrong, joyfully wrong, and Mrs. Quill shushing them, and the shushing failing.

Edmund almost smiled. He put the thought away again—she saw him do it, fold it up like the letter in his pocket—but he reached out and squeezed her hand, hard, once.

"Not yet," he said. "Soon. But Ivy—when I do tell you, remember tonight. Remember the singing. Because whatever it is, it doesn't change what's down those stairs. I need to believe that, and I need you to make me."

Ivy held his hand a moment longer. Above them the clock gathered itself toward the hour, and she thought: *we are all of us keeping something here—him his secret, Mother her lies, Father his false-bottomed jars, me my copybook of names—and the trick of it is that the keeping holds.*

"All right," she said. "But supper first. Secrets keep better on a full stomach."

She was halfway down the tower stair when the bell above her head stirred in its yoke, gathered the hour, and struck ten—and underneath its bronze complaint, flat by a quarter-tone just as Bram had said, she heard the cellar answer it, softly, with the chorus sung right this time.
