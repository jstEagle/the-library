## 11. One Berth, Five Names

The kitchen at Ashgrove's was the warmest room in Hallowmere, which was why the coldest conversation of their lives happened in it.

Edmund sat at the scrubbed pine table with his hands wrapped around a mug of tea he had no intention of drinking. The fire ticked and settled behind its grate. Ivy had been sent to the cellars to organize blankets — she'd gone with the particular brightness she wore when she suspected the grown-ups were about to do something foolish without her — and so it was four of them: Edmund, Marigold at the stove though nothing needed cooking, old Tom in his chair by the window with his bad knee propped on a stool, and young Peter, who was eleven and had been told to sit still, which for Peter meant fidgeting like a kettle coming to boil.

One berth.

The word had come down from the requisition office that afternoon, carried by Mrs. Quill from the bakery, who had counted twice to be sure. Five civilian berths remained on the last transport out of the valley before Captain Vane sealed the roads. Five berths, and the ledger listed six souls under the Ashgrove roof: Tom, Marigold, Ivy, Peter, the lodger Mr. Fenwick from the far bedroom — and Edmund, entered years ago in Marigold's careful hand as *apprentice, household*.

Five berths. Six names. And somewhere below them, in the observatory cellars, nine more names who weren't on any list at all.

"Right," said Tom, and pushed himself up out of his chair with the groan of a man sixty-eight years old who had spent forty of them bending over watchmaker's benches. "Let's have it done sensibly."

Edmund's stomach dropped, because Tom Ashgrove being sensible was a force of nature, like weather or taxes.

"I'm sixty-eight," Tom said, standing there with one hand flat on the table as if taking an oath. "I've had my span. My knees went twenty years ago, my eyes are held together with a loupe and stubbornness, and I snore loud enough to wake the garrison, which will be a mercy to everyone when they're gone." He looked around the table, daring anyone to interrupt. Nobody did. "The berth is mine. I'll take the transport, see the century in somewhere warm, and write you all letters so tedious you'll beg me to stop. That's the arithmetic. Done."

"It isn't done," Marigold said quietly. She hadn't turned from the stove. "Because you've forgotten the second half of your sums, love."

* * *

Marigold Ashgrove had raised four children on a clockmaker's wage and had learned long ago that love, in practice, was mostly arithmetic conducted at speed and in secret — how much bread could stretch, whose boots could last another winter, which child got the egg when there was only one. She had never once let the arithmetic show. She wasn't going to start now, in front of Peter, in front of Edmund, with her heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out of a case.

She turned around, drying her hands on her apron because her hands needed something to do that wasn't trembling.

"The second half," she said, "is that a man of sixty-eight traveling alone, with no family at journey's end, goes to the bottom of every housing list in every town between here and the coast. Whereas I—" she made herself smile, made it reach her eyes if it killed her, "—am a woman of fifty-three with two children to raise, and the relief committees fall over themselves for women with children. I'll be housed by Thursday. You'd be housed by spring, if the spring was kind."

"That's not—" Tom began.

"It is exactly how it works, Thomas Ashgrove, and you know it, because you're the one who taught me the arithmetic in the first place." She crossed the kitchen and put her hands on either side of his face, this dear stubborn man with his white stubble and his ink-stained fingers. "Ivy needs me. Peter needs me. You know they do. A father's letters are lovely but a mother's hands are what gets children fed and forms filled in and beds found." Her voice caught only slightly, and she rode over the catch. "So. The berth is mine."

"Mum," said Peter, who had been silent as long as an eleven-year-old can be silent, which is to say four minutes, "can't we just—"

"Peter."

"But Mr. Fenwick said he'd give up his berth anyway, he said so this morning, so really it's five names and five berths and Edmund could—"

"Mr. Fenwick has a sister in Lowmark he hasn't seen since the flood year," Marigold said, "and he is not giving up anything, because nobody asked him to, because that is not how this family does things." She heard herself say it — *this family* — and felt the words go through Edmund like a splinter finding its depth. He was sitting very still at the end of the table, the way he always sat, the way he had sat for fourteen years: present, useful, careful not to take up more room than he'd been given.

Oh, her boy. Not her boy. Her boy.

She looked at him and thought: *fourteen years and he has never once asked us for anything.* Not a bigger portion. Not new boots when his split. Not even, after his first terrible winter here, when he woke screaming with dreams none of them were ever told the shape of — he had never asked to be told he was allowed to stay.

And now the ledger men had come with their polished boots and their lists, and the whole valley was learning what things cost, and Marigold looked at this quiet young man at the end of her table and understood with terrible clarity that Edmund would rather walk into the snow than cost them a single berth.

Which was why the berth could not, under any circumstances, be left where he might refuse it.

"Mine," she said again, firmly, to the table in general. "Settled."

"It is not settled," said Tom.

"It is entirely set—"

"Mum," said Ivy from the doorway.

She'd come back early. Of course she had. She stood there with cellar dust on her skirts and her dark hair escaping its braid, nineteen years old and furious, and Marigold saw her open her mouth to say something magnificent and doomed — *give mine away, I'll stay, I'll hide in the cellars with the others, I'm not leaving* — and Marigold moved faster than she had moved in years.

"Bed," she said. "You've been up since four. Bed, Ivy Ashgrove, and don't think I didn't see the flour tin."

For once in her life, Ivy went. Though she went backwards, glaring, mouthing something at Edmund that Marigold chose not to translate.

* * *

Edmund lay awake in the narrow attic room that had been his for fourteen years, staring at the beams he knew by heart, listening to the house settle around him like a great clock winding down toward morning.

They were going to fight over it. That was the unbearable thing. They would sit at that table tomorrow, and the next day, and they would fight over the single berth the way families fought over the last slice of cake at a funeral — politely, ferociously, each of them certain that love meant losing.

He knew what he ought to do. It was the simplest sum in the world, simpler than any Tom or Marigold had proposed. He was the apprentice. He was the one with no entry in the town ledger, no birth record Corliss could verify, no name that would survive ten minutes of honest cross-examination. When they discovered what he was — and Sergeant Corliss already had questions; Edmund had watched him flag the Ashgrove page with a pencil the way a man marks a horse he doesn't trust — the army wouldn't strike him from the transport list. They'd strike him from the *town*. An undocumented colony-born foundling, discovered hiding in a clockmaker's household? There were worse places than a winter road, and Vane's clerks knew all of them.

So the answer was simple: give up the pretense now, spare them the exposure, take the road south on foot before the gates closed. He'd survived once already on less.

He turned his face to the wall.

Except.

Except that yesterday, in the tower, with the escapement finally running true beneath his fingers, Tom had climbed the stairs — slow, one hand on the rail — and stood watching him work for a long time without saying anything. And then Tom had said, gruffly, to the gears rather than to Edmund: *"Your mother would have liked this. She always liked watching you fix things."*

Not *your foster mother*. Not *the woman who took you in*. Just — *your mother*, plain as noon, as if the question of whether the name fit had never once been worth asking.

Fourteen years of Sundays. Fourteen years of a chair kept at the table without discussion. Fourteen years of Marigold darning his socks and lying to the neighbors about his cough and entering him in her own Bible's family pages in her round clerk's hand, *Edmund, our own*, as if she had the authority, as if love were a registry office.

He could not make that arithmetic come out. He lay in the dark and tried, and failed, and failed.

Below him, faint through the floorboards, he heard the murmur of his parents' voices in the kitchen, low and urgent, going back and forth past midnight — *mine, yours, mine* — arguing over a single seat on a train as if it were the last warm place on earth, and God help him, to Edmund it sounded exactly like grace.

He decided, somewhere in the small hours, what he would do.

It was not walking south. It was not noble surrender, either — he was too angry by then, too tired, too full of his mother's letters hidden under his floorboard, to manage noble. No. What he decided, staring at the beams, was something quieter and far more dangerous.

There were six names and five berths, and the army believed in ledgers, and ledgers could be written.

He had an engraver's hand. He had steady nerves. He had, as of three nights ago, a reason.

* * *

Tom Ashgrove won the morning, in the end, by cheating.

He did it at breakfast, which Marigold should have anticipated, because Tom had been winning arguments at that table since before she married into the family and she ought to have known better than to leave him unsupervised overnight.

"Sit down, all of you," Tom said, when Edmund came down the stairs and found the family assembled, which was itself suspicious — Ivy glowering with wet hair, Peter mid-yawn, Marigold with her arms folded. Tom stood at the head of the table holding his good cane like a chairman's gavel. "I've consulted the relevant authorities."

"There are no relevant authorities," Marigold said.

"There's me," said Tom. "I'm the oldest. In this family, age outranks everything except your mother, and your mother has agreed to hear me out." He waited until she gave a stiff nod — and Edmund, watching, saw that she *had* agreed to something overnight, those murmured voices below his floorboards, some treaty signed between them while he lay awake. His chest went cold.

"Here's the thing about a berth," Tom said. "Everyone keeps treating it like a prize. It's not a prize. It's a *job*. Somebody has to go, and somebody has to go for reasons, and I'll tell you my reasons and then we'll vote and be done with it." He held up one ink-stained finger. "One: I am the only person at this table whom the army cannot use against the rest of us. Think about it. Vane wants leverage. He wants the clockmaker's household behaving itself because the clockmaker matters. If I'm gone, there's nothing to hold over anyone. Two:" a second finger, "someone has to be at the far end. Someone respectable, with papers in order, to meet these trains, find the housing, sign whatever needs signing. A woman alone with two children arriving in a strange city is a story with a sad ending. A woman arriving to join her husband? Different story entirely. I go ahead and I *make ready*. Three—"

His voice did something Edmund had never heard it do. It wavered.

"Three," Tom said. "I built that girl's first cradle. I built that boy's first kite. I have spent sixty-eight years making things ready for other people, and I am not going to stop for a train ticket." He set the cane down, gently, like laying down a sword. "That's my speech. Vote."

Nobody voted. Peter started crying, quietly, the way boys cry when they're furious about it, and Marigold put her arms around him and looked at Tom across the top of their son's head with an expression that contained an entire marriage — fury and admiration and grief all braided into one look — and said, "You absolute *man*."

"Aye," Tom agreed. "Often been said."

"And it's settled, then?" Edmund heard himself ask, and hated how hoarse his voice came out.

"It's settled," Tom said, and then his eyes came up and found Edmund's, and held them, and there was something in the old man's face that Edmund couldn't read — something that looked almost like apology, or warning, or both. "All but the last detail. Which is yours."

"Mine?"

"You'll want to argue," Tom said. "Everyone at this table wants to argue, it's our chief export. But I'm asking you not to. I'm asking you to do the sums instead, the real ones, and tell me what you get." He leaned forward on the table. "Because here's what *I* get, lad. I get to leave knowing my wife is provided for, my children are safe, and the finest pair of hands this shop ever produced is staying behind to keep the only working clock in the valley running for nine people hiding in a cellar. That's not sacrifice. That's *placement*. Like setting a jewel."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a door thumped — Ivy, listening at the landing, pretending otherwise.

"I'm not staying," Edmund said carefully. "I'm not — Tom, I'm not one of your—"

"You're the keeper," Tom said simply. "You've been the keeper since you were twelve years old and you fixed the regulator clock I'd given up on. Names are just what people call the keeping." He rose, joints cracking, and squeezed Edmund's shoulder once on his way to the door — one squeeze, hard, brief, the whole inheritance delivered in a grip. "Train leaves day after tomorrow, dawn. We've got today to pack and tonight to say what needs saying. Now if you'll excuse me, I'm going to go oil the lathes one final time, because if I'm leaving this shop to history, history can have it clean."

He went out. Marigold followed him with the teapot, because in that house grief was always served hot.

And Edmund stood alone in the warm kitchen, in the room where he had been fed and scolded and forgiven for fourteen years, and looked at the empty chair where Tom had argued himself onto a train, and felt the decision he'd made in the night turn over inside him like a gear engaging.

Six names. Five berths. One family that would rather die than count itself honestly.

Well then. If the ledgers were going to decide who lived and who traveled and who stayed —

— then someone in this house had better learn to write them.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Edmund looked at the little drawer in the dresser where Marigold kept the household accounts, the good pen, the iron-gall ink. Tomorrow night, when the house slept, he would need them.

He memorized the drawer's position, and went to pack Tom's tools.
