## 12. Forging Midnight

The house had gone quiet the way a clock goes quiet—completely, and with a sense of held breath. Edmund lay in his narrow bed under the eaves and listened to it: Tom's slow snore through the floorboards, Marigold turning over, Ivy's candle guttering out at half past eleven as it always did, because Ivy believed in sleep the way she believed in most things, enthusiastically and only when convenient.

Below all of them, fainter than any of it, ticked the town.

He could hear the master clock from here on still nights—a sound less like ticking than like thinking. One beat, then another, patient as a heartbeat under the floor of Hallowmere. Everyone in the valley set their lives by that sound. Bakers rose to it. The ferryman counted his crossings by it. Sergeant Corliss, Edmund suspected, even timed his sighs to it.

And tomorrow—or soon, very soon—the ledger would be inspected again, and somewhere in its pages there was a gap shaped exactly like him.

Edmund got up.

He dressed in the dark out of long habit; an apprentice learns to dress without waking anyone or he learns to find other lodging. He took the candle but didn't light it. He knew these stairs the way he knew the inside of a fusee cone—by feel, by faith, by the memory of a thousand small corrections.

In the kitchen he lit the lamp low and turned its wick down until the flame was no bigger than a thumbnail. Then he sat at the workbench where Tom mended boots and Marigold salted beans, and he laid out before him the tools of his trade, which were also, it now appeared, the tools of his crime.

A graver. A burnisher. A fine needle file. A stick of sealing wax he'd taken from the requisition office two days ago, along with a scrap of the official foolscap that had fallen from Corliss's satchel—fallen, Edmund told himself, in the way that things fall when you arrange for them to fall.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about engraving: it is the art of making a lie look inevitable.

Every letter Edmund had ever cut into brass or silver or the soft face of a watch case had been a kind of promise. *This watch belongs to J. Whitfield.* *Repaired by E. Ashgrove, Hallowmere.* His master in the old days—he'd been eight then, or nine, already apprenticed twice over to families who weren't his—had said that a good engraver's letters should look like they'd grown there, like they'd always been part of the metal, like no other word was ever possible in that place.

That was the whole art. To make the false thing look like it had never been anything else.

Edmund turned the scrap of foolscap toward the lamplight. On it, in Corliss's careful clerk's hand, ran a practice entry:

*Whitfield, Jonas. Cooper. b. 1861. Hallowmere.*

It was nothing. A discarded draft, a name from nowhere, a man who might exist and might not. But it was written in the register hand, and the register hand was what mattered, because the great ledger of Hallowmere was kept in a single consistent script across forty years of clerks—one hand teaching the next, so that the book itself would seem to have no author, only authority.

Edmund had spent three evenings watching Corliss write. That was the shameful part, or one of them. He'd sat in the requisition office repairing the office regulator—a legitimate job, a real job, his honest hours—and the whole time his eyes had been elsewhere, cataloguing. The way Corliss crossed his t's with a single flat stroke, not curved. The way his capital W began with a hairline upstroke before the heavy descent. The slight forward lean of the whole line, like a man walking into wind. And the ink—iron gall, mixed thin, going brown at the edges as it aged, which meant a fresh entry would sit on old paper looking black and wet and wrong unless Edmund aged it.

He had thought about how to age ink the way he thought about how to fit a broken tooth in a wheel train: as a problem with a solution. Strong tea. A warm iron. Time pressed flat between boards.

The craftsman in him was taking notes. The craftsman in him was *interested*. That was the worst of it. Somewhere underneath the dread and the guilt lived a small bright thing that looked at a forged ledger entry and saw not sin but *workmanship*, and wanted, horribly, to do it well.

* * *

He practiced first on the boot leather. Not the writing—the cutting. Tom's bench had awls and edge-tools, and Edmund took a scrap of harness leather and cut into it the letters of his own false history: *Ashgrove, Edmund. Apprentice.* He cut them shallow, then deeper, feeling for the moment when a stroke stopped being a scratch and became a letter—that point of commitment where the tool bites and there is no undoing, only finishing. In metal you can chase out a mistake. In paper you cannot. Paper forgives nothing. Paper is the most honest material there is, which is why lying on it felt like such a specific crime.

Then he tried the pen.

His first attempt at Corliss's hand was a humiliation. It looked exactly like what it was: an engraver imitating writing, all the strokes too deliberate, every curve showing its construction the way a badly fitted pivot shows its filing marks. He'd made letters the way he made letters on brass—measured, symmetrical, *true*—and true was precisely wrong. Corliss's writing wasn't true. It was human. It hurried at the ends of lines. Its rhythm varied because a man's wrist varies, because at the top of a page a clerk is fresh and at the bottom he wants his supper.

Edmund put down the pen and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

"You're doing this," he said aloud, to the kitchen, to himself. "You are actually doing this."

The kettle on the hob ticked as it cooled, a small domestic clock counting nothing. From upstairs came the creak of Tom turning over, the particular groan of a man whose knees had opinions. Ordinary sounds. Sounds belonging to a house that had fed him for fifteen years without once asking him to account for himself.

Fifteen years ago, someone had carried a child out of a debtors' colony in the bottom of a cart, wrapped in sacking, with a forged paper pinned to his blanket—and that child had grown up believing that honesty was the family trade. That was the joke, if it was a joke. Edmund had been a forgery all along. Every document about him was false. The Ashgroves had simply chosen never to read the fine print.

So why did adding one more lie to the pile feel like breaking something?

Because they hadn't lied, he thought. *They* hadn't. They'd taken in a stranger's boy and raised him clean. The falseness in his papers wasn't theirs. If he forged the ledger now, he'd be the first Ashgrove ever to put his hand to it—not the smuggled child, but the man, choosing it, doing it, doing it *well*, with tea-stained ink and a steady graver's wrist.

Unless he did it for them. Unless the berth went to Tom, who was sixty-three and coughed in October like the winter had a personal grievance against him—

"No," Edmund said, and surprised himself by how much voice was in it.

Not Tom. Not Marigold, who hadn't slept properly since the drums came, and whose hands shook now when she kneaded bread, though she'd die before admitting it. Not Ivy, who would give her berth away with a laugh and then spend the rest of her life finding new strangers to save, and God help whoever tried to stop her.

Himself, then. Himself was the arithmetic answer. Himself was what a sensible man would forge toward—a documented name, a legitimate trade, a berth on the last transport out of a valley about to fill with soldiers.

But sensible men, Edmund was beginning to understand, were just people who hadn't yet found the thing they couldn't be sensible about.

* * *

He worked until the candle had burned a thumb's width and the sky past the window had gone from black to the grey of unpolished pewter. By then he could produce four lines of Corliss's hand that would have passed, he thought, at arm's length, in bad light, examined by a tired man. Which was to say: they would pass exactly as long as nobody cared enough to look. And Captain Vane, everything about him said, was a man who looked.

So Edmund did what he always did when a mechanism wouldn't hold. He took it apart and rebuilt it finer.

The problem, he decided, was uniformity. His forgeries were too *good*—every t crossed identically, every entry the same confident weight. Real ledgers weren't real because they were accurate; they were real because they were worn. Forty years of clerks, forty years of cold mornings and aching wrists, and the evidence of all that humanity baked into the script like wear-marks on a pallet stone.

So he introduced flaws. Deliberate ones. A tremor in the third entry, as if the pen had skipped. A blot absorbed carefully with blotting sand. An entry begun, abandoned, restarted half a line lower—because surely, in forty years, some clerk had misheard a name and corrected it. He built imperfection the way he built clearance into a bearing: precisely, purposefully, so that the finished thing could move.

By the time the first birds started up in the gable, he had a sheet of foolscap that looked like it had been torn from the ledger's living body. Six entries. Six people who had never existed, in trades that did—cooper, seamstress, farrier, widow—each with a birth year chosen to match the wear on the page, each aged with tea and pressed overnight between Tom's two heaviest pattern books.

They were beautiful. That was the horror of it. He held the sheet up to the greying light and felt the craftsman's pride rise in him like warmth from a stove—*look what your hands can do*—and felt, directly beneath it, the conscience answering, cold as a plunge: *your hands can do this to people.*

Because that was what a ledger was. Not a book. A machine. Every name a tooth on a wheel, every entry meshing with the next, and the whole vast apparatus of the state—transports, berths, rations, rifles—driven by it, turning because of it. Corliss had shown him that much without meaning to. You altered one tooth and everything downstream moved differently. Someone ate. Someone didn't. Someone stood on a frozen platform in January holding a paper that said they existed, and someone else stood behind a rope and watched the train leave without them.

Edmund had spent his life among machines that were allowed to be exact because nothing depended on their mercy. A clock could be perfect. A ledger shouldn't want to be. A ledger that was too perfect was a weapon with the safety off.

He was still holding the sheet when the stair creaked.

He knew the tread before he knew the person—light, uneven, landing hard on the left foot because Ivy had rolled that ankle sliding down the observatory banister at fourteen and had never once let it slow her down. Edmund slid the foolscap under the bean-salter's lid with a smoothness that would have appalled him under any other circumstances.

Ivy came into the kitchen in her shawl, hair escaping its plait, and stopped dead.

For one horrible second he thought she'd seen. Then he followed her gaze to the workbench and understood: the pen, the ink bottle, the practice scraps of leather with letters cut into them, the whole suspicious litter of a man caught mid-crime.

"You're writing letters," Ivy said. "At four in the morning."

"I couldn't sleep."

"You're *engraving* letters." She came closer, picked up the harness scrap, squinted at it with the frank rudeness of a sister. "On leather. Why would you—" And then he watched it land, the way ideas landed on Ivy, all at once and sideways: her eyes went from the leather to the pen to his face, and widened, and he saw her arrive somewhere he had not invited her to arrive.

"Edmund," she said, very quietly, in a voice he'd only heard her use once before—the night she'd come home having hidden the Okonkwo family in the cellars and dared the whole street to say a word about it. "What are you making?"

"Nothing."

"That's the worst lie I've ever heard, and I've heard Tom explain the billiards score." She set down the leather. She didn't raise her voice. That was the frightening thing about Ivy; her anger was quiet and her kindness was louder. "There's one berth left. There's one berth and five of us and you've been walking around all week like a man carrying something breakable, and now I find you at four in the morning practicing somebody else's handwriting."

"It's not—" He stopped. Because it was, and because lying to Ivy had never once worked in fifteen years, and because some exhausted, cracked-open part of him wanted, desperately, to put the thing down. "Ivy. If I tell you, you'll have to decide whether you're part of it. You don't get to unknow it."

She pulled out a chair and sat, folding her hands on the table like a woman settling in for a sermon. "Edmund Ashgrove. I have spent this entire month hiding strangers in a cellar and lying to a captain's face about the number of blankets we need. Do you genuinely believe there is a version of 'it' I'm not already part of?"

The lamp flame bent in the draught from the door. Down in the town, faint and faithful, the master clock ticked on, keeping its count, keeping everyone's hours, keeping—Edmund thought suddenly, with a lurch that had nothing to do with gears—everyone's names.

He reached under the bean-salter's lid and drew out the sheet of forged entries, and laid it on the table between them, and turned it toward the light.

Ivy looked at it for a long time. He braced for horror, for the sermon, for the sharp Ivy verdict. Instead she traced one finger above the ink—carefully, not touching it, the way you'd hover over a mechanism you respected—and said:

"These are *good*."

"They're crimes."

"They're both," Ivy said. "Most worthwhile things are." She looked up at him, and her eyes were shining and absolutely steady. "How many can you do before the inspection?"

And Edmund, who had come downstairs to betray his conscience in secret and alone, heard himself answer:

"As many as the night allows."
