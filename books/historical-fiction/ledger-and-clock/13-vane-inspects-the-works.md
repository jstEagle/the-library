## 13. Vane Inspects the Works

Captain Ambrose Vane had a theory about towns, and it was this: a town was simply a machine that had forgotten it was one. Hallowmere, viewed from the ridge road that grey morning, confirmed the theory beautifully. Smoke rose from chimneys at regular intervals. Carts moved along lanes with the predictable rhythm of escapements. And above it all, the observatory tower stood like a spindle around which the whole mechanism turned.

He found it restful to think of things as machines. Machines could be valued. Machines could be repurposed. Machines did not look at you with the wounded dignity of a shopkeeper whose premises you had just requisitioned.

"Captain," said Sergeant Corliss, falling into step beside him on the observatory path. "The clockmaker's apprentice is up in the tower. Has been since dawn."

"Good. I want to see him work."

Corliss glanced sideways. "May I ask why, sir?"

"You may not." Vane adjusted his gloves. "But I'll tell you anyway, because you sulk otherwise. Brass is brass, Corliss. There are four hundredweight of it in that tower, by my estimate—gears, weights, fittings, bell mounts. The Ordnance Office wants munitions fittings, and the foundries are short. A clock that tells time to a valley of farmers is a luxury. A clock that becomes rifle bolts is a necessity."

"Yes, sir."

Vane heard the *yes, sir* for what it was—a door closed politely in his face. Corliss had a way of agreeing that constituted disagreement. It was a talent Vane might have admired if he hadn't been its target.

They climbed. The tower stairs were stone, worn into shallow smiles by two centuries of feet, and Vane counted them out of habit—one hundred and six—and emerged into the clockroom, where the cold hit first and then the sound.

It was not ticking. Ticking was what parlor clocks did, small and nervous. This was something else: a deep, patient respiration, a great heart of wheels turning somewhere behind the case, so slow and so vast that you felt it in your sternum before you heard it with your ears.

Vane stopped on the threshold and permitted himself three full seconds of honest admiration.

The master clock filled the far wall of the chamber—or rather, it *was* the wall, a case of black oak going up past the reach of any ladder, its face open where panels had been removed, exposing the movement like the ribs of some enormous sleeping animal. Brass wheels the size of cart tires. Steel arbors thin as needles. And everywhere, everywhere, the gleam of metal kept clean by hands that cared.

*Four hundredweight,* he thought again, and this time the number tasted less like arithmetic and more like vandalism. He put the taste away. Sentiment was a tax other men paid.

The apprentice was up on the platform beside the movement, sleeves rolled, a jeweler's loupe screwed into one eye, holding a wheel no bigger than a saucer up to the light from the clock face. He didn't hear them come in. He was entirely elsewhere—the particular elsewhere of craftsmen, which Vane recognized because he'd once been an engineer before the army had decided he was better suited to being a hammer.

"Apprentice," Vane said.

Edmund Ashgrove started so badly the wheel nearly went over the rail. He caught it—caught it without looking, actually, snatched it out of the air with a reflex that told Vane more than a page of testimony would have—and turned, and there was that face again. Young. Watchful. The kind of quiet that wasn't emptiness but compression, like a spring held under a cloth.

"Captain." He set the wheel down with exaggerated care, as though it might be evidence. "I didn't—I'm sorry, I didn't hear the door."

"No one ever does. That's rather the point of this room, isn't it?" Vane walked forward, hands clasped behind him, and let his eyes travel up the movement with undisguised appetite. "Who built her?"

"The case is older than the town records, sir. The escapement's been rebuilt four times that we know of. The last major work was—" Edmund hesitated. "My master's grandfather, I believe. Before me it was old Tom Ashgrove who tended it."

"Ashgrove." Vane smiled thinly. "Everything in this valley is Ashgrove. Ashgroves keep the clock, Ashgroves sign the ledger, Ashgroves appear in every register like ivy on a wall. Tell me, apprentice—are you an Ashgrove?"

"I have the honor of the name, sir."

"That isn't what I asked."

Edmund met his eyes. Good—Vane noted it—not defiant, not cringing, just steady. Most people looked away when he pressed. This one held, the way a bearing holds under load.

"I was taken in, Captain. Seven years ago. They've never treated me as anything but their own."

"How touching." Vane ran a gloved finger along the edge of the great barrel, checking for dust. Found none. His respect for the boy ticked upward another notch; his plans for the brass remained unchanged. "And how goes the repair? Your master promised the clock running by month's end. The army has little patience for a garrison that can't tell noon from midnight."

"It goes well." Something flickered across the young man's face—too quick to name, gone before it finished arriving. "The chime train was the difficulty. A cracked lifting piece, and the strike lever worn past true. I've made replacements."

"Show me."

Edmund showed him. And here Vane gave the boy his due: he explained the fault clearly, without padding, pointing out where the crack had propagated along the grain of the casting, why the replacement had been filed and drilled the way it had, how the timing of the hour lift depended on a clearance measured in the width of a hair. He spoke the way engineers speak when they love the thing they're describing—which was, Vane reflected, precisely the wrong way for a young man to speak to a man who intended to melt the thing down.

"You're proud of this work," Vane said.

"I'm—yes, sir. It's good work."

"It will make excellent rifle bolts."

The silence that followed had texture. Vane watched the boy absorb it—not flinch, absorb, the way a foundation absorbs weight—and thought, *there it is.* Everyone broke differently. Some men went hot, some went cold, some went straight to pleading. The quiet ones went somewhere private and locked the door. He'd need to remember that about Ashgrove. Locked doors were either very safe or very dangerous, and rarely anything between.

"Of course," Vane continued pleasantly, "that's contingent on the audit. Requisitioned property must be inventoried before it's stripped. Every compartment opened, every cavity inspected. Standing orders." He said it lightly, watching. "You'd be surprised what people hide in machines. Documents, mostly. Papers someone wanted kept close and thought no one would look for inside a clock. It's an old trick. Older than you."

The boy's hands, resting on the platform rail, did nothing at all. That was the tell, Vane thought. Not a twitch, not a stilling—nothing. Absolute nothing, the stillness of a man performing stillness.

"Is that common, sir?" Edmund asked. His voice was mild as milk.

"In my experience? Yes." Vane stepped closer to the case, letting his gaze drift along the framework with the idle thoroughness of a buyer at auction. "In fact, I'll give you a story, apprentice, since you've given me a lesson in escapements. Years ago, in another posting, we dismantled a chapel organ. Lovely instrument. Inside the wind chest, wrapped in oilcloth, we found the parish's entire debt book—every promissory note owed by every family in the district. Someone had hidden them there during a bankruptcy scare, years before, and died without telling anyone. Do you know what struck me most?"

"No, sir."

"It wasn't the hiding. Anyone hides things. It was the *place*. Whoever chose that spot understood something important—that people revere mechanisms. They assume a working thing is only itself. A clock tells time. An organ makes music. Nobody thinks to ask what else they carry." He turned back and found the boy watching him with those compressed, careful eyes. "I've never forgotten it. Whenever I see a fine mechanism now, I find myself wondering what it's keeping besides the hours."

"That's a strange thing to wonder, Captain."

"It's a profitable thing to wonder." Vane smiled. "This tower has hidden cavities. Old cases always do—spaces left by dead craftsmen, sealed up and forgotten. My surveyor marked three of them yesterday. False bottoms in the weight trunk. A void behind the dial panelling. And—" he consulted a small notebook, unhurried, theatrical, because he was enjoying himself and saw no reason to pretend otherwise, "—a panel in the lower case, here, hinged rather than fixed. Hinged, apprentice. Someone wanted access."

Edmund said nothing. Which was, itself, an answer, and both of them knew it.

"The inventory team opens all of them tomorrow," Vane said. "Standard procedure. Anything found is logged, read, and entered into the record. Letters, books, papers of any kind." He paused, delicately. "You understand, of course, that in a requisitioned district, documents concerning persons of irregular status become matters of official interest. A letter naming a birth, say. Or a colony. Or a child."

The word *colony* landed in the clockroom and lay there between them, and the great clock breathed its slow breath over it, indifferent, keeping its own counsel the way it had kept everyone's counsel for two hundred years.

"I wouldn't know anything about that, sir," Edmund said quietly.

"No," Vane agreed. "I don't imagine you would."

He let the moment sit. Then he clapped his gloved hands together once, briskly, a man concluding business. "Well. Keep at your repairs. I want that movement running before it comes apart—it amuses me to know exactly what we're losing. And apprentice—" he paused at the stairhead, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was the most dangerous register he owned, "—if anything should occur to you between now and tomorrow. Anything you'd care to bring to me privately, before the inventory team finds it publicly. My door is open. Discretion, in my experience, buys more than defiance. Ask anyone who's dealt with me twice."

He descended the stairs counting them again—one hundred and six—and outside, in the grey light, Sergeant Corliss fell in beside him.

"Well, sir?"

"He knows I know," Vane said. "Or he suspects. Either way, he'll move tonight or he'll do nothing, and I confess I can't decide which I'd prefer."

"And if he moves, sir?"

"Then we learn what he's protecting, and who sent him to protect it, and whether the Ashgrove family's virtue survives contact with a cell." Vane pulled his collar up against the wind coming off the ridge. "You disapprove, Corliss. You have the disapproving walk."

"I have a normal walk, sir."

"You disapprove of pressing the boy. Of the inventory. Possibly of the entire requisition, though you'd sooner resign than say it."

Corliss walked several paces in silence. When he spoke, it was in the flat tone of a man reading from a document he wished were different. "I disapprove of wasting effort, Captain. If the letters matter, they'll be found tomorrow regardless. If they don't matter, we've spent a day frightening a clockmaker's boy for nothing. Either way—" he shrugged, soldierly, economical, "—the brass gets melted either way."

"Yes," Vane said. But the word came out slower than he intended, because a thought had snagged in him like thread on a nail.

The brass got melted either way. That was certain. So why had the boy looked at the great barrel—with its four hundredweight of destiny sitting fat and gleaming in front of him—why had he looked at it not like a man losing a fight, but like a man who had already decided something?

Vane had spent twenty years learning to read faces in interrogation rooms, and he knew the difference between fear and resolve, and what he'd seen up on that platform, underneath the milk-mild voice, had not been fear.

It had been a craftsman looking at a mechanism and thinking, *I know something about this machine that you don't.*

"Corliss," he said, stopping. "Post a man on the tower tonight. Quietly. And another at the clerk's office—watch the ledger room especially."

"The ledger room, sir? We've already audited the ledger."

"We've already audited what the ledger *says*." Vane looked back up at the tower, where a small figure had returned to the open movement, bent over his work, patient as the clock itself. "Machines aren't the only things people hide things inside, Sergeant. Names are compartments too. And I have a growing suspicion that our quiet apprentice has learned to pick locks of both kinds."

That night, Vane wrote in his own ledger—neat, precise, a page headed *HALLOWMERE: IRREGULARITIES*—a single line beneath the boy's name:

*Knows more than he says. Says more than he knows he's saying. Watch the hands.*

He underlined *hands* twice, blew out the lamp, and slept poorly, dreaming of organs that played debts instead of music, and of a clock that tolled not hours but names—one for every person it had swallowed—while he stood beneath it with a melting pot and nowhere near enough fire.
