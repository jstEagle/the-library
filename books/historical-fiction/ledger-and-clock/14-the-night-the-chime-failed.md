## 14. The Night the Chime Failed

For three days after Vane's inspection, Edmund did not sleep so much as he dozed in installments, like a miser counting out coins he could not afford to spend.

The trouble was that the clock was *nearly* right. That was the worst of it. A broken clock is an honest adversary—you can see the wound and go at it. But the master clock of Hallowmere had entered that maddening middle country between broken and whole, where it ran for six hours like a choirboy and then lost ninety seconds as if embarrassed to be caught doing well. It had done this twice on Tuesday, once on Wednesday at an hour chosen apparently by spite, and not at all on Thursday morning, which Edmund found more alarming than any of its failures.

"It's sulking," Ivy said, bringing him tea up the tower stair on Thursday evening. She set the mug on the case shelf among his files and tweezers, displacing a small brass bracket, which she then could not find again. "You've hurt its feelings with all your poking."

"Clocks don't sulk."

"This one does. It's been in this family longer than you have, and it learned from the best." She leaned against the case, watching him work, her sleeves rolled and her forearms still smudged with cellar dust from the morning's errands—the ones nobody spoke about above a whisper, moving blankets and bread down into the dark. "Tom says you talked to it when you were twelve."

"I said *please* to it. Once. When I couldn't find the strike train fault."

"And?"

"And it kept its secret for another four years."

Ivy laughed, and the sound went around the clockroom and came back warmer than it left. Edmund bent over the escapement and let the laugh settle somewhere behind his ribs, next to the letters from the panel, next to everything else he was carrying now. He had not told them. He had told no one what the letters said, though Marigold watched him at breakfast with the particular patience of a woman who has raised four children and knows precisely when one of them is carrying a stone in his boot and pretending it is a pebble.

He checked the chime train for the hundredth time. The lifting levers sat clean. The pins on the great wheel were true—he had trued them himself, under Vane's eye, two days of the captain's shadow falling across his hands while the man asked pleasant questions with unpleasant edges. The locking plate was sound. The fly had been rebushed. Every part of the mechanism that could fail had been examined until Edmund knew its face better than his own.

Which meant, he told himself for the third night running, that whatever remained was either in a place he hadn't looked—

—or it wasn't the clock's fault at all.

He didn't let himself finish the thought. Not yet. Accusing the garrison of sabotage was the kind of thought that, once let loose in your head, made every other thought look different, and he had a midnight deadline and a town full of names depending on that clock striking honest hours until the transport came.

"Go home," he told Ivy. "Get some sleep. If it fails tonight I want witnesses who aren't half-dead."

"If it fails tonight," Ivy said, "you'll be standing here alone with a mob, which is a stupid way to arrange things." But she kissed the top of his head—she had done that since before she was tall enough to make it easy—and clattered down the stairs, and the tower was quiet except for the tick, tick, tick of the thing itself, which sounded, tonight, almost smug.

* * *

At half past eleven Edmund stood in the belfry with his ear against the frame of the great bell, listening to it breathe. Below him, Hallowmere lay under a thin crust of snow, and beyond the town the garrison camp burned with cookfires and lantern light—a second town grown up around the first in six weeks, all canvas and drill squares and horses steaming in the cold. Two thousand soldiers waiting for their valley. One clock expected to keep time for both.

He heard boots on the stair and knew the tread before he saw the face: Sergeant Corliss, cap in hand, breath fogging.

"Came up to say I'll be below," Corliss said. "Captain's orders. An officer is to witness the midnight strike personally, and the captain—" he paused, choosing, "—the captain has delegated the witnessing. There's a reception at headquarters. The colonel from the division is visiting."

"He wants it noted that he wasn't here if it goes wrong."

"I want it noted," Corliss said mildly, "that some men delegate for convenience and some delegate because they've already written the report in their heads and only require the event to confirm it." He looked up at the bell, at the wheelwork, at Edmund. "How is she?"

"Honest, as far as I can tell."

"As far as you can tell." Corliss turned his cap over once. "Mr. Ashgrove. In my trade we have a saying: when a lock behaves strangely and every pin looks true, you stop looking at the pins and start looking at who's been near the door."

Edmund kept his face still. "Something's been near the clock?"

"I don't know. But there's a requisition clerk named Hobbes who has taken a sudden interest in horology. He's been up here twice this week with inventories. Measuring the case. Weighing things." Corliss put his cap back on. "Hobbes reports directly to Captain Vane, and Captain Vane has a requisition order drafted for this clock that only awaits, I'd wager, a failure worth filing it under. I'm not telling you anything. I'm just a tired sergeant talking to a tired apprentice about locks and doors."

He went back down the stairs. Edmund stood a long moment with the sergeant's words ticking through him alongside the clock's own beat.

Then he climbed down into the clockroom and began, very quietly, to go over the mechanism again—not the parts he knew, but the spaces between the parts. The places a man with an inventory and a reason could leave something small.

It took him twenty minutes, kneeling in the lamplight with a mirror on a stick, and he found it in the count-wheel housing: a sliver of hardened steel, no bigger than a thumbnail, wedged with wax into the path of the locking detent. Positioned so it would hold—for a day, maybe two—and then work itself free of the softening wax at exactly the wrong moment and jam the strike train mid-chime. Not a wrecking. A *scheduling*. Whoever placed it had wanted the clock to die publicly, at a witnessed hour, with an officer below and a captain conveniently elsewhere.

Edmund knelt there holding the sliver on his palm, and felt something in his chest go very still and very cold, the way the works of a stopped clock are still and cold.

So it was war, then. He had thought himself a repairman. He understood, in that moment, that he had been conscripted into a different trade entirely.

He did not remove it.

He looked at the sliver a long time—looked at the wax, the angle, the craft of it, and found himself grudgingly admiring the hand—and then he wrapped it in cloth, tucked it into his coat, and replaced it with a piece of soft pewter filed to the same shape. Pewter would shear clean when the detent struck it, jamming nothing, leaving behind a broken fragment that would tell any examiner exactly the story the saboteur wanted told: the clock failed of its own age, its own decay, its keeper's neglect.

Because if the trap sprang and nothing happened, Hobbes would simply try again, and Edmund could not stand guard over every gear tooth until the transport came. But if the trap sprang and *failed*, publicly, harmlessly, in front of witnesses—then Vane would have his failure, filed and signed, and Edmund would know his enemy had committed himself, and the requisition order would rest on sand.

It was a gambler's plan. Edmund had never gambled in his life. He wiped his palms on his apron, set the detent path true, and waited for midnight with his heart going faster than any escapement he'd ever regulated.

* * *

Midnight came up the valley like weather.

Below in the square, despite the hour and the cold, people had gathered—the garrison off-duty men, and among them, unmistakable even at distance by the set of her shoulders, Ivy, and beside her Tom Ashgrove in his good coat, and Marigold with a lantern. Word had gone round, the way word does in a small town, that the master clock would strike the hour for the first time truly mended since autumn, and half of Hallowmere had come to hear it, because a town that is losing its homes will stand in the snow to hear a bell ring true. It was the nearest thing to a vote anyone had taken in weeks.

Corliss stood at the tower door with two privates, checking his watch against the observatory signal. Edmund stood at the crutch of the great escapement, hands flat on the frame, feeling the seconds come up through the metal into his bones.

One minute. The minute hand gathered itself toward the top of the dial.

Thirty seconds.

Ten.

And the clock struck.

Twelve times, the great bell swung and spoke over the valley—twelve strokes, spaced and weighted, each one landing in the snow-hush and rolling out across the garrison camp, across the ridge, across the frozen fields, and each one met below by a murmur that grew, stroke by stroke, into something that was not quite cheering and was better than cheering. It was the sound of two thousand frightened people being given, for one minute, a thing they could rely on.

On the twelfth stroke, Edmund allowed himself to close his eyes.

When he opened them, he was already moving, because the plan required the next part and the next part was the hard part: he descended the tower stair at a pace that looked like urgency and arrived at the tower door just as Corliss turned, watch in hand.

"Struck true," Corliss said quietly. "Every interval. You heard it."

"I heard something worse." Edmund kept his voice low, pitched under the noise of the crowd. "Sergeant—the strike train. Something's loose in the count-wheel. I need to secure it tonight or tomorrow noon may not strike at all. I need the room sealed. Nobody in but me."

Corliss studied him for a long second with those tired fair eyes, and Edmund understood that the sergeant understood—not everything, perhaps, but the shape of it, the way you understand a lock is not behaving though every pin looks true.

"The room will be sealed," Corliss said. "By my own detail. And Mr. Ashgrove—" he glanced at the crowd, at the lantern-light on upturned faces, "—whatever you're about to do up there, do it fast."

* * *

What Edmund did, alone in the clockroom with the door barred and his lamp turned low, was commit his second crime of the week.

The pewter fragment had sheared exactly as calculated—he found the bright little corpse of it sitting in the detent path, a false wound, evidence of a failure that had never happened. With his finest graver he widened the score mark in the count-wheel housing, deepened a wear-groove that had taken thirty years to form, and aged the fresh cuts with graphite and oil until they read as decades. Then he took the true culprit—the steel sliver in its cloth—and hid it where no inventory clerk would ever weigh it: inside the hollow leg of the great case, behind a panel whose existence only he and, once, his mother had known.

He was screwing the last cover plate home when he heard, faintly, through two floors of timber and stone, the sound of the crowd below changing key.

Cheering curdling. Voices rising. A whistle—army whistle, two sharp notes.

Edmund crossed to the casement window and looked down into the square, and his stomach dropped through the floor of the tower, through the rock beneath it, and kept going.

Lanterns were converging on the tower door from the direction of headquarters—not the loose scatter of curious soldiers but a column, ordered, purposeful, with an officer at its head whose greatcoat and bearing Edmund could read even at that distance and in that light. Behind the column, harried and white-faced, trotted a small clerkish figure clutching a ledger to his chest like a breastplate.

Hobbes.

And at the head, unhurried, his breath smoking in the lantern light, Captain Vane.

They filled the square. They arranged themselves below the clock face as if for a portrait, and Vane sent Corliss—Ivy's account of it later, shouted up the stair—up to fetch the keeper of the clock, and Corliss came up slowly, heavily, and stood in the doorway of the clockroom looking at Edmund with an expression that contained apology, warning, and something that might, in a harder light, have been shame.

"Captain Vane," Corliss said. "Wants the works inspected. Tonight. On the grounds of—" he consulted a paper he plainly did not need to consult, "—irregularities reported during the midnight strike. Reported by Clerk Hobbes."

"But it struck true. Everyone heard it strike true."

"Yes," Corliss said. "Everyone heard it strike true." He let that sit between them a moment. "That's rather the problem, son. A clock that strikes true is of no use whatsoever to a man holding a requisition order for scrap."

* * *

The inspection took forty minutes, and Edmund spent it learning what it feels like to be dissected beside the thing you love.

Vane moved through the clockroom like a man touring a property he had already bought. He admired the going train aloud. He complimented the pinion work—"your hand, I think, not old Tom's; steadier"—and wrote things in a pocket book. Hobbes hovered at his elbow with the ledger and a candle, directing attention with little dry coughs: the count-wheel, Captain; the detent, Captain; observe the scoring, Captain, consistent with metal fatigue, consistent with neglect.

Vane crouched at the count-wheel housing for a long time. He took out his own glass. He looked at the sheared pewter fragment lying in the detent path, and at the scored groove above it, and he looked for a while longer than belief required.

Then he straightened, and brushed off his knee, and turned to Edmund with an expression of such perfect, courteous regret that Edmund's skin went cold all over, because he recognized it. It was the expression his mother's letters had taught him to fear: the face of a man reading figures in a ledger, deciding which ones to move.

"Ashgrove," Vane said. "This clock is dying. You know it, Hobbes knows it, the whole valley heard it laboring tonight. Fatigue in the count-wheel. Scoring in the detent path. Metal that should have been replaced years ago by a competent keeper." He closed his notebook. "The army will take it into requisition effective immediately. Brass of this quantity is needed for the war effort—fittings, cartridge cases, instruments. More useful than a bell that lies about the hour." He paused, delicately. "There is also the matter of responsibility. An apprentice, unregistered, unqualified, left sole charge of a civic instrument on which the discipline of an entire garrison depends. If the clock had failed tonight—if those twelve strokes had fallen wrong, with the colonel from the division standing in that square—it would have been your failure, Ashgrove. Yours, and your family's. I want you to understand how narrowly they've escaped sharing it."

"They haven't escaped anything," Edmund said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "You mean to melt it regardless. Tonight proved it runs true, and you're calling true-running evidence of failure. Sir." He added it late and made it sound like a nail.

Something flickered behind Vane's eyes—annoyance, quickly banked, and under it a thin seam of respect that was somehow worse than anger. "Sergeant Corliss," he said, without turning. "The apprentice questioned the findings of a commissioned inspection, in the hearing of the garrison. Take his statement. Formally. At headquarters." He drew on his gloves. "We'll begin the dismantling at first light. Hobbes will draw up the schedule."

He went down the stairs. Hobbes scuttled after him, and at the turn of the stair the clerk glanced back up at Edmund—one quick, narrow, satisfied glance, the glance of a man checking whether his trap had sprung on the animal he'd aimed it at—and finding instead a keeper standing calm amid his works, the satisfaction faltered into something warier. He had seen the clock run true too. He knew what the pewter fragment meant, even if Vane did not. And he now knew that Edmund knew.

The door boomed shut below. Corliss lingered, taking his time over the arrest papers that were not yet arrest papers.

"You planted it," Edmund said quietly. "The fragment. You let them find what I left for them."

Corliss did not deny it. He folded the blank statement forms away into his coat, unfinished, unsigned. "I delayed the paperwork," he said. "That's all I've got, and it isn't much, and Captain Vane counts other men's delays the way you count pendulum beats." He paused at the door, and for a moment the tired fair eyes were not tired at all. "First light, Ashgrove. Whatever you're going to do, the clock has until first light."

The stairs rang with his going. Edmund stood alone in the clockroom with the lamp burning low, listening to the mechanism tick—his clock, his mother's hiding place, the town's one honest voice—and understood with perfect cold clarity that the enemy was no longer the failing mechanism, or the requisition, or even Vane, who was merely winter. The enemy was a clerk with a grudge and a wax pot, and the war would be won or lost not in the square but here, in the dark, gear by gear, name by name, before dawn.

Above him, the great bell hung silent, holding its tongue for the first time in ninety years.

He picked up his tools, and began.
