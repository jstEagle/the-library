## 15. Corliss's Quiet Choice

The paperwork for a detention escort, Corporal Fenwick explained, was very simple: one form in triplicate, the prisoner's name entered thrice—once for the escort roster, once for the detention register, and once, inexplicably, for the quartermaster's ration count, since even a prisoner had to eat.

"Three times," Fenwick said, laying the forms on the requisition desk like a man dealing out a losing hand of cards. "Same name, three places. You'd think the army would trust itself by now."

"You'd think," said Sergeant Corliss, who had spent twenty-two years learning exactly how much the army trusted itself, which was to say: not at all, and with good reason.

Fenwick hovered. He was a young corporal with the unfortunate habit of standing the way a door stands when it can't decide whether it's open or shut. He wanted to be dismissed. He also wanted to be thanked. He also, Corllis suspected, wanted someone else to carry the actual message up to Captain Vane that the Ashgrove apprentice had been taken into custody, because delivering bad news to Vane was rather like delivering a live adder to a man holding a fork.

"Anything else, Sergeant?"

"No, Corporal. Well done." Corliss did not specify what had been done well, precisely, but Fenwick brightened as though a medal had been pinned somewhere invisible and left.

Corliss sat down at the desk and looked at the three forms.

The clockroom above him—the observatory's great tower, where the master clock hung like a brass heart in a stone chest—was silent. That was the thing he couldn't stop noticing. He'd been in Hallowmere six weeks now, long enough to have stopped hearing the clock entirely, the way you stop hearing your own pulse, and then yesterday it had stopped striking midnight before the whole assembled garrison, and now the silence was louder than any bell he'd ever stood under. The whole town flinched at it. Even the soldiers flinched, though most of them were too proud to say why. A garrison could survive without bread longer than it could survive without knowing what hour it was. Time, Corliss had come to understand, was not a luxury of civilians. It was the floor under everyone's feet.

And the boy who kept the floor had been arrested for fraud, sabotage, or insolence—depending on which officer you asked, which was itself an education in how the army arrived at truth.

Edmund Ashgrove was sitting in the guard room two doors down, awaiting escort to the detention shed behind the commissary. Corliss had put him there himself an hour ago, at Lieutenant Harrow's order, and the boy had gone quietly, hands ink-stained from the ledger they'd found open on the clerk's table, looking less like a criminal than like a man who'd been caught doing arithmetic in church.

Corliss picked up the pen. Dipped it. Held it over the first form, where it said, in the army's own printed confidence, *Name of Prisoner*.

He thought about his father's name.

This was the trouble with ledgers, Corliss had decided somewhere around his fortieth year, having given the matter more attention than a sergeant ought. They looked so innocent lying closed. A book is a book. But open one, and every name in it becomes a fact, and every fact becomes a handle, and sooner or later somebody comes along who likes pulling handles.

His father's name had been Aldous Corliss, and it had been written in a ledger in the port town of Wrenholt, and what it had said beside it was *cooper, debt outstanding*, and what had happened because of it was that on a Tuesday in March, thirty-one years ago, a clerk with clean cuffs had read the entry aloud and two men with a cart had made it true. His mother's name had been in the same book. His sister Bett's name had been in it too, though she'd been nine years old and owed nobody anything except perhaps an apology for breaking a neighbor's window, which the ledger had not thought relevant.

Aldous Corliss had died inside two years in the works colony at Marrow Fen. His wife had lasted four. Bett had been apprenticed out to a dye house and lost to letters that came thinner each year and then stopped altogether. And Thomas Corliss, aged eleven, had been taken in by an aunt who resented him in a quiet, durable way, and had learned early that the safest thing a person could be was legible—clean boots, correct forms, a signature in every box, nothing for anyone to pull.

He had joined the army at seventeen because the army, of all institutions, at least told you honestly that it owned you.

And here he was, twenty-two years on, holding a pen over a form, about to write a boy's name into a system that ate names, because the boy had done the one unforgivable thing: he had tried to make the ledger kinder than its rules allowed.

*It's not kindness,* said the voice of Lieutenant Harrow, who was not a monster, only a man who had never been on the wrong page of anything. *It's fraud against a military requisition. There are procedures.*

There were always procedures. In Wrenholt there had been procedures too. The clerk with clean cuffs had followed them beautifully.

Corliss set down the pen.

* * *

The guard room smelled of lamp oil, wet wool, and the particular despair of army tea. Edmund Ashgrove sat on the bench with his wrists free—they hadn't bothered with irons; the boy had the look of furniture, not flight risk—and he straightened up when Corliss came in, which was polite of him, considering.

"Sergeant."

"Apprentice." Corliss pulled the other chair round and sat, unhurried, and set the tea down between them. Two cups. He didn't explain this. The boy didn't ask. They both drank tea that tasted like hot regret and let the silence do some work first.

"You mended my bootlace loop last week," Corliss said finally. "On the gate hinge. It had been rattling since we arrived."

Edmund blinked. "That was a strap, not a lace."

"It was a miracle, is what it was. That hinge had beaten three men before you." He drank his tea. "You're wondering why I'm here instead of marching you to the shed."

"I assumed you'd get to me eventually."

"Assumed correctly. Only I find I've got a problem, and you're the only man in this valley who might appreciate it." Corliss took the three forms from his jacket and laid them on the bench between them, face up, blank as fresh snow. "To take you into detention, properly, I have to enter your name three times. Once here, once there, once for rations. Three entries. And every entry is a nail."

"A nail?"

"In a coffin. Or a door. Depends who's holding the hammer after me." He turned the top sheet so the boy could read the heading. "Captain Vane has ordered a full audit of the transport rolls before the last convoy leaves. Every name checked twice against the ledger. Any irregularity—any at all—goes to him personally." He paused. "You understand what that means for the people Ivy Ashgrove has tucked into her cellars."

Edmund went very still. It was a craftsman's stillness, Corliss noticed—not fear, but the total attention of a man listening for a tick that shouldn't be there. "How many do you know about?"

"That's not a question I'm going to answer, and it's not one you should either. Let's say the number is *more than none*, and leave it in the dark where it's currently safe." He tapped the forms. "What I will tell you is this. Vane means to strike undocumented persons from the list entirely. Not detain them—there's no room and no appetite for it. Just remove them. Erase them from the record, and then the record becomes true by force. Whatever happens to people whose names aren't in the book happens outside the book, where nobody has to read about it."

"He'd turn colonists out in January."

"He'd turn *entries* out in January," Corliss corrected gently. "In his mind those are different things. That's the whole trick of a ledger, boy. It lets a decent man do indecent sums and call it arithmetic."

Edmund looked down at his ink-stained fingers. "You said you had a problem."

"I did say that." Corliss leaned back. The chair creaked with the authority of long service. "Here it is. Twenty-two years ago I was a corporal's clerk in a coastal district, and my job was to copy transport manifests for civilian evacuations—famine year, if you want the history. And I copied them faithfully. Every name, every berth, every crossing-out. Faithful as a church bell. And one night I noticed that a family named Corliss—no relation, I told myself, though of course—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I noticed their names had been struck from a manifest because a merchant had bought the berths. Bought them. Legal as sunrise. And I copied the correction faithfully too, because it was my job, and because I was nineteen and legible, and because I had learned that the safest thing a person could be was exactly what the forms required."

The lamp flickered. Neither man moved to fix it.

"When I got my mother's letter telling me where my father had died," Corliss went on, "it was the manifest that let me work out the date. My father's death and my own faithful copying shared a week. Isn't that something? The army gave me a trade, and the trade taught me to kill my own family neatly, in triplicate, and file it."

Edmund's voice, when it came, was careful. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." Corliss stood, crossed to the small barred window, and looked out at the yard, where the snow was doing its patient, indifferent work. "I've spent twenty-two years being faithful. I'm good at it. Ask anyone. And I am telling you, apprentice, that last night I lay in my bunk and listened to your clock not strike, and I thought: that silence is the sound of my whole life. Everything running perfectly and meaning nothing."

"What are you asking me to do?"

"I'm not asking you to do anything. I'm a sergeant. I don't ask prisoners to do things; that's entrapment, and I've got standards." He came back to the bench and, deliberately, tore the top form in half. Then the second. The third he held a moment, ration count, almost comic in its pettiness, and tore that too. "Your name will appear nowhere tonight. Detention requires paperwork, and paperwork, as any soldier knows, takes time. Sometimes days. Sometimes, when a sergeant is old and slow and keeps mislaying things, indefinitely."

Edmund stared at the torn paper. "They'll notice."

"They'll notice eventually. Which brings me to the useful part." From his inner pocket Corliss drew a folded sheet—cheap paper, penciled columns—and set it in the boy's ink-stained hands. "Vane's audit schedule. Final inspection of the master rolls is Friday at ten o'clock, conducted personally, in the clerk's office. After Friday, nothing can be changed without his signature. Before Friday—" he shrugged, elaborately, the shrug of a man who understood nothing whatsoever about forgery, engraving, or hope, "—well. Paperwork takes time."

"Why?" Edmund asked it simply, no accusation in it, which somehow made it harder. "Why risk this? You don't know me. You don't know these people."

Corliss considered several answers. The honest ones all involved a dye-house girl named Bett and a window broken in 1868, and he wasn't drunk enough, and probably wouldn't ever be, to hand those over.

"Because I've spent my life guarding a machine," he said instead, "and lately I've started wondering whether I was ever on the right side of the gears." He buttoned his coat. "Friday, ten o'clock. If I were a man inclined to advice—which I'm not, being merely a faithful servant of the Crown—I'd say whatever needs doing wants doing before then, and doing *well*. Vane misses nothing. Except, historically, the things carried out by men he considers too dull to matter." A dry pause. "Dullness is the last honest disguise left to us. Wear it."

At the door he stopped, hand on the latch, and didn't turn around.

"The tea was terrible," he said. "Drink it anyway. Cold cells make cold men, and cold men sign things faster."

Then he was gone, boots ringing on the stone, leaving behind him one prisoner, two ruined forms, three cups' worth of regret, and a schedule that weighed, in Edmund Ashgrove's hands, considerably more than paper.

Above them both, in the dark of the tower, the master clock waited—withheld its next hour like a held breath—and somewhere below the flagstones, beneath the cellars where hidden families counted heartbeats instead of bells, the longest night of the century had already begun keeping time of its own.
