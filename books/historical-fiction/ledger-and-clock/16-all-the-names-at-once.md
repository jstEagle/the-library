## 16. All the Names at Once

*Edmund*

The cellar smelled of lamp oil, wet stone, and eleven people pretending to sleep.

Edmund sat at the workbench beneath the great clock's foundations, where the tower's heartbeat came through the masonry as a tremor he could feel in his teeth. He had spread the ledger open before him — Corliss's stolen audit schedule tucked under its leather corner like a bookmark that could get them all hanged — and beside it, a saucer of ink, three pens ground to different points, and Ivy.

Ivy was eating an apple.

"You're going to have to stop doing that," he said.

"Doing what?" She took another bite. The crunch echoed up the stairwell like a gunshot in a library.

"Being *cheerful*. It's nearly midnight. We're committing fraud against His Majesty's Army."

"We're not," Ivy said comfortably, "committing anything yet. You're just staring at the page as though it might bite you. And it's not fraud if it's true, is it? That's what I keep coming back to. Mrs. Halloway *is* a laundress. Mr. Petrosian really does mend boots — he's mended mine twice and charged me fair. We're not inventing people, Edmund. We're only writing down the truth a year late." She gestured with the apple core. "The army's the one lying. They're lying by omission. Whole pages of omission."

He wanted to argue. He found, instead, that her logic had already crept into him the way her cheerfulness always did — through a door he hadn't known he'd left open.

Because she was right, and he knew it in his hands before he knew it in his head. He had spent the last two nights practicing letterforms on scrap paper until his eyes burned: clerk's hand, registry hand, the slightly cramped italic of the parish rolls. Forgery was engraving's ugly cousin, and he had discovered he had a talent for it that made him feel ill. Every hour he'd ever worked honestly seemed to sit in the room with him now, arms folded, disappointed.

But then he looked at the names on Corliss's list. Eleven names from the cellars, plus the Ashgrove household, plus himself — a boy who did not exist in any book in England except, perhaps, one written in a dead woman's hand and hidden inside a clock.

"The berth," he said quietly. "We still only have the one."

"I've been thinking about that." Ivy pulled a stool over and sat close enough that their shoulders touched. "Tom says the transport manifest lists berths by household. Family groups travel together — one line per family, head of household named, dependents counted but not itemized. Corliss told me. Well. Corliss told me most of it and I charmed the rest out of his corporal, who has a weakness for ginger biscuits."

"Ivy—"

"So we don't need five berths for five names. We need *one family* that happens to contain everyone." She said it simply, the way she'd once said *we'll hide them in the cellars, obviously*, as though rearranging the fates of eleven strangers were a matter of common housekeeping. "Mrs. Halloway becomes my aunt. The Petrosians become cousins on Tom's side — nobody can prove they aren't, because nobody knows anything about Tom's side, because Tom claims to have been born fully grown out of a hedgerow."

"He says that about the hedgerow?"

"Every harvest festival. It's practically doctrine."

Edmund laughed — a short, startled sound that the cellar swallowed immediately, and he pressed his hand over his mouth like a boy caught laughing in church. Eleven people shifted in their blankets below the floorboards. Somewhere among them a baby slept, which meant somewhere a mother lay awake listening to every sound the tower made.

He sobered. He picked up the pen.

* * *

*Ivy*

Her brother — not her brother, her brother; she had decided sometime around ten o'clock that both things were true and she refused to be talked out of either — wrote like a man defusing something.

Ivy watched him work and ate her second apple, because watching Edmund suffer made her hungry, which she suspected said something unflattering about her character and which she chose not to examine. He hunched. He held his breath between letters. He'd write a name, then sit back and stare at it as though the ink might apologize for itself.

"You're going to faint," she told him, "if you don't breathe. And if you faint you'll land face-first in the ledger, and then we'll have to explain why Mr. Petrosian has an ink mustache."

"I'm thinking."

"You're *agonizing*. Thinking looks different. Thinking looks like me."

That got half a smile out of him, there and gone. He dipped the pen and wrote: *Halloway, Ruth — laundress — b. Whitstable, 1851.* Then, beneath it, in the dependent column: *niece to Ashgrove, I.*

"That's you," he said. "You're the anchor. If the kinship runs through your name, the trail leads to a girl whose whole life is documented in this town — school records, church records, the incident with the maypole."

"The incident with the maypole is *documented*?"

"Reverend Lyle keeps notes."

"Reverend Lyle is a menace." But she felt something loosen in her chest when she looked at the entry. Her own name, standing in the ledger not as herself but as a door — a doorway with Ruth Halloway walking through it, a woman Ivy had known for nine days and already loved with the fierce, unreasonable love she reserved for people who deserved it, which was nearly everyone, which was the whole trouble with her, according to Marigold, who said it while beaming.

Ruth Halloway had come up the hill road in November with frostbitten feet and a debtors' colony's worth of silence in her mouth. She had wept the first night — quietly, so as not to wake anyone — and the second night she had insisted on scrubbing the kitchen floor at four in the morning because idleness frightened her more than discovery did. Ivy had sat on the stairs and watched her and thought: *this is what it costs, being erased. This is what it takes to give someone their name back.*

"Next one," Edmund said. "Petrosian. What trade do we give the son?"

"He mends boots. Give him bootmending. And the daughter — she's clever with figures, she helped me tally the stores. Seamstress? No—" Ivy snapped her fingers. "Bookkeeper's assistant. Clerk's daughter. Make her respectable and dull. Nobody interrogates a dull girl."

"Dull girls," Edmund said gravely, "are the backbone of the empire."

"And don't you forget it."

He wrote, and she watched the ledger change. It was strange — she had grown up believing the book was a kind of monster. It lived behind the clerk's rail at the requisition office like a fat black toad, and grown men went pale before it, and her mother spoke of it in the voice other people used for weather warnings. But seen from this side of the desk, lamplit, open, waiting, it wasn't a monster at all. It was just paper. It was whoever held the pen.

Which meant, she supposed, that it had always been whoever held the pen. Someone had written the colony families out of existence with this same tidy alphabet. The pen didn't care. The pen was a tool, like an awl or a bell-rope, and it would do whatever a pair of hands asked of it.

Tonight her hands were asking it for mercy.

* * *

*Edmund*

By the third hour, something happened that Edmund could only describe as the work taking over.

It was like the escapement. For years he had fought the delicate parts of clocks — gripped too hard, breathed wrong, wanted the mechanism to obey him. Then old Tom had put a loupe in his eye and said, *you're not driving it, lad, you're keeping time with it,* and everything changed. Craft was not conquest. Craft was listening so closely that your hands stopped being yours.

The forgery became craft somewhere around the name Aldous Wren.

Wren was the hardest case: a man of fifty with no trade anyone could vouch for, who had spent nineteen years in the colony breaking limestone and who flinched, still, at raised voices. A blank. A hole in the world shaped like a person. Edmund had stared at his line for twenty minutes while Ivy raided the biscuit tin, and then he had remembered the letters.

His mother's letters, spread in a folder under his elbow all night, consulted again and again for dates and spellings — his inheritance, his evidence, his wound. And in the third letter, the one written in the colony's eleventh winter, a single line: *Aldous Wren teaches the children their letters when the teacher is ill. He has a beautiful hand. He says a beautiful hand is a kind of freedom no one can confiscate.*

Edmund looked across the cellar stairwell, where Wren sat propped against the wall, unable to sleep, whittling nothing in particular.

"Mr. Wren," he called softly. "Your letters. In the colony. You taught reading?"

The old man looked up. In the lamplight his face was a landscape of quarried lines. "When they let me. Which was never often enough."

"What hand did you use? Your own script — what did it look like?"

And Aldous Wren smiled — the first smile anyone in the cellars had gotten out of him — and held out his palm. "Give me the pen, boy, and I'll show you."

So it went, for the rest of the night. One by one, the hidden came up the stairwell into the lamplight, blinking like creatures from underground, which is precisely what they were, and gave Edmund the pieces of themselves that the colony had failed to grind away: a trade, a birth village, a grandmother's maiden name, the date a daughter was born during a snowstorm, the fact that Marta Petrosian had been baptized in a river because the colony had no church. Edmund took each fragment and set it into the ledger like a jewel into its setting, and Ivy stood at his shoulder supplying kinships — *cousin, niece, goddaughter* — weaving the strangers into the Ashgrove tree until the family groaned with relatives nobody had ever heard of and everybody suddenly had.

At some point Ruth Halloway began to cry again, but differently this time. She stood looking at her own name in the book — *laundress, b. Whitstable, 1851* — and she put her fingers on the ink before it dried and said, "That's me. That's — oh. Oh, that's me," as though Edmund had performed a resurrection rather than an entry.

"It's you," Ivy said firmly, hugging her round the shoulders. "It always was. The book was late, that's all. Books are terrible about being on time. Ask my brother — he's engaged to one."

"I am not *engaged* to the—"

"Hush, you're working."

* * *

*Ivy*

Toward dawn, the last line was the hardest, and it was Edmund's own.

They had saved it deliberately, the way you leave the smallest gear till last because you want to be fresh-handed for it. Everyone else was entered, cross-referenced, kinshipped, safe. The manifest, when Corliss's schedule came due, would read like a family reunion of the mildly eccentric: the Ashgroves and their sprawling, improbable, entirely invented-and-entirely-true relations, claiming the last civilian berth as a single household.

Everyone but the boy holding the pen.

Ivy watched him look at the empty space after his own non-name and understood, with a lurch, what he was going to do. She'd understood it for days, probably — since the night he'd shown her the letters and said, *they weren't even the first ones to lie, Ivy, the colony books were forged before my mother ever ran.* He had a look he got when he was solving something backwards, starting from the answer and working toward the grief.

"No," she said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're going to leave yourself off. Or enter yourself as 'keeper of the clock' like you're a piece of furniture." She grabbed his wrist. His pulse was going like a small anxious bird under her fingers. "Edmund. Look at me. There are ways—"

"There aren't. Thirteen names, one berth. The arithmetic doesn't bend, Ivy. I've tried bending it every way there is." He said it gently, which was worse. "If I'm on that manifest, someone else isn't. And I know what's down those cellar steps now. I know all thirteen of them by name and birthday and the color of the blanket they cry into at night. I'm not trading Marta Petrosian for myself. I won't. So the question isn't whether I stay. The question is what I am, officially, when I stay — because Vane will want a name for the arrest warrant either way, and I'd rather choose it myself."

She hated him. She loved him so much she could hardly see and she hated him completely, and she told him so.

"I know," he said. "Me too."

For a long moment neither of them wrote. Above them, through six floors of tower, the master clock — half-gutted, half-healed, Edmund's patient — ticked on with the few works he'd managed to restore, marking out the hours they were spending. Somewhere beyond the ridge road, the garrison bugles would sound reveille within the hour. The century was running out like sand.

Then Ivy leaned over the ledger, took a second pen, and wrote — in her own untidy, unmistakable, entirely undocumented hand — a single word in the margin beside the space where her brother's name should have been.

*Keeper.*

"Not furniture," she said. "Keeper. There's a difference. Furniture gets left behind." She tapped the word. "Keepers *stay*. On purpose. It's the oldest job in the world and nobody's ever had to forge it, because it's true."

Edmund looked at the word for a long time. Then he did something she would remember for the rest of her life, however long the rest of it turned out to be: he copied it into the ledger proper, in perfect clerk's italic, added the date, blotted it, and closed the book.

Thirteen names saved. One name transformed. Not one of them left alone.

And it was exactly then, with the ink barely dry and the east windows graying, that they heard boots on the observatory gravel — many boots, unhurried, in the rhythm of men who expected to be obeyed — and a voice Ivy knew from nightmares and requisition notices calling up the stairwell:

"Open the tower. Captain's inspection. Bring the ledger."
