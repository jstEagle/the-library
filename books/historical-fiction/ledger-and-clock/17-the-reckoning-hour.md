## 17. The Reckoning Hour

The last wheel went into the great clock's train at a quarter past six in the morning, and Edmund Ashgrove sat back on his heels and let himself, for one full minute, feel proud of it.

The escapement sang. That was the only word for it. After three weeks of dead silence—after the sabotage, the arrest that hadn't happened, the humiliation Vane had tried to hang around his neck like a millstone—the master clock of Hallowmere ticked again with the deep, patient heartbeat of a thing that intended to go on telling the truth for another hundred years. Edmund had re-cut the pallet faces himself, by lamplight, with a graver finer than anything the army owned and never would.

He wiped his hands on his apron and permitted himself a small, private smile. *Let it strike noon,* he thought. *Let it strike noon in front of all of them.*

Then he heard boots on the tower stair, and the minute hand of his contentment jumped forward an hour.

Captain Vane climbed into the clockroom the way he did everything: precisely, without wasted motion, and as though the room ought to be grateful. Two soldiers waited at the head of the stairs behind him, which was new. Edmund's stomach performed a slow, deliberate turn like a heavy flywheel starting up.

"Apprentice." Vane's breath smoked in the cold of the tower. He wore his grey greatcoat buttoned to the throat, and under his arm he carried—not a sword, not a pistol, but a ledger. The ledger. The town's ledger, bound in scuffed blue leather, its corners worn round by forty years of clerks' hands.

"Sir." Edmund stood, keeping his voice level. "You're up early."

"I'm always up early. It's a habit I recommend to men whose futures are uncertain." Vane set the ledger down on Edmund's workbench, on top of a clean cloth Edmund had laid out for exactly this purpose and now resented having been right about. He opened it. He turned pages. He did it slowly, and he watched Edmund while he did it, the way a man watches another man's hands rather than the cards.

"You've done good work here," Vane said. "I'll say that plainly, because I say everything plainly. The mechanism runs. I heard it from my quarters this morning—that quarter-chime. I haven't slept well in this valley, apprentice, and your clock woke me, and do you know what I thought?" He paused over a page. "I thought: there is one honest thing left in this town."

Edmund said nothing. In his experience, saying nothing was the most versatile tool in the kit.

"And then," Vane went on, turning another page, and another, his gloved finger travelling down columns of names, trades, births—"then I came to the Ashgrove household. And I found something curious. Curious things bother me more than dishonest things, did you know that? A lie has a shape. You can hold a lie up to the light and see where it's been mended. But curiosity—" he looked up— "curiosity means somebody built something I can't see the shape of yet."

* * *

The trouble had started, Edmund would later realize, with a comma.

Not a comma—a stroke. Ivy had entered the Hollis family from the cellars as cooper's kin, and her pen had caught on the rough paper and thrown a tiny hair of ink off the tail of the *l* in Hollis. A hair of ink no bigger than an eyelash. Corliss had missed it. Ivy had missed it. But Captain Vane, who audited requisition rolls the way other men counted their own heartbeats, had put a magnifying glass to every entry made after the fourteenth of December, and eyelash-sized hairs were exactly the sort of thing magnifying glasses existed for.

"There are eleven entries in this ledger," Vane said, "written in ink that is fresher than the entries above and below them. Eleven households, apprentice. Cooper's kin. Wheelwright's cousins. A tanner's brother-in-law who does not appear in any parish register between here and the coast. And every single one of them traces back, if you follow the trades, to families that came out of the debtors' colony at Marshendon." He closed the ledger gently, almost tenderly, which was somehow worse than slamming it. "Somebody has been writing names into this book that God and the Crown never wrote there first."

The clock ticked. Somewhere below them the garrison was waking; he could hear the far-off clatter of the mess hall, a bugle clearing its throat. Above them, through the louvres, the sky was going the colour of pewter.

"It wasn't the townsfolk," Edmund said. "If you're going to punish anyone for the handwriting—"

"Oh, I know it wasn't the townsfolk." Vane walked along the great case, trailing two fingers across the wood the way a man might trail fingers along a fence he intended to buy. "Your sister Ivy holds a pen like she holds a broom—enthusiastically and at arm's length. No, these alterations are fine work. Controlled strokes. An engraver's hand." He stopped. Turned. "Yours, I'd have said. If I didn't know you for a man who has spent his whole life terrified of doing anything wrong."

The words landed harder than they should have. Edmund kept his face still, but inside him something shifted, some load-bearing beam groaning under new weight.

"The penalty for falsifying a Crown register," Vane said, "is transportation. Which is ironic," he added, with the ghost of a smile that made Edmund's skin crawl, "since transportation is precisely what everyone in this valley is so desperate to arrange for themselves. So here we are. You, me, a ledger full of crimes, and a clock that strikes in five hours." He glanced up at the dial movement overhead. "Shall we talk about what you'd like to trade?"

* * *

They talked. Or rather, Vane talked, and Edmund listened, and between them, without either man quite saying so, a market opened.

Vane wanted three things, and he wanted them in order. First, the fraud contained—no scandal reaching the audit board in the capital, because a captain who couldn't keep his own requisition clean didn't get his brigade, and a man who didn't get his brigade was just a major with better handwriting. Second, the brass: the clock's fittings were already on his seizure manifest, and he had no intention of un-writing that paperwork, because the brass was worth real money and real money was the only argument his superiors had ever respected. Third—and this was the one he pretended not to want—he wanted the truth about the hidden panel.

"You knew," Edmund said quietly, when Vane mentioned it. "About the panel. Before today."

"I suspected documents were concealed in this clock before I ever arrived in Hallowmere. The colony's records office reported papers missing when Marshendon closed. Letters, principally. Correspondence that certain people preferred not to survive." Vane's eyes were pale and patient as winter light. "And then a clever young man opens the very case those papers vanished from, and repairs the clock nobody else could repair, and looks at me with guilt written on him in letters a foot high. You've been carrying something in this tower since the day you came, apprentice. I merely wondered what."

Edmund looked at the clock. At the case his own mother's hands might once have stood beside, in another life. At the small panel near the chime mechanism, empty now except for dust and the ghost-shape where paper had lain pressed for twenty years.

And he understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only at the point of no return, what he had to spend.

* * *

"If I give you the truth," Edmund said, "you protect the eleven entries. All of them. They travel on the last transport under the names in that ledger, and no clerk of yours ever questions them again. And the Ashgroves—all four of them—board that transport with their own berths untouched. Those are my terms."

"Those are outrageous terms."

"They're the only ones I have."

Vane studied him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a short, dry sound with no warmth in it at all, like a lock clicking. "You've changed, apprentice. Three weeks ago you couldn't meet my eye. Now you're negotiating with a captain in his own garrison." He pulled out a chair and sat, which Edmund took as a good sign; men preparing to refuse you don't sit. "Show me."

So Edmund knelt at the base of the clock case, and with hands that were steadier than he had any right to expect, he showed Vane where the panel had been. And then he went to his tool chest, to the bottom tray beneath the false bottom Ivy had helped him build, and brought out the bundle.

His mother's letters. Twenty years old, soft-cornered, tied with a ribbon that had once been red.

"My mother," Edmund said, "was born Elowen Pryce. She entered the Marshendon colony in 1877 for a debt of nine pounds, fourteen shillings—a debt her husband incurred and died owing. The colony's intake record lists her as unmarried. It lists her son as stillborn." His voice held. He was surprised by that, and grateful. "Neither statement is true. Here—" he untied the ribbon with care, as though the letters were escapements, as though rough handling might stop them— "here is her own hand, describing the birth. Her own hand, naming the midwife. Her own hand, recording how she gave the child to a passing family of clockmakers rather than watch him enter the colony's foundling rolls, where children had a way of becoming numbers and then becoming nothing."

He laid the letters out on the workbench one by one, in order, the way he'd lay out a disassembled movement. Vane read. To his credit—or whatever passed for credit in a man like him—he read thoroughly, tilting each page to the greying window light.

"The colony's records were falsified first," Edmund said. "Before I ever touched a ledger. Before Ivy dipped a pen. The Crown's own clerks erased a living child to make their arithmetic tidy, and hid the proof in a clock, and called it order." He met Vane's eyes. "You said curiosity bothers you more than lies. Then be curious about this: which of us forged first? Because it wasn't me. I'm only the second man in this valley to write a name where the record says there wasn't one. My mother's clerks were the first."

* * *
