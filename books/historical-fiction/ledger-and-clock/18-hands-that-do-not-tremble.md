## 18. Hands That Do Not Tremble

The morning of the reckoning came down cold and clear, the kind of winter light that showed everything and forgave nothing. Edmund had been awake since four, not from fear—or not only from fear—but because his hands would not stop rehearsing the work. They moved against his blanket in the dark, fitting a pallet to an escape wheel that wasn't there, over and over, until dawn grayed the window and he gave up pretending.

He dressed by candle stub. Downstairs, the Ashgrove house was quiet in the particular way it had been quiet for three days now, ever since Vane had stood in the clockroom with the altered ledger under one arm and made his arithmetic plain: the clock runs true at noon, or the family travels nowhere at all—and certain irregular persons travel somewhere considerably worse.

Marigold was already up. Of course she was. She sat at the kitchen table with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she hadn't touched, and when Edmund came in she looked up the way mothers do when they have been listening for footsteps all night.

"There's bread," she said.

"I'm not hungry."

"You'll eat anyway. A man's hands shake on an empty stomach, and yours have no business shaking today."

It was such a Marigold thing to say—love disguised as practical instruction—that Edmund almost laughed. He cut two slices, buttered them badly, ate them standing up because sitting felt like wasting time he might need later. Tom appeared at the top of the stairs in his nightshirt, looked down at him, and said nothing at all, which from Tom was a whole sermon. Then Ivy's door opened and shut again quickly, as if she'd only been checking he was real, and then the house went back to its held breath.

* * *

The observatory hill was white to the treeline, and the garrison flag snapped above it like something impatient. Edmund climbed the road with his toolbag on his shoulder and felt every eye of the sentries tracking him—not with hostility, exactly, but with the flat curiosity soldiers give to anything that might be trouble. He had become trouble. It still astonished him, a little. He had spent twenty years being the opposite of trouble, being furniture, being useful rather than known, and now his name—or the names he had written—were the most dangerous objects in Hallowmere.

Vane was waiting inside the clockroom. He had arrived early, which told Edmund the captain had slept no better than he had, whatever he wanted anyone to believe. The man stood before the great clock's open case with his hands clasped behind his back, studying the gear train the way a man studies a horse he intends to buy and possibly eat.

"Apprentice."

"Captain."

"The escapement." Vane did not turn around. "You've replaced the pallets."

"Two of them. Recut from brass stock. The originals were worn past truing—I could have adjusted them, but adjusted worn pallets are a promise you can't keep."

Something flickered across Vane's face—annoyance, perhaps, that the answer was competent. "And the sabotage? The clerk's little arrangement?"

Edmund had spent the previous evening undoing it: a sliver of tin wedged against the fourth wheel, invisible unless you knew where to look, timed to stop the train just short of midnight. He had removed it, examined it, and kept it in his waistcoat pocket, where it sat now like a small cold coin. He had not yet decided what to do with it. Evidence was a currency too, and he was learning to count.

"Repaired," he said simply. "The clock holds no grudges. That's rather the point of it."

Now Vane turned. In the gray light through the tower lancets his face was unreadable, but his eyes went once—quickly, involuntarily—to the hidden panel behind the chime barrel. Empty now. Edmund had emptied it himself, the letters sewn into his shirt lining, riding against his heart like contraband of the soul.

"Noon," Vane said. "If it errs by so much as a second against my chronometer, we will discuss your family's passage in different terms."

"And if it doesn't err?"

"Then I will be forced," Vane said, with the ghost of something that was not quite a smile, "to admire craftsmanship I intend to melt down in the spring. Wind it. Let us see what you've made."

* * *

Winding the great clock took forty minutes and both arms, and Edmund did it with the crowd gathering below without looking down even once. Word had traveled, the way word does in a town under occupation, moving faster than any courier: the master clock would strike noon today, first time since the failure, and the officer who owned the valley would stand beneath it while it happened. By eleven the square below the tower held half of Hallowmere, breath steaming, faces tipped upward like a field of pale flowers. Edmund could see them between the louvers when he allowed himself to look: Mrs. Fenwick with her market basket though there was nothing to buy; the Hollis brothers; old Widow Crane who hadn't climbed the hill in nine years, helped along by two of the cellar families whose names existed nowhere except in a ledger Edmund had rewritten by candlelight.

They had come, he understood, to watch him bet his life in public. Or not his life—that was too dramatic, too much like the penny dreadfuls Ivy smuggled home. They had come to watch him bet *them*. Every swing of the pendulum was their passage, their spring papers, their names spoken aloud at last. No craftsman had ever worked under heavier weights, and yet the strange mercy of it, the thing Edmund could not have explained to anyone, was this: once his hands were inside the mechanism, the weight vanished.

This was the secret he had never told anyone about clockwork. Fear lives in the person, not the work. Inside the movement there was no Captain Vane, no ledger, no colony, no dead mother—there was only the third wheel's mesh, the drop of each pallet, the beat of the pendulum, true or not true, and a truth that could be measured and corrected. The clock was honest all the way down. Whatever else Edmund had become—a liar, a forger, a keeper of other people's secrets—he had built this repair the way his father-figure Tom taught him: every hour accounted for, nothing fudged, nothing hidden in the works. Well. Almost nothing.

At quarter to twelve he set the hands. Vane stood beside him with his chronometer open, a silver disc catching the light, and Sergeant Corliss stood at the stairhead pretending to check a clipboard he had checked eleven times already. Corliss would not meet Edmund's eye, and Edmund loved him for it. There are men whose loyalty can only survive if it is never acknowledged, and Corliss was one of them.

"One minute," Vane said.

Edmund wiped his palms on his apron and folded his hands behind his back, because a man watching a clock he has repaired should look like a man who expects it to work. His heart was going like the flywheel of the governor, but his hands—his hands were still. He noticed this with detached wonder, the way you notice rain stopping. Twenty years of filing, drilling, poising wheels, burnishing pivots, had done this: trained the trembling out of him so thoroughly that even terror couldn't find the way back in. Tom used to say a clockmaker's hands belong to the work, not the man. Today, for the first time, Edmund understood it as a mercy.

The chronometer ticked. Below, the crowd had gone quiet in that way crowds do, hundreds of people breathing shallowly at once.

The minute hand touched the hour.

The great clock drew its breath—the lift of the striking weight engaging, a shudder through the floor, felt in the teeth—and then the hammer fell, and the bell spoke.

Twelve times. Deep, unhurried, perfect. Each stroke separated from the next by precisely the interval the design demanded, no crowding, no hesitation, the count clean as a ledger line. On the twelfth stroke the sound rolled out over the frozen valley and came back off the ridge road, and the crowd below made a noise Edmund had never heard from a crowd before—half cheer, half exhalation, as if the whole town had been holding its breath for three days and had finally, together, let it out.

In the silence after, Vane closed his chronometer with a small decisive click.

"Not a second," he said.

"Not a second," Edmund agreed.

They regarded one another. Here was the hinge on which everything turned, and both of them knew it. Vane could not deny the workmanship—the entire valley had heard it strike; a captain who called a true clock false would be confessing either incompetence or fraud, and Vane was neither incompetent nor brave enough for the second. But neither could Vane walk away, because walking away meant honoring a bargain struck with an apprentice of no record, ratified by a ledger full of names that Vane had privately begun to suspect—and could no longer prove—were not what they claimed.

What he did, in the end, was the most military thing possible. He converted necessity into policy.

"The mechanism is army property," Vane said, "as I stated. However—" and here he paused, choosing words like a man crossing ice—"a requisitioned asset requires a keeper. Civilian maintenance staff being unavailable in the numbers required." He looked at Edmund with those cold, weighing eyes. "You are hereby entered on the rolls as keeper of the master clock, Hallowmere Station. Your... household arrangements are your own affair, so long as the clock runs. If it stops again, apprentice, the arrangement stops with it. Am I understood?"

"Perfectly, sir."

"See that you remain perfectly understandable." Vane gathered his coat. At the stairhead he paused, back still turned. "The panel behind the chime barrel. It was well concealed. Whoever built this clock hid things competently." A beat. "So, apparently, do you. Good day."

His boots rang down the iron stair, and Corliss lingered a moment longer, and when he finally glanced up, the sergeant's face did something complicated—relief, apology, and a promise all at once—and then he followed his captain down into the square, where the people of Hallowmere were already turning toward one another, touching sleeves, laughing in the careful way of people who have been given back a piece of tomorrow and don't want to jostle it.

* * *

Edmund stayed in the clockroom a long while after they'd gone. He sat on the winding stool with his back against the case, feeling the movement breathe above him—tick, tick, tick, the escapement letting time out in equal measures, honest as bread. From his waistcoat pocket he took the sliver of tin, the saboteur's wedge, and turned it over in his fingers. He thought about carrying it to Corliss. He thought about keeping it. He thought about the clerk who had placed it, and about how a man like Vane would use it—against whom, and for what.

Below, the bells' echo had settled into the bones of the tower, and the afternoon light came through the lancets gold now instead of gray. Somewhere down in the cellars, Ivy was telling the hidden families the news; he could imagine her voice, bright and reckless, spending itself as always. Somewhere on the platform, in six days' time, the last transport would board, and the Ashgroves would climb aboard it—all but one berth, quietly reassigned, quietly refused, quietly inherited.

He got up, took the oil can, and began his rounds. There was a week of running-in ahead, pivots to watch, the rate to regulate over days, and after that a century to keep. His mother's letters pressed warm against his chest. The pendulum swung. And Edmund Ashgrove—who was, depending on the page, several different people, and none of them, and someone new entirely—put his steady hands to the work, and kept the hour.
