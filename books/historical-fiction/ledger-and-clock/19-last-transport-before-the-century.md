## 19. Last Transport Before the Century

Marigold Ashgrove had packed the same carpetbag four times.

The first time, she had packed it properly—woolens rolled tight, the good kettle wrapped in a dish towel, Tom's spare spectacles in their tin because he would forget them, he always forgot them. The second time, she had unpacked it to add Edmund's scarf, the grey one with the fraying end that he pretended not to love, and then stood holding it for a full minute before putting it back in the drawer, because Edmund was not going, and pretending otherwise even for the space of a scarf felt like a small cruelty to herself. The third time, she had repacked it entirely because Ivy had burst into the room weeping about something Marigold couldn't follow, involving a ledger and a boy named Petch who was apparently now legally a cooper, and in her distress Ivy had sat down on the open bag and crushed everything flat as a pressed flower.

The fourth time—the fourth time she packed it slowly, and carefully, and left one corner of it empty on purpose, though she could not have said why if you'd asked her.

It was the morning of the last transport, and Hallowmere had frozen overnight into something out of an old woodcut. The eaves wore beards of ice. The puddles along Cooper's Lane had become small silver mirrors lying faceup in the ruts, each one showing a sliver of pale sky. Breath hung about the streets like the town itself was sighing. And everywhere, everywhere, there were soldiers—soldiers checking lists, soldiers nailing notices to doors already furred with notices, soldiers stamping their feet at the corners and blowing on their hands and looking, most of them, cold and young and not nearly so frightening as they were meant to be.

"Right," said Tom, coming into the kitchen with his coat already buttoned wrong, which was how Marigold knew his heart wasn't in anything this morning, including buttons. "Right. Bags by the door. Ivy? Ivy, love, where's your—"

"Here." Ivy appeared in the doorway with her own bag over one shoulder and her chin set at the angle Marigold knew from thirty years of trouble. She looked at her mother. "You're going to try to talk me out of being angry."

"I hadn't planned to waste the breath," Marigold said. "Anger's the correct response. I'm saving mine for the government."

Ivy made a sound halfway between a laugh and a sob and crossed the kitchen and held onto her mother hard enough to creak ribs, and Marigold held her back and let herself creak.

Because here was the thing Marigold had learned somewhere between the third packing of the carpetbag and dawn: she was not going to win this argument, and she had known it since two nights ago, when Edmund had come down to breakfast with shadows under his eyes like bruises and said, pleasantly, over the porridge, "The clock's keeper position is official now. Corliss saw to the papers. Someone has to stay and mind the works while the garrison's in residence—it's in the requisition terms, they can't let it run down, Vane signed off on it himself."

And Tom had said, "That's right," in a voice Marigold had never heard him use before, steady and settled, like a man setting the last weight into a case.

And Ivy had said, "That's right," too quickly, wiping her eyes with her sleeve, and gone out to organize something, because organizing things was how Ivy cried without anyone having to acknowledge it.

And Marigold had sat there with her spoon in her bowl and understood, with the terrible clarity that mothers are issued along with the child, that the three of them had arranged all of this without her—that Tom's berth had been quietly given back to him, or rather that Edmund's berth had been quietly erased, that Corliss's forged authorization existed, that the whole machinery of her family's leaving had been oiled and set running behind her back by the two people she loved most in the world, who had done it precisely because they knew she would fight it, and had done it anyway because it was right.

She had wanted to rage. She had opened her mouth to do it.

And then Edmund had passed her the butter without looking up, the way he'd done ten thousand mornings, and she had thought: *he is twenty-three years old and I have loved him since he was seven and small and silent and afraid of loud noises, and loving someone means letting them carry what they've decided to carry.*

So instead she had said, "Write every week," and Edmund had said, "There's no post from a garrison valley in winter," and she had said, "Then write anyway, and give it to me in spring," and he had smiled—that rare, whole-faced smile of his—and said, "Yes, Mum," and that had been the end of the fighting and the beginning of the leaving, which took considerably longer and hurt considerably more.

Now the bags were by the door, and the sky was the color of old pewter, and it was time.

* * *

The platform had been thrown up in six weeks where the sheep pasture used to be, and it still smelled of fresh-cut timber under the frost. The transport train stood breathing steam against the white hills, its carriages crusted with ice, soldiers moving along it with clipboards and chalk, marking names on the sides of doors like a giant tallying a very sad flock.

Marigold walked between her husband and her daughter with her hand through Tom's arm and her spine held straight by pure stubbornness. Behind them came the neighbors—the Harrows, the Pells, old Widow Quill with her canary cage covered in flannel—all the families who'd spent the autumn becoming one large anxious organism, all of them shuffling toward the same doors.

And there, at the edge of the platform, apart from everyone, stood Edmund.

He had dressed properly for it—his good coat, the one Marigold had made him take to the tailor twice because the man kept leaving the shoulders wrong. His hands hung loose at his sides. In his breast pocket, she noticed, there was a square shape, and she thought: *the letters,* and then thought: *no. He gave those to me to carry. It's the watch. He's wearing the watch I gave him when he finished his apprenticeship.* The little silver one, with the caseback engraved with a wren, because he'd been small and quick and quiet when he came to them, and she had wanted him to have a bird of his own.

He came to meet them halfway across the platform, and the crowd parted around the Ashgroves without quite meaning to, the way water parts around a stone it has grown fond of.

"Mum," Edmund said.

"Don't you 'Mum' me in front of the army," Marigold said, and her voice cracked on the second word like ice on a pond, and she didn't care. She took his face in both her mittened hands and looked at him—really looked, the way you look at a thing you are trying to keep—and he let her, standing patient as he had always been patient, since the first day a silent seven-year-old had stood in her kitchen watching her knead bread like it was the most important thing happening anywhere in England.

"You will wind it every day," she said. "Not every other day. Not when you remember. Every day, because a clock that runs down once learns bad habits, like men."

"Every day," Edmund agreed.

"And you'll eat. You'll eat actual meals, not stand over the works nibbling bread like some—some mechanical heron."

"I'll eat actual meals."

"And if Vane—" Her voice caught. She steadied it. "If that man gives you trouble, you send word through Corliss. That sergeant owes us. He knows he owes us."

"He knows," Edmund said softly. "Mum. I have to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you argue."

"I'm listening," said Marigold, who was not listening, who was memorizing—the exact grey of his eyes, the small scar on his chin from the chisel when he was eleven, the way his breath made a little cloud between them.

"The clock was failing because of a worn pallet stone in the escapement. I found it in September. I've had the replacement cut since October." He paused. "It strikes true now. It will strike true at midnight on New Year's Eve, and at noon every day after, for as long as anyone keeps it wound. And when you hear it—wherever you are, however far the rails go—you'll know it's me. Not the clock. Me. Keeping your hours."

Marigold Ashgrove had raised three children and buried one parent and survived forty years of Tom's puns, and she did not cry easily, and she did not cry prettily. What she did was press her forehead against her son's chest, right over the hidden watch, and stand there while the steam sighed and the sergeants called and the century prepared to turn without asking anyone's permission.

Tom put his hand on top of her head, and then on top of Edmund's, making a small bridge of himself, which was the most architectural thing he'd ever managed.

"I never told you this," Tom said, gruffly, above her. "When we took you in, your mother—her letter, the one that came with you—said *give him work and he'll be well.* That's all. Give him work and he'll be well." He cleared his throat. "Best advice I ever followed. You were the best work I ever did, lad."

"That's terrible," said Edmund, muffled, into Marigold's hair. "That's the worst compliment ever given."

"I've had forty years to practice," said Tom, and his voice broke on *practice*, and none of them mentioned it.

* * *

They boarded in the order the lists demanded: trades first, then families, then elders with priority berths. A corporal with a clipboard checked them off—Ashgrove, Thomas, wheelwright; Ashgrove, Marigold, née Bellamy, seamstress; Ashgrove, Ivy, laundress and organizer-of-everything, listed officially as *laundress*, which made Ivy snort even through her tears.

At the carriage door Marigold turned around one last time.

Edmund stood on the platform beside Sergeant Corliss, who had materialized there in his careful, weary way, holding a document folder like a shield. The sergeant said something low; Edmund nodded; and Corliss squared the folder and spoke up in his parade voice, pitched to carry:

"Keeper of the master clock, Hallowmere Observatory—one berth retained per requisition schedule, section nine! Ashgrove, E., keeper of works, resident by authorization!" He glanced sideways at Edmund, and just for a moment his tired face did something almost like a smile. "In order, Keeper. Mind the escapement."

"In order, Sergeant," Edmund said, and saluted—a real one, crisp, learned from weeks of watching soldiers—and Corliss returned it, and turned away quickly, in the manner of men who have decided not to feel things until after the train leaves.

The whistle screamed. Steam swallowed the platform edge. Marigold climbed the iron step and stood in the doorway of the carriage, gripping the rail, looking back down the length of the frozen valley at her son getting smaller—not smaller, she corrected herself fiercely, *standing still while we move*, which was different, which was a choice, which was his—and she raised her hand, and Ivy raised hers beside her, and Tom raised his, and Edmund raised his, and held it up long past the point where any of them could properly see faces anymore.

She sat down at last across from Tom and Ivy, and the empty corner of the carpetbag pressed against her ankle, and she understood at last why she had left it. It was not for anything she had forgotten to pack.

It was for what she intended to bring back.

"Spring," she said, to no one, to everyone, to the rattling window and the receding hills. "We come back in spring. All of us. And I'm bringing the kettle."

From somewhere down the corridor, Widow Quill's canary began, absurdly, to sing.

* * *

And in Hallowmere, in the tower above the sleeping garrison town, Edmund Ashgrove climbed the stairs he would climb every day for the rest of the winter, and stood before the great case with its brass heart waiting, and took out his watch—the wren, the bird she'd given him—and set it by the master's pendulum, winding it first, because a clock that runs down once learns bad habits, like men.

On the desk below, the new ledger lay open to a clean page. At the top, in his engraver's hand—steady, deliberate, made for exactly this—he wrote:

*Hallowmere Register of Hours and Names. Kept by E. Ashgrove, Keeper of Works.*

He looked at the blank lines beneath it, all those rows waiting, all those cellar names he'd promised to speak aloud at midnight, and beyond them, further down the page than any eye could see yet, the spring entries. The amnesty papers. The returning footsteps on the tower stair.

And the first of those, he suspected, would arrive sooner than anyone expected—for even as he capped his ink, he heard, faint and unmistakable from the direction of the requisition office, Captain Vane's voice rising in anger, and Sergeant Corliss's answering it, calm as a pendulum, saying words that sounded very much like *the audit schedule, sir, was altered, sir, and I can explain exactly how.*
