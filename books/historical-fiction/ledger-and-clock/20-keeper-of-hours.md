## 20. Keeper of Hours

The tower at dawn was a country unto itself.

Edmund had learned this in the weeks since the last transport pulled out of Hallowmere, its whistle fading down the frozen valley like something being swallowed. He learned it the way he'd once learned gear trains: by touch, in the dark, one small discovery per day. The tower had moods. It had weather of its own — draughts that moved through the stairwell like slow animals, a smell of oil and cold stone and pigeon that he had come to think of as honest. It had hours when the light came through the east lancets and laid long gold bars across the floor, and you could stand inside them and feel, briefly, like a man being vouched for.

He climbed now with the winding crank over his shoulder, breath steaming, boots ringing on the iron treads. Ninety-one steps to the clockroom. He counted them without meaning to; counting was a habit his hands refused to give up.

The great clock stood waiting, as it always stood, patient as an elephant. Edmund set the crank into the barrel and began to wind.

It was not, strictly speaking, necessary to wind it every morning. The weight train would run four days unaided. But Corliss's forged authorization had made him Keeper of the Master Clock, Hallowmere Observatory, and keeper was a word Edmund had decided to take seriously — the way Ivy took *family* seriously, as a verb, as something you did rather than something you were handed. So he wound each morning, all three barrels, feeling the weights rise hand over hand, and each morning he listened while the escapement took up its work again: tick, and tick, and the small deep sigh of the pendulum swinging through the dark above him like a patient heart.

*You're early today,* the clock did not say, but he heard it anyway.

"I know," he said aloud. He talked to it more than was probably sane. "Couldn't sleep. There's a baby in cellar two."

The pendulum swung on, noncommittal.

The baby — a girl, born three weeks past, in the cellar beneath the observatory where the colony families still waited for spring papers — had been named, after some debate among the eleven adults crammed into that particular stone room, *Marigold*. Edmund had been summoned down to witness it by lamplight, mostly because he could be trusted to know what time it was, and a birth wanted recording at the right hour. He had stood there with his pocket watch open and said, "Four minutes past two," and Mrs. Hollis from the colony had laughed through her exhaustion and said, "Well then, she'll be punctual," and everyone had laughed, and Edmund had felt something crack gently in his chest that was not entirely grief.

He finished the third barrel, hung the crank on its peg, and stood a moment with his palm flat against the case. The wood was warm near the movement, faintly alive with vibration. Somewhere inside it, in the hidden panel behind the chime mechanism, there was space where letters used to be. He had taken them out months ago and read them until they were soft as cloth. The panel stayed empty now. He couldn't bring himself to put anything else there. Some places in a machine were meant to keep things, and some were meant to have kept things, and both deserved respect.

"Right," he told the clock. "Down to the cellars. Try not to miss me."

The clock, having struck noon perfectly under Vane's eye and survived everything since, declined to answer.

* * *

Cellar two smelled of paraffin and boiled cabbage and, lately, of new baby — that astonishing warm-milk scent that seemed designed by Providence to make stone rooms bearable. Eleven people lived down there in what had been the observatory's coal store, and they had made it, against every law of architecture and common sense, into a home. Blankets partitioned sleeping corners. A stove sat under a tin flue Ivy had rigged herself, badly, then well, after two attempts and one small fire. Children's drawings papered the walls: the sun, a horse, a tall thin tower with a face drawn in its clock dial.

That last one was Edmund's portrait, apparently. He tried not to look at it too closely.

"Keeper!" called Mr. Hollis, looking up from a board game he was losing to a seven-year-old. "What's the word?"

"Word is the papers are moving," Edmund said. "Corliss came by yesterday. They've cleared the first batch for spring amnesty — ours included, if the signature comes through from the district office."

A murmur went round the room, the careful kind, hope kept on a short leash because hope had been leashed before. Mrs. Ferro, who had spent forty years weaving before the colony took her loom, reached over and squeezed Edmund's wrist without looking up from her mending. That was the whole conversation. It was enough.

Edmund made his rounds the way a doctor might, if doctors were trained on clocks instead of bodies. He checked the water stores, topped the lamp oil, tightened the loose hinge on the door to the old cistern passage — the escape route nobody hoped to use but everybody slept better knowing existed. Little Tam Ferro trailed him the whole way asking questions, which was the arrangement between them: Tam asked, Edmund answered or didn't, and neither of them acknowledged that the questions were really about something else. Whether the world outside was safe. Whether anyone would come for them. Whether Edmund himself would stay.

"You live in the tower all alone," Tam said, apropos of the hinges. "Isn't that lonely?"

"It's not alone," Edmund said. "There are about four thousand parts up there, and every one of them needs me."

"That's not the same as people."

"No," Edmund admitted, tightening the last screw. "But it's not nothing, either. A thing that depends on you is a kind of company. You should try it sometime — I hear your mother's got a mending basket that depends on someone desperately."

Tam grinned and ran off to report this witticism, and Edmund stood a moment in the lamplight of the coal store doorway, watching the colony families live their interrupted lives with such ferocious ordinary grace — cards played, bread stretched, names taught patiently to children who'd never seen the villages those names came from — and he thought, not for the first time, that Ivy had understood something fundamental back in autumn when she'd started hauling strangers into these cellars. Family wasn't the people who shared your blood or even your roof. Family was the ledger you kept open on purpose. Every name entered was a door left unlocked.

He climbed back up the ninety-one steps carrying a heel of black bread Mrs. Ferro had pressed on him, refusing no for an answer, and ate it standing in the clockroom with the afternoon light coming slantwise through the west lancets, gold going to copper.

Then there was nothing between him and the evening except the thing he had been circling for weeks, the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

The ledger.

* * *

He brought it down from the requisition office himself, with Corliss's blessing and Corliss's averted eyes — the sergeant had a genius for seeing exactly what needed doing and finding urgent business elsewhere while it was done. The ledger was the town's second copy, the working book, fat and scarred, its spine cracked from years of clerks leaning on it. Edmund carried it up the tower steps like something rescued from a fire.

He cleared a space on the workbench. He trimmed the lamp. And then he sat for a long time with his hands flat on the cover, not opening it, the way you stand at the edge of cold water.

Because here was the arithmetic of it, the terrible simple sum. His mother had died in the debtors' colony with her name struck from every record the colony kept — Vane himself had confirmed it, in the end, trading truth for truth across the clockroom table: the colony's rolls had been falsified first, debts invented, entries erased, a whole machinery of forgetting running smooth as any escapement. She had smuggled him out in a laundry cart at six years old with a false name stitched into his coat, and the name had been *Ashgrove*, given by a family who never asked questions, and the Ashgroves had loved him so thoroughly that the lie had become truer than most truths.

And nowhere — not in the colony's books, not in Hallowmere's ledger, not anywhere in the world — was her own name written anymore. Not as a living woman. Not as a mother. Not as anything but an absence shaped like a person.

"Well," Edmund said to the empty clockroom. His voice came out rougher than intended. "We can't have that."

He opened the ledger to the current page. The ruled lines waited, patient and indifferent, ink-browned at the margins. At the top of the page, in the clerk's faded copperplate: *Hallowmere — Registry of Residents, Trades, and Births.*

He took out his pens. He was, by now, a very good forger — the thought still stung, though the sting had changed temperature, cooled into something like pride. He had practiced all those nights with Ivy asleep across the table, copying the registrar's hand until his own wrist dreamed in copperplate. But this entry he would write plainly, in his own hand, because forgery was for protecting the living. For the dead, you owed honesty.

He dipped the pen. He hesitated one last time, hearing — or inventing — his mother's voice, the one from the letters, dry and quick and fond of him beyond all sense: *Mind how you go, little cog.*

"Mind how you go yourself," he answered, and wrote:

*Maren Ashgrove née Yarrow. Born Hallowmere parish, 1841. Trade: seamstress. Mother of Edmund. Entered this day by her son, who was given away to save him, and who has taken rather a long time to say thank you.*

His hand shook only on the last line. He let it. A ledger that recorded only steady hands would be lying about people.

He sat back. The ink dried slowly in the cold room, and he watched it dry the way he watched glue set on a repair — with the craftsman's superstition that witnessing the setting helped it hold. Above him the movement ticked, the escapement letting time fall one grain at a time onto the great wheel below, and the light failed gradually through the lancets until the lamp was the only sun in the room and the brass gears caught it and threw it around the walls in slow golden coins.

Grief, he found, did not leave when you wrote a name down. But it changed address. It moved out of the chest, where it had sat like a stopped weight for twenty years, and into the ledger, where it became a line among lines, part of the town's account, kept and carried. That was all a record ever was, he supposed — grief and joy and ordinary Tuesdays, all filed under dates so that none of it would be lost. He was the keeper of that now. Not just the clock. The keeping.

He blew out the lamp at half past ten, as the regulation required, and climbed the last flight to the dial room, where the great faces looked out over the garrison town. Below, the valley lay blue-white under a rising moon, chimneys smoking, sentries stamping, the barracks where Vane had wintered his ambition and lost it piece by piece to paperwork and a repaired clock. Somewhere in that sprawl of lights, Ivy was walking home from the cellars with her collar up, tired and triumphant. Somewhere, Corliss was writing reports that said less than they appeared to. Somewhere south, on a farm with good light, Marigold and Tom were reading his latest letter aloud to each other, arguing happily about whether he ate enough.

And here, ninety-one steps above all of it, a man wound the weights, oiled the pivots, entered the names, and kept the hours.

The clock reached midnight as he watched, and struck — twelve notes rolling out over the frozen valley, unhurried, exact, each one landing in the dark like a stone dropped into deep water. Edmund stood in the dial room with his forehead nearly touching the cold glass and let the sound come up through his boots and bones.

On the twelfth stroke, far below, a lantern bobbed out of the observatory yard and started up the ridge road toward the tower. Small, quick, familiar — a walk he'd know anywhere, a walk that had once organized an entire town's worth of hiding places between supper and dawn.

Ivy. At midnight. In February. Carrying whatever it was she was carrying — he could already tell from the swing of the lantern that it was heavy — straight toward him.

Edmund looked at the ledger sitting closed on the bench below, one new name drying in its pages, and then at the lantern climbing the hill, and felt the peculiar sensation of a man whose next chapter has arrived ahead of schedule.

"Well," he said to the clock. "Here we go again."
