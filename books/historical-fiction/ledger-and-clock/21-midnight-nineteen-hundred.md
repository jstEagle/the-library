## 21. Midnight, Nineneteen Hundred

### Ivy

The cellars of the observatory smelled, on the last night of the century, of boiled mutton, beeswax, and forty-one people's worth of hope.

Ivy Ashgrove had organized a great many things in her twenty-three years—a sewing circle, a boycott of the butcher who shorted widows, and once, memorably, a funeral for a hedgehog—but she had never organized a New Year's feast for more souls than the parish register could comfortably hold. The cellar stretched the whole length of the tower's foundation, and by half past eleven every inch of it was occupied: long trestle tables, candles stuck in jam jars, a fiddler named Oskar who had been smuggled down in a flour barrel three weeks ago and had not stopped apologizing since.

"Oskar," Ivy said, passing with a tray of buns, "if you apologize one more time I shall put you back in the barrel."

"I only meant to say the buns—"

"Are excellent. Eat them."

The hidden families sat shoulder to shoulder with the townsfolk who had sheltered them, and if you did not know—and by now, after the ledger, after the reckoning, after Vane's grudging signature, the whole garrison knew—you would have taken them for one big, argumentative family. Which was, Ivy reflected, exactly what they were. Kinship, she had learned this winter, was not something you were born into like a nose or a temper. It was something you did, nightly, with your sleeves rolled up.

Her mother was presiding over the mutton with the gravity of a field marshal. Her father sat with Oskar's little daughter on his knee, letting the girl wind and unwind an old pocket watch that no longer ran, explaining that the spring inside was tired but could be made new. And Edmund—

Edmund was not there yet. Edmund was upstairs, as he had been every night for a month, with his sleeves rolled and his hands black with brass polish, persuading the great clock to live again. Ivy had gone up at dusk to bring him a bun and had found him lying under the escapement like a man under a horse, talking to it in a low, coaxing voice.

"You know it can't hear you," she'd said.

"It can," he'd said, without surfacing. "It's just shy."

That was Edmund all over. A man who had spent a whole life being shy himself, coaxing shy things.

Now the cellar hushed as the fiddle tuned. Ivy climbed onto a chair, and the room—forty-one souls, plus babies, plus one dog that had come down with the coal delivery and refused to leave—turned toward her.

"I'll keep it short," she said, "because we've all got somewhere to be at midnight, and it's upstairs, and it's loud."

Laughter, a little ragged, a little wet.

"This time last year," she said, "I didn't know half your names. Some of you I didn't know at all. Some of you were names in a ledger that somebody had tried very hard to make disappear." She looked down at her hands. "I want to say that hiding you wasn't charity. My brother—because he is my brother, whatever the blood says, and the blood can mind its own business—my brother taught me that a name is a thing you keep for other people. Like a coat in winter. You don't ask why they lost theirs. You just hand it over."

Silence, the good kind, the kind that has weight.

"So," Ivy said, "before the clock strikes, we're going to do what they said couldn't be done. We're going to say every name. All of them. Out loud. In a garrison town, in a cellar, on the last night of a century that tried to erase you." She lifted her candle. "And when the clock strikes twelve, every soul in this room is going to be standing in the new century under their own name. Nobody borrowed. Nobody forged. Nobody hidden."

Oskar's daughter, from Tom's knee, said clearly: "What about the dog?"

"The dog too," Ivy said solemnly. "The dog has suffered greatly."

The reading of the names took twenty minutes. Ivy had written them all out in her round, stubborn hand—every colonist, every child, every old man from the valley who'd been struck from some other roll in some other year and sheltered here besides. Mother read the first column, Father the second, Ivy the third, and when her voice tired, Mrs. Pell the midwife took over, and then Oskar himself, his accent wrapping around the names like a coat two sizes too big and warm anyway.

Each name was answered by its owner: *Here. Here. Here.*

Ivy found herself crying by the letter M and did not trouble to hide it. Upstairs, faint through the floor, she could hear Edmund's muffled footsteps, the small metallic breathing of the great clock being readied—and once, distinctly, the sound of a man laughing at nothing, alone, in a tower, on the last night of the old world.

### Corliss

Sergeant Corliss had spent thirty years learning that the army ran on paper the way a mill ran on water, and that paper, unlike water, could be dammed, diverted, and made to drown people. He had signed his share of drowning orders. He had one name—his daughter's, though no one in Hallowmere knew it, and he meant to keep it that way a while longer—scratched into the lining of his cap like a private prayer.

Tonight he stood in the clockroom with his hands clasped behind him, ostensibly on duty, actually off the leash of duty entirely. Captain Vane had wanted the clock guarded against "sentimental sabotage," which was Vane's way of saying he still did not quite believe a thing could be repaired rather than seized. Corliss had volunteered for the watch. Vane had assumed it was ambition. It was, in its way.

Above him the great dial's works ticked with the sound of a heart deciding to go on. Below him, muffled by three floors of stone, the cellar feast murmured like a beehive in winter. And at the center of it all, Edmund Ashgrove—no, not Ashgrove, that was the beauty of it, Edmund *of no surname at all until he chose one*—stood at the escapement with a file, making the last adjustment of the old century.

"You'll want to stand back at the strike," Edmund said. "The hammer's got opinions."

"So do I," said Corliss. "Nobody listens to mine either."

Edmund smiled. He had a way of smiling that was mostly in the hands; his face stayed modest but his fingers went loose and glad. Corliss had watched those hands all month—steady under Vane's inspection, steady under arrest, steady under the whole rotten weight of the requisition—and had come to a conclusion he would never put in a report: this boy's hands were the most honest thing in the valley, and the army had very nearly taught them to tremble.

"Five minutes to," Edmund said. "You could go down and join them, Sergeant. I can mind the clock."

"I've spent thirty years minding other people's clocks," Corliss said. "Tonight I'd like to watch one work." He paused. "Besides, I've a mind to hear it strike true. I've heard it strike wrong. A man should balance his accounts."

The candle flames stood straight. Somewhere below, the fiddle started a tune that was either a waltz or an argument. Corliss took off his cap—out of habit, out of something older than habit—and stood in the great ticking room like a man in a church he'd only ever passed on the road.

"Edmund," he said, as the last minute began. "In the spring, when the amnesty papers come through. There'll be a line for next of kin."

Edmund's hands went still on the winding crank.

"I'd be obliged," Corliss said, gruff, looking at the dial rather than the boy, "if you'd put a name there. Any name. I'm told Ashgrove's going spare."

The minute hand climbed. Edmund said, quietly, "You'll have to write it yourself, Sergeant. My hands will be busy."

Corliss laughed—actually laughed, a rusty sound, like a gate opening after years—and the clock gathered itself, and the whole tower leaned forward into the new century.

### Edmund

He had spent the final hour doing what he always did before a strike: listening.

The layman heard a tick. Edmund heard a conversation—escape wheel and pallet, pendulum and gravity, the anchor and the arc, all of them talking at once, all of them agreeing. He had rebuilt that agreement from wreckage. He had re-cut two pallet faces, re-hung the pendulum on a fresh suspension spring he'd rolled himself from a strip of the old, sabotaged steel—*their* steel, the requisition clerk's sabotage, melted down and reborn, which he considered the finest joke the winter had produced. He had oiled every pivot with the patience of a man writing an apology in the world's smallest handwriting.

And at five minutes to midnight, with Corliss standing bareheaded behind him and the cellar humming below like a struck bell, Edmund had done the last thing. He had taken his mother's letters from the breast pocket where they had lived since autumn, and he had read the final one through once more, silently, in the clocklight.

*Tell him the hours are not the debt,* she had written, in a hand that shook only at the end. *Tell him the hours were always his.*

He folded the letters away. He put his hands on the winding crank.

The last sixty seconds of the nineteenth century were, in the clockroom, remarkably ordinary. That was the thing about time, Edmund thought—everyone assumed the great moments arrived with trumpets, but really they arrived the way a train arrives, exactly as scheduled, whether or not you are ready, whether or not the platform is full. The minute hand crossed the twelve. The gathering mechanism released. And the great hammer rose and fell on the bell like a heart deciding, once and for all, to beat.

*One.*

Below, he heard the roar come up through the stone—forty-one voices and a dog.

*Two.*

Corliss's hand landed on his shoulder, heavy as a benediction.

*Three. Four.*

Edmund watched the escapement release, catch, release, catch—his own work, his own hours, running true.

*Five. Six.*

He thought of his mother, who had never heard this bell. He thought of the colony ledger with its neat, murderous columns. He thought of a boy smuggled out in a laundry cart who had grown up believing that being useful was the only safe way to be loved.

*Seven. Eight.*

He thought of Marigold's mutton. Of Tom's pocket watch, wound and unwound by a child all evening. Of Ivy on her chair, crying without shame, handing out names like coats.

*Nine. Ten.*

He thought: *I am the keeper of the hours now. Mine, hers, all of theirs. Nobody's hours are a debt.*

*Eleven.*

The room held its breath. Corliss held his. Somewhere below, Ivy was surely already planning something for spring, because Ivy planned things the way other people breathed.

*Twelve.*

The twelfth stroke rolled out over the garrison town—over the barracks and the requisition office, over Captain Vane's quarters where the man himself stood, no doubt, auditing the new year for irregularities—over the frozen ridge road and the dark valley and the cellar where every hidden name had been spoken aloud and answered *here, here, here*—and the bell did not crack, and the clock did not falter, and the century turned on a pivot Edmund had polished himself.

The last stroke faded. The silence after it was enormous, and warm, and belonged to everyone.

"Well," said Corliss, putting his cap back on, his voice not entirely steady. "That's the accounts balanced."

"Not quite," Edmund said. He took down the great ledger—the real one now, the lawful one, with Corliss's forged-but-official authorization tucked in its cover like a pressed flower—and opened it to the fresh page headed, in his careful engraver's hand, *Nineteen Hundred.*

Below, the fiddle struck up something fast, and the cellar roared its approval, and the tower shook very slightly with dancing.

Edmund dipped his pen. The first entry of the new century was waiting to be written, and he already knew whose name it would be—though the letters from the south, from the Ashgroves on the far platform, from Ivy's inexhaustible fountain pen, would not arrive until the thaw.

He wrote the date. He waited, smiling, for spring.
