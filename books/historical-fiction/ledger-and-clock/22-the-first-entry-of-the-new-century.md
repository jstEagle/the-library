## 22. The First Entry of the New Century

The thaw came to Hallowmere all at once, the way spring does in high valleys—no polite gradual melting but a great groaning surrender, three days of water running down every gutter and eave until the whole town sounded like a clock being wound. Edmund heard it from the tower and thought: *there she goes. On time after all.*

He had learned to measure the seasons by the clock now, or perhaps the clock by the seasons. In January he had noticed that the pendulum ran two seconds slow on bitter mornings, when the wire contracted; in February he had fitted a compensating rod from brass and steel that corrected it, and had stood afterward with his hand flat against the case, absurdly proud, as though he'd taught an old dog a new trick instead of the other way round. By March the garrison's bugler no longer bothered checking his own watch before sounding reveille. He simply waited for the tower. The whole valley waited for the tower.

And on the fourth day of April, when the snow on the ridge road had gone rotten and gray and the first green was showing along the beck, a boy came up the observatory hill carrying a canvas mail sack nearly as big as himself.

"Post for the Keeper," the boy announced, breathless, holding out the sack like an offering at an altar. He was maybe ten, red-cheeked, one of the cellar children who had wintered below the clockroom floor and come out in February blinking like moles. His name was Pip Wrenn. Edmund knew this because he had entered it into the ledger himself, in October, in a steady hand, under the trade *scholar, aged nine*—the first lie of kindness he had ever committed to paper, and still, he thought, the truest line he'd ever written.

"That's me," Edmund said, and still, four months on, the words fit strangely, like a coat cut for someone slightly taller. *Keeper.* Corliss's forged authorization hung framed above the clockroom desk, because Ivy had insisted that official documents ought to be displayed where liars could admire them.

The boy dug through the sack. "Three for you. One's got a stamp I never seen." He handed them over, lingered hopefully.

Edmund fished a farthing from his pocket. "There's another sack coming up tomorrow, isn't there?"

"Yes, sir."

"Then there'll be more stamps you've never seen." He winked, and the boy grinned and clattered back down the hill, and Edmund stood a moment in the doorway of the tower with the letters warm from some other place, feeling the particular happiness of a man about to open mail from his family.

Because that is what they were. There had been a long, strange season—he was honest enough with himself now to admit it—when he would have flinched at the word. Family. It had seemed like a thing you were assigned, like a berth, like a name in a ledger, and he had spent twenty years believing he'd been assigned his by accident. But the Ashgroves had taught him otherwise, and the cellar families had confirmed it, and Ivy had hammered it home with the subtlety of a trip-hammer: family was not what you were given. It was what you did.

He took the letters up into the clockroom, where the light was best and the company was excellent.

* * *

The first letter was from Marigold, and it smelled faintly of her kitchen, which meant she had written it on the table where she kneaded bread, which meant she had written it quickly, between tasks, the way she did everything—with her sleeves rolled and half her attention elsewhere and yet somehow nothing ever dropped.

*Dear Edmund,*

*Your father says I am not to fuss in this letter, so I will do my fussing here in the margin where he cannot see it. Are you eating? Do not say the garrison feeds you, because I have met army cooks. I have enclosed sausages. Do not ask how.*

*The house is very quiet without you, and also somehow not quiet at all, because we talk about you constantly as if you can hear us, which your father insists is superstition and which I know to be fact. Ivy has worn a groove in the floor pacing. She pretends it is about the amnesty papers but we both know it is about you. Tom sends his love and his rheumatism, in that order.*

*We are coming at Whitsun. This is not a question. Your father has already argued about it with himself and lost.*

*Eat the sausages. Write back. Tell the clock I said behave.*

*Your mother,*
*Marigold Ashgrove*

*P.S. — You will notice I have signed "your mother" without any hedging or explanation. This is deliberate. Twenty-three years is long enough. Blood is a rumor. Sausages are real.*

Edmund laughed out loud alone in the clockroom, which was a thing he had done perhaps five times in his life before October and now did weekly. He set the letter down on the desk, then picked it up again and read the postscript twice more. *Blood is a rumor.* She had written it as a joke. He suspected she knew it was not one.

The second letter was from Tom, one page, in handwriting like fence posts:

*Boy —*

*Sausage is from your mother. The rest is from me. The apple trees survived the winter. The east one wants pruning and I want to be alive to see you do it wrong. Vane was reassigned in January, south, somewhere with a desk. Small mercies. Corliss writes that you have not broken the clock yet. I have bet him a shilling you break it by June. Do not make me lose money, but do not make it easy either.*

*— T.A.*

*P.S. — Your mother says I am to tell you she loves you. I told her you know it. She said tell him anyway. So: she loves you. And the other thing. You know the other thing.*

Edmund pressed his thumb flat against the page for a moment. *The other thing.* Old men should not be allowed to write postscripts; they went straight through a fellow like a gimlet. He cleared his throat, checked that no gear train was watching, and folded the letter away.

The third envelope was thick, soft-cornered, crammed full—the unmistakable architecture of an Ivy letter—and the stamp, indeed, was one he had never seen: bright green, printed with a mountain goat. Inside were eleven pages, both sides, in her fountain pen's confident slant, and a newspaper clipping, and a pressed flower he chose not to inquire about, and at the bottom, separate, a small official document with a seal on it that made his hands go still.

The amnesty papers.

*Dearest Ed,*

*It is DONE. All of it. Every name on our list, every cellar soul, every forged kinship and invented trade — AMNESTIED, certified, stamped, sealed, and (I made sure) personally apologized for by a clerk in the Ministry who looked like he'd rather apologize to a wasp. I have the documents HERE. Corliss countersigned everything, the darling bureaucratic rebel, and I have told him he must come to Hallowmere at Whitsun too, and he has pretended to be too busy, which in Corliss means yes.*

*I showed the clerk the ledger copy — the one we made, ours, the true one — and I watched his face while he read it. Ed, he CRIED. Well. His eyes went glassy, which for a Ministry man is sobbing. He said, "These entries are irregular." I said, "Yes." He said, "They're also correct." And I thought, there it is, the whole secret of the universe, and a man in a gray suit just said it out loud in an office in the capital: the correct thing is often irregular, and the regular thing is often a crime wearing a tie.*

*(I have put that on a card. I am having cards printed. You may expect several.)*

*The clipping is from the Gazette — they ran a piece on the debtors' colonies, the falsified records, all of it, YOUR MOTHER'S LETTERS are cited, Ed, did you know that? Corliss sent copies to the inquiry and they actually READ them. Her name is in the paper. Ellen Ashgrove — no. Forgive me. Ellen Voss. They print her name as evidence. She is evidence now, real evidence, undeniable, the kind they teach clerks with. I cried on the train reading it and a woman gave me a boiled sweet and asked if it was bad news and I said NO, it is the best news I have ever received, and she moved seats.*

*Now the important part. I am coming back. Not visiting. COMING BACK. I have taken a position — guess — no, you cannot guess, nobody ever guesses — KEEPER OF RECORDS for the resettlement commission, Hallowmere district. Which means, dear brother-in-all-ways-that-matter, that I will be sitting across a desk from you with the ledger BETWEEN US, and I warn you now that I intend to be insufferable about it.*

*Also Mother says Whitsun. Also Father says Whitsun. Also I say the day after tomorrow.*

*All my love, more than the pages allow,*
*Ivy*

*P.S. — I found a fountain pen that holds twice the ink. The century does not know what it has let itself in for.*

Edmund sat back. Through the clockroom window, the valley lay washed and dripping, the ridge road a brown ribbon through new grass, and somewhere down that road, two days out, was Ivy, with a government post and an improved pen and a groove worn in the parlor floor.

He looked up at the great dial, its hands moving with their old unhurried certainty, and he thought about how a clock tells you nothing except what matters: that things continue. That the escapement lets go and catches, lets go and catches, and the letting-go is not failure. It is how the keeping works.

Then he opened the sealed document and read his mother's name, restored to the world's record, witnessed and true, and he did not check whether any gears were watching that time either.

* * *

She arrived, naturally, ahead of schedule, having talked the mail driver into abandoning his route's dignity for an hour. Edmund heard the cart before he saw it—Ivy had never in her life entered a room quietly, and apparently this extended to valleys—and he was halfway down the tower stair when the door burst open and she filled it, travel-dusted, hatless, holding the amnesty papers aloft like a torch.

"They're legal!" she announced to the entire observatory, which contained only Edmund and, through the wall, the tick of the master clock. "Every last irregular entry! We won, Ed! Bureaucratically!"

"You're a week early."

"I'm exactly on time. Everyone else is late." She dropped her bag, hugged him hard enough to relocate a rib, held him at arm's length, and inspected him the way she inspected ledgers: thoroughly, looking for discrepancies. "You've been eating."

"Marigold sent sausages."

"Thank heaven for that woman. Come on, then—show me the ledger. I've crossed a county to see it."

They climbed together, arguing happily about margins, and in the clockroom she stopped dead at the sight of the framed authorization on the wall and put her hand over her mouth, and for once in her eloquent life said nothing at all for a full four seconds.

The ledger itself lay open on the desk, as it always did now. Since October, Edmund had kept it the way he kept the clock: daily, faithfully, without flourish. The names of the cellar families marched down its pages in trades that had become, by spring, simply true—because Pip Wrenn really did attend lessons now, because the Ferro sisters really had reopened the bakery, because half the hidden colonists had stayed on in Hallowmere after the garrison thinned, drawn back by the strange gravity of the town that had lied for them. Forgery, Ivy liked to say, was just the truth arriving ahead of schedule.

But the newest page was blank except for the date, written in October and left waiting.

"The first entry of the new century," Ivy said softly. "You never wrote it."

"I was waiting."

"For what?"

"For whoever it was going to be." He turned the book toward her. "I couldn't write my own name. That was the whole point of giving the berth away. And I wasn't going to invent anyone. So I waited to see who the century would send."

Ivy looked at the blank space beneath the date for a long moment. Then a slow grin spread across her face, the dangerous one, the one that preceded every scheme she'd ever hatched.

"Ed," she said. "Have you seen the mail cart?"

* * *

The answer was standing in the observatory yard at dusk, small and mud-splattered and furious at the entire world.

She was perhaps six years old, with a scraped chin and a carpetbag bigger than her torso and the expression of a duchess forced to travel third class. She had arrived on the mail cart without papers, without escort, with nothing but a note pinned to her coat in a shaking hand: *Her people are gone. The colony records burned. If anyone can enter a child somewhere safe, it is the keeper of the clock at Hallowmere. Forgive the imposition. — A friend of Sergeant Corliss.*

Corliss, Edmund thought. Even in resistance, the man filed things properly. He'd routed her like a document, through channels only the right people could read.

Her name, she informed them, chin up, voice wobbling only slightly, was Josephine Marchetti. She was six and three-quarters. She could whistle. She was, she added, with the gravity of a treaty, *not* going to cry, and then she did, briefly and ferociously, while Ivy knelt and gathered her in and Edmund stood by feeling something crack gently open in his chest—not painfully. The way ice cracks off a stream in April. The way a case panel gives up its letters after thirty years.

That evening, after soup and sausages and a demonstration of the whistling (impressive; the cellar families downstairs applauded), Ivy carried the lamp up to the clockroom and set it beside the ledger.

"Well," she said. "Here's your first entry."

Edmund dipped his pen. He thought of his mother writing her smuggled son's new name into a stranger's household, hoping someone someday would make it official. He thought of the blank space waiting since October, patient as a pendulum. He thought of Marigold's postscript, *blood is a rumor*, and of all the names he had forged and all the names he had spoken aloud in a candlelit cellar while the century turned overhead.

His hand did not tremble. He noticed that it didn't, and smiled.

He wrote, in his best engraver's hand, steady as a balance wheel:

*Josephine Marchetti. Born the twelfth of May, 1893. Trade: scholar, aged six. Kin: the keeper of the clock, and all his.*

He blotted it. Below, in smaller letters, he added the line the ledger had been waiting a hundred years to learn how to carry:

*Entered freely. Verified by love.*

Ivy read it upside down, as she read everything, and blew her nose loudly on her sleeve. "Irregular," she managed.

"Completely," Edmund agreed.

Downstairs, the cellar families had begun to sing. Above them the great clock let go and caught, let go and caught, keeping faith with the dark, and outside the window the last snow slid off the ridge road, and the valley, running a little fast with spring, hurried toward morning.
