## 1. The Last Crossing

The mule knew before I did.

It stopped at the crest of the pass with its ears pricked toward the valley below, breath steaming in two white columns, and would not be urged forward. I had spent eleven days with that animal — bought it in Ambérieu under a name I had since discarded like a coat outgrown — and it had never once refused me. It was a stupid, honest creature, which is precisely why I had chosen it. Stupid animals do not attract questions. Honest ones tell you when something is wrong.

I stood in the stirrups and looked down.

Val-Brume lay in its bowl of grey stone and black pine, smoke rising from a hundred chimneys in threads so thin they seemed drawn by a pen rather than born of fire. The river ran through the middle of it, wide and slate-colored, and across the river the bridge — a wooden span on stone piers, older than any of the kingdoms that had claimed it, hung with chains at both ends like a beast that might be led away if only someone held the leash. On the far bank, men were already working at the near chain. Even at this distance I could see the rhythm of them: one turning the windlass, two feeding the great links onto the drum, another walking the length of the bridge with a lantern though the sun was still above the ridge, checking each plank as though he meant to say goodbye to every one of them individually.

The last crossing. Every year it was the same; the ferryman's wife in Saint-Rémy had told me as much while she sold me cheese and asked no questions, which had marked her at once as either very wise or very foolish. When the ice began to run in the gorge, the town chained the bridge and let winter have the valley until the thaw. No post riders. No carts. No couriers.

No way out.

"Come along," I said to the mule, and clicked my tongue, and after a moment's further judgment of me — I felt certain it was judging me — it consented to descend.

* * *

You learn things as a courier that no manual teaches. You learn that a wool merchant's clerk travels with samples, so you carry samples: little squares of broadcloth and flannel wrapped in oilcloth, tagged in a clerk's careful hand, priced in a clerk's careful arithmetic. You learn that clerks complain about their masters, so you have a master — Marchand & Fils, drapers of Lyon — and a grievance ready: old Marchand is a miser, young Marchand is a fool, and between them they pay their clerks in promises. You learn that the best disguise is not silence but abundance. Give them a story with furniture in it, with dust in the corners, and people will believe it because they can see themselves living in it.

And you learn to read a road the way a priest reads a face.

The road down into Val-Brume was bad in the ordinary way — rutted, half-frozen, running beside a stream that had gone quiet and glassy with cold — but there were marks on it that were not ordinary. Wheel ruts cut deep and then refrozen, meaning heavy traffic days ago and nothing since. A milestone with the republic's painted letters scraped off and, beneath the scraping, older letters showing through like a scar under new skin. And twice, in the space of a league, places where the snow at the roadside had been disturbed and smoothed again by hands rather than feet — someone had dragged something heavy off the road and buried it, or pretended to.

I noted all of this without letting my face note anything. That is the first rule of the trade, and the one they cannot teach you, because if you must remember the rule you are already lost. Your face belongs to your cover, not to you. My face belonged to Mademoiselle Elodie Vernet, clerk to Marchand & Fils, twenty-three years old, tired, mildly resentful, entirely unremarkable. She had been riding for eleven days and wanted a fire, a meal, and a bed, in that order.

The trunk strapped behind my saddle weighed more than wool samples could account for. Its false bottom weighed more than everything else together. I did not think about it. Thinking about it was a habit I permitted myself exactly once a day, at dusk, for the length of a prayer, and then I put it away again. A courier who thinks about what she carries begins to carry it differently. People notice how you carry things long before they ask what they are.

At the second turn of the descent the wind came up the valley to meet us, smelling of iron and pine resin and, faintly, of woodsmoke and bread — the smell of a town preparing to shut itself away from the world. Somewhere ahead a bell rang three notes and stopped, as if even the church had decided against saying too much.

The mule sighed through its nose, a long sound of resignation, and walked on.

* * *

The toll house stood where the road met the river road, a squat building with a stovepipe breathing and a board nailed above the door on which the tolls were chalked: CART 4 SOUS, MULE 2, FOOT TRAVELER GRATIS. Beneath, in smaller and newer chalk: BRIDGE CHAINS GO UP AT DUSK. AFTER THAT, WAIT FOR THE THAW.

A woman sat in the doorway on an upturned barrel, shelling walnuts into her apron with a small knife, cracking each shell with a neat economy of violence that I found myself admiring. She looked up as I reined in.

"Dusk," she said, before I had spoken.

"I'd hoped to make it," said Elodie Vernet, and heard the clerkly weariness in her own voice settle correctly over the words like a well-fitted glove.

"You'll make it." The woman nodded at the sky, then at the bridge. "They always say dusk and they always mean full dark. Gaspard likes his work. He'll drag it out till the light's gone." She cracked another walnut, flicked the meat into her apron, dropped the shells in a growing pile at her feet. "Wool?"

"Samples." I swung down, grateful for the excuse to move my legs, and loosened the girth. "Lyon house. I'm to take orders through the valley before the roads shut."

"In the valley?" Her eyebrows went up a fraction, and I understood the error at once — a clerk who did not know the bridge closed had just admitted ignorance of the whole region's most basic fact. I covered it the way water covers a stone: smoothly, immediately, without a ripple.

"My master's idea, not mine. He has maps from before the war and believes maps are still true. I've been correcting his geography since Ambérieu." I smiled the small, self-deprecating smile of a subordinate discussing her superior, and the woman laughed, one short bark, and the stone was covered.

"He'll get no orders from here till April."

"Then I'll take his disappointment back to him in spring, with interest."

She studied me a moment longer than comfort allowed, and I let her. This too is technique, though it sounds like madness: when someone looks for the lie, show them a truth. Not the truth — a truth. I was tired. I did have sore knees and a blister forming under my left glove and eleven days of road dust worked so deep into my collar that no amount of washing would ever fully reclaim it. Let her see all that. Tired women carrying wool samples are not worth suspicion.

"Gaspard will want to look at your papers," she said finally. "He looks at everyone's. Don't take offense; he took offense himself once, years ago, and now he takes it out on the rest of us."

"He can read mine aloud if he likes. They're duller than the tolls."

"Mm." She went back to her walnuts. "Inn's the cooperage. Aurel Vasse keeps lodgings above his shop. Tell him Marguerite sent you and don't tell him I told you to say so, he pretends he sets the price himself."

I thanked her, remounted, and rode the last quarter league with the walnut-woman's eyes between my shoulder blades like a small warm spot of attention. I did not turn around. Turning around confirms to a watcher that being watched matters to you.

The name Marguerite I filed away without expression. Names were inventory. So were glances, ruts, milestones, scraped paint. Everything went into the same ledger I kept behind my eyes, the one with no paper in it, the one I would burn page by page if ever I were searched — except that it could not be burned, which was its genius and its horror. It lived in me. Whatever happened to me happened to it.

Behind me, in the false bottom of the trunk, the real ledger slept in its oilcloth, thirty-one leaves of ciphered columns that weighed, by my reckoning, about as much as a cathedral.

* * *

Gaspard the bridge-keeper was exactly as advertised: a broad man past fifty with a windlass-keeper's forearms and a face that had decided long ago to trust nothing that moved. He stood at the near end of the span with a lantern already lit at his feet, absurd in the afternoon light, and he made me dismount and open the trunk while four other late travelers waited behind me in a small frozen queue — a tinker, two brothers with a cart of salted pork, and an old priest who kept his hood up against the wind.

Papers first. I handed over Marchand & Fils's letter of introduction, my travel pass countersigned at Bourg, and the bill of lading for the samples. Gaspard read them the way he presumably turned his windlass: slowly, thoroughly, with visible satisfaction in the resistance.

"Wool samples," he said.

"Yes, citizen."

"For whom, in this valley? We've weavers enough for our own backs and none to spare."

"The valley, and beyond the pass east — I'm told there are houses over the mountain that buy before the thaw, for spring cloth." This was true. I had verified it myself, weeks ago, from a trade gazette in Lyon. Lies go farther on a spine of truth; that was practically the family motto of my profession, though no profession admits to having a motto.

Gaspard grunted, unconvinced and unwilling to admit being satisfied, which is its own kind of satisfaction. Then he lifted the sample packet out of the trunk, weighed it in his hand, and set it on the bridge rail to open it.

My pulse did nothing. That is the discipline. In my first year I had once stood in a customs shed in Geneva with my heart hammering so hard I was certain the officer could hear it, could see it beating in my throat like a bird trapped under skin, and he had waved me through while I nearly fainted with relief. Afterward my handler had told me: your fear was useful. A courier with no fear is a courier who stops checking. But the fear must live in the body and never reach the face. The body may scream. The face files paperwork.

So my pulse did nothing, and my face showed the mild boredom of a clerk watching her samples manhandled by a stranger's dirty hands, and inside, in the dark vault where the real ledger lived, a door stayed quietly shut.

Gaspard unfolded one square of broadcloth — good stuff, honestly priced, because I had bought real samples with real money for exactly this moment — rubbed it between finger and thumb, snorted, and wrapped it again.

"Lyon thinks a lot of its broadcloth," he said.

"All Lyon thinks a lot of Lyon," I agreed.

He almost smiled. Then he repacked the trunk himself, carefully, closing the false bottom over the oilcloth bundle without a glance — why should he glance? Wool samples sat on top; the bottom of a trunk is the bottom of a trunk — and waved me across.

"Back before dusk if you're returning," he said. "After the chains, nobody crosses. Nobody. I've pulled drowned fools out of the gorge in March who thought ice was a road. Ice is not a road."

"No, citizen," I said, and led the mule onto the planks.

* * *

Halfway across, I made my mistake.

Not a mistake anyone saw — perhaps not even a mistake, only a lapse, a single unguarded breath — but I felt it, the way you feel a stitch come loose in a seam. Midway over the river I looked back.

I looked back at the road I had come by, climbing away northward through the pines toward the pass, toward the world, toward the route: Grenoble, then Chambéry, then whatever safe house would receive my report and issue the next instructions. Eleven days behind me lay that road, and it was still open, and it would be open for perhaps five hours more, and then the chains would go up and the map of my life would fold along a line drawn through this valley, and I would be on the wrong side of it.

I had known this when I planned the crossing. I had chosen it. The ledger had to go south and west, the route ran north and east, and the arithmetic of the season allowed no elegant solution — only this one: cross into Val-Brume, wait for the contact, transfer the package, and ride out over the eastern pass before the high snows closed that too. A window of perhaps ten days. It had seemed ample in Lyon, in October, in a warm room with a map.

Standing on the swaying planks with the river sliding grey beneath the gaps, ten days looked like a coin tossed into dark water.

Because here was what I knew and did not say to myself in daylight: the contact's letter had been brief, and brief letters are expensive, and someone had paid dearly for brevity. *Wait in Val-Brume. The Notary will find you. Trust the cooperage.* No name for the Notary. No second meeting point. No counter-sign except the phrase — *the winter counts everything* — which I was to speak and await recognition. If the Notary did not come, I would be a wool clerk stranded in a sealed town with a trunk full of treason and no exit but the one Gaspard had just promised me was not an exit at all.

And behind me, somewhere on that road over the pass, I was fairly sure I had been followed since Bourg. Not closely — no rider had appeared twice — but followed the way winter follows autumn: patiently, inevitably, by a power that did not need to hurry.

I turned forward again and finished the crossing with my eyes fixed on the far bank, and I told myself that looking back was simply prudence, a courier's habit, checking her line of retreat. Both things were true. That is the trouble with this trade: the true things and the frightening things are so often the same things, wearing different coats.

* * *

Val-Brume received me with the particular indifference of towns that count everything and welcome nothing. A boy swept the bridge approach with a birch broom, sweeping snow that was not there yet, sweeping anyway, because the ritual mattered more than the result. A woman drew water at the well and watched me pass over the rim of her bucket. Two men outside the smithy paused their conversation as I went by and resumed it a half-beat after — resumed it, I noticed, at exactly the volume and cadence they had left off, which meant they had not been talking about anything at all, only performing talk for the benefit of whoever passed.

Good, I thought grimly. A town that performs is a town that watches. Towns that watch are dangerous, but they are also legible. It was the towns that genuinely didn't care that you had to worry about.

The cooperage announced itself by smell before sight — oak shavings and char and the sour-sweet breath of new barrels — a low stone building with its yard stacked to the eaves in staves and hoops, and above it, under a steep roof, a row of windows with geraniums long dead in their boxes. A sign, hand-painted and much weathered: A. VASSE — TONNELIER. LODGINGS FOR TRAVELERS, ENQUIRE WITHIN. Someone had added, in fresher paint and smaller letters: AND BE CIVIL ABOUT IT.

Inside the yard a girl of perhaps fifteen stood on a barrel head, driving hoops with a hammer that was nearly too big for her, each blow landing with a precision that made the work look less like labor than like argument — her side of an ongoing disagreement with the wood, which was losing. She did not stop when I came through the gate. She finished the hoop, drove the last inch home, straightened, and looked at me with eyes the color of the river under ice: grey, quick, and with something moving underneath.

"You're the fourth traveler today," she said. "The others took the rooms by the stairs. There's the corner room left. It's cold and it's quiet and Papa charges the same for it, so think whether you want quiet."

"I want quiet," I said.

"Everyone says that." She set down the hammer and wiped her palms on her apron, studying me with an frankness that would have been rude in an adult and was merely unnerving in a child. "Wool?"

"Samples. Clerk to a Lyon house."

"Hm," she said, in a tone identical to the toll-keeper's woman's, and I thought: this valley breeds a single syllable and distributes it to everyone, and every one of them means *I don't believe you, but proceed.*

"Aurel Vasse your father?"

"Papa!" she called over her shoulder, not taking her eyes off me, and from the dimness of the workshop a man emerged wiping his hands — big, slow-moving, with a beard going to salt and a face like a door left thoughtfully ajar. He looked at me. He looked at the mule. He looked, one beat longer than necessary, at the trunk.

"Mademoiselle Vernet," I said, offering the name like a coin. "Clerk. Your neighbor by the toll house said—"

"Marguerite sent you." It was not a question. "Then you'll have the corner room. Supper's at seven, there's one sitting and no exceptions, and the privy's round the back and freezes by December, so plan accordingly." He took the mule's bridle with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about him. "Girl, take her trunk up. Mind the stairs, they're steeper than they look."

"They're exactly as steep as they look," the girl said, and hauled the trunk off the saddle as though it weighed nothing — though I saw her adjust her grip at the weight, saw the fractional surprise, saw her file it away behind those river-ice eyes the way I filed things away behind mine.

Her name, I learned at supper, was Sabine. She served the barley soup and the rye bread and the pickled cabbage without ceremony, answered her father's remarks in monosyllables that somehow contained entire paragraphs, and watched everything: the priest from the bridge eating silently in the corner; the two salt-pork brothers arguing over the bill; me, longest of all, though never when I was looking directly back — which is, of course, how you watch someone properly, and how I was watching her.

We recognized each other. Neither of us said so. It was like two fencers saluting before a bout neither had agreed to fight.

* * *

The corner room was cold and quiet, as promised. One window, facing the bridge, so that from my bed — if I stood on the bed — I could see the far bank, the windlass, the lantern Gaspard would light at dusk. Below, through the floorboards, came the tap of the girl's hammer, working late, driving hoops in the lamplight.

I locked the door, moved the trunk to the wall, and knelt beside it with my ear to the wood for a full minute, listening to the house breathe. Cooperages creak; barrels whisper as the staves take up or give back the damp; a house full of barrels is never silent, and that is a mercy, because it drowns other sounds.

Only then did I permit the day's single prayer.

I lifted out the false bottom — three fingers of seasoned oak, fitted so finely that I myself needed the hidden brass tab to find its edge — and looked at the ledger.

Thirty-one leaves. Columns of figures in a fine, cramped, beautiful hand, each number standing innocently in its row, requisitions and disbursements and payments rendered, the very picture of bookkeeping. Above them, in the same hand, the cipher: groups of letters that resolved, under the key I carried sewn into my waistband, into names. Dates. Places. Sums paid to men whose portraits hung now in government buildings. Sums paid for things that had no legitimate column anywhere in the republic's accounting.

Somewhere south of here, a man called the Notary was supposed to receive this. Somewhere north of here, the road was closing. Somewhere behind me, patient as weather, something followed.

I touched the top leaf once, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal you do not wholly trust, and closed the false bottom, and slid the trunk back against the wall, and blew out the candle.

Through the window, dusk had come at last to the valley. Down at the bridge a lantern bloomed — Gaspard's, punctual as grief. I watched the tiny figures move at the windlass, watched the first links of the chain rise off the drum and swing out over the water, and the sound reached me a heartbeat later, faint across the frozen air: iron, settling into iron.

The chains went up. The map folded. The year was ending, and I was on the wrong side of the river from every person alive who knew my true name.

From below, through the floorboards, the hammer stopped. In the sudden silence I heard, quite distinctly, the girl Sabine pause outside my door — one breath, two — and then continue on down the stairs, unhurried, as though she had merely been passing.

I lay in the dark and did not sleep, and the winter settled over Val-Brume like a lid.
