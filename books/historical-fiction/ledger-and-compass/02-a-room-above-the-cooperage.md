## 2. A Room Above the Cooperage

The cooperage smelled of oak shavings and hot iron, and Elodie Sandoval stood in its doorway for the length of three breaths before she let herself be seen.

She had learned that trick from her mother, back when her mother was still a woman who taught codes at a kitchen table instead of a name in a list of the purged: *let them find you, never arrive.* A person who appears already inside a room has made decisions; a person who stands in the door is simply weather, and weather is easy to underestimate.

The workshop below roared with the cooper's trade — the wet hoops hissing as they were driven over staves, a boy singing tunelessly about a girl from the valley — but above it all hung the particular quiet of a house that had recently lost someone and had not yet decided how to arrange itself around the absence. Elodie had been in enough such houses to know the sound. It was the silence of a chair moved out of place and never moved back.

"Mademoiselle." The man who came wiping his hands on his apron was broad as one of his own barrels, with a beard going iron-grey at the chin and eyes the color of river water in November. "You'd be the clerk the inn sent down. Vasse. Aurel Vasse."

"Clerk to Maison Perrin et Cie, wool factors, Grenoble," she said, and produced the letter of introduction she had forged eleven days ago in a roadside inn, holding it just long enough to be read and not so long as to seem proud of it. "They told me at the bridge you take lodgers."

"Winter lodgers, when the bridge goes up." He glanced past her shoulder at the street, where the first true snow of the season was beginning to come down in fat, indifferent flakes. "You cut that close, mademoiselle. The chains go up tomorrow at dawn."

"I know." She let a small, rueful smile touch her mouth. "My master said I'd have until the feast day. His arithmetic and the river's don't agree."

Vasse laughed — a short bark, quickly folded away again, as though laughter were something the household rationed now — and jerked his head toward the stair. "Room's small and the roof leaks at the corner if the wind comes off the glacier. You'll hear the shop below from five in the morning. I ask twelve francs the month, firewood included, bread not."

"Eleven, and I'll mend my own firewood."

He looked at her again then, longer this time, and Elodie felt the familiar cold slide along her spine — the moment when the mask is weighed. She kept her face pleasant and slightly tired, a clerk's face, a face that thought about ledgers and warm beds and nothing else. She even allowed herself to shift her weight, because a spy stands too still; she had learned that too.

"Twelve," Vasse said. "I've a daughter to feed, and she eats like the winter itself."

"Done."

* * *

The room above the cooperage was exactly what he had promised: small, low-beamed, with a bed under the eaves, a table scarred by a century of other people's knives, and one window that looked out over the frozen arm of the river toward the dark mass of the mountains where the road went south and did not open again until April.

Her trunk came up on the boy's shoulder — the same boy who had been singing — and Elodie tipped him a sou and waited until his footsteps faded down the stairs before she closed the door and put her hand flat against the trunk's lid and simply breathed.

The ledger rode in the false bottom, beneath a layer of wool samples and a merchant's sample book and the boring, blameless correspondence of a wool firm that existed mostly on paper. She had built the compartment herself in Lyon, with a joiner's patience: four inches of depth, the seam hidden under a beading of walnut veneer, opening only to a blade worked into the left rear corner. Four inches of pine between her neck and the rope.

She did not lift it out. Not yet. Daylight was dying and the house was full of ears.

Instead she unpacked the way a real clerk would unpack — carelessly, sighing, hanging two dresses on the pegs by the window, setting out a hairbrush and a tin of tooth powder, arranging her writing case on the table where anyone glancing in would see exactly the tools they expected: pens, an inkhorn, blotting sand, the sample book. Performance, her mother used to say, is not the lie you tell. It is the thousand small truths you arrange around the lie until the lie has nowhere left to stand.

Then she sat down on the bed and let the smile fall off her face, because there was no one to see, and for a moment — only a moment — she let herself feel the true shape of her situation: sealed in. The bridge chained at dawn. Two hundred miles of republican France behind her, the passes ahead closed like a fist. Whatever happened in Val-Brume this winter, she would happen in it too. There would be no slipping away down the valley if things went wrong. There was no *away* anymore until the thaw.

Somewhere below, the hammering stopped. A bell rang somewhere in town — six o'clock, or seven; mountain towns rang their bells early — and the smell of onions and salt pork began to climb through the floorboards.

Elodie washed her face in the basin, rebuilt the smile, and went down to supper.

* * *

There were four of them at the table: Vasse at the head; the boy, whose name turned out to be Gaspard, a nephew some cousin had left on them like an unwanted foal; a journeyman cooper named Brisson with forearms like hams and opinions he dispensed freely; and the daughter.

Sabine Vasse could not have been more than sixteen, but she had her father's river-water eyes set in a face that was all edges and attention, black hair scraped back, sleeves rolled to the elbow, a burn scar across one wrist from the hoop-fire. She served the soup without asking anyone's preference and sat down last, and while the men talked — Brisson about the price of oak, Gaspard about a dog fight, Vasse about the ice, which thickened or failed according to some private liturgy only he understood — Sabine watched Elodie.

Not rudely. Worse than rudely. *Professionally.*

"So you're shut in with us till spring," Sabine said, in the gap when Brisson stopped for breath. "What does a wool clerk do all winter in Val-Brume?"

"Counts," Elodie said. "Bales in, bales out, weights, moisture, spoilage. My master says wool is honesty with fleas."

That got a laugh out of Vasse and even a grunt of respect from Brisson, and Elodie felt the small warm click of a lie seating itself well — and hated, as always, how good the clicking felt. That was the trap of the trade nobody warned you about. Not the danger. The *pleasure*. Every successful falsehood was a little coin of approval paid directly into your own hand, and a courier who came to enjoy the payment was a courier halfway to believing her own fictions.

Sabine didn't laugh. She tore her bread and looked at Elodie with those unblinking eyes and said, "Your hands are wrong for counting."

The table went briefly quiet. Even Gaspard stopped chewing.

"Wrong?" Elodie said pleasantly.

"Ink on the right middle finger, that's right enough. But the callus." Sabine pointed with her spoon. "There. On the thumb, against the second finger. Papa's got one from hauling rope. The smith's got one from the tongs. Counters get soft hands." She shrugged and returned to her soup. "Maybe your master makes you carry the bales too."

For one instant the room tilted, and Elodie saw with perfect clarity the whole architecture of her death: the trunk at the top of the stairs, four inches of pine, a girl with a ferryman's grip and a witness's eye, a town sealed in snow with nowhere to run. Then she laughed — easily, warmly, the laugh of a woman caught out in something harmless — and held up her palms.

"Guilty. I rowed my brother's boat on the Isère every summer of my childhood. He drowned two years ago and I still pull an oar when I dream. Old habits live in the hands longer than the heart, my grandmother said."

It was beautiful. It was seamless. It was even, in its way, almost true — she had pulled an oar once, on another river, in another life, with another teacher — and she watched the story do its work: Vasse's face gentling, Brisson nodding into his wine, Gaspard losing interest entirely. Only Sabine held her gaze a heartbeat past comfortable, and then, deliberately, looked away first.

Elodie ate her soup and told herself the girl was sixteen and no threat, and did not quite believe it.

* * *

She woke at three in the morning out of a dream of bridges closing, and lay listening to the house.

The cooperage spoke at night. Timber ticked as it cooled; wind worried the loose corner of the roof she'd been promised; and beneath it all, faint and constant, the river ran under its new ice like a voice reading aloud in an empty church. She got up, wrapped the blanket around her shoulders, and knelt beside the trunk.

Now. Three in the morning was when the truth came out of its hiding place. She had learned to keep hours like a nun keeps hers — inverted, secret, devoted.

The blade clicked into the corner seam. The walnut beading lifted. She drew the ledger out and set it on her knees: a plain merchant's ledger, bound in mottled calf, its pages filled with columns of figures in a fine, cramped hand. To any searcher it was exactly what it appeared — a factor's book, dry as August. But the figures were not figures. They were a cipher of her own service's devising, page after page, and somewhere in those columns were names, sums, dates, orders — the accounting, someone had risked their life to say, of what the republic had actually done with the people it had liberated.

And somewhere in the republic, a contact called the Notary was supposed to meet her and tell her what to do with it.

Except the Notary hadn't come. The dead-drop letter — sewn into her cloak lining at Lyon — had named the town, the tavern, the sign (a painted compass rose over the door of the Auberge des Deux Rives), and the interval: wait nine days. Tomorrow was the ninth day, counting from her arrival tonight or from the crossing, depending on which convention her handlers meant, and the ambiguity was either sloppiness or design. Her handlers were not sloppy.

She ran her fingers over the binding, feeling for the thing she had found once before and refused to look at twice: a slight swelling near the spine's foot, where the leather had been opened and restitched. Someone had hidden something inside the ledger besides its contents. Or taken something out.

Outside, the snow had stopped. Through the frost-furred window the valley lay blue-white and silent, and far down the frozen river she could just make out the black line of the convent wall, and beyond it, invisible at this hour, the mill, the bridge, the road going south into country that wanted this book burned or buried or delivered quietly into certain hands — everyone, it seemed, except the people in its pages.

A board creaked on the stair.

Elodie's body moved before her mind finished the sentence: ledger into the compartment, beading down, blade withdrawn, blanket off, back into bed, candle pinched — all of it inside eight seconds, a drill so old it lived in her wrists. She arranged her breathing into sleep and watched through her lashes at the strip of light under the door.

Footsteps passed. Light ones. They paused — she counted the pause, one, two, three — outside her door, then continued up to the attic rooms, unhurried, familiar with the stair. A house member, then. Going to bed late or rising early. In a house that had lost someone, people kept strange hours.

But as the footsteps faded, Elodie lay staring at the beams and admitted what the pause at her door had told her: she had been counted. Weighed. Logged. The girl with the sharp eyes had come up the stairs in the dark to stand outside the wool clerk's door and listen, and whatever she had heard — breathing too controlled, or not controlled enough — she had carried away with her like a coin in a pocket.

Elodie closed her eyes and began, methodically, to compose tomorrow's version of herself: a little more clumsiness, a yawn at the wrong time, ink spilled on purpose. Give the watcher something small and true to catch, and she'll stop hunting the large false thing.

It was nearly dawn when she finally slept, and her last waking thought was not of Duvall's seal, which she had not yet seen, nor of the Notary, who was already four days late by one reckoning and none at all by another — but of the letter she had sewn into her cloak, and the instruction she had read so many times she knew its very creases:

*Wait for the sign. If no sign comes by the ninth day, proceed alone and trust no one bearing the republic's name.*

Tomorrow was the ninth day.

And at breakfast, folded under the salt cellar where no hand had visibly placed it, Elodie found a slip of paper written in cipher — her mother's cipher, the one only two living people should have known — with a single line decoded and waiting:

*The Notary is dead. Do not wait.*
