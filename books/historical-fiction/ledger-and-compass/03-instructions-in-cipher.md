## 3. Instructions in Cipher

The letter was waiting for her before she knew there was a place for letters to wait.

She had been in Val-Brume four days — long enough to learn the creak of the third stair above the cooperage, long enough to know that Aurel Vasse took his bread to the oven on the rue Basse at half past eight and his daughter followed with the kindling — when Sabine came up the stairs two at a time and said, without preamble, "There's a woman at the baker's who says she has flour for the wool clerk. We don't have a baker's woman who delivers flour."

Elodie set down the inventory she had been pretending to keep. "Did you answer her?"

"I told her Madame Vasse buys from the mill on the other side of the water and always has." Sabine leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, watching Elodie with that frank, weighing look she seemed unable to suppress. "She said she must have been mistaken. Then she bought a twist of sugar and left. She looked at me the whole time like I was a horse she was pricing."

"What did she wear?"

"A grey cloak. Good boots under it, too good for the town. And a pin at her throat shaped like a wheat sheaf." Sabine paused. "Silver, but old silver. Tarnished in the grooves."

Wheat sheaf. Elodie felt the words settle into her chest like a stone dropped into a well — she heard them land, but the sound came back wrong. The wheat sheaf was the mark for *provisioned, route open*, and its bearer was supposed to be a man named Pellisier whom she had last seen in Lyon eleven months ago, a fat cheerful man who smelled of tobacco and called everyone *cousin*. A woman in good boots was not impossible. Networks changed hands the way towns changed hands. But the well-sound was wrong all the same.

"Thank you," she said. "You're quick."

"I'm bored," Sabine said. "There's a difference." But she lingered a moment longer, studying Elodie's face as though the boredom were a door she might yet be invited through, and then went back down the stairs, taking each step loudly, deliberately, so that anyone listening would know exactly where she was.

* * *

The drop itself told her more than the woman had.

It was behind the salt house, in the gap between two leaning planks of the old fish-drying rack, a place chosen — she realized with a cold, professional admiration — by someone who had read the town the way she had been trained to read towns: a spot visible from three approaches but shielded from the fourth, dry under the eave of the salt-house roof, reachable by a child without seeming odd. She waited until the hour when the salt workers took their soup, then walked past with her basket angled correctly and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth no larger than a communion wafer.

Inside: one sheet, folded twice, written in the figures cipher she had learned at fourteen on a kitchen table in Besançon while her mother pretended to teach her arithmetic.

She did not open it in the street. She did not open it in her room, where the floorboards spoke to the cooperage below. She carried it up through the birch scrub behind the town to the ruined millrace above the falls, where the water still ran unfrozen in its channel and made enough noise to drown a conversation or a cry, and there, sitting on a stone with her back to the wind, she worked the figures with a pencil stub and the patience her mother had beaten into her — *never hurry a decryption, Elodie; a hurried hand makes errors, and an error in cipher is a confession.*

The text resolved line by line:

*To the clerk of M. Roussel, wool merchant, Lyon. Acknowledge receipt of winter stock. Hold position. Your buyer will present himself within the fortnight under the name of NOTARY. He will carry half a playing card — the queen of spades, torn diagonally — which will match the half sewn into your left glove lining. Deliver nothing to any other party. Trust no approach not bearing the card. If he has not come by the feast of Sainte-Luce, burn your correspondence and make what arrangements you can. The republic remembers its servants.*

She read it three times. The first time for content. The second for style. The third for the thing underneath the style, the way she had been taught to listen for footsteps under music.

The cipher was correct. The key-figures matched hers exactly, down to the little irregularities — the transposed seventh, the doubled null at line-end — that made every cipher system as individual as handwriting. Whoever wrote this had the true key. That should have comforted her. It did not, quite, and it took her a long moment sitting in the wind to understand why, and when she understood it she wished she hadn't.

The phrase *the republic remembers its servants* was meant to be warm. It was the sort of sentence a handler put at the bottom of a letter the way a grandmother stitched a heart into a hem. But she had spent eleven months carrying papers through a country where sentences like that were written by men who then burned the servants' names off their own lists, and warmth, in her trade, was a costume worn by exactly two kinds of people: the honest ones, who were rare, and the ones setting a trap, who were not.

And there was something else. Something small and cold.

Her instructions, given in person in Dijon by a man with a scarred cheek who had never given his name, had been explicit: *cross into Val-Brume, hold until the thaw, deliver the ledger to the contact at the spring crossing.* Nothing about a Notary. Nothing about a playing card. Nothing about Sainte-Luce. Either the plan had changed since Dijon — possible; plans changed weekly now, the way governments did — or there were two plans, and she had been given the smaller one.

She fed the letter bit by bit into the millrace and watched the water take it, the ink blurring first, then the paper softening into pulp that vanished downstream toward the falls and whatever lay beyond them. Her left glove sat on her knee. She turned it inside out along the seam she had never had occasion to open, and there it was, exactly as promised: half a queen of spades, torn diagonally, the ragged edge precise. Sewn with fine thread. Sewn, she noted, by someone left-handed; the stitches leaned the wrong way.

So they had planned this before Dijon. Before her. The card had been waiting in her glove the whole journey north, riding silently against her palm through checkpoints and snow while she believed herself to be following the only script that existed.

She sat on the stone until her fingers went numb, and the river talked to itself below her, and she thought: *someone is managing me, and I do not know who, and I do not know how many of us are being managed toward the same door.*

* * *

That evening the cooperage smelled of oak shavings and lamp oil, and Aurel Vasse was mending a barrel hoop by the fire with the unhurried competence of a man who had repaired the same object forty times and expected to repair it forty more. His wife had been dead three years; the house kept her shape the way a glove keeps a hand — a chair angled to the window, a second cup that appeared at table and was filled with chicory though no one drank it. Elodie had learned not to look at the second cup.

Sabine was doing sums by lamplight and getting them wrong on purpose to see if Elodie would correct her, which was apparently a game. Elodie played it badly on purpose, which was its own game.

"You went walking," Aurel said, not looking up from the hoop. "Above the millrace. In this wind."

"The clerk wanted air."

"Mm." He tapped the hoop true against the barrel head. "Air above the millrace. That's where my brother used to go for air, God rest him, and he'd come back with trout in his coat and lies in his mouth. You don't fish. So either you go for the view, which is poor this time of year, or—" He shrugged, a big slow shrug that moved his whole shoulders. "It's no business of mine. A lodger's walking is a lodger's walking. Only I'll say this once, and then I won't say it again: whatever a body carries up that hill, she ought to carry back down lighter. Paper weighs nothing on the way up and a great deal on the way down. I've seen men bent double under it."

Elodie kept her face pleasant and empty, the merchant-clerk's face, mildly amused, faintly dim. But her pulse had gone loud in her ears. Did the ferryman know? Could he know? Or was this only the talk of an older man who had lived through a decade in which everyone carried something?

"That's very philosophical," she said, "for a barrel-maker."

"For a smuggler," Aurel said placidly, "you mean. Everyone in Val-Brume knows what I was before the bridge chains. Half the goods on this river crossed it without paying duty for twenty years, and the tax men knew and let it run because the alternative was knowing harder things." He set the finished hoop aside and finally looked at her, and his eyes were pale grey and entirely unfoolish. "I'm telling you I know what contraband looks like going up a hill. And I'm telling you I've never asked a lodger a question in my life, and I don't intend to start with a girl whose boots cost more than my boat. But if the day comes when you need a hole dug, or a thing moved across water at night, or a question answered without it being asked—" he returned to his hoop, "—I'm cheap, and I'm quiet, and I owe no one anything except dead people."

The fire ticked. Sabine had stopped pretending to do sums. She was watching her father with an expression Elodie couldn't name — something between alarm and fierce pride, the look of a girl discovering her father was made of more layers than she'd guessed.

"I'll remember," Elodie said, and it was the truest sentence she had spoken aloud in four days.

* * *

Six days later, the second letter came to the drop, and it was the letter that broke something open.

She decoded it in the same place, at the millrace, with the wind trying to tear the page out of her hands. It was shorter. It read:

*Delay confirmed. The Notary is redirected. Await further word. Do not attempt independent delivery under any circumstance. The contents are now considered sensitive at a level above your station. Compliance is noted in your favor. — W*

She read it twice, and then a third time standing up, because sitting felt suddenly unsafe, as if the stone beneath her had shifted.

*Above your station.*

In eleven months of service she had been addressed as *cousin*, as *colleague*, once memorably as *the most reliable pair of legs in the southeast*. She had never been condescended to, because couriers were precious and handlers knew it. You did not tell a courier her station. You flattered her, you paid her promptly, and you lied to her only about things that would not get her killed — because a courier who suspected her handler was a courier who might start thinking, and a thinking courier was a courier who could be lost.

This letter did everything wrong. It withheld. It threatened softly. It used the word *compliance*, which belonged to quartermasters and prison clerks, not to comrades. And it signed with a single letter — *W* — where the network's practice, drilled into her at Besançon until it was reflex, was to sign with a location and a date-code so that a compromised letter could be traced to its origin and the damage contained.

No location. No date. Just *W*.

She stood at the rail of the old millrace and watched the black water go over the falls and thought about her mother — properly thought about her, which she avoided doing on schedule the way she avoided thinking about the sea — and about the last thing her mother had ever said to her that wasn't cipher. It was in the kitchen in Besançon, four years ago, the winter before the purge took her. *Elodie. When the day comes that the people giving you orders start talking to you like you're furniture, that's the day you find out what the furniture is for.* She had laughed when she said it. She had also, Elodie remembered now with a strange delayed horror, packed her daughter's bag that same night and taught her the figures cipher by candlelight, fast, as though there were not time enough in the world.

The wind came down the valley with the smell of coming snow. Somewhere below, the town rang its evening bell, counting the hours the way Val-Brume counted everything.

Two letters. Two different temperatures. The first had been warm and specific and had given her a protocol — the card, the Notary, the deadline of Sainte-Luce. The second was cold and vague and had cancelled the protocol without replacing it, leaving her holding a package that was, by its own admission, worth more than her life, and no instruction except to keep carrying it into indefinite darkness.

Either the network was dissolving faster than anyone had admitted — handlers arrested mid-operation, successors improvising with half the key-book — or the network was intact and she was being handled by someone new, someone who signed *W*, someone who had perhaps never intended for the Notary to arrive at all.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not stop doing, standing over the falls with her gloves pulled tight: a courier who waits forever is a courier who can be collected at leisure. A courier told to trust no one is a courier who reports to no one. And a courier whose delivery is deferred indefinitely, whose cargo grows more *sensitive* by the week, whose death would be attributed to the winter, the loyalists, the ice —

A courier like that was not a servant of the republic.

She was a loose end learning to walk.

Elodie folded the letter, fed it to the falls piece by piece, and stood a long moment after the last scrap vanished, her breath smoking in the cold, feeling the first true crack run through the edifice of her obedience — hairline, high up, inaudible, but running.

Then she turned back down toward the town, toward the light in the cooperage window and the second cup at the table, and made herself a promise with no oath attached, the quietest kind: she would wait for the Notary. She would watch for the queen of spades. But she would also, starting tonight, begin to count — days, names, inconsistencies, the weight of paper going up the hill and coming down.

Because if no one was coming, then someone had decided what she was for.

And Elodie Sandoval, alone above the frozen falls with a dead woman's trade in her hands, found that she was not prepared to be furniture.

Below her, at the edge of the dusk, she saw a figure come out of the salt house and stand a moment looking up the hill — too far away to name, too deliberate to ignore — before turning back into the town.
