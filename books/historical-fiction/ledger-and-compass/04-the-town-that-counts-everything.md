## 4. The Town That Counts Everything

The notice was nailed to the church door the morning after the chains went up, and Elodie read it twice before she understood what it was asking of her.

*All inhabitants having entered the commune since the feast of Saint Martin will present themselves to the office of Maître Corbineau, notary, within three days, to be entered in the register of winter residents. Failure to comply will be noted.*

Not punished. *Noted.* In Paris, in Lyon, in every town she had passed through since the republic began eating itself, notices promised punishment. This one only promised to remember.

She stood in the square with her breath smoking and her hands buried in her muff, wearing Anselme Fournier's clerk face — the mild, slightly put-upon expression of a young man who would rather be anywhere than standing in snow. Around her, Val-Brume went about its business with the unhurried economy of a place that had survived a thousand winters by wasting nothing: not heat, not words, not steps. A woman beat a rug against a rail with strokes as regular as a clock. Two boys dragged a sled stacked with split pine. Somewhere behind the cooperage an axe found its rhythm and kept it.

And over everything, like weather, hung the fact of the bridge — chained now, the great links crusted white, the far bank a gray suggestion beyond the ice fog. She was on this side of the river until spring. The knowledge sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Three days. She gave herself one.

* * *

Maître Corbineau's office occupied the ground floor of a narrow stone house between the church and the salt store, and it smelled of ink, tallow, and cold paper — a smell that went through Elodie like a needle finding an old scar. She had grown up in that smell. Her mother had kept their little apartment in Grenoble smelling of it deliberately, saying that a room full of paper was the safest room in the world, because paper could be made to say anything and therefore everyone watched their tongue around it.

Elodie had been nine years old when her mother taught her that. She had been seventeen when they told her the purge had taken her mother's name off the rolls of the living.

The bell above Corbineau's door announced her with a cheerful indifference she distrusted on principle. Inside, shelves rose from floor to ceiling along every wall, sagging under ledgers bound in calf and canvas, their spines labeled in a small, severe hand: *Baptisms. Marriages. Deaths. Weights & Measures. Grain Movements. Bridge Tolls, 1771–. Fires. Floods. Debts Discharged. Debts Unpaid. Strangers.*

Strangers. There was an entire ledger for strangers.

At a desk beneath the one clean window sat a man of perhaps sixty, thin as a lath, with a scholar's stoop and a face like a folded letter — creased, closed, and clearly meant for opening by someone patient enough. He wore his hair powdered but his coat was darned at both elbows, and he was writing, in that same severe hand, without looking up.

"Sit," he said. "You'll wait while I finish the entry. Half-finished entries are how towns go blind."

Elodie sat. There was exactly one other chair, wooden, straight-backed, positioned — she noted with something like professional admiration — where a visitor's eyes would fall naturally on the shelf of registers, as if the room itself were saying: *everything you do here is already written down.*

Corbineau finished his line, blotted it, and looked up. His eyes were pale gray and went over her the way a weighmaster goes over a sack of grain: not unkindly, and missing nothing.

"You are entered at the Vasse house," he said. "Wool merchant's clerk, out of Ambert, name of Fournier." It was not a question. "Your trunk came up from the crossing on Aurel Vasse's sledge on the twenty-second. The toll book notes one trunk, one satchel, and a parcel of wool samples declared at the barrier. The samples were weighed."

"Were they?" said Elodie-Fournier, mildly bored.

"Fourteen pounds, two ounces," said Corbineau. He opened a ledger — *Strangers, Winter of the Eighth Year* — to a fresh page, dipped his pen, and waited. "Name?"

"Fournier. Anselme Fournier."

"Age?"

"Twenty-four."

"Employed by?"

"Messrs. Roche & Vidal, wool factors, Ambert. I'm to buy fleece on credit for spring delivery, except the bridge shut before I'd seen so much as a ewe, so I expect I'll be buying nothing and drinking wine until thaw." She let a shade of resentment color it, the small honest grievance of a young man whose plans had been ruined by weather. Liars, her mother had taught her, should always have a grievance. Grievances explain everything and accuse no one.

Corbineau wrote it all down. Then he set down his pen, folded his hands, and regarded her across the desk with the expression of a man about to commit a small act of violence against etiquette.

"Monsieur Fournier," he said, "I am going to tell you something I tell no one, because it saves time. Everything in this town is written down. Everything. The weight of bread on fast days and feast days. The depth of the river at each of the thirteen soundings posts, read every morning by whoever lives nearest. How many cords of wood the hospice burned last winter and who grumbled about the price. Which families fed which neighbors during the famine year, to the quart. Which men signed the inventory of Citizen Perrin's goods when he was arrested, and what the inventory claimed he owned, and what his widow swore he actually owned, and the difference between the two, which is a number I keep in a separate column because it interests me."

He said none of it proudly. He said it the way a physician says *you will feel a small pain.*

"People find it comical," he went on. "Until they need something. Then they come to me, and I open a book, and there it is: the day, the hour, the witness, the amount. A town that counts everything cannot be lied to about anything. Not by strangers. Not by its own heroes." He uncapped the pen again. "Occupation of your father?"

"Clerk also," Elodie said. "Dead."

"Mother?"

The pause lasted half a heartbeat too long. Elodie heard it herself, the way one hears a false coin ring. "Also dead. Fever."

Corbineau wrote it. He did not look at her. But she felt, leaving that office with her false name drying in the register of strangers, that she had been weighed to the ounce — fourteen pounds, two ounces — and that somewhere in those shelves, a column had been opened in which the difference between what she claimed and what she was would be kept, because it interested him.

* * *

She should have stayed away after that. Instead she invented reasons.

There were legitimate ones, or legitimate-shaped ones. A wool clerk needed to know the town: who sold fodder, who repaired boots, who might hire sledge work come thaw. And Val-Brume being Val-Brume, every such errand ended sooner or later in the notary's orbit, because half the transactions in town passed under his pen. Elodie found herself back in the ink-smelling room on the pretext of witnessing a bill of sale for Sabine's aunt, then for a dispute over a shared woodpile, then simply — she told herself — to ask Corbineau's advice on which farmers kept merinos worth the name.

But the truth, which she admitted only in the privacy of the false-bottomed trunk late at night, was simpler and more dangerous: she wanted to see the books.

It was the archivist's itch, the one her mother had planted in her and she had spent years pretending was merely a talent for detail. Other couriers memorized routes and faces; Elodie memorized filing systems. Give her ten minutes alone in any chancellery and she could tell you where the dangerous papers slept. And Corbineau's shelves were the most beautiful thing she had seen in five years of war and paperwork. Not beautiful the way the Tuileries was beautiful — that gilded lying beauty — but beautiful the way a fortress wall is beautiful: everything load-bearing, nothing ornamental.

Because that was the thing she understood slowly, over two weeks of visits and borrowed errands, watching the old notary work: the town itself was a ledger. Not metaphorically. Literally. Every door in Val-Brume was an entry. The baker's chalk board was an entry. The thirteen sounding-posts on the frozen river, each with its daily notch cut into a painted stake, were entries. When Madame Roux's cow wandered and was returned by the miller's boy, someone told Corbineau, and it went in the book, and thereby became true forever. The town had built itself, generation after generation, out of the same material Elodie carried against her spine in coded columns: *witness, fixed in ink.*

And like any great ledger, it recorded crimes as readily as kindnesses, in the same fair hand, on facing pages.

She saw it plainly one gray afternoon when Corbineau, needing an apprentice's extra pair of hands to shift a fallen stack, let her into the inner room — the room behind the room, where the older registers lived. Dust lay on them like first snow. He pulled a volume at random — *Year II, Commune of Val-Brume* — hunting some land boundary, and it fell open near the middle, and Elodie's eye, traitor that it was, went straight to the page.

Requisitions. Dated, witnessed, itemized. So many bushels of barley taken for the armies of the republic, so many horses, at prices set not by market but by decree. And below the requisitions, in the same hand, same ink, same day: *Paid to Widow Marchal for the keeping of three orphans of the late Antoine Marchal, conscript, per month, until majority.* Kindness and theft, side by side, balanced in a single column of arithmetic. The republic, entering its own soul in double-entry.

"Careful," Corbineau said quietly, taking the volume from her hands. Not sharp. Just quiet, the way a man quiets a dog that has scented something across a valley. "Some of these pages are heavier than they look."

"I can carry heavy pages, Maître."

He looked at her then — the pale gray weighmaster's look, held a moment too long — and set the volume back with its spine aligned precisely to its neighbors. "So I begin to suspect," he said, and asked no further question, which frightened her more than any interrogation would have.

* * *

That evening she climbed to her room above the cooperage with lamplight bleeding up through the floorboards — the Vasses below, Sabine's voice rising and falling as she argued some point of boat geometry with her father — and took out the ledger.

She did not open it. She had learned discipline the hard way: never work the cipher in a house where the light shows through walls. Instead she sat with it on her knees, wrapped in its oilcloth, feeling its weight change as her understanding of Val-Brume deepened. Fourteen pounds, two ounces, minus the wool samples she had actually declared. What remained was a book of names, dates, sums — requisitions, payments, signatures — and she had assumed, carrying it over the mountains, that its power lay in its secrecy. That was what her orders implied. Deliver it sealed or not at all; the courier's eyes were not part of the cargo.

But Corbineau's shelves had shown her something else. Secrecy was fragile; it died with its keeper, and couriers, everyone knew, died easily. Records were different. A record copied was a record multiplied. A record witnessed was a record armed. The republic's men could burn a manuscript — they had burned plenty — but they could not burn a town that had spent two hundred years writing down the weight of its own bread.

Her mother had taught her codes. Her mother, dead these six years, purged with a hundred others whose names existed now only in files this ledger might enumerate. Elodie ran her thumb along the oilcloth seam and thought: *if the names in here are who I believe they are, then somewhere in this town of obsessive recorders there may exist the second testimony — the mundane, undeniable, civilian corroboration that turns accusation into proof.*

A requisition signed by a celebrated liberator means one thing. The same requisition, dated and witnessed in a mountain notary's register, means another entirely. One is a claim. The other is a fact.

Below her feet, Sabine's voice broke off mid-argument, and Aurel's low laugh followed it, and the axe behind the cooperage had gone silent for the night. Elodie blew out her candle and sat in the dark doing what she did instead of praying: counting. Thirteen sounding posts. Two hundred years of registers. One widow's column of difference. And in her lap, fourteen pounds minus samples, waiting.

In the morning there was a knock at her door, early, before the light. Sabine stood on the landing in her father's oversized coat, cheeks red with cold, holding out a pair of nailed boots.

"If you're going to be here till thaw," she said, "you'll want to learn which parts of the river hold a man and which parts eat him. Father says the ice has moods. Come and meet them."
