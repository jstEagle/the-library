## 5. Sabine's Map of the Ice

The frost came for the windows on my third morning in Val-Brume, feathering the glass so thickly that the light through it turned the color of skimmed milk. I woke in the room above the cooperage with my breath hanging over the blanket like a small ghost, and lay still a moment, listening to the house below me: Aurel's boots on the stair, the clatter of the stove door, Sabine's voice rising in some argument or other — she seemed to argue the way other people breathed, continuously and without malice.

I had been in the town four days. Four days of playing the wool clerk, of keeping my hands ink-stained and my questions dull, of measuring every sentence twice before I let it out. Four days, and the Notary had not come.

But that morning something else came instead: a knock at my door, three sharp raps, and then Sabine's voice through the wood. "You're awake. I can hear you not moving."

I opened the door. She stood in the corridor with her arms crossed and a wool cap jammed down to her eyebrows, fourteen years old and built like a spar, with her father's long jaw and her dead mother's dark eyes — eyes that did not glance at things but took inventory of them.

"You said yesterday," she began, "that you wanted to see the river."

"I said I thought the river might be worth seeing."

"You said you were a merchant's man and merchants' men don't walk on ice." Her mouth curved. "So which is it? Because I'm going either way, and I'd rather have company than talk to myself."

There was no refusing Sabine Vasse. I suspect there never had been. I pulled on my coat, wound my scarf high enough to hide the lower half of my face from habit, and followed her down into the cold.

* * *

The river at Val-Brume was not a river anymore; it was a road pretending otherwise. From the bluff behind the cooperage it ran white and vast between its wooded banks, a mile of frozen water gone smooth as poured tallow in the bends and ridged like plowed ground where the current had fought the frost and lost. Downstream the old ferry landing stood empty, Aurel's flatboat hauled up and chained, its season over. The bridge chains had gone up behind me the night I arrived. Now the river itself was the only way in or out, and it belonged entirely to winter.

"Mind where you step until I say," Sabine told me, stepping off the bluff onto the ice with the easy confidence of someone who had done it since she could walk. "Everyone thinks ice is one thing. It isn't. It's a dozen things wearing one face."

She crouched and rapped her knuckles against the surface — a bright, ringing knock, like a door. "Hear that? That's good ice. That's ice you can trust with a loaded sledge." She moved six paces left and knocked again: a flatter sound, a drum with a cloth over it. "That's ice over running water. It'll hold you today and eat you Thursday." Six paces more, near a dark seam where two floes had frozen together, and a third knock: dull, dead, apologetic. "And that's ice over nothing at all. Spring feed. The river never really stops under here; it just gets quiet about it."

"And how do you know the difference?" I asked.

"You listen. You watch. You learn what the river looks like when it's lying to you." She glanced up at me, wind-flushed, triumphant. "Nobody ever teaches you this because nobody thinks a girl needs to know it. My father taught me anyway. He says the river doesn't care who you are, so it's the fairest teacher in the world."

I looked at this child — this fierce, unarmored creature walking a mile of hidden water as if it were her own kitchen floor — and felt something in my chest shift sideways. I had spent five years being underestimated professionally. It was the whole architecture of my trade: the dull clerk, the timid widow's daughter, the girl who couldn't possibly matter. I wore underestimation the way other women wore shawls. And here was Sabine Vasse, who had never chosen hers, chafing against it like a horse against a halter, and teaching herself the river precisely because no one had asked her to.

"Show me everything," I said.

Her whole face lit. "Everything takes all day."

"We have all day."

* * *

She was true to her word. She took me downstream along the eastern bank first, where the ice rode high over submerged boulders in pale domes she called the river's knuckles. She showed me where the mill race entered and the ice thinned to grey glass, thin enough that we could lie flat — she made me lie flat, laughing at my dignity — and look straight down through a foot of clear cold crystal at pebbles turning slowly in the current beneath, like coins at the bottom of a jeweler's tray.

"That's the prettiest place to die on the whole river," she said cheerfully. "People come to look. The ice lets them look for exactly as long as it likes them."

Past the mill she turned us upstream instead, along the western shallows, and here her manner changed. She stopped joking. She walked slower, and she talked in the low, even voice of an instructor who has decided her pupil is worth instructing.

"This is the part that matters," she said. "If you ever have to go somewhere in winter — really *have* to, when the passes are shut and the roads are watched — you go by the river. Everyone knows the bridge closes. Nobody thinks about the river opening." She pointed to a line of willow scrub marking the bank. "See those? Willows mean springs under the bank. Ice near willows is treacherous except right here—" she indicated a narrow lane of blue-white ice hard against the far channel "—where the current sweeps clean under the frost and makes it thick fast. There's a path. It's not marked. My grandfather found it running brandy past the toll stations in the last war, and my father kept it, and now I know it, and if you're very polite to me I might show you where it starts."

"Why would you show me?" I asked. "You've known me four days. You don't know anything about me except that I carry too many trunks for a wool clerk and write letters at strange hours."

Sabine stopped walking. She turned and looked at me fully, and I saw that behind the wind-chapped bravado there was a mind doing arithmetic.

"I know you listen," she said. "When my father talks about rope weights and freight, you actually listen. When Madame Perrin downstairs talks about her rheumatism for the hundredth time, you listen to that too, same face. Most people pretend to listen while they wait to talk. You don't." She kicked a chip of ice ahead of her, watching it skate. "Also, you lie."

I felt the cold go through me in a new way. "I beg your pardon?"

"You lie beautifully. About wool. Yesterday you told Monsieur Ferrand that Lyon prices were softening and he believed you, and I could tell from your face that you invented it on the spot and weren't even slightly sorry." She shrugged. "I don't care. My father smuggled brandy through two wars; lying is a trade skill in this family. But I think you should know that I know. In case you were wondering whether anyone here is watching you." A pause, and then, with terrible fourteen-year-old gentleness: "Somebody is, by the way. The man at the inn who says he's waiting for the thaw to travel — he watches the coopers' yard every evening. He counts the windows."

My blood performed a slow, cold turn. Duvall. Or one of his creatures — the town was already acquiring eyes, and I had been four days among them. I kept my face pleasant and stupid, the wool-clerk face, and said, "A curious fellow, is he?"

"He's a bored one," Sabine said. "Bored men count windows. Anyway." She resumed walking, briskly, as though she had not just dismantled my security in a sentence. "The path starts past the third willow stand. Come on. If we hurry we can make the standing stone before the light goes."

* * *

The standing stone stood a half-league upriver, alone on a shelf of rock above the western channel: a granite finger older than any nation, crusted with lichen and older carvings worn nearly smooth. Someone had cut fresh initials beside the ancient ones — S.V., and beside it, smaller, A.V., and above both, faint and weathered, another pair of letters that might have read M.V.

"My mother," Sabine said, when she caught me looking. She said it plainly, without ceremony. She touched the older carving once, lightly, the way you touch a doorframe you grew up under. "She used to bring us up here. Father won't come anymore. He goes as far as the second bend and finds a reason to stop." She sat down on the stone's base and dug in her satchel and produced bread, hard cheese, a flask of something that smelled like it predated the republic. "Sit. This is where you get fed. It's tradition. I just decided."

I sat. We ate. Below us the river ran white to the horizon, and the afternoon sun struck it so that the whole valley floor blazed like hammered silver, and for perhaps ten minutes neither of us said anything at all, and it was the best silence I had kept in five years.

Then Sabine said, "Your mother's alive?"

The question went through me like a splinter finding its depth. "Why do you ask that?"

"Because of how you hold your trunk. When you move it, you hold it from underneath, like it's heavy in a particular way — like there's something in one corner." She chewed her cheese. "And because when I asked about your mother just now, you went still. People whose mothers are dead go sad. You went still. Still means deciding." She looked out at the river. "I'm not prying. I just notice things. It's the most annoying thing about me, according to everyone."

I should have lied to her. I want to be honest in this account, wherever honesty leads, and honesty requires me to record that every trained instinct I possessed instructed me to lie: to give her a dead mother, a merchant uncle, a sick aunt in Grenoble, the whole furniture of the false life I maintained like a stage set. Five years of discipline said: *the fewer true things a witness holds, the less they can be made to surrender.*

But the stone was warm in the sun. The river blazed. And Sabine Vasse had shown me the river's secret grammar in one day and named the watcher at the inn in one sentence, and asked for nothing back, and I was so tired — God, how tired I was, tired in the marrow, tired of being a locked room that walked around in a coat.

"She may be dead," I said. "I was told she died. Seven years ago, in the purges. I've recently begun to doubt the telling."

Sabine considered this with the seriousness she gave to ice conditions. "Doubt's worse than grief," she said at last. "Grief at least sits still." Then she unscrewed the flask and held it out to me. "Here. It's cherry brandy. Grandfather's recipe. It fixes nothing but it argues with everything."

I drank. It burned splendidly. We passed it back and forth like conspirators, which in a sense we were, though neither of us yet knew of what.

* * *

We came home by the willow path, and she showed me the courier's lane as promised: a thread of trustworthy ice threading the treacherous banks, invisible unless you knew to read the willows and the current's sweep, a road that existed only in the knowledge of three generations of Vasses and now, uninvited, in mine. I memorized it as I memorized everything — landmarks, bearings, the count of paces between safe lanes — but I memorized this differently. Not as an asset. As a gift.

At the edge of the coopers' yard the light was failing, and the lamps were coming up along the street, and there in the doorway of the inn across the square stood the man Sabine had named: broad-shouldered, well-booted, a traveler's greatcoat with a traveler's boredom, ostensibly studying the weather. Studying, in fact, the windows of the cooperage. Counting them, perhaps. I did not know his name yet. I would learn it soon enough, and learn it with a chill.

Sabine saw him too. She didn't point; pointing is for amateurs. She simply said, quietly, "Third evening running. He always stands there while the lamplighter works. Lamplighters are useful that way — everyone watches the lamps, so nobody watches the watcher."

"How old are you?" I asked her.

"Fourteen."

"Remind me never to play cards against you."

"You'd lose," she said comfortably, and then she did something that undid me more thoroughly than the brandy had. She put out her hand — a work-roughened, chilblained hand, offered formally, the way gentlemen seal bargains — and said, "Whatever you're carrying in that trunk, and whoever's chasing you for it: the river path is yours. That's not a loan. It's yours. I don't give things twice."

I took her hand. Her grip was strong and cold and absolutely certain.

"Sabine," I said, "one day I may need to ask you for something much harder than a path."

"I know," she said. "I'll probably say yes. Don't tell my father."

We went in to supper smelling of river and cherry brandy, and Aurel looked at the two of us — at his daughter flushed with triumph and his lodger flushed with something she hadn't felt in years — and shook his head with the resignation of a man who understood that certain alliances form above his authority.

That night I wrote nothing in my cipher journal. For the first time since I'd taken up the trade, I was afraid to set down the truest thing I knew, which was this: I had acquired, in a single day, a loyalty that owed nothing to the republic, nothing to my handlers, nothing to duty — a loyalty to a girl on the ice and a widower's table and a carved stone with three sets of initials. Such loyalties were liabilities in my profession. Couriers were supposed to be weather, passing through, touching nothing.

I was no longer weather.

And downstairs, through the floorboards, I heard Sabine telling her father in a low, urgent voice that the man at the inn had asked her, very casually, very politely, how many people slept above the cooperage —

— and I reached for my boots, because the counting had begun.
