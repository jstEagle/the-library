## 6. A Man Who Claims the Road

The bells of Val-Brume did not ring for the arrival of Agent Duvall; they were too busy ringing for the bridge.

Elodie heard them at first light from her window above the cooperage — a slow, deliberate tolling she had not heard in her three weeks in the town, and which Sabine had described once with a shrug: *that means the chains are going up. That means no one leaves and no one comes until the thaw forgives us.* The sound was meant to be ceremonial. To Elodie it landed like a door closing behind her, and behind that door, all the roads back to Paris, to Lyon, to any place where her orders might still be waiting.

She dressed slowly, listening to the iron note fade over the rooftops, and told herself what she always told herself: a courier cut off is not a courier lost. The cargo matters more than the road. But her hands were cold in a way the stove in the corner could not account for.

The commotion began an hour later.

She heard it before she saw it — hooves on frozen mud, more of them than Val-Brume usually hosted in a week, and then voices raised near the market square in that particular register of official grievance that Elodie had learned to recognize across three republics and two kingdoms. She pulled on her coat, settled her merchant's-clerk posture around her shoulders like a second garment, and went down the stairs past the smell of oak shavings and hot pitch that lived permanently in the Vasse house.

Aurel Vasse stood in his doorway with his arms folded, watching the square. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with the permanent squint of someone who had spent thirty years reading water instead of print. He didn't look at her when he spoke.

"They've come," he said. "A gentleman with papers. Says he has authority over the winter crossing."

"Over the crossing? The crossing is chained."

"So he says. He says it's been chained *wrong*. Says there are exceptions to be made." Aurel's mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely without warmth. "In my experience, mademoiselle, when a man arrives claiming exceptions, the exception is himself."

* * *

The market square of Val-Brume was small enough that a crowd there felt like a family argument and large enough, this morning, to feel like a verdict. Elodie positioned herself as a clerk would — half a step behind the knot of townspeople, close enough to hear, far enough to watch hands rather than faces.

The man on the gray horse wore a traveling coat of good wool, unadorned but expensively cut, the kind of plainness that cost more than decoration. He was perhaps fifty, lean, with the colorless complexion of a man who worked indoors at other men's business. His voice was the worst thing about him. It was courteous. It never rose. It addressed the crowd of frostbitten tradespeople as though they were a committee whose cooperation he regretted having to request.

"—and I want to be clear," he was saying, "that no one here is accused of anything. The Directory's concern is the movement of goods and correspondence during the closed season. Smuggling flourishes on frozen rivers. Sedition travels by sledge as easily as salt does. I am here to ensure that nothing crosses the ice out of Val-Brume that ought to stay in Val-Brume."

"And who says what ought?" That was Maître Corbineau, the town notary, standing at the front with his ledger-bound arms crossed over his chest like a man defending his own rib cage. He was a dry, precise man of about sixty whom Elodie had met twice in her capacity as a wool clerk — both times he had asked her questions so meticulous about her employer's shipments that she'd briefly wondered if he suspected everyone of everything. Perhaps he did. "By what commission do you claim authority over a municipal crossing, citizen?"

"By this."

Duvall — for it was already being murmured that this was his name — produced a document from inside his coat. Corbineau read it, and Elodie watched the notary's face do something small and terrible: not fear exactly, but the recalculation of a careful man measuring how much trouble had just arrived in his town and finding the sum larger than expected.

"The seal is irregular," Corbineau said at last. "I don't know this signature."

"You wouldn't," said Duvall pleasantly. "It's from above your level, maître. With respect. Most things are."

A murmur went through the crowd. Somewhere behind Elodie, she heard Sabine Vasse whisper to a friend, loud enough to carry, "*Above his level* — imagine thinking such a thing." A few people laughed. Duvall did not laugh, but neither did he appear wounded. He simply turned in the saddle and looked at the crowd with the mild patience of a veterinarian among animals, and the laughter died of natural causes.

Elodie should have looked away. She knew the discipline: never let your eyes rest longer than anyone else's. But she was watching his gloved hand fold the commission away, and on the fold of the paper, catching the low winter sun, was the wax.

Black wax. A compass rose pressed into it, its needle broken off clean at the center — a seal she had seen twice before in her life, both times on documents she had carried and never been permitted to read, and once more recently, three nights ago, in a margin.

Her mother's margin.

The recognition went through her like river water through a boot: sudden, freezing, total. There, in the coded ledger sewn into the false bottom of her trunk, in the annotations surrounding the ciphered requisitions, the same device appeared again and again beside initials — a broken compass rose, drawn small and quick in faded ink, marking payments *approved*, movements *authorized*, names *cleared for disposal*. She had assumed it was a faction's mark, some committee within the machinery of the purge. She had not imagined the man wearing it would ride into the same snowbound town as herself.

He had signed, or countersigned, or at least touched — the ledger. And now he claimed the road.

Cold moved up her spine and settled between her shoulder blades like a hand. She thought: *he is here for the same reason you are.* Or worse: *he is here because of what you carry.* The last courier before her had vanished somewhere south of the mountains — she knew this much, and no more. Perhaps the vanishing had a name, and the name was riding a gray horse.

Duvall's gaze swept the square. It passed over her the way a lantern passes over a wall — evenly, thoroughly, pausing nowhere. That was the frightening part. A man who stared was a man who suspected. A man who looked at everyone the same was a man who suspected everyone.

"Good day, citizens," he said, and dismounted, and walked toward the inn as though the town had been expecting him for years.

* * *

That evening the cooperage smelled of pitch and woodsmoke, and Sabine sat cross-legged on the floor mending a net while Elodie pretended to review invoices by candlelight. Aurel had gone to the inn — "to drink and listen," he said, "which are the same activity in Val-Brume" — and the two women had the workshop to themselves.

Sabine worked the net without looking up. "Papa says the gentleman took the best room. Says he tipped the boy a whole livre for carrying one bag." A pause. "One bag, mademoiselle Sandoval. What sort of agent travels light?"

"A confident one." Elodie kept her eyes on the invoice. "Or a man who expects everything else to be provided."

"He asked about the ice paths. Which ones hold weight, where the springs run under and make rotten patches. Old Gaspard told him true, mostly. Papa says he'll find out the rest by falling through, God willing."

"Sabine."

"I'm only saying—"

"*Sabine.*" Elodie set down the invoice and made herself meet the girl's bright, defiant eyes. Sabine was nineteen and fearless in the way of people who had never yet lost anything worth losing, and Elodie liked her enormously, and that was precisely why the next sentence cost something. "You must not speak of him. Not mock him, not describe him, not tell me or anyone what you see him do. Do you understand me?"

The girl blinked. For a moment Elodie saw the hurt arrive, and then, remarkably, saw understanding arrive behind it — quick as her father reading water. "Because he counts who talks," Sabine said quietly.

"Because he counts everything. That is what such men are *for*."

Sabine wound the net cord around her fist, considering. Then, in a voice carefully stripped of its former mischief: "Then I saw nothing today. I was mending nets."

"Perfect," said Elodie. "Be boring. Be invisible. Boring people survive winters."

But when Sabine had gone up to bed, Elodie sat alone in the workshop with the candle burning down, and admitted to herself what she had been refusing to admit since the square.

She was afraid.

Not the clean fear of a border guard lifting her papers — that fear she knew how to use, how to spend like coin. This was the other kind, the cold banked kind, the fear of being *known*. Because the seal in the ledger's margin was not merely similar to the seal on Duvall's commission. It was identical down to the break of the needle, and broken seals did not occur twice by accident. Whoever drew that little ruined compass in the margins had known the original — had handled it, used it, perhaps worn it. And whoever drew those marginal marks had annotated requisitions for rope and lime and timber in quantities that mapped, page by page, onto the executions Elodie had not yet deciphered but already understood in her bones.

If Duvall's hand had touched that ledger — even indirectly, even years ago — then the document in her trunk was not merely evidence against celebrated men. It was evidence against the man sleeping fifty paces from her tonight, with one bag and a great deal of confidence.

And a further thought arrived, unwelcome as a debt collector: if Duvall had authorized the ledger's contents, why would he hunt it? Unless he wanted it not to suppress it but to complete it — unless there were pages still missing from her packet, pages that named him, and he had come to collect his own indictment before it could be delivered.

Or unless he had sent it. The thought turned itself over, showing its underside. Someone had routed this ledger through mountains in winter, through a courier chain that had already swallowed one messenger. Someone had designed a delivery so dangerous that failure was likelier than success. Who designs a route meant to fail? The sender who wants the cargo buried with the carrier.

She blew out the candle and sat in the dark, and let the fear finish its work. Because that was the old training, her mother's training, from the years when her mother was still alive and teaching a sharp-eyed girl to cipher: *fear is information, Elodie. Feel all of it. Then ask what it is telling you.*

What it told her was this: she had spent three weeks in Val-Brume guarding a sealed box, waiting obediently for a contact who had never come, treating the ledger as a parcel. A parcel waits. But Duvall's arrival changed the arithmetic. If he claimed the winter roads, then every day the ledger sat hidden was a day it could be found, seized, burned. Waiting was no longer prudence. Waiting was surrender with better manners.

The ledger had to be read. Now, beginning tonight, page by page, whatever it cost her sleep. If her mother's hand had written in those margins — and Elodie had begun, guiltily, hungrily, to suspect it — then the least she owed the dead was to know what they had risked everything to say.

Resolve, she discovered, feels very little like courage. It feels like cold turning to purpose. It felt, tonight, like a woman climbing the stairs to her room, kneeling before her trunk, and drawing out the false-bottom panel with steady fingers, while below her the town slept and the agent with the broken-compass seal slept too, and the ice on the river creaked and settled in the dark like a thing counting the days until spring.

She untied the oilcloth. She lit two candles instead of one.

The first ciphered page waited under her hand, and in its margin, faint as breath, the little broken compass rose pointed — she noticed it for the first time now, truly noticed — not north, but toward the next page. Her mother, or whoever her mother had become, had left a trail.

Elodie dipped her pen, copied the first line of cipher into her notebook, and began.
