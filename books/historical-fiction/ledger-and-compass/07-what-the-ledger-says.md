## 7. What the Ledger Says

The cooperage below her window had gone quiet an hour past midnight, and still Elodie sat at the little table with the candle drawn close, its flame bent by the draft that came under the door like a curious animal. She had waited three nights for this — three nights of lying in the dark above Aurel Vasse's yard, listening to Sabine's even breathing through the wall, counting the hours until the house slept deep enough.

Now she opened the trunk.

The false bottom lifted with a sound she had learned to make silent: a quarter-turn of the brass catch hidden beneath the leather strap, then a slow, even pull, as if she were drawing out a drawer of linen. The ledger came up wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath the oilcloth it was only a book — a merchant's daybook, someone had written on the flyleaf in a clerk's careful hand, *Inventaire des marchandises, année VII*. A lie on the very first page, which was fitting. Everything about it lied. That was what had kept it alive.

She set it flat, weighted the corners with two candlesticks and the stone she'd taken from the riverbank, and began.

Her mother had taught her the cipher when she was nine years old, at a kitchen table in Lyon, with sugar spilled across the wood to stand for letters. *Numbers are honest*, her mother had said, sweeping the grains into rows. *People lie with words all day long, but a number only says what it is. So we hide the truth where no one thinks to look for it — in arithmetic.* The system was old-fashioned, deliberately so, built on a base key that changed with the year and a running offset drawn from the page numbers themselves. Elodie had thought herself clever at fourteen for cracking a letter meant for her father. Her mother had laughed until she cried, then spent the rest of the evening showing her every way the cipher could be broken, so that she would never trust it too much.

*Never trust a lock*, her mother said. *Trust only that you have time.*

She had time now. The winter had seen to that.

The first pages were almost disappointingly mundane. Requisitions — grain, salt, cloth, boots — dated from the spring of Year VI, each entry keyed to a name rendered in figures. She worked the offsets slowly, checking each line twice against her memorized tables, writing nothing down until she was certain, because a half-deciphered page in her own hand was a confession waiting to be found. The candle burned down; she trimmed it with scissors and kept working. Frost climbed the inside of the window glass in ferns. Somewhere below, a barrel stave settled against its neighbors with a knock like a knuckle on a door, and she froze, hand flat on the ledger, and listened for a full minute before she breathed again.

By the second hour the names began to resolve, and the mundanity curdled.

*Requisition, 14 Germinal: 340 sacks wheat, commune of Saint-Aubin-le-Froid. Compensation: none recorded. Authorized:* — and here the figures gave way to a signature block, three names, and Elodie knew them. Not because they were obscure, but because they were everywhere. They were on the commemorative prints sold in the valley markets. They were in the schoolmaster's lessons that Sabine recited over supper with a teenager's contempt. They were the men whose portraits hung in the prefecture at Grenoble, liberators of the south, defenders of the Republic against its enemies, their faces engraved in profile like Roman coins.

One of them had signed away 340 sacks of wheat from a village whose granaries, if the requisition was any measure, had been stripped to the boards before harvest. There was no compensation recorded. There was never compensation recorded. She checked. Page after page, the same column, empty as a held breath.

She told herself it was wartime. Requisitions happened; the Republic ate its own countryside the way any army eats. She had carried dispatches that ordered worse things than hunger, or so she had assumed without ever reading them — couriers were not supposed to read, and she had been a good courier, obedient in the specific way of a woman who has learned that curiosity is a debt collected later, with interest.

But the ledger did not stop at grain.

* * *

The executions began on the ninth leaf, and Elodie knew it before she deciphered a word, because the handwriting changed.

That was the thing about ciphers that no one warned you: they concealed content, but not character. Whoever had kept this ledger wrote his requisitions in a brisk, cramped hand, all economy, a man filling in forms. But on the ninth leaf the figures grew taller, more deliberate, spaced apart like mourners at a graveside. He had slowed down. He had made himself write each number carefully, because each number was a person.

*Executed by order of the Commission of Public Safety, departmental section, 2 Floréal.* Four names. Then six, three days later. Then eleven, in a single entry, with a note appended in the same tall, grieving figures: *of whom 3 under age of sixteen; property seized per schedule annexed.*

Under age of sixteen.

Elodie sat back. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were listening. Below her floorboards the cooperage ticked and settled, ordinary sounds, the sounds of a town asleep around its small honest trades — hoops and barrels, bread and rope — while somewhere in the past two years these lines had been written by lamplight by a man who counted children among the condemned and could do nothing but count them accurately.

She should stop. Some cold, sensible voice in her — the courier's voice, the voice that had kept her alive through four years of roads and checkpoints — said clearly: *You were given this to carry, not to read. Every page you turn is another weight tied to your ankles if you're taken. Read nothing. Deliver everything. That is the whole of your duty.*

But the sensible voice had grown thin lately, worn like an old coin passed through too many hands. And there was another consideration, one she could not argue with: whoever had compiled this ledger had wanted it read eventually. Why else encode it so laboriously? A dead-drop message was encoded to survive interception. A ledger of two hundred pages was encoded to survive *forgetting*. Someone had believed that someday, somewhere, a person with the key and the patience would sit down exactly as she was sitting, and turn these leaves, and know.

She turned the next leaf.

The payments followed the executions the way shadow follows a body. This was the part the ledger's compiler had perhaps hated most; she could feel it in the hand, which lost its funereal spacing and became tight, furious, the figures pressed hard enough to emboss the paper. Sums disbursed. To whom, the cipher gave in plain designation after designation — and here Elodie's breath caught, because the recipients were not faceless quartermasters. They were the same three names. The liberators. The men on the coins.

They had signed the requisitions that starved the villages, countersigned the commissions that shot the villagers who protested, and then drawn payment — from a fund labeled, with a bureaucrat's unblinking irony, *extraordinary expenses of public salvation* — for the efficiency of the operation. There was even a column for *incidental losses*, and beside it, once, in the margin of the coded text, a notation that decoded to a single word:

*Priced.*

Elodie put her hand over her mouth. It was not horror, exactly, or not only horror. It was vertigo — the specific vertigo of a floor that turns out to have been painted on air. She had spent four years believing she served a side, however battered, however compromised; the Republic was bleeding, yes, besieged, yes, forced into hardness, yes — she had repeated the litany a thousand times on cold roads, and the litany had kept her warm. Men like Duvall were the enemy: men who served the old order, who wanted the ledgers of the Revolution burned so the aristocracy could come home. She carried truth *for* the Republic. That was the shape of her life. She had never examined the shape, because a courier who examines the shape of her cargo starts asking what else is in the box.

And the box, it turned out, held this. Held wheat priced in blood. Held boys of fifteen entered under *executed* between a shipment of muskets and a note about horseshoes. Held the signatures of heroes, in the same confident hand that had signed the proclamations pasted on walls from Lyon to Nice, promising justice, promising bread, promising that the Republic's enemies were only ever *them* — the aristocrats, the émigrés, the foreign kings — never us, never ours, never the men whose profiles glittered on the coins in Elodie's own purse.

She looked at the coin purse hanging on the bedpost. One of those profiles looked back at her from a worn five-franc piece, and for the first time in her life she saw it as a mask, and wondered whose face had really been underneath.

* * *

Toward dawn she found the annotations.

They were faint — pencil, mostly, worked into the margins between the coded lines, in a shorthand that was itself a second cipher, simpler, older. She recognized it the way you recognize a song heard once in childhood: her mother used it for grocery lists, for jokes, for small treasons of affection. *Buy the good butter, tell your father it was cheap.* Elodie had learned it at seven without knowing it was a cipher at all, which was, of course, the point. Her mother had understood concealment the way fish understand water.

The annotations were sparse and merciless. Beside the requisition of 14 Germinal: *granary emptied 3 days BEFORE harvest due. Deliberate starvation to force compliance.* Beside the execution roll of 2 Floréal: *names taken from denunciation list sold by [illegible] for 200 francs. No evidence reviewed.* Beside the payments: *same fund paid for the printing of the proclamation praising mercy.*

And once, alone on a margin near the middle of the first gathering, in letters so small she had to hold the page to the candle and nearly singe her sleeve:

*If you are reading this, you have the key, which means I taught you, which means I am dead or worse. Do not deliver this to anyone who asks for it. Ask instead who benefits from its arrival. Count, my love. Numbers are honest. People are not.*

Elodie did not move for a long time.

The candle had burned to a stub; the light shrank and the shadows leaned in over the table like men reading over her shoulders. Her mother had died — had *been killed*, the family had always said, though the story shifted with each telling — in the purge of Year IV, swept up with her printing circle in Lyon, denounced, disappeared. Elodie had been thirteen. She had built her whole adult self on that grave: the orphan of the Republic's martyrs, serving the cause her mother died for, carrying her mother's codes down her mother's kind of roads. Grief had been the foundation stone. She had never once thought to test whether the stone bore a true inscription.

But the hand in the margins was her mother's hand. Not similar. Identical — the particular way the numeral 4 was left open at the top, the little hook she added to sevens, the habit of crossing t's with a single impatient stroke that trailed off like a sentence abandoned mid-thought. Elodie knew that hand better than she knew her own face. She had watched it move across a thousand pages. She had inherited it, in fact; her own writing drifted toward it whenever she stopped guarding against it, the way her voice drifted toward her mother's accent when she was tired.

Which meant her mother had lived past Year IV. Had lived long enough to compile this — or to begin it, or to gather what another man had begun, the brisk cramped hand of the main entries, the mourner's hand of the execution rolls. Had lived, and had not come home, and had let her daughter grow up on a false grave.

Or — the other possibility arrived like ice water — had lived, and had been made to disappear so thoroughly that even her death was a fiction, and the fiction had been useful to someone. Useful enough to leave a daughter primed, trained, loyal, and blind. Useful enough that when a ledger needed carrying out of the south by someone with the key in her head and no family left to ransom, there was exactly one candidate, already vetted by grief, already grateful for the work.

*Ask who benefits from its arrival.*

Elodie closed the ledger. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she had expected them to shake. Perhaps steadiness was simply what happened when something broke cleanly, all at once, rather than crumbling.

The window had gone from black to the deep grey-blue that in Val-Brume preceded sunrise by an hour. Down in the yard, Aurel was moving already — she could hear the muffled ring of the well-chain, the scrape of his boots — and the smell of the first fire reached her through the floorboards, woodsmoke and cold iron. The town was waking into an ordinary day. Sabine would be up soon, sharp-tongued and half-dressed, demanding bread. Duvall's men would walk their rounds along the frozen quay, breath steaming, eyes working. Somewhere in the valley, the men whose signatures filled these pages were being toasted as saviors.

She rewrapped the ledger in its oilcloth, lowered it into the false bottom, turned the brass catch, and sat for a moment with her palms flat on the trunk lid, as if taking a pulse.

Then she took out her small stock of paper, sharpened her penknife against the stone from the riverbank, and began, in her mother's open-topped fours and trailing sevens, to copy the first deciphered page from memory — not the cipher, but the meaning, translated into plain French, compressed, exact. If the original was taken tonight, tomorrow, any night, the numbers would still exist somewhere. In her head, at least, they were honest.

She was three lines into the second entry when she heard footsteps on the stair outside her door — unhurried, formal, entirely wrong for that hour of the morning — and a soft, precise knock, and a voice she recognized from the notary's office, dry as registry paper:

"Mademoiselle Marchand. Forgive the hour. Maître Corbineau wonders if you might spare him a few moments — on a matter of records."
