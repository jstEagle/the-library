## 8. The Notary's Request

The notary's office smelled of ink, cold stone, and tallow — the smell, Elodie would think later, of a town that had decided to remember itself on paper. She had come on the pretext of registering her presence as the law required of travelers wintering more than a fortnight: name, trade, purpose, duration. A wool merchant's clerk named Mademoiselle Léonie Fargeau, of Grenoble, waiting out the season for her employer's return.

Maître Corbineau had received the fiction with the courtesy of a man who filed fictions daily.

"Sit, if you please." He indicated a chair before his desk, a great slab of walnut scarred by two centuries of elbows. Behind him the shelves rose to the ceiling in ranks — bound registers, boxes of parchments, drawers labeled in a small, severe hand. *Baptêmes. Ventes. Procès-verbaux.* The town of Val-Brume, reduced to its paper skeleton.

She sat. He did not begin writing at once, as clerks did, but studied her instead, and she felt the particular discomfort of being read by a man whose trade was reading.

"You crossed on the last day," he said. "Before the chains went up."

"The bridge was closing. I was told I'd have an hour."

"You had eleven minutes." He opened a register, dipped his pen. "I record the crossings myself. Eleven minutes, and the ferryman Vasse argued with the chain-master to let your sledge come through. Aurel is generous that way. It has cost him before."

"I'll thank him again."

"You thanked him. I saw." He wrote her name in his careful, upright script, each letter a small act of testimony. "Now. Your purpose in Val-Brume."

"Wintering. My employer's wool contracts are concluded until the thaw. There is nothing to do here but wait."

"Mm." He did not look up. "And the trunk you carried. Heavy for wool samples. I noticed the porters grumble."

Her pulse performed its small, disciplined acceleration, the one she had trained into herself the way other women trained their hands to needlework. *You are Léonie Fargeau. You are bored. You are mildly offended.* "Samples of the fine fleece, monsieur. Merchants weigh everything. My employer weighs twice."

"Of course." He set down the pen and folded his hands, and for the first time he looked at her directly — a long, level look, gray eyes in a face the color of the stone the town was built from, a face that seemed carved for the specific purpose of outlasting weather. "Mademoiselle Fargeau. I will tell you a thing about myself, and then I will make a request, and you may refuse it, because I am not the police and I have no authority over anyone, which is precisely why I am telling you instead of summoning you."

She waited.

"I keep the records of this town," he said. "All of them. Births, deaths, sales, debts, the flood of 'ninety-one, the hail of 'ninety-four, the price of salt in every winter since the old king's grandfather. I have kept them through three constitutions and two coups, and I will keep them through the next thing, whatever it is, because records are the only form of government I believe in." He tapped the shelf behind him with one knuckle, gently, the way one taps a horse one trusts. "In 'ninety-three, men came from the valley with a list. They required me to certify an inventory of seized property belonging to persons declared enemies of the republic. It is what notaries do. We certify. We make things true by writing them down. I certified."

The fire cracked in the little stove. Elodie kept her face still, her hands loose in her lap. *Léonie Fargeau is listening to an old man's grievances the way one listens to weather.*

"One of the names on that inventory was my brother's," Corbineau said. "Anselme. He kept the mill accounts. He was not an enemy of anything except, perhaps, his own bluntness. The inventory said he had fled. The inventory was false — he had been taken, and I have spent six years learning in what direction, and I have learned nothing, because the men who wrote the inventory also wrote everything that came after it. Paper eats its own tracks, mademoiselle. That is its great talent and its great sin."

He rose, went to a shelf, and took down a slim register bound in green. He did not open it. He held it against his chest like a breastplate.

"I am telling you this because you are the second courier to winter here since the bridge closed, and the first one carried a trunk that heavy as well."

The room went very quiet. Elodie heard her own breath, and made it slower.

"The first one," Corbineau continued, "was a young man calling himself a surveyor. He lodged at the inn, he registered in this book, and he waited three weeks for someone who never came, exactly as you have been waiting, if the innkeeper's wife is to be believed, and she is, because she believes everything and therefore reports everything accurately." He set the green register down on the desk between them, still closed. "He left in the spring. Or he intended to. He was taken on the road below the pass. I know because the gendarmes came back through and asked me to certify the seizure of his effects, and I did, because I certify things. It is what I do. It is what I am."

"You're telling me I'm in danger," Elodie said. It came out flat, unimpressed, and she was grateful to her voice for its discipline.

"I am telling you that you are in a town," Corbineau said. "That is the danger. A town sees. A town counts. I count." He opened the register at last, to a page already marked with a ribbon, and turned it toward her. It was the record of crossings, his own private ledger of who had come into Val-Brume and when and how heavy their luggage had been — eleven minutes, the Vasse sledge, one trunk, *lourde*. Beside it, in the same hand, the surveyor's entry from three years past. One trunk. *Lourde.*

"I am not accusing you of anything," he said. "I do not know what you carry and I do not want to know, because knowing is a weight and I am already carrying enough of this town. But I will make my request, as promised." He closed the book. "Whatever it is — the papers, the letters, the list, the confession, whatever shape it has taken in your trunk — do not make it speak here. Do not bring another fire into Val-Brume."

"Another fire."

"There was a fire in 'ninety-four. The chapel of the convent on the ridge burned with most of its archive, and the men who burned it lodged at the inn and registered in this book as timber inspectors." His mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "I certified the loss. I wrote down that the papers were destroyed, because that is what the report said, and my certification made it true, and somewhere in the valley a family will never learn what became of their mother because I made a lie into a fact with my signature. So you understand, mademoiselle, that I am not a righteous man making demands of a stranger. I am a compromised man making a plea. It is worth less. It is what I have."

* * *

She walked back through the town with her collar up and her mind running like water finding cracks.

*He knows. Or he knows enough. The trunk, the waiting, the surveyor — he has assembled the shape of her without ever seeing inside it, the way a man can know a letter's contents from the weight and the seal.* She should have been afraid. She was, a little — a notary who counted crossings was a notary who could be compelled to count them aloud for the right visitor. Duvall had not yet come to Val-Brume, or had not yet shown himself, but the republic's paper reached everywhere, and a man who certified things could be made to certify her.

And yet beneath the fear there was something else, and it took her the length of the rue des Tanneurs to name it: he had not asked her to surrender anything. He had not asked what she carried. He had asked her to carry it somewhere else, and in the asking he had told her what he was — a man who had signed a lie and had spent six years pressing his thumb against the bruise of it.

*Complicity is not always a closed door,* she thought. *Sometimes it is a door left ajar, with a man standing in it, ashamed.*

The cooperage was warm when she came in, barrels in ranks like patient livestock, the smell of oak shavings and char. Sabine Vasse was in the yard splitting staves with an economy of motion that made the axe look like an extension of her arm, and she glanced up as Elodie passed, that quick, weighing glance she gave everyone — measuring, cataloguing, eleven minutes and one heavy trunk — and Elodie felt the town's eyes on her everywhere now, the whole of Val-Brume a single slow instrument taking her measure.

Aurel Vasse was mending net by the kitchen fire that evening, and she sat with them and ate the barley soup and said nothing of the notary, and the nothing she said sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Because here was the thing she could not say, even to herself, except that she said it to herself now, walking the corridors of her own skull in the dark after the house slept:

She had known for two days what the ledger was. She had not read it — the cipher was dense, layered, the work of a professional — but she knew its weight, its care, its terrible neatness. And she had told herself the courier's catechism: *carry, don't read; deliver, don't decide.* The catechism had kept her alive through three searches and one interrogation at a checkpoint where a lieutenant had smiled at her for four hours. The catechism was her mother's first lesson, in fact — her mother, dead six years, purged with a thousand others, the woman who had taught her that a courier's virtue was a closed mouth and a straight back.

*Do not make it speak here,* the notary had said.

But it had been written to speak. That was the horror she was only now admitting, the thought that had been forming in her like frost on glass since the bridge closed behind her: whatever was in that trunk had been composed by someone who wanted it to survive, wanted it to arrive, wanted it — someday, somewhere — to be read aloud in a room with authority in it. A courier who destroyed it was a censor. A courier who buried it was a grave-digger. And a courier who delivered it blindly, into hands she could no longer verify, might be handing a weapon to the very men it indicted.

The dead-drop letter had ordered her to wait for the Notary. The Notary had not come. The letter's seal was republican; the winter was deepening; and somewhere on the other side of the pass, the men who had sent her might already be revising their arithmetic — a courier, a cipher, a ledger, one variable too many.

*They sent you to be the lock that never opens,* she thought, and the thought had teeth.

* * *

Three days later, the notary's clerk — a stooped boy of fifty named Gaspard — brought a note to the cooperage, addressed to Mademoiselle Fargeau in that severe upright hand. It contained no accusation and no plea. It contained a page number.

*Register of the parish of Val-Brume, volume nine, folio 112,* it read. *Anselme Corbineau, miller's accounts, certified seized 14 March 1793. The inventory lists one strongbox of papers. The strongbox was entered in the seizure wagon at dawn. No strongbox was entered in the registry at Grenoble, where such seizures were logged, folio 41. The papers vanished between the mill and the valley. I have checked eleven times in six years. — M.C.*

She read it twice by the light of her candle, sitting on the edge of her bed with the trunk at her feet, and understood that the old man was not merely confessing. He was showing her his work. Six years of checking, eleven times, folio against folio, the mill against the valley — he had been running his own investigation inside his own office, the only rebellion available to a man whose rebellion was paper.

And he was telling her, in the language they apparently now shared — the language of records, of gaps, of the thing that vanished between one folio and the next — that he knew what she was, and that instead of denouncing her he had chosen to hand her a thread.

Her hands were not quite steady as she folded the note. She told herself it was the cold.

*He asked you not to bring a fire,* she thought. *And then he handed you a lamp. What do you imagine he wants, old man? Do you even know? Does he?*

She thought of her mother teaching her ciphers at the kitchen table, before the purges, before the word *enemy* became a profession: *A code is only a promise, Elodie. It says: someone meant this to be understood, by the right person, at the right time. Break it and you break the promise. Keep it and you keep the secret. The whole art is knowing which promise you are keeping.*

She had thought the lesson was about cipher-work. She was beginning to understand it had been about everything.

That night she did not open the trunk. She sat with her hands flat on its lid, feeling through the wood and the false bottom and the oilcloth the dense, patient weight of the thing she carried, and she made the courier's prayer she no longer quite believed: *let me deliver it, let it be enough, let me be a key and not a judge.*

The prayer lasted as long as the candle.

Because under her palms, through the wood, she could almost feel the pages wanting to be read — and worse, she could almost feel her mother's voice in them, though that was impossible, that was grief talking, that was the dead speaking through coincidence and candlelight. Her mother had died in the spring of '93, one name among hundreds. The ledger's earliest entries were dated from that same winter.

It meant nothing. Dates meant nothing. Grief drew patterns the way frost drew ferns — beautiful, and made of nothing.

She blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the cooperage settle, the barrels ticking as the cold tightened them, and she thought about fires. The chapel on the ridge. The mill accounts that vanished between folios. The young surveyor taken on the road below the pass, his heavy trunk seized and certified and gone. Everyone who carried the truth into the mountains, it seemed, fed it to the mountains.

*Not this time,* she thought, and did not know if it was resolve or only the stubbornness of the tired. *Not this one.*

In the morning there was frost ferns on the window and Sabine in the yard, chipping ice from the water barrel, and the girl looked up and said, without preamble, "You walk like you're being followed even when you're not. If you ever need to go somewhere without being followed, I know the ways the river doesn't watch." And she went back to her chipping as if she had said nothing at all, leaving Elodie standing in the cold with her heart knocking, wondering how much the ferryman's daughter had counted, and what she would do about it — because the notary had asked for silence, and the town had begun, in its small voices, to offer her everything else.
