## 9. Aurel's Ferry, Burned

The fire came in the last hour of a Tuesday night, when the town was at its deepest sleep and even the dogs had given up their arguments with the cold.

Elodie woke not to sound but to light — an orange bloom against the shutters of her small room above the cooperage, wrong for that hour, wrong for that season, when the only honest light after dark came from candles and was yellow and tame. She lay still for three heartbeats, the way her mother had taught her: *first you count what you hear, then you decide what you are.* No boots on the stair. No voices in the yard. Only the wind working at the eaves and, beneath it, a low rushing roar like water running under ice.

But it wasn't water.

She dressed without lighting the lamp — she had learned to dress in the dark years ago, in rooms where a struck match could cost a life — and pulled the ledger's false bottom open with two fingers, weighing whether to take it. She took it. She would always take it; that was the truth of what she had become, and some nights, standing at windows like this one, she understood it was also a kind of sickness. She slid it inside her coat against her ribs, where it sat like a second heart, and went down the back stair on the balls of her feet.

The cooperage yard was full of light.

Aurel's ferry burned at its mooring below the town, down on the shingle where the river ran slow and black before the ice took it fully. The boat was old — older than Aurel, he liked to say, older than his father, older than the republic and possibly than the monarchy it ate — tarred oak and river-bleached planking, forty feet of patient wood that had carried everything Val-Brume had ever needed to move from one bank to the other: hay, salt, coffins, brides, soldiers in three different uniforms, all of them equally dead by now. It burned the way old boats burn, completely, greedily, the tar catching first so that the whole hull lit at once like a wick, and the flames stood off the deck in a sheet ten feet high, and the heat of it reached across the water and touched Elodie's face even from the yard above.

Aurel Vasse stood in the middle of the yard in his shirt, barefoot on the frozen mud, watching it. He did not shout. He did not run toward it. He stood the way a man stands at a graveside he has been expecting for years, and that frightened Elodie more than the fire did.

Sabine reached him first. She had come out wrapped in a blanket with her hair loose and unbraided, looking younger than her seventeen years and older than Elodie at once, and she put her hand on her father's arm and said nothing, because there was nothing in any language to say, and both of them knew it.

"The chains," Aurel said finally. His voice was flat as slate. "Someone cut the mooring chain first. Cut it clean. Then they fired her."

Elodie looked at the water and saw it: the ferry had drifted a dozen yards downstream from its berth and hung up on a shoal, burning there, so that the light of it fell on the whole lower town and no part of the upper. Whoever had done it had wanted it seen — but seen from a distance.

"They didn't want the boat," Sabine said. Her breath smoked. "They wanted everyone awake and looking at the river."

*Or,* Elodie thought, *they wanted one person to know why.* And the knowledge went through her like a fishhook going through a finger — small entry, deep hold.

Because she knew about the courier before her. Not much. A shape of a thing, gathered from half-heard words since she'd arrived: that in the autumn, before the bridge closed, another traveler had come through Val-Brume heading north, a quiet man who paid in good coin and asked questions about the winter crossings, and that men had come asking after him a week later, polite men with papers, and that the man had gone over the pass into the high country and no one in Val-Brume had ever learned what became of him. The mountain had swallowed him, people said. The mountain swallows everyone eventually.

But mountains do not cut mooring chains with files. Mountains do not carry pitch and lamp oil down to a shingle in the dark. Men do those things, and men who do them send messages, and this message was written in flame across the whole face of the river:

*We are still here. We are still hunting. Show us what we want and we will stop breaking things.*

* * *

By dawn the whole town had stood in the street and looked at the blackened hulk wedged on the shoal, smoking like a dying animal. By mid-morning they had begun to talk, and by noon they had begun to argue, which in Val-Brume was nearly the same thing.

Elodie heard it in fragments through the day, because she was a wool merchant's clerk named Madame Lefèvre and clerks run errands, and running errands meant ears in every doorway. The miller said it was smugglers, settling scores. The baker's wife said it was the tax men, though there were no tax men anymore, or there were too many, depending on who was speaking. Old Madame Roux, who remembered the Austrian occupation of her girlhood, said darkly that when a war turns, it always comes back to burn somebody's boat, and she'd seen it twice and expected to see it again.

And one voice — the saddler's apprentice, a boy of nineteen with a soldier's coat bought secondhand — said, too loudly, near the well, that maybe the fire was meant for the woman lodging at the cooperage. Maybe the stranger brought it with her. And the well went quiet around him in that particular way a crowd goes quiet, and the smith's wife, a broad woman with flour on her forearms, said, "Maybe it was meant for your tongue, Julien, since it seems to be the only thing in this town that burns anything," and the laughter that followed was thin and hard, and the boy went red and left.

But Elodie had heard the question under the joke. The town had heard it too. And the town answered it, in its way, by doing nothing at all — by not sending anyone to the cooperage to look at her sideways, by leaving a basket of late apples on the step, by the smith touching his hat to her in passing as if she had lived among them twenty years.

They knew she was a stranger. They knew strangers brought trouble the way carts brought dust. And they chose, deliberately, in the space of one morning, to stand between her and the trouble anyway.

She could not decide, walking home with her errands done, whether it was the most beautiful or the most terrible thing she had ever seen.

* * *

Aurel spent the afternoon at the shingle with a hatchet and a grappling line, trying to think what could be salvaged. The answer, by evening, was: the iron fittings, the anchor, and a quantity of char that might be sold for pigments. He worked without speaking, hauling scorched planks out of the shallows, and Elodie went down to help because standing in a warm kitchen while a man salvaged the wreck of his livelihood felt like a kind of theft.

"You needn't," he said, when she took the other end of a plank.

"Neither need you."

He almost smiled. Under the soot and the gray stubble, it moved his whole face like weather. "No. But she's mine to bury." He looked at the hulk. "Forty-one years I've worked her. My father thirty before me. There were winters she was the only road in the world, that boat. Whole valley fed because of her." He set his boot against the plank and levered it free of the mud with a crack. "And now some men come in the night with a file and a lantern and end all that in a quarter hour. That's what I keep thinking. Forty-one years. A quarter hour."

"Did you see them?"

"No." He said it too quickly, and then, hearing himself, more slowly: "I sleep heavy. Always have. Sabine says a cannonball couldn't wake me." He would not look at her while he said it, and Elodie understood that Aurel Vasse had seen something, or heard something, or suspected something, and had decided — with the same patience he gave to rivers — to keep it under the ice.

"I'm sorry," she said. The words felt small as buttons.

"Aye." He handed her the plank. "Carry that up, then. Sorry doesn't haul."

* * *

That night the family sat late around the stove in the cooperage kitchen, and Madame Vasse — Aurel's sister, a widow herself, sharp-faced and tireless, who kept the household books and the lodgers' accounts with a quill like a weapon — set out bread and dripping and a pot of barley soup stretched with turnips, and said grace over it as if the fire had been a death, which in a way it was.

"We'll manage," Aurel said, to the table generally. "There's the winter trade on the ice road yet, sledges cross at the narrows. We'll take a share of the toll. Come spring—" He stopped. Everyone heard the sentence he didn't finish: *come spring there will be a ferry again, somehow, and somehow means money we don't have.*

"There's the insurance money," Madame Vasse began, and stopped too, because they all knew there was no such thing as insurance money in Val-Brume, and never had been.

Sabine pushed her bread around her plate. She had been silent all evening, which was unlike her, and Elodie watched the girl's eyes go around the table — father, aunt, the lodger, the stove, the dark window — counting, always counting. Then, abruptly: "It was Duvall's men."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well.

"You don't know that," Aurel said.

"I know the man who bought lamp oil at Fournier's on Monday was wearing riding boots with a heel iron, left foot dragging, and I know the same boots stood in our yard yesterday morning asking after the winter traffic, and I know the smell of the tar they used because I've helped you tar the ferry every autumn of my life and Fournier's sells a different grade." She said it evenly, without triumph, the way she recited the ice paths. "Left heel drags. I watched the boots walk away from the fire along the towpath, and the left foot dragged."

Nobody spoke. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind worried the cooperage signs.

"Sabine." Aurel's voice was very quiet. "You followed them?"

"I followed the smoke. From the ridge. Two men, one waiting with horses past the mill." She lifted her chin. "You taught me to read water. Smoke is just water upside down."

"It's not the same thing—"

"It is exactly the same thing. You watch where it moves and you learn what's underneath."

"And what happens," Madame Vasse said, in the voice she used for arithmetic, "the day they notice a girl reading their smoke? What does that cost us? What does it cost *her*?" And her chin jerked, barely, toward Elodie, and the room's silence rearranged itself around the gesture.

Elodie set down her spoon. Whatever she decided in the next minute would be a decision she would live inside for a long time; she understood that the way you understand weather coming.

"Madame Vasse," she said, "you've been generous to me, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. So I'll say plainly what I can say: whoever fired that ferry was not hunting me for my wool accounts." A breath. "I have enemies. I've had them longer than I've had this address. If staying puts your family in their road, I'll leave tomorrow, and I mean that, and I'll find some way—"

"Sit down," Aurel said.

She sat.

"My wife," he said, "took lodgers for eleven years. Soldiers, priests, a theater company, a tax collector once, God forgive us. She had a rule: whoever eats at this table is under this roof, and whatever they're fleeing stays outside the door until spring thaws it or it goes away. She died four years ago believing that rule held the house up better than the beams did." He turned his cup slowly on the table, once, twice. "Men came in the night and burned the family boat to send a message to someone in this house. Fine. Message received. Here's mine back: nobody leaves this house in winter. Nobody gets handed to anybody. Not for money, not for safety, not for being asked politely with papers." His eyes came up, brown and level as the river in August. "Not even if the someone in question tells us nothing about herself, which I notice she hasn't."

"That's not prudence," Madame Vasse said. "That's pride."

"That's the house rule," Aurel said. "My wife's. I don't get to break it just because the price came due."

Sabine was looking at Elodie with an expression she couldn't read — not warmth exactly, something harder and more durable, the look of a person watching a debt get written down in a book that would be collected later. And perhaps that was fair. Perhaps that was exactly right.

"Thank you," Elodie said, and found her voice rougher than she intended. "I'll not forget it."

"Don't thank me," Aurel said. "Thank her." He nodded at the empty chair at the head of the table, the chair no one had moved in four years. "And mind what it costs. Everything costs. That's the other lesson of this house."

* * *

She climbed to her room after, carrying the candle low, and stood a long time at the window looking down at the black hulk on the shoal, a whale skeleton of char in the moonlight. Against her ribs, the ledger pressed its familiar weight, and for the first time it felt less like cargo than like contagion — a thing that burned whatever stood near it. A courier had come through Val-Brume in the autumn carrying whatever he carried, and the mountain had gotten him, and now the boat that might have carried others to safety was ash, and the family that sheltered her was poorer by forty-one years of work, and the saddler's boy was already asking the right questions in the wrong tone.

*I should go,* she thought. *Walk out on the ice road tonight, north or south, anywhere, take the net with me and let this town keep its own troubles.*

Her hand was already on the trunk, on the false bottom, checking the ledger — the reflex of eleven years, the body's own loyalty, deeper than argument. And beneath her palm the boards of the floor carried up, faintly, the sounds of the house: Aurel moving heavy things below, making ready for the salvage work at dawn; Madame Vasse's quill scratching, entering losses in a column; Sabine, somewhere, awake too — Elodie could feel it, the way one feels a lit window across a dark valley.

*Nobody leaves this house in winter.*

She took her hand off the trunk.

Very well, then. If they would spend themselves on her, then the spending would purchase something. She would not simply survive in this house; she would make her survival worth its price. Somewhere in the cipher pages there was a name attached to the file-cut chains and the lamp oil — Duvall's seal was in the ledger's margins, she had seen it, she had felt it like frost through a glove — and somewhere in this town there were men with dragging heels who answered to that seal. The courier before her had died alone in the high country because no one had stood for him. She would not die alone, and she would not be the reason these people burned, and between those two vows there was work enough to fill every night until thaw.

Below her window, on the black river, the wreck ticked and settled as the cold got into it, cooling toward the temperature of everything around it.

Three days later, a sledge came up the ice road from the south with the winter mail, and the postmaster's boy ran it straight to the cooperage because the name on the parcel was hers: a plain paper-wrapped package, no sender, containing a single folded sheet and nothing else. The handwriting on the fold was elegant, unhurried, faintly foreign.

*Madame Lefèvre,* it began. *You are harder to kill than advertised. Let us discuss terms over dinner. — D.*
