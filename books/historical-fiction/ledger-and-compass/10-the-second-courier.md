## 10. The Second Courier

The dog started it — the Vasse household's mongrel, Brutus, lifting his head from the hearthstones with a low sound Elodie had never heard him make. Not a bark. Something older than barking.

She was in the kitchen helping Sabine render tallow when it happened, her hands black to the wrist, and she felt the change in the room before she understood it: Aurel setting down his pipe without a word, Marguerite-less silence falling over even little Céleste by the fire.

"Someone's on the ice," Aurel said.

Elodie's first thought was Duvall. Her second was that no one sent by Duvall would come across the ice at dusk in this cold; he had horses, men, the road along the bank. Whoever crossed the river tonight came alone, or desperate, or both.

They went out together into the blue-black evening, breath pluming, boots crunching on the packed snow of the yard. The river lay below like hammered tin, and on it, a figure. It moved the way no healthy person moves — lurching, pausing, one arm clutched across the body, falling and rising again with a stubbornness that hurt to watch. Halfway between the far shore and the ferry landing, it fell and did not rise so quickly.

"He'll not make it," Aurel said, already moving.

"Father—" Sabine caught his sleeve. "It could be a trap. They've used wounded men before, in the war stories. Bait."

Aurel looked back at her, and Elodie saw the whole argument pass behind his eyes: prudence, pity, and something third and heavier. "Then they chose a man who bleeds real blood," he said, "because I can smell it from here."

He went down onto the ice with the rope coiled over his shoulder, walking the way he walked his ferry route in summer — flat-footed, certain, reading the surface. Elodie followed before she had decided to, and hated herself for the hesitation she could not quite name. Every instinct trained into her said *strangers are instruments; instruments have operators.* But the figure on the ice had cried out when he fell — a raw, animal sound — and instruments did not cry out like that. Men did.

He was younger than she expected. Twenty-five perhaps, maybe less, frost in the stubble of his beard, his left side dark where the snow had soaked through his coat. His right hand was locked around something at his chest, fingers frozen half-curling over it, and when Aurel knelt and tried to loosen them the man's eyes opened — fever-bright, terrified — and he said, in French flattened by some southern accent:

"Not them. Anyone but them. You're not them?"

"No," Aurel said. "We're nobody. Who sent you?"

The man laughed, which cost him visibly, a wet, rattling sound. "Nobody sent me. That's rather the point." His gaze slid past Aurel and found Elodie standing in the torchlight spilling down from the yard, and something in his face changed — recognition, relief, and beneath both, an apology so profound it frightened her more than the wound had.

"Mademoiselle Sandoval," he said. "Thank God. Thank God it's you."

* * *

Sabine watched through the kitchen window while the men carried him up, and she counted what she saw the way her father had taught her to count everything: two carriers, one stranger, weight distributed wrong because the stranger kept twisting to protect whatever he held against his chest. She counted the blood on the snow — too much, spread over thirty paces of ice, meaning he had been bleeding a long time, meaning the wound was dressed and had failed, meaning he'd dressed it himself, meaning he'd been alone on the river for hours at least.

She counted, too, the look on Elodie's face.

Elodie had been in their house five weeks now, and Sabine had made a private study of her, the way she studied everything her father pretended not to notice. Elodie the wool clerk smiled exactly enough and never more. Elodie at prayer — though Sabine doubted she prayed — had a stillness that wasn't stillness at all but listening. And tonight, hearing a dying stranger say her true name on their doorstep, Elodie Sandoval had gone white in a way no performance could fake, and then, within three breaths, had arranged her face again — but too late. Sabine had seen underneath.

"You know him," Sabine said, finding her in the passage outside the sickroom, where the doctor's boy was boiling water and everyone else had been shooed away. "That man. Don't lie to me about it, please. Everyone lies in this house lately. Father lies about the smuggling routes. You lie about the wool. I'd like one honest thing before Christmas."

Elodie looked at her for a long moment. Candlelight moved on her face. "His name is Tomas," she said finally. "I don't know more than that. I've never met him." A pause, and then, quieter: "But he knew me. And he knew my mother's codes. He said—" She stopped.

"Said what?"

"Nothing. Later." And Elodie went into the sickroom and closed the door, which was its own kind of answer, and Sabine stood in the passage doing sums she didn't like: a stranger who knew secret names, crossing a frozen river half-dead, arriving five days after the man Duvall with the fine coat and the questions. Things were converging on Val-Brume the way weather converged before a storm off the mountains, and everyone older than her seemed determined to pretend it was only clouds.

She went down to help Céleste feed the animals instead of standing there, because there was nothing a fourteen-year-old could do about storms except keep the goats alive through them.

* * *

Tomas lasted through the night, which surprised the doctor's boy, who had given him until midnight, and surprised no one more than Tomas himself.

Elodie sat with him through the small hours while the fire burned down and the house creaked around them. She had cleaned the wound herself — a saber cut, long and deep along the ribs, not a hunting accident, not a fall on the ice — and packed it with the goose grease and yarrow Marguerite Vasse's recipes still called for, and all the while the man watched her with those fever-bright eyes and spoke in fragments, saving his strength, rationing his words like a man rationing bread in a siege.

"The Notary," he said, an hour past midnight. "You waited for him."

"Yes."

"He isn't coming. There is no Notary anymore. They took the network apart in October. Lyon, Grenoble—" a breath, wet and terrible, "—I'm what's left on this line. Me and the ledger."

Her hands went still in the basin of water. "What ledger?"

He almost smiled. "Don't insult us both."

Outside, the wind found the gap under the eaves and sang its thin winter note. Elodie wrung out the cloth and laid it across his brow, and thought: *they sent me into a town with orders to wait for a man who was already dead or taken, and I waited, and every day I waited was a day someone wanted me here, stationary, predictable.* The thought arrived fully formed, cold as the window glass, and she set it aside because the dying man was speaking again.

"I carried a key," he said. "Duplicate. In case anything happened to the original courier. In case the cipher died with her."

"With her," Elodie repeated carefully.

"In case the cipher died with *you,*" Tomas amended, and closed his eyes as if the correction had cost him everything he had left. "There's a habit of yours. When you copy figures. Your mother did the same. The sevens with the crossbar slanted left. I've handled forty pages of her hand. I'd know it anywhere."

The room tilted. Elodie gripped the edge of the bed frame and made herself stay upright, made her face stay composed, because the doctor's boy was dozing in the corner and grief was a luxury she had been trained out of — and yet her mother's name rose in her throat like floodwater, and for one unguarded moment the whole careful architecture of Elodie the wool clerk collapsed, and she was eleven years old again learning substitution ciphers at a kitchen table, and the woman teaching her had been dead for six years, executed, officially, in the purges, and here was a stranger on the ice saying *her hand* as if he'd held pages of it recently enough to know the slant of her sevens.

"Tomas." She leaned close. "Who wrote the ledger? Tell me."

But his breathing had changed — shorter pulls, each one shallower — and when he opened his eyes they had gone past fever into something clearer and farther away. He fumbled at his chest, at the oilskin packet strapped there beneath his shirt, and Elodie helped him work it free, and pressed it into her hands with the last of his grip.

"For the judge," he whispered. "When you find the judge. Mother told me—"

He stopped. Not because he died — not yet — but because his face flooded with confusion, the words snagging somewhere behind his eyes.

"Mother told me," he tried again, and then, softly, wonderingly, as if the sentence had opened onto a country he hadn't known he was traveling to: "Why did I say that?" A pause. "Whose mother?"

And Elodie, holding the packet, feeling its shape through the oilskin — flat, rectangular, book-sized — said nothing, because she could not yet speak.

He died an hour before dawn, quietly, mid-breath, between one rattle and the next, and the doctor's boy woke and did what boys do in such rooms, and Elodie stood at the window watching the sky go grey over the ice and did not weep, because she had been taught not to, and hated the lesson with a completeness that frightened her.

*For the judge,* he had said. Someone besides herself, then, had known there was a judge worth finding. The net she had sensed closing around Val-Brume had other strands than Duvall's. Somewhere, someone had built a plan with redundancy in it — a second courier, a duplicate key, a fallback — the way careful people build things, the way her mother built things.

*In case the cipher died with you.*

Which meant her own handlers had planned for her death the way one plans for a lost key. Which meant—

She stopped the thought. Opened the packet by candlelight instead. Inside: a folded paper, and wrapped around it a strip of linen embroidered, nearly invisibly, with tiny letters in thread. She recognized the stitch. Her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

The paper was a key — the same cipher she carried, in a variant, with substitutions noted in a cramped, beautiful hand. She knew the hand. She had spent twenty-three years knowing it.

The embroidery read: *If you have this, trust the registers.*

* * *

Sabine buried the stranger two days later, because the ground was iron and the men needed four of them and a crowbar, and because someone in the family should witness it properly, and her father chose her — "you've the stomach for it, girl, and your brother hasn't" — which she took as the compliment it was meant to be.

They put him in the churchyard at the edge of the row where strangers go, under a stone Corbineau himself came out to cut, gratis, which told Sabine the notary knew more than he said too. Everyone in this town was a locked drawer. The stone said only: TOMAS. RESTING FROM THE ROAD. Her father had insisted on no dates. "Dates invite questions," he said, and Sabine, chiseling nothing, understanding everything, had agreed.

Afterward, standing at the grave with the wind cutting across the yard, she found Elodie beside her. The wool clerk's mask was gone — had been gone since the night on the ice, mostly, though it flickered back sometimes like a bad lamp — and what remained was someone harder and sadder and, Sabine thought, more interesting.

"He called me mademoiselle Sandoval," Elodie said to the stone. "My true name. First word out of him."

"That's not the part keeping you awake."

"No." Elodie's breath smoked. "The part keeping me awake is what he said after. He said, *for the judge.* As if he knew—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and Sabine watched her decide, visibly, how much truth this friendship could carry. "There are people who planned for my death, Sabine. Politely. On paper. And there is at least one person who planned against it. And I don't know which side of my own war any of them stand on."

Sabine considered this. Below them the river lay white and silent, holding its secrets under the ice until spring, the way the town held everything.

"My father says the river gives back what it takes," she offered, "if you know where to look. Under the ice there's channels. Currents. Things move all winter that look like they've stopped." She glanced sideways. "You've got something under your floorboard. Third stave from the hearth, false bottom, I've known since week two. I haven't looked, and I won't. But if it needs to move someday — across the ice, through the channels, where nobody watches —" she shrugged, fifteen years old and offering, "— I know where to look. Is all."

Elodie turned and looked at her fully, and Sabine saw the exact moment the woman decided to stop lying to her: a settling in the shoulders, like a load being shifted to stronger legs.

"There's a convent up the valley," Elodie said. "Suppressed years ago. Sealed. Tomorrow, when the light holds — walk with me past it. Just past. I want to see whose windows face the river."

Behind them, at the churchyard gate, a rider in a good coat had stopped his horse and was watching them with the pleasant, patient attention of a man counting sheep. Duvall raised his hat to the mourners. Sabine raised hers back, sweetly, and kept her father's oldest rule in the front of her mind where it belonged: *on the ice, never show them where the current runs.*
