## 11. Interrogation by Courtesy

The invitation arrived on the third of December, carried by a boy in a coat too fine for Val-Brume, and it was written in a hand so elegant it looked embarrassed to be in the mountains at all.

*Citizen Vasse and household are requested at supper, Thursday next, at the house taken by Citizen Duvall. Seven o'clock. Dress as suits you.*

Elodie read it twice by the light of the cooperage's back window, where the afternoon came down thin and gray through the ice-fog on the glass. *Household.* The word was doing work. It meant her — the wool merchant's clerk, Mademoiselle Léonie Favier, papers in order, references invented, three weeks lodged above the cooperage with a trunk that had a false bottom and a secret that could hang a town.

"He means to look at us," Sabine said over her shoulder. She had come up the stairs without a sound, as she always did, and now stood with her arms crossed, reading upside down with the ease of someone who'd been reading other people's letters since she was nine. "All of us. In one room. Under lamps."

"Then we'll be worth looking at," Elodie said.

Sabine snorted. "Papa will wear his funeral coat. He'll say it's his good coat, because to Papa they are the same coat." She took the invitation, turned it over, held it near the candle as if flame might make ink confess something. "He's not a soldier, this Duvall. Soldiers send soldiers. Men who send invitations want you comfortable. Comfortable people talk."

Elodie thought: *I know.* She thought: *I have been comfortable in eleven houses in four years, and I have talked my way out of ten of them, and I cannot tell you which house I liked best because none of them were mine.*

What she said was, "We should bring something. Wine. A courtesy."

"A courtesy," Sabine repeated, tasting the word like spoiled milk. Then she grinned, quick and crooked, and Elodie felt the familiar treacherous warmth — the one she had promised herself she would not feel for anyone in Val-Brume. "There's a cask of the black cherry behind the vinegar. Papa pretends it doesn't exist so he doesn't have to share it. We'll share it with a man we hate. That's very republican of us."

* * *

Thursday came down cold and windless, the kind of stillness that made the whole valley feel held, like a breath before a question. Aurel Vasse wore his funeral coat, which was also his good coat, and had oiled his hair flat in a manner that made him look less like a ferryman than like a drowned man respectfully dried. He said nothing on the walk up from the river road; he simply offered Elodie his arm at the frozen ruts, an old courtesy performed without looking at her, and she understood that he knew exactly what kind of evening this was and had decided, in his slow deliberate way, that whatever happened inside that house would happen to all three of them together or not at all.

Duvall had taken the former customs house, the largest dwelling in Val-Brume, and he had changed almost nothing about it except the light. Where the old customs officers had worked by day in gloom, he had installed candles — dozens of them, beeswax, not tallow — until every surface shone and every shadow had been pushed into corners where it waited politely to be summoned. A fire burned too hot. Two soldiers stood at the door with muskets they did not appear to need, and a hired cook from the valley moved through the side room with the resigned competence of a man who had fed worse men than these.

"Citizen Vasse." Duvall came forward with both hands extended, a big man gone soft at the edges but soft the way a snowdrift is soft — there was weight under it. His voice was warm, unhurried, faintly amused, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it because everyone around him raised things for him. "And the celebrated Sabine. And — forgive me — Mademoiselle Favier. The wool clerk. We have not been properly introduced, though I feel I know you already. Your handwriting is much admired at the counting-house."

"My handwriting is adequate," Elodie said, and gave him the smile she kept for customs men: modest, slightly dull, a smile that promised no surprises. "My figures are better."

"I believe it." He drew her glove off her hand himself — a liberty so smooth it was over before it was a liberty — and pressed her fingers once, briefly, like a man testing fruit. "Adequate hands do not survive a winter here. Clever ones do. Come. Sit. Everything tonight is adequate and nothing is clever, and we shall all be the better for it."

The table seated eight. Besides the four of them there sat the customs clerk Grellet, a damp nervous man who sweated even in the overheated room; Madame Roux, the innkeeper's widow, magnificent in black velvet and suspicion; and a young officer of Duvall's escort, Lieutenant Marchat, whose face was still soft with the conviction that the war was a story being told correctly. Elodie noted the seating the way she noted everything: Duvall at the head, herself at his right hand, Sabine directly across from him, Aurel far down at the corner nearest the door — put there, she suspected, so that the ferryman could watch the door, or the door could watch the ferryman.

The soup was chestnut, thick and good. The wine was the black cherry, and when Sabine saw it poured she did not blink, only lifted her glass toward Duvall with a small ironic tilt that said *you are drinking our secrets and don't know it*, and Elodie loved her so fiercely and so dangerously that she had to look down at her spoon.

"So," Duvall said, when the plates were cleared. "Traffic. I have taken an interest in traffic." He said it the way other men said *weather*. "The bridge chains go up, the river freezes, and suddenly Val-Brume believes itself closed to the world. It is not closed. It is merely — selective. Things still cross the ice. Letters. Ledgers." He let the word sit among them like a dropped coin. "Persons."

"Persons cross to buy flour," said Madame Roux, "and to bury their mothers, and to get away from their husbands. Mostly the last."

Laughter ran down the table, obedient laughter, and Duvall smiled and let it run its course. "Mostly," he agreed. "But I am paid to attend to the remainder. And the remainder interests me, citizens, because the remainder has been busy. Since the first frost, this town — this quiet, pious, bookkeeping town — has produced more sealed correspondence than it has in the previous five years. Someone is writing. Someone is carrying. Someone, I think, is sitting at my table eating my chestnut soup."

He looked, as he said it, at no one. Which was how Elodie knew he was looking at everyone, and cataloguing.

"You flatter us," Corbineau said.

Oh — yes. The notary was there too. She had marked him late, which frightened her; Maître Corbineau sat at Duvall's left hand, dry and precise as ever, his napkin folded with archival exactitude, and he had the air of a man attending a burial he had personally certified. "You flatter us, citizen. Val-Brume produces correspondence the way it produces cheese: locally, and mostly about prices."

"And yet." Duvall turned his glass by its stem, watching the candlelight swim in it. "And yet in October a courier passed through heading south, and in November another heading north, and neither appears in your registers, Maître. No letters posted. No manifests. No tolls. As though they walked through your meticulous town like ghosts."

"Perhaps they were ghosts," said Sabine.

Everyone looked at her. She was seventeen and sat with her spine straight and her chin level, and she met the agent's gaze the way she met the frozen river: as a surface to be read, not obeyed.

"Ghosts," Duvall repeated, delighted. "Explain."

"The dead cross the ice all winter, citizen. Coffins going to the churchyard at Saint-Rémy, because our ground is too hard to dig till thaw. Nobody registers a coffin. It would be indecent." She sipped her wine. "Maybe your couriers were dead men walking. It happens, in a republic this age."

Marchat laughed — a real laugh, quickly strangled. Duvall's eyes stayed on Sabine a moment longer, warm and terrible, and then moved on, and Elodie understood that he had just added the girl to some list, and that there was nothing she could do about it, and that the girl knew it too and had decided the price was acceptable. *Do not be brave,* Elodie wanted to say. *Bravery is a currency and he is rich.* But she said nothing, because she was already composing her own lies, and composition required the whole of her.

* * *

The meat was venison, shot somewhere it probably ought not to have been shot, and it was during the venison that Duvall began the real work, which he conducted the way a careful man fishes: no splashing, long patience, the line always moving.

He asked Aurel about the ferry — the burned ferry — with gentle sympathy. A tragedy. Arson, surely. Old grudges? Smuggling debts? Aurel said the river took what the river took, in a voice like a closing door, and Duvall nodded and wrote nothing and remembered everything.

He asked Madame Roux which of her lodgers paid in new coin and which in old, and whether any of them had southern accents, and she answered with such a torrent of irrelevant detail — the price of oats, the immorality of the salt tax, her late husband's stomach — that he was forced to laugh and retreat, and Elodie filed away a sliver of respect: the widow had done this before, or lived through enough versions of it.

He asked Grellet about the counting-house, and Grellet, sweating, mentioned — God help him — the wool accounts. "Mademoiselle Favier's figures are the cleanest we've seen. Not a sou unaccounted. Her master must trust her absolutely."

"Her master," Duvall said pleasantly. "Tell me about her master."

And there it was. The line, moving past her bait, coming for the hook.

"Monsieur Berthelot, of Grenoble," Elodie said, before Grellet could fumble further. "Wool and dyestuffs. He does not travel in winter — his lungs. He trusts me with the ledgers because I am cheap and because I do not drink, and he trusts me with the roads because I am not pretty enough to be robbed comfortably." She smiled her dull smile. "You may verify all of it in spring, citizen, when the pass opens. He answers letters within the month. He complains about the postage."

"Berthelot." Duvall tasted the name. "Grenoble. And before Grenoble?"

"Lyons. Before Lyons, nowhere worth mentioning. My father was a cooper." A true lie, the best kind — her grandfather had been one, and the trade sat in her mouth naturally. "I keep figures because figures do not ask questions."

"No," Duvall agreed softly. "They don't. People ask questions. For instance—" and he turned to her fully, and the warmth in his face did not change by a degree, which was how she knew the temperature underneath had dropped, "—for instance, I wonder why a woman of your evident talents winters in a town like this at all. Adequate clerks winter in Lyon. Clever clerks winter in Paris. Only necessary clerks winter in Val-Brume."

"Necessary clerks winter wherever the wool is," Elodie said. "The fleeces came down from the high valleys in November. Someone must grade them, weigh them, argue with the growers, and see them carted at thaw. My master says a clerk who fears cold is a clerk who fears arithmetic." She refolded her napkin, a small domestic motion, a motion belonging to no one dangerous. "You may check the weighing books at the counting-house, citizen. I sign every page. I have nothing in me but wool."

It was, she thought later, a masterpiece. It was fluent, textured, verifiable in half its details because she had spent three weeks actually doing the work, and the other half was built on the bones of a real grandfather and a real skill. Duvall studied her for a long moment — long enough that the candles seemed to lean in — and then he laughed, warmly, and raised his glass to her.

"To wool," he said. "May it keep us all honest."

They drank. And Elodie, setting down her glass, felt the thing she always felt after a performance like that, the thing she had never said aloud to anyone: a small clean click, like a lock turning, somewhere behind her own ribs. As though a room in her had shut and she had heard it shut and could not now remember what she kept in there.

She was good at this. That was the horror of it. Not the danger — danger she had chosen, danger had a shape and a price. The horror was the ease. Four years of invented fathers and invented routes and invented selves, and the inventions came now faster than the truth, arrived smoother, fit better. Somewhere in her was the girl her mother had taught the cipher at a kitchen table, laughing, flour on her hands — and that girl had to be dug for, these days, like water in frozen ground. What came to the surface instead was Léonie Favier, adequate, dull, signed on every page, a woman made entirely of signatures.

*Dinner ends,* she told herself. *Survive the sweet. Survive the coffee. Count the exits.*

* * *

The exits were two: the front door, guarded, and the kitchen passage, unguarded but narrow. She had mapped both before the soup. Now, over coffee — bitter, burnt, excellent — Duvall rose and strolled the length of the table with his cup, and the room rearranged itself around him without his asking, the way rooms did.

"One more matter," he said, "and then I release you to your beds. A formality." He stopped behind Elodie's chair, and she felt his presence the way one feels weather through a wall. "In the spring, when the bridge opens, traffic resumes. All traffic. And certain — materials — currently mislaid will find their way to their proper destinations. When that day comes, I expect the good citizens of Val-Brume to assist. To remember. To produce." He set a hand, lightly, on the back of her chair. "Whatever is carried across my ice, mademoiselle, will carry my seal. Is that understood?"

"Perfectly," said Corbineau, dry as dust.

"Wonderfully," said Madame Roux.

"It's understood," Elodie said, and smiled up at him, and hated him, and hated more fluently than she had ever hated anything the fact that her smile did not waver at all.

Later — boots on, shawls gathered, the cold waiting outside like a debt — Duvall shook each hand at the door. When he took hers, he held it a half-second past courtesy, and said, quietly, beneath the farewells of the others:

"Your figures are very clean, mademoiselle. Almost too clean. In my experience, only two kinds of ledger balance perfectly." His eyes were friendly. His voice was a knife wrapped in wool. "Honest ones, and ones that have been rewritten."

"Then I hope yours is honest, citizen," Elodie said, "since mine is only wool."

Outside, the cold hit like a slap, and the stars were brutal and clear, and the three of them walked down toward the river road without speaking until the customs house lights were well behind them. Then Aurel said, to the night in general, "He knows something. Not everything. Something."

"He knows there's a courier," Elodie said. "He doesn't know it's me. Not yet."

"Not yet," Sabine repeated, and there was no fear in it, only arithmetic, and Elodie thought: *she learned that tone from me, God help us both.*

At the cooperage door, Aurel paused, and put one broad hand briefly on Elodie's shoulder — the first time he had touched her since the ferry burned. "You lied well tonight," he said. "Too well. Eat something tomorrow. Sleep. A person who forgets what her own voice sounds like can't hear when it matters."

He went in. Elodie stood in the dark a moment longer, listening to the river muttering under its ice, and made herself name the truth, because naming was the last discipline she trusted: she had enjoyed it. Parts of it. The fencing, the fluency, the perfect balance of the false ledger of herself. And a woman who enjoys lying is a woman halfway to being her own forgery.

Inside, in the dark of her room, she knelt at the trunk and ran her thumb along the false bottom's edge, feeling the paper beneath — her mother's paper, her mother's hand, the one true thing left.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would decipher the next pages. Tonight she simply lay in the dark and listened, and it was near dawn when she heard it: footsteps on the frozen lane, stopping below her window — two sets, unhurried, patient — and the scratch of chalk, once, against the cooperage wall.
