## 13. The Convent Archive

The convent of Sainte-Ambroise stood above the town like a ship run aground, its bell tower leaning a degree or two off true, as though it had grown tired of standing watch and could not quite be persuaded to care anymore. Its windows had been boarded by order of the Directory three years past — *suppressed*, the decree said, as if the building itself were a person who had committed an offense — and snow had drifted against its doors in pale dunes that no one had bothered to break.

Elodie had walked past it perhaps forty times since her arrival in Val-Brume. She had catalogued it the way she catalogued everything now: exits, sightlines, who passed, how often. It had never once occurred to her to look inside.

It was Sabine who looked up.

"Your mother's marks," she said. They were standing at the cooperage door, breath steaming, and Sabine had taken to carrying a copy of one deciphered page folded inside her mitten — a habit Elodie had forbidden twice and which continued regardless. "The little crosses she draws in the margins. Aurel says they're surveyor's marks. He's seen them on old river charts."

"They're cipher markers." Elodie said it automatically, then stopped. She took the page from Sabine's mittened hand and held it to the grey light. Three small crosses, inked beside a line about requisitions of grain in the autumn of '93, each cross followed by a number. She had read them as key-values, part of the cipher's arithmetic. But her mother had taught her that a good cipher hid nothing that wasn't already hiding something else.

Crosses. Stations. *Places.*

"Sabine," she said slowly, "how many suppressed buildings does this town have?"

* * *

They went on the third morning after, when Duvall's men had settled into their routine of knocking on doors before noon — a routine, Elodie noted, that left the upper town unwatched between first light and the angelus-that-no-longer-rang. They told Aurel they were going to buy tallow. It was even half true; Sabine carried a basket with a lump of it on top, camouflaging whatever else might go in or come out.

The climb was steep and silent. Val-Brume under snow makes its own discipline; even the dogs seemed to understand that sound carried too far across the frozen air and traveled with it. By the time they reached the convent wall, Elodie's lungs burned and her thoughts had gone clean and narrow, the way they did before a crossing or a dead-drop. She hated how familiar the feeling was. She hated more that some traitor corner of her loved it.

"There." Sabine pointed to a postern in the wall, half-buried, its door hanging by one hinge. "The sisters used it for the laundry path. Nobody's touched it since the expulsion."

"You know this how?"

"Aurel ferried two of them out, the night they were turned off. Me and Papa rowed them downriver in the dark while they prayed and I bailed." Sabine's jaw set in the particular way it did when she spoke of her father's better self. "Sister Béatrice gave me her psalter. I can't read most of the Latin but I like the pictures."

Inside the wall, the convent grounds lay under snow so undisturbed it looked poured rather than fallen. The cloister walk ran along one side, its arcade choked with drifts. Everything was grey and white and patient. Elodie felt the strangest sensation, walking there — that they were trespassing not on property but on time, on a life that had been interrupted mid-sentence and left standing.

"The records," she said. "When they suppress a religious house, what happens to the papers?"

"Carted off, mostly. To the district capital, for inventory." Sabine led them along the arcade, sure-footed. "But Maître Corbineau complained last year at the council meeting that half the Sainte-Ambroise archive never arrived. He wanted to file a protest about it. The mayor told him to stop making trouble."

Of course he did. Elodie filed the fact away next to all the others she was accumulating about Corbineau — the man was a magpie of grievance and record both, and either might prove useful.

"Then where is it?"

"If I were a nun who didn't want soldiers reading my letters," Sabine said, "I wouldn't send my letters anywhere."

* * *

The chapel was the largest room, and the coldest; their breath hung in it like incense. The altar had been stripped, the statues toppled and stacked in a corner like cordwood, faces turned to the wall as if ashamed to be seen dismantled. But it was the floor that caught Elodie's eye — flagstones, ordinary, except that a ring of them near the rood screen were darker than the rest, worn in a pattern no congregation would make.

Her mother's marginal note came back to her whole, in that handwriting she now knew as well as her own pulse: *the crosses mark where the counting was kept safe from the counters.*

Counting. Notaries counted. Convents kept accounts — dowries, rents, alms, bread. A convent was a ledger with a roof.

"Help me," she said, and knelt.

They worked with the trowel Sabine had brought and their bare hands, prying at the darkened stones until the third one shifted with a grinding sigh and lifted free. Beneath it: a cavity lined with oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth, wrapped tight against damp, bundles of paper.

Elodie drew the first bundle out and unwrapped it with fingers that refused, quite stubbornly, to be steady.

Letters. Correspondence, dozens of pieces, tied in packets with faded ribbon and labeled in a fine, disciplined hand — the same hand, she realized within four seconds, that annotated the ledger. Her mother's hand. Labels like *Requêtes des familles, 1793–94.* *Registre des passages.* *Ce qui fut pris — témoins.*

"What is it?" Sabine whispered, though she could see.

"It's an archive." Elodie's voice came out strange, low, reverent, as if they were in church after all. "A real one. Hidden under their altar stone because—" She stopped, because the shape of it was assembling itself in her mind with terrible clarity. "Because someone knew the republic would come for the papers, and so the sisters buried the truth under God's floor and let the soldiers carry off the empty boxes."

She cut a ribbon with her knife — carefully, wanting to preserve even that — and opened the first packet.

* * *

For two hours, kneeling on frozen flagstones with the cold coming up through her skirts like a rising tide, Elodie read.

The letters were petitions, chiefly — families writing to the convent during the Terror, begging intercession, reporting arrests, describing seizures. A widow named Gontard listing what had been taken from her house down to the copper pots, and by whom, and on whose written authority. A man from the mill village naming the officer who had ordered his brother shot without trial, and the date, and the place in the road where the body was left. Page after page of exactly the kind of testimony that never survived, because the people who collected it were the people it condemned.

And in the margins — her mother. Small annotations, dates cross-referenced, names underlined, occasional initials Elodie now recognized as the cipher's author-marks. Her mother had been here. Had sat in this cold chapel, or in some warmer room before the suppression, collecting these letters, sorting them, indexing them, weaving them into the ledger Elodie carried against her ribs. Not dead in a purge. *Working.* Building a case the way other women built trousseaus, stitch by stitch, over years.

"She was the archivist," Elodie said aloud. It did not feel like a discovery; it felt like remembering. "Or she became one. Sister—someone. She must have been sheltering here under a religious name after she fled Paris." She looked at Sabine, and her eyes were hot. "Sabine, she planned this. She didn't just write an indictment. She built it to be found. The crosses aren't just cipher keys — they're a map. For whoever carried the ledger. For *me.*"

"Then why hide it so deep?" Sabine asked practically, turning a letter toward the light from the boarded window. "If it's meant to be found."

"So it isn't found by the wrong hands first." Elodie heard her own voice harden. "You bury treasure from thieves, not from heirs."

There was a noise then — soft, at the edge of hearing — and both of them froze, Elodie's hand going flat over the open letters as if she could shield them with her palm alone. A rat, probably. Or ice shifting in the eaves. But she stayed motionless a long moment, listening, aware suddenly of how far the nearest help was, of how a girl and a woman kneeling in a ruined chapel would look to any loyalist eye.

Nothing came. The silence resettled. Sabine exhaled through her nose, deliberately loud, and Elodie understood it for what it was: permission to keep going.

They repacked everything exactly as found — ribbon re-tied, oilcloth refolded, stone lowered back into its bed and dusted with snow-dirt until the seam vanished. But Elodie kept out three letters, copying them into her pocket-book in a compressed hand while Sabine stood watch at the broken window, and one item besides: a single loose sheet, not a petition but a covering note, unsigned, in her mother's hand.

*To the one who finds this,* it read. *If you are reading this, the ledger reached you and you are still alive, which means you are cleverer than they hoped. Do not trust the drop. Do not trust the men who sent you. Trust the registers — every town keeps its own honest books, if anyone will read them. And trust whoever shares your risk. That is the only oath that cannot be forged.*

Elodie read it three times. On the third reading she had to press the heel of her hand against her eyes for a moment, and Sabine, watching, said nothing at all, which was its own kindness.

*Trust whoever shares your risk.* Across the chapel, the ferryman's daughter stood silhouetted against the boarded light, fifteen years old and armed with a tallow basket and a psalter she couldn't fully read, guarding a dead woman's secrets as though they were live ammunition.

"Come on," Elodie said finally, voice rough. "Before the town wakes properly."

* * *

They came down off the hill as the sun broke through — the first clear hour in days, the whole valley lit up sudden and merciless, every chimney smoke standing straight as a drawn thread. Somewhere below, a door slammed; Duvall's men beginning their rounds.

At the turn by the tannery, Sabine caught her sleeve. "The letters we copied. The widow Gontard, and the mill man. You recognized something. I saw your face."

Elodie hesitated. Then, because the pact they'd been circling deserved at least one plain sentence: "The officer the mill man named. Who shot his brother on the road without trial." She watched the words land. "Duvall. It was Duvall. He signed it himself — the letter has his description, his rank, the scar he doesn't bother hiding. He's been doing this work for ten years and getting promoted for it."

Sabine was quiet for the length of a heartbeat. Two. Then she nodded, once, and pulled her cap lower against the wind.

"Then we'd best finish copying everything," she said, "before he burns it. Because he'll burn it. Men like him always do."

They parted at the cooperage door, and Elodie went up to her room above the barrels and sat with the pocket-book open on her knee, the copied letters drying, her mother's instruction folded inside like a second heartbeat.

*Do not trust the drop. Do not trust the men who sent you.*

She thought of the dead-drop letter in her trunk, ordering her to wait for the Notary who never came. She thought of Corbineau — the town's obsessive keeper of registers, whose brother had vanished in a purge, whose protests about missing archives the mayor had silenced. A man who counted everything. A man whose honest books, her mother wrote, waited only for someone willing to read them.

That evening, she would knock on the notary's door.
