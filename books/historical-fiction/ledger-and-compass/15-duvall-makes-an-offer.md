## 15. Duvall Makes an Offer

He sent the invitation by a boy in a red knitted cap, which was itself a kind of message: I know where you lodge, and I can reach you whenever I please.

*Agent Duvall requests the pleasure of Madame Lefèvre's company at the Hôtel de la Poste, tomorrow at four o'clock. He regrets that business prevents him from calling on her.*

The card was printed, not written, but beneath it someone had added a single line in ink gone brown with age: *Regarding the wool contract you were unable to complete before the crossing closed.*

Elodie read it three times by the light of the cooperage window, while below her Sabine's mallet rang against staves and Aurel sang something tuneless about herring. The line was innocuous to any eye but hers. It was also, precisely and deliberately, the phrase she had used to Duvall at the dinner — *my wool contract died with the bridge* — repeated back to her word for word, as a man might hold up a mirror to show a woman the exact angle of her own lie.

She burned the card in the stove and sat for a long while watching the paper curl, asking herself the question she should have asked weeks ago: what did Duvall actually know?

Not enough to arrest her. If he had proof, there would have been no invitation, no courtesy, no boy in a red cap — only men at the door in the night, the way they had come for the last courier, whose name Elodie had never learned and whose fate she could guess. No. What Duvall had was suspicion shaped like politeness, and patience, and a winter with nothing else to do. He was fishing. The trouble with being fished for, Elodie thought, was that eventually one grew curious about the bait.

She went down to tell Sabine where she would be, and found the girl already watching her with those quick grey eyes that missed nothing in a town where nothing happened.

"You're going to him," Sabine said. Not a question.

"He asked."

"He doesn't ask." Sabine set down her mallet. "My father says when a wolf sends invitations, you bring your own knife." She paused. "Or don't go at all."

"If I don't go, he comes here." Elodie touched the trunk at the foot of her bed — the false bottom, the weight sleeping inside it. "I'd rather choose the ground."

Sabine looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her turn away first. "Then wear your good boots," she said. "The ice on the north walk is treacherous, and men like that always find the crack in you eventually. Best not to give them weather to blame it on."

* * *

The Hôtel de la Poste was the finest building in Val-Brume and wore its fineness like a borrowed coat. Its salon had been furnished by some optimistic owner in the old king's time — gilt mirrors, a marble mantel, chairs with legs carved into dolphins — and now stood half-empty, the rest of the furniture requisitioned or sold or burned for warmth, so that Duvall sat alone in a vast room like a man who had eaten all the guests.

He rose when she entered. He poured the wine himself, holding the bottle to the light first.

"Madame Lefèvre. You honor me. In a town of two hundred souls, an afternoon caller is an event."

"Your note mentioned my wool contract." She took the chair he indicated — the one facing the window, she noted, so that the light fell on her face and his remained in shadow. A man who arranged shadows knew their value. "I'm afraid it's dead until thaw."

"All contracts die in winter. All contracts revive in spring." He settled across from her, long legs crossed, hands loose around his glass. "The question is always who is holding the pen when they do."

Outside the window, the frozen river lay white and silent under the late sun, a road of ice running south toward the valley, toward everything Elodie could not reach. She let herself look at it a moment too long. She knew she had done it even as she did it, and hated herself for it.

Duvall saw. Of course he saw.

"Beautiful, isn't it," he said softly. "For six weeks now I've watched people stand at that window and look at that river the way starving men look through a baker's glass. The ferryman's daughter looks at it because she has never been anywhere else. The priest looks at it because he wants to be somewhere holier than here. And you, madame—" he lifted his glass an inch, "—you look at it like a woman doing arithmetic."

"I look at it like a merchant whose goods are spoiling."

"Yes. Your goods." He smiled. It was a good smile; that was the worst thing about him. At the dinner she had catalogued it among his weapons: the smile that disarmed, the voice that soothed, the questions that arrived dressed as small talk and left carrying more than they brought. "Let us speak plainly, since we are both such honest merchants. You are not a wool clerk. I have known this since the second day. Your hands are wrong for wool — ink stains, calluses from a pen, not a bale-hook. Your accent is Paris learned from books, not streets. You flinched — very slightly, very politely — when I mentioned the name Vasse at dinner, though you had claimed to have met the family only days before, and no one flinches at a name unless the name owes them money or they owe the name something worse."

Elodie kept her face still. Inside, cold water was rising. *Since the second day.* Every careful step since the second day had been walked on a stage he had built.

"And yet," Duvall continued, "you are still free. Do you wonder at that? A man in my position, with my commissions, finding an unregistered traveler of obvious intelligence stranded in the one town between the mountains and the valley — do you wonder why I have not simply had her searched, questioned, detained?"

"The thought had crossed my mind," Elodie said, and was proud of how level it came out.

"Because detention produces nothing but silence. Silence is worthless. I am not interested in your silence, madame. I am interested in your cargo." He leaned forward, and for the first time the pleasant mask slipped just far enough for her to see the machinery behind it — the tired, patient, ruthless apparatus of a man who had spent years turning other people's fear into paperwork. "And I believe I can offer you a better price than whoever sent you."

"There is no cargo."

"Of course not." He waved this away like smoke. "But indulge a hypothetical. Suppose a woman carried something — papers, say — out of a country that is about to stop existing. Not metaphorically. Practically. You've seen the dispatches pass through here; you know what the armies are doing on both fronts. By summer there will be new flags on every customhouse from here to the coast, and the men giving orders today will be either ministers or fugitives, depending entirely on which side of which border they're standing when the music stops." His eyes never left hers. "Suppose that woman's employers promised her protection, passage, payment. Ask yourself: from whom? A republic that cannot protect its own generals? A committee that has already burned every courier it ever sent?"

The cold water rose higher. *Every courier it ever sent.* He said it lightly, a throwaway, but Elodie felt it land like a blade laid flat against the skin. Did he know? Could he possibly know? Or was it simply true, and spoken as truth, and dangerous precisely because it needed no proof?

"You paint a grim picture of my hypothetical employers," she managed.

"I paint an accurate one." He stood, crossed to the mantel, and picked up a folded paper lying there. "Here is mine. Passage out. Not over the ice, not through the passes — those close next week regardless, and half the fools who try them feed the crows. My route. Down the valley under seal, as my clerical assistant, with documents that will open every gate between here and Lyon. Safe lodging, wages in coin, and at the end of it — freedom. A new name if you want one. A life." He held the paper out without offering it quite far enough for her to take. "In exchange, the item. For safekeeping."

"For safekeeping." She let the words sit in her mouth like a stone. "Whose keeping?"

"Mine."

"And after the thaw? After the new flags? What happens to the items you keep safe, Monsieur Duvall? Because I've watched your men carry crates out of the convent annex, and I've smelled the mill chimneys, and I notice that the crates go in and nothing comes out."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Recalculation. She had shown him more than she meant to again; she seemed constitutionally incapable of spending a quarter hour with this man without bleeding information. But he absorbed it without changing expression, and when he spoke his voice was almost gentle.

"You mistake me for a vandal. I am an archivist of a different kind. Papers destroyed in panic are lost forever; papers entrusted to me survive every regime. That is the entire secret of my career, madame — I have served three governments and buried none of them. I keep what is useful, destroy only what is dangerous to my principals, and forget nothing that might someday matter." He tilted his head. "Which raises a question I have wanted to ask you since the dinner. You strike me as a woman who reads. Who taught you your letters? Your hand is remarkable — I noticed it on the manifest you signed. That looping descender on the g. Very distinctive. Very... southern."

The room tilted. Only slightly, only for a heartbeat — the gilt mirror swaying in its frame, the fire settling — but Elodie felt the floor of the world shift beneath her the way ice shifts before it goes. Her mother's hand. Her mother's loops, her mother's southern schooling, reproduced in Elodie's own writing the way a voice reproduces a parent's cadence without either of them choosing it. He had seen it. He had remembered it. He had waited three weeks to place it in front of her like a card turned face up.

"My mother," she said. "Dead these six years."

"My condolences." He said it with genuine smoothness, and then, softly, watching her the whole time: "Was she? Dead, I mean. People say that so often, and so often it's merely convenient. In my experience the dead are the most industrious correspondents in France. They write constantly. They simply use living hands."

Elodie stood. She did it slowly, deliberately, so that it would read as boredom rather than flight. "You've drunk my evening, Monsieur Duvall, and offered me a position as your clerk and a lecture on the habits of ghosts. I'll consider the position. Clerks are respectable work."

"Do." He bowed, unhurried, and did not move toward her or away. "Consider it seriously. The offer stands until the ice breaks. After that, madame, everyone's options become much simpler and much worse." As she reached the door, his voice followed her, light as dust: "Give my regards to the Vasses. And to Maître Corbineau — a fine man. Complicated. They usually are, the ones who certify things."

She walked out into the blue dusk with her spine straight and her heart going like a trapped bird, and she did not let it falter until she was three streets away, standing in the dark between the cooperage and the frozen well, breathing steam into the cold.

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She sat wrapped in her cloak at the window of her room above the cooperage, the ledger's false bottom an arm's length from her boot, and she conducted the argument Duvall had planted in her, honestly, because she had learned that lies told to oneself rotted fastest of all.

Take the offer.

It would be so easy. That was the obscene part — how easy. One trunk, one signature, one morning ride south under a loyalist seal while Val-Brume shrank behind her into snow. No more cipher by candlelight with her heart in her teeth. No more listening for boots on the stairs. No more Sabine looking at her with those grey eyes that deserved better than the danger Elodie had brought into her father's house. She could be in Lyon in nine days. She could be anyone. The century was dying anyway; regimes were dying; what was one ledger among so many dead things? Let the victors write what they liked. History was always written by whoever held the pens, and pens, unlike people, could be bought.

And then she thought of the pages she had deciphered by candlelight — the requisitions dated to the week of the purge, the sums paid, the signatures of liberators on orders that had emptied villages — and beneath them, in the margins, in that looping southern hand, the annotations. *Counted twice. Lied once.* Her mother's dry furious bookkeeping, each correction a wound. Her mother had known what obedience cost and had chosen to make the cost visible. Had she still been alive when she wrote it — alive, hiding, hunted, writing in the margins of stolen documents by whatever light she could find — or had she died with the work unfinished, leaving her daughter to find it the way one finds a letter under a floorboard years too late?

Either way, the hand had trusted the reader. *Whoever counts, remember:* the margin said on the third page. *Numbers are the only witnesses that cannot be tortured.*

If Elodie handed the ledger to Duvall for "safekeeping," it would burn. She knew it the way she knew the weight of her own head. Whatever else he was — and he was clever, patient, almost admirable in the way a well-built trap is admirable — he was the instrument of men who needed certain numbers never to be counted. The crates went into the mill and nothing came out. The couriers went onto the road and nothing came back. He had told her himself, wearing that good smile: he destroys what is dangerous to his principals.

And he had dangled the one bait she could not ignore: her mother. *The dead are the most industrious correspondents in France. They simply use living hands.*

He didn't know. She was almost sure he didn't know — he was fishing with a wide net, throwing the word *mother* into the water to see what rose. But almost sure was a currency Elodie had learned not to spend. If there was any chance — any — that Duvall knew what had become of Marguerite Sandoval, then the ledger was not the only thing at stake. There was a thread here, running backward through six years of grief, and Duvall had just shown her he was holding one end of it.

Which meant the easy exit and the hard truth had just become the same road, and neither of them ran south.

Below her, she heard the cooperage door open quietly, and footsteps she knew — Sabine, crossing the yard in the dark, moving fast and purposeful toward the north edge of town, where the mill chimneys smoked at hours when no lawful work was done. Elodie frowned. Eleven o'clock was no hour for a seventeen-year-old to be abroad, and Sabine Vasse had been restless for days, full of some knowledge she hadn't shared, watching the mill road with an expression Elodie recognized from her own mirror.

She should follow her. She should wake Aurel. She should do something other than sit here counting reasons.

But she stayed one moment longer, her breath fogging the black glass, and made herself finish the arithmetic. Duvall's offer expired when the ice broke. Everything expired when the ice broke. Which meant the winter itself was the deadline now — not her handlers' schedule, not the republic's collapse, not even her own courage, but plain water and warm wind, coming for all of them at the same indifferent speed.

Whatever Elodie was going to do with the truth in her trunk, she would have to do it soon.

Down in the yard, the last echo of Sabine's steps faded toward the smoking mill, and Elodie pulled on her boots.
