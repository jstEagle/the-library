## 16. Sabine Follows the Smoke

The smoke told her before the wind did.

Sabine Vasse had grown up reading the river's weather — the way ice sang at dusk when it meant to thicken, the gray-green bruise on the clouds that promised snow by morning. She knew every smell that could come off Val-Brume in a hard winter: woodsmoke from the hearths, tallow from the chandlery, the sour breath of the tannery pits frozen half-shut. This was none of those. This was paper burning — a dry, bitter sweetness like scorched hair and old glue — and there was only one direction it could be coming from: the old mill on the Grange road, where no one had ground flour since the army requisitioned its wheel two years ago.

She was carrying water up from the hole in the ice when the wind shifted and brought it to her, a thread of it, gone almost as soon as she caught it. She stood on the riverbank with the yoke cutting her shoulders and counted to ten, the way her father taught her to count when she wanted to shout — not to calm herself but to make sure the feeling was real and would last.

It lasted.

Three of Duvall's men had passed through the square an hour past, muffled to the eyes, hauling a sledge stacked with oilcloth-wrapped bundles. She'd watched them from the cooperage window while pretending to sort staves, and she'd noticed what anyone else might have missed because everyone else had learned not to look at Duvall's men: the bundles were the wrong shape for provisions and too heavy for tents, and one of them had slipped its wrapping just enough to show a corner of vellum, official cream-colored, with a wax seal still clinging to it like a barnacle.

Papers. A sledge of papers going up to a dead mill on the coldest afternoon of the winter.

Her father said the word *careful* so often it had worn smooth as a river stone in her ears. Elodie said nothing at all about such things, which was its own kind of instruction — the courier's silence, the look that said *you are safer not knowing*. But Sabine had spent seventeen years being told she was too young for the ferry, too young for the ledgers, too young to know why her mother had died coughing in a room above a cooperage, and she had discovered something about herself in this strangest of winters: being underestimated was a wound, but it was also a door. Nobody guarded a door they didn't believe you could reach.

She set down the water buckets inside the door, took up her hooded cloak, and told the empty room, in her mother's voice, "I'm going to see about the smoke."

* * *

The Grange road climbed out of town between walls of drifted snow taller than she was, packed by the plows into blue-white ramps. Sabine kept to the top of the drifts where her boots left the shallowest prints and the alder scrub broke her silhouette. The cold had teeth that evening. It found the gap at her wrist, the seam at her collar, the place where her breath froze on the wool over her mouth until she was breathing through a crust of her own frost. She didn't mind. Cold was honest. Cold told you exactly what it would do to you if you stayed. People — soldiers, agents, notaries, even well-meaning novel-heroines like Madame Elodie — never told you anything that plainly.

Halfway up she heard voices and dropped flat behind a drift, heart slamming, and listened to two of Duvall's men argue their way back down toward town.

"—said burn it all, I don't care if the colonel's grandmother wrote it—"

"Not here. Too much ash. He wants it at the lime kiln, mixed in. Nobody sifts lime."

"He wants a lot of things. He wants us to haul a sledge up a mountain twice. In February. For paper."

"For paper he'd hang us over if we read it. So don't read it."

Their laughter came out ragged in the cold, and then they were gone, boot-scrape fading under the wind, and Sabine lay in the snow letting her pulse settle and turning over what she'd heard. Not the mill after all. The lime kiln. There was a kiln above the mill, older than the mill, a squat stone throat in the hillside where the Grange farmers had once burned limestone for whitewash and mortar. It still drew a draft even in winter — she knew because she and Tomas—

She stopped that thought the way you stop a sled at the top of a slope, leaning back, refusing it momentum. Tomas was three weeks dead and buried under the chapel snow, and thinking of him on a dark hillside alone was a luxury she couldn't afford yet. Later. When the war of paper was over. She'd grieve him properly then, with time enough to do it.

But it was true that Tomas had shown her the kiln, back when he was still whole, still laughing at how seriously she took everything. "Every valley has its furnace," he'd said, tapping the map she'd drawn of the ice roads. "You should know yours. Couriers think in roads, but smugglers think in furnaces. Roads can be watched. Furnaces erase." She hadn't understood then why a man carrying a duplicate cipher key would teach a ferryman's daughter about erasure. Now, lying in a drift with her eyelashes freezing, she thought she was beginning to understand everything too late and all at once.

She went on.

* * *

The mill first, because the smoke had come from there and because she wanted to know what they'd already done.

It crouched against the hillside, black timber gone silver-gray, its wheel long since carried away so the race ran empty and the sluice gate hung open like a slack jaw. Snow had drifted through the broken doors and lay in the millrace channel in a long unbroken tongue. No tracks near the doors themselves — the men had used the loading side, where the old cart platform still stood.

Inside, moving carefully along the wall where the floorboards were soundest, Sabine found the room where the grain sacks used to be stored. It smelled of mouse and mildew and, underneath both, that same scorched-paper bitterness, stronger now, thick enough to taste.

They had made a fire in the middle of the floor, on a bed of stones dragged from outside, and burned a good part of whatever the sledge had carried. Ash lay ankle-deep in a gray fan, and in the ash — this was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that would sit behind her eyes when she tried to sleep — were the bones of documents. Not loose sheets, which burn fast and clean, but bound things: registers, ledgers, correspondence tied in ribbon whose charred ends still held their knots. Someone had fed whole volumes to the fire intact, covers and clasps and all, so that what remained were these black husks shaped exactly like books, hollow, fragile as cicada shells, some of them still legible along their scorched spines in gold or blind-stamped letters she couldn't make out in the failing light.

One husk had split open. Inside it she could see the ghost of lines — ruled columns, entries in a clerk's patient hand — and across one surviving corner, where the fire had licked but not consumed, a fragment of a heading in formal script: *Inventory of Goods Seized by Order of—* and then nothing, eaten.

Seized goods. Inventories. The republic's own bookkeeping, fed to a fire on a hillside above a town full of people who had lost fathers and brothers and farms to those inventories.

Sabine knelt at the edge of the ash and let herself understand what she was looking at. This wasn't carelessness. This wasn't a soldier warming himself and letting a stray page catch. Someone had carted records up here — records that belonged, by every law she'd ever heard Maître Corbineau mutter over, to the public registry, to the towns, to the courts — and destroyed them deliberately, in winter, in secret, forty paces at a time so the smoke wouldn't draw attention.

And the smoke had drawn her anyway.

She looked around the mill once more, memorizing: the fire-bed stones, the depth of ash, the split register husk, the tracks of four sets of boots (she counted again to be sure) coming in from the loading side and going out the same way. Then, because she was her father's daughter and her father believed that anything worth risking your neck for was worth proving, she did the most dangerous thing she did that night.

She took one page.

Not from the burned pile — those crumbled at a touch, collapsing into flakes. Near the wall, half-tucked under a fallen shelf board, lay a single sheet the men had evidently dropped in transit, damp enough that it had survived its neighbors' fate. She worked it free with numb fingers, folded it small, and pushed it deep into her mitten, against her palm, where her own heat could keep it from cracking.

She never read it there. That took discipline she surprised herself with. But she felt its weight the whole climb to the kiln, lighter than a coin and heavier than the water yoke, heavier than anything she'd ever carried.

* * *

The lime kiln sat in a fold of the hill a quarter mile beyond the mill, and Sabine reached it by leaving the road entirely, circling through the pine stand where the snow lay soft and silent under the boughs. She came at it from above, from the quarry lip, and lowered herself behind a granite outcrop that gave her a view straight down into the kiln's mouth.

Below her, lit by a lantern hung from a hook in the kiln arch, two men worked. One she recognized — Perrin, the big one with the scarred lip who stood guard outside the salt house. The other was smaller, quicker, a man she'd seen only in passing and had privately named the Weasel for the way his hood framed his face. Between them, on a hand-sledge, lay the remaining bundles from the mill run, oilcloth gleaming dully.

The kiln itself was a stone throat maybe eight feet deep, its base choked with old lime and rubble, its draft still alive — she could see it pulling the lantern flame sideways, could feel in her memory the faint warm breath it exhaled even in the worst cold, the mountain's own slow digestion.

"Colonel said the kiln, not the open fire," Perrin was saying. His voice bounced oddly off the stone, arriving at her outcrop flattened and strange, like a voice from underwater. "Open fires leave edges. Kiln leaves nothing but powder."

"The colonel," the Weasel said, untying an oilcloth bundle with brisk little motions, "says a great many things. Here — hold the light closer."

What came out of the bundle was paper. Reams of it, bundled in twelves and twenties, each bundle wrapped in a printed cover sheet. Even from above, even in the lantern's stingy circle, Sabine could see the covers bore stamps — the double-barred seal of the Commission for Public Accounts, the rounder seal of the Departmental Registry. Official paper. Records on their way somewhere legitimate, redirected here instead, to a stone throat that turned them into whitewash.

"They're not even copying them first," Perrin said, with the dull resentment of a man watching waste. "There's rules. Destruction of records needs a warrant, countersignature—"

"There's rules," the Weasel agreed pleasantly, "and there's the commission's discretionary authority, which supersedes them, and there's Colonel Duvall's signature on this requisition, which supersedes that. You want to write to Paris and check?"

Perrin didn't answer. The Weasel began feeding bundles into the kiln's mouth, six at a time, and struck flint to a twist of tow soaked in lamp oil.

Sabine pressed herself flat against the granite and made herself watch.

This was the cost of knowing, and she paid it in full, right there on the quarry lip with her cheek against frozen stone. Because the bundles weren't anonymous. As the Weasel tossed them in and the flames took hold, cover sheets curled and lifted and showed their faces to the light for a heartbeat before the fire closed over them — and she read them. Bundle after bundle, she read them, because she was quick and the light was close and someone had to.

*Register of Confiscations, District of Sermont, Year VI.*
*Correspondence, Tribunal of—*
*Petitions of Appeal, marked DENIED, 214 items.*
*Muster Rolls and Pay Ledgers, Provisional Legion.*
*List of Persons Detained, with Dispositions.*

Dispositions. She turned the word over. She was the ferryman's daughter; she knew what disposition meant for cargo. For people it meant something else, and the fire below her was eating the only list of what.

A gust moved the smoke and it rose straight at her, hot and bitter, and she shut her eyes against it and in the darkness behind her lids saw the ash-husks in the mill, hollow books, the shapes of records with nothing left inside, and she thought: this is what it looks like when a country decides its own history is contraband. Not armies. Not banners. Two tired men and a lime kiln and a signature, and by spring there would be no proof any of it had happened except in the heads of people like her father, who remembered, and people like Maître Corbineau, who wrote things down, and people like Elodie, who carried things — and how many of those could one colonel burn?

The fire roared. The Weasel worked steadily, untiring, a clerk at his devotions. Perrin smoked and complained about his feet. And Sabine lay in the snow above them and felt something settle in her chest, cold and clear and permanent, the way ice settles over running water: a lid over a current that does not stop.

She had come up the hill to satisfy curiosity. Curiosity was a girl's vice; everyone said so. What she carried back down would not be curiosity. It would be knowledge, and knowledge, she understood now, watching the last bundle go in, was not a thing you could give back. You couldn't unknot it from yourself. From tonight on, she was one of the places where the record lived, and everything that followed — every risk, every lie she'd have to tell, every moment someone like Perrin looked at her too long — would be the interest accruing on that debt.

Knowing was a price. She paid it and stayed to see the receipt.

* * *

They finished within the hour. The Weasel raked the kiln mouth, stirred the ash, and scattered quicklime from a sack over the embers — "so no scholar comes digging in the spring," he said, and laughed at his own joke, and Perrin laughed too, and the sound of it followed Sabine all the way down through the pines.

She gave them a long head start, then went back past the mill, keeping wide, and cut down to the Grange road where the plowed drifts could take her prints without complaint. Night had fully come. The stars were out in their terrible winter numbers, hard and white, and the town below glimmered with its few dozen hearth-lights, each one a family that believed the war was happening somewhere else.

In the cooperage, lamplight still showed above the stairs. Elodie would be awake — Elodie was always awake, bent over that trunk of hers with its false bottom, doing whatever silent arithmetic couriers did. Her father would be asleep or pretending to be, one ear open the way he always was since the ferry burned.

Sabine stamped the snow from her boots in the doorway longer than she needed to, giving her hands time to stop shaking, giving her face time to arrange itself into the expression of a girl who had been out checking her ice-path stakes, nothing more.

Then she climbed the stairs.

Elodie looked up from the table, candle-guttered low beside her, and Sabine watched the other girl read her in one glance the way she herself read the river — surface conditions, what was moving underneath, what was likely to break. She saw the exact moment Elodie saw it. The too-careful blankness. The question held back like a held breath.

"I walked my stakes," Sabine said. "The east path is holding. The ice by the willow pool is rotten — nobody should take it, not even with the poles."

"Good to know," Elodie said quietly.

"And Elodie." Sabine pulled the mitten from her right hand. The folded page came out with it, creased with the heat of her palm, one edge browned where it had been near fire before either of them touched it. She laid it on the table between them, in the ring of candlelight, and turned it so Elodie could read the heading — the ruled columns beneath it, the stamp in the corner, the list of names and numbers in a clerk's hand, seized and inventoried and now ash on a mountainside.

Elodie went very still, in the particular way she went still, total and silent, like the river the night before it locks.

"I followed the smoke," Sabine said. "Up past the mill. They're burning the records, Elodie. All of them. Registers, petitions, lists of detained persons — whole cartloads, at the lime kiln, so there'll be nothing left but powder. Duvall's orders. I heard them say his name." Her voice stayed level, which astonished her; inside she was ringing like a struck bell. "I know what I wasn't supposed to do. I know I've made myself worth something to them now, if they find out, and that means I've made myself a danger to everyone under this roof. I did it anyway. Because somebody has to be left who knows, and I was the one on the hill."

She stopped, out of breath she hadn't known she'd spent.

Elodie picked up the page. Read it once, twice. When she looked up, her eyes were wet and fierce at once, and she said, in a voice Sabine had never heard from her — lower than her courier's murmur, older, like something handed down —

"You took this from the fire?"

"From beside it. They dropped it."

"Then listen to me." Elodie laid the page flat and put her hand flat upon it, as if taking an oath on it, and perhaps she was. "From this minute, you and I have the same enemy and the same work. But hear the cost plainly, Sabine, because you chose it with your eyes open and I'll not insult you by pretending otherwise: what you know, they will kill to un-know. Not arrest. Erase. Like those bundles. Do you understand what you've become tonight?"

Sabine thought of the hollow book-shapes in the ash. Of Tomas, dead on the ice with a key in his coat. Of her father's ferry, burned for a courier who wasn't even her.

"Yes," she said. "I've become one of the copies."

For a moment Elodie said nothing at all. Then she reached across the table and gripped Sabine's wrist, hard, the grip of someone anchoring herself as much as offering comfort, and outside, far up the dark valley, the wind changed direction and brought with it, faint and bitter and unmistakable, the last thread of the smoke — the smell of a republic eating its own testimony, delivered all the way to their door like a warning.

By morning, Sabine would learn, the whole town would smell it too. And by noon, a stranger would be asking at the salt house who had been seen on the Grange road after dark.

She just didn't know yet that the stranger would be describing her boots.
