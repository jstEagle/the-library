## 17. The Burning at the Mill

Sabine came back to the cooperage with frost in her lashes and a look on her face that Elodie had learned, over these weeks, to read the way sailors read weather. It was not fear. It was worse than fear — it was certainty.

"They're moving them tonight," Sabine said, pulling off her mittens finger by finger, not looking at Elodie but through her, toward some middle distance only she could see. "Sledges from the garrison house to the old mill below the weir. I counted four loads before dark. Two men guarding each, and Duvall's clerk walking behind with a lantern like he was leading a funeral."

"Which mill?"

"The Grange mill. The one that burned once already and never got its roof mended." She flexed her fingers, wincing as feeling returned. "There's no work done there since autumn. Nobody grinds anything. So what are they grinding tonight?"

Elodie stood at the window, the shutter open two fingers' width, watching the blue dusk settle on the river. Below her, in the cooperage yard, Aurel was staving hoops onto a barrel with the patient rhythm of a man who had decided the world could be held together one stave at a time. The smell of oak shavings and woodsmoke rose through the floorboards. Somewhere in this building, hidden in the false bottom of a trunk and now distributed through a dozen barrel heads she had hollowed with a cooper's own auger while pretending to learn the trade, lay the ledger her mother had written with dying hands.

*They are going to burn it all,* she thought. *Everything like it. Everything that says what happened.*

"You shouldn't have followed them," Elodie said.

"You shouldn't have needed me to," Sabine said, and there was no cruelty in it, only truth laid flat on the table between them like a knife. "You'd have gone yourself and been seen, because you walk like a courier even when you're pretending to be a clerk. I walk like a girl carrying bread to her aunt. Nobody looks twice at bread."

Elodie almost smiled. Almost. "What time did the last sledge arrive?"

"Full dark. They posted a man at the bridge end of the mill lane too, which they didn't bother with before. That means tomorrow they burn it and tonight they make sure no one wanders close." Sabine finally looked at her. "You mean to go."

It was not a question, so Elodie did not answer it as one. She went instead to the trunk and lifted out the false bottom, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, took the single loose page she had carried since the night of Tomas's death — the page from his coat lining, half a cipher key written in a hand she now knew better than her own. Her mother's hand. She touched it once, the way one touches a relic, then put it back.

"If I'm not back by matins—"

"You'll be back by matins," Sabine said. "Because you'll take me as far as the weir path, and I know every stone on it, and I'll be your eyes when yours are full of smoke." She said it the way she said everything: as though the argument had already been won, elsewhere, by someone more reasonable than either of them, and they were merely carrying out the verdict.

Elodie wanted to refuse. She catalogued the reasons for refusing — the danger, the girl's age, the debt Elodie already owed this family that grew heavier by the week — and found, examining them honestly, that none of them were really about Sabine. They were about Elodie's wish to keep one clean thing untouched by the dirty business she carried. But nothing here was clean. Not the town, not the ledger, not herself. And Sabine had earned her place at the edge of this fire the moment she had followed those sledges alone.

"Warm clothes," Elodie said at last. "And if I say run, you run, and you do not come back for me. Swear it."

"I'll swear the first part," Sabine said.

* * *

The moon was three days off full and rose late, which suited them. They went out the cooperage's river door, past the stacked barrels breathing their sour wine smell into the cold, and down the frozen towpath with the ice groaning under the town like something turning in its sleep. Sabine moved ahead with the sure, low stride of an animal that knows its ground, and Elodie followed, the cold finding every seam in her clothing, her breath pluming and freezing on the wool of her collar.

The weir path ran along the north bank, above a channel where water still moved black and quick beneath lacework ice. Twice Sabine stopped and put out a hand without looking back, and twice Elodie froze mid-step, and both times, seconds later, a figure passed along the lane above them — a guard, stamping, lantern swinging — and moved on.

"He walks his post in eleven minutes," Sabine breathed against her ear. "My father times everything. He says a river teaches you that all order is just a rhythm, and every rhythm can be slipped between." A pause. "He also says never tell anyone that, because it's how smugglers think."

"Your father sounds like a wise man."

"My father sounds like a man who lost his ferry and pretends he doesn't lie awake counting what comes next." But there was love in it, roughened by the season. "Eleven minutes. The gap after he turns at the willow stump is long enough. Go fast, go low, and whatever you see in there—" Sabine caught her sleeve, and in the starlight her young face was suddenly very serious, older than its years by a decade of hard winters. "Whatever you see, don't let it stop your feet."

"What do you think I'll see?"

"I think," Sabine said slowly, "that men who burn papers at midnight in a roofless mill are men who've burned things before. Papers are just what they can carry."

She was right. Of course she was right.

* * *

The Grange mill crouched at the bend of the weir like a skull half-buried in snow — walls standing, roof open to the sky, the great wheel seized in its collar of ice. Through the empty window sockets light pulsed: firelight, deepening and fading, deepening and fading. And with the wind coming off the water toward them, the smell arrived before any sound. Burning paper has a particular smell, sweeter than woodsmoke, thinner, with something almost intimate in it, like the breath of a room where people have lived.

They came around the wall on the downstream side, where a collapsed loading dock made a ramp of broken timber, and Elodie pressed herself into the shadow of the doorway and looked in.

Four men. A bonfire built in the middle of the mill floor, fed by broken furniture and old barrel staves — Aurel would have wept at the waste — and beside it, stacked in neat piles like grain sacks awaiting threshing, the documents.

Not loose papers. Bundles. Each bundle bound with cord and sealed at the knot with wax, and each seal she recognized, because she had spent a winter learning seals: the republic's commission stamp, the intendant's office, the bureau of requisitions at Lyon. These were not scraps swept up by accident. This was an archive, curated and transported, brought across mountains in winter at enormous cost — brought here, to a dead mill, to die.

Duvall's clerk stood at the nearest pile with a ledger of his own, checking bundles off against a list. Two men fed the fire. A third stood at the doorway on the far side, musket grounded, bored. And near the fire, warming his hands with the unhurried comfort of a man doing honest work, stood Duvall himself.

Elodie felt the fury come up through her like water rising in a well shaft — slow, then sudden, then unstoppable. It was not the hot fury of the barricades; she had never had that kind. Hers was cold and precise and it sharpened everything, the way frost sharpens a windowpane until you can see every branch of every tree outside. She watched Duvall hold his palms to the flames and understood, with total clarity, that this man believed himself to be performing a service. That was the horror of him. He was not a butcher gloating. He was a functionary tidying. In his mind the past was a debt column and he was balancing it, and the ashes would be cleaner than the truth, and cleaner, he believed, was kinder.

A bundle went into the fire. Even from the doorway Elodie saw the corner of a name page catch, curl, flare — and for one instant a face of ink showed in the flame, a list of names, dozens of names, and then it was gone into white.

She was moving before she knew it.

Not into the mill — Sabine's hand was iron on her arm, and some buried discipline, the courier's oldest instinct, held her at the threshold — but along the outer wall, to where the collapsed dock had spilled its timber inside the wall line. Because the wind had shifted. She had felt it change against her cheek, swinging from the water to the land, and the smoke that had been rolling up through the open roof now eddied, curled back down, and began to pour along the mill floor toward the far doorway where the sentry stood.

The sentry coughed. Stepped out into the lane for air. The doorway stood empty.

"Eleven minutes," Elodie whispered. "His relief isn't due. He thinks the cold is his enemy, not me."

"Don't," Sabine whispered.

But the fire was eating names, and somewhere in those bundles — she could feel it the way one feels weather in a scar — there were pages that corroborated hers. Pages that named the same officers, the same dates, the same sums her mother had entered in cipher. Every bundle that burned was a witness dying, and she had spent her whole life delivering witnesses safely, and she would not stand in the dark and watch them murdered.

She went in low, along the wall, into the smoke.

* * *

Inside, the heat hit her like a hand. The fire roared in its bed of broken furniture, and the light leapt up the bare walls and out the empty windows so that the mill seemed to blaze from within, a lantern of ruin. The smoke hung at head height, thickening, and she bent beneath it, eyes streaming, and reached the nearest stack of bundles.

Her hands shook. She hated that. She made them stop.

The cord was waxed hemp, tight, and she had no knife — a courier learns sometimes to carry nothing — so she worried the knot with her fingernails, nails splitting, blood slicking the string, and the knot gave all at once and the bundle fell open in her arms.

Requisitions. Grain levies from the valley, 1794, with the amounts taken and the amounts actually recorded, and the difference between them written in a clerk's neat marginal hand: *where does the rest go?* A question, asked on paper, three years ago, by someone who had probably died asking it.

Behind her, the clerk's voice: "That's the last of the intendant's boxes. The prefect wants confirmation of destruction by seal and date."

"Then bring your book," Duvall said, "and we shall certify it beautifully. That is what certification is for."

The words went through Elodie colder than the river air. *Certification.* Corbineau's word. Corbineau's trade. Somewhere a notary's register, somewhere in some obedient town, an entry was being prepared that would say these documents had never existed or had existed blamelessly, and the paper record — the thing she had staked everything on, the thing her mother had bled into — could be unwritten by the same instrument that wrote it. Unless. Unless something survived outside their seals. Outside their fires. Something scorched, damaged, deniable to them and undeniable to everyone else.

The sentry was coughing again, closer. The two fire-feeders had stepped back from the smoke, wiping their eyes. The clerk was fumbling with his lantern. Thirty seconds, perhaps less, before someone glanced at the stacks and noticed one bundle missing.

Elodie looked at the pile — forty bundles, fifty, a thousand names — and understood she could not save them. That was the lesson of the night, arriving like a blade: archives are saved by institutions, and she was one woman, and the distance between those two facts was exactly the width of this fire. So she did what couriers do. She chose what could be carried.

She pulled the top bundle free — requisitions, the ones with the marginal questions — tore a handful of leaves from its heart, and thrust them inside her coat against her body, where the warmth would hide them from casual search. Then, as the smoke drove her back, her eye caught a leaf that had fallen loose by the fire itself, blown there by the shifting draft: a single page, already browned at one edge, its lower corner charred to a black lace of ash. She snatched it up — it bit her fingers with heat — and folded it, and folded it again, smothering the glowing edge against her palm until the pain flared white and the ember died.

Then she was at the wall, and through the gap in the fallen dock, and out into the cold, and Sabine's hand closed on her wrist and hauled her down behind the timbers as the sentry, drawn by the drifting smoke, wandered back through the far doorway shaking his head.

* * *

They did not speak until the weir path was behind them and the town's roofs rose black against the stars. Then, in the lee of the cooperage's woodpile, Elodie unfolded what she had saved, and Sabine held the shuttered lantern so its thin blade of light fell across the page.

It was an order.

Not a requisition, not a list — an order, signed, with the commission seal at its foot and a date in October of '94. *Upon receipt, destroy the records herein enumerated. No inventory to be kept. Destruction to be certified by the regional commissioner. This instruction itself is not to be filed.*

Read it twice, Elodie told herself. Read it as your mother taught you, the way you read a cipher — not what it says, but what it proves.

It proved everything. That the burnings were ordered from above, years ago, systematically, with the explicit design that no trace remain — including the trace of the order itself. The man who signed it was a name she knew. The whole nation knew it. He was in the schoolbooks now. Children recited his virtues. His statue had gone up in Lyon the summer before the war turned, on a pedestal paid for by public subscription.

"They aren't hiding crimes," Elodie heard herself say. Her voice came out strange, level, quiet, the voice of a woman reading a balance sheet. "They're hiding the orders to hide the crimes. It's turtles all the way down, each document authorizing the erasure of the one beneath it. Except this one didn't get filed. Someone disobeyed. Someone tucked this into a box instead of a fire, five years ago, betting that someday—" Her throat closed. *Betting that someday someone would come looking. The way my mother bet, writing in cipher, hiding pages in a ledger, sending them out with couriers who died carrying them.*

Tomas. Had this page passed through Tomas's hands? Had he carried it over the ice toward the same dead-drop she had waited at, toward the Notary who never came? Was this the cargo he died keeping?

"Your hand," Sabine said sharply. "You're burned."

Elodie looked down. Across her left palm, where she had crushed the burning corner, a welt was rising in the shape of the fold lines, a red map of the page itself printed into her skin. She almost laughed. Her body had taken an impression of the evidence, a fair copy in flesh.

"It's nothing."

"It's not nothing. Come inside. My father has goose fat and my grandmother's recipe for burns, and he'll ask questions, and you'll lie to him beautifully, and I'll hate watching it, but we'll do it, because that's what tonight costs." Sabine paused at the door, and in the spill of lantern light her face was fierce and young and absolutely certain. "But first say what it is. Out loud. I carried you to that mill and I waited in the dark listening to names burn, and I have the right to hear you say what we're carrying now."

Elodie looked at the scorched page in her good hand. The firelight of memory played over it: her mother's annotations in the ledger, the marginal cipher, the careful furious hand of a woman who had refused to be erased. *They will call it disorder,* her mother had written once, decoded by candlelight weeks ago. *Order is only what the winners finish writing. Keep the unfinished pages.*

"This," Elodie said, "is proof that the burning is not an accident of war. It is policy. It descends from the men we're taught to call liberators, and it was designed to leave no witness — and tonight it failed, because a girl who walks like she's carrying bread and a clerk who lies for a living stole one page back out of the fire." She met Sabine's eyes. "Tomorrow they'll sweep the ashes and find a bundle short and a page loose, and they'll count, because clerks always count, and then they'll start hunting the thief. Which means everything changes tonight. For both of us."

"Good," said Sabine Vasse.

And Elodie, holding a piece of the century's crimes warm against her burned skin, believed her.

* * *

They came into the cooperage quietly, but Aurel was awake — Aurel was always awake now, since the ferry burned — and he took one look at the smoke smell in their hair and the bandage already going onto Elodie's hand and asked nothing, which was its own kind of speech. He poured mulled wine, set the kettle for the fat and linen, and went back to his bench, where he began, softly, deliberately, in the dark, to hammer hoops onto a barrel that did not need them yet.

Later, when the house slept, Elodie sat by the banked stove with the scorched page flattened between two boards, and beside it she laid her mother's deciphered sheets, and compared, line by line, date against date, name against name. They matched. They matched like two halves of a broken seal.

One page. One witness pulled from a pyre that was still, at this hour, consuming the rest. The original indictment — her mother's ledger, hidden in the crypt beneath the convent — was safe for now, but Duvall was closing, and the copies she had begun were few, and scattered, and known to only two people in the world.

Two people. Elodie looked at the red map of the page printed on her palm and thought: *a republic of two, then.* If that was all the nation of the truth amounted to tonight, it would have to be enough — enough to swear on, enough to build from.

Outside, the wind swung back toward the river, carrying the last thin sweetness of burning paper off the water, and somewhere beyond the weir a dog began to bark at the ashes.
