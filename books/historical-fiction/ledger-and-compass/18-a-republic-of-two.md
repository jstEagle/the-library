## 18. A Republic of Two

The blizzard had spent itself by morning, leaving the town buried to its window sills and the world reduced to a single color that was not white but the pale grey of old paper. Elodie woke in her room above the cooperage with the candle long dead and her fingers stiff from cold, and for a moment she did not remember where she was — only that she had been dreaming of ink. Her mother's ink, the brownish black that faded toward violet at the edges of a stroke, drying slow on cheap paper because good paper was never what spies were given.

She lay still and let the dream finish dissolving. Then she got up, dressed in the dark, and took the ledger out of its hiding place behind the loose brick of the chimney breast, because she could not help herself; she had begun waking with it the way a sailor wakes reaching for railings that are not there.

Eleven pages deciphered now. Eleven pages of requisitions signed by heroes, of sums paid for rope and lime after certain nights in certain villages, of names struck through in her mother's small, level hand — not crossed out carelessly, the way a clerk cancels an error, but struck through deliberately, once, like a line drawn under a debt that would be collected later. *Later* had been her mother's whole life, apparently. Later had been the purge, the false report of her death, the years of silence while her daughter learned codes at another woman's knee and believed herself orphaned.

Elodie wrapped the ledger in oilcloth and put it back. Her hands were steady. That frightened her more than trembling would have.

Downstairs, the cooperage smelled of oak shavings and woodsmoke, and Sabine Vasse was already awake, which was no surprise; Sabine treated sleep as a creditor who could be kept waiting. She sat at the workbench with a slate across her knees, drawing the river.

"Come look," she said, without turning around. She always knew. It was unnerving, and Elodie had stopped pretending otherwise.

The map on the slate had grown since November. What had begun as a few confident lines — the channel below the mill, the shelf of blue ice along the eastern bank where the water ran slow and froze thick — had become a whole cartography of winter: the pressure ridges marked with little teeth, the springs under the ice marked with circles like open eyes, the place where the current bent around the Île aux Corbeaux and thinned the ice to something you crossed only if you had made your peace first.

"This is better than anything Corbineau has," Elodie said. "He keeps records of everything except the river."

"The river can't be deposed." Sabine tapped the slate with the chalk stub. "It just kills you and takes your body downstream to be someone else's news. Papa says the ice is the only honest witness in Val-Brume — it never lies about what it will bear."

"He sounds like a man who has carried people across anyway."

"He carries them every year until the chains go up, and then he lies awake listening for cracking." Sabine looked up. There was flour or ash or chalk on her cheekbone; with Sabine it was impossible to say which, and it hardly mattered. "Your hands are cold. You've been reading it again."

Not a question. Elodie sat down across from her at the bench, close enough that their knees nearly touched, and said nothing, which was itself an answer.

"You read it every morning," Sabine said. "Before anyone else is up. I hear you move the brick. I've heard it since before the ferry burned, if you want to know how badly you hide things in this house." A pause. "I've never looked. I want you to know that. It's yours, whatever it is. But I know it's why Duvall came, and why Tomas died on my father's floor, and why there are men watching the convent from the tannery roof who think nobody counts them."

"They'd be right," Elodie said. "Nobody counts them. That's the trouble with this whole country — everyone watches, no one is counted."

"And you're going to count them." It was not quite a question either. Sabine set down the chalk and folded her hands, and for a moment she looked so much like her father sitting at his own table weighing freight that Elodie had to smile.

"I'm going to try."

"No." Sabine's voice didn't rise; it sharpened, the way ice sharpens under a boot heel. "You're going to do it, or you're going to give it up. Those are the only two things left. You can't sit here all winter reading it by candlelight and being afraid in the mornings. I've watched you do both for weeks and it's eating you alive. So." She turned the slate around and pushed it across the bench. On it, beneath the river, in the corner where a cartographer puts the compass rose, she had drawn a small square — a page, a leaf, a ledger entry — and beside it, two letters: *E.S.* "Either we're in this together or you tell me tomorrow you're burning it. But stop doing it alone."

Elodie looked at the little compass rose for a long time. Her mother had owned one — brass, dented, its needle drunk near iron — and had taught her daughter to read it in a garden in another life, back when north was just north and not a direction in which armies moved. *A map tells you where things are,* her mother had said. *A compass only tells you what you've already decided.*

"Sabine," she said slowly, "do you understand what you're asking? Not to carry it. To *know* it. Once you've read those pages you can't unread them, and knowing things in this republic is how people end up on lists. Your father has lost enough. The ferry—"

"My father lost the ferry because he chose to shelter you, and he'd choose it again tonight, and you know it, and insulting him by deciding for him is worse than anything Duvall could do." She said it flatly, without heat, and Elodie felt the truth of it land like a stone dropped through thin ice. "Tomas died in our house. We washed his body ourselves. You think I don't already know what it costs?"

Elodie thought about the night they had washed him — the boy's frozen hands, the duplicate key sewn into his coat lining, the cipher identical to hers, which meant someone had sent two couriers on one road, which meant someone had expected at least one of them to die. She thought about how Sabine had wrung out the cloth without flinching and then gone out to the shed and been sick, privately, and come back in and finished. That was courage, the real kind — the kind that vomits and continues.

"All right," Elodie said.

Sabine waited.

"Not today. Tonight. After the house is asleep. If you're going to be a party to this, you'll read it the way I read it — properly, in order, with the key — because a half-understood document is more dangerous than none." She hesitated, then reached across and touched the chalk-drawn page with one fingertip, as though it were the thing itself. "And afterward, if you want out — if you decide it's too much — you say so, and you forget it, and I will never hold it against you. Sworn."

"I won't want out."

"Sworn anyway."

"Sworn," Sabine said, and something in the room changed register, the way air changes before snow.

* * *

They told Aurel at breakfast, because it was his house and his risk and because lying to him further had become, without either of them quite noticing, intolerable. He listened over his coffee — chicory and barley, mostly, the real bean long gone from Val-Brume — with the expression of a man hearing a tide report. When Elodie finished, he turned his cup over on the saucer, a habit she'd learned meant he was done drinking and thinking.

"My wife," he said, "kept accounts for the parish poor fund. Thirty years. Every sou in and every sou out, in her own hand, even the year the fund itself was seized and she was keeping the book for no one." He looked at the overturned cup. "People thought it was stubbornness. It wasn't. She used to say a record is a promise you make to people who haven't been born yet. They'll need to know what happened here, and they can't ask us, so somebody has to write it down true." He raised his eyes to Elodie. "If you're writing something down true, girl, you write it in this house. That's all I have to say about it."

That was all he ever said about it. But that evening he brought up from the cellar a length of candle stubs bound together with tarred string — enough light for a week of nights — and set it on the stairs outside her door without comment, and went down again whistling, off-key, the way brave men whistle when they are afraid and intend to be brave anyway.

* * *

At midnight the town was silent in the way only snowbound towns are silent — not quiet but *muffled*, as if the whole valley had been wrapped in wool and set away in a chest. Elodie laid the ledger open on the table between them, weighted at the corners with two flatirons and a loaf. Sabine sat opposite her, very straight, hands folded, and Elodie saw that beneath the steadiness her knuckles were white, and loved her for it, and began at the beginning.

She taught her the key the way her mother had taught her: not as a trick but as a grammar. Numbers for the common words, because common words betray frequency; the shifting alphabet keyed to the date, because dates were the one thing a spy never forgot; and above all the discipline of writing plain text only in the mind, never on paper, so that a raid found nothing but figures. Sabine learned fast — faster than Elodie had, she admitted, and said so, and Sabine flushed with pleasure and covered it by demanding the next rule.

Then the pages themselves.

Elodie read the cipher aloud in a low voice and translated as she went, and the room filled with the republic the way a flooded cellar fills with water: gradually, then all at once. Requisitions for the prison at Charenton-le-Haut — timber, nails, *lime, twelve sacks*, dated three days before the executions the town of Saint-Aubin still called the fever. A payment schedule. Names. And in the margin, in the violet-edged ink, her mother's hand: *Verified against parish register, Saint-Aubin. Forty-one dead. Cause listed as typhus. The register does not lie; the man who signed this order baptized half of them.*

Sabine did not speak for a long time. The candle burned. Somewhere below them Aurel turned in his sleep, and the beams of the old cooperage ticked and settled around them like a ship.

"The liberator," Sabine said finally. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "The one on the print in the schoolroom. With the sword and the olive branch."

"Him. And eleven others. Some of them are dead already and can't defend themselves, which is its own problem. Most of them are alive, promoted, and writing the history." Elodie turned a page. "This isn't gossip, Sabine. This is arithmetic. Sums, dates, signatures. My mother built it so it couldn't be called opinion. That's why it's worth killing for — theirs *and* mine."

"Yours?"

"They sent me to deliver it to a contact who doesn't exist anymore. Or who never existed. Either way—" She stopped. She had not said it aloud to anyone, not fully, not even to herself in daylight. Now she did. "Either the plan changed and they'll kill me for the ledger, or the plan never changed and I was always meant to die carrying it. A courier who arrives is a witness. A courier who disappears is a rumor. They prefer rumors."

The fire shifted. Sabine reached across the table and closed the ledger with both hands, gently, the way you close a coffin lid or a door against weather.

"Then here's what I think," she said. "You were sent by people who wanted it buried. Fine. They've lost the right to it. It's not their document anymore. It's your mother's, and your mother gave it to *you* — not to the Committee, not to the Notary, not to whoever's pretending to be the Notary. To you." She tapped the cover once. "So who do you serve?"

The question hung there. Elodie realized she had been circling it for weeks the way the river circles the island, wearing a channel, getting nowhere. Loyalty. She had been loyal since she was fourteen — loyal to handlers, to routes, to passwords, to a republic that existed mostly as a set of instructions delivered in cipher. Loyalty had been the whole architecture of her self, load-bearing walls everywhere, and now she stood inside the ruined building looking at the sky through the roof.

"Not them," she said at last. "Maybe not any of them. Not a state. States change their minds; my mother died of a state changing its mind." She looked down at the closed ledger, at the worn leather her mother's hands had handled in cellars and safe houses across half the country, and felt the grief come again, but differently now — not the drowning grief of the first recognition but something with edges, something she could grip. "The record. That's all. Whoever keeps it honest. A judge, a printer, a schoolchild fifty years from now who wants to know why Saint-Aubin has forty-one names and no graves. The record is the country I'm loyal to. It's the only one that hasn't lied to me."

Sabine nodded slowly. Then she did something Elodie did not expect: she pulled the slate toward her, wiped the river clean, and wrote across it in her careful schoolroom hand:

*We, Elodie Sandoval and Sabine Vasse, keep this record. It survives us. Signed this night, in Val-Brume, before God and the stove.*

"Read it," Sabine said. "Then sign it. Both of us. Not a vow to each other — vows between friends break when friends get scared. A vow to the *thing*. You can't break a promise to a piece of paper without becoming a liar in front of yourself, and I've noticed you'd rather die."

It was almost funny and neither of them laughed. Elodie took the chalk. Her hand hovered a moment over the slate — the same hand that had copied ciphers for six years, forged manifests, lied in three accents — and then wrote her name beneath Sabine's, the letters small and exact, the way her mother signed.

"There," Sabine said softly. "Now it's a republic of two. Population: us. Laws: one. Territory: wherever this book is." She blew the chalk dust from the slate with great ceremony. "Small, but honestly governed."

Elodie laughed then — really laughed, for the first time since the bridge chained shut behind her, a short helpless sound that made them both grin like conspirators, which was precisely what they were. And underneath the laughter she felt something settle in her chest, some final beam swung into place: she had spent her whole adult life being trusted by institutions, and this was different. This was being *known*. Sabine knew everything now — the ledger, the danger, the likelihood of dying — and had picked up the chalk anyway.

"We'll need more than a slate," Elodie said, sobering. "Slates wipe. If this is going to survive us, it has to go onto paper, in several places, in several hands, soon. And there's a copyist's discipline to it — hiding pages, splitting sets — that will take weeks of nights."

"Then start teaching me tomorrow. Tonight I have one more question." Sabine leaned forward, firelight catching the sharp bones of her face. "The drop. The one your orders name — under the third bridge stone, past the mill. Is it still live?"

Elodie went still. She had not mentioned the drop to anyone. She was certain she had not.

"How do you know about the drop?"

"I read your instructions the second week you were here, when you left your trunk unlocked like an amateur." Sabine said it without a flicker of apology. "I told you — you hide things badly in this house. Well? Is it live?"

"It was the last order I received. Wait for the Notary; confirm receipt at the drop." Elodie stared into the candle flame. An idea had been forming for days at the edge of her thought, shapeless and dangerous, and now it took form: if the drop was watched, it was watched by whoever had killed Tomas. If it was empty, the network was dead and she was free to act alone. Either answer was worth having. "Tomorrow," she said slowly, "I'm going to find out."

Outside, the wind rose again off the glacier and moved down the valley toward the town, and somewhere in the dark the frozen river groaned — a long, deep sound, like a door opening very far away.

* * *

Morning came white and windless. Elodie was awake before the others, ledger rehidden, brick replaced, the slate wiped clean and standing innocently by the hearth. She ate bread and chicory with Aurel and Sabine and talked about salt prices and the tannery's frozen vats, and it was the easiest conversation she had had in months, because there was nothing left between the three of them that needed hiding.

Afterward she pulled on her coat and wrapped her scarf twice and told Sabine she was going to see Corbineau about a certified copy of a bill of lading — a lie so comfortable now that it fit her like an old glove, and she hated the glove, and wore it.

The streets were empty. Smoke stood straight up from every chimney in the still air. As she passed the mouth of the lane by the mill she let her eyes flick, casually, the practiced flick of six years' trade, toward the third bridge stone.

There were fresh footprints in yesterday's snow. Two men's, coming and going, stopping at the stone.

Someone was keeping the appointment.

Elodiе walked on without breaking stride, her heart beating hard and slow, like footsteps descending stairs — toward the mill, toward the drop, toward whatever was waiting under the third stone for a courier who no longer intended to obey anybody.
