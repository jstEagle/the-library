## 20. Winter Deepens

The snow began on a Tuesday, the feast of some saint Sabine could not be troubled to remember, and it did not stop for nine days.

It came down first in fat, leisurely flakes that made the town beautiful — softening the cooperage roof, silvering the pines above the ferry landing where her father's burned boat lay like a black rib picked clean. By the third day the beauty was gone. The wind found its teeth and turned the snow sideways, then upward, then into a single white howl that erased the mountain, the river, the church steeple, and finally the house across the lane, so that Val-Brume became a handful of lit windows adrift in a whiteness without edges.

Sabine counted those windows from the loft each evening. Eleven, then ten when the Bessons' fire guttered out and they moved in with the Tardieus. Nine when old Madame Roche's shutter blew in and she fled to the convent ruins with her goat. Eight. Seven. The town shrinking to itself like a fist closing.

Her father stood at the door each morning with a shovel and cleared a trench to the woodshed and back, a journey of forty feet that took him an hour against the wind. When he came in his beard was a slab of ice and his eyebrows were white as a priest's.

"It's not weather," he said on the sixth day, stamping the feeling back into his boots. "It's a siege."

He was right. Sabine had grown up with hard winters — every child in Val-Brume had — but this was different in kind, not degree. The bridge chains had gone up before the first flake fell; the road over the col had closed a week earlier than anyone remembered; and now the sky itself had shut the last door. No carts. No letters. No salt pork coming up from the valley, no grain, no lamp oil, no word from anywhere at all.

The town had been counting everything since before Sabine was born — Maître Corbineau saw to that, every birth and bushel entered in his registers — but counting was one thing and having was another. By the seventh day the granary's lock had been broken twice. By the eighth, Madame Roche's goat had been stolen from the convent ruins, slaughtered, and eaten by persons unknown, and Madame Roche sat weeping in the church over an empty rope.

On the ninth day, Sabine went down to Tardieu's mill for flour and learned what hunger did to arithmetic.

* * *

There were eleven people in the queue when she arrived, standing shoulder-deep in the drifts the Tardieu sons had cut through, breath rising from them all like a congregation at prayer. She took her place at the end and waited, and waited, and heard what they were saying.

"—took three measures and he has four mouths, that's fair—"

"Fair? His brother-in-law is the tax farmer. Let *him* eat at the tax farmer's table—"

"I saw the Gauthier girl with white bread Tuesday. White! Who grinds white flour in a winter like this?"

"Someone who put something by, is who."

The voices were low and flat and terrible, because they were reasonable. That was what frightened Sabine — not rage, which burns itself out, but the cold bookkeeping of grievance. Every family in the queue knew exactly how many sacks every other family had laid down in October. Val-Brume had always been a town that counted. Now the counting had turned inward, like a snake eating its tail.

Inside the mill, Père Tardieu doled out flour behind a barricade of his own furniture, his two grown sons standing behind him with iron bars. He had a list — of course he had a list; everyone in this town had lists now — and he read each name aloud and matched it against his ledger of deposits, and where the deposits ran short, he gave short measure, and where they ran out entirely, he gave nothing and would not meet the man's eyes.

The Roussels got nothing. Old Roussel stood there holding his empty sack while Tardieu explained, gently, that the Roussels' deposit had been consumed in October, that everyone had known it, that there simply wasn't —

"You'll let my grandchildren starve," Roussel said. Not loudly. That was the worst of it. He said it the way a man reads figures aloud.

"The town will help—" Tardieu began.

"Which town?" said Roussel, and looked around the mill, at the faces pretending not to watch, and Sabine watched the word land in each of them like a splinter. *Which town.* As if there might be more than one. As if there had always been more than one.

She carried her two measures home through the trench with the sack clutched inside her coat, against her body, like a stolen thing, and hated herself for clutching it, and clutched it harder anyway.

* * *

At supper that night her father pushed half of his portion onto Elodie's plate without comment, and Elodie pushed it back, and the two of them conducted the whole argument in silence across the bread board until Aurel won by simply looking away, which was his way of winning arguments, and always had been.

"Eat," he told Sabine, catching her measuring her own spoonfuls. "You're growing."

"I'm counting," she said.

"Don't." He said it quietly enough that Elodie, at the end of the table, bent over her cipher-work by candlelight, would not hear. Or would pretend not to. Sabine had noticed long ago how carefully their lodger never quite listened, and how much she somehow knew anyway. "Counting's Corbineau's trade. You're a Vasse. We keep the water open."

"The water's frozen, Papa."

"Then we keep the ice honest." He smiled, but it was an old man's smile, worn thin, and after she'd blown out her candle Sabine lay in the loft listening to the wind try the shutters one by one, like a thief testing doors, and thought: *how long before honesty runs out too?*

Elodie's candle still burned below. Sabine could see the light through the floorboards, a thin gold seam, and the faint scratch of the quill. Their lodger worked every night now, hours of it, page after page, hiding whatever she wrote whenever footsteps passed in the lane. Sabine had promised not to ask. She kept the promise. But she lay awake doing her own kind of cipher, reading the shape of things: the way Elodie flinched at knocking; the way she'd stopped going out except wrapped in her plainest cloak; the way she looked at Sabine sometimes — not at her face, at her hands, as if weighing what they could carry.

Everyone in this town was weighing everyone else now. It was in the air, like the snow.

* * *

On the eleventh day the blizzard broke, and what it left behind was a town wearing a mask.

Snow stood higher than the fences. The lane between the houses had become a canyon with walls of blue-white ice, and people moved through it in single file, passing one another close enough to touch and looking nowhere near each other's eyes. The church bell rang for a burial — old Roussel himself, it turned out, though whether the cold or the hunger or the shame had finished him, the priest did not say, and no one asked, because asking had become a form of accusation.

Sabine went with her father to the burial because her father believed you went, and stood in the frozen graveyard among thirty neighbors, and heard the priest say the words over a hole hacked through four feet of frost, and watched the mourners' eyes move — while the words were still being spoken — to the widow, to the widow's stores, to the question underneath every other question now: *what have you got, and how long can you hold it?*

Afterward, in the churchyard, she heard Madame Gauthier telling the baker's wife, in a voice pitched to carry, that the Vasses were feeding a boarder who paid in coin.

Coin. In a starving town, the word was worse than an insult. Coin meant outside. Coin meant someone had money while your children ate bran porridge, and where had a wool clerk's clerk got coin, and what else did she have under that plain gray cloak?

Sabine's face went hot. She opened her mouth to say something — she didn't know what, only that her fists had closed — and her father's hand came down on her shoulder, heavy as a beam.

"Home," he said.

They walked home through the ice canyon, and halfway along it he stopped, leaned against the blue wall of snow, and for a moment covered his eyes with his mittened hand. She had never seen him do that. Her father, who had ferried strangers across black water for thirty years, who had stood in the ashes of his burned boat and said *we rebuild*, her father looked — for one unguarded second — afraid.

"Papa?"

"Thirty years," he said, "I've fed whoever the river sent me. Rich men, smugglers, priests, soldiers, once a woman with a newborn in a market basket. Never asked what they carried. That's the ferryman's law: you don't ask what a soul weighs, you just carry it." He straightened up. The fear went back wherever he kept it. "But the river's frozen, little one, and men aren't. Men get hungry, and hungry men start asking. So we're careful now. All three of us. Careful as mice in a wall."

That night she caught Elodie watching her from the table, the quill still, the candle throwing her shadow huge against the rafters.

"Madame Gauthier talks," Sabine said. "In case you wondered why I came home angry."

"I know what she says." Elodie's voice was even. Too even. "I'm sorry. I'll pay more—"

"Don't you dare pay more." It came out sharper than Sabine intended, loud in the small kitchen, and she lowered it. "Coin's the problem, not the cure. If you pay us more, we're not hosts anymore, we're — suppliers. And suppliers get robbed when times turn." She heard herself and stopped, appalled at the fluency of it. When had she learned to think like this? When had the arithmetic gotten into her?

Elodie was looking at her with something strange in her face — recognition, maybe, or regret. "You sound like a courier," she said, almost smiling. "Or a fugitive."

"I sound like Val-Brume in February," Sabine said. "Eat your soup."

But later, lying in the dark with the wind gone quiet at last, she understood what frightened her most about the day. Not Gauthier's gossip, not the empty grave, not even the look on her father's face. What frightened her was that when Madame Gauthier had said *coin*, for one flickering instant — less than a heartbeat — Sabine had felt the temptation herself. Had seen, in a flash, how easy it would be to solve everything: give the woman up. Point at the gray cloak. *She's not a wool clerk, I don't know what she is, take her and feed us.* One sentence, and the Vasse household would eat like kings through March.

One heartbeat. Then it was gone, drowned under something hotter and better. But it had been there. It lived in her, the way the wolf lives in the village dog, and everyone in Val-Brume had one now, and the snow had only to keep falling long enough for the wolves to start choosing.

* * *

The thaw did not come. Instead, on the fifteenth day, the cold returned with a cruelty that felt personal — twenty degrees of frost, wind out of the north like a whetted blade, the kind of cold that kills the careless and the old within an hour. The well at the crossroads froze to the bottom and men had to haul blocks up with grappling hooks. A chimney fire took the end house off the tannery lane and the bucket chain froze solid before it could do anything, and the Besson family stood in the lane watching their home burn and nobody had anything to offer them, because offering had become spending, and spending had become betrayal of your own children.

That was the rehearsal. Sabine saw it clearly that night, watching the fire glow die and the crowd disperse in silence, each family retreating into its own lit window like garrisons into forts. This was what the war had done to the nation, writ small enough to hold in one town's hands: not battles, but this — neighbors becoming accounts, accounts becoming grievances, grievances becoming permission. Somewhere beyond the mountains, she supposed, generals were doing exactly this with provinces.

"We need a judge," she said abruptly. She and her father were banking the fire. Elodie was out — she went out some nights, to God knew where, and Sabine had stopped asking, another wolf-pact of silences between them.

"A judge?" Aurel snorted. "For what? To sentence the weather?"

"To make us stop." She surprised herself with how certain her voice was. "Everyone in this town knows what's right. They knew tonight, watching the Bessons stand in the street. They knew at the mill. Knowing isn't the problem. There's no one left whose *saying so* counts. Tardieu's scared. The priest's scared. Corbineau writes everything down and changes nothing. Everyone's waiting for someone with authority to tell them the famine doesn't excuse them — and nobody comes, so everybody decides they've been given leave."

Her father was quiet a long moment, poking the coals. "You've been talking to Corbineau."

"I've been talking to everyone. It's what people do when they think I'm just the ferryman's daughter — they talk." She pulled the blanket tighter. "And here's what else they talk about, Papa, since we're being careful as mice: there's a woman wintering at the Roche house since Advent. Keeps to herself. Pays in — " she almost laughed at the irony — "coin. Old. Proud. And Madame Roche swears blind, because Madame Roche does her accounts with God and has no reason to lie, that the woman is Madame Liorans. The judge. From the parlement. Retired up here years ago, or exiled, depending who tells it."

Aurel's poker stopped moving.

"A judge," he said slowly, "who winters here. Through all this. And says nothing?"

"Maybe she's been waiting," Sabine said, "for somebody to ask her."

The fire settled. Out in the lane, the cold creaked against the houses like a ship's timbers, and somewhere far off a dog began to howl and was answered, then answered again, until the whole frozen town sounded like a place where something had just died.

Her father looked at her across the embers for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had changed — the ferryman's voice, flat and decided, the voice that meant the current had shifted and there was work to do.

"Tomorrow," he said, "you and I are going to pay our respects to Madame Roche's lodger. Take her a loaf. Ferryman's law — you don't ask what a soul weighs." A pause. "But you find out what it can carry."

* * *

They never made the visit.

Before dawn, hammering woke the whole lane — not knocking but hammering, fists on planks, and voices, and Sabine was at the window with her heart slamming before she was properly awake, and saw lanterns bobbing in the ice canyon below. Four men. Five. Armed. And at their head, wrapped in a fine military greatcoat with the collar up against the cold, a lean figure she knew at once, because the whole town knew him now and feared him politely: Agent Duvall, the man who claimed the road.

But Duvall wasn't looking at the Vasse house.

His lantern swung up, and its light climbed the front of the Roche house opposite — the shuttered windows, the door with its old brass knocker — and stopped there, steady, deliberate, the way a man looks at a thing he intends to take.

And the door opened before he knocked again, which meant the woman inside had been awake all along, watching him come.

Even across the lane, even in lantern-light, even muffled in shawls, Sabine saw it: the straightening of the spine, the unhurried lift of the chin, the absolute stillness of a person who has spent a lifetime in rooms where everyone else must ask permission to speak.

Duvall's men shuffled. Duvall himself did not move forward.

From the doorway, clear across the frozen silence, the voice of Madame Liorans carried the length of the lane:

"Well, sir. You have woken my street. I suggest you account for it — in writing, if you please. I find I have grown very fond, this winter, of things in writing."
