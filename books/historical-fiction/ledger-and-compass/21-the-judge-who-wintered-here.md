## 21. The Judge Who Wintered Here

She had been in Val-Brume for eleven days before Elodie understood who she was.

The woman came down from the pass in the first week of December, ahead of the snows, with one trunk and one servant and the unhurried gait of someone who had never once been chased. She took rooms at the house of the widow Perrin, near the church, and paid her rent quarterly, in advance, in coin that had no republic's face on it. She walked each morning along the frozen river road as far as the millrace and back, leaning on a black cane she did not seem to need, nodding to those who nodded to her.

The town called her *madame la juge* behind her back and nothing at all to her face — the particular silence of people who have lived through purges and learned that titles are dangerous things to say aloud.

Elodie heard it first from Sabine, who collected the town's secrets the way other girls collected ribbon.

"Madame Liorans," Sabine said, crouched over her map of the ice drawn in charcoal on the floor of the cooperage loft, tracing the safe path across the river with a finger. "She was on the high court at Grenoble. Before. My father says she signed judgments against men who are ministers now. Or against men who were ministers then and are corpses now. He can't remember which, and he says it doesn't matter, because either way she has enemies on both sides of every river in France."

"And she comes here? To the edge of nowhere?"

"She comes every winter. She says the air is honest." Sabine looked up, and there was the sharp gleam in her eye that Elodie had learned to fear a little and love more. "Father says the air isn't why anyone winters anywhere."

---

Elodie thought about her for three days.

Three days of carrying the ledger's weight in the false bottom of her trunk, three days of copying by candlelight until her hand cramped and the candle guttered low — pages of requisitions and payments, names and sums, the arithmetic of men who had signed away lives with flourishes they must have practiced in front of mirrors. Three days of asking herself the question she had not dared ask since the night she recognized her mother's hand in the margins:

*What is a record worth if no one with power will read it?*

Corbineau would corroborate. Corbineau had already begun, in his careful way, pulling registers from his shelves when Elodie visited under the pretext of settling the wool accounts. But Corbineau was a compromised man, a man who had certified a false inventory to save his own family, and a tribunal could shred him like wet paper. The town's testimony would be testimony. It needed something else. It needed judgment — the kind that wore robes, the kind that carried the law like a blade, the kind that even soldiers paused before.

And here, freezing politely at the edge of Val-Brume, walked exactly one such creature.

*Hope with a knife in it,* Elodie thought. Because showing a page to Madame Liorans meant trusting her. And trust, in Elodie's short and disciplined life, had been the currency she dealt in least.

But Tomas had died on the ice carrying a duplicate key to her cipher, and Duvall sat every evening in the inn's best room drinking wine paid for by an authority no one could produce, and somewhere south her handlers waited for a courier who was never meant to arrive. The ledger could not stay hidden forever. Hidden things rot. Hidden things get found by the wrong hands. A record only lives when it passes into keeping strong enough to hold it.

On the fourth morning, Elodie wrapped a deciphered page in oilcloth, tucked it inside her mitten against her palm, and walked the river road to find the woman with the black cane.

---

She found her at the millrace, standing very still at the rail where the water ran black and fast between the ice shelves, the only thing in Val-Brume that refused to freeze. Steam rose off it. The judge watched it the way other people watched fires.

"Madame Liorans." Elodie stopped three paces off, close enough for courtesy, far enough for retreat. "Forgive me. I'm told you don't care for interruptions on your walk."

The old woman did not turn at once. She was smaller than Elodie had imagined, straight-backed, with a face like a dried apple — all planes and creases — and eyes that were startlingly, unbearably awake. When she finally turned, she looked at Elodie the way Corbineau looked at a forged signature: not with suspicion, but with instant, complete understanding of what she was being shown.

"You're the wool clerk," she said. "Vasse's lodger. The one who counts everything twice."

"I count once," Elodie said. "I only look twice."

Something moved at the corner of the judge's mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, perhaps, buried so long ago it had forgotten how to surface. "And what does the wool trade want with a retired judge? Wool doesn't need judging. It needs weighing."

"This needs judging."

Elodie took the oilcloth packet from her mitten and held it out between them.

For a long moment Madame Liorans did not take it. She looked at Elodie instead — down, then up, the full survey, unhurried, the way she might have looked at a witness taking an oath, checking for tremors, for rehearsed calm, for the tells of a trap. The wind came down the valley and cut through Elodie's coat, and still the old woman looked.

"You understand what you're doing," Liorans said at last. It wasn't a question. "You understand that once I've read it, you can't un-read it into my head. Whatever this is, you are handing it to me the way one hands a lit fuse."

"Yes, madame."

"Then we should not stand in the open wind like two conspirators in a bad play." She took the packet — her fingers closed over it with surprising firmness — and turned back toward town. "Come. Widow Perrin keeps a good fire and asks no questions. It is the single greatest virtue of this town."

---

The room above the widow Perrin's was small and fiercely warm, hung with drying herbs, its single window frosted into opacity. A fire burned behind a low screen; a cat regarded Elodie from the hearthstone with the flat disdain of its species. Madame Liorans dismissed her servant with a glance, poured two glasses of a dark local wine without asking whether Elodie drank, and sat in the chair nearest the light.

She unwrapped the oilcloth as if it were a wound dressing.

Elodie had chosen the page with a copyist's care. Not the worst page — the worst pages, the execution lists with their neat columns of names and prices paid to the muskets' owners, would come later, would need to be earned. This one was almost gentle: a requisition order, dated fourteen months ago, for grain seized from three villages in the valley below Val-Brume. Grain taken "for the defense of the republic" and sold, within the month, at profit, to a merchant house whose director had since been decorated for his service. Two signatures authorized it. One belonged to a man now spoken of in schoolrooms as a liberator. In the margin, in a fine slanted hand Elodie knew better than her own heartbeat, her mother had written: *The grain fed no soldiers. Check the tolls at Bourg-Neuf.*

Madame Liorans read it twice.

She read it the way Elodie imagined she had once read ten thousand documents — left column first, then right, then the margin, then the dates cross-checked against some internal calendar of the republic's crimes that she apparently kept in her head. Her face did not change. That was the terrible thing about judges, Elodie thought; they had learned to keep their faces the way couriers kept their trunks, with false bottoms over live cargo.

"The tolls at Bourg-Neuf," Liorans said finally. "I know those tolls. I audited them myself, in another life, before they gave the collection to political appointees." She set the page down on her knee and looked up. "Who wrote the marginal note?"

Elodie had prepared for many questions. This was not one of them, and it went through her defenses like an awl.

"My mother," she said. Her voice held. She made it hold. "She is dead."

"Is she." The judge picked up the page again and touched the marginal writing with one fingertip, lightly, the way one touches a relic. "Dead women write very precise notes about toll roads. I have found that living ones rarely do." She raised her eyes. "This cipher — the numbers keyed to feast days, the substitution broken at intervals of seven — I have seen it before. Long ago. In dispatches that were supposed to have been burned with everyone who could read them."

The fire cracked. The cat stretched. Somewhere below them, the widow Perrin dropped a pot, swore gently, and the ordinary world went on around the two women sitting in a warm room with a lit fuse between them.

"There were four of us," Liorans said quietly. "In the early years. Four who saw what was coming and kept copies of what passed through our hands, against the day the victors began writing their histories. Two are dead — I attended neither funeral, funerals being how one identifies the mourners. One vanished. I had begun to believe I was the last." She turned the page over, examining the blank verso, the fold marks. "And now a wool clerk walks up to me on a frozen road and hands me a document in a dead courier's cipher, annotated in a dead archivist's hand. Tell me, girl — and think carefully, because I will know a lie — do you know what you are carrying? Truly? Or are you a courier with sealed orders, delivering a package you've never opened?"

"I opened it," Elodie said. "Page by page. By candlelight. It took me nine nights to break the interval key, and every night I wished I hadn't."

"Why?"

"Because the signatures belong to heroes."

Liorans nodded slowly, as if Elodie had confirmed something she'd known since the bridge at Grenoble, since before. "Yes," she said. "That is always the discovery, isn't it. First you learn your enemies are monsters — that part is easy, they tell you themselves. Then, years later, you learn your heroes signed the same orders with better handwriting." She set the page down and folded her hands over it, possessively, and Elodie felt a flare of alarm so sharp it was almost physical — *she'll keep it, she'll lock it away, I've given my mother's voice to a stranger* — but the judge's next words doused it.

"I will not take this from you," Liorans said. "Understand that. I am not a repository. I am seventy-one years old, I winter in a town of four hundred souls, and any morning the thaw could bring soldiers with a warrant and a sledge. If I die holding your mother's page, it dies in a trunk in a widow's house, and we have both wasted a fine dramatic scene." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught the planes of her face and filled the creases with shadow, so that for a moment she looked carved rather than aged. "What I will do is this. I will read whatever you choose to show me. I will tell you what will stand before a lawful tribunal and what will be laughed out of court. I will tell you which of these signatures carries weight and which can be bought or excused. And when the time comes — if the time comes — I will put on whatever authority I still have left and stand where it needs standing. That is my offer. It is not safety. There is no safety in this, for either of us. It is address. Justice, if it exists at all, exists at addresses. Yours has just acquired one."

Elodie found she could not speak immediately. She had crossed a mountain pass with a nation's crimes strapped to her back; she had lied fluently to Duvall across a dinner table; she had copied pages until her fingers bled at the corners. None of it had prepared her for the simple, terrifying experience of being believed.

"The rest of it," she managed. "The ledger. There are — it's substantial. Requisitions, payment schedules, execution orders with compensation lines. Names on both sides. Men your government celebrates. Men the other side will want to hang."

"Good," said Madame Liorans.

Elodie stared. "Good?"

"A ledger that indicts only one side is propaganda. Propaganda burns; it always has. A ledger that indicts *everyone* — that is history, and history is the one thing victors cannot burn without admitting what they're burning." The old woman lifted her wine glass and studied Elodie over its rim. "Your mother understood that, didn't she. That's why she recorded both sides' sins in one book. Clever, brave woman. You have her chin. Don't look so startled — I taught her sister mathematics, forty years ago, in Grenoble. The family resemblance in the handwriting alone would have told me whose child you are."

The room tilted, very slightly. Forty years. Grenoble. A thread running back through the whole of Elodie's life, through her mother's lessons at the kitchen table — *count twice, lie never, write small* — to this frosted window and this dying fire and this woman who had been waiting, all along, at the end of the thread like a knot.

"Why here?" Elodie asked. "Why winter in Val-Brume, year after year, if you were waiting for—"

"For a ledger?" Liorans smiled then, fully, and it transformed her face into something fierce. "Child, I winter here because Val-Brume is the only town in France that writes everything down. Your notary Corbineau records births, deaths, sales, quarrels, ferry tolls, the weight of every barrel that leaves this valley. I have read his published abstracts for a decade. A man who keeps records that thorough is either a fool or a fortress, and Corbineau is nobody's fool." She drained her glass. "I have been waiting for someone to connect his fortress to my memory. I simply did not expect the messenger to be a wool clerk with her mother's hand."

---

Elodie stayed an hour more. They spoke in low voices, methodically, judge and witness: what to copy first, what to corroborate against the civic registers, which names required proof beyond denial before they could safely be spoken aloud. Liorans gave her three rules, delivered like sentences handed down.

"One: never show me anything you cannot bear to lose. Two: never let the original and the copy sleep under the same roof. Three—" she paused, and for a moment the fierceness dimmed into something older and sadder, "—three: decide now, tonight, whom you serve. Not which side. Whom. Because the hour is coming, and it comes to every courier eventually, when the people who sent you will require you to disappear, and you will need to have decided beforehand what your obedience is actually worth."

"I serve the record," Elodie said. It came out steadier than she expected, as though the words had been waiting in her mouth for weeks, dressed and ready.

Liorans studied her for a long moment. Then she returned the page — wrapped again in its oilcloth, pressed back into Elodie's hands — and something in the gesture was almost ceremonial, almost a laying-on of office.

"Keep it safe," she said. "Bring me more when your courage matches your caution. And Elodie—" the judge's voice followed her to the door, quiet and level and carrying, "—your handlers chose their cipher well and their courier poorly. No one breaks a nine-night key out of duty alone. Ask yourself, before the thaw, what they intended for you after delivery. Couriers who carry indictments are rarely meant to survive as witnesses."

Elodie walked home through the blue dusk with the page against her heart and the judge's last words walking beside her like a second shadow.

At the cooperage, Aurel was mending staves by lamplight and Sabine was sharpening the charcoal point of her ice-map, and everything was warm and ordinary and utterly changed. Elodie climbed to the loft, took out her orders — the ones sewn into her coat lining, the ones she had recited like scripture since the day they were pressed into her hand — and, for the first time since leaving the capital, sat down to read them not as commands.

She read them as evidence.

By the third reading, her hands had gone cold, and the lamp seemed very small, and the words she had trusted all autumn began arranging themselves into a sentence she had never allowed herself to see.
