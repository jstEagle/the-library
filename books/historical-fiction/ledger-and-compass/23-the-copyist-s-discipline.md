## 23. The Copyist's Discipline

The cooperage smelled of oak shavings and cold iron hoops, and by the third night of my work I had learned to love it the way a monk loves his cell — not because it was comfortable, but because it was the one place in the world where I knew exactly what I was meant to do.

Aurel Vasse kept his workshop behind the house, down three steps and through a door that groaned on leather hinges. He had shown me the room in autumn, when I first arrived with my merchant's trunk and my merchant's lies: *the staving bench, the croze, the bung borers, mind the adze, it bites.* I had nodded the way clerks nod, half-listening, my attention already on the window and the sightline to the bridge. Now I came down those steps after midnight like a woman descending into a chapel, and the tools that had once been furniture of someone else's trade became the instruments of mine.

I worked by two candles. Not one — two, set at opposite ends of the staving bench, so that if a draft killed one I would not be plunged into blackness with the ledger open before me. Sabine had laughed at this when she discovered it, that particular laugh of hers, quick and unkind to fools. "You plan for your candles the way other people plan for their funerals."

"Other people only die once," I said. "I intend to be careful about everything."

She had stopped laughing then, and looked at me the way she looked at the river ice in late December — measuring where it would hold.

* * *

The discipline began with the hands.

My mother taught me cipher when I was nine years old, in a kitchen that smelled of rosemary, with flour on her wrists and her voice low as confession. She never said *this is dangerous.* She said, instead, *a code is a promise you make to the future. The future is impatient and clumsy. You must be neither.* Then she made me copy the same passage four times until my letters stopped trembling, and she burned the first three attempts in the stove while I watched.

I understood her now. Sitting alone in the dark with the ledger open on its oilcloth, I understood that every skill she had pressed into me — the cipher, the false bottoms, the flat voice, the face that gave nothing away — had been preparation for this winter, for this bench, for these pages. She had known she might not live to finish what she started. She had built me as her second pair of hands.

So I copied the way she had taught me: slowly, twice over, without mercy toward myself.

The method was simple and therefore demanding. Each evening I deciphered no more than six pages of the original — six, though some nights I could have done twelve, because fatigue makes errors and errors make corpses. I transcribed them first into plain French in a hand that was not mine: rounder, more provincial, the writing of a wool clerk who had learned her letters from a village curé rather than a city schoolmistress. Then I enciphered the plain text again into a second key — not the ledger's key, which lived folded inside my left boot heel, but a new one of my own construction, built on my mother's system but altered, so that even a man holding both keys could not read the copies without knowing the alteration. The alteration existed nowhere but in my head. That was the price and the point of it. If they took me, they would take it with me, and the copies would sleep as soundly as stones.

Two copies of each page. One went into the staves. One went somewhere else entirely, and I will come to that.

* * *

The staves were Aurel's idea, though he did not know he'd had it.

He had been repairing a wine cask that first week of deep cold, tapping out a stave that had split along its grain, and I watched him fit the new oak into its groove with a wooden mallet, tap-tap-tap, until the seam vanished under his thumb. A barrel, he said, is a lie told well. Thirty boards that want nothing more than to fly apart, held in a circle by iron and pride, and everyone believes it's one solid thing. *That's all craftsmanship is, mademoiselle. Making people believe the seams aren't there.*

I thought about that for three days.

Then, on a Sunday when the family was at Mass — I did not go; God and I had an arrangement of mutual avoidance — I asked him, in my best clerk's voice, whether he ever made casks with double walls. For brandy, I said. For expensive things. Merchants in the valley paid premiums for barrels that could survive rough roads.

He squinted at me. Aurel Vasse was not a clever man in the way Corbineau was clever, all quickness and citation, but he had the slow deep cunning of a man who has spent forty years reading water, and I felt his eyes go from my face to my trunk and back again, weighing something he chose not to name.

"For brandy," he repeated.

"For anything that must arrive intact," I said.

He rubbed his jaw. "Double-staving costs weight. But there's a trick — you rabbet the inner stave thin, fit it proud against the outer, and seal the channel with pitch. From outside, no one sees it. From inside—" he shrugged, "—it's just a barrel."

"I should like to learn," I said. "A clerk who can't mend her own packing is a poor clerk."

He taught me that afternoon, patient as a father, showing me how to plane the inner stave to the thickness of two fingers, how to cut the rabbet with the shoulder plane so the joint would close invisible, how to run the pitch hot and let it cool to a shell. We made three such staves together, for practice, he believed. Afterward I made eleven more alone, working evenings when the workshop was empty, and each one held a hollow exactly wide enough for a folded quarto sheet wrapped in waxed linen.

Eleven staves. Twenty-two pages per hiding cycle, once I learned to fold tight enough. It was not fast. It was not meant to be fast.

It was meant to be certain.

* * *

There is a particular silence that falls over a person who works with total concentration, and I had not heard it in myself since before the republic began to eat its children. In Paris, in Lyon, on the roads, there had always been the listening — ears cocked for boots on stairs, eyes flicking to mirrors, the whole body bent toward survival like a blade bent toward a whetstone. Even sleep had been a form of listening.

But at the staving bench, with the two candles burning and my mother's cipher unfolding under my fingers, the listening stopped. Not because the danger had gone. Because the work demanded everything, and everything is a great deal more than fear can spare.

I came to know the ledger's rhythms the way a priest knows his breviary. The early pages were requisitions — grain, cattle, firewood, taken from villages whose names I knew from my childhood geography, signed with the neat initials of men whose portraits now hung in government offices. Then the payments. Sums disbursed to informants, sums disbursed to executioners — the ledger was honest about what they were called, which the official record never was — sums paid to carters for transport of persons to places from which persons did not return. And threaded through it all, in the margins, in a hand I had wept over and now touched only with my eyes, her annotations. Dates cross-checked. Names underlined twice. Once, beside a requisition of 4,000 francs marked *miscellaneous*, a single word in her small fierce script: *Where?*

Where indeed. My mother had spent her last years asking that question of paper, and the paper had answered, and now the answer sat in a cooperage in Val-Brume disguised as a wool shipment, waiting for me to give it a voice.

Some nights the work was almost peaceful. Some nights I set down the pen because my hand shook too badly to trust, and I stood at the cold window looking at the frozen river and hated every man in that ledger, including — especially — the ones who had sent me. They had sent me to carry this thing to a destination where it would be buried, and me with it. I had read my orders a dozen times since the realization came, and each reading stripped another layer of illusion. *Deliver to the Notary. If the Notary does not appear, hold. Do not attempt independent delivery. The contents are state property.*

State property. As if truth were a sack of grain, to be locked in a granary and rationed by whoever held the keys. My mother had died — had *lived*, hidden, hunted, writing in the margins of damned documents — so that this record would speak someday, and her own handlers meant to make sure it never spoke at all. Very well. Their courier had died on the road somewhere between obedience and Val-Brume. What remained was a copyist, and copyists serve a different master.

* * *

Sabine found out on the ninth night, because of course she did. I have wondered since whether I grew careless or whether she simply refused to be deceived by a person she had decided to respect. With Sabine, deception was less a wall than a curtain; she drew it aside whenever she pleased.

She came down the steps without knocking — her house, her rules — and stood in the candlelight with her arms crossed, looking at the ledger, the two pens, the ink, the stack of finished pages squared and weighted under a stave offcut.

"Well," she said.

"You should knock," I said, because there was nothing else to say.

"You should burn fewer candles. Duvall's man counts the light in windows. He counted ours tonight and asked me why the cooperage glows at midnight." She crossed the room, picked up a finished page — the enciphered copy, meaningless to her — and turned it over as if the reverse might confess. "How long?"

"Since the blizzard."

"And Aurel? Does he know?"

"He knows I'm learning barrel-making. He doesn't ask why a clerk practices at midnight."

"He knows." She set the page down with terrible gentleness. "Papa has carried smugglers' cargo since before I was born. He doesn't ask questions the way other men don't breathe — automatically, and to stay alive. But he knows." She pulled the stool to the bench and sat, uninvited, permanent. "Show me the folding. If I'm going to be hanged for this eventually, I'd rather be useful first."

I should have sent her away. Every discipline of my trade said send her away, knowledge is a debt the innocent cannot afford, etcetera, etcetera — the catechism of couriers, recited so often it had worn smooth as a prayer bead. Instead I looked at her face, sixteen years old and lit gold by my criminal candles, and I thought: *she already knows too much to be safe. Better she should know enough to be safe.*

I showed her the folding. Quarters first, then quarters again, edges inward, so the creases shielded the ink. Waxed linen, tied with waxed thread, knot positioned so a blind hand could verify it. She practiced with blank sheets until her folds were tighter than mine, because of course they were; she had been making precise things with her hands since she could hold a knife.

"The second copy," she said, when we paused. "You hide one set here. Where's the other?"

"Away from here."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind that keeps you alive."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide — visibly, the way weather decides — not to fight it. "Fine. But tell me this much: if soldiers come for the cooperage, how long do I have to get the staves out?"

"Minutes. Perhaps less."

"Then we need a better answer than minutes." She tapped the bench. "Tomorrow I'll show you the ice cellar under the salt shed. Papa thinks I don't know about it. There's a cavity behind the north wall where he winters eels. Cold, dry, and no one on earth looks for eels in a salt shed."

And that — that absurd, practical, eel-smelling generosity — was the moment I stopped being merely grateful to the Vasse family and became bound to them. Debt hardens into obligation, obligation into something the old books call fidelity, and fidelity, I was learning, weighs more than any oath to any republic. Republics issue papers. Fidelity issues itself.

* * *

The weeks took on the shape of liturgy.

Mornings I was Madame Giret's wool clerk, entering figures in a fair hand, complaining mildly about prices, being boring in public with the thoroughness of a professional. Afternoons I walked the town — to the bakery, to the notary's office to collect a fictitious invoice, past the salt house where Duvall's men stamped their feet and watched the street — laying down, page by page, the harmless biography of a nobody. Evenings, supper with the family, bread and beans and Aurel's stories, Sabine's elbow finding mine under the table when either of us needed reminding that we were two people and not one conspiracy. And then the candles, the bench, the pen, the fold, the pitch, the mallet's soft knock sealing another page into its oak coffin until resurrection day.

Twenty-two pages a week, at first. Then thirty, as my hand sped and my errors fell away. I kept a tally in my head — never written, never spoken — of the ledger's total against the copies made, a fraction that climbed like water rising in a lock. By the second week of January I had passed a quarter. By the third, a third. The arithmetic was my rosary: *so many pages between the truth and safety, so many nights remaining before the thaw brought soldiers, so many ways to fail.*

I failed twice, and recorded the failures the way my mother would have.

The first was trivial and nearly fatal: I caught myself carrying the ledger's key in the wrong boot after mending a hole in the heel, and spent a sick hour reconstructing where it had been before I corrected it. Trivial. Fatal. The difference between a courier and a corpse is often a boot.

The second failure was of the heart. I reached the pages describing the purge at Saint-Aubin — the town where my mother had supposedly died, where the ledger recorded, in her own marginal hand, the names of thirty-one people shot behind the granary, and the payment of 200 francs to the officer who ordered it, and the signature above that payment of a man whose statue now stood in the Place de la République in Lyon. I read the names. All thirty-one. Aloud, alone, at midnight, in a whisper, because they had been written down to be erased and I would not let them pass through my hands in silence. And then I could not work for two days. I sat in my room above the cooperage and stared at the ceiling and let the grief have me, wholly, the way a fever has a body.

On the third day I went back down the steps and took up the pen, and I understood that this too was discipline — not the absence of breaking, but the return. Monks break their fasts and their vows and their hearts, and the bell rings, and they go to the office anyway. The bell rang. I went.

* * *

Corbineau came to the cooperage on a Thursday, ostensibly about invoices.

He examined Aurel's stock with the air of a man appraising witnesses, praised a butter cask he clearly had no intention of buying, and then, while Aurel was out fetching a sample, stood beside me at the bench and said, very quietly, without turning his head:

"Mademoiselle Giret. A hypothetical question, purely archival. If a person wished to preserve a document against fire, flood, and the attention of armed men — and wished to do so in duplicate — what would the profession recommend?"

I kept my eyes on the stave I was planing. Shavings curled away under the blade. "The profession," I said, "would recommend that the person stop asking questions in rooms with windows."

"Ah." A pause. "And the windows are watched."

"The windows are always watched. That is what windows are for."

He nodded slowly, absorbing this, and I felt rather than saw him decide to say the thing he had actually come to say. "There is a clause in the old registry law — pre-revolutionary, never repealed, because repealing things requires energy and the republic prefers inventing. Any citizen may deposit a sealed instrument with the civic registrar for preservation, and the registrar may not open it, may not surrender it, may not acknowledge its existence except upon the depositor's death or written order. It is called *dépôt de sûreté*. Safekeeping deposit." He adjusted his spectacles. "I mention it hypothetically. The penalty for a registrar who violates it is severe. The penalty for a registrar who honors it has, historically, also occasionally been severe. But the law is the law, and I am, whatever else I am, a man of records."

He set a card on the bench — a plain card, bearing only his name and office hours — and left through the groaning door, and I stood among the shavings with my heart going like a hammer on a hoop.

A legal vault. A place in the town's own iron-bound memory where a sealed truth could sleep, untouchable, deniable, waiting. He was offering me the one thing my hiding places could not guarantee: permanence under color of law. And he was offering it knowing what it might cost him, this man who had certified a false inventory to save his family and had been paying for it in shame ever since. This was his penance taking shape. I understood that. I accepted it the way one accepts a gift that is also a wound.

That night I added a new line to my mental arithmetic: *copies for the staves, copies for the eel cellar, and one sealed original-in-truth for the registry, when the moment comes.* Three lives for the record. My mother would have approved. She had always said a document worth keeping was worth keeping three times.

* * *

I was sealing the thirty-ninth page into its stave — pitch cooling, mallet raised — when Sabine came down the steps faster than the steps deserved, and I knew before she spoke that the arithmetic had changed.

"Duvall's man was at the door," she said, breathless, quiet. "Not the street. The *door*. Asking Papa how many lodgers keep late hours." Her eyes went to the bench, to the ledger lying open under its oilcloth, to the two candles I had not yet snuffed. "Elodie. He wasn't asking about us. He was asking about the *light*."

I set down the mallet. Outside, the wind moved along the river ice with a sound like paper turning, page after page after page, and somewhere in the town a watchman called the hour — two bells, ordinary, indifferent — and I began, calmly, to count in my head how many pages remained, and how few nights were left to copy them.

Fewer, now. Far fewer than I had planned for.

The thaw was coming, and it was not bringing only water.
