## 24. Duvall Closes In

The knock came at the cooperage door on a morning of iron-grey light, and I knew before Sabine opened it what the knocking meant.

Three raps, measured, unhurried — the knock of a man who owns every door he stands before. Below me, through the floorboards, I heard Aurel set down his adze. I heard Sabine's boots cross the workshop. And I heard, faint as a pulse under snow, my own heart beginning to count.

I had slept badly. The copying had gone past three bells again, my eyes swimming with columns of requisitions and names of the dead, and when I finally blew out the candle I had lain in the dark doing arithmetic I could not stop doing: pages remaining against nights remaining, Duvall's patience against the thaw, the thaw against the soldiers it would bring. Now the knock arrived like an answer to all that arithmetic, and the answer was *too soon*.

"Open in the name of the winter commission," said a voice below — not Duvall's; one of his men, the tall one with the chapped hands whose name I had never troubled to learn because I had told myself I would be gone before names mattered.

Sabine opened the door. I could not see her from the loft, only hear her, and her voice was perfect — bored, fifteen years old, a girl interrupted at her chores. "We're salting fish. If you want to help, there's a knife."

"We're searching lodgings. Everyone's lodgings. By order."

"Order of what?"

"Of Monsieur Duvall."

"Monsieur Duvall," Sabine repeated, in the tone she used for men who bought wool without haggling — the tone that said *I have weighed you and found you lighter than your coat*. "Papa," she called, not moving aside, "there are gentlemen here who want to look through our things. They have an order. From a man."

Aurel came up the stairs slowly, wiping his hands, and I heard the particular quality of silence he made when he was being careful — the silence of a man who has been searched before, in another life, on the wrong side of another border. "Gentlemen," he said. "You'll want to wipe your boots. Salt brine eats leather."

They did not wipe their boots.

---

I had perhaps four minutes. Perhaps five if they argued about the ferry wreckage, which they would, because men always argue about wrecks — it flatters them to pronounce upon other people's losses.

The trunk sat at the foot of my bed, innocent as furniture. Its false bottom had been built by a cabinetmaker in Lyon who was paid too well and asked no questions, and it had survived two inspections already, but false bottoms have a grammar, and men who search for a living learn to read it: the way dust lies differently along a seam, the way a trunk sits a half-finger proud of the floor, the way a lock is oiled but its hinges are not. My trunk had been moved eleven times since I came to Val-Brume. Eleven times the dust had lied for me, and dust, I knew, was a liar with a short memory.

Below, drawers opened and shut. A voice read aloud the inventory of a widow's linen chest with relish, as though reading charges. Somewhere a dog began to bark and was kicked quiet.

I took off my nightgown and dressed in my merchant's-clerk clothes — waistcoat, plain collar, the ink-stained fingers that were my best disguise because they were real — and I lifted the ledger out of the trunk.

It was heavier than it had ever been. Not in weight; in meaning. Forty nights of candlelight lived in it now, my own annotations threaded through my mother's cipher like veins through marble, and beneath them the original pages, her hand, her fury pressed flat between covers that had crossed a republic to reach her daughter. I wrapped it in oilcloth, and the oilcloth in a sacking apron, and I looked exactly like what I claimed to be: a clerk carrying goods down to a cellar.

The window. The cooperage backed onto the lane behind the salt house, and from the loft the drop was eight feet onto frozen mud. Eight feet with forty thousand words in my arms.

I went over the sill the way Sabine had taught me to go down the riverbank — back to the wall, hands finding the seam of the boards, letting myself down joint by joint until my boots hit and my knees took the rest. The cold came up through the mud like a hand closing around my ankles. Above me, floorboards creaked under strange feet.

Then I walked. Not fast — fast draws the eye — but with the brisk, put-upon gait of a clerk sent on an errand, head down against the wind, ledger against my stomach beneath the apron so that any man who looked would see only a woman made shapeless by wool and grievance. Down the lane. Past the salt house. Past the well where old Madame Ferrand was drawing water and gave me the nod she gave everyone, which was no nod at all, just the smallest acknowledgment that we had both survived the night.

At the corner of the rue Basse I permitted myself one glance back. Two of Duvall's men stood in the cooperage doorway. One held up something to the light.

My spare shift. They had found my spare shift, and they were holding it up as though linen could testify.

I turned the corner and did not run, though every nerve in my legs screamed to run. Running is a confession written on the body. I walked until the lane bent, and then I walked faster.

* * *

The convent had been suppressed six years ago — the sisters dispersed, the chapel stripped, the bells taken down and melted for cannon that had probably missed whatever they were aimed at. It stood above the town on the shelf of rock where the road began its descent to the bridge, grey walls going green at the base, roof mostly sound, windows mostly not. The townspeople used it as a shortcut and a landmark and nothing else. No one prayed there anymore. But Corbineau had once mentioned — in that archivist's murmur he fell into when talking about records rather than people — that the convent sat over vaults older than the convent itself, burial crypts from the priory that preceded it, dry as chalk and cold enough, he'd said, to keep butter till Lent.

Cold enough to keep other things.

Sabine met me at the lych-gate, out of breath, having come the long way around the mill on the pretext of checking snares. Her cheeks were scarlet and her eyes were bright with the terrible joy she took in danger, which frightened me more than Duvall did, some days.

"They took the shift," she said.

"I know. I watched."

"Papa says let them. A shift is nothing. He told them you're a clerk and clerks hoard shifts because ink ruins them." She grinned. Then the grin faded, because she had seen my face. "Elodie. What's in the apron?"

"The original."

She went still. Wind moved through the dead nettles along the wall with a sound like whispering. "You're putting it *here*?"

"I'm putting it where their order doesn't reach. Think, Sabine. They can search lodgings because lodgings belong to the town and Duvall claims the town. But consecrated ground—" I stopped. Corrected myself. "Deconsecrated ground. Which means it belongs to no jurisdiction at all. It's a hole in the map. Every regime leaves holes in its maps; the trick is finding them before the mapmakers do."

"You've done this before."

"No." I pushed open the lych-gate, and its hinge cried out like something small being hurt. "But my mother did. That's how I know."

Inside, the chapel was a barn with pretensions. Straw lay in drifts across the flagstones where the pews had been pried up; pigeons had colonised the rood beam and regarded us with the flat insolence of squatters. The air smelled of birdlime and old stone and, beneath it, faintly, of incense that had soaked into the walls so deeply that six years of neglect could not burn it out. I felt it enter me — that smell — and do something to my breathing I did not authorise.

Sabine found the crypt stair behind the collapsed confessional, exactly where the geometry of the building said it must be: chapels face east, and east-facing chapels keep their dead below the sanctuary, nearest to God. The door was oak, swollen shut. We shouldered it together, twice, and it gave with a groan that scattered the pigeons like a shot.

Stairs went down into blue-black dark. Cold rose up the stairwell like water rising in a hold.

"Wait here," I said. "If anyone comes—"

"Nobody comes here." She was already lighting the stub of candle she'd brought, cupping the flame. "Elodie. Nobody comes here. That's the whole point of here."

So we went down together, which was against everything I had been taught — never take a witness, never share a burden — and which was, I understood even as I did it, the first rule of my new country breaking the first rule of my old one.

* * *

The crypt was a low groin-vaulted room, maybe twelve feet by twenty, its niches stacked like a wine cellar of the dead. Most of the coffins had rotted to shapes — a suggestion of boards, a suggestion of bones — and in the corner someone had piled the broken altar stones. The cold was total and ancient, a cold that had never once been interrupted by fire or sun. My breath hung in front of me like a small ghost refusing to move on.

Sabine held the candle while I knelt among the ruined niches and chose one near the floor, third from the end, where a slab had cracked and could be levered aside to make a cavity deep enough for a book and shallow enough to be forgotten. I worked the oilcloth tight. I wedged the bundle in. And then, before I slid the stone back, I stopped — because kneeling there in the dark with my mother's whole indictment in my hands, I found I could not let go of it.

It was absurd. It was a book. I had carried it across a mountain pass in a trunk full of wool samples. I had slept beside it for forty nights. And now my fingers simply would not open, and my mind presented me with the image, unbidden, of Duvall's chapped-handed men lifting my shift to the light, turning it over, examining the stitching — and of this stone slab, cracked as it was, being noticed by some pigeon-scared soldier on some future day, prised up, the bundle pulled out into daylight—

"Elodie." Sabine's voice, very quiet. "Your hands."

I looked down. My knuckles were white. The candle flame shivered.

"If they burn it," I said, and my voice sounded strange down there, flattened by stone, "it isn't a copy that's lost. It's her. Everything she did to write it, everything she risked to hide it, everything she—" I stopped. I had never said the next part aloud, not even to myself. "She didn't die in the purge, Sabine. She *survived* it. She survived it and stayed inside it, inside the machine, taking notes, year after year, knowing that every day she stayed might be the day they caught her. And then she got it out. To me. The one thing she spent on herself in her entire life was the belief that I would carry it."

"Then carry it," Sabine said. "Carry it right now, back up those stairs, and hide it somewhere else, if that's what your hands want. But hiding it twice in one morning means hiding it worse both times. You taught me that. First rule of the ice: commit to the crossing or don't step on."

I laughed. It came out cracked, but it came. "Did I teach you that?"

"You said it about the river. Same thing." She crouched beside me, and in the candlelight her face was suddenly older than fifteen, older than me. "Put her down, Elodie. She's waited six years in a trunk. She can wait a few weeks under a stone. And we'll come visit."

*I*. We. She had already made herself a party to it, and there was no undoing that, and I found — God forgive me — that it steadied me more than anything else had all winter.

I slid the stone back. The crack in it faced upward, honest-looking, ordinary. Kneeling dust and grit into the seam, I broke a dried nettle stem and scattered the fragments, and pressed a pigeon feather into the dust for the finishing touch that no searcher reads but every eye slides off. Then I stood, and my knees ached, and the cold had gotten into the marrow of my fingers and would not leave.

We climbed the stair. At the top I looked back once into the dark and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that the stone hadn't heard.

* * *

The cooperage, when we returned, wore the expression of a house after a fever passes.

Duvall's men had been thorough and had enjoyed being thorough. Drawers stood open like tongues. Aurel's tools had been laid out on the bench in a row — inspected, catalogued, replaced a finger's width from where each belonged, which for a craftsman is a kind of vandalism. My trunk sat in the middle of the loft floor with its lid up and its contents folded with military neatness, my shift draped over the edge like a captured flag. The false bottom was exposed, empty, its mechanism plain to the room's cold light.

Aurel stood beside it, arms folded, watching me climb the ladder with an unreadable face.

"Well?" I said.

"They counted your wool samples." He picked up one of the cards, turned it over, set it down. "Twice. The tall one wrote the number in a little book. Then the short one said a funny thing. He said, 'Funny kind of clerk, travels with more paper than wool.'" Aurel's eyes came up to mine. "I told them the Republic pays its clerks in paperwork instead of wages. The tall one laughed. The short one didn't."

"The false bottom—"

"He saw it. Of course he saw it." Aurel said it mildly, terribly. "He ran his thumb along the seam like a man reading a wife's letters. Then he looked at me, and I shrugged, and he wrote that down too. Empty compartments aren't proof of anything, girl. But they're proof of *something*, and he knows it, and I know it, and he knows I know it."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards into the frozen mud beneath. "How long?"

"How long what?" He began replacing his tools, one by one, each in its exact place, and I understood that this ritual was how he kept his hands from shaking. "How long before he stops writing things in little books and starts doing what the books are for? I'd say the ice gives you till the thaw." He set the last adze down. "Maybe less. Men like that get promoted by results, and he hasn't any yet. A hungry dog doesn't wait for the thaw either."

That evening Duvall himself came to the cooperage.

I heard his voice from the loft before I saw him — that courteous, unhurried baritone, the voice of a man performing civility the way I performed clerkhood, fluently and without believing a word of it. He apologised to Aurel for the inconvenience of the search. He praised the workmanship of the barrels. He remarked that the roads would open soon, and that travellers with unfinished business in Val-Brume ought to think carefully about finishing it before the soldiers came, because soldiers, unlike himself, were not patient men.

And then he said, lightly, to the room, to the ladder, to the loft where I sat very still with a pen in my hand and a page of honest wool figures before me:

"And my compliments to Mademoiselle Sandoval. I understand she keeps remarkably clean accounts. So few clerks bother." A pause I could feel through the floor. "One hopes she finishes them before the season turns. Unfinished accounts attract such *attention*, in my experience."

His boots crossed the workshop. The door closed. The latch clicked home like a period at the end of a sentence.

I sat in the dark of the loft for a long time afterward, pen dry in my hand, listening to the wind turn pages along the river ice. Below, Sabine was saying something to her father in a low voice, and Aurel answering, and the ordinary sounds of a family closing up a house against the night — and I thought: *this is what it costs*. Not the fear. Fear I could carry; I had been carrying it since Lyon. What I had not budgeted for was this — that the thing I hid would grow heavier the better I hid it, and that the weight would settle on everyone under whose roof it rested, and that Duvall had just informed me, with perfect courtesy, that he intended to collect.

I took out my little book of cipher and, by feel, in the dark, added a line to the margin where my mother's marks guided me — a date, and one word: *crypt holds. time does not.*

Then I blew out even the memory of the candle, and lay down, and listened to the town breathe, and tried not to hear, beneath the wind, beneath the watchman's bell, the far-off grinding of the ice on the river — the sound of winter loosening its grip, week by week, night by night, on everything I had hidden inside it.

Two days later, before dawn, boots came up the lane to the cooperage door, and this time the knock was not polite, and the voices were not Duvall's alone, and when Aurel opened the door they took him by both arms and told him, in the name of the winter commission, that there were questions about his ferry — and about cargo it had carried, years ago, that he had never declared.

They marched him past the window where I stood. He did not look up at me. That, more than anything, told me he had known this was coming, and had chosen, all along, not to let it show — so that I would sleep, and copy, and finish.

The door closed behind them, and Sabine came down the stairs white as the frost on the glass, and neither of us said the obvious thing: that the price of the record had just acquired a name, and a workshop, and a daughter.
