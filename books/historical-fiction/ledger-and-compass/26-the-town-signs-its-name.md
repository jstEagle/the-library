## 26. The Town Signs Its Name

Maître Corbineau had kept his registers locked for eleven days.

He told himself it was prudence. He was a man who mistook prudence for virtue so often that the two had become indistinguishable to him, like a face seen so long in a darkened glass that the glass and the face seemed one thing. The registers sat in their cedar cabinet beneath his counting room, forty-one volumes bound in grey calf, each one indexed in his own small hand, each one a complete account of Val-Brume from the year of the bridge fire onward: births, deaths, sales, seizures, oaths sworn and oaths withdrawn, taxes levied on a town that paid them because paying was easier than asking what the money bought.

Eleven days since he had signed the deposition of Aurel Vasse in the salt house, with Madame Liorans presiding and the ferryman's hands still ink-stained from the gaoler's ledger across the street. Eleven days since Corbineau had stood in front of his neighbors with his brother's name caught in his throat like a fishbone, and said aloud what he had certified with a lie fifteen years ago.

Since then, nothing. He had opened his office at the usual hour. He had stamped the usual papers — two land transfers, a marriage contract, an affidavit about a disputed goat. He had drunk his coffee and wound his clock and been, to all outward appearance, exactly the man Val-Brume had always known: precise, punctual, harmless.

But the cabinet stayed locked, and every night he lay awake listening for the sound of boots in the street, and every morning the boots did not come, and the not-locking became its own kind of cowardice, quieter than the first but made of the same material.

On the twelfth morning, Sabine Vasse came to his door before the coffee was poured.

She did not knock the way children knocked. She knocked the way bailiffs knocked — three measured blows, evenly spaced — and when he opened to her she stepped past him into the hall without invitation, shaking snow from her hood, and stood in the middle of his counting room as though she owned the deed to it.

"You haven't opened the registers," she said.

"Mademoiselle—"

"Aurel is still in the gaol. They feed him once a day now, not twice. My father noticed. My father notices everything." She pulled off her gloves finger by finger. "You signed your name once, Maître. One page, one witness. Everyone in this town knows it happened and everyone pretends they don't, which means everyone is waiting to see if you'll do it again or if you were only frightened that night."

"I was frightened that night," Corbineau said. It came out more honestly than he intended. He had meant to say something about procedure, about proper channels, about the danger to the town if the civic records were drawn into what was plainly becoming a contest between armed men. Instead he heard himself admit the plain truth, and once admitted, it would not be recalled. "I am frightened every night. That is not the same as being wrong."

"No," Sabine agreed. "It isn't. But you're keeping the truth locked in a cedar box while my father eats once a day, and I've come to ask you which frightens you more." She looked at the cabinet. "Open it."

* * *

The registers smelled of cedar and old iron-gall ink, and opening them was like opening a vein.

Corbineau carried them up to the long table in the public room, one volume at a time, and laid them open under the south window where the light came strongest. He had decided — no, he corrected himself; Elodie Sandoval had decided for him, three nights ago, standing in this same room with a deciphered page spread flat between them and her voice steady as a surveyor's line — that the records would not simply be shown. They would be *deposed*. Witness by witness, name by name, each testimony written out fair, read aloud to the deponent, corrected, and signed. Not rumor. Record. If the republic's men came for the town, they would find that the town had already spoken, formally, in the language of the law, and that un-speaking it would require burning the whole registry — and burning registries, Corbineau knew better than any man alive, left ash that could be read.

The first witness was the widow Marchand.

She was eighty-one and half-blind and she arrived at nine o'clock sharp in a cloak older than the republic, guided by her granddaughter, and when Corbineau asked her to state her name for the record her voice cracked on the second word and she wept quietly through her entire deposition without ever stopping her account.

"I saw the wagons," she said. "Ninth of Ventôse, year four. Four wagons going north on the mill road, and I counted the men on them because my eyes were better then. Eleven men tied. And at the tollhouse the officer — the tall one with the scar, the one they put on the commemorative medal after — he signed the waybill, and I watched him sign it, and the waybill said *voluntary transfer of detainees to the interior*, and those men were tied hand and foot, Maître. Voluntary." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "I never said anything. My husband said, keep your mouth shut, we have daughters. So I kept it. Forty years I have kept it. Write it down. I want my name against his."

Corbineau wrote it down. When he read the deposition back to her, her granddaughter held the paper close to the old woman's face so she could see her own signature space, and Widow Marchand signed with a hand that shook and did not hesitate.

By noon there were seven witnesses waiting in the street outside the notary's office, stamping their feet in the snow, and Corbineau understood that something had moved through the town overnight the way frost moves through ground — silently, everywhere at once.

* * *

They came all afternoon.

The smith, Grolleau, who had shod the requisition horses in '95 and could describe every brand burned into their haunches, and whose deposition established, horse by horse, the route of the grain convoys that the ledger recorded as *delivered to the western depots* and that had in fact gone, wagonload by wagonload, to the estates of the men who signed the orders. Grolleau spoke in a flat blacksmith's cadence, hammering each fact down as though driving a nail, and when he finished he asked Corbineau to read it twice and then said, "Put down also that I knew it was wrong and said nothing, because I wanted the work. Put that in the record too. A record with only heroes in it is a lie with better posture."

The schoolmaster, Pélissier, who had been ordered to strike certain names from the roll of pupils in year six — boys whose fathers had been taken — and had struck them, and had kept the original roll hidden inside the binding of a Latin grammar for fourteen years, and now set it on the table like a relic. His deposition took two hours because he insisted on reciting the struck names from memory first, all nine of them, before he would permit the register comparison to begin.

The innkeeper's son, who at fifteen had driven a cart to the tollhouse one February night and seen crates being unloaded from the seized boats and reloaded onto sledges with the customs seals already broken and new seals — a particular seal, a compass-and-quill — pressed over them fresh. Corbineau's pen stopped at the description of that seal. He knew it. Every man in this room knew it. It hung now above the hearth of the commandant's quarters, and it had hung there through three administrations, because the men who changed the flags never changed the seal.

Elodie Sandoval sat in the corner through all of it, taking a second copy in a hand smaller than Corbineau's, and she did not speak until the innkeeper's son mentioned the seal. Then she looked up, and met Corbineau's eyes across the table, and he saw in her face the thing he had spent fifteen years avoiding: that his little town's careful bookkeeping, the obsession his colleagues in the provincial capital had laughed at — *the notary of Val-Brume, who counts everything* — was not pedantry. It was ammunition. It had always been ammunition. It had merely waited, loaded and capped, for someone to pick it up.

"Page thirty-four," she said quietly, when the boy had gone. "Volume twenty-nine. The seizure inventory of the *Sainte-Anne* cargo. Your certification is on it."

"I know what's on it," Corbineau said.

"Then you know what comes next. When they compare your registers against the cipher, they will find your name on the false inventories too. Yours and no one else's. Every forged count in fifteen years carries your stamp." She said it without cruelty, the way a physician states a prognosis. "Duvall knows it. It's the lever he holds over you. Which means it's the lever you can take out of his hand — but only by putting it on the record yourself, before he does."

"That is what tonight is for," Corbineau said.

She waited. Outside, the light was failing and the queue of witnesses had thinned to three, and the snow had begun again, fat slow flakes that stuck to the window glass and melted and stuck again.

"Read it back to me," he said. "My deposition. Before I lose my nerve, which I give you fair warning I intend to do at least twice."

* * *

His own statement ran to eleven pages.

He had written it the night before, alone, with the good lamp trimmed and the bad lamp unlit, in the hand he used for nothing but final copies. He had written everything. The purge of year two, and how the order had come down demanding inventories of the arrested men's property, and how the inventories sent up from Val-Brume had been found insufficiently complete — *incomplete, they wrote, meaning honest* — and how a second courier had arrived with a private letter explaining that the families of insufficiently diligent notaries sometimes suffered transport accidents on the mountain roads.

His brother Étienne had been a deputy, a loud one, a man who laughed in the wrong meetings. The letter had not named him. It had not needed to.

"And so I certified the false inventory," Corbineau dictated, and Elodie wrote it, and the pen scratched in the quiet room like something small walking across paper. "And I altered the counts of coin, plate, and grain to match the figures provided me, and I affixed my seal, and the property went north, and my family lived. I do not offer this as excuse. I offer it as fact, entered into the record, so that the record may show how these things are done — not with violence, in the beginning, but with arithmetic. A man is not threatened. A man is *corrected*. And the correction is written in a fine hand, and filed."

He stopped. Elodie's pen stopped.

"Say the next part," she said gently. "It's the part that matters most."

He looked at her. She was twenty-some years younger than he and had lost more than he could reckon — a mother erased, a life of aliases, a trunk with a false bottom — and yet she sat there holding his confession like it was worth carrying, which no one had ever made him feel his guilt was. Worth carrying. Weighable. Part of the load.

"The next part," he said, "is that Étienne died anyway. In the spring. On a road, in an accident, exactly as promised for the insufficient, except that I had by then been sufficiently corrupt, and so his death served no purpose except to teach me that compliance purchases nothing. It only postpones. And the last part—" his voice went thin and he steadied it against the table edge, "—the last part is that I have known, for fifteen years, that the true inventories existed somewhere, because I made them. I made them both. The false ones I sent, and the true ones I kept. Volume thirty-one, pages sixty through seventy-eight. Sealed in wax, misfiled among the livestock assessments of year three, because no one in the history of administration has ever voluntarily opened a livestock assessment."

For the first time all day, Elodie Sandoval smiled — a quick, fierce, entirely unprofessional smile.

"You've had the evidence the whole time."

"I have had the *evidence*," Corbineau said, "and no courage, and the two together are heavier than either apart. Fetch volume thirty-one."

* * *

They worked past midnight.

Word of what was happening traveled the town the way lamplight travels — window to window, without anyone ordering it lit. By ten o'clock people had begun leaving things at the notary's door: letters, receipts, a christening gown with dates embroidered in the hem, a soldier's pay book, anything with a date and a name that contradicted the official story of any given season. Corbineau catalogued each item as it came, assigned it a number, wrote a provisional description in the day-book, and did not sleep.

At some hour after midnight, Sabine Vasse came back with two loaves of bread and the news that her father had eaten twice that day, because the baker's wife had walked to the gaol window personally and held the loaf up where the turnkey could see it was for Vasse and dared the man with her face to refuse it.

"The whole town's awake," Sabine said. "You can feel it. Like before a flood."

"Floods destroy," Corbineau murmured, sealing the day-book. "This is more like—" he searched for it, "—like spring. When the river decides, all along its length at once, that the ice is finished. No single crack breaks it. It simply stops agreeing to hold."

Sabine looked at him with sudden respect, the look of a girl revising an estimate upward. "That's what Madame Liorans says. Almost word for word."

"Then perhaps the judge and I finally agree on something," Corbineau said, "after forty years of finding her terrifying."

* * *

In the grey of the morning, with nineteen depositions signed and sealed and the duplicate copy dry, Corbineau stood at his window and watched the street.

The snow had stopped. The sky over the ridge had that hard clean look of deep winter mornings, blue-black at the top and burning pale at the edge, and the smoke rose straight up from every chimney in Val-Brume like a forest of thin grey trees. Somewhere down the lane, the cooperage hammer had started early. Across the square, the salt house windows glowed where Madame Liorans kept her hours.

And at the corner by the fountain, doing an extremely poor job of pretending to examine the ice in the basin, stood one of Duvall's men.

Corbineau had watched enough people watch things in his career to know surveillance when it stood in front of him wearing a borrowed coat. The man had been there an hour. Another, he noted, was warming himself opposite the church — also an hour, also motionless, also interested in nothing.

They had let yesterday happen. That was the arithmetic Corbineau could not escape, standing there with his breath fogging the glass. Nineteen signatures, taken in an open room with the shutters wide, and not one interruption — not one summons, not one polite knock. Either Duvall's reach had shrunk overnight, or Duvall had chosen to wait. Men who chose to wait were men expecting reinforcements.

Behind him, Elodie was wrapping the duplicate deposition in oilcloth, binding it the way couriers bind things, with the knot of a trade he pretended not to recognize.

"They're watching the office," he said.

"I know. They've been watching since midnight. One of them followed Sabine home and lost her at the tannery, which she will be insufferable about." She tucked the packet into her coat, against her body, where the cold would not crack the wax. "The question is whether they're watching to count us or to catch us moving."

"And if they're waiting for soldiers?"

"Then every hour matters," Elodie said, "and we spend none of them discussing it." She pulled her hood up, and paused at the door, and looked back at the long table — the forty-one grey volumes, the scattered loose papers, the nineteen signatures drying in their ranks like a field planted overnight.

"Maître Corbineau. Yesterday you asked me what a notary could possibly add to all this. This is what. Any fool can burn a town. But a town that has signed its name—" she touched the nearest register, lightly, almost affectionately, "—that town has to be erased. And erasure leaves evidence of the erasing. You've made Val-Brume impossible to delete."

She went out into the blue morning, and Corbineau watched her cross the square, and watched both watchers turn their heads to follow her — and saw, with a cold drop in his stomach, that they were not following her at all.

They were watching the cooperage.
