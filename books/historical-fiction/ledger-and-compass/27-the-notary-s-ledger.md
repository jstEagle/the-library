## 27. The Notary's Ledger

The salt house smelled of brine and tallow, and Maître Corbineau had turned half of it into a kingdom.

Elodie stood in the doorway the first morning and simply looked. Shelves rose from floor to ceiling along three walls, each one crowded with bound registers, their spines labeled in a hand so small and so even it might have been set by a printer: *Births, 1771–1789. Deaths, 1771–1794. Grain Requisitions. Bridge Tolls. Ferry Licenses. Militia Levies. Confiscations.* The last word was written twice — once in ink, once, years later it seemed, scratched over and rewritten as though the notary could not decide whether to name the thing plainly or soften it.

"You are staring," Corbineau said, without turning from his desk. He sat behind a slab of oak worn pale at the elbows, spectacles low on his nose, a goose-quill moving with the patience of a man who had never once hurried in his life.

"I'm counting," Elodie said.

"And how many?"

"Two hundred volumes. Perhaps more."

"Two hundred and thirty-one." He set down his quill and removed his spectacles, folding them with care. "My father began the collection. I have continued it. When the republic sent its commissioners to tell us what we were permitted to record, I recorded the commissioners." A dry note entered his voice, not quite humor. "They found it flattering. No one suspects a man who writes everything down of hiding anything."

"Because you hide nothing," Elodie said. "That is the trick."

"That, Madame Sandoval, is the entire architecture of my life." He gestured to the chair across from him. "Sit. You said you wished to compare pages. We will compare pages."

* * *

They had agreed on the method the night before, in the cooperage kitchen, with Aurel's mended nets drying by the stove and Sabine pretending to sleep in the loft above. It was Sabine who had insisted on the precaution, actually — the girl had a courier's instincts she'd never been taught.

"No originals leave this house," Sabine had said, arms crossed, fifteen years old and immovable. "Not the ledger, not the cipher key, nothing. Papa lost his ferry. Tomas died on the ice. If Duvall takes a page off you in the street, he takes everything."

"So we copy," Elodie had said.

"So we copy *twice*," Sabine had corrected. "One for the judge. One for somewhere no one has thought to look yet."

Now Elodie laid her deciphered pages on Corbineau's great oak slab — fair copies only, her own careful script translating her mother's cipher into plain French — and watched the notary put on his spectacles and begin to read.

She had expected him to be slow. She had not expected him to be *devastating*.

"Requisition order, eleventh Ventôse, Year Three," he read aloud, his finger resting beneath her transcription. "Four hundred sacks of flour taken from the valley mills, signed by Citizen-Commissioner Armand Vasselet, for the provisioning of the Army of the Alps." He looked up over the rims of his glasses. "Wait."

He rose, crossed to the shelves, and ran his finger along spines with the certainty of a man finding a book in his sleep. He pulled down a register bound in gray cardboard, blew dust from its cover, and opened it flat on the desk beside Elodie's page.

"*Grain Requisitions,*" he said. "Eleventh Ventôse, Year Three. Four hundred sacks, valley mills. Signed receipt by the miller Girard of Pont-Saint-Martin." His finger tapped the faded entry. "And here — my annotation, made at the time because the figure troubled me." He turned the book toward her. In the margin, in that same small even hand: *Mills reported 380 sacks capacity. Discrepancy unexplained.*

Elodie felt something move through her chest that she could only describe, later, as vertigo standing still.

"Twenty sacks vanished between the mill and the army," she whispered.

"Or four hundred were taken and eighty sold," Corbineau said. "Girard hanged himself the following spring. The commissioners called it despair over poor harvests." He took off his spectacles again, and this time he did not fold them; he held them loosely, turning them in his fingers like a man handling a weapon he wasn't sure he trusted himself to carry. "I have wondered for six years whether I imagined the discrepancy. Whether a clerk's arithmetic could really indict a commissioner of the republic."

"It can," Elodie said. "It does. Turn the page."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the morning became days, and the days built a structure neither of them had dared hope for.

Her mother's ledger gave the skeleton: dates, sums, names, orders passed between men whose signatures Elodie now knew better than her own heartbeat. Vasselet. Rougon. The young officer Sainte-Colombe whose statue stood in the capital's new square with laurels on his brow. Each page of cipher was a cold column of fact, stripped clean by a woman who had understood that adjectives could be disputed but arithmetic could not.

Corbineau's registers gave it flesh.

For every requisition, a local receipt. For every execution listed in the cipher — *Citizen Delorme, miller, shot at the crossroads, 3 Prairial, Year Two; goods inventoried* — a corresponding entry in Val-Brume's death register, and then, more damning than either, the confiscation inventory itself, certified by a deputy notary whose name Elodie recognized from her mother's margin notes.

"The inventories were false," she said on the third day, laying two documents side by side. "Look. The cipher says Delorme's goods included two horses, a wagon, and eleven hundred francs in coin. The official inventory lists one horse, no wagon, and forty-three francs."

"Because the difference went somewhere," Corbineau said quietly.

"Where?"

He didn't answer at once. He went instead to a shelf she hadn't seen him open before — a low one, near the floor, behind a stack of bridge toll records — and brought out a slim volume labeled, innocently, *Personal Memoranda, 1792–1796.*

"My brother's account book," he said. "Étienne kept a tavern on the valley road. He was not a political man. He was a man who noticed things, which amounts to the same crime." He opened it to a marked page. "Here. March, Year Two. 'Bought wine, cheap, from the deputy. He said it came from an estate cleared out up-mountain.'" Corbineau's voice did not change at all, and somehow that was worse than if it had broken. "Delorme's estate. Cleared out. Eleven hundred francs in coin becomes casks of wine sold at discount to a tavern keeper, and the deputy notary pockets the rest, and the commissioner signs his name to a fiction, and everyone is paid, and my brother asks one question too many at the wrong table."

"He's the one who vanished," Elodie said. "In the purge."

"In the *inventory*, Madame. My brother appears nowhere in any official record after March of Year Two. There is no arrest warrant, no sentence, no death certificate. He simply ceases." Corbineau closed the little book with both hands, as though closing a coffin lid. "I searched for him for two years. Then I understood that the absence of a record was itself the record. That is when I began writing everything down."

Elodie reached across the desk and touched the memoranda volume — just briefly, two fingers on the cover, the way one touches a grave marker.

"Then your brother is in this too," she said. "Page by page. Every false inventory your registers contradict puts another stone on the pile that buried him."

Corbineau looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes — grief, yes, but underneath it, unmistakably, a kind of terrible joy, the joy of a man who has carried a weight alone for six years and has just heard another person agree to lift one end.

"Then we had better be thorough," he said.

* * *

Thoroughness became ritual.

Each dawn Elodie crossed the frozen lane from the cooperage to the salt house with her deciphered pages sewn into the lining of her coat, and each dusk she returned with fresh transcriptions hidden in the hollow staves Sabine had bored into old barrels — a system the girl had designed with the same precision she used to read the ice, mapping every hiding place in a diagram she kept only in her head.

By day they worked in silence broken only by the scratch of quills. Corbineau read aloud in his flat notary's voice, and the flatness itself became a kind of music: *entry against entry, date against date, signature against signature.* Elodie wove the threads together in a master draft she began to think of, privately, as the weave — her mother's columns running warp, Corbineau's registers running weft, until no single thread could be pulled without the whole cloth showing the tear.

On the fifth day, the pattern closed around a name she had been circling since the first deciphered page.

"Sainte-Colombe," she said. "Every route. Every payment. Every execution order that needed a soldier willing to carry it out without questions — his countersignature. And in the margins, my mother wrote the same annotation eleven times." She recited it from memory; she could not help herself; it lived in her now. "*Paid in full. Paid in full. Paid in full.*"

Corbineau pulled down the militia levy registers, the pay records, the transport manifests. And there he was, in plain civic ink, exactly where the cipher said he must be: *Sainte-Colombe, escort duty, requisition convoy, 14 Germinal; escort duty, prisoner transfer, 2 Floréal; disbursed, special fund, 500 francs, authorization Vasselet.*

"Eleven times," Corbineau confirmed, marking each match with a small red cross in pencil. "Cipher says it; my registers say it. Two independent records, kept by two different hands, in two different places, agreeing to the day and the livre." He sat back and removed his spectacles, and for the first time since she had known him, Maître Corbineau smiled — a thin, cold, entirely satisfied expression, like frost forming on a window. "Do you know what this means, Madame Sandoval? It means it is no longer testimony. Testimony can be dismissed as malice, as error, as the grudge of a provincial clerk. This is *corroboration*. To destroy it, they would have to burn my two hundred and thirty-one volumes and your mother's ledger and every copy we have made, and even then—" he tapped the master draft "—even then, Madame Liorans holds a set, and a judge's memory is a very inconvenient thing to assassinate."

Elodie looked down at the growing stack of pages and felt the thing she had been afraid to feel since Val-Brume: hope, sharp-edged and heavy, like a coin with a blade's rim.

All winter she had carried the ledger like a coal in her chest — something that burned her, something she must protect or perish with. Now it was becoming something else. Not a secret. A *document*. Documents do not belong to couriers. They belong to whoever reads them, forever, and no chain on any bridge can call them back.

"We should number the pages," she said. "Both sets. So that if one is stolen or destroyed, the gap shows. A missing page proves tampering as surely as a present page proves guilt."

Corbineau raised his eyebrows. "You think like an archivist."

"I was raised by one," Elodie said, and did not explain, and he did not ask, and in the silence between them lay the whole story: a woman who had written an indictment in cipher while serving the men she indicted, and had died — or not died — for it, leaving her daughter a trunk with a false bottom and a language all their own.

* * *

Sabine came for her at dusk on the sixth day, as arranged, stamping snow from her boots in the salt-house doorway with more force than the weather required.

Elodie read her at once. The girl's face was composed — too composed, the way it went when she was carrying news like a full bucket she refused to spill.

"Walk me home," Sabine said.

They walked. The lane between the salt house and the cooperage was blue with evening shadow, the eaves dripping in the brief false warmth of the afternoon, refreezing now in glassy spikes. Halfway along, Sabine spoke without looking at her.

"There's a man lodging at the Gilded Pike. Came in with the last mail before the blizzard. Says he's a wool factor waiting out the season."

"Wool factors wait out seasons," Elodie said evenly. "I was one."

"Yes. But you learned the ice paths and asked about the ferry schedules and bought a good coat. This one drinks alone, tips too much, and yesterday asked Papa — very casually, over cards — how many people use the back stairs of the cooperage." Sabine stopped walking and finally turned. In the dying light her eyes were bright and hard as river stones. "Papa laughed and said nobody, because the back stairs don't exist. But Elodie — he *asked about the stairs*. Nobody asks about stairs that don't exist unless someone told him they do."

The cold moved up Elodie's spine, patient and familiar. She thought of the master draft lying under the loose floorboard in Corbineau's office. She thought of the staves in the barrels. She thought of Tomas, dead on the ice with a duplicate key in his pocket, and of her handlers' orders, reread and understood at last: *the courier is part of the cargo.*

"Someone talked," she said. "Or someone watched."

"Does it matter which?"

"No." Elodie resumed walking, forcing her legs to stay slow, unhurried, the walk of a wool merchant's clerk going home to supper. "It only matters that we finish before they act. How many pages remain?"

"Of the weave? You'd know better than me."

"Nine," Elodie said. "Nine pages of cipher left to reconcile, and then the fair copies for the judge and the printer." Nine pages, and then the year would turn, and the thaw would come, and every force in the country — loyalist, republican, and the arriving soldiers Duvall had summoned — would come down the opened road into Val-Brume like water through a breached dam.

"Then we work faster," Sabine said.

At the cooperage door, Aurel was waiting with a lamp, and beyond him, up the dark stairwell, Elodie could hear the lodgers moving — the ordinary sounds of an ordinary house, one of which, perhaps, was listening.

She climbed the stairs with her coat buttoned to the throat, nine pages of her mother's handwriting warm against her ribs, and did not look back.
