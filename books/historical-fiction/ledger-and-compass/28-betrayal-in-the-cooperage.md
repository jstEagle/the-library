## 28. Betrayal in the Cooperage

The snow had stopped sometime before dawn, and Elodie woke to the particular silence that followed it — the muffled, listening quiet of Val-Brume under a fresh white shroud. She lay in her narrow room above the cooperage and counted the sounds she knew by heart: Aurel's boots on the yard stones as he went to see what the frost had done to his half-built ferry; the groan of the great vats settling in the cold; Sabine's voice below, low and quick, arguing with her father about whether the ice at the second bend would hold a sledge.

She had learned to count these sounds the way other people counted money. They were her assurance that the house still stood, that its people still moved through their ordinary morning, that nothing she carried — or had buried, page by patient page, in the dark beneath the town — had yet cost them anything.

For three weeks she had copied the ledger. Every evening after supper, when the cooperage lamps were doused and the Vasses went up to bed, she sat with her mother's cipher pages spread before her and wrote out the indictment in a fair hand no one could trace to the original: requisitions signed by men whose statues now stood in the capital; payments to informants; dates and names of the executed, entered in a neat column like so many bushels of grain. She made two copies. One set she sewed into oilcloth and hid in the false bottom of her trunk, where any searcher would find it and believe they had found everything. The other — the true copy, the one she trusted — went into the barrel staves.

It was Aurel who had shown her how. A good cooper could split a stave, hollow a channel the width of a finger down its length, and glue it shut again so seamlessly that not even the hoop-maker who'd raised the barrel would know. Forty-one staves, forty-one pages, distributed across eleven casks stacked in the far bay among the vinegar works, where the smell alone discouraged curiosity. *No one robs a vinegar merchant*, Aurel had said, with the first smile she'd seen on him since the ferry burned. *Not even in a famine.*

She had believed, until this morning, that the plan was sound.

The mistake was not in the hiding. The mistake was Gaspard Rieu.

Rieu had lodged in the cooperage's third room since October — a tinsmith from Ambert whose workshop had failed, he said, whose wife had died, he said, who paid his rent promptly in coin that never quite rang true and kept to himself in the way of a man with nothing left to hide. He was pleasant at supper, useful with small repairs, forgettable in every particular. That was precisely why he had frightened her, later; but fear is a luxury of hindsight, and in December Elodie had been too tired, too stretched between Duvall's dinners and Corbineau's registers and the candle burning down beside her cipher each night, to give more than a passing thought to the quiet man downstairs who always seemed to know when the household slept.

He knew now. He had known for some time.

* * *

She heard the knock on the cooperage doors just past eight, while Sabine was still braiding her hair by the kitchen fire.

Not a knock — a blow. Three strikes of something heavy against oak, spaced with the deliberate rhythm of authority rather than urgency. Then a voice, carrying the polished courtesy that Elodie had come to dread more than shouting:

"Master Vasse. Open in the name of the Commission for Winter Traffic."

Aurel's boots crossed the yard without hurry. Through the floorboards Elodie heard the bar drawn back, the door swing, the cold pour in.

"Agent Duvall." Her landlord's voice was flat as slate. "What does the Commission want of a cooper?"

"A search. Nothing personal." A pause, the sound of several sets of boots entering, stamping snow. "You have lodgers."

"You know I do. You've asked me twice before."

"And your answer has been satisfactory both times. Circumstances change." Duvall's voice moved into the main room; she pictured him removing his gloves finger by finger, setting them on the table as though he owned it. "There has been an incident at the salt house. Papers missing from an official cache. I am authorized to turn over every building in Val-Brume, beginning with those that house strangers."

Elodie was already moving. The trunk came out from under the bed; her fingers found the hidden seam; the oilcloth packet with the decoy pages went back where it belonged, arranged exactly as it had lain for weeks, dust resettled along the seam with a paintbrush bristle she kept for the purpose. The cipher key — her mother's key, the thing she could not replace and would not surrender — she pressed flat between the lining of her bodice and the whalebone stay, where it sat against her ribs like a second heart.

Then she made herself stop. Made herself stand in the middle of the room and breathe. Panic searched rooms badly; panic knocked things over. Whatever happened in the next hour, it would happen to a wool merchant's clerk named Madelaine Ferrand, who kept accounts for her uncle in Clermont and had been stranded since the bridge closed and wanted nothing in this world except the thaw.

Below, the raid began.

* * *

They turned the cooperage inside out with terrible efficiency. She heard it all from above: the scrape of furniture across boards, the clatter of tools swept from the bench, Aurel's voice rising once — once only, in anger — when a soldier drove a bayonet into a finished cask and vinegar flooded across the floor. Sabine's footsteps darted along the hall toward the stairs and were intercepted.

"Not you, mademoiselle," said a man's voice, not Duvall's — one of his sergeants, the one called Bressac, who had a way of smiling that never reached his eyes. "Stay where you are, if you please."

"I live here."

"Today everyone stays where they are."

Elodie knelt at her window, behind the curtain's edge, and watched the yard. Six soldiers. Duvall in his grey coat, directing with small movements of one hand, unhurried as a man inspecting his own orchard. And near the gate, apart from the others, hands in his pockets, watching the upstairs windows rather than the search —

Gaspard Rieu.

He did not look away when she saw him. He looked at her window deliberately, for a long moment, and then he took his hands from his pockets and folded them, and inclined his head — barely, the smallest courtesy, a bow one might give across a card table.

Her blood went cold and then hot. He had sold them. Not betrayed in passion or terror — there had been no raid until now, no pressure, no gun at his throat. He had watched for weeks, noted the light burning late above the cooperage shop, noted the trips to the vinegar casks, perhaps even found a loose stave or seen the glue-shining seam — and then walked, calmly, on some grey afternoon, to the salt house, and traded the information for whatever Duvall paid for such things. Coin, or passage out before the thaw, or simply the pleasure of being on the winning side. It didn't matter which. What mattered was that the face she had passed at supper a hundred times, the man who had complimented her stew and fixed the latch on the privy door, had weighed a family's safety against his own comfort and found the arithmetic easy.

*And you*, said a cold voice in her that sounded uncomfortably like Duvall, *have done the same arithmetic yourself. You weighed these people against your ledger the day you arrived.*

She silenced it. There was a difference — there had to be a difference — between spending oneself and spending others. But the voice did not entirely go away, and it would be weeks before it did.

Boots on the stairs. Two at a time. She rose, smoothed her apron, opened her door before they could break it, and stood waiting with the expression of mild, put-upon patience she had practiced since Lyon.

"Madelaine Ferrand?" Duvall filled the stairwell, Bressac behind him. Up close he smelled of cedar and ink, absurdly clean for a winter campaign. "Forgive the intrusion."

"My room has been intruded upon twice already this winter, Agent. I'm beginning to feel flattered."

"Search it," he said to Bressac, and then, to her, pleasantly, "You'll want to watch. Guests are often anxious about their belongings."

"I'm anxious about my trunk lining. Your last men cut it open and I had to mend it myself."

Bressac went through the room like a flood through a mill: mattress split, floorboards sounded with a knife hilt, the trunk emptied onto the bed with a violence meant to frighten. The false bottom yielded the oilcloth packet. Duvall unwrapped it himself, standing by the window, and read.

Elodie watched him read and let nothing move in her face. Inside her, everything screamed. These were the copies — the decoy set, the ones she had written in a hand deliberately stiffened, deliberately flawed, with small errors seeded through the figures, errors that would make any expert declare the documents forged. If Duvall had the eye to see the errors, she was finished. If he did not, he would carry away proof of her guilt and believe it victory.

He read for a long time. The paper trembled very slightly in his fingers — with rage, she thought, or with triumph; from the window's distance she could not tell which.

"Well," he said at last, softly. "Well, well. Clerk Ferrand."

"Those are private papers—"

"They are copies of a ciphered ledger concerning military requisitions of '95 and '96." He rolled the packet closed with great care. "Which a wool clerk keeps in a false-bottomed trunk. In a cooperage. In Val-Brume." He smiled at her, and it was the worst smile she had ever seen on him, because it held no anger at all — only satisfaction, the deep settled satisfaction of a man whose patience has finally been rewarded. "I wondered about you from the first week. The hands, mademoiselle. Wool clerks have cracked skin. Yours are a copyist's hands."

"Agent Duvall—"

"Bind her," he said.

But Bressac had scarcely crossed the room when Duvall lifted one finger, and stopped him, and stood listening.

Everyone listened. Below, in the yard, Sabine Vasse was shouting.

* * *

Elodie saw it from the window, and for the rest of her life she would see it exactly that way — framed, frozen, lit by the flat white glare off the snow, like a scene cut from a play at its most unbearable moment.

Sabine had gotten past the sergeant holding her. Later Elodie learned how: a elbow to the ribs and a handful of ash from the hearth thrown into his eyes, tricks learned from a childhood among dockmen and smugglers. She came out of the cooperage door like weather, like fury, sixteen years old in her father's old coat with her braid half-finished hanging down her back, and planted herself in front of Duvall's horse — Duvall had brought his horse, Elodie realized; he had known this raid would end in departure — and shouted at him in a voice that carried to every window in the square:

"She's done nothing! Whatever you think you've found, it isn't hers — it isn't anyone's — you can't take her, my father will have the law on you, Maître Corbineau will—"

"Sabine!" Aurel lunged from the doorway and two soldiers caught his arms and wrenched them back, and the big ferryman made a sound Elodie had never heard from him, a sound like timber going.

Duvall descended the steps slowly. He did not draw a weapon. He did not need to. He simply walked toward the girl until he stood close enough that she had to tilt her head back to keep shouting at his face, and then he said something to her, quietly, too low for the windows to catch — and whatever it was, it stopped Sabine's voice mid-word.

Elodie saw the girl's shoulders change. Saw the fury drain out of her and something worse arrive in its place: understanding. Sabine turned her head, deliberately, and found Elodie's window, and looked at her — one long look, unreadable from that distance, accusation or absolution or both — and then Duvall took her arm, not roughly, almost gently, the way one takes the arm of a guest one intends to honor, and led her toward the horse.

"No—" Elodie was moving before she knew it, down the stairs, through the vinegar-stinking shop, out into air so cold it seared her throat. "Take me instead! The papers are mine — mine alone — the girl knows nothing, the family less—"

"I know precisely how much they know." Duvall paused with his hand on the saddle. Snow was starting again, fine and dry, ticking on the leather. Behind him, held by two men, Aurel had gone terrifyingly silent, and silence from Aurel Vasse was a more frightening thing than any shout. "That is rather the point, mademoiselle. I could arrest you today. I could put you in a cell in the salt house and interrogate you until spring, and perhaps learn where the rest of your papers are, and perhaps not — couriers are trained to die slowly and say little; your handlers were thorough people." His eyes went to the packet under his arm, then back to her, and there was something almost intimate in his expression, almost kind. "Or I can keep the daughter of the ferryman as my guest. Comfortably. Fed, warm, unharmed — provided certain documents find their way to me. All of them. The originals, not these clever counterfeits — yes, mademoiselle, I know a forgery when I hold one; I have spent this entire war surrounded by forgeries. You have three days. The ice at the second bend holds until Thursday, and after that we shall all have a long, dull winter together, and I shall begin to be less courteous."

"This is not law," Elodie said. Her voice shook and she hated it. "Even by your own commissions, hostage-taking—"

"My commissions, mademoiselle, are exactly as lawful as yours." He mounted, drew Sabine up before him — she did not struggle; she had gone rigid and pale and terribly composed, and that composure frightened Elodie more than screaming would have — and gathered the reins. At the edge of the square he turned the horse and looked back once, not at Elodie but at the cooperage as a whole, the smoking chimney, the broken cask bleeding vinegar into the snow, the whole modest edifice of the life she had sheltered in.

"Three days," he said. "Merry Christmas, should you still observe it."

They went down the street toward the salt house, and the townspeople who had gathered at their doors — Madame Fouchard with her market basket, the two Delaunay brothers, old Piquet with his broom half-raised — parted before the horse and closed behind it, and no one spoke, and the snow fell into all the footprints equally, effacing everything.

* * *

They got Aurel inside eventually. Elodie did not remember the walk back up the stairs or the minutes that followed; her body performed them without her. She remembered coming back to herself sitting on the floor of the Vasse kitchen, her back against the cold stove, with Aurel across from her in his chair by the dead hearth, his huge hands hanging between his knees, staring at the floorboards as though the answer to something were written in the grain.

"I told you," he said at last, and his voice was gravel dragged over stone. "The day you came up my stairs with your wool samples and your soft clerk's manners. I said to myself, trouble follows paper the way gulls follow fish. And I let you in anyway." He looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely dry. "Because Sabine liked you. Because she hadn't laughed like that since her mother died. So if there is blame in this house, woman, understand that I claim the largest share of it."

"There is no blame in this house." Elodie heard her own voice come out steady, and marveled at the lie of it, the fluency of it, how easily the performance ran even now, even with her chest full of broken glass. "The blame is mine and Duvall's, and only ours."

"The tinsmith," Aurel said. "Rieu. Where is he?"

She hadn't thought of it. Neither, evidently, had Duvall bothered to collect his informant — or perhaps Rieu had slipped out ahead of the raid, wanting no part of the aftermath. They found his room empty: bed stripped, tin box gone, and on the bare table, squared neatly to the edge, the little brass latch he had repaired for them in November, polished bright, laid out like a tip left for service rendered.

"He watched us for two months," Aurel said, turning the latch over in his palm. "Two months, eating at my table."

"He watched because someone pays him to watch. This wasn't greed or spite — it was his trade. He's Duvall's instrument the way I was the republic's." She said it and felt the truth of it land somewhere beneath her ribs, cold as the river. "We are all instruments. That is what this century taught us. Instruments don't apologize."

"Mine doesn't get to philosophize either," Aurel said, and set the latch down with a click that ended the conversation. He rose, took his coat from its peg, and stood a moment with his back to her, a broad silhouette against the grey window. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — quieter, stripped, the voice of the man who had poled a ferry through flood seasons for twenty years and knew exactly what a river cost.

"Thursday," he said. "The second bend. You'll go to him with whatever you decide to carry, and I cannot tell you what to carry, because I am not a wise man and never pretended to be. But I will tell you one thing." He shrugged into the coat. "My daughter walked into that square this morning for your sake. Not for the ledger, not for the republic, not for justice — she couldn't spell *justice* in cipher if you gave her a week. For you. So whatever else you trade on that ice, Elodie Sandoval, you will not trade her. Do you hear me? Whatever it costs. Whatever it burns. Not her."

He went out. The door closed. The kitchen was silent except for the tick of the cooling stove and, faintly, from the street, the sound of the town talking in lowered voices, spreading the news from doorstep to doorstep the way water spreads under ice — everywhere at once, visible nowhere.

Elodie sat a long time with her hand pressed flat against her bodice, against the cipher key that lay over her heart like a blade laid flat on a table.

Three days. The original ledger slept in the convent crypt beneath thirty feet of frozen ground, and the convent's abbess owed her nothing, and Duvall would be watching every path to it. The decoy copies were in his possession and would buy her perhaps one expert's examination — a day, no more. Corbineau's registers could indict an empire but could not free a girl from a horse. Madame Liorans had writs, and writs were paper, and this morning had proven what paper was worth against a man with six soldiers and no scruples.

And beneath all of it, rising like groundwater, the guilt: *your cause costs others*. Tomas dead on the ice. The ferry burned. Aurel's livelihood in ashes and now his child in a hostile house. The town's peace cracked open like the cask in the yard, vinegar and snow and shame soaking into everything. She had told herself for months that the ledger was worth any price because it was the truth, and truth was owed to the dead. But the dead did not sit in Duvall's kitchen tonight, rigid with terror, composing her face into courage for the benefit of her captors. The living did that. The living paid in coin the dead never saw.

Elodie got up. Washed her face. Bound her hair. Took out pen and paper, and began — because discipline was all she had left, and discipline was her inheritance from a dead woman whose handwriting she knew better than her own — to write.

Not a plea. Not terms. A letter to Madame Liorans, laying out every fact of the morning: the informant, the raid, the seizure, the deadline, the names of the soldiers. Dated. Signed with her true name, the first time she had written it since Lyon. If Thursday went wrong, if the ice took her or Duvall's pistol did, then this page would exist, in a judge's keeping, in a notary's registry, in a town's memory. Whatever happened on the second bend, it would happen on the record.

Outside, the snow went on falling, filling the streets, filling the footprints, filling the world — patient as a ledger, white as an unwritten page.

Three days. Somewhere across the town, in the salt house, Sabine was counting them too.
