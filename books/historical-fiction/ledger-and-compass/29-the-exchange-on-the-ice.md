## 29. The Exchange on the Ice

Sabine had learned the ice the way other children learned prayers.

Her father taught her before her mother died, back when the river was still a thing that carried boats instead of carrying people on foot. *Listen first,* he said, kneeling beside her on the black glass of a December morning, his mittened palm flat against the surface. *The ice talks. Thin ice sings high. Thick ice says nothing at all.* She had been six years old, and she remembered thinking that the river was like people — the dangerous ones were always talking, filling the air with noise, and the safe ones kept their own counsel.

Now she stood in the cooperage doorway at first light and listened to the crossing sing.

It was not singing. That was the trouble. The thaw had come early to the shallows below the mill weir, and where the current ran fast beneath the crust, the ice went hollow-voiced, a low drumbeat when the wind pressed it. Three days ago she could have walked that stretch blindfolded with a barrel on her shoulder. Today it was a liar's surface, pretty and white, hiding black water that moved at the speed of a galloping horse.

And tomorrow morning, if Duvall kept his word — and Duvall kept no words, only accounts — Elodie would walk out onto it to buy her back.

Sabine pulled the door shut behind her and went down through the sleeping town to find out whether anyone but her understood what was about to happen.

* * *

They had kept her two nights in the salt house, and Duvall had been courteous the whole time, which was the worst part.

He had given her a chair by the stove. He had given her bread and cheese and watered wine, served on a tin plate by one of his men with an apologetic shrug, as though the man were embarrassed to be part of the play. He had asked her questions in the tone of a schoolmaster asking a clever student to show her work. Where did the courier keep her papers. What did she know of the convent crypt. Had she ever seen the inside of Maître Corbineau's strongroom.

She had answered nothing, which was easier than lying, because lying well was a skill she was only beginning to have and she could feel it changing the shape of her mouth.

On the second night Duvall had sat across from her for a long while, turning his signet ring with his thumb, and said: "You know what I find remarkable about this town? Everyone in it is brave in exactly the way that costs them least. The ferryman won't inform because informing would shame him before his neighbors — that costs him nothing, since I already intend to ruin him regardless. The notary certifies whatever he's told to certify, because he has done it once and so must do it forever or admit the first time. And you—" he had leaned forward, firelight on the wet stone of his eyes, "—you won't talk because you think silence makes you loyal to someone. It doesn't, Mademoiselle Vasse. Silence just means you haven't yet met the person you'd trade it to."

"I've met you," Sabine had said. "I wouldn't trade you a dead dog."

He had laughed — genuinely, she thought, with real pleasure — and stood, and brushed off his coat, and said, "Then we'll see what your friend trades."

That had been last night. This morning they had walked her to the bridge chains and let her see the message nailed to the toll post, written in Duvall's own neat hand: *The courier will present herself at the crossing at dawn tomorrow, alone, carrying the ledger. The girl goes free when the book is in my hands and the courier is under my authority. No ledger, no exchange; the girl remains a material witness.*

*A material witness.* Sabine knew what happened to material witnesses. Her father's ferry had burned over less than what she knew now.

So she went looking for Elodie, and found her where Sabine knew she would be — in the crypt-cold back room of Madame Liorans's winter lodging, at a table strewn with paper, copying.

* * *

Elodie looked worse than the night the second courier died. Sabine had catalogued her face over the winter the way she catalogued everything — the set of her shoulders when she lied, the small tic at the corner of her jaw when someone touched her trunk unasked, the way she went very still and very polite when she was most afraid. Politeness was her tell. Nobody else in Val-Brume seemed to notice that the more courteous Elodie became, the closer she was to violence or grief.

This morning she was extremely polite.

"You shouldn't be here," Elodie said, not looking up. "If Duvall's men see you out before the exchange—"

"They're not watching for me. They're watching for you." Sabine shut the door and put her back against it. "I need to say something and you're going to listen, because everyone in this town keeps deciding things for everyone else and I am finished being decided about."

Elodie set down her pen. Ink had stained the side of her right hand, a dark crescent along the smallest finger, and Sabine thought absurdly that she would remember that crescent all her life, whatever life turned out to be.

"Say it," Elodie said.

"He's going to kill us both." Sabine made herself say it plainly, without drama, because drama gave people somewhere to hide. "You've read his letters. You know how he writes — every promise phrased so it can mean its opposite. 'Under my authority' isn't freedom, it's custody. Custody of a courier who knows what the ledger proves, plus custody of the ledger itself, equals one hole in the river ice and one story he tells Paris about a tragic accident at the crossing. He doesn't want an exchange. He wants witnesses, plural, removed, singular location. The ice is doing his work for him. He won't even have to dirty a knife."

For a moment Elodie said nothing. Then, quietly, with that terrible calm: "I know."

"You *know?*"

"That's why I've been copying since midnight. That's why the duplicate set leaves tonight — not with me, with someone else, by a route Duvall can't predict." Elodie's hand flattened on the pages. "I'm not walking onto that ice expecting to shake it, Sabine. I'm walking onto it because if I don't, he keeps you indefinitely and comes for the ledger anyway, and then everything we've done — Corbineau's registers, the depositions, the judge's writ, Tomas dying on the ice with the key in his coat — all of it was for nothing. If he takes me, at least he thinks he's won. A man who thinks he's won gets careless. A careless man can be beaten in front of witnesses."

"By whom?" Sabine heard her own voice crack and hated it. "You'll be in the river by noon."

"Not if you tell me the route." Elodie looked up then, and there was something in her face that stopped Sabine's breath — not fear, and not resolve exactly, but the fierce attention of a woman reading a page she intended to memorize. "You know this crossing better than any living soul. You traced Duvall's men to the cache when three grown men couldn't. So I'm asking you not as the woman buying your freedom, but as one courier to another: where is the ice honest, and where does it lie?"

* * *

They spent the day preparing, and it was the strangest day of Sabine's life, because they prepared her death as carefully as her rescue.

Duvall's terms allowed one concession: the family might attend the crossing, at a distance, to witness the girl's release. Aurel took that concession and turned it into a weapon, moving through the town with his burned hands wrapped in wool, saying almost nothing, and by evening half of Val-Brume had a reason to be standing near the bridge at dawn. Corbineau brought his registry clerks with their ledgers under their arms, ostensibly to record a lawful prisoner transfer, actually to make certain that whatever happened on the ice happened in front of forty pairs of eyes and a legal record. Madame Liorans sent word that she would attend, in her furs, seated in a chair carried down by two of Corbineau's men, and that she expected the proceedings to be conducted according to forms she intended to name aloud.

But the true preparation happened in the back of the cooperage, among the barrel staves, where Sabine knelt on frozen sawdust and drew the river in spilled flour.

"The channel runs deep here, under the old ferry landing — that ice is a foot thick even in thaw, it sings low and holds. But from the landing to the far pilings, the current bends around the weir pool and the ice is shell ice — white on top, hollow beneath. It'll bear a child. It won't bear a man with a satchel full of lead-weighted paper." She dragged her finger through the flour. "Duvall will want you to cross toward the far bank, because that puts you on the bad ice where he controls both approaches. Refuse. Insist the exchange happen at the landing itself, close enough to the crowd that his riflemen can't shoot clean. He'll agree, because agreeing costs him nothing — he plans to drown you wherever you stand."

"And you?" Elodie asked. "Once I'm taken?"

"Once you're taken, I go downriver on the mill race path with the copies. Papa stows me in a flour barrel on the ice-road sled — Gaspard drives, and Gaspard owes Papa nine years of ferry favors. The sled passes Duvall's pickets twice a week hauling grain. They never search it, because searching it would slow the mill and the mill pays the garrison's bread." Sabine sat back on her heels. "You made me learn the routes. You said the student becomes the courier. I didn't understand what you meant until it was this."

Elodie was quiet a long moment. In the lamplight her face was all planes and shadow, and Sabine saw her swallow once, hard, the way you swallow when grief arrives too early to be permitted.

"If I get free," Elodie said finally, "I'll come down the race path after you. Give me a mark. Something I'd know, that Duvall wouldn't."

Sabine thought of winters, of her mother's hands, of the first code Elodie had ever taught her, scrawled in flour on this same floor. "A compass rose," she said. "Drawn in charcoal on the third bridge piling, north point filled solid. My mother used to draw them on the ice with her boot heel when she was bored waiting for Papa. You'd only know that if I told you. So if you see it, it's me telling you I'm alive."

"A compass rose with a solid north point," Elodie repeated. Then she reached into her bodice and drew out the scorched page — the single leaf saved from the burning at the mill, its edges black as lace — and folded Sabine's cold fingers around it. "Keep this. Not because it's safest with you, though it is. Because if I drown, someone in this valley has to still be holding proof. You held it once when you followed the smoke. Hold it again."

Sabine closed her fist around the brittle paper and felt it crackle like a small live thing, and understood suddenly what Elodie had understood all winter: that being trusted with the truth was heavier than being trusted with anything else, and that the weight did not crush you so much as forge you.

"I'll hold it," she said. "Come take it back from me yourself."

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, the worst kind of dawn for listening.

The town gathered along the near embankment in a loose dark line — Aurel standing straight as a mast with his bandaged hands, Corbineau clutching his registry to his chest like a breviary, Madame Liorans enthroned in her carried chair with a face carved from judgment itself. Somewhere among them, sewn into the lining of a grain sled bound for the valley road, were forty-one pages of copied indictment, and Sabine lay against them in the dark of the barrel, breathing the flour-dust sweetness, listening through a knothole.

Elodie came down to the crossing alone, as agreed, wearing her gray traveling cloak, the ledger in a canvas satchel slung across her body. From the knothole Sabine watched her stop at the edge of the good ice and stand very still, and she knew — she *knew*, the way she knew the river — that Elodie was listening to it. Reading it. Hearing where it sang and where it kept counsel.

Duvall came out from the far side with four men, two ahead and two behind, and stopped at the boundary of the landing ice. Even at this distance his voice carried flat and clear across the frozen water, pitched for the crowd.

"Madame Sandoval. Or should I say clerk of Messrs. Aubert et Fils, wool merchants, of Grenoble?" A smile in it. "Your papers are irregular. Your presence in this jurisdiction is unauthorized. Fortunately for you, I am authorized for everyone. Bring the satchel to the middle of the crossing, place it on the ice, and step back ten paces. When my man confirms the contents, the girl walks to you and you both walk to the town, and I ride out within the hour. Those are the terms. They are generous. Do not test them."

"The middle of the crossing is shell ice," Elodie called back, and her voice did not shake, and Sabine felt an absurd burst of pride. "You'd have me sink with your evidence before your man touches it. We conduct this at the old ferry landing, in view of the town, per your own posted terms — *in view of the town*. Or does Agent Duvall renegotiate his own agreements?"

A pause. Wind moved the smoke from the town chimneys sideways. On the sled, in her barrel, Sabine pressed her eye to the knothole and prayed to nothing in particular.

"The landing, then," Duvall said at last, silk over gravel. "Since the courier insists on an audience."

They came together on the thick ice above the deep channel, twenty paces apart, then ten. One of Duvall's men stepped forward for the satchel; Elodie unbuckled it slowly, theatrically, and set it on the ice between them, and stepped back the ten paces as ordered, her boots ringing on the black surface.

From the far side, two of Duvall's men released Sabine's guards and walked her out — no, not walked, *marched*, gripping her arms, and she made her legs do the work and kept her eyes on Elodie's face because that was the only fixed point left in the world. Ten paces. Five. Close enough now to see the ink-stain crescent on Elodie's hand, the pulse jumping in her throat, the terrible politeness gone entirely out of her expression and replaced with something naked and fierce that Sabine would carry longer than any scar.

"Now walk," Duvall called to her. "To the town. Don't run. Running cracks ice."

Sabine walked. Behind her she heard the satchel open, heard Duvall's voice drop into satisfaction, heard him say softly, almost tenderly, "Hello, Mother's little book"— and she was three steps from the embankment when the sound came.

It was not loud. That was the horror of it. A sound like a giant knuckle cracking under the world, low and wrong, and then the whole landing shivered, and the crowd screamed all at once, and the ice between Elodie and the satchel — between Elodie and Duvall's men — opened its white mouth.

Later, Sabine could never assemble the sequence properly. There was the crack, and there was the black seam racing out from the weir pool like a struck match, and there was Elodie — Elodie going down on her knees, sliding, catching the seam's edge with both hands while Duvall shouted contradictory orders and one of his men floundered waist-deep in the sudden hole where the satchel had been, the ledger's canvas strap vanishing into the current like a snake into grass. There was the crowd surging against Corbineau's clerks, there was her father's roar, there was Madame Liorans on her feet in her carried chair with her arm thrust out like a drawn blade, her voice cutting through everything:

"*Hold! Hold, in the name of the writ! Agent Duvall — you will produce lawful commission before any man moves on my authority, or by God and the Code I declare this crossing under judicial seal!*"

And there — closest of all, seared forever into the flour-and-fear dark behind Sabine's eyes — there was Elodie's hand slipping from the ice-edge, and the black water taking her gray cloak, and Sabine screaming her name into the wind as the river closed over the woman who had taught her that thin ice sings high and honest ice says nothing at all.

The last thing Sabine saw, before strong arms dragged her back from the edge, was the empty hole in the world, steaming gently, and Duvall's face across it — no longer smiling, no longer courteous, calculating at terrible speed what a drowned courier and a lost ledger would cost him, and whom he would blame.

Then, downstream, faint under the wind: a splash. A gasp. A gloved hand catching the root-ball of the fallen alder at the weir's tailrace.

Someone had taught the river's lies to someone else, after all.
