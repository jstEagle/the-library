## 31. The Hearing in the Salt House

They convened the hearing in the salt house because it was the only building in Val-Brume large enough to hold a town and too cold for anyone to pretend at comfort.

Elodie arrived before dawn, escorted by two of Madame Liorans's hired men — former soldiers, she guessed, from the way they walked — and found the great room already transformed. Sacks of salt had been dragged against the walls to make a kind of amphitheater floor. A trestle table stood at the far end with three chairs behind it: one for Madame Liorans, one for Maître Corbineau, and one, conspicuously empty, that no one explained. Lanterns hung from the curing beams on iron hooks, their light falling yellow on frost-furred stone. The air smelled of brine and old wood smoke and the breath of a hundred people who had come in from the dark.

She had not slept. She had spent the night with Corbineau's registers spread across the cooperage floor, cross-referencing dates against the pages of her mother's ledger until the candles drowned in their own wax. Sabine had brought her bread and coffee at midnight without being asked, set them down beside her elbow, and said only, "Eat, or you'll shake when they look at you." Elodie had eaten. It was the sort of instruction her mother would have given.

Now she stood at the edge of the salt sacks with the scorched page folded inside her glove and her heart beating like something trying to get out of a locked room.

Duvall arrived last, and he arrived as a performance. He wore his grey coat brushed clean, his boots polished, his commission case under one arm like a hymnal. Two men flanked him — the same hard-faced pair Elodie had seen at the mill burning — and behind them came Aurel Vasse, walking between a third guard with his wrists bound in front of him and his chin up, as though he were attending a wedding rather than serving as leverage. Elodie's stomach turned over when she saw him. Aurel caught her eye and gave the smallest shake of his head: *don't do anything foolish on my account.* It was the most fatherly thing she had ever seen him do.

"Madame Liorans." Duvall bowed from the neck, precisely deep enough to be respectful and precisely shallow enough to remind everyone he was not obliged to be. "I must protest this proceeding before it begins. There is no lawful court in Val-Brume. You are retired."

"I am wintered," Madame Liorans said. She sat at the center chair like a blade in its sheath — small, silver-haired, wrapped in a shawl that might have belonged to her grandmother, and utterly unimpressed. "And you are a stranger invoking emergency powers in a jurisdiction whose records I can read. Sit down, citizen. The writ is archaic but it is not dead, and neither am I, though I suspect both disappointments are related."

A ripple went through the crowd. Someone laughed and strangled it into a cough.

Corbineau took his seat with his ledgers stacked before him in a tower of green spines, and Elodie saw that his hands trembled — not with fear, she realized, but with something worse and better: the shaking of a man about to do publicly what he had spent years doing privately, and hating himself for the difference.

"The matter before us," Madame Liorans said, "is narrow. Citizen Duvall claims authority over all traffic, persons, and papers in this valley by virtue of commissions issued in Paris. Citizen Vasse stands accused under those commissions. Before any man in this town is carried out over the ice, we will examine whether the paper that condemns him is worth the ink. That is all this is. Proceed."

* * *

Duvall was good. Elodie had to give him that, watching him work the room the way she herself worked rooms — the easy posture, the reasonable tone, the small jokes offered to the back rows so that the townspeople would feel included in his confidence rather than subjected to it.

"Citizens," he began, "I understand your unease. These are frightened times. But order is not tyranny. I hold commissions signed by the Committee itself—" he produced the case, opened it with a flourish "—empowering me to secure this valley against enemy correspondence. Every courier I have stopped carried proof of the danger. Ask yourselves what passes through your town on the frozen river while you sleep."

He let the silence do its work. Elodie felt the crowd lean toward him the way crowds always leaned toward certainty, and understood suddenly how much of her own life had depended on that lean.

Then Madame Liorans said, mildly, "May I see the commissions?"

He passed them up. She read them the way Corbineau read inventories — slowly, twice, running one finger beneath each line. The salt house grew so quiet Elodie could hear the lantern flames eating their oil.

"Witness Corbineau," she said at last. "You keep the civic registers of this commune."

"Thirty-one years, madame."

"You are familiar with the form of official instruments — their seals, their serial notations, the manner in which dispatches are countersigned for transit?"

"I am, madame. It is my trade."

"Then examine these."

The moment stretched as Corbineau took the documents. Elodie watched his fingers move over the paper — feeling it first, the way an archivist feels a page before reading it — and she thought: *he is checking the rag content. Winter dispatches travel on heavy stock.* Her mother had taught her that. Her mother had learned it somewhere, from someone; the chain of small knowledges ran back through the ledger and through the purge and through every name in it, and here it ended in a salt house in Val-Brume, in the hands of a compromised notary choosing, line by line, to be worthy of his trade.

"These are well made," Corbineau said at last, and there was something almost gentle in it, which made it crueler. "Very well made. The seal is a good copy — pressed from an impression, not drawn, which takes skill. But." He turned a page. "The serial notation here, *Comité de Sûreté, série B, numéro 114*. I certified a transit register in October listing série B through numéro 96. Numbers above 96 were not yet issued when this document purports to be dated. I did not know it then. I know it now, because I spent last night with my own registers open, checking."

"You could have miscounted," Duvall said pleasantly.

"I never miscount," Corbineau said. "It is the only virtue I possess, and I have been saving it."

Someone in the crowd did laugh then, and could not fully stop.

"There is more," Corbineau continued, steadier now, climbing onto his own evidence as though onto solid ground after years of marsh. "Official commissions bear a countersignature for routing — the clerk who logs the instrument out of Paris. This one bears the name *Levêque*. I corresponded with a Levêque at the Ministry for nine years, on questions of vital statistics. He signs with a hooked final letter, like this—" he drew it in the frost on the table with one finger "—and he has done since 1791. This signature is straight-tailed. Whoever forged it knew the name but not the hand. And finally, madame, the watermark. Hold the second page to the flame-light."

Madame Liorans lifted it. The lantern behind it threw the paper's ghost into visibility: a faint shield-and-anchor, the mark of a papermill near Lyon.

"That mill," Corbineau said, "supplied royalist presses in the south. Republican instruments are printed on Paris stock. Whoever made these bought his paper from the wrong side — which suggests either remarkable ignorance or a certain irony I do not think he intended."

The room turned. Not all at once, but the way ice shifts on a river — a groan, a settling, a long crack running through the crowd's attention until it reached Duvall standing alone at the center of it.

* * *

"They're forgeries," Elodie heard someone say behind her, an old woman's voice, flat with wonder. "The man's papers are forged."

"He's still got soldiers," someone else muttered, lower.

That was true, and everyone knew it. Law had entered the room, but law was thin, and Duvall still had men with muskets at the door and a prisoner in chains and the particular composure of a man deciding, in real time, how much the truth was going to cost him.

He decided it would cost nothing yet.

"Forged," he repeated, smiling, spreading his hands. "By whom, citizens? By a notary who admits certifying false inventories? By a woman—" his eyes found Elodie across the room, and the smile warmed by degrees into something intimate and awful "—who arrived in your town as a wool merchant's clerk and is, I believe, neither? Shall we ask her what she carries in her trunk? What she buried under the convent floor? What her mother wrote?"

The temperature of the room changed. Elodie felt every eye swing to her, felt the crowd's sympathy curdle into curiosity, and understood that this was his real weapon — not the commissions, which were lost, but the suggestion that everyone in the room had something to hide, and that if the digging started, it would start everywhere.

"Perhaps we shall," Madame Liorans said. "In orderly fashion. Witness Sandoval, come forward."

Her legs carried her. She was distantly proud of that. She crossed the salt-strewn floor past Aurel, past Sabine standing rigid against the wall with her fists closed, and took the witness place between the table and the lanterns, where the light was strong enough to show every face in the room to her and hers to every face.

"Do you carry documents?" Madame Liorans asked.

"Yes, madame."

"Are they yours?"

"No."

"Whose, then?"

"A dead woman's," Elodie said. "Mine, in the way that a child inherits. They are a record of requisitions, seizures, and executions conducted in this valley and three others, with names, dates, and payments. Some of the men who signed them are now called liberators. I did not write it. I was sent to deliver it to people who intended me not to survive the delivery. I have since read it, copied it, and compared it against the registers of this commune, where it agrees page for page. I am not here to save myself. I am here because a record that dies with its courier dies entirely, and I decline to be its grave."

Silence. Somewhere in the rafters, ice cracked and shifted.

Duvall moved first. "This is confession, madame judge! Unlicensed correspondence, possession of seditious—"

"It is testimony," Madame Liorans said, "given voluntarily to an officer of the old courts, in the presence of the commune's registrar, which makes it, whatever else it is, *on the record*. You may note the difference, citizen. It will matter later." She looked down at Elodie. "You say the record agrees with the registers. Show us one instance. One page, one entry, one agreement. We are not trying the republic tonight. We are weighing paper."

Elodie drew the scorched page from her glove.

She had meant to save it — meant to keep it hidden until the copies were safe — and for one long breath she stood with it in her hand and weighed exactly that. Then she unfolded it and laid it on the table before the three chairs: brown-edged, heat-warped, half its lines eaten by fire, but the central column legible. A requisition of grain from three valley communes, dated the previous spring. An authorization for seizure signed with a name the whole country knew and sang in its cafés. And below it, in a clerk's cramped hand, the destination of the seized grain: *transit south, per Agent D.*

"Maître Corbineau," Madame Liorans said. "Your registers. Spring, last year. Grain levies."

He was already opening the green spine, already turning pages, and Elodie watched him find it — watched his finger stop on the line — and saw something move through the notary's face that was not triumph but its opposite: the grief of a man whose meticulousness had finally cornered the thing he'd spent years helping to hide.

"Twelfth of April," Corbineau read aloud. "Requisition of wheat, oats, and salt-pork from the communes of Val-Brume, Roche-Plate, and Saint-Aubin-le-Haut. Receipted locally by my own predecessor's office. The quantities match the burned page to the hundredweight." He looked up. "But the grain never entered the communal stores. I noted the discrepancy at the time. I was told it had been rerouted for the army. I recorded the explanation." His voice went quiet. "I recorded the explanation, madame. That is what complicity looks like in a registry. It looks like neat handwriting."

No one spoke. The lanterns burned. Elodie thought of her mother writing these entries in some cold safehouse, knowing no court would ever read them, writing anyway — and felt the inheritance land in her chest like a weight she had been carrying for months without knowing its name.

"The grain went south," Elodie said. "To be sold. The proceeds appear in the ledger's payment columns, disbursed to officers of the occupation. The signatures include two men currently honored in the Pantheon's orbit and one whose statue stands in Grenoble. Your town fed them. My ledger says who ate."

"Allegations," Duvall said, but the word came out thinner than he wanted it to.

"Numbers," said Corbineau. "Allegations are yours, citizen. Mine are numbers."

* * *

It went on for another hour, and Elodie lived every minute of it the way one lives a fever — vividly, painfully, unable to look away.

Duvall fought. He was skilled and he was desperate, and desperation made him nearly honest: he attacked Corbineau's certification of the purge inventories — "ask the notary what he signed to save his own skin" — and Corbineau, white-faced, answered, "I did sign them. I have confessed it to this town already, and I will carry it. But a false inventory certified in fear does not make a true commission forged in ambition. Examine the difference. One of us lied to survive. The other lied to command."

He attacked Elodie — her alias, her trunk, her nights of candlelight — and Madame Liorans cut him off each time with the same mild sentence: "Relevance, citizen. Weigh the paper or sit down."

He attacked the court itself — "you have no jurisdiction, this pantomime binds no one" — and here Madame Liorans rose for the first time, small and straight-backed, and the room rose with her.

"No jurisdiction," she agreed. "None whatsoever. No republic, kingdom, or committee has granted me authority over this valley, and I claim none. What I claim is older and less convenient: that in the absence of lawful power, free persons may examine evidence in open assembly, record what they find, and bind themselves to act on it. Call it a hearing, call it gossip with minutes — call it what you like. But understand what happens next, citizen. If your commissions are genuine, produce the issuing office's confirmation when the roads open, and I will apologize before this whole town. If they are false, then everything you have done here — the arrests, the seizures, the burning at the mill — was done by a private man with private soldiers, and this commune will remember it as what it was."

"And what is that?" Duvall asked softly.

"Tyranny," said Madame Liorans, "in miniature. Which is how it always begins."

She turned to the room. "I put the question. Has this assembly examined the instruments presented by the man Duvall, and found them false?"

"Yes," said the salt house, a hundred voices at once, and Elodie felt the word go through her like warmth through frozen hands.

"Does this assembly find the testimony of the witness Sandoval, corroborated by the registers of Maître Corbineau, worthy of record?"

"Yes."

"Then let it be entered. Maître — enter it. Every word. Sign it yourself, and let any citizen who wishes add their name beneath. Let this town do what it does best." Her eyes swept the room, and something almost like humor touched them. "Count everything."

They came forward in ones and twos and then in a press — farmers, coopers, the innkeeper, women with shawls over their hair, the blacksmith with his burn-scarred hands — signing Corbineau's register by lantern light while the guards at the door looked at their boots and Duvall stood very still in the center of the room, doing arithmetic. Elodie watched the names accumulate and thought: *this is what she wanted. Not revenge. Accumulation.* Her mother's ledger had been one hand in the dark. Now it was a hundred.

Aurel's bonds were cut by the blacksmith before anyone thought to object, and no one objected.

* * *

Afterward, in the churned snow outside the salt house, the town dispersed in clusters, talking in the low urgent voices of people rewriting their understanding of the last month. Sabine found Elodie and gripped her arm hard enough to bruise, saying nothing, which said everything.

"We won," Sabine whispered.

"We testified," Elodie said. "Winning is what happens afterward, and afterward hasn't happened yet."

She was looking at Duvall. He stood apart from his men by the salt house wall, taking the news from a runner who had slipped in late — a boy with river ice in his hair, come up from the valley road — and Elodie watched the agent's face as he read whatever the boy carried, and watched the composure settle over him again like a mask going back on. But underneath it she saw the recalculation finish. A man who had lost the argument for legitimacy did not surrender. He changed currencies.

He looked up and found her across the snow, and he did not glare, which frightened her more than glaring would have. He inclined his head slightly — acknowledgment, courtesy, *until next time* — and turned to speak quietly to his two men.

"What is he doing?" Sabine breathed.

Elodie was already counting. His guards. The boy gone. The direction of the convent hill, black against the stars.

"He just learned something," she said. "And whatever it was, it wasn't defeat." Her hand closed around the ghost of the scorched page in her glove. "Sabine — run home. Wake your father. Wake Corbineau. Tell them the original is under the convent, and tell them I think he knows it too."

The bells of Val-Brume rang the hour — four strokes, thin and cold — and down in the dark by the frozen river, unhurried, a man who commanded nothing now but violence began giving orders.
