## 32. Duvall's Counterstroke

The hearing had not even finished its last cup of wine when the news came.

Elodie heard it first as a sound — boots on the salt house stairs, too many of them, moving with the particular rhythm of men who had been told to hurry and were enjoying it. Then the door opened without a knock, and a young man in Duvall's livery stood in the doorway with snow melting off his shoulders and something like triumph poorly concealed behind his breathlessness.

"Agent Duvall's compliments," he said. "He regrets he was called away on urgent business."

Madame Liorans did not rise from her chair at the head of the improvised table. She had spent two hours dismantling Duvall's commissions before the town's eyes — Corbineau's registers open beside her, the forged seals laid out in a row like dead birds — and she looked now at the messenger the way she had looked at the forgeries, with a patience that was worse than anger.

"Urgent business," she repeated. "At nine o'clock at night."

"The agent said you would understand, madame. He said to tell you that the law is a fine thing in a warm room, but that winter does not wait on procedure."

And then he was gone, boots receding down the stairs, and Elodie understood before anyone spoke. She knew it in her body before her mind caught up — a cold dropping through her chest like a stone through ice.

*The crypt.*

She was already standing when Madame Liorans said it aloud: "He is going to the convent."

* * *

They went through streets that had not yet finished celebrating. The hearing had been a victory — the first one, real and public, forged papers exposed before forty witnesses — and Val-Brume had poured out of the salt house giddy with it, as if naming a lie were the same as killing it. Now Elodie ran through those same celebrants, Sabine beside her, Corbineau wheezing behind, and the town's joy curdled around them into confusion and fear. Someone laughed. Someone else shushed them. A woman pressed against a wall with her child and watched them pass with the expression of people who had just remembered that victories could be reversed.

The convent stood above the town on its shelf of rock, black against the snowfield, its suppressed chapel roofless these six years, its cloister walls furred with ice. The crypt beneath it was older than any republic or kingdom; the nuns had buried their dead there since before Val-Brume had a name. And three nights ago, wrapped in oilcloth and hidden behind a collapsed stair, lay the original ledger — her mother's hand, every page of it, the thing from which all copies were only echoes.

Elodie had moved it there during the search of the lodgings. It had felt like safety then. Stone, cold, consecrated ground no soldier would think to profane.

She had forgotten, in her exhaustion, the simplest arithmetic: Duvall had been in the room when Corbineau described the town's records corroborating "the document." He had watched her face. He had lost the hearing, and a man who loses everything in a room does not slink away — he goes to take back the only thing that matters.

They came up the switchback path and saw the light before they saw anything else. Lanterns at the convent gate. Not two, not three — eight, ten, carried by men moving among the ruins with the unhurried efficiency of an occupation.

"No," Sabine said. It was not a word; it was a wound. She had led Elodie to the crypt through the ice tunnels, had learned the convent's bones the way she knew the river. "No, they can't—"

"Stop." Elodie caught her arm. "Sabine. Stop. Look."

Down in the cloister yard, torchlight swung across broken arches. Men were digging where there was nothing to dig — theater, Elodie thought, a search staged for anyone watching — while two others descended the exterior stair toward the crypt door with crowbars and a lantern between them. And apart from all of them, at the edge of the light, stood Duvall himself, hatless in the falling snow, looking up at the ruined chapel as though appraising a property.

Corbineau arrived, hands on knees, breath steaming. "We cannot go down there," he whispered. "There are too many. If we rush them, we lose, and if we lose, he takes us too, and then there is no one left to testify."

"He'll burn it," Elodie said. Her voice came out flat, strange to her own ears. "That's what he does. That's what they do. You don't carry evidence out of a country that's watching — you destroy it and call the ash order."

"Then we watch," Corbineau said miserably. "We witness. It is the only weapon left tonight."

So they crouched behind the low wall of the sheep pen above the path, three figures in the dark with the snow gathering on their shoulders, and watched.

It took the men less time than Elodie expected. The crypt door had been sealed with a hasp and padlock — her own precaution, her own stupidity; a lock announces a treasure — and the crowbar made short work of it. The sound carried up the hillside, wood splintering, iron tearing free, obscene in the quiet. Then lantern light sank below the earth, and the night held still, and Elodie found she had stopped breathing.

When the men came back up, one of them carried the oilcloth bundle under his arm like a sack of meal. No ceremony. No reverence. The ledger that contained executions and requisitions signed by liberators, the ledger written in her mother's hand — passed from one pair of gloved hands to another and set down in the snow at Duvall's feet.

Duvall knelt. Even at this distance Elodie could see him unwrap the oilcloth with care, turning pages, checking — verifying, she realized, that it was what the hearing had accused him of hiding. He stayed kneeling a long moment. When he rose, he said something to the man beside him, and the man laughed.

"They're taking it to the mill," Sabine breathed. "No — the guardhouse. They're—"

But Duvall gave other orders entirely. Two men shouldered the bundle and started, not down toward the town, but along the ridge path east, toward the old signal post above the river gorge. And Duvall remained with the rest, and sent a runner back down the way they had come, fast, toward the valley road.

"What is east?" Corbineau asked.

"Nothing," Sabine said. "The signal tower. It's a ruin. There's a stove in it from when they used to—" She stopped. Elodie saw it land on her at the same moment it landed on Elodie.

"A stove," Elodie said. "He's going to burn it himself. Up there. Where the smoke won't reach the town, where no one can stand in front of the fire the way I stood in front of the fire at the mill." Her hands had gone numb inside her mittens; she could not tell whether it was cold or fury. "And the runner — he's sending for the soldiers. The ones coming with the thaw. He means to have them here before the year turns."

Corbineau made a small sound, half prayer, half arithmetic. "Soldiers take three days from the valley fort in this season. Four, if the drifts hold. The thaw is—"

"Nine days away," Elodie said. "I know. I've counted every one."

Below, the lanterns were extinguishing one by one. Duvall's party split: some back toward the town, some after the ledger-bearers along the ridge. Duvall himself stood alone a moment longer in the ruined cloister, and then — Elodie would swear this to the end of her life, though no one else could confirm it — he turned his face upward, toward the sheep pen, toward the dark where they hid, and inclined his head. A small bow. An acknowledgment between professionals.

Then he walked away, and the snow closed over the place where he had been.

* * *

Back at the cooperage, Aurel Vasse met them at the door with a lamp held high, and behind him the whole household seemed to be holding its breath. Word had traveled — word always traveled in Val-Brume faster than any courier — and the lodgers stood in the common room wearing the expressions of people who had backed a horse and watched it fall at the first fence.

"It's true, then," said Gautier, the tanner's factor, the one whose eyes had been too busy all winter. "He has it. Everything you risked, and he simply walks in and picks it up."

"We have the copies," Elodie said.

"Do you? Do you actually? Or do you have paper and hope?" He spread his hands. "I have children in this house's debt structure, madame. I supported your cause because it seemed to be winning. Tonight it does not seem to be winning."

"Gautier—" Aurel began.

"No. Hear me. The agent will have soldiers within the week. The judge's writs are worth the parchment they're inked on when there are thirty muskets in the street. I am asking, politely, that whatever remains of this business be conducted somewhere other than over my head."

"You may leave," Aurel said quietly. "The road is chained, but the ice is not. No one in my house stays by force."

Gautier looked around the room, found no allies, and went upstairs with his dignity arranged carefully around him. His wife did not follow immediately. She paused at the foot of the steps, looked at Elodie, and said nothing — but her eyes went briefly to the ceiling, toward the rooms, and then to the door, and Elodie understood that the betrayal, when it came, might come from that quarter too, and that the woman was warning her in the only currency she had left.

Later, in the cold loft above the cooperage floor, Elodie took inventory like a merchant facing ruin.

The original: gone, in Duvall's hands, bound for a stove in a ruin where no one could interfere. The copies: one set complete, hidden in barrel staves that Gautier now knew the location of, or suspected, or could be persuaded to describe. The second set, entrusted to the plan already forming — Sabine's route down the frozen river — not yet begun. Corbineau's registers: intact, but registers are only powerful when there is a court to read them into, and courts require soldiers not actively hunting the judge. The scorched page from the burning at the mill: still sewn into her bodice, small as a hymnal page, proof of deliberate destruction — and now, ironically, about to have its argument repeated on a grander scale.

And the calendar: nine days until thaw. Soldiers perhaps four days out once summoned. Which meant the window — the whole window in which truth could outrun power — had narrowed to something like three days.

Sabine climbed the ladder and sat down across from her, cheeks still red from the hill, jaw set in the way Elodie had learned meant a decision already made.

"The ridge path to the signal tower passes the widow Marchand's pasture," Sabine said. "It's a two-hour climb in daylight. Less if you don't care about breaking your neck. He won't burn it tonight — too dark, and the stove in that tower hasn't drawn properly in twenty years; my father says the chimney backs up. He'll wait for morning. Men like him always wait for morning; they like their destruction to be orderly."

"And?"

"And there's a second way into that ruin." Her eyes were bright and terrified and absolutely certain. "The ice cave under the gorge. The one I showed you. It comes up behind the tower foundation. The nuns used it — there are steps cut in the rock, old ones. If someone went up tonight—"

"No."

"You didn't let me finish."

"I don't need to. It's suicide, and worse, it's pointless. Even if you reached it, the ledger will be guarded, and even if it weren't, you cannot carry it down a cliff face in the dark."

"*You* could," Sabine said. "You crossed the bridge the day it closed. You've done harder things than this all winter. And I'm not saying we steal it back tonight — I'm saying we look. We find out how many guards, where they sleep, whether the stove really draws. You taught me that. Know the ground before you trust it."

Elodie looked at her — seventeen years old, frost-nipped, fearless in the specific way of people who had never yet lost something they couldn't imagine living without — and felt the full weight of what this winter had built. She had come to Val-Brume trusting no one. She would leave it owing everything to a ferryman's daughter who mapped ice the way scholars mapped heaven.

"Not tonight," Elodie said at last. "Tomorrow, before first light. Both of us. We look, we count, we come back. And Sabine — if it comes to choosing between the ledger and yourself, you choose yourself. Swear it."

Sabine didn't answer. Down in the cooperage, Aurel's hammer rang once against a stave, mending what could be mended, and outside the snow kept falling on the town that counts everything, and somewhere east along the dark ridge, in a ruined tower above the frozen gorge, her mother's ledger waited for the morning's fire.

Elodie sat down at the table and began, by candlelight, to draw the ridge path from memory — every bend, every boulder, every place a body could hide — because tomorrow they would go up the mountain after it, and this time there would be no judge's writ, no crowd of witnesses, no law at all.

Only the ice, the dark, and whoever got there first.
