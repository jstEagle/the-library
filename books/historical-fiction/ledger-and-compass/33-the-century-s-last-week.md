## 33. The Century's Last Week

There were six days left in the century when Elodie spread the last copied page across the cooperage table and let herself, for one breath only, feel it.

Forty-one pages in her mother's cipher, transcribed into plain French in two hands — hers and Corbineau's — in duplicate. Forty-one pages of requisitions signed by men whose faces now stared down from official prints in every prefecture in the republic: *Liberators of the South, Benefactors of the Mountain Departments.* The ink was dry on both sets. The wax seals Corbineau had pressed over his notarial attestation were cooling on the windowsill like small red wounds.

Then she counted what remained to be done, and the breath went out of her.

"A printer," she said. "A press that will set this type before New Year's Day. In the valley."

"Roussillon has one," Corbineau said. He had aged ten years since he had opened his registers to her, or perhaps he had simply stopped pretending otherwise. "Old Marchand. A jobbing printer — almanacs, bills of lading, funeral notices. His politics are..." The notary's mouth worked. "His politics are whatever keeps his press from being confiscated. Which is to say he is exactly the man we need, because no one watches him."

"He is also forty versts away," Elodie said, "down a river that has been frozen since November, through country where Duvall's soldiers are already marching with the thaw."

The word *thaw* sat in the room with them. Outside, the eaves of the cooperage dripped in the midday sun and froze again at dusk; each day the ice on the Val-Brume crossing grew a little more treacherous, a little more hollow at its edges, like a promise going bad.

"The thaw opens the bridge road in eight days at the earliest," Aurel said. He stood by the door with his arms crossed, a ferryman without a ferry, and there was a hardness in him now that had not been there before the arrest, before the town had stood up for him in Corbineau's parlor and refused to let the charges stick. "The soldiers come by the bridge road. Your copies go by the ice. You have a week, maybe less, where you can move faster than they can."

"And after that?"

"After that," Aurel said, "the river belongs to everyone, and no one outruns anyone."

Elodie looked down at the two stacks of paper. Identical. Forty-one pages each, wrapped in oilcloth, thin enough to be carried against the body, light enough to drown with.

Two couriers, she thought. Her mother had always insisted on duplicates. *A record that exists once does not exist,* Marguerite Sandoval used to say, sharpening her daughter's pencil with a knife, back when the codes were games played across a kitchen table. *Ink is cheap. Memory is cheaper. But paper survives fire better than memory survives fear.*

She had thought, all these weeks, that she understood the ledger. She understood it now in her bones: it had never been meant to reach a ministry. It was meant to be multiplied.

"We need a second courier," she said, and hated the sentence even as she said it, because she knew which name would rise in every mind around that table, and she knew it had risen first in her own.

* * *

They argued about it until the candles burned to their sockets.

Corbineau argued for himself — he knew the valley, he knew Marchand, he could carry papers under a notary's immunity that might still hold if anyone bothered to respect it. Aurel argued that a notary's disappearance would be noticed within a day and investigated within three, whereas a girl going downriver to visit an aunt was nobody's business. Elodie argued for hiring a stranger, some carter heading down for the winter markets, and watched both men look at her with the same expression: *you know better.*

She did know better. Strangers sold out lodgers in the cooperage for thirty francs and a kind word. Strangers were how couriers died in ditches with their throats open and their cargo confiscated.

Which left Sabine.

"I'm not asking her," Elodie said finally, flatly, as if saying it could make it true. "I've cost this family enough. The ferry. Aurel's week in the lockup. Tomas died on your ice because of what I carry—"

"Tomas died because men like Duvall burn things," Sabine said from the doorway.

No one had heard her come in. She was nineteen years old, frost in the wool of her hood, cheeks red from the river path, and she had that look Elodie had learned to dread and admire in equal measure — the look of someone who had already decided something and was merely informing the room.

"You've been listening," Aurel said.

"I've been listening since October." Sabine came forward and put her hands flat on the table, on either side of the oilcloth packets, claiming them the way she claimed everything: directly, without apology. "I know the river better than any of you. I've walked the ice every winter since I was nine. I know where the current runs shallow under the crust and where the springs keep it rotten clear through February. I know which farmhouses will take a traveler in and which ones sell travelers to the garrison for brandy money. And I am the fastest walker in Val-Brume, which Madame Liorans herself said last summer, and she doesn't flatter."

"Sabine—" Aurel began.

"You want to send Papa," she said to Elodie, ignoring him entirely. "Papa, who is known to every customs man between here and Roussillon, who has been arrested once already this season, whose face is in Duvall's notebook. Or Maître Corbineau, whose signature is on half the documents we're trying to publish, so that any clerk who sees him will guess what he carries inside an hour. Or you." Her eyes came to Elodie's and held there, unblinking. "Except Duvall knows your face too, now, doesn't he? From the hearing. From the salt house. From the drop, where you walked into his ambush and he got a good long look at you while Maître Corbineau vouched for your life."

The silence stretched. In the walls, the barrels ticked as the cold tightened the staves.

"I taught you the river," Sabine said, softer now, and that was worse than the argument. "You told me that myself. That knowing a route is the whole trade, and everything else is just carrying weight. Well. I know the route."

Elodie looked at her and saw, with terrible clarity, her own reflection from four months ago: a young woman underestimated by everyone, carrying something heavy, walking anyway. She had sworn a pact with this girl on the frozen river — *the record survives even if we don't* — and she had meant it, and she had somehow believed, in the cowardly part of her heart that makes oaths it doesn't expect to collect on, that the price would be hers alone to pay.

"If she goes," Elodie said to Aurel, "she goes with everything I can give her. Every trick my mother taught me. And she goes armed with more than a knife."

"She goes," Aurel said, "because she'd find a way regardless, and I'd rather she went prepared." He rubbed his beard with a hand that shook slightly, and then steadied. "God help me. When do we start?"

"Now," Elodie said. "We have five days."

* * *

The days took on the rhythm of a siege conducted against time itself.

Each morning Elodie rose before the light and worked at the kitchen table while the Vasse household slept, drafting the route in her head and then in cipher on slate, wiping it clean at dawn. Each afternoon she drilled Sabine — not in walking, Sabine needed no drilling there, but in the other discipline, the one Elodie had lived inside for six years until it had grown into her skin like a splinter the body learns to tolerate.

"Say who you are," Elodie said, pacing the cooperage floor among the barrel staves.

"Julienne Roche. Seamstress. Going down to Roussillon to my aunt, Madame Roche, widow, rue des Tanneurs, because my mother is ill and wants her sister near." Sabine recited it without stumbling. They had built the aunt out of whole cloth and half a truth — there was a Roche family in Roussillon, distant cousins of the Vasses, who had been written to and, astonishingly, had written back agreeing to shelter a niece they barely remembered. Corbineau's correspondence had done that much good already: people who had deposed their testimony wanted the testimony to travel.

"Your aunt's husband?"

"Died in the conscription. '09."

"What's in your bundle?"

"Shirts. Linen thread. A letter from Mama." Sabine tapped the oilcloth packet sewn flat against her ribs beneath the shirts, invisible under a seamstress's padding. "And nothing else, ever, if anyone asks, because Julienne Roche is a boring person and boring persons are never searched twice."

"Again."

They ran it until the answers came faster than the questions, until Sabine could lie about her aunt's dead husband with the mild, incurious fluency of someone describing weather. Elodie watched her and felt the old self-alienation rise in her throat — *look what I'm teaching her, look how good she gets at it, look how young she is to learn that lying well is a survival skill* — and then made herself swallow it. Better a fluent liar alive with the truth in her shirt than an honest girl in Duvall's cellar. The century had made its terms. They were simply meeting them.

At night, Corbineau came with additions. Word had gone quietly through the town — through the salt house hearing, through the registers, through the simple fact that a town that had once turned on itself during the blizzard now kept secrets together like a family keeping a grave — and Val-Brume had begun to feed them.

"The miller's boy saw soldiers at the bridgehead Tuesday," the notary reported, spreading a hand-drawn sketch on the table. "Twelve men, sappers. They're cutting timber for a pontoon. Which means the thaw detachment is closer than we thought — they don't build bridges for garrisons that are a fortnight out."

"How close?"

"Three days' march. Four in bad weather." Corbineau's pen hovered. "And there's more. Madame Liorans sent word — she has eyes at the inn. Duvall has hired two local men as guides for the river road. He's not waiting for the bridge. He means to send riders along the valley path as soon as it's passable, to intercept anything moving toward Roussillon."

"He knows," Elodie said. Not a question. After the empty staves, after the raid that had found nothing but furious, humiliated silence, Duvall knew the ledger existed and knew it would move. A man like Duvall did not guard a door; he guarded every road away from it.

"He suspects," Corbineau corrected. "It is not the same thing, and the difference is worth a day, perhaps two. A suspect casts a wide net. We need only pass through one hole in it."

"One hole," Aurel muttered, "in a net held by a man with soldiers, strung across a valley, in winter." He looked around the table. "I've ferried this river twenty-two years. There's a stretch below the Gorge du Loup where the banks narrow and the ice runs thick over fast water — thick enough to walk, if you know it, suicide if you don't. No road there. No path a horse can take. If Sabine crosses at the Loup, she comes out below the gorge, past where any rider can follow, a full day ahead of anyone watching the valley road."

"Below the gorge," Sabine repeated, and Elodie saw her file the words away with the same hunger she'd shown over the ice maps in October, back when friendship was new and the ledger was still a stranger.

"It adds half a day to the journey," Aurel said. "But it subtracts Duvall entirely."

"Then she takes the gorge," Elodie said.

* * *

On the third night, Elodie climbed alone to the frozen crypt beneath the ruined convent.

The fire that had taken the convent above had spared these depths — stone does not burn — and the original ledger lay where she had moved it, wrapped in waxed linen, sealed in a mason's jar against the damp, behind loose blocks that had once held the bones of nuns. She knelt in the dark with a shuttered lantern and lifted it out one last time, not to read it — she knew every line now, had copied every line twice — but to say goodbye to it as an object, and to speak to the hand that had written it.

"Mama."

Her voice came back off the stone, flattened, intimate. Somewhere above, snow sifted through the burnt rafters.

"They're going to print you." The words sounded foolish in the crypt, and she said them anyway. "Not your hand. Your meaning. Forty-one pages, plain French, two copies, and a notary's seal on each. By the time the thaw comes, there will be hundreds of you, and then thousands, and no fire reaches a thousand fires at once. That was your lesson, wasn't it? All those years I thought you were teaching me codes. You were teaching me multiplication."

She pressed her palm flat against the linen wrapping, over the place where the annotations began — her mother's slanted hand in the margins, patient and damning, the handwriting Elodie had recognized in a candlelit agony of grief that seemed now like it belonged to another life.

"I'm sending Sabine down the river," she said. "You'd like her. She's exactly the kind of student you'd have terrified and adored." A pause. "I'm staying as decoy. You'd hate that. You'd tell me the courier is the cheapest piece on the board and should spend herself freely. Maybe. But I've spent other people long enough."

From above, faint through the broken floors, came the sound of the town: a dog, a cart, the bell of Saint-Aubin tolling the hour. Nine o'clock. Four days left in the year. Somewhere south, twelve sappers were hammering a pontoon together, and somewhere north a printer named Marchand was setting type for almanacs that predicted a mild spring, and between those two facts Elodie Sandoval intended to drive a wedge that a hundred years of historians would have to reckon with.

She rewrapped the ledger, replaced the blocks, and left the lantern burning a moment longer on the shelf where the bones used to be.

"For the record," she said to the dark, and climbed back toward the living world.

* * *

The fourth day was for goodbyes that couldn't look like goodbyes.

In the morning, publicly, in front of the inn and half the street, Sabine Vasse quarreled with her father — a performance rehearsed until it stung — over wages, over her refusal to work the winter market stall, over a fictional uncle in Roussillon who had offered her respectable employment. She stormed off with her bundle. Neighbors shook their heads sympathetically at Aurel. By noon, "Julienne Roche" was registered in Corbineau's travel book with a legitimate exit visa stamped in genuine notarial wax, and the story of the Vasse girl's departure was the best entertainment in a town starved for anything but bread and rumor.

In the afternoon, privately, in the loft above the cooperage, they packed for real.

Elodie sewed the second packet into the lining of a spare cloak and showed Sabine where it would ride — high on the back, where a casual pat found nothing. She broke a stick of charcoal and wrote out, from memory, the recognition signs: the chalk mark that meant *safe house ahead*, the knot pattern that meant *turn back*. She gave her the half-sovereign sewn in the heel of a boot and the poison of practical advice instead of comfort.

"If they stop you," Elodie said, "be slower than they expect. Stupid people get waved through; clever people get searched. Let your hands shake a little. Fear is the most honest costume there is."

"And if they search me anyway?" Sabine asked, threading a needle with steady fingers.

"Then the packet is in the cloak, not the bundle, and the cloak stays on your shoulders, and you weep about your sick mother until they're ashamed." Elodie met her eyes. "And if they take it from you — if it comes to that, truly — give them the copy in the bundle. There is no copy in the bundle. But there will be, tonight. Two layers, Sabine. My mother's rule. The outer layer is bait. The inner layer is the truth."

Sabine nodded slowly, and then, unexpectedly, laughed. "You've become terrifying. Do you know that? In October you flinched when the door slammed."

"In October I was still obeying someone." Elodie threaded her own needle. "It's remarkable what it frees up."

They worked until the light failed. Below them, through the floorboards, came the smell of Aurel's stew and the murmur of Corbineau reading aloud the final attestation for Madame Liorans's review — the judge had insisted on checking every legal joint in the machine, muttering about writs and jurisdictions, arming the paper the way other people armed men. The town had become, in the space of a week, a single instrument aimed at the valley: the judge sharpening the law, the notary sealing it, the ferryman charting the ice, the archivist's daughter carrying it, and Elodie —

Elodie stayed behind. That was the plan and she had chosen it herself and she despised every hour of it in advance. Decoy. Bait. The visible courier, moving ostentatiously about Val-Brume with her trunk and her lies, drawing Duvall's watchers like moths while the real cargo walked out the back of the world on a river only one living person knew how to read.

"When you get to Marchand," Elodie said, as the last light left the loft window, "make him set the title page first. Before anything else. One finished sheet, pulled, dried, and hidden in his press room. Even if they seize everything else, even if they burn his shop — one printed sheet exists, and it says what it says, and it cannot be unsaid."

"'Indictment of the Signatories,'" Sabine quoted. She had memorized it. "'Compiled from the private ledger of M. Sandoval, the civic registers of Val-Brume, and sworn testimony taken before Madame C. Liorans, retired magistrate of the appellate court.' Then the forty-one articles." She tied off the last stitch and bit the thread. "I'll watch him pull it myself."

"I know you will."

For a moment neither of them spoke. The pact they had sworn on the ice in January — *even if we don't survive, it survives* — hung in the cold air between them, and both of them heard it, and neither needed to repeat it.

"Four days," Sabine said at last, shouldering the innocent-looking bundle. "If I push hard on the good ice, I reach Roussillon the morning after New Year's. Marchand sets type on the first of January. The new century starts with your mother's name in print."

"Not mine," Elodie said. "Hers. And yours, if you sign the deposition."

"Corbineau's making me." Sabine grinned, sharp as her father's ferry hooks. "Apparently a witness needs a witness."

She went down the ladder. Elodie heard the door, heard Aurel's low voice, heard the ordinary sounds of a family at supper, and stood alone in the loft with the needle still warm in her hand, looking south through the frost-feathered glass toward a valley she could not see and a press she had to trust to strangers.

Then she heard the second set of footsteps on the stairs — quick, deliberate — and Sabine reappeared in the hatchway, cheeks flushed, holding something out.

"You forgot this. From the crypt run." It was the scorched page from the burning at the mill, the single salvaged fragment proving the destruction had been ordered from above, mounted now on card and sealed under Corbineau's stamp. "Madame Liorans says it travels separately. Never with the main copy. So — I'm taking it. Sewn in my boot, next to the coin."

Elodie took it. For a heartbeat she almost refused — the page was the heart of everything, the proof of arson, of orders from above — and then she understood. That was exactly why it must not stay with her, the known courier, the marked woman, the decoy. Its safest place in all the world was inside a bored seamstress trudging to her aunt's.

"Walk the shallow crust at the Loup," Elodie said. "Where the water talks underneath, the ice is lying. Where it's silent — trust it."

"That's what Papa said. You two are starting to agree on things. It's very unsettling." Sabine's grin faded into something quieter, fiercer. "First of January," she said. "Watch for the news."

She was gone. Elodie stood at the window and watched the small dark figure take the river path north along the bank, unhurried, a seamstress with a bundle, until the dusk folded her in.

Behind her, at the edge of town where the bridge chains waited for the thaw, torchlight moved — twelve points of it, maybe more, around the skeleton of the rising pontoon. Three days' march, Corbineau had said. Perhaps fewer.

Elodie touched the cold glass, over the spot where Sabine had vanished, and began to count the hours.
