## 34. Sabine's Route

Sabine had walked the river her whole life, but she had never walked it like this — at night, in the black hour before the moon rose, with a satchel of paper against her chest and her father's compass needle trembling in its brass bowl.

The ice spoke to her. That was what Elodie had never quite learned to hear, no matter how many times Sabine made her stand still on the frozen crossing and listen. The river had a grammar. A low groan meant the current shifting beneath a foot of grey ice — safe, so long as you kept moving. A sharp crack like a whip was worse; it meant the frost was splitting along an old seam, and you went around it wide, wide, even if it cost you an hour. And when the ice sang — a high, thin whine, almost beautiful, like a bow drawn across a wet finger — that was the river telling you it was thin where you stood, and you got off it *now*.

"You'll know it when you hear it," she had told Elodie on their first lesson, back before any of this, back when the worst thing they carried between them was contempt for men who thought girls couldn't read a ledger.

Elodie had heard it exactly once since, on the night they moved the original ledger down into the convent crypt, and Sabine had watched her face go white in the lantern light and thought: *she's afraid of the wrong things.* Elodie could stare down Duvall across a dinner table without blinking, could lie to his face with a smile, could carry an indictment that would hang half the heroes of the republic — but a singing river frightened her more than soldiers did. It was the one honest fear she allowed herself, maybe because the river didn't care who won.

Now Sabine carried the duplicate set herself. Thirty-one pages of her mother-of-the-cause's ciphered hand, copied twice over by Elodie's patient candlelight and hidden in oilcloth inside a fish-salting box that smelled so strongly of brine that no searcher would want to open it for long. Under the false bottom where Elodie had once hidden the original, Sabine's father had built a second compartment, snug against the salt, and packed around it with wool.

"Salt ruins paper," Aurel had said, tamping the wool down with his big careful hands.

"Not in one night," Sabine said.

"And if it takes two?"

She hadn't answered. There wasn't an answer that helped.

* * *

They had argued about it for three days, she and Elodie, in the low voice they'd both learned — the voice of people who lived under other people's roof beams.

"It has to be me," Elodie said, pacing the cooperage loft while Sabine sat on the trunk with the map of the ice spread across her knees. "Duvall is watching every road out of Val-Brume. He watches *me*. If I leave, he follows, and whoever carries the ledger carries him straight to the printer."

"So let him follow you into the empty hills while I walk the river."

"He knows your face now. After the exchange—" Elodie stopped. She never liked finishing that sentence. The exchange on the ice, where Sabine had stood with Duvall's hand on her arm and watched Elodie walk toward him with her chin up, offering herself like coin. "He knows your face."

"He knows my face the way a man knows a face he's held for ten minutes," Sabine said. "In the dark, in the wind, wrapped in wool. He doesn't know how I move." She tapped the map. "*I* know how the river moves. You'd take the valley road because it looks safe, and the valley road has his men on it. I'll take the ice, because the ice only kills the careless, and I'm not careless."

"And if the ice decides otherwise?"

"Then the ledger drowns with me, and you still have your copy hidden, and the town still has Corbineau's registers, and Madame Liorans still has her page, and the truth has four chances instead of one. That's the whole arithmetic of what we've built, isn't it? Copies. Redundancy. A record too scattered to burn." She looked up. "You taught me that. Don't unteach it now because you're fond of me."

Elodie had gone very still. In the candlelight her face was all planes and shadow, and Sabine saw something move behind it that she recognized from her own mirror after her mother died — the look of a person deciding to survive a thing by putting it down.

"If anything happens to you," Elodie said finally, "your father will never forgive me."

"My father," Sabine said, "will be too busy being proud."

* * *

Aurel walked her as far as the old ferry landing, which was no longer a landing at all since the fire took the boat — just charred pilings standing in the ice like burnt teeth. He carried the salting box himself, though it wasn't heavy, because carrying things was what he knew how to do, and because his hands needed work when his heart was full or they'd shake.

"The mill pool first," he said. "Where the eddy turns under the point. Even in the coldest winter the middle of the pool stays thin — you can see the dark water through it if the snow hasn't drifted over. Go up onto the bank and come back down past the willows."

"I know, Papa."

"And the falls shallows below the quarry—"

"*I know.*" She softened it by taking his arm. "You've been teaching me this river since I was six. You didn't teach me wrong once. Don't start doubting yourself tonight."

He stopped at the edge of the landing and set the box down, and then he did something he almost never did: he put both hands on either side of her face, mittens and all, and looked at her the way he used to look at her mother's portrait before he stopped hanging it up.

"Your grandmother ferried this river," he said. "Her mother before her. We're not a family that stays on the bank." His voice caught, roughened. "Come back anyway."

"I'll come back," Sabine said. "Bring the spring with me, maybe."

He laughed — a short, wet laugh, half sob — and let her go.

* * *

The first three miles were easy, and she hated them for it, because easy miles bred careless feet. She kept the discipline Elodie had drilled into her during the copying nights, when they'd turned the loft above the cooperage into a scriptorium: count your paces, check your bearing every hundred steps, stop and listen every time the wind dropped. The wind dropped often. The night was one of those vast, ringing silks of cold where sound traveled forever — she could hear a dog barking in Val-Brume two miles behind her, and once, much farther off, the long lonely note of the quarry horn that the watchman blew at midnight.

Against her chest, the pages rode like a small warm animal. Not warm, really — cold paper against a cold body — but alive. That was the feeling she couldn't shake: that she was carrying something living, something that wanted to get where it was going. Her mother's handwriting had been in those margins too, before the cipher swallowed it. Elodie's mother. But somewhere in the weeks of copying and deciphering, the woman had become theirs — *our author*, Sabine called her, and Elodie had smiled for the first time in days when she said it.

At the mill pool she climbed the bank as her father said, boots crunching through the crusted snow, and came down again past the willows, and there it was: the dark eye of the eddy, open water breathing faint steam into the starlight, ringed by ice that sagged inward like the skin on cooling milk. She gave it the width of a hayfield and thanked the river quietly, the way her grandmother's generation still did — old habits from before the republic renamed everything.

Past the quarry the character of the river changed. Here it narrowed between walls of rock, and the current ran fast underneath, and the ice grew treacherous — thick in some places, hollow in others, laced with pressure ridges where two winters' freeze had collided and shoved up slabs like broken crockery. This was the passage Sabine had scouted three times by daylight, memorizing it until she could draw it blindfolded. She drew it now in her head, step by step: keep to the north shore shelf for two hundred paces, cross at the gravel bar where the water braided shallow, then back along the south bench below the overhang.

She was halfway along the north shelf when the ice sang.

The sound went up through her boots and into her teeth — that high, glassy whine, sustained, rising. She froze mid-stride, one weight forward, and made herself breathe. *Don't run. Running is rhythm, and rhythm travels.* She shifted her weight back, slow as honey, and looked down.

Beneath her feet, through a palm-width of clear black ice, the current ran silver and fast, and she saw — with the peculiar calm that comes on a person in exactly such moments — a long crack opening beneath the surface, traveling upstream faster than a running man, a dark thread unspooling through the river's body like ink in water.

It passed beneath her. The singing stopped. The ice held.

Sabine discovered she had been holding the salting box in both hands, pressed to her sternum, the way a child holds a stolen loaf. She laughed once, silently, breath smoking, and kept walking.

* * *

The drop point was a ruined charcoal burner's hut in the alder bottoms where the river bent south toward the valley — half a day's walk downstream, chosen by Elodie and Madame Liorans together, because it lay close enough to the valley cart-road that a trusted carrier could reach it, and far enough from Val-Brume that no eye from the town could watch it. Liorans had a cousin among the valley printers, a man named Perrin whose press had survived three regimes by printing almanacs, hymnals, and nothing else anyone remembered.

The plan was simple, and Sabine had insisted on hearing it recited back twice: she would cache the box inside the hut's stone foundation, mark it, and return by a different route — the high path through the pine saddle, slower but invisible. A carrier from Perrin's would collect the pages within two days. No names exchanged. No faces shown. The courier who carried truth, Elodie always said, should be a rumor, not a person.

Sabine reached the alder bottoms an hour before dawn, moving now on the packed trail along the bank rather than the ice itself — the river widened here, and the ice spread thin and unreliable across the flood plain. The hut rose out of the frosted alders like a broken tooth, its roof fallen in, its stones furred with hoarfrost. She knelt at the foundation's north wall, where a course of stones had slipped and left a gap the size of a bread oven, and began working the box into the cavity.

That was when she heard the voices.

Two men, downstream, maybe three hundred yards — coming up the bank along the very cart-road the plan depended on. The words wouldn't resolve, but the cadence did: patrol rhythm, bored, unhurried, the way men talk when they're walking a route they've walked every night for a week.

Sabine went flat behind the foundation wall, the box half-hidden in the gap, her heart slamming so hard she was sure it would drum through the stones. *Think.* If they came to the hut — and patrols came to huts; huts were where deserters hid and smugglers stashed — they would find the box within minutes. Salt-crusted, wool-wrapped, and full of treason.

She couldn't outrun them to cover; the alders were bare and the ground was snow, and dawn was graying the east. She couldn't fight them; she had a knife and they had muskets, probably, and certainty.

So she did what Elodie would do. What Elodie had *taught* her, without ever meaning to, in all those nights of watching her lie beautifully at Duvall's table.

Sabine stood up, brushed the snow off her skirts, picked up the salting box, and walked openly down the trail toward the voices, coughing.

They materialized out of the alders — two soldiers in the grey-blue of the valley garrison, muskets sloped, young, chapped red with cold. The taller one leveled his chin at her. "You there. Where are you walking at this hour?"

"To my aunt's smokehouse, citizen, unless the republic has claimed that too." She coughed again, deep and wet, and let her shoulders hunch with it. "Forgive me. The lung fever went through our quarter last month. I'm delivering salt fish to her before the thaw spoils the barrels — she's an old woman and eats nothing else in Lent."

The tall one wrinkled his nose. Up close the box reeked gloriously of brine. "You're out alone? On these roads? There's a bandit notice posted in Saint-Aubin."

"Bandits don't want salt fish and they don't want the lung fever," Sabine said, and coughed again, and this time spat theatrically into the snow. "My uncle was supposed to walk with me but his rheumatism—" She waved a hand. Everything about her was ordinary, irritated, cold, sick. She had never loved her own face less or used it better.

The shorter soldier, who hadn't spoken, looked at her a moment too long. "Val-Brume girl?" he asked.

"Downriver. Vasseur's niece, on my mother's side." A name plucked from nowhere, common enough, attached to a family two villages away that might or might not have a niece. The lie was good because most of it was true-shaped: she *was* delivering fish, in a sense. Truth was the best packing material for lies. Elodie had said that too.

"Get home before full light," the tall one said, already turning away. "And cover that cough, girl. You'll give the whole valley your fever."

"Citizens," she said, with just enough sourness, and walked past them down the trail, coughing, hunched, ordinary — while behind her, in the ruined hut, thirty-one pages of indictment lay half-tucked into a hole in a wall.

She made herself keep walking a full quarter mile before she stopped, shaking, in the lee of a fallen alder. Her hands wouldn't steady. She laughed at them, breathlessly, silently — the same wild silent laugh as on the ice — because there was nothing else to do with it.

Then she turned around and went back.

* * *

It took her twenty minutes, moving in the gray pre-dawn with her ears stretched to their limit, to ease the box fully into the cavity, wedge it with two loose stones, drag a dead alder branch across the gap, and scatter frost-loose snow over the whole of it until the foundation wall looked like nothing — a ruin doing what ruins do, falling apart patiently. Then she marked it the way they'd agreed: a cairn of five stones at the fork of the trail, the sixth stone set apart, pointing.

By the time she climbed into the pine saddle, the sun was coming up over the valley in long pink blades, lighting the frozen river below her all the way back toward Val-Brume — a white ribbon stitched through the brown hills, and at its end, small and smoke-stacked, the town that counted everything.

She stood a moment and looked at it. Somewhere down there, Elodie was making herself visible on purpose — walking the market square in her red wool cloak, the cloak Sabine had teased her about, calling at the notary's office, taking tea within sight of Duvall's window at the inn, being loudly, tediously, unmistakably *present*, so that every eye in the occupation stayed on her while the real cargo of the winter slid away under the ice. Decoy work, Liorans had called it. Martyr work, Sabine thought, and hated the word, and made herself think it anyway, because refusing to think a thing had never yet stopped it from being true.

*Be boring,* Elodie had instructed her, in their last hour together, pressing the compass into Sabine's hand — her compass, the one thing she'd carried out of another life. *Be early, be quiet, and trust the river more than the road.*

"I'm trusting the river," Sabine told her now, aloud, to the sunrise, to the smoke, to the woman she couldn't see. "Trust yourself back to me."

She turned uphill, toward the pine saddle and the long way home, and had taken perhaps fifty steps when the wind shifted and brought her the smell.

Smoke. But not woodsmoke — not the clean, homely reek of a town's chimneys waking. This was thicker, darker, with a bitterness under it that her nose knew from the night the ferry burned: tar, pitch, old timber, and under all of it something chemical and wrong, the smell of papers and parchment and glue giving themselves up.

She turned back to the ridge. Below, in the town, a column of black smoke was rising — and it was not rising from the inn, or the cooperage, or the salt house.

It rose from beyond the church, from the low grey shape at the town's edge that she knew as well as her own hand.

The convent.

Sabine stood on the ridge with the sun in her eyes and the compass burning cold in her fist, and understood, with a courier's terrible clarity, exactly what Duvall had found, and what he was burning, and who had gone down into the crypt to try to stop it — or had already been inside when the first torch touched the timbers.

Then she ran.
