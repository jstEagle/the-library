## 38. The Arrest of Agent Duvall

Madame Liorans had not worn her robes in eleven years.

They had traveled with her from the capital folded into a cedar chest — she would not say why, even to herself — and they smelled now of camphor and cold storage, of a life she had set down like a pen mid-sentence. She stood before the small mirror in her rented rooms above the salt house and fastened the collar with fingers that did not entirely obey her. In the glass, an old woman looked back: broad-faced, white-haired, her eyes still carrying the particular flat patience of a person who has spent thirty years listening to men lie.

*You retired*, she reminded herself. *You buried your husband. You came here to be no one.*

And yet here was the morning of it — the thaw opening the bridge, soldiers on the road, and a courier half-dead from fire and collapse waiting downstairs with a bundle of papers that would either save this town or bury it.

She took up her sash of office, the faded violet that marked her as a judge of the assizes, and tied it with a single decisive motion.

"Enough," she said aloud, to the mirror, to the woman in it. "The law does not retire. It only waits."

* * *

The bridge at Val-Brume was a thing of black timber and older iron, and for five months the chains had held it shut while the river beneath ground its teeth against the ice. Now the water ran loud and brown and victorious, and the chains lay coiled on the near bank like sleeping serpents, and across the span came the first travelers of the season: two drovers, a peddler with his cart, and behind them, at a pace that made the timbers sing, a column of soldiers in the gray-blue of the Provisional Command.

Thirty men, maybe thirty-five. Bayonets fixed. At their head rode a horse Elodie had described to her twice — a tall bay with a white star — and on the horse sat Citizen-Agent Duvall in a new greatcoat, his face composed into the expression of a man arriving to collect what was owed him.

Liorans watched from the window of the tollhouse, Corbineau beside her with a ledger open on his knees, and Elodie Sandoval standing a little apart, wrapped in a borrowed shawl, her burned hands bandaged, her eyes never leaving the bridge.

"He will demand the prisoner first," Liorans said. "Then the papers. Then he will thank us all very courteously and burn the town's records on his way out."

"You cannot know that," Corbineau said, but without conviction. His hands trembled on the ledger. Eleven months ago he would have closed it and stepped back. She watched him hold it open instead, and thought: *there is the whole revolution of this winter, in the grip of one frightened notary.*

"I know men," Liorans said. "I have spent my life knowing them. It is the only expertise I have left." She turned. "Elodie."

The girl looked at her. Twenty-four years old, perhaps less, with the face of someone who had been old since childhood. Her voice, when it came, was steady. "He'll claim the writ covers everything. He showed me forged commissions once already. He's fluent too."

"Fluency meets fluency, then." Liorans allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "But hear me. When I call you forward — and I will call you forward, because the record must have its witness in the open air where everyone can see — you will speak slowly. Not for him. For them." She nodded toward the square, where the people of Val-Brume were gathering the way water gathers, drawn downhill by gravity and rumor: Aurel Vasse released from custody only yesterday, gray and unbowed; Sabine with river-mud still dried on her boots; the miller, the smith, the cooper, the widow Bresson whose son had been taken in '96. "A reading is not a performance, child. It is a planting. You put the words down in front of witnesses and you let them take root where no fire can follow."

Elodie's jaw tightened. "And if his soldiers don't care about roots?"

"Soldiers are the easiest part." Liorans drew out a folded paper from inside her coat — the one Sabine had brought downriver, intercepted at the ferry crossing, bearing the Provisional Command's seal. "Men under orders want nothing so much as permission to stop obeying the wrong man. We are going to give it to them."

* * *

Duvall dismounted at the foot of the bridge with the unhurried confidence of ownership.

"Citizens." His voice carried beautifully; Liorans noted the training in it, the court polish laid over the agent's gravel. "I am pleased to find the town assembled. It saves announcements. By authority of the Provisional Command, I require the surrender of the fugitive known as Elodie Sandoval, all documents in her possession, and the civic registers currently withheld from lawful seizure—"

"Withheld by whom?" said Madame Liorans.

It was a quiet question and it cut through the square like a knife through wet canvas. Heads turned. Duvall found her — a small old woman in faded judicial violet, standing on the tollhouse steps with a notary at her elbow — and Liorans saw the precise moment he placed her: the flicker in the eyes, the recalculation behind them.

"Madame Liorans." A courteous bow, flawless. "I understood you had retired."

"I have. The law has not." She descended one step, no more; elevation was a tool, and tools should be used sparingly. "Before any requisition issues in this jurisdiction, I will see your commission. The original. Not the fair copy you showed the innkeeper in December, which bore a countersignature belonging to a clerk who, according to Maître Corbineau's registry, died at Besançon in October. Show me the paper that authorizes you, Citizen-Agent, or stand on this bridge with nothing but your voice."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere a crow complained. Liorans felt the town leaning in — three hundred people breathing at once.

Duvall smiled. "The commission is military business, madame. You of all people—"

"Then produce a military officer above your rank to vouch for it. Is one attached to your column?" She let her gaze travel past him to the soldiers, rank and file, stamping cold from their feet. "No? Then we proceed otherwise." She raised her voice — not shouting; a judge never shouts — pitched to carry to the last row of the column. "Soldiers of the Provisional Command. I am Marguerite-Angélique Liorans, formerly of the assizes at Dijon, and I am invoking the old writ of *présentation des pouvoirs*: no agent may exercise coercion within a jurisdiction until his powers are shown and found true. It is archaic. It survived four regimes precisely because it works. Your orders, gentlemen, whatever they are, do not supersede it — and if your own marching papers contradict the man you are following, you will want to know that now, in daylight, rather than later, in a courtroom."

She nodded to Corbineau.

The notary came forward with his ledger held before him like a breviary, and his voice shook, and he did not let it stop him. "By entry dated the twelfth of January, the garrison paymaster at Pontarlier certified the detachment now present: twenty-eight men, commanded by Captain Roze, under sealed orders for the valley road — orders which named no agent and granted no seizure rights. Entry witnessed and countersigned. I hold the paymaster's receipt." He turned a page with deliberate care. "By entry dated the fourth of February, one 'Agent Duvall' presented a commission countersigned by Clerk Henri Vasse—" a breath, a pause, the notary's grief and shame visible as he spoke his own brother's name into the record, "—who was executed in the September purge. Dead men sign nothing, citizens. I have kept this town's books for thirty-one years. I know ink. And I know when a page has been written backward to look old."

He held the ledger high, turned toward the soldiers, letting them see the columns, the dates, the meticulous hand.

"The man leading you," Corbineau said, "is not authorized. The papers he carries are forged. Everything done in their name — every door broken, every man jailed, every document burned at the mill — was done outside the law. I certify it. My register certifies it. Come and read it yourselves. That is what it is for."

* * *

Liorans watched the column the way she had once watched juries: not their faces but their feet, their rifles, the small physics of doubt traveling through a body of men.

It began at the rear. A soldier — young, farm-broad, probably conscripted off some field not unlike these — shifted his weight. Another glanced at his sergeant. The sergeant, a weathered veteran with a scarred jaw, did not move, but he did not correct them either, and that omission rang louder than any order.

Duvall heard it too. Liorans saw him hear it — the tiny stiffening at the shoulders, the recalibration of a man discovering the ground had opinions. He turned back to the steps with his smile reassembled, but a half-beat late, and the lateness told the whole square everything.

"Madame." Smooth as poured oil. "These are provincial superstitions. The nation is at war with its own past. There is no time for—"

"There is always time for the law," Liorans said. "That is what the law is *for*. Everything else — speed, necessity, emergency — those are the arguments of every tyrant since Rome, and every one of them ended the same way: with a clerk forging a signature and a grave dug in advance." She lifted the second paper then, the one taken from the courier pouch at the ferry crossing, and unfolded it slowly, making the soldiers watch. "Captain Roze. Will you step forward?"

The scarred sergeant blinked. "Madame?"

"You are Captain Roze of Pontarlier. This is your sealed order." She read aloud, clearly, each word set down like a stone in a wall: "*Proceed to the valley road upon the opening of the crossings. Secure the printworks at Sainte-Ombre against all irregular forces. Deliver enclosed indictment to the civil tribunal.*" She lowered the paper. "Enclosed indictment, Captain. Which was removed from your pouch before departure — by whom, I wonder — and replaced with instructions to seize this town's records and detain its inhabitants. Do those instructions bear your seal?"

"They do not, madame." The captain's voice was flat and getting flatter, the voice of a man doing arithmetic he did not like. "My orders name no agent Duvall. They never have. I was told he'd been... attached at the last minute. Verbally."

"Verbally," Liorans repeated, savagely gentle. "The favorite word of every forger in France. Thank you, Captain. Your honesty is entered into the record of this day." She looked past him at the column. "Soldiers. Your captain holds lawful orders. The man beside him holds paper signed by a dead clerk. Whom do you serve?"

For a long moment nobody moved. The river roared under the bridge, brown and triumphant, carrying the winter's ice down toward the sea.

Then Private Yves Marot — she learned his name later, from Corbineau's deposition, entered in the same neat hand as everything else — grounded his musket. Butt on stone. Deliberate. Audible.

One after another, down the line, the rest followed, until the sound of it filled the square: twenty-eight muskets set down in the mud and slush of a mountain town, a sound like doors closing on a long bad year.

Duvall stood alone on the bridge with his horse shifting beneath him, and Liorans watched the last of the performance fall away from his face. What remained underneath was not rage, exactly. It was smaller than rage — the naked arithmetic of a man counting exits and finding none. His eyes went to the far bank, the open road, the thawed world beyond. Then to Elodie. Then, briefly, horribly, to Sabine Vasse, standing at the front of the crowd with her father's arm across her shoulders — and Liorans saw the calculation flicker there, the old habit of hostages, and saw it die unborn, because three hundred people stood between him and the girl, and every one of them had stopped being afraid.

"Madame Liorans." Even now, the courtesy held; it was the last uniform he owned. "If I might return to the capital and answer these—"

"You might not." She descended the remaining steps, Corbineau at her side, and stopped an arm's length from the foot of the bridge. "Citizen-Agent — or whatever you truly are, for I suspect even that name is stolen property — by the authority vested in me as a judge of the assizes, sitting under the old writ in the jurisdiction of Val-Brume, I take you into custody on charges of forgery of public instruments, false imprisonment, arson of registered property, theft of judicial evidence, and conspiracy against the persons of this commune. Maître Corbineau will enter the charge sheet. Captain Roze, your men will escort the prisoner to the salt house cellar, which has served as a cell all winter and will serve once more — this time holding the right man."

Roze saluted — actually saluted, the old-fashioned way, palm out — and two soldiers mounted the bridge and took Duvall's arms.

He went quietly. That was somehow worse than struggle. At the third plank he paused and looked back, and found Elodie Sandoval watching him from the steps, bandaged hands folded, absolutely still.

"You'll learn," he said to her, conversationally, almost kindly. "What it costs, to keep things. Paper burns. People talk. Regimes turn over like soil. In ten years nobody will remember this town exists."

"Perhaps," Elodie said. Her voice carried; the crowd heard every word. "But the record won't need remembering. It only needs existing. You taught me that, Citizen-Agent — you and every man who ever tried to burn something. Fire is how cowards argue with paper." She tilted her head. "Paper always wins eventually. It just needs somebody patient enough to copy it out."

The soldiers walked him on. Liorans remained on the bridge a moment longer, feeling the planks hum under the running water's weight, and permitted herself one long breath — eleven years of retirement exhaling out of her at last.

Then Corbineau touched her sleeve, and she turned back to the work.

* * *

The charge sheet took until dusk. She insisted on dictating every count herself while Corbineau wrote, because a record made hastily was a record easily attacked, and somewhere downstream — she did not doubt it — the copies were already moving toward a printer, and everything written here today would someday be weighed against them.

When it was done, she climbed the stairs to the little room where Elodie sat by the window, watching the light go gold on the water, the same gold Sabine had spoken of, like a line of fresh ink.

"Well," Liorans said. "It is done. Lawfully. With signatures."

"Yes." Elodie did not turn around. "It feels strange. I expected to feel triumphant."

"Do you?"

"No. I feel..." The girl searched. "Like a courier who's finished a delivery and doesn't know who she is anymore."

Liorans lowered herself into the opposite chair, joints protesting, and studied the young woman — the burned hands, the mother's ledger somewhere safe below the earth, the whole terrible inheritance of it. "That feeling has a name, Mademoiselle Sandoval. It is called the beginning." She smiled faintly. "Tomorrow the valley road opens properly, and the first post-rider of the season comes through. Whatever word reaches us from the capital, from the printers, from the century that is ending out there — it arrives tomorrow. Rest tonight. Tomorrow, we learn whether the truth travels as fast as we hoped."

Far below the window, the river ran on toward the valley, carrying the last of the ice, and somewhere down that darkening water a girl in a borrowed coat was racing the calendar with a satchel full of pages — and the first day of a new hundred years was coming up the road to meet them all.
