## 39. New Year's Print Run

The news came the way the most important things in Val-Brume always seemed to come: quietly, carried by someone ordinary, at an hour when no one was watching for it.

Elodie was in the cooperage loft, mending a barrel hoop by lamplight because her hands could not bear to be idle anymore, when she heard boots on the stair — two sets, one light and quick as a wren's heartbeat, the other heavy and deliberate. She knew both walks better now than she had ever known any route on any map.

Sabine came through the door first, snow crusted in the wool of her hood, her cheeks scalded red from the river wind, and Elodie felt the world tilt before a single word was spoken. Sabine had left nine days ago with the duplicate set sewn into oilskin bundles beneath her coat. Nine days down the frozen river paths, through the gorge where the ice sang and cracked like distant cannon, past the burned-out mill, all the way to the valley town where a printer named Vasseur had once, years ago, set type for pamphlets that got his press confiscated twice. Nine days. And here she stood, upright, breathing, grinning.

"You're—" Elodie began, and found she had no sentence ready. She had rehearsed grief on this stair. She had rehearsed every version of loss except this one.

"Behind me," Sabine said, still catching her breath, "is Maître Feron, who rode up from the valley with the post, and who has been trying to keep pace with me since dawn and is very cross about it."

Feron filled the doorway a moment later, stamping snow from his boots, a stout man with ink stains permanent as birthmarks on his fingers. He had been Corbineau's correspondent for thirty years; Elodie had seen his name in the margins of the civic registers more times than any other living hand. He looked around the low loft — the barrels, the lamp, the girl with the mended hoop — with the expression of a man confirming that a rumor he had half-disbelieved was true.

"Mademoiselle Sandoval," he said. "I have come from Vasseur's shop. I watched them do it myself, so you would not have to trust a letter."

Elodie set down the hoop. Her hands were suddenly unsteady, and she let them be.

"They finished the setting yesterday," Feron went on, "the last forme went to press this morning before first light. Two hundred impressions of the full indictment — your cipher pages transcribed, Corbineau's register entries set alongside them line for line, the judge's certification printed at the head of every copy." He drew a folded sheet from inside his coat, wrapped in waxed cloth against the wet, and held it out. "And this. The first proof off the press. Vasseur kept it back for you."

* * *

She did not open it right away. That surprised her. She had imagined this moment through the worst weeks of the winter — through the burning convent, through the exchange on the ice, through the long nights of copying until her eyes bled candlelight — and in every imagining she had torn the wrapping open at once, greedily, the way she had once opened her mother's letters as a child.

Instead she sat with the packet on her knees while the others talked around her, and let the weight of it settle. It weighed almost nothing. A single printed sheet. Two hundred brothers and sisters of it, somewhere down in the valley, stacked in a printer's back room waiting for the world.

It was Aurel who finally nodded at it — Aurel, whose arrest had taught the whole town what silence cost, who had come through that winter with his dignity intact and his ferry a charred skeleton on the riverbank. "Well," he said gently, "are you going to read it, or are we?"

So she opened it.

The paper was good stock, the valley mill's best, and the ink was black and certain. At the head of the page, in type large enough that no magistrate could pretend to misread it: *A True Account of Requisitions, Executions, and Payments, Certified by the Civic Records of Val-Brume and Sworn Before Madame Liorans, Judge in Retirement, This Thirtieth Day of December.* Below it, the columns ran — names, dates, sums, signatures. The requisitions signed by men whose statues stood in three provincial capitals. The payments drawn on accounts belonging to heroes of the republic, funding operations the histories called acts of mercy. Page after transcribed page, cipher made plain, ledger made public.

And there, in the third column, an annotation she had copied herself a hundred times without knowing whose hand had written it in the original: *Let the record show what the record shows. No more, no less.* Her mother's handwriting, translated into movable type. Somewhere below in the valley, a compositor had set those words letter by letter, never knowing they had belonged to a dead woman who was not dead when she wrote them, and now they would outlive everyone in this room.

Elodie read it twice, then a third time, hunting for errors the way a courier hunts for seals broken and wax tampered with. There were none. Feron saw what she was doing and permitted himself a dry smile.

"Vasseur checked every figure against your transcript twice, and Corbineau's certified copy went with the bundle as well. Nothing was altered. Not a name softened, not a sum rounded. He asked me to tell you that especially — that he understood what kind of document it was, and what it would mean if it were imperfect."

"It isn't imperfect," Elodie heard herself say. Her voice sounded strange to her, very calm. "It's the truest thing I have ever held."

* * *

That evening the salt house filled.

No one had sent invitations. In Val-Brume, word simply traveled — along the cooperage stairs, over the fences, through Corbineau's office door, which stood open now more often than shut — and by the time the lamps were lit, half the town had crowded in out of the cold to hear Feron tell it. He told it well, standing near the great iron stove with his ink-stained hands gesturing like a man setting type in the air: the press room lit by tallow candles, the compositors working through the night unpaid because Vasseur had read the first deciphered page aloud to his shop and then locked the door so no one could leave until it was done.

"He said," Feron recounted, and the salt house quieted to hear it, "'I have set type for liars and for cowards and for men who paid me to forget what I printed. Thirty years I thought the trade was only ink. Tonight it is a witness.' And then he pulled off his coat and worked beside his apprentices until dawn."

Corbineau stood near the wall, as he often did now, a man still learning how to occupy space he had spent decades making small. When Feron finished, it was Corbineau who spoke, and his voice carried the particular gravity of a notary reading from a register.

"Two hundred copies," he said. "They will go out with the thaw — to judges, to archivists, to printers in four towns who owe Vasseur favors or money or grudges, which in the printing trade amount to the same thing. Some will be seized. Some will be burned. Some will sit in drawers until the men they name are dust." He paused, and Elodie saw him gather something up from deep in himself, some old shame repurposed at last into resolve. "But copies cannot be summoned before a tribunal and made to recant. Copies cannot sign false inventories under threat. They can only be read. And somewhere, someday, one of them will be read by someone with the power and the spine to act on it. That is not justice. But it is the ground justice stands on, and we have laid it."

Madame Liorans, seated near the stove with a blanket over her knees despite the heat — the winter had taken something from her stamina that even vindication had not returned — raised her cup a few inches. "To the ground," she said, and the room drank.

Elodie stood at the edge of the lamplight and did not drink. She was watching Sabine.

The girl had changed in nine days on the river, though she could not have said how — not taller, not older exactly, but settled somehow, the way ice settles into its winter thickness, sounder than it looks. She was holding court near the door, telling the story of the gorge crossing to a ring of younger children with embellishments Elodie recognized as pure invention, and catching Elodie's eye mid-exaggeration with a look of perfect, unrepentant complicity.

Later, when the crowd had thinned and the lamps burned low, Sabine appeared at her elbow with two cups of mulled wine and handed one over without asking.

"For the record," Sabine said, "I want it noted in whatever ledger you're keeping now that the gorge ice did sing. Exactly the way I told them. The children didn't believe me."

"The record shows what the record shows," Elodie said. "No more, no less."

Sabine laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the first Elodie had heard from her since before the raid on the cooperage — and then grew serious in the abrupt way she had. "My father says you're staying through spring. Corbineau says it too. Says you'll help him index the registers properly, cross-reference everything, build the town an archive worth the name." She turned her cup in her hands. "Is it true? You could go anywhere now. With the copies out, no one needs to hunt you. You could disappear the way you were always going to disappear, before any of this."

Elodie looked into her wine. She thought about the answer honestly, because Sabine deserved honesty and because, for the first time in longer than she could measure, she had the luxury of choosing her own words rather than performing them.

"When I came over the bridge in December," she said, "I was carrying something that wasn't mine, to deliver to people I'd never seen, on behalf of masters who meant for me to die with it in my arms. Everything I did after that — every lie, every hiding place, every risk — was in service of getting rid of it. Passing the burden. Making it someone else's problem." She shook her head slowly. "But it was never a burden, Sabine. It was my mother's voice. It was a testimony that needed a keeper, not a courier. And the keeper's work isn't finished when the copies go out. Someone has to stay with the original. Someone has to know where every page sleeps, and why, and be able to swear to it. Someone has to teach the next person who asks." She met Sabine's eyes. "Besides. Someone in this town has to fact-check your stories about the gorge."

* * *

Near midnight, the year began to die.

They gathered — the ones who mattered, the ones the winter had bound together whether they had chosen it or not — in the loft above the cooperage, because it was warm and small and had survived everything done to it. Aurel brought brandy he claimed he had been saving for a worthy occasion and admitted, pouring, that he had bought it in November expecting nothing of the kind. Liorans sat in the best chair and refused, firmly, all suggestions that she should be resting. Corbineau brought his pocket watch, because a notary does not let a century end unrecorded.

Outside, the cold was absolute and the sky was swept clean, dense with stars, and beyond the dark shape of the ruined bridge the river ice groaned once, long and low, like the earth turning over in its sleep.

Elodie stood at the window with the proof sheet folded inside her coat, over her heart, an affectation she would have mocked in anyone else and permitted herself without apology. She thought of her mother — not the legend, not the martyr the purge had manufactured, but the woman at the kitchen table teaching a seven-year-old to substitute letters, laughing when the child got it wrong, saying *a code isn't a trick, little one, it's a promise that the truth can travel*. She had written that promise into a ledger and smuggled it toward a future she would never see. And the future had arrived. It was standing in a cooperage loft drinking bad brandy with a ferryman and a judge and a notary and a girl who guided people across ice.

"Ten minutes," Corbineau announced, watch open on his knee.

They talked softly then, the way people do at the ends of things. Liorans spoke of the spring session she intended, in defiance of her retirement, to hold in the salt house — depositions, sworn and registered, so that the town's testimony would exist not only in print but under oath, unimpeachable. Corbineau spoke of the archive: shelves already commissioned, a catalog system he had designed in his head over thirty years and never had cause to build until now. Aurel spoke of a new ferry, come thaw, and of charging Duvall's soldiers double if ever they dared show their faces at his landing again.

Sabine said nothing. She stood beside Elodie at the window, shoulder almost touching, watching the stars the way she watched the river — reading it.

"One minute," said Corbineau.

Elodie found she was holding her breath, and made herself stop. What difference did a minute make, after everything? And yet. The old century had taken her mother's name, her orders, her obedience, her belief that duty was a thing handed down from above like a sealed packet. Let it take those. Let the new one find her holding something else.

"Now," said Corbineau, and wrote in his watch-book by lamplight, in his precise notary's hand: *The century closed tonight in Val-Brume. Present: persons as listed. Conditions: cold, clear, hopeful.*

Hopeful. Elodie turned the word over. From any other mouth it might have sounded sentimental. From Corbineau's, entered in a civic register, it became a fact — dated, witnessed, certified, as real as any requisition or payment or execution the old records held. The town counted everything. Now it had counted hope.

"To the new century," Aurel said, raising his cup. "May it read more carefully than the last one."

They drank. Outside, far downriver, someone in another house fired a musket into the air in celebration, and the report cracked flat across the ice and echoed off the gorge walls, and for a moment Elodie's body tensed with an old reflex — gunfire, ambush, the net closing — before she remembered where she was, and whose voices surrounded her, and let the tension drain out through the floorboards.

Sabine nudged her. "You flinched."

"I'm retiring from flinching," Elodie said. "Effective immediately. It's in my new terms of employment."

"And what are those? Archivist. Keeper of the original. Teacher of codes." Sabine grinned. "Apprentices?"

"Applicants considered in spring," Elodie said gravely. "References required. Must be able to cross a singing gorge without exaggerating afterward."

"I'll put my name down anyway," Sabine said. "I hear the archivist owes her life to at least one person in this town. Might get favorable treatment."

Elodie laughed, and the laugh hurt a little, the way laughter does when it has been rationed too long, and she did not care.

* * *

Long after the others had gone down to their beds, Elodie stayed at the window, and the town slept below her in the blue-black snow-light, chimney smoke rising straight as plumb lines in the windless cold. Somewhere south, in the valley, in a locked shop that smelled of ink and metal, two hundred sheets of truth lay stacked in the dark, patient as seeds. They did not need her. That was the miracle of print, the thing her mother had understood and she was only now learning: a record, once loose in the world, keeps traveling without a courier. It waits in drawers and archives and the memories of people who read it once and never forgot. It outlasts regimes the way rivers outlast bridges.

She took out the proof sheet one last time and read her mother's line by starlight — *let the record show what the record shows* — and beneath it, in the colophon Vasseur had added unbidden at the foot of the last column, a line Feron had not mentioned:

*Set in type by those who would not be silent. Val-Brume remembers.*

She folded it away. Spring would bring the thaw, the soldiers, the tribunals, the long reckoning — and she would be here for all of it, with ink on her fingers and a shelf to fill and a town's testimony to keep. But that was spring's business.

Tonight the record was safe, the century had turned, and for the first time in her adult life, Elodie Sandoval owed her next day to no one's orders at all.

Down in the valley, unbeknownst to any of them, a rider was already saddling a horse in the dark — carrying the first reply from the world outside, addressed to Madame Liorans, marked with a seal none of them had ever hoped to see again.
