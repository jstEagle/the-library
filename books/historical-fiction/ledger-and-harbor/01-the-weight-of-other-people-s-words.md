## 1. The Weight of Other People's Words

The fog came off the harbor before dawn and stayed all day, the way it did in October, as if the sea had decided the town had seen enough of itself for one year. Jonah Blackwood drove through it with the lamps lit at half past nine in the morning, hands easy on the wheel of the Marlowe motorcar, thinking about nothing in particular because thinking about things in particular was a habit she'd given up along with belonging to anybody.

She was twenty-six years old and she drove for a living, which was more than most women in Harrow Point could say, and less than it sounded. Driving meant being useful. Useful meant present. Present meant invisible — and invisibility, Jonah had learned, was a kind of rent-free lodging. You could live your whole life in other people's houses that way, if you didn't mind never once being expected.

The letters sat on the seat beside her in a leather satchel, forty-one of them by her count, because she always counted. She counted them when Edith Marlowe handed the satchel over — *the post run, Miss Blackwood, if you'd be so good* — and she counted them again at each stop, and she would count them once more when she brought back whatever replies the day had shaken loose. Forty-one envelopes, sealed, addressed, none of them hers.

She had read every single one.

Not this morning's batch — those were still virgin wax and folded secrets — but she'd read their kind for three years now, and she had become, without ever deciding to be, an expert in other people's correspondence. It wasn't difficult. It wasn't even dishonest, she told herself, not really. Steam from the kettle, a letter held at an angle over the spout, patience, and the flap lifted like a small mouth opening. She resealed them with a dab of flour paste she kept in a tin behind the garage shelf, and in three years no one had ever noticed, which told her something either about her skill or about how little attention anyone paid to a driver.

*It's not as though they're addressed to me*, she reminded the fog. The fog declined to answer. The fog had heard the speech before.

The first stop was the Verrall house on Chapel Row, where Mrs. Verrall received her sister's letters from Canada and read them aloud to anyone trapped in the parlor, whether they wished to be or not. Jonah carried the envelope to the door, was pulled inside against her will by the gravity of Mrs. Verrall's loneliness, endured eleven minutes of news about wheat and a nephew named Albert who had opinions about the church, and escaped with a reply that smelled of lavender and desperation.

Back in the car she held the reply up to the gray light, considered it, and slid it unopened into the satchel.

Some letters you didn't need to steam. Some people told you everything whether you wanted it or not. That, too, was a form of literacy she'd acquired on the job.

* *

The harbor was working, barely. Three boats out, the rest tied up with their hawsers slack, and men standing along the pier in knots of two and three, not fishing, not mending nets, just standing the way men stand when there's a number being argued about somewhere above their heads. The shipping company had cut the tonnage rates again in September. Jonah knew this the way she knew everything — sideways, secondhand, through other people's talk that passed over her head and settled around her shoulders like the fog.

"Morning, missus." A boy of maybe fourteen, hauling a barrow of ice, nodded at her as she passed the fish market. He called every woman in a motorcar *missus*. It was his entire taxonomy of the world.

"Morning," she said, and drove on, and thought: *I'm nobody's missus. I'm nobody's anything.* Then she thought: *don't be maudlin before lunch, Blackwood, it's undignified,* and took the hill road toward Tregenna Street, where the next six letters waited in houses whose occupants she could have described to a portrait painter without ever having been introduced to a single one of them.

There was Mr. Cade at number 9, who wrote to his son every week and got nothing back, and whose replies therefore had to be written by Jonah herself at the kitchen table while he dictated, pacing, his good hand clenched behind his back. There were the Pellow sisters at 14, who between them produced correspondence with a missionary society, a Member of Parliament, and — this was the village's worst-kept secret — each other's suitors, in a long-running campaign of sabotage conducted entirely by post. There was Widow Trevanion at 22, who received one letter a month from a solicitor in Plymouth, and whose face went the color of the harbor water each time, and who always asked Jonah to wait while she read it alone in the parlor, and who always cried, and who always came back to the door composed and tipped her sixpence as though the crying had been part of the service.

Jonah delivered to all of them. She smiled at all of them. She knew all of them down to the grain of their griefs, and not one of them knew her at all.

That was the arrangement. That was the bargain she'd struck with the world somewhere around the age of nineteen, when the alternative had been going home to her father's house and watching him drink himself through what remained of the family money. Her father — dockworker, thirty years on the pier, struck off the company books two winters ago for what the foreman called *agitation*, meaning he'd said a sentence aloud that other men had only thought — her father lived now in a rented room above the Anchor with a bottle for company, and when Jonah visited him on Sundays he talked about the old days as though the pier had been a country that had since sunk beneath the sea.

"You drive for that Marlowe woman," he'd said last Sunday. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's coat.

"I drive," Jonah had agreed. "That's all it is."

"That's never all anything is." He'd looked at her with eyes gone yellow at the rims. "You think the world runs on driving? The world runs on names, girl. On who keeps them and who crosses them off."

She'd left early that Sunday. She'd driven the long way home along the cliff road with the fog pressing at the windows like something wanting in, and she had decided, firmly, not to think about it. This was her principal talent, she sometimes suspected: not the driving, not the steaming of envelopes, but the firm decision not to think.

* *

By noon the fog had thinned to a gauze and the town showed through it in patches: wet slate roofs, the square tower of St. Elwyn's, the great pale block of the shipping company offices at the harbor head, four stories of granite that watched the waterfront the way a bailiff watches a debtor. Edith Marlowe's organizing rooms occupied the ground floor of a narrow house on Fore Street, wedged between a chandler's and a shuttered dressmaker's, and it was there that Jonah pointed the car at half past twelve, satchel lightened to nine letters and heavy with perhaps fifteen replies.

Miss Marlowe — she insisted on *miss*, though she was fifty-one and had buried the man who might have made her *mrs* some thirty years ago and had never once sounded wounded by it — Miss Marlowe was at the big table in the back room with her glasses pushed up into her iron-gray hair, writing. She wrote the way other people breathed; it was simply what her body did between decisions.

"The Verrall woman cornered you," she said without looking up. "You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face of a woman who has heard about Albert."

"Albert has opinions about the church," Jonah said, setting the satchel down. "I now know several of them. One involves the organist."

"Heaven preserve us." Edith signed something, blotted it, and finally looked up, and her eyes did the thing they always did, the quick inventory — coat, hands, face, the particular set of Jonah's jaw — as though reading a letter Jonah hadn't known she'd written. "And the rest?"

"Delivered. Replies in the bag. Mrs. Trevanion sends her regards and sixpence."

"Poor soul." Something moved behind Edith's eyes — not pity exactly; pity was too soft a word for the machinery that turned there. "Put the kettle on, would you? And then there's the afternoon round. The Perranworthys' cook has been asking after you."

"Asking after me or asking after the motorcar?"

"With Mrs. Perranworthy's cook the distinction is largely theoretical."

Jonah found herself smiling — the real one, the rare one, the one she kept in a drawer and only opened in this room — and went to fill the kettle, and hated how much she liked being known here, and knew that even here she was staff. Belonging everywhere as the help; nowhere as Jonah Blackwood. Her whole life was a servants' entrance with a view.

In the front room, the organizing rooms proper, the morning's debris of the cause lay scattered: leaflets in stacks, a typewriter with a ribbon gone gray, a wall map of the county bristling with pins, and pinned beside it a list of women registered to attend the meeting in Penzance next month. *Votes for Women* in Edith's own upright hand across the top. Jonah glanced at it the way she glanced at everything — quickly, thoroughly, filing it away — and felt the familiar detachment settle over the subject like a coat. Suffrage. Strikes. The great machinery of history grinding along overhead. All very fine for bolder souls. For women like Edith Marlowe, who had been born with a spine like a keel. For girls like the Pellow sisters, who had nothing to lose but sixpences. Jonah had a motorcar to keep fueled, a father to be disappointed by, and a talent for not thinking, and those, taken together, were a full life. Weren't they?

The kettle began its low climb toward whistling.

"Miss Blackwood." Edith had come to the doorway, wiping her pen-nib on a cloth, and there was a tone in her voice that Jonah had learned to hear the way sailors hear weather. Not alarm. Weight. "When you've done the tea. There's a matter I'd like your opinion on."

"My opinion," Jonah repeated. People asked Edith Marlowe for many things. Opinion, from her driver, was not among the standard requests.

"You're the only person I know," Edith said, "who reads absolutely everything and takes responsibility for none of it. Sometimes that's precisely the perspective required."

Jonah stood with the teapot in her hand and felt, for the first time that day, genuinely cold — a draft from somewhere, or the fog finding a way indoors, or the sensation of a floor shifting a half-inch beneath a person who had believed the floor was the one solid thing in her life.

"There's a book," Edith said quietly. "A ledger. I keep it in the back room, under the floorboard by the stove. And I think it's time somebody besides me laid eyes on it — somebody with no name worth crossing off."

Somewhere out on the water a foghorn spoke, twice, low and patient, like a man clearing his throat before saying the thing that can't be unsaid. Jonah set down the teapot. Her hands, she noticed with distant interest, had gone perfectly steady — steadier than they'd been all day — as though some buried part of her had already understood that the life she'd been driving through these three years had just come to a fork in the road, and that neither direction led anywhere she recognized.

"All right," she heard herself say. "Show me."
