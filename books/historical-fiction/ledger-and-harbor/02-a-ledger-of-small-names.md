## 2. A Ledger of Small Names

The rain came in off the water that October morning the way it always did in Harbor's End — not falling so much as arriving sideways, with intent, as if it had somewhere to be and resented the town for standing in its way. I drove through it with the windshield wipers going like a metronome and Edith Marlowe beside me reading a letter by the grey light, her spectacles low on her nose, her mouth doing that thing it did when she read: a small, involuntary tightening at one corner, like a door being held against wind.

"Mrs. Tregaskis writes that her son was turned away from the hiring hall again," she said. "Third time this month. He signed the association card in September."

"That seems to be the price of signing," I said.

"It is the price of *being seen*," Edith corrected, and folded the letter into her bag with the particular care she gave to documents — not the tenderness one shows love letters but the firmness one shows ammunition.

I had been driving for Edith Marlowe fourteen months by then, and I could have recited the geography of her days blindfolded: the organizing rooms above the chandler's on Quay Street, the chapel basement where the women met on Thursdays, the long run up the coast road to Truro where the solicitor kept the suffrage society's papers because he believed — correctly, as it would turn out — that no paper was safe within ten miles of the harbor. She paid me eleven shillings a week and never once asked about my family, my history, or my opinions, which suited me admirably. A driver is furniture that talks. I had spent most of my life being furniture, and I had come to regard invisibility not as an insult but as a professional qualification.

The trouble with Edith Marlowe was that she refused to let furniture remain furniture.

"Jonah," she said, as we came down out of the hills toward the gray smear of the harbor, "stop at the house before the rooms. There is something I want you to see."

"Something to drive, or something to carry?"

"Neither." She put away her spectacles. "Something to know."

I want to record, for the sake of accuracy, that I did not want to know things. Knowing was a liability. In fourteen months of reading other people's mail — a habit I will account for elsewhere, and which I defended then as idle curiosity, the way a man defends drinking by calling it thirst — I had learned precisely one lesson: that information is a weight, and that most people are crushed by far lighter loads than the truth. I delivered letters. I did not open doors. That was the arrangement I had made with my own conscience, and it had held for thirty-one years without so much as a squeak of the hinges.

But she said *something to know* in the voice she used when she had already decided, and so I turned the motorcar up Fore Street instead of toward Quay Street, and the rain followed us all the way to her door.

* * *

Edith's house stood at the top of the hill where the fog went last and arrived first, a narrow gray terrace house with geraniums in the window that she grew with more success than she grew anything else, though she'd deny it. Inside it smelled of coal smoke and ink. The front room was given over entirely to work: two desks, a filing cabinet with a broken lock she'd replaced with a leather strap, maps of the county pinned to the wall with pins colored red and black according to some system she had never explained and I had never asked about.

She locked the front door behind us. Then, and I noted this even then, she crossed to the window and looked down the street both ways, an old woman checking for weather, except there was no weather to check for but rain, and we were both of us soaked in it already.

"Sit down, Jonah."

"I'm dripping on your carpet."

"The carpet has survived worse." She knelt at the hearth, moved aside a loose brick I had never once noticed in a room I knew better than my own lodgings, and drew out a book.

Not a grand book. That was the first shock of it, if shock it can be called — I had expected, from the ceremony of it, something bound in calfskin with a clasp, and what she set on the desk between us was a school exercise book, the sort sold three for tuppence at the stationer's, its cover soft with handling, its corners furred. A cheap blue notebook such as any child might carry to arithmetic lessons.

"What is it?" I asked, though I knew. Some part of me had known since summer, since the night I'd seen her writing by lamplight past two in the morning and heard, through the wall of the lodging where I then stayed across the lane, the scratch of a nib going on and on without pause, like a small animal digging.

"It's a list," Edith said.

"You make lists all day. Subscription lists, attendance lists—"

"This is a list of names." She opened the book flat on the desk and turned it to face me. "Every name the company wants gone."

Now. Before I go further, let me tell you what Harbor's End was in the autumn of 1913, because the word *company* will not do its work otherwise. The Cornish Consolidated Shipping Company owned the pier, the warehouses, the hiring hall, the cottages on Company Row, the company store where Company scrip passed for money, and — though no deed said so — the constable, the coroner, and half the clergy of the parish. When the men struck in September over the piece rates and the safety of the east quay, which had killed two boys in August, the company did not send negotiators. It sent lists.

That much I knew. Everyone in the town knew it the way you know the tide tables. What I did not know was that anyone had thought to write it down.

I looked at the page.

It was ruled in Edith's upright, unhurried hand, each entry taking its own line, dated, initialled. Not accusations — records. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole book.

*11 Sept. — William Tregaskis, 34, roper. Dismissed same day as strike vote. Wife, Maud, struck from cleaning roster at Company Hall. Four children.*

*13 Sept. — Peter Curnow, 51, craneman. Eviction notice posted on cottage door, no grounds stated. Family sheltering with sister-in-law, Penzance Rd.*

*15 Sept. — Mrs. Ellen Pascoe, widow. Blacklisted from company store after speaking at Thursday meeting. Purchases now made by neighbors; she signs their books.*

Page after page. Dockworkers dismissed. Women struck from rosters. Boys of sixteen denied their fathers' berths. Men summoned to the hiring hall and offered reinstatement on condition they sign statements renouncing the association — and the names of those who signed, recorded too, in a different ink, because Edith Marlowe did not hold a frightened man's signature against him, only the fear that produced it.

"How many?" I asked. My voice came out strange, flattened, like a voice heard through a wall.

"One hundred and forty-two names as of Tuesday." She said it the way another woman might say how many eggs she needed. "Some entries have three names under them — a man, his wife, his son. The company punishes households. So I record households."

"And what is it *for*, Edith?" And here I confess the wretchedness of what I felt, which was not horror, not yet — horror came later, late as always, arriving like a guest who missed the train. What I felt first was the driver's cold arithmetic. "One day someone will ask you what this book is, and every answer is a bad one. If you say it's evidence, you're admitting you expect a crime. If you say it's memory, you're admitting you expect the truth to be destroyed. Either way you're holding a document that says the shipping company disappears people, written in your own hand, dated and initialled, and the moment anyone official sees it, it stops being insurance and becomes a confession. It isn't a ledger. It's suicide written in ink."

She listened to all of it with her hands folded, the way she listened to deputations of angry fishermen, giving the argument the respect of her stillness.

"Say it again," she said, when I finished. "The last part."

"Suicide written in ink."

"Yes." She nodded slowly, almost pleased. "You've understood it exactly. Now tell me why I keep it anyway."

"Because you're stubborn?"

"Because names are the whole war, Jonah." She turned the pages back to the beginning, and the lamplight moved across the entries like water. "A company can dismiss a man and call it economy. It can evict a family and call it property. It can starve a widow and call it discipline. Every one of those acts leaves no mark unless someone marks it. They are counting on there being no record — on the town's fear being so complete that what happens here happens nowhere, that these hundred and forty-two people simply *cease*, quietly, the way a name wears off a gate. Well." She closed the book gently, the way you close a coffin lid, I thought, and hated myself for thinking it. "They cease in this book instead. This book says: these people existed. These things were done. On these dates. By this company. And one day, when there is an inquiry, or a journalist, or a government with a conscience, this book will stand up in a court of law and say their names aloud."

"And until that day?"

"Until that day it sits under a brick."

"And if the wrong person learns it's there?"

She looked at me then, and I saw — for the first time, I think, truly saw — that she had already asked herself that question every day for months, and had answered it, and had gone on writing anyway. That is the thing about courage that the songs get wrong. It is not the absence of fear. It is fear that has been given a job.

"Then I will have been foolish," she said, "in excellent company."

* * *

I should have left it there. I should have driven her to Quay Street, carried her bags, read somebody's letter on the sly that evening, and remained what I had always been, which was present and uninvolved, the fly on history's wall.

But as she lifted the book to return it to the brick, a page slipped loose — a loose leaf, tucked between the ruled pages, covered in a different hand. Not hers. A cramped, hurried hand, the letters leaning like men walking against wind. And halfway down the page, in that stranger's scrawl, I read a name I knew better than my own.

*Blackwood, T. — quay gang no. 3. Warned off association, Aug. Refused. Shifted to night unloading, no extra pay. Reported "accident-prone" to foreman, Sept. — watch him.*

Thomas Blackwood. My father. Thirty years on the quays. A man who had once, in my childhood, lifted a bale of jute the size of a church pew as easily as a loaf of bread, and who now climbed into lorries with a cough he hid from my mother. A man whose entire rebellious life consisted of refusing to sign one piece of paper — and who had therefore been entered into another.

"Jonah." Edith's voice, quiet. "You've gone white as your collar."

"My father," I said. It did not sound like my voice. "That's my father's name."

She did not pretend surprise, and somehow that was worse — and better too, I suppose, because it meant she had known whose daughter drove her motorcar all this time, and had said nothing, and had let me find my own family among the disappeared at my own pace, the way you let a wound announce itself.

"He refused to sign the renunciation," she said. "In August. He stood in the hiring hall in front of forty men and told the foreman where he could put his paper. He used language I won't repeat." Almost, she smiled. "He is braver than he knows, your father. Most of them are. That is rather the point of the book."

I sat down on the edge of her hard little chair and looked at the loose page again. *Watch him.* Two words in a stranger's hand, and my chest went tight as a drumhead. All those years I had kept myself out of everything — the strikes, the suffrage processions, the petitions, the quarrels at supper — on the theory that history was a game played by other people, louder people, and that my business was to stay dry on the pavement while they marched through the rain. And all the while my own father's name had been accumulating consequences in a cheap blue notebook, entry by entry, date by date, and I had been driving past the hiring hall every week of my life without once looking at the men on the pier as anything but background. As scenery. As staff.

"Take the book back," I said finally. My hands were not steady. "Under the brick. Please."

She slid the loose leaf inside, closed the covers, and knelt, fitting the notebook into its hollow in the hearth with the ease of long practice, replacing the brick so exactly that no seam showed.

"Jonah," she said, rising, brushing ash from her skirt. "I didn't show you this to frighten you. And I didn't show you this to recruit you. I showed you because if anything should happen to me — illness, accident, a fall on wet cobbles, whatever form their tidiness takes — someone besides me must know it exists. Someone with a steady hand on a steering wheel and no reputation to speak of." Her eyes, behind the spectacles, were quite dry and very clear. "You belong everywhere as staff and nowhere as yourself. I've watched you for fourteen months. It's a fine disguise. But disguises fail, and when yours does, I want you to know where the brick is."

I said nothing. What was there to say? That I wanted no part of it? That my part had been chosen for me the day I was born to Thomas Blackwood of quay gang number three?

We drove down to Quay Street through the thinning rain. At the corner of Fore Street, where the road bends and you can see the whole harbor laid out below — the cranes, the masts, the east quay with its new timber looking raw as a scar — Edith leaned forward suddenly and touched my arm.

"Stop the car a moment."

I stopped. Below us, at the foot of the pier, a lorry stood with its engine running, and men in uniform were climbing out of it — not dockers' uniforms, not navy. Soldiers. Six of them, then eight, unloading crates marked with stenciled letters I could not read at that distance but understood perfectly nonetheless. And at the head of the pier, talking with the harbormaster, stood a tall officer in a colonel's cap, gesturing at the buildings with a rolled paper, the way a man gestures at rooms he is deciding how to furnish.

"Well," said Edith Marlowe softly, beside me, watching her town begin to be taken. "That's sooner than I expected. Drive home, Jonah. Quickly — and take the long way past my house."
