## 3. Requisition

The gulls woke me before the soldiers did, which is the last ordinary thing I can say about that morning.

I had slept badly in the little room above the garage where I kept my bed — not from worry, for I had no worries worth the name in those days, but because the fog had come in thick off the water and pressed against the window like a hand. The harbor town of Marrow's Reach does its best work in fog. It muffles the fish carts and the argument at the chandlery and the sound of the five o'clock whistle, and it lets a person believe the world is smaller and kinder than the maps claim.

I dressed, went down, and cranked the motorcar awake — Edith's Hupmobile, a black, high-shouldered thing that smelled of petrol and leather and, increasingly, of me, since I spent more hours in its driver's seat than anywhere else on earth. I was to collect her at half past seven for the run out to Trelawney's farm, where the poultry women were meeting to discuss the cooperative. A dull errand. A good errand. The kind of errand I preferred: useful, unremarkable, beneath anyone's attention.

The fog was lifting off the harbor in rags when I came down Fore Street, and that is how I saw them first as silhouettes — figures moving along the pier with a purposefulness that did not belong to fishermen. Fishermen lope and slouch and stop to spit. These men walked in step.

I stopped the car and watched, telling myself it was a drill. The navy drilled sometimes; the coastguard too. Men liked uniforms and marching; it was a harmless appetite, like whistling.

Then I saw them take the telegraph office.

Two of them went up the steps and one remained at the door, rifle slung, and the clerk — young Mr. Cade, who blushed when he sold you stamps — stood in the doorway arguing, his hands going like a man swatting flies, until a sergeant put one palm flat against his chest and moved him back inside without any violence at all, just a sort of patient arithmetic, and shut the door behind him.

My hands were cold on the wheel. I remember noticing that as if it were happening to someone else — *my hands are cold* — while the rest of me kept insisting there was an explanation, there was always an explanation, the world did not simply change its face between one dawn and the next.

It had, though. By the time I reached the square, the town hall had soldiers on its steps and a Union Jack that had not flown there since the coronation, and a corporal was nailing a paper to the noticeboard with four blows of a hammer that sounded, in the empty morning, like someone knocking on a door that would never be answered.

I should have driven past. Any sensible person would have driven past. But I got out — I still don't know why, except that Jonah Blackwood has always had a fatal instinct for standing where she shouldn't — and read the notice.

*By order of Colonel R. Ashworth, commanding, martial law is declared within the borough of Marrow's Reach and environs for the duration of the labor dispute. Assemblies of more than three persons are prohibited. The pier is closed to all traffic. All correspondence is subject to inspection. Offenders will be dealt with according to military regulation.*

*All correspondence is subject to inspection.*

I read that line three times. Somewhere behind me a door opened and shut quickly, the way doors open and shut when whole streets of people are watching through their curtains and pretending not to.

"For the duration of the labor dispute," I said aloud, to nobody, and heard how absurd it sounded. The strike was three weeks old. Durations, I understood even then, are whatever the man with the rifles says they are.

A soldier near the steps caught me looking and looked back — not hostile, merely cataloguing. Young. Freckled under his cap. He had the bored, embarrassed face of a boy doing a job he'd been told was important and hadn't yet found a way to believe. I got back in the car. My legs worked. That surprised me.

* * *

Edith was already dressed when I reached her house on Harrow Lane, which meant she had known before I did, or guessed, or simply refused to be caught abed by history. She opened the door before I knocked.

"You've seen," she said. Not a question.

"The town hall. The pier. The telegraph office." I hesitated. "They're inspecting correspondence, Edith."

Something crossed her face — quick, gone, like a fish under ice. "Then we'll drive to Trelawney's as planned," she said, "and behave like two women going to count chickens."

"Edith—"

"Jonah." She took my arm at the elbow, her grip light and absolute, the grip she used on crowds and colonels alike. "Panic is a luxury. We can't afford it before breakfast."

Inside, the house was exactly as always — the suffrage journals squared on the table, the teapot under its cozy, the smell of toast — but I saw it differently now, the way you see your own street from a train window, suddenly strange and finite. And on the side table by the window lay the ledger.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that morning. It lay there as it always lay there: a fat exercise book, marbled covers gone soft at the corners, wrapped in brown paper and string as though it were nothing more than household accounts. Every threatened dockworker. Every woman struck off the shipping company's lists for asking about wages. Every name the company wanted unwritten. Insurance, Edith called it. I had called it something else, privately, and had been ashamed of the word even as I thought it.

"Put it away," I said. "Today of all days. Put it somewhere."

"It lives here." She poured tea as though the world were intact. "Hiding it would be an admission."

"An admission of what? Sense?"

She smiled at me over the pot, that patient smile that made me feel simultaneously nineteen years old and eighty. "Jonah. If they come, they come. Tea?"

They came at nine o'clock.

* * *

I heard the lorry first — engines carry strangely in that town, down the lanes and around the church, so that you hear everything twice — and then boots on the cobbles, many boots, in step, and Edith set down her cup with a small click that I will hear until I die.

"Back door?" I said.

"And run where?" she said. "The garden gate opens onto a lane full of soldiers. Sit down, dear. You're a driver. Drivers sit."

There were six of them at the door and an officer I didn't know, a captain with a list, and the whole transaction was conducted with such courtesy that it took my mind a full minute to understand what was being done to us. Warrant — well, not a warrant precisely, a requisition order, signed under the emergency provisions. A search for documents bearing names. Nothing personal, madam, purely administrative. Would Mrs. Marlowe remain seated. Would the young lady identify herself.

"Miss Blackwood," I said. "Mrs. Marlowe's driver."

"Very good, miss." He wrote it down. My name, going into their book. Even then some part of me noted the symmetry: everyone keeping ledgers now.

They went through the house the way tide goes through a wreck — thorough, unhurried, impersonal. Drawers slid out and slid home. Letters shaken from their envelopes and read standing up, four hands apiece. One soldier held Edith's photograph of her dead mother and asked if she required it returned to its place, and she said thank you, yes, and he set it down exactly as it had been, and I thought: *they are polite, they are so polite, why does politeness frighten me more than shouting would.*

Because it meant they expected no resistance. Because it meant this was routine now, or intended to become routine. Because a man who shouts has decided you are an enemy, but a man who is gentle has decided you are furniture.

They found the suffrage journals. They found the minutes of the women's cooperative. They found a bundle of letters from dockworkers' wives — begging letters, Edith called them, though I knew from long practice that they were more than that, they were testimony written by women who didn't know they were testifying — and the captain stacked these with a rubber band and said, "These will need to be retained, madam," and Edith said, "Naturally," in a voice like a drawn blade wrapped in flannel.

And the ledger sat on the side table by the window, under its brown paper and string, ten feet from the captain's elbow.

I saw him see it. I am sure of it — sure the way you're sure of a thing afterward, reconstructing the moment a hundred times. His eyes passed across it the way eyes pass across anything in a room being searched: lamp, clock, parcel, done. A parcel among parcels. Why would a searched house's most dangerous object sit in plain view wearing wrapping paper?

Because I had moved it. That was the thing I could not explain then and cannot fully explain now. Between the lorry engine and the knock — perhaps ninety seconds — I had crossed the room, taken the ledger off the side table, and carried it out the front door and down the path to the motorcar, walking at exactly the pace of a woman fetching something forgotten from a car, a scarf, a driving rug, nothing. I had knelt at the rear of the Hupmobile, opened the floor panel over the spare tire well, laid the book flat atop the canvas-covered spare, and closed the panel, and dusted my hands, and gone back inside, and all of it without one conscious thought, the way you catch a falling cup without deciding to.

Fear arrived late. That's the truth of it. All through the search I stood by the mantelpiece with my hands folded, answering questions about my employment and my people — father a stevedore, deceased; mother kept lodgings — steady as a servant, invisible as one, and felt nothing but a curious professional interest in how searches were conducted. The fear was still in the lane somewhere, walking toward me at its own pace.

It arrived all at once when the captain said, "That will be all," and clicked his heels, and the six of them tramped out, and the front door shut, and Edith and I stood in the ruined quiet of her drawing room with the letters gone and the journals gone and every drawer pulled slightly wrong, like a face after crying.

"They didn't find it," I said.

"No."

"They didn't find it, Edith. It's in the car. In the tire well. I put it—" and that was when my knees informed me what my composure had been withholding, and I sat down abruptly in a chair that wasn't mine, and began, horribly, to laugh, or something adjacent to laughing, while Edith watched me with an expression I had never seen on her before and would not learn the name of for several days.

It was not gratitude. It was appraisal.

* * *

We drove to Trelawney's after all. Edith insisted; the poultry women were meeting, and meetings of fewer than four, she observed grimly, appeared to remain legal. I drove the way I always drove, both hands on the wheel, obeying every regulation, and underneath the floor of the car, under my feet the whole way, rode the names of forty-one people the army wanted to forget.

At the crossroads by the mill, a sentry stepped out and raised his hand, and I braked, and my heart performed a slow, enormous somersault.

"Papers?" he said.

"I'm driving Mrs. Marlowe to a farm," I said. "Is a paper required to drive to a farm?"

He looked in the windows. He looked at Edith, who gazed back with the mild interest of a duchess at a horse show. He looked at me longer — drivers are furniture too, but furniture gets inspected — and then he waved us on, and I drove fifty yards before my hands started shaking, and a hundred before I could speak.

"That will happen every mile from now on," Edith said quietly. "You understand that."

"I understand it."

"And the thing under your feet makes every one of those miles a hanging offense. For you, not for me. I'm old and notorious; they'll only question me. You they'll make an example of." She turned and looked at me directly, and I kept my eyes on the road because I could not bear whatever was in hers. "I didn't ask you to move it, Jonah."

"I know you didn't."

"I want that understood. Whatever comes of it, I want it understood that I never asked."

"Understood," I said, and drove on, and the fog was burning off the fields in long white banners, and the day was turning beautiful, which felt like an obscenity.

We counted chickens. God help us, we actually counted chickens, Edith taking notes in a shorthand notebook while Mrs. Trelawney explained the economics of eggs, and outside the farmhouse window the coastal road ran empty toward town, and once, far off, we heard the lorry engines going back and forth along it like a dog patrolling a fence line.

It was on the drive home, in the gold of late afternoon, that Edith said the thing that ended my old life, though I didn't know it yet.

"They'll come back for the ledger," she said. "Today was a census. Next time is a harvest. And I can't hide it, Jonah — they watch this house now, and an old woman burying a book in her garden is a pantomime. It needs to leave Marrow's Reach entirely. To someone who prints things." She named no name, but I had read enough of her correspondence over three years to supply one myself: Samuel Reeve, the city desk man who wrote under initials because his last employer had been sued into ruin.

"Ninety miles," I said. "Three checkpoints between here and the ferry gate, once they finish building them. They're building them, Edith. I saw the timber stacked by the bridge."

"Yes," she said. "I know."

And she looked at me then — really looked, the appraisal again, weighing me like cargo, like ballast, like something whose weight mattered enormously — and I gripped the wheel and watched the road and said nothing, because the terrible truth was that some voice in me, a voice I had spent thirty-one years refusing to hear, had already answered *yes*.
