## 4. The Colonel's Courtesy

The motorcar came up the lane at half past ten, moving at the pace of a funeral procession, and Jonah knew before she saw the pennant on its bonnet that this was not a social call. Nobody drove that slowly in Brackwater unless they wanted to be seen — unless they wanted the whole town to watch them arrive and remember it.

She was polishing the windscreen when the staff car rounded the hedge. Two soldiers in the back, rifles between their knees like sleeping dogs. A third beside the driver. And in the rear seat, alone, a man in a colonel's uniform so immaculate it looked ironed onto him, his hands folded over a leather document case as though he were on his way to church.

Jonah set down the cloth. Her heart did something strange — it didn't race; it went very quiet, very deliberate, like a clock deciding how much time remained.

"Edith," she called through the open door of the house. "Company."

Edith Marlowe appeared in the doorway wiping flour from her hands — she had been baking bread all morning, an act Jonah now understood as either defiance or denial — and looked down the lane at the car with the expression of a woman assessing weather.

"So soon," she said. Not a question. "Put the kettle on, Jonah. We'll not have them say we're uncivilized."

* * *

Colonel Reginald Ashworth stepped out of the car the way a man steps into his own drawing room. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with a face built for portraits — strong jaw, level eyes, the kind of handsome that photographs well and remembers nothing. He removed his cap and held it against his chest.

"Mrs. Marlowe." His voice was warm. That was the first horror of him: the warmth. "Forgive the intrusion. I'm Colonel Ashworth. I've taken temporary command of the district."

"Temporary," Edith repeated, extending her hand. "How long is temporary, Colonel?"

"However long the labor dispute requires." He shook her hand with genuine courtesy, and Jonah, watching from the doorway with the tea tray not yet assembled, saw his eyes make their circuit of the house — the windows, the garden path, the shed where the motorcar sat, the curtains, the chimney. He counted everything. She understood, in a cold drop through her stomach, that he had already been here. Or someone had. The search two days ago had turned the house inside out and found nothing, but men like this did not search once. Men like this searched until they found, or until they made finding unnecessary.

"Will you come in, Colonel?" Edith said. "I've fresh bread, and my housekeeper makes tea that could reconcile England and Ireland."

A flicker crossed Ashworth's face — amusement, or its professional imitation. "I'd be honored."

"Jonah," Edith said pleasantly, "tea for four."

Four. The two soldiers would wait by the car, then. Which meant the fourth cup was for whoever else came through that door, or for no one, or for the dead. Jonah went inside and put her hands to work because her hands knew what to do even when the rest of her had gone somewhere high and distant, watching from the ceiling like a fly.

* * *

Edith watched the colonel settle into her good chair — the one by the window, the one her late husband had bought in Brighton thirty years ago — and thought: *he has done this before.* Not here. Somewhere else. India, perhaps, or South Africa, or some other place where Englishmen in beautiful uniforms had arrived at the homes of troublesome people carrying courtesy like a bayonet wrapped in silk.

He complimented the room. He admired the view of the harbor, visible past the fishing boats, past the pier where his soldiers stood guard with fixed bayonets. He remarked that Brackwater was a fine town, hardworking, loyal.

"Loyal," Edith said, pouring. "That's an interesting word for a town you've occupied."

"I haven't occupied it, Mrs. Marlowe. I've *protected* it." He accepted his cup. "There is a difference, though I understand why the distinction might be lost on people who are frightened. Frightened people see soldiers and think of force. But force is only ever the last resort of a man who has failed at everything gentler."

"And what gentler things have you tried?"

"The shipping company reduced hours rather than dismiss the agitators outright. The magistrates offered mediation. The strike committee was invited to negotiate twice and refused both times." He sipped his tea and nodded approval at it — a small performance, she noted, the guest honoring the hostess. "You see? Patience. Courtesy. And what has it earned us? A pier that won't unload, a telegraph office full of saboteurs, and women—" his eyes rested on her, mild as milk "—carrying papers about the countryside like peddlers."

The word landed precisely where he intended it. Edith felt it land, and kept her face still, and was grateful for sixty years of practice at stillness.

"Papers," she said. "I write letters, Colonel. I am a correspondent of long standing. My correspondence concerns votes for women, which I understand is now legal to discuss, if nothing else."

"Votes for women." He smiled. "My wife has opinions on the subject. Strong ones. She marches in London when I'm posted away, which she assures me is coincidence." He set down his cup. "I don't arrest women for opinions, Mrs. Marlowe. I want to be clear about that. I have no quarrel with suffragists, with strikers, with any man or woman who believes the world should be otherwise than it is. Belief is free. It's the *paperwork* that troubles me."

"There it is," Edith said quietly.

"Pardon?"

"You've been circling it since your car came up the lane. Say it plainly. You'll enjoy it more."

Ashworth studied her for a moment, and something behind his eyes shifted — not anger, but a kind of professional respect, the look of a chess player whose opponent declines the opening gambit. "Very well. There are rumors, Mrs. Marlowe, of a document. A list. Names of dockworkers, of their families, of persons who have suffered various... difficulties during this dispute. A record." He folded his hands. "If such a document exists, it is dangerous. Not to me — to *them*. To every name written in it. Because while it exists, it marks those people as enemies of the Crown, and I cannot protect marked people. Do you see? I am offering protection. All I ask in return is any documents bearing names. Freely given. No searches. No arrests. A clean slate for everyone concerned."

*Insurance,* Edith thought. *He calls it insurance too.*

"You're offering to burn the evidence of what your soldiers have done," she said, "in exchange for the names of the people it was done to. So that afterward there will be no proof they ever suffered, and no proof anyone promised them anything. That isn't protection, Colonel. That's amnesia with a gun behind it."

The warmth left his face. It didn't become cruelty — that was the terrible thing — it became *administration*. A ledger closing. "Mrs. Marlowe. I have read your file. Fourteen years of organizing. Two imprisonments, hunger strikes, forced feeding — I know what you've endured, and I confess I admire it more than I can usefully say. You are a serious person. So let me be serious with you." He leaned forward. "Wars end badly for people who keep lists. I have seen it. In every country, in every decade. The list-keepers are always the last to understand that the list has made them the story. Don't be the story, Mrs. Marlowe. Be the old lady who writes letters about votes. Live to be ninety. Have a statue someday, if you like. Just give me the paper."

In the kitchen, Jonah heard every word through the door that Edith had left deliberately ajar, and understood that she was listening to a coffin being measured — politely, in centimeters, with tea.

* * *

Jonah carried in the second pot because the first had gone cold in her hands, which she chose to attribute to the kitchen window being open. Her hands were steady. She had checked them twice against the porcelain and they were steady, and this steadiness frightened her more than shaking would have, because it meant some part of her had already decided this was survivable, and she did not trust that part.

"Thank you," Ashworth said as she poured, and then, with the terrible casualness of men who hunt for sport: "You're the driver. Miss Blackwood, isn't it?"

"Yes, sir."

"They tell me you drive Mrs. Marlowe everywhere. Every meeting, every rally. Ninety miles to the city and back, sometimes in fog." He smiled at her. "Loyal staff are worth more than soldiers, in my experience. Soldiers follow orders. Staff follow *people*." He turned back to Edith. "You must trust her completely."

"With my life," Edith said, "several times over."

"How rare." He said it lightly, but Jonah felt the sentence close around her throat like a hand testing its grip. He wasn't asking whether Edith trusted her. He was telling her that she had been noticed, weighed, entered into some ledger of his own — because of course the man hunting a ledger kept one — and filed under *assets, hers.*

She poured his tea without spilling a drop and withdrew to her station by the sideboard, and there she stood, hands folded, spine straight, the perfect servant, invisible, while the two most dangerous sentences of her life were spoken across her mistress's tea table.

* * *

Edith watched Jonah pour and thought: *she is better at this than I am.* The girl had gone white as chalk when the car arrived — Edith had seen it, had seen the fear arrive late and then all at once, exactly as it always did with the young — and now she stood by the sideboard carved out of marble, pouring like a duchess's butler. Where had she learned that? What years of standing in other people's rooms, holding other people's coats, reading other people's— 

Edith stopped the thought. Not here. Not now.

"Colonel," she said, "let me offer you something plainer than tea. You believe the strike collapses if the organizers lose their nerve. You believe I am an organizer. You are offering me a trade dressed as mercy, and you are doing it in my parlor because you want me to feel that refusal happens *here*, in comfort, among the china — so that later, whatever you do, I will remember that I chose it over civility." She set down her cup. "Am I close?"

Ashworth laughed. It was a real laugh, brief and genuine, and somehow worse than everything before it. "They said you were formidable. They undersold you."

"They usually do. Now hear my counteroffer, since we're trading. Leave this town. Take your soldiers off the pier, reopen the telegraph office, and go back to wherever the War Office keeps colonels between unpleasantnesses. In exchange, I will forget your face, which will be a kindness, because it is not a face I wish to remember."

"Mrs. Marlowe—"

"No." For the first time her voice carried the full weight of fourteen years — the platform voice, the voice that had held three thousand women silent in Manchester so a single speaker could be heard. "You asked me to be serious, so I will be. I am an old woman, Colonel. I have buried a husband, two sisters, and every illusion I was born with. There is nothing in this house you can take from me that I will miss. But there are names — hundreds of them — belonging to people who are young, and frightened, and have children, and those names are *kept*, sir. Kept somewhere you will not find them, by means you have not imagined, and every day this occupation continues, the keeping gets more careful. That is my protection, and I extend it to no one."

The silence that followed had texture. Jonah could feel it pressing on her eardrums.

Ashworth rose. He collected his cap, squared it under his arm, and became once again entirely courteous. "You've given me a great deal to consider."

"I've given you nothing. That's the point."

"At the door, he paused — and this, Jonah thought afterward, was the moment she would replay for months, the moment she would testify about — he paused and said, almost gently:

"One thing more, Mrs. Marlowe. Your clerk. Mr. Pettifer. A nervous sort of fellow. Debts, I'm told — his brother's debts, actually, which is a cruel inheritance. Nervous men talk, eventually. Not from wickedness. From arithmetic." He settled his cap on his head. "I mention it only because I'd hate for you to learn it too late. Good day, ladies. Thank you for the tea. It really was excellent."

The door closed. The car pulled away at that same funeral pace, wanting to be seen, wanting the town to watch it leave and do the arithmetic themselves.

* * *

For a long moment neither woman moved. Then Edith sat down in the colonel's vacated chair, quite suddenly, like a puppet with a cut string, and pressed both hands flat against her knees, and Jonah saw that they were trembling.

"He knows about the ledger," Edith said. "Not where it is. But that it exists, and that I have it, or had it. Someone told him."

"Pettifer," Jonah said. The name came out flat and strange, a stone dropped in a well.

"He hasn't confirmed it. He *offered* it — that's different, that's a hook dressed as honesty. But yes." Edith closed her eyes. "Oh, that poor wretched boy. They've bought him with his brother's neck."

"Then we run. Tonight. Both of us, in the car, we drive straight through—"

"Through three checkpoints with every road out of this town wired and watched?" Edith opened her eyes, and they were dry and terribly clear. "No, Jonah. Listen to me. If we both run, we confirm everything, and he takes the town apart plank by plank until he finds it. One of us stays and gives him a face to watch. The other goes and makes sure the names get *out* — to the city, to the newspapers, to a man named Samuel Reeve who owes me a favor and prints things the government wishes he didn't." She reached out and gripped Jonah's wrist, hard, her strength sudden and astonishing. "Ninety miles. Three checkpoints. You drive laundry deliveries to the city every month and no soldier alive looks twice at a laundry van."

"Edith—"

"It has to be you." Her voice cracked, just once, just barely, and then reassembled itself. "Because I'm the one he's watching, and because you—" she stopped, and something moved across her face that might have been apology, "—because you're the only one I trust to carry it. God help me, girl, I've known that for a year. I've watched you read my post."

The floor tilted. Jonah heard herself say, "You knew?"

"I've known since the winter. You hold the envelopes too long. You think no one notices, but I notice everything — it's the whole of my profession." Edith almost smiled. "And I never stopped you, because whatever you found in those letters made you into someone who could do this. Now go and look at the spare tire well, and then pack a bag, and then drive me into town so I can be seen buying ribbon like a foolish old woman, because tomorrow morning, Miss Blackwood, you are going to be the bravest liar in England."

Outside, the gulls wheeled over the occupied pier, screaming their one note, and the fog was already coming in off the water like a page being turned.
