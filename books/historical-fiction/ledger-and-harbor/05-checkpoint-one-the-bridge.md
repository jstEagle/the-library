## 5. Checkpoint One: The Bridge

I volunteered before I understood what I was volunteering for.

That is the honest accounting of it, and I have had three years of practice in honest accounting — the private kind, conducted at midnight over other people's correspondence — so I might as well be exact. Edith did not ask me. She stood in her wrecked parlor with the drawers pulled out around her like entrails, soldiers' boot-prints still ghosted in the carpet dust, and she said, "They will come back for it, Jonah. They know it exists." And I heard myself say, "Then let me take it to Reeve," as though my mouth had been waiting years for permission to say something useful.

She looked at me for a long moment. Edith Marlowe's look has weight; you feel it settle on your shoulders like a coat made of lead and expectation.

"You understand what you'd be carrying," she said.

"A book of names."

"A book of dead men's names. Of ruined women's names. If they stop that car and find it, they won't arrest you, my dear. Arrest implies a trial implies a record implies a world where such things are done in daylight." She touched the ledger where it lay wrapped in oilcloth inside the spare tire well — my hiding place, discovered by her with one raised eyebrow and a single word: *clever*. "They'll simply drive you out past the last lamp and forget to bring you back."

"I understand," I said, which was a lie, because understanding is not a thing you can do in advance. Understanding arrives afterward, wearing its uniform, saluting you with what you should have known.

But I said it anyway, and she believed me, or chose to believe me, which may be the same act of faith, and by seven o'clock the next morning I was on the coast road with ninety miles between me and the city, the ledger riding beneath the spare tire like a heart beating under floorboards.

* * *

The fog came off the harbor in ropes that morning, thick enough to chew. I drove with the windows down because the glass was fogged from the inside too, and the cold salt air felt like something clean after two days of soldiers in the streets — their boots on our cobbles, their voices in our town hall, their colonel smiling at us with all the warmth of a drawn bath.

The town fell away behind me in stages. First the harbor itself, the fishing boats idle at anchor because no one would buy what the strikers wouldn't load and no one dared load what the strikers had refused. Then the terraced houses climbing the hill, their windows dark or curtained, every third door marked with chalk — Mrs. Penhale's quiet arithmetic of who was watched. Then the last of the lamplighters' territory, and then nothing but gorse and granite and the road unspooling ahead through country that didn't care about any of us.

I had driven this route perhaps forty times. Letters to lawyers in the city, letters to newspapers that never printed what Edith sent, letters to a cousin in Bristol whose existence I knew only through her handwriting. Forty times I had made this drive as furniture. A woman behind a wheel was invisible in 1913; a woman behind a wheel in a good motorcar was doubly so, dismissed before she'd finished parking. I had built a whole life inside that invisibility, warm as a den, small as one too.

This morning the invisibility had freight in it.

Every quarter mile I performed the same ritual: *it is not there.* The ledger does not exist. You are delivering laundry. You are nobody going nowhere. And every quarter mile the ritual failed, because my hands remembered what they'd done at four in the morning — kneeling on Edith's gravel drive in my nightdress with a spanner and a lantern, loosening the tire well's false bottom while the fog pressed close enough to smother a shout. My knees still bore the gravel's signature, two purple constellations I could feel through my stockings like a second pulse.

*It is not there,* I told myself, and my knees said otherwise.

* * *

The bridge came up out of the fog at half past nine, and I heard it before I saw it — the iron report of boots on planking, the wet slap of the river below, and then the barrier itself resolving out of the white: a timber arm across the road, painted in alternating bands of black and white like the rib of some enormous animal, and behind it sandbags, rifles, and men.

Three checkpoints between here and Samuel Reeve's office. Edith had said it like a weather report. This was the first.

A corporal stepped out and raised his hand, palm flat, and I brought the car to a stop with an ease I did not feel. Up close the barricade was shabbier than it had looked from a distance — the paint already chipping, the sandbags leaking gray grit, a kettle steaming on a camp stove behind the emplacement as though martial law were a camping holiday someone had organized badly.

"Morning, miss." The corporal was maybe nineteen and had the complexion of someone who'd been seasick his whole life on dry land. "State your business."

"Laundry," I said, and the word came out steady, which surprised me. "For the Harrowgate household in the city. Linens and the mistress's winter things. They've a house party coming and everything must be seen to." I produced the delivery docket I had forged at Edith's kitchen table at five in the morning, my first act of counterfeiting, executed in a hand I invented on the spot — a housekeeper's round, unhurried script. "I'm expected by noon."

He took the docket and squinted at it. Behind him, a sergeant emerged from the sentry box, and my stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution.

The sergeant was young. That was the first thing. Younger than the corporal, younger than me, with the kind of face that hadn't yet decided what it would look like at thirty — soft at the jaw, freckled across the nose where the coastal sun had found him even through army discipline. He wore his cap slightly too far back, and he read the docket upside down in the corporal's hands with the expression of a boy checking another boy's homework.

"Harrows," he said. "Harrowgate. Like the school?"

"The family, sir. Old money. Very particular about their linens."

"Open the boot, please, miss."

There it was. The sentence I had rehearsed against all night, arriving anyway, indifferent to rehearsal. My hands went to the wheel and stayed there one heartbeat longer than natural — I knew it was a heartbeat too long even as I did it, the way you know you're blushing while you blush — and then I stepped out into the fog-smell and the river-smell and walked around to the back of the car with my spine held straight by pure architectural stubbornness.

The spare tire well. Beneath the jack, beneath the tool roll, beneath the false panel I had fitted by lantern light with hands that shook so badly I'd stripped one screw twice. Ninety miles of names. Fourteen dockworkers threatened off the piers. Thirty-one women blacklisted from the canneries. Two men taken from their beds who had not been seen since, whose wives had asked Edith to write their names down because writing them down was the only place left they existed.

The sergeant lifted the boot lid. He moved the tool roll. He looked at the spare tire, and he looked at me, and he smiled — a quick, unarmy smile, gone almost before it arrived.

"Bit far to drive linens," he said.

"People are particular," I said. And then — God help me, because I have examined this moment a hundred times since and cannot find the seam where decision became instinct — I smiled back. Not Edith's smile, not the suffragist's public smile. Something smaller and more human, tilted, conspiratorial, the smile of a woman sharing a joke with a boy who was clearly bored out of his mind standing guard over a river in the fog.

"You're a long way from home, Sergeant," I said. "Where do they keep you when you're not guarding bridges?"

"Colchester, mostly." He said it with the weariness of a man reciting a sentence. "Before that, Salisbury. Before that I thought the army would show me the world." He glanced at the fog, the gorse, the gray water. "The world's been slow to arrive."

"And instead you get laundry."

"Instead I get laundry." He laughed — actually laughed, a short bright sound that made the corporal glance over uneasily — and then, with the briskness of a boy remembering himself, he dropped the tire back into its well, swung the boot shut, and slapped the lid twice. "You're clear, miss. Mind the fog on the far bend; there's a cart went off it Tuesday and they've only just dragged it clear."

"Thank you, Sergeant."

"Perkins," he said, and then colored faintly, as though he hadn't meant to give a name. "Sergeant Perkins. In case the next lot gives you trouble. Tell them Perkins cleared you at the bridge."

*In case the next lot gives you trouble.* As if kindness were a pass I could carry forward. As if he were handing me something.

I got back behind the wheel. The barrier arm rose. I drove across the bridge at a law-abiding fifteen miles an hour, waving — actually waving — at the sentry box, and only when the road bent and the checkpoint dissolved into the fog behind me did I notice that my hands on the wheel were trembling so violently the whole car seemed to vibrate with them, and that I was laughing, high and helpless, alone in the front seat with the wind coming through the window and the ledger riding silent beneath the spare.

I had done it. I had *done* it.

* * *

The exhilaration lasted eleven miles. I know because I counted them, watching the milestones tick past — twelve, thirteen, fourteen — each stone a little tombstone for the feeling, until at the fifteenth mile the fog burned off into pale gold morning and I pulled the car onto the verge and sat with the engine ticking and understood what I had actually done.

I had not been good. I had been *lucky*.

The distinction arrived with the force of a slap. Because here is what happened at that bridge, stripped of the glow: a frightened woman lied badly — the docket was forged, the story thin, my pause at the wheel a confession in itself — and a homesick boy of perhaps twenty chose, for reasons he himself probably couldn't name, not to look closely at a spare tire. That is all. The ledger survived not because of my nerve but because of his boredom, his loneliness, his three seconds of wanting to talk to someone who wasn't a corporal. I had bet fourteen lives and thirty-one livelihoods and whatever Edith Marlowe was worth on a young man's small hunger for conversation, and the bet had paid, and I was calling it skill.

Edith would never have made that bet. Edith would have counted the cost of losing before she rolled the dice, and having counted it, would either have found another way or accepted the cost with her eyes open. I had driven onto that bridge with my eyes shut and my heart hammering *please please please*, and please is not a strategy. Please is what you say when you have nothing else.

And underneath the shame, colder, a second thought surfaced like a body coming up under river ice:

Two more checkpoints. And Sergeant Perkins's goodwill did not travel. Whatever strange grace had covered me at the bridge was spent, used up, left behind on the planks with the sandbags and the steaming kettle. The garrison road checkpoint would have a different sergeant, a different mood, perhaps a colonel's direct order to search everything with wheels. And the ledger did not care how lucky I had been. It only cared whether I was careful, and I had just learned, at fifteen miles' distance, that I did not yet know the difference.

I sat on the verge until the trembling stopped. Then I did what I should have done before the bridge: I thought.

The tire well was finished. Perkins had handled the tire once; the next search would go deeper, and a false panel is only clever until someone taps it. The ledger needed a new home, and it needed one tonight, somewhere with light and time and a needle. I thought of the coat folded on the back seat — my heavy traveling coat, lined in brown twill, the lining already loose at one shoulder where I'd been meaning to mend it for a year. Cloth takes a book better than wood does. Cloth moves when a body moves. No soldier searches a woman's seams; the very idea offended their sense of propriety, and propriety, I realized, sitting there in the gold light with my heart finally slowing, was a weapon too. Edith had taught me that without ever saying it. Politeness as armor. Expectation as camouflage. The world's assumptions, turned and used.

I would sew the ledger into my own coat. I would wear the names against my skin.

It was a better plan than the one I'd had. It was also, I noted grimly, the plan I should have had before I ever turned the key in the ignition — and the fact that I was improving only after the first mortal danger, rather than before it, told me precisely how much of my success so far had been mine.

I restarted the engine. The city was seventy-eight miles on. Somewhere behind me, Edith was drinking tea in a searched house, and somewhere ahead, Samuel Reeve was waiting for a package he didn't yet know existed, and between us lay two checkpoints, ninety miles of road, and my own raw inexperience dressed up in a driver's uniform.

At the next milestone I stopped again, briefly, and wrote in my notebook — the little one I kept for mileage and petrol costs — a line I did not fully understand at the time:

*Lucky once. Do not spend luck twice.*

Then I drove on toward the garrison road, and the fog of the coast gave way to open country, and the coat rode on the seat behind me with its loose lining flapping softly at the shoulder, like something breathing.
