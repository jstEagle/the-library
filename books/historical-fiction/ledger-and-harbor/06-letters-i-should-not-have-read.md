## 6. Letters I Should Not Have Read

The first letter I ever read was not meant for me, and I have never decided whether that made it theft or rescue.

It was the spring of 1910. I had been driving for Edith Marlowe four months — long enough to learn that she wrote her letters in a hand so small and upright it looked like fencing, and long enough to know the smell of her study: lamp oil, lavender water, and the particular dry sweetness of old paper. She had gone down to the harbor to argue with a shipping agent about a hall rental and had left a stack of sealed envelopes on the hall table with instructions to post them before the two o'clock collection.

I posted them. All but one.

I cannot tell you why that one. It was addressed to a Mrs. Tregose in Porthenny, three miles up the coast, and there was nothing about it to distinguish it from its sisters except — I suppose — that I was twenty-two years old, and alone in a house that was not mine, holding other people's words like a servant holds silver: permitted to touch, forbidden to keep. The seal gave under my thumb with almost no resistance at all, as though the letter had been waiting to be opened by someone, anyone, who was not Mrs. Tregose.

*Dear Mrs. Tregose,* Edith had written. *You asked me whether it is foolish to join us at your age, with your circumstances. It is not foolish. It is the only sensible thing you have told me you mean to do.*

The rest of the letter was kindness of a very specific kind — the kind that has no audience except the person receiving it. Edith wrote about courage as if it were arithmetic, something one could do in small sums daily. She wrote that Mrs. Tregose's dead husband's family need never know, that the meetings could be reached by the cliff path after dark, that her name would appear on no list unless she herself put it there.

And then the last line, which I read standing in the hall with my cap still on:

*History will not record what we do here, Mrs. Tregose. But history has never once recorded the thing that mattered most, and I have stopped minding.*

I sealed the letter again with a kettle and a steady hand and posted it, and I have thought about that line for three years, the way one worries a loose tooth.

* * *

After that it became — I want to say an accident, but honesty requires a different word. A habit. A hunger wearing the mask of an accident.

I drove Edith everywhere: to district meetings in hired rooms above bakeries, to drawing rooms where ladies served sherry and asked careful questions, to the harbor offices where men spoke to her over their desks without rising. She carried correspondence the way a surgeon carries instruments — in a leather case, sorted, every letter an instrument too. And twice or three times a month, when she left me idling outside some door while history happened indoors, I would open the case and read.

I learned to steam seals properly. I learned which stationers' glue released under heat and which held like a grudge. I learned to fold letters back along their original creases — always the original creases; paper remembers — and I learned how long a woman could sit in a motorcar on a cold street before anyone wondered where she'd got to. Eleven minutes, usually. Twelve if the meeting ran warm.

I told myself, in those early months, that I was only curious. Curiosity seemed a small sin, nearly a virtue, the sort of thing one confesses to a priest who smiles. But curiosity does not make a man memorize handwriting. Curiosity does not teach a man to reseal a widow's confession so well that even grief cannot find the seam.

The love letters came first, because love letters are the commonest cargo any town ships inland. There was a schoolmistress in Truro writing to a married man in Bristol with a restraint that broke my heart more than abandon could have — pages that said everything by saying almost nothing, *the crocuses are up early this year, they came while you were away*, when what she meant filled the white space between the lines like water fills a hull. I delivered that letter into her own hands once. She looked at me — a young woman in a chauffeur's cap, wind-burnt, ordinary — and thanked me for the safe delivery, and I stood there holding her whole secret life in my gloved hands and said, "Mind the step, miss," as if I were nothing but wheels and petrol.

That was the strange discovery of that first year: I could carry anything. Grief, adultery, debt, hope — all of it passed through my hands and left no mark on them, or appeared to. I was staff. I was furniture with an engine. People said things in front of drivers the way they say things in front of horses.

But the letters left marks on *me*. That was the part nobody had warned me about, because nobody imagines the driver reading. Each letter went out of my hands and into the world and did its work or didn't, and meanwhile it stayed in me, filed away in some interior drawer I hadn't known I owned. I began, without meaning to, to know the town through its correspondence the way a doctor knows a body through its complaints. The Harrow Lane house with the quarreling brothers — I knew it from a letter begging a creditor for another month. The chapel minister whose sermons were all fire — I knew him from a letter that was all ash, written to his sister about how tired he was, how tired, how he feared God had stopped listening and perhaps had good reason.

Private lives. That was where it all lived. Not in the newspapers, which printed the strike as a number and the suffrage march as a nuisance and the drowning of a trawler crew as nine inches of column beneath an advertisement for corsets. In the letters. In the crocuses coming up early. History, I began to suspect, was not what happened to nations. It was what happened to kitchens.

* * *

There was one letter I have never been able to put down, even now, even in memory. It came through Edith's hands in the winter of 1911, and it was not going anywhere — it had been brought to her by hand, which was unusual, and I read it not by steaming a seal but because Edith herself, uncharacteristically, left it lying open on the seat beside me while she ran into the post office at Pentowan. Perhaps she forgot it. Perhaps she wanted me to see it. With Edith I have learned that the two are often indistinguishable.

It was from a woman named Celia Voss, and it was a confession, though of what crime I never learned, because the confession mattered less than the sentence in the middle of it:

*I am not ashamed of what I did. I am ashamed of how easily the world let me do it, and of how no one will ever write down that it happened, and of how in fifty years it will be as if I was never standing here at all.*

Edith came back with her stamps and her change, took up the letter, read my face in half a second the way she read everything, and said nothing. She never said anything, not once in three years. Whether she knew what I did in those eleven minutes I cannot swear. But she began leaving the case within reach instead of locked in the footwell, and once — only once — she said, as we pulled away from a delivery, "Mrs. Penhale writes a beautiful hand, doesn't she," in a tone that was not quite a question, and watched the road, and let me decide what she knew.

That was Edith's method, I understood later. She did not catch people. She arranged circumstances in which people might catch themselves, and then she waited, patient as tide, for them to arrive.

* * *

By 1912 I had stopped pretending the reading was curiosity. Curiosity looks and closes the drawer. What I did was closer to collecting — no. Closer to hoarding. No. I will say it plainly, since this chapter is meant to be a confession of sorts: I was starving, and the letters were food.

Understand what my life was. I lived in the room over Edith's coach-house, a good room, a warm one, with a bed and a basin and a window that caught the morning light off the water. I ate at the kitchen table with Mrs. Penhale's girl and was treated — genuinely, kindly — as one of the household's necessary creatures, like the cat but with wages. I had come to the Marlowe house at nineteen with a reference from a livery stable in Falmouth and a father lost to the sea and a mother buried a year before him, and no living soul to whom I was anyone in particular. The town knew me as *Miss Marlowe's driver.* The movement knew me as *Miss Marlowe's driver.* Even my own reflections in shop glass, for years, resolved into *Miss Marlowe's driver.*

Everywhere as staff. Nowhere as myself.

And then the letters. In the letters I was admitted — illegally, invisibly, but admitted — into the actual country where people lived. I read begging letters from dockers' wives whose husbands had been blacklisted for attending a meeting, women who wrote to Edith in pencil because ink cost a penny more, women whose spelling wandered and whose dignity did not. I read a boy's letter home from the naval training ship at Devonport, full of brave lies his mother surely saw through. I read the schoolmistress's crocuses. I read Mrs. Tregose's answer, which came back through our hands a fortnight later, and which contained the sentence *tell no one, but I walked the cliff path and my legs shook and I have not felt so alive since Arthur died,* and I sat in the motorcar outside the bakery and cried, quietly, into my glove, for a woman I had never met and never would.

That was the year I understood what Edith had understood all along, and what the colonels and the shipping agents and the newspapermen had not: that a ledger of ordinary lives is not a lesser document than a treaty. That the names in pencil matter more than the names in print. That when the men in authority speak of *the situation at the docks* they mean a thousand kitchens, and that the kitchens are where the situation actually bleeds.

I also understood, around that same winter, that I had a conscience now where before I had only had rules. The distinction sounds fine, but it is the whole distance between a fence and a spine. Rules kept me from stealing money from Edith's purse. Conscience — conscience was what made me start, in the spring of 1912, keeping my little notebook of mileage and petrol costs, and then, in the back pages, in pencil, in a shorthand of my own devising, keeping something else. Names. Dates. *Blacklisted, Harrow Lane, March.* *Wage cut enforced, second notice, Penhale's cousin.* Small entries, the kind no historian would ever want and every historian should.

I told myself it was habit. A reader keeps records. But the truth is that somewhere in the steamed seals and the eleven-minute waits, without ever deciding it, I had become a keeper of what other people suffered. The letters had done it to me. Private lives had gotten into my blood like ink under a fingernail, and no scrubbing would take it out.

* * *

The last letter before the soldiers came — the one that stands at the end of those three years like a door — arrived in the autumn of 1912, and I read it in eleven minutes outside the Corn Exchange while Edith argued indoors about permits.

It was from Tom Quill.

I knew the name before I saw the signature. Everyone in the harbor town knew it — the organizer, the one who could quiet a hall with his hands or set it alight with his voice, the man I had driven past a hundred times on the Harrow Lane corner, standing with his cap in his fist talking to men who worked the cranes. I had never spoken to him. Drivers don't. But I knew his face the way you know a lighthouse: always there, always turned toward the weather.

The letter was to Edith, and it was business — dates, numbers of men, the names of ships expected — but at the bottom, below the signature, in a smaller hand, almost shy, he had added a line that had nothing to do with business:

*Tell your driver the roads past the quarry are washed out again. She'll want to know, taking the coast way.*

He had noticed me. Standing a hundred yards off in all weathers, silent as a lamppost, *Miss Marlowe's driver* — and he had noticed that I took the coast road, and that the roads past the quarry washed out in rain, and he had thought it worth a line of ink to save me the trouble.

I folded that letter along its creases and sealed it and sat in the motorcar with my heart doing something entirely unprofessional, looking down the gray street toward the harbor where, if I leaned forward, I could just see the corner of Harrow Lane, and the small crowd of caps gathered there, and one figure among them taller than the rest, hands moving as he talked, turning toward the weather.

Three years of letters had taught me where history lived. It lived in kitchens and cliff paths and penciled names. And now, apparently, it lived on a street corner, and it knew which roads I took, and it had noticed me when I had made a profession of being unnoticed.

I started the engine. Somewhere behind me the Corn Exchange doors opened and Edith came out with her leather case and her fencing-hand and her terrible patience, and I pulled away from the kerb and drove, as I always did, toward whatever she intended.

I did not yet know that within a season I would be carrying ninety miles of other people's suffering sewn into my coat lining. I did not yet know that the ledger existed, or what my father's name was doing inside it. I knew only that for the first time in three years of reading other people's hearts, my own had been read — casually, kindly, in a single postscript — by a man waiting at Harrow Lane.

And I found, driving home through the washed-out light of the coast road, that I had begun composing a letter of my own. The first I would ever write that was meant for someone to read.
