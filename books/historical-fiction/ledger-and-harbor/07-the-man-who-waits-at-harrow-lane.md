## 7. The Man Who Waits at Harrow Lane

Harrow Lane began where the cobblestones gave out and the harbor's pretense ended. Jonah had driven it a hundred times — the Marlowe motorcar bumping over rutted clay, past the fish-press and the cooperage, past the row of cottages whose windows had been painted black since the strike began so no soldier could count the faces inside. She had driven it with letters on her lap and Edith's voice in her ear, *slow down here, Jonah, there's always someone at Harrow Lane.*

There was always someone at Harrow Lane.

Usually it was a woman with a basket, or a boy running messages between the picket line and the town. But this morning, three days after the requisition, with the fog sitting on the water like something drowned that refused to sink, it was Tom Quill.

She knew him without wanting to. That was the trouble with driving a man past his own front door two hundred times: you learned him in pieces. The set of his shoulders under the shabby coat. The way he stood with his weight back on his heels, as if perpetually about to step out of something's way — a cart, an argument, a life he hadn't chosen. He had a face that would have been handsome if it had ever been allowed to rest: jaw always set, eyes always working, cataloguing everything the way she catalogued everything, which was perhaps why she had never once spoken to him. You don't greet your own reflection across a barricade.

Today he stepped into the road before she reached him, one hand raised, palm flat. Not waving. Stopping.

Jonah braked harder than she meant to and sat with her hands on the wheel, heart going like a loom. Soldiers at the bridge had taught her what a raised hand meant now. Everything meant soldiers now.

But it was only Tom Quill, and he was looking at her through the windscreen with an expression she couldn't file under anything — not menace, not pleading. Recognition, maybe. The terrible kind.

"Miss Blackwood," he said, when she cranked the window down. His voice was roughened by cold and by the habit of speaking to crowds. "You're driving for Mrs. Marlowe."

"I'm delivering laundry." It came out before she could stop it, smooth as oil, and she could have bitten her tongue off. Three days of lying had already worn a groove in her, and here she was sliding into it like a cart into rails.

Tom Quill almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Laundry," he repeated. "In a car with army mud on its wheels and a spare tire well that doesn't sit flush."

The fog pressed close. Somewhere behind the cooperage a dog started up and thought better of it.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Jonah said.

"No," he agreed mildly. "I expect you don't. I expect nobody told you anything, because you're just the driver, and drivers are furniture." He said it without cruelty, which was worse. He said it the way a man describes weather he has lived under all his life. "I've been furniture myself. Twenty years on those docks, lifting other men's cargo, and I never once got asked my opinion of where it should go."

He leaned down then, forearms on the door, and looked into the car. Not at her. Past her, at the seat, at the floor, at the shape of things. He had the look of a man who had spent three days watching soldiers turn out his neighbors' houses and had learned precisely how little effort searching took.

"They went to Mrs. Marlowe's last night," he said. "Six of them. I saw the lanterns from the picket hut. They turned out every drawer and pulled up the floorboards in the parlor, and they left at four in the morning empty-handed, and do you know what I thought?" He straightened, and now he did look at her, and she wished he wouldn't. "I thought: somebody moved it first. Somebody small and quick and unremarkable. Somebody who drives."

The engine ticked beneath them. In the tire well, wrapped in sacking and lies, ninety pages of names rode against the wheel like a stowaway with a death sentence.

"If you know what's good for you," Jonah began, and heard Edith's own cadence come out of her mouth, stern and sensible, and hated herself for it.

"I've never known what's good for me," Tom said. "That's the whole history of my family. My grandfather knew what was good for him and kept his head down through the famine years and died anyway, of nothing, quietly, in a room the size of a coffin. Knowing what's good for you is how they win, Miss Blackwood. Everybody knowing what's good for themselves and keeping it shut." He tapped the doorframe once, lightly, almost affectionately, the way you'd touch a horse. "Where are you taking it?"

"I'm not—"

"Don't." For the first time his voice dropped its patience, and underneath it was something raw and enormous, held down the way a man holds down a hatch cover in a storm. "Please don't lie to me. I've spent three days listening to the colonel's officers tell me the docks are closed for my benefit. I've watched Mrs. Penhale stand in the rain with eleven women because twelve counts as an assembly. I haven't the strength left for one more polite lie from one more decent person. Just tell me true, and I'll ask nothing else."

Jonah looked at him. A hundred passings-by, and she had never seen him closer than ten feet, never heard him say her name, and here he was asking her for the one thing she had promised a woman in a cell — though Edith wasn't in a cell yet, only detained, only *questioned*, words doing their soft terrible work — that she'd keep.

"The city," she said. Her voice came out low and unsteady. "To Samuel Reeve. At the *Clarion*. Ninety miles."

Something moved through Tom Quill's face like weather. Relief, fear, grief, all of it at once, none of it settling.

"Good," he said finally, roughly. "Good. Now listen, because we've no time and I mean that literally — Sergeant Doyle rides the Harrow road at nine, and it's gone half eight." He glanced up the lane, then back. "You can't make that run alone. A single woman in a motorcar carrying heaven knows what, stopped twice on the garrison road — they'll take the car apart bolt by bolt, and they'll take you too, and no one will ask after you because no one asks after furniture."

"So what do you propose? That you drive?"

"That I ride beside you," he said, "as your husband. Or your intended, if you've scruples. There's a wedding Saturday in Millbrook — the Kelleher girl marrying a fisherman from up the coast — and half the town's traveling to it. A couple going to a wedding is the most invisible thing in England. Nobody searches a couple going to a wedding. They ask about the bride's dress and whether there'll be dancing, and while they're asking, they're not looking."

"And you'd be searched instead of me."

"I'd be searched *with* you," he corrected. "It's different. Two people share a story, and a story holds more than one person can carry alone." He paused, and something gentler came into his voice, reluctant as dawn. "Besides, I've been waiting at this lane for three mornings hoping you'd come. Hoping you were the one who'd moved it. I'd have stood here till winter."

"You don't even know me."

"No," he agreed. "But I know you drove Mrs. Marlowe through the picket lines every morning last month while the shipping agents spat at the wheels. I know you never once flinched, and never once stayed to be thanked either. I watch, Miss Blackwood. Watching's all a dockworker gets." He rubbed his jaw, embarrassed suddenly, a boyish motion that undid twenty years of him. "And I'll say the rest plain, since we're trading in truths: I've wanted an excuse to speak to you for the better part of two years, and God forgive me, I never imagined it would take a war on our own streets to manage it."

The fog shifted. Down the lane, faint but unmistakable, came the clop of a horse and the creak of leather, and both of them went still as herons.

"Doyle," Tom breathed. "Get out. Walk with me. We're courting — we've been courting since spring, everyone knows it, ask anyone in the row. Your name's Jonah. Mine's Tom. We met at the Penhale's harvest supper. Can you remember that?"

"It's a lie," Jonah whispered, gathering her skirt in one fist and stepping down onto the clay.

"It's a cover," he said, offering his arm as casually as if they'd done it a hundred times, as if the whole geography of her life hadn't tilted. "There's a difference. Lies are what they tell us. Covers are what we tell them." And then, lower, as the hoofbeats grew near and the gray shape of a mounted trooper resolved out of the white: "Miss Blackwood. Jonah. Breathe. You've carried worse than this past braver fools than me."

She took his arm. His sleeve was damp and patched at the elbow, and his arm beneath it was solid as a pier post, and she found that her breathing did steady, which alarmed her more than the trooper did.

Sergeant Doyle drew rein beside them, a young man with a chapped red face and the weary authority of someone given power he hadn't earned and knew it. "Morning. Out walking?"

"Courting," Tom said easily, with a grin she had never seen on the grim watcher of Harrow Lane, wide and warm and utterly convincing. "Though her mother says I'm courting above my station. What do you think, Sergeant — does a dockworker have a chance with a lady who rides in motors?"

Doyle looked at Jonah, and Jonah made herself smile — shy, lowered lashes, the whole performance — and felt shame move through her like cold water, because the smile was easy, because the lying had gotten easy in three short days, and because part of her noticed the sergeant was barely older than the boys whose names were in the well of her car, and another part of her was already calculating how best to use that.

"Mind the curfew," Doyle said at last, bored. "Dusk bell rings at six now." And he trotted on into the fog, and the fog took him.

They stood a moment in the white silence. Then Tom exhaled, long and shaky, and Jonah realized his hand, resting over hers on his sleeve, was trembling.

"You were frightened," she said.

"Terrified." He laughed, quiet and cracked. "Every time. That's the secret nobody tells you about brave men, Jonah. We're frightened the whole while. Courage's just fear that's found somewhere to be useful." He turned to her, and up close his eyes were brown shot through with amber, tired and fierce and honest, and she understood abruptly why Edith had trusted this town's dockworkers with everything. "Now. The wedding story won't hold past Millbrook, and the garrison road checkpoint turns out motorcars proper — mirrors under the chassis, seats up, the lot. So before we go another mile, you need to tell me exactly where that ledger is, and we need to get it out of that car and onto one of us where no mirror will find it. And Jonah—" he hesitated, and the hesitation cost him; she watched him pay it, "—when this is done, whatever happens at the end of it, I'd like it noted that I asked you to marry me before I asked you to smuggle contraband. Even if only for a weekend."

Despite everything — despite the fog and the checkpoints and Edith in a bare room answering questions, despite the ninety miles and the names riding in the spare tire well — Jonah laughed. It startled her, the sound of it, bright and alive and hers.

"Noted," she said. "Provisionally."

"Provisionally I'll take." He grinned, then nodded back toward the car, and the grin faded into workmanlike resolve. "Come on, then. We've a wedding to attend and a road full of soldiers between here and it. And Jonah—" he caught her eye once more, and the warmth in it was real, and so was the gravity, braided together like rope, "—whatever's in that book, I want you to understand what you're carrying. Those aren't papers. That's the town. Every name in it is somebody's lad, somebody's mother. You're not smuggling evidence."

"What am I smuggling, then?"

"Homeland," Tom Quill said simply. "The only one we've got."

They walked back to the motorcar together, arm in arm, courting, and behind them Harrow Lane emptied into the fog, and ahead the garrison road waited with its mirrors and its bayonets, and Jonah Blackwood — driver, reader of stolen letters, keeper of other people's words — got behind the wheel of her own history at last, and drove.

* * *

They made the outskirts of Millbrook by early afternoon, the fog burning off into a thin, washed-out sun that lay on the hedgerows like a sheet spread over furniture in a closed house. Tom had been right about the wedding: the Kelleher girl's banns had drawn carts and bicycles and one rattling charabanc from three villages over, and the lanes around the church were thick with good coats and borrowed hats, and no one — no one — looked twice at a couple arriving in a motorcar.

Which should have felt like safety. Instead it felt like standing in a field the moment before lightning chooses.

They ate at a public house called the Anchor, wedged at a corner table among Kelleher cousins, and Tom performed husband-to-be so well that an aunt pinched Jonah's cheek and pronounced her a lucky girl, and Jonah smiled and let her, and inside herself she kept two ledgers now: the paper one under her coat, and the other one, unwritten, of every kindness she accepted under false pretenses. The aunt's pinch went in it. The free bread the landlady pushed across for "the happy pair" went in it. The choirboy who held the door went in it.

"You've gone quiet," Tom murmured. "Quieter."

"I'm counting," she said. "Everything I owe."

He studied her a moment, chop halfway to his mouth. "That's a heavy way to travel."

"It's the only honest way I know."

"Then let me carry some." He set the fork down, and his voice dropped beneath the din of the room. "The garrison road tomorrow. I've been thinking on it all through this dinner. They'll search the motorcar — fine, let them, it's clean. But they'll search persons too, and a coat lining is the first place a man's hands go, because it's the first place *his* coat lining hides things. So here's what I say: we split it."

Jonah went very still. "Split what?"

"The book. Ninety pages. If we're stopped together, they find half on each of us or nothing on either — but if they take one of us, they don't take all of it. Half the names walk on no matter what happens to the other half." He said it steadily, reasonably, a man proposing to divide a loaf, and Jonah looked at him and saw with terrible clarity that he had already decided which half he would carry, and that he expected — had planned on — being the one taken.

"No," she said.

"It's arithmetic, Jonah."

"It's suicide with better bookkeeping, and I've seen that sum done before." She heard Edith again, in her own mouth, and this time didn't hate it. "We don't split it. We don't split *anything*. We go through together or not at all."

For a long moment he just looked at her, and something passed between them that neither of them named — the thing that had been passing between them for two years across ten feet of road and never once spoken. Then he nodded slowly, and picked up his fork, and said, "Provisionally," and she laughed again, that startling bright sound, and the aunt at the next table mistook it entirely and beamed.

* * *

The innkeeper's daughter brought them candlelight at dusk and the news with it, breathless, having run from the telegraph office where her sweetheart worked.

"They've moved her," the girl said. "Mrs. Marlowe. Not released like they said — transferred. Up to the garrison at Corven Cross. My sweethe— Mr. Aldous saw the requisition order himself. And they've posted new notices at the bridge." She lowered her voice as though the walls had enlisted. "Notices with descriptions. Of persons assisting the agitators. One of them's a woman, dark-haired, travels by motorcar."

The candle flame leaned in the draft. Tom's hand found Jonah's under the table, and neither of them pretended it was accidental.

"Dark-haired," Jonah repeated. Her voice sounded far away to her, someone else's voice reading someone else's description. "Travels by motorcar."

"Well," said Tom Quill, thumb moving once across her knuckles, steady as a keel, "then it's fortunate we're only a couple going to a wedding." He met her eyes across the candle, and the warmth in his face was real, and beneath it, plain as bones under skin, was the knowledge they shared without saying: tomorrow the garrison road would put its mirrors under their lives, and one of them might not come out the other side.

Outside, the dusk bell rang at six, exactly, and the whole village fell silent around them like a held breath.
