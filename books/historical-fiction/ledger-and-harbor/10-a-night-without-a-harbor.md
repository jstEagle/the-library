## 10. A Night Without a Harbor

The inn stood alone where the garrison road bent toward the coast road, a low stone building with a sign that had been repainted so many times the original picture was only a rumor beneath the layers — perhaps a ship, perhaps a swan, impossible to say. THE HARBOR'S REST, said the letters on top, which struck Jonah as a cruel joke for a place with no harbor in sight and a landlady who looked like she'd never rested in her life.

She took a room under the name of Mrs. Ferris, of Penzance, traveling to her sister's. It was the third false name she'd used since the garrison checkpoint, and each one sat more comfortably in her mouth than the last, which frightened her more than the soldiers had.

The room was small and clean and smelled of beeswax and cold ash. A bed with a quilt the color of weak tea. A washstand. One window, its glass old and wavy, looking out on a yard where a gig cart stood under a tarpaulin and beyond it nothing but hedgerow going black in the dusk. No sea. She hadn't realized until now how much she'd counted on seeing the sea, even from a distance, even as a gray line — some proof that home still existed behind her, that the harbor was still there with Edith in it, somewhere behind walls now, and Tom in custody, and the whole town holding its breath under martial law.

She lit the candle, set her case down, and then stood in the middle of the floor for a long moment, listening to the inn settle around her: a door closing below, water moving through pipes, a man's laugh cut short by his companion's hush.

Her coat still hung heavy on her shoulders. The ledger lay against her ribs where it had lain since Harrow Lane, sewn into the lining by a woman in Tom's circle with a cobbler's needle and no questions asked, and Jonah had been aware of it every waking minute for two days — its weight, its edges, the way it made her move differently, sit differently, hold her arms slightly away from her body like a woman guarding a pregnancy. She had eaten lunch with it against her heart. She had answered a sergeant's questions with it against her heart.

Now, alone, she unbuttoned the coat and lifted it off and laid it across the chair, and felt immediately lighter and immediately bereft, as though she'd set down not paper but a person.

She should sleep. That was the sensible thing. She'd driven since six that morning; there were forty miles yet tomorrow, and Reeve's office, and whatever came after. Sensible people slept.

Jonah Blackwood had been telling herself she was sensible for thirty-one years, and look where it had got her: hiding in an inn with other people's names stitched into her coat.

She carried the coat to the window, where the candlelight was strongest, and picked open the stitches with the point of her sewing scissors, working by feel mostly, the way she'd watched the woman do it in reverse. The thread gave reluctantly, one stubborn stitch at a time. When the seam opened wide enough she drew the ledger out — wrapped in oilcloth now, because she'd learned that much caution — and unwrapped it on the quilt, and sat down on the floor beside the bed with her back against the mattress, because somehow sitting on the bed felt wrong, felt like presuming, and this was not a book to be read casually, legs crossed, waiting for supper.

It was a book of the dead and the disappeared. Some of them not dead yet. But she had begun thinking of them as dead, or buried alive, and she couldn't stop.

* * *

She had read pieces of it before. Of course she had. She had read everything Edith Marlowe had ever let within reach of her hands — that was the whole shameful architecture of Jonah's character, three years of opened letters and steamed envelopes and one memorable afternoon with Edith's diary when the older woman had gone into Truro and left it, insultingly, unlocked. Jonah knew herself well enough by now to know the compulsion wasn't curiosity exactly, or not only. It was hunger. History happened to other people, bold people, named people, and Jonah read their mail the way a starving woman reads a menu through a window.

But she had read the ledger the way she read everything: quickly, greedily, for the shape of the story. Names had skimmed past her like fenceposts from a motorcar. Even after her father's name had stopped her cold — *Blackwood, J., senior; dockhand, 22 years' service; dismissed without hearing, 4 March; warned against association* — even then she had absorbed it as a shock rather than a study, and then the soldiers came, and the searching, and the bridge, and Tom, and there had been no time for anything but motion.

Now there was nothing but time. The inn was quiet. The candle burned. And the ledger lay open on her knees with its columns of Edith's small, upright, merciless handwriting, and Jonah began at the first page and did not allow herself to skim.

*Verran, Cledwyn. Stevedore. Spoken to by agents of Treloar & Voss, 11 January: "There are men who would work your berth for half wage and thank the company for the chance." Wife expecting third child.*

She tried to see him. That became her discipline, her penance: every name, she stopped and built the man or woman behind it. Cledwyn Verran would be big — stevedores were big — with hands scarred from hawsers, and he would have come home on the eleventh of January and stood in his kitchen with his cap still on because he always forgot to take it off, and he would have told his wife, or maybe he wouldn't have told his wife, maybe she'd have read it in his shoulders, and she was expecting a child, and what do you do, seven months pregnant, when the work disappears out from under your husband for the crime of asking what the cargo was worth?

*Tresidder, Mary Ann. Laundress. Blacklisted from company houses, 19 February, "for seditious talk." Seditious talk: asked at company office why her rent rose when her wages did not.*

Mary Ann Tresidder. Jonah could see her — everyone in the town could see her, she was one of those women whose voice carried across a yard, who laughed like a gull. Seditious talk. A question about rent. Jonah put her hand flat on the page as if the name might get up and walk away.

The candle guttered once and steadied. Downstairs someone dropped a boot, and a dog outside answered it, and then the night closed over again.

She turned pages. The entries thickened as winter went on — January sparse and almost polite, March crowded, April written faster, the ink blotted, the margins filling with Edith's annotations in pencil, dates cross-referenced, initials of witnesses. You could read the escalation of a small war in the pressure of a nib. By April Edith was writing at night, Jonah realized; these were entries made after dark, after the day's errands, in the same lamplit hours Jonah herself had spent reading love letters and feeling clever.

*Pascoe, Ellen. Charwoman at the company offices. Dismissed 2 April after refusing to identify women attending the meeting at the Wesleyan hall. Four children. Eldest boy now selling papers on the pierhead; youngest ill; no doctor.*

Ellen Pascoe. Jonah had probably held a door for her. Had probably taken her fare in the motorcar, or nodded to her on Chapel Street, one nodding woman to another, and neither of them knowing they were characters in the same book. Four children. The youngest ill and no doctor, and the eldest boy selling papers on the pierhead where the soldiers now stood with rifles, and Jonah understood suddenly, with a physical lurch, that she was not reading evidence.

She was reading a town. Her town. Every name was a window with a light behind it, and she had walked past all of them for years with her collar up and her eyes on the road, delivering other people's words, believing that history was conducted elsewhere, in London, in Parliament, by people whose names got printed. And here it all was, conducted in kitchens, conducted in rents and dismissals and sick children and the price of coal — conducted in small cruelties administered by men named Treloar and Voss who would call themselves reasonable at dinner parties, and here was Edith Marlowe, writing it all down in an exercise-book ledger because nobody else would, because the newspapers wouldn't, because the magistrates were the company's cousins, because memory kept by ordinary hands was the only kind left.

Jonah wept.

Not elegantly, not the way she wept at novels, one controlled tear deployed like punctuation. She wept the way she had not wept at her father's funeral, which she had attended dry-eyed and furious at herself for it, and she wept for Cledwyn Verran and Mary Ann Tresidder and Ellen Pascoe and the boy on the pierhead, and for her father whose name sat in these pages among strangers and who had never told her, had come home every evening of her childhood with dock dust in his creases and had said *nothing*, had said *good day at the yard, my lover?* and smiled, and all along he had been one of the erased, one of the names, and she had been driving the enemy's postmistress around town in a fine motorcar while he stacked crates for whoever would pay.

She cried until her face was swollen and the candle had burned down two inches, and then she wiped her face on the quilt — poor Mrs. Ferris of Penzance, weeping into strange linen — and went back to the beginning and started again, because she had skipped none of it the first time but she wanted, needed, to prove to herself she could bear it twice.

Somewhere past midnight she fell asleep sitting up, the ledger open on her lap, her cheek against the quilt, and dreamed of windows with lights behind them, hundreds of them, a whole hillside of lit windows going dark one by one.

* * *

A knock woke her. Gray light stood in the window; she had slept four hours at most, in her clothes, and her neck complained as she straightened. The ledger — she was reaching for it before her mind finished the thought — was shut and under the quilt, and she had no memory of doing it.

"One moment." She stuffed it into the oilcloth, the oilcloth into her case, kicked the case under the bed just as the knock came again, softer.

"It's only me, miss. Brought your hot water."

A maid — young, sixteen perhaps, with chapped red hands and a face that seemed assembled from apologies. Jonah admitted her and stood awkwardly while the girl poured from the jug into the basin, steam climbing, and studied Jonah with sidelong eyes that weren't curious so much as afraid on her behalf.

"You slept poorly, miss, if you don't mind my saying. I heard—" The girl caught herself, pressed her lips together.

"Heard what?"

"Nothing, miss."

"You heard crying," Jonah said. "It's all right. Bad news from home. My father's ill."

"I'm sorry, miss." The girl set the jug down with great care, as if the morning depended on its placement, and moved toward the door, and then stopped, with her hand on the latch, and looked at the floor between them for so long that Jonah thought she'd simply forgotten what doors were for.

Then, in a rush, barely above a whisper, watching the door instead of Jonah:

"You'll want your breakfast downstairs, miss, and you'll want to eat it quick and be on your way. And you'll want to have paid your bill before you eat, if you take my meaning, and you won't leave your case in the room, and you won't write anything in the register but what's true-seeming, because—" Her voice dropped further. "Because Mr. Trevail writes it all down. Every guest. Names and towns and which way they're headed and what time they leave. Takes it to the corporal at the crossroads every Saturday, and twice this week besides, since the trouble at the coast started. There's a man came Tuesday asking after a woman traveling alone in a motorcar, and Mr. Trevail told him about the motorcar in the yard, didn't he, and the man sat in the taproom till near ten o'clock looking at everyone that came down the stairs."

The room had gone very still, or Jonah had. She could hear her own blood.

"This woman," Jonah said, keeping her voice mild, bored, a trifle slow — Mrs. Ferris of Penzance would be all three. "What did she look like?"

"Youngish, miss. Dark hair. Alone." The girl's eyes flicked up at last, and held hers for one bare second, and in that second something passed between them that neither of them would ever speak of again — the maid had known, had known since she brought the water, had perhaps known since the motorcar rolled into the yard, and had climbed the stairs this morning anyway with her hot water jug and her chapped hands, to spend her courage on a stranger.

"Thank you," Jonah said. "You've been very kind. What's your name?"

"Lowdy, miss." And then, horrified at having given it, she was gone, the door clicking shut behind her soft as a moth landing.

Jonah stood in the middle of the floor with the gray light rising and her heart going hard against the ledger-shaped absence under her breastbone.

A man asking after a woman traveling alone. Not a description of her — not yet, or the girl would have said more, would have said *they showed a photograph* — which meant the army didn't know precisely who she was. They knew a ledger existed. They knew a courier might carry it. They were casting a net of the coarsest mesh: any woman, alone, moving fast, heading for the city.

Alone.

Tom's face rose in front of her, absurdly clear, the way faces do at bad moments — his crooked smile in the fog at Harrow Lane, the way he'd said *a wedding, we can pass for a wedding party anywhere,* and then the garrison road, and the sergeant's hand on his shoulder, and the last thing he'd said to her, shouted half-joking across the yard as they marched him to the guardroom: *Forty miles, Jonah! Don't stop to read!*

He'd known. He'd known exactly what she'd do with a night alone and a book of names.

She dressed quickly, packed the ledger deep in the case beneath a change of linen — no, she thought, and took it out again; the case could be seized, searched, left behind in a panic, but her coat went where she went — and resewed the lining with clumsy, hurried stitches, her fingers remembering her mother's hands over mending, pricking herself once and not minding the blood. In the glass her face was pale and her eyes were rimmed red and she looked, for the first time in her adult life, like somebody.

Mrs. Ferris of Penzance paid her bill in advance, ate a rapid breakfast of eggs and bread in the empty taproom under the innkeeper's bland, weighing gaze, complimented the kippers to a man who had never cooked them, and asked — idly, folding her gloves — whether the gentleman who'd sat in the taproom Tuesday might still be lodging, as she fancied she might know his sister, from Penzance.

"Gone Thursday," said Mr. Trevail. "Back toward the coast, I'd say. Army gentleman."

Back toward the coast. Back toward the checkpoints. Toward Harrow Lane, where Tom Quill was in a guardroom; toward the town where Edith Marlowe was answering questions she must not answer; toward a web with a hole in it shaped like a woman alone — unless the hole had already been mended overnight, unless the description had already firmed up, dark hair, youngish, motorcar, and the net was drawing closed behind her even as she smiled at the innkeeper and thanked him for his trouble.

She stepped out into the raw morning, cranked the motorcar, and was halfway down the coast road before she let herself think the thought that had been forming since Lowdy's whisper:

Someone had told the army about the ledger. Someone who knew Edith's household, her habits, her papers. Someone close enough to know that a courier would be sent and roughly when.

And Jonah had left that someone behind her, in the town, walking free.
