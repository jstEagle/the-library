## 11. The Informer's Face

The rain came sideways off the harbor all that week, and I drove through it with the ledger sewn against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

I had left Tom at the garrison road four days ago. Four days, and I still caught myself turning to speak to him in the passenger seat, still reaching to adjust the scarf he'd wound carelessly around his neck, still listening for the low hum he made when he was thinking — some half-remembered music hall tune, always slightly out of key. The motorcar was a coffin of silence now. I filled it with my own breathing and told myself that was enough.

I had come back to the harbor town because there was nowhere else to go. Reeve wanted corroboration before he printed a word; Mrs. Penhale was collecting sworn statements from families who were brave by daylight and terrified by dusk; and Edith was somewhere behind the barbed wire of the garrison, held "for questioning," which everyone in town understood to mean held for breaking. I could not free her. I could not even see her. But I could do what she had taught me without ever meaning to: I could pay attention.

It was Mrs. Penhale who put me on the scent, though she didn't know she was doing it. She had come to the back room of the bakery on Corder Street — one of the few places three women could gather without technically violating the colonel's arithmetic of permitted assemblies — with a list of names and a face like wet slate.

"Someone talked," she said, before the kettle had even finished. She said it the way you'd say *someone died*. Not angrily. Worse than angrily. With the flat certainty of a woman doing sums.

"How do you know?"

"Because they knew, Jonah. Ashworth's men knew which houses to search and which to leave. They went straight to the Prynnes' and took Daniel Prynne's union card out of the flour tin — *the flour tin*, mind, where only his wife knew it was. They went straight to Widow Cargill's lodgings and asked after letters she'd burned a fortnight since. You don't find a flour tin by luck. You don't ask after burned letters by accident. Somebody gave them the map."

I turned my cup slowly on its saucer. Outside, two soldiers strolled past the bakery window with the unhurried insolence of men who owned the weather.

"Who knew about the flour tin?" I asked.

Mrs. Penhale looked at me for a long moment. "That's the question, isn't it," she said. "And here's the answer that keeps me up nights: only family. Only the people inside the walls."

* * *

I made a list that night, sitting in the motorcar with the rain drumming the roof, because making lists is how Edith thinks and I have been infected by it. Everyone who had known about the ledger. Everyone who had known about its contents. Everyone whose knowledge could have produced the searches Mrs. Penhale described.

Edith herself — no. Whatever else they were doing to her, she had not given them the map; if she had, they'd have stopped searching houses and started hanging men.

Tom — no. Tom had learned of the ledger's existence barely a week ago, on Harrow Lane, in the rain, and everything he'd done since had been bought with his own skin.

Mrs. Penhale, the women of the vigil, the Prynne family, the strikers on the pier — none of them had known enough, broadly enough, to direct the colonel's house-to-house courtesy.

Which left, when I struck everyone through, one name standing alone on my page like a man left on a platform after the train has gone.

Albert Fenwick. Edith's clerk.

* * *

I want to say I was surprised. I wasn't, quite, and that frightened me more than surprise would have. Because I had known Albert Fenwick for three years — had watched him ink Edith's fair copies, dust her cabinets, wind the office clock every Thursday — and there had always been something in him I'd registered without naming: a watchfulness that wasn't diligence. A man who counted things not because he loved order but because he loved knowing where everything was, in case knowing became useful. I had filed that observation away among the hundred small facts I hoarded about other people's lives, and I had never once acted on it, because acting is for bolder souls.

Well. I had driven ninety miles through three checkpoints with a dead man's coat lining and a living woman's handwriting against my heart. Bolder souls were in short supply. I would have to manage.

* * *

Before I tell you what happened in Albert Fenwick's rooms above the chandler's shop on Rope Walk, you should know what it looked like from inside his skin. This is not charity. It is accuracy. I read other people's letters for three years, and if those letters taught me anything, it is that no one is the villain of his own story — not the widow who lied to her son about his father's death, not the lover who wrote two women the same sonnet, not the dockworker who scabbed for a month to buy medicine his union couldn't cover. And not, God help us both, Albert Fenwick.

He sat at his kitchen table that evening with a bottle of gin and his brother's debt papers spread out before him like a losing hand of cards, and he did the arithmetic again, though the numbers never changed.

His brother Wilf owed eleven pounds, nine shillings to a moneylender whose name was spoken softly even by men who spoke softly of nothing else. Wilf was a simple soul with a strong back and a weak head, and the debt had been incurred over a boat — a boat, of all idiotic things — and the moneylender had already sent one warning that had cost Wilf two teeth. The next warning, everyone understood, would cost more than teeth.

Eleven pounds, nine shillings. Albert earned eighteen shillings a week copying Edith Marlowe's correspondence in a fair hand. He had calculated, more than once, how many weeks of going hungry stood between him and the sum. The number had too many weeks in it.

And then Colonel Ashworth's adjutant had appeared at the office door on a Tuesday, polite as a bank manager, and asked whether Mr. Fenwick was a man who valued his brother's continued good health, and whether Mr. Fenwick might be aware of certain documents — lists, registers, records bearing names — kept by his employer.

They had not asked him to steal the ledger. That was the genius of it. They had only asked him to say whether it existed, and where it lived, and who saw it. Information, the adjutant had called it, tapping ash into Edith's good china cup. Just information, Mr. Fenwick. Words are cheap. Words aren't treason.

Wilf's teeth, Wilf's kneecaps, Wilf's life, weighed against words. Even a man with a weak head could do that sum. Albert did it in the time it took the adjutant to finish his tea, and he had hated himself with a thoroughness that surprised him ever since — hated himself in the mornings, hated himself when Mrs. Marlowe thanked him for his careful work, hated himself most of all when she let him lock up the office, trusting him with the key as though trust were a thing he hadn't already sold.

He told himself, in the way men tell themselves things, that he had saved a life. One life, his brother's, against names on paper — paper, after all, just paper. He told himself that whatever the soldiers did with the information, that part wasn't his; he had merely handed over a map, and maps don't kill anyone, men with rifles kill people, and the rifles weren't his either.

But at night, alone with the gin, the story rearranged itself into shapes he could not look at directly: Daniel Prynne's flour tin tipped out onto a soldier's palm. Widow Cargill questioned about letters she'd already burned. Mrs. Marlowe taken away in a closed lorry while he stood at the office window and watched, and did not run after it, and did not shout, and locked the office door behind him out of habit, out of the same wretched habit of carefulness that had made him valuable to her and dangerous to her in equal measure.

The knock came at half past eight. Three knocks, soft, and then a pause, and then one more — Mrs. Marlowe's knock, the one she used on his door when she worked late, and hearing it now, from a voice he did not expect, made the gin turn cold in his stomach.

He opened the door to Jonah Blackwood, the driver, and saw at once — the way guilty men see everything at once — that she knew.

* * *

"You look terrible, Albert," I said, stepping past him into the room. It was true. He had the gray, bloated look of a man who had stopped sleeping honestly and started drinking instead, trading one liar for another.

"Miss Blackwood. To what do I—"

"Don't." I took the chair opposite his table. The debt papers lay there, and I looked at them deliberately, one by one, so he would know I had seen them, and understand what seeing them meant. "Sit down, Albert. I'm not here to hurt you. I'm here because I want to hear you say it."

"Say what?" His voice cracked on the second word. He sat, because his legs had decided for him.

"What you told them. About the ledger. About her."

"I don't know what—"

"Daniel Prynne's union card, Albert. Out of the flour tin. Widow Cargill's burned letters. They searched eleven houses in four days and didn't waste a minute on the wrong ones. Somebody drew them the map, and everybody else who could draw it is in prison or on a vigil line, and that leaves you." I leaned forward. "So say it. Say it out loud, to my face, and then we'll both know exactly what we're dealing with."

For a moment he tried one last time to assemble the lie — I watched him reach for it, watched it crumble in his hands like wet bread. Then he put his face in his palms and made a sound I will carry longer than any name in the ledger: not sobbing, exactly. Something lower and older than sobbing. The sound of a man discovering the exact weight of what he has done.

"They had my brother," he said into his hands. "You don't know what they do down at the fish quay after dark. They had Wilf by the collar and they had papers on the table and they asked me a question, Miss Blackwood, they asked me one question, and I answered it, and I swear to God on my mother's grave I thought — I *thought* —"

"You thought words were cheap."

He flinched as though I'd struck him.

"Words aren't cheap, Albert. Words are the whole price. Every name in that ledger is a word. Every warrant they write is a word. Your one answer has traveled further than you'll travel in your whole life, and everywhere it's gone it's grown teeth." I kept my voice level. I found, to my own horror, that I was not even angry — anger would have been simpler, cleaner, a fire I could walk away from. What I felt instead was the terrible patience of someone reading a letter she should never have opened, and finding herself in it.

"They'll hang her," Albert whispered. "Will they hang her?"

"No. Hanging is honest work. They'll do worse. They'll make her small. They'll take a woman who spent thirty years insisting that ordinary people count, and they'll try to convince her, hour by hour, in a cell, that she counts for nothing — that she's a mad old spinster with delusions of consequence. And when she comes out, if she comes out, they want her to believe it. That's what your eleven pounds, nine shillings bought, Albert. Not a rope. An eraser."

He looked at me then, and his eyes were the eyes of a drowned man looking up through ice. "What are you going to do?" he asked. "Report me? Turn me in? They'd—" He swallowed. "They'd give me to the moneylender's friends, or the colonel's provosts, whichever got there first. You'd be signing my death warrant."

"Yes," I said. "I've thought about that."

And I had. All the way up Rope Walk in the rain I had composed the speech in which I condemned him, and beneath it, quieter, the colder calculation in which I used him. Both speeches waited in me like loaded chambers. And both, when the moment came, refused to fire — because I had read too many letters, because I knew too precisely what a hostage's logic looks like from inside, because some traitor's part of me understood that the distance between Albert Fenwick and Jonah Blackwood was shorter than I liked. He had sold words to save one man he loved. I had left a man I loved on a garrison road to save a book of names. We had both done arithmetic with human beings. The difference between us was that my sums had so far come out right, and rightness makes no one innocent. It only makes them lucky.

"I'm not going to forgive you," I said. "Forgiveness is Edith's to give, if she ever gets the chance, and it isn't mine, and I won't spend what isn't mine. And I'm not going to condemn you, because condemnation is cheap and you've had enough of it from yourself — I can see it all over this room, in the gin and the sleeplessness and the way you can't look at her desk through the window anymore. Here's what I am going to do instead, Albert. I'm going to remember you."

He blinked. "Remember me?"

"Just that. Your face, your voice, this room, the shape of the lie you told and the reason you told it. I'm going to keep you the way Edith kept the names in her ledger — accurately, and without mercy, and without hatred either. Because somebody in this town has to hold the truth of what happened, all of it, the betrayals along with the courage, or nothing we're fighting for means anything. You're part of the record now, Albert. That's your sentence. You get to live inside my memory for the rest of your life, and so do I."

He stared at me. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog shifted in its kennel and settled.

"That's crueler than reporting me," he said at last, and his voice held something that was almost awe.

"Yes," I said. "It is. Now listen, because cruelty isn't why I came." I leaned across the table until the debt papers trembled under my breath. "You handed them a map. Fine. You're going to hand me one back, and this one's going to cost you nothing but the truth. Where is Edith? Which building, which floor, who guards her, when does she move? You're the clerk, Albert — you hear everything. The adjutant talks around you because you're furniture to him. Furniture hears everything. So tell me what the furniture knows, and maybe — *maybe* — the record remembers you kindly."

* * *

He told me.

It came out of him in pieces at first, then in a flood, the way water goes when a man finally stops holding the door. She was in the cell block under the old customs house, not the garrison proper — the colonel kept her close, out of sight of the rank and file, because a suffragist beaten publicly makes martyrs and one beaten privately makes paperwork. Two sentries on the street door, a rotation change at midnight and six. She had refused food for two days; the colonel had ordered force-feeding postponed, wanting her pliable, not broken — yet. There was an inquiry coming from the city, Albert said, whispers of it even in the adjutant's office; the article was beginning to move through the channels that articles move through, and Ashworth was nervous, and nervous colonels do one of two things.

"What things?" I asked, though my stomach already knew.

"They accelerate," Albert said simply. "When a man's house starts burning, he moves the furniture out early. There's talk of moving her. Transport. Within the fortnight, maybe sooner."

I stood. My legs held, which surprised me. At the door I paused with my hand on the latch and looked back at him — at the ruined, ordinary, unforgivable face of him — and I memorized it properly, the way I had promised: the thinning hair combed carefully over the shame, the ink stain on his right forefinger that three years of faithful service had planted there, the eyes that had once looked at Edith Marlowe with something close to devotion and now could not look anywhere at all.

"Albert," I said. "If you breathe one word of this conversation to anyone — if the adjutant learns I was here — I won't report you, and I won't condemn you. I'll simply add a new entry to my memory, dated tonight, and the record will note that the informer informed twice. Do you understand the difference between punishment and witness?"

He nodded, small, like a boy.

"Good night, Albert."

* * *

The rain had thinned to a drifting mist by the time I reached the motorcar. I sat behind the wheel without starting the engine and let the harbor fog press against the glass, and I thought about faces — Tom's, Edith's, Albert's, the young sergeant's at the bridge whose sister's name slept in my coat lining — a whole gallery of faces, each one carrying its own private ledger of debts and loves and compromises, and history passing through all of them like water through a net.

A fortnight, maybe sooner. A transport. A colonel accelerating.

I started the engine and turned toward the bakery on Corder Street, where Mrs. Penhale would still be awake, because grief keeps her hours, and where the planning would have to begin tonight.

Whatever Ashworth meant to do with Edith, he would have to do it past us — and I intended to learn, before his wheels moved, exactly which road he meant to take.
