## 12. Edith, Interrogated

The cell smelled of lye and cold iron, and Edith Marlowe decided within the first hour that she would not apologize to it for being there.

She had been given a cot with a blanket the texture of sacking, a bucket behind a screen too short to be called privacy, and a high slit of window through which she could see — when the fog lifted, which it rarely did this time of year — a wedge of sky the color of dishwater. The walls were stone below and whitewashed plaster above, scarred where generations of the impatient had scratched at them. She ran her thumb along one groove and thought: someone counted days here. Someone before me needed to know how long, because not knowing is its own punishment.

So she began to count. Not days — she had no way to be sure of days — but names.

They had taken her spectacles at intake. A small cruelty, efficiently administered: without them the world went soft at every edge, faces became suggestions, print dissolved into gray weather. She suspected Sergeant Doyle, who had processed her with an almost apologetic gentleness, had been instructed to take them. *For safekeeping, ma'am.* Everything was for safekeeping now — her papers, her pen, her keys, her house turned inside out by men who folded her linens badly and put the books back on the wrong shelves. They had kept her waiting three days in this cell before anyone came to question her, which told her they wanted her softened, uncertain of time, hungry for the sound of a voice that was not her own.

Edith Marlowe had been hungry for many things in fifty-one years, but never for that.

*One,* she thought, lying flat on the cot with her hands folded on her breastbone like an effigy. *Thomas Quill, stevedore, Harrow Lane. Threatened with the black list on the fourteenth of March, his name posted at the pier gate.*

*Two. Margaret Quill, his wife, dismissed from the cannery without wages owed.*

She went on down the ledger's first page in the dark behind her eyes, entry by entry, date by date, and found that memory was a muscle like any other: it complained at first, then warmed, then carried her.

* * *

They came for her on what she judged to be the fourth day, at an hour that felt mid-morning though the window offered no opinion. Two privates to escort her, walking her down a corridor that smelled of boiled cabbage and floor polish, into a room with a table, two chairs, and a shaded lamp angled so that whoever sat opposite her would sit half in glare and half in shadow. She recognized the staging. She had read enough reports of suffragette interrogations passed hand to hand among the movement to know the furniture of the thing. The lamp was not for illumination. It was for hierarchy.

Colonel Ashworth sat on the far side of the table, in civilian clothes, which she understood immediately as a second piece of staging. He wished to be a gentleman this morning, not an officer. He rose when she entered. He drew out her chair.

"Mrs. Marlowe. I hope they've made you comfortable."

"They've made me a guest of His Majesty," Edith said, "which I understand involves discomfort as a matter of course."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — appreciation, quickly banked. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with the careful good health of a man who rode regularly and drank moderately and considered both moral achievements. He waited until she was seated before sitting himself, and she gave him credit for the courtesy even while noting that he had placed her chair facing the lamp.

"I want to begin," he said, "by making something clear. No charges have been brought against you. This is not a trial. You are here under the Defence of the Realm regulations, for questioning, and my sincere hope is that you walk out of this building today and go home."

"How kind. My home, I'm told, was thoroughly dusted by your men on Tuesday."

"We were looking for documents."

"Were you? How vague of you." She folded her hands. "You'll forgive me if I don't pretend to misunderstand. You are looking for a ledger. Your clerk—" she watched his eyes, saw nothing move there, filed that away; either he didn't know about the clerk or he was better than she was— "your officers tore apart every room I own looking for it, and you have detained me without charge in hopes that I will produce it or tell you where it is. Let us not waste each other's morning pretending otherwise."

Ashworth leaned back and regarded her for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed registers — lower, more intimate, the voice of a man confiding rather than pressing.

"Mrs. Marlowe, do you know what you actually are, in the eyes of the law?"

"A nuisance, I should think."

"A private citizen," he said, ignoring this. "An unmarried woman of no office, keeping records of matters that touch national security — troop movements, labor discipline, wartime supply. Do you understand what that means? It means your opinions carry no weight. It means that whatever you imagine you are protecting, you have no standing to protect it. You are a schoolmistress who has wandered onto a battlefield and picked up something that fell off a wagon." He spread his hands. "I am not saying this to wound you. I am saying it because I believe you are an intelligent woman who has been flattered into thinking she matters to events far larger than herself, and that flattery will get people hurt."

There it was. Edith felt the shape of the thing settle over her like the fog outside — not violence, never violence, not yet; something colder and more patient. They would not break her body. They would break her *significance*. They would teach her, politely and repeatedly, that she was nobody, that her ledger was a woman's scrapbook, that her years of work were gossip, that history was conducted elsewhere by men whose names she needn't trouble herself to learn.

She had spent thirty years watching women swallow exactly this lesson in drawing rooms and parish councils and wage negotiations, and she had built a life on the refusal of it.

"You're quite right about one thing," she said pleasantly. "I am a schoolmistress. Which means I know precisely what happens to a class when the teacher leaves the room, Colonel, and I promise you it is nothing you would enjoy governing."

* * *

They came again the next day, and the next. Sometimes Ashworth himself; sometimes a captain named Voss who lacked the colonel's polish and therefore the colonel's danger; once a thin, quick man in civilian clothes who asked questions about persons in London and whom Edith correctly identified as someone else's instrument entirely, a fisherman trawling different waters. They varied the hours — dawn, midnight, mid-afternoon — so that sleep would come unstrung. They varied the temperature of the room. Once they left her under the lamp for six hours with no one asking anything at all, which she understood was meant to let her imagination do their work.

She divided her days into shifts, as a matron runs a ward.

Mornings: exercise. She walked the cell, forty paces wall to wall, reciting the ledger under her breath, and if the duty guard thought the madwoman was praying, so much the better. Afternoons: correspondence she could not send — letters to Jonah, to Mrs. Penhale, to the women of the vigil, composed in full and delivered nowhere, because composition itself kept the mind furnished. Evenings: the counting.

It was in the evenings that the ledger stopped being a document and became, again, what it had always been: a congregation.

*Eleven. Silas Crane, boilermaker, beaten by company constables on the twenty-second of April, denied treatment at the company infirmary.*

*Nineteen. Ruth Ellery, laundress, evicted with two children, three days after her husband joined the picket.*

She knew them all. That was the thing Ashworth could not grasp, the thing his whole apparatus was designed never to learn: she had not collected these names the way a magistrate collects evidence. She had *sat* with them. She had taken down Ruth Ellery's statement at her own kitchen table while Ruth's children slept in the next room, and Ruth had wept, and Edith had made tea, and the tea had gone cold because neither of them touched it, and when it was done Ruth had asked, *Will it help, writing it down? Will anyone ever read it?* And Edith had said yes, and meant it, and understood even then that the yes might cost more than the book was worth.

Let it cost. A debt honestly incurred is not a regret.

On the sixth day — she believed it was the sixth — Ashworth changed tactics, and she felt the change the way sailors feel a shift in wind.

* * *

"Tell me about your father," he said.

Edith looked up from the table. The lamp did its work; his face was half-shadowed, and she had learned to read the shadowed half as attentively as the lit one.

"My father has been dead thirty-four years, Colonel."

"And yet you keep his habits. A customs clerk, wasn't he? A man of entries and columns. Dates, tonnage, duties paid." Ashworth tilted his head. "You've become him. Did you know that? A little woman at a little desk, recording the comings and goings of people who will never record you back."

The barb was well-made. She gave it its due weight, felt it sink, and examined the wound with interest. Yes — there was her father in her, the ink-stained patience of him, the belief that the world could be held still by writing it down. But Ashworth had misread the inheritance entirely, and she decided to let him keep his mistake, the way one lets a chess opponent castle into a trap.

"He taught me that every name in a column belongs to a mouth," she said. "A ship's manifest is not paper, it is cargo and crew and the families waiting on the quay. Perhaps that is what separates a clerk from a soldier, Colonel. The soldier sees numbers. The clerk knows they eat breakfast."

"Sentiment," Ashworth said mildly. "Now. The ledger. I'll ask you plainly, and I'd like you to consider answering plainly, because I'll tell you exactly how this ends otherwise." He produced a paper from his folder and laid it face down beside the lamp, a gesture of theater she almost admired. "Your associate Miss Blackwood has disappeared from the district. We believe she took something with her. If that something reaches a newspaper, there will be consequences — not legal ones, real ones. Men who have already been threatened will be ruined. Families already evicted will not be rehoused anywhere on this coast. And you—" he turned the paper over; it was a transfer order, blank in the signature line— "will be removed to a facility considerably less comfortable than this one, under a regulation that does not require me to inform anyone, including your solicitor, of your location. Women have been lost in such places, Mrs. Marlowe. Quietly. Permanently."

He let the silence do its labor. She let it labor in vain.

"You've described a confession," she said, "and a threat, and you've wrapped them in concern for the very people you threatened. It's well done. Truly. You'd have made a fine vicar."

"Where is the ledger?"

"In a place you cannot reach."

"Everything is reachable."

"Not everything," said Edith Marlowe, and smiled at him, and watched him decide, visibly, not to strike her.

* * *

That night she lay awake longer than usual, doing honest arithmetic instead of the comforting kind. Ashworth had mentioned Jonah. He had said *disappeared*, which might mean fled, might mean caught, might mean worse — and the not-knowing was a hook set deep, designed to make her trade the many for the one. Give us the book and we'll tell you about your girl.

She turned the trap over and examined its springs. If Jonah were caught, Ashworth would have said so — he would have *shown* her something, a belt buckle, a torn glove; men like him could not resist displaying a capture. If Jonah were dead, he would have used it as a club. *Disappeared* was the word of a man who did not know, and a man who did not know was a man who had lost her.

Jonah was free. Jonah was driving somewhere with ninety miles between her and the sea, and if she had any of the sense Edith had slowly come to believe she possessed beneath the irony, she was past the bridge by now.

Edith lay back on the cot and addressed the ceiling, silently, in the tone she used for wayward committee members: *Well, girl. The rest is yours. Mind the checkpoints, and don't trust your own cleverness further than you can throw it.*

Then, because despair was a luxury the named could not afford, she began again at the top.

*One. Thomas Quill, stevedore...*

* * *

They came at what she calculated was three in the morning, which told her the campaign had entered its final phase. Captain Voss this time, alone, no lamp theatrics, just the bare overhead bulb and a fatigue on his young face that she noted with clinical pity. They were exhausting themselves too. Institutions always imagined that weariness flowed only downhill.

"Mrs. Marlowe. Last chance to do this easily."

"Captain, I am fifty-one years old and I have been awake since four o'clock yesterday. There is nothing easy left in me. Proceed."

He laid a single sheet on the table. A list — she couldn't read it clearly without her spectacles, but she knew its shape, the shape of a manifest, a census, a roll call. Her own territory.

"These are names we believe appear in your records," Voss said. "I'm going to read some of them. If you confirm the existence of the ledger — merely its existence, nothing more — this stops tonight."

And he began to read. Quill. Ellery. Crane. Penhale. A dozen names, twenty, read in the flat administrative drone of a man checking boxes, each name stripped to a surname and an initial, reduced to a tick awaiting its box.

Edith listened to the first five with her jaw tight. By the tenth, something had shifted in her chest — not fear, not anger exactly, but a rising, tidal recognition, and she understood that Voss had made the only mistake available to a man in his position: he had brought her congregation into the room.

When he paused for breath, she stood up.

"Sit down, Mrs. Marlowe."

"I will stand," she said, "because what follows is not a conversation. It is testimony, and one stands for testimony."

Voss glanced at the door. No one came. He was, she realized, alone by choice — ambitious, wanting to close this file himself, wanting to carry Ashworth the confession unwitnessed. Ambition, she had learned across three decades of organizing, was the most reliable lever in any institution, including armies.

"That is Thomas Quill, stevedore, Harrow Lane, threatened with dismissal and the black list on the fourteenth of March after refusing to unload blackleg cargo," she said. "His wife Margaret was dismissed from the cannery the following week, wages withheld, in reprisal. That is Ruth Ellery, laundress, evicted with her children Daniel and Susanna on the ninth of April, three days after her husband joined the picket — the eviction was ordered personally by Mr. Garvey of the shipping company, witnessed by two constables who declined to sign the order. That is Silas Crane, boilermaker, struck by company constables on the twenty-second of April and refused treatment—"

"Stop."

"—at the company infirmary by instruction of the infirmary steward, who told him injured strikers were not patients but costs. That is Alma Penhale—"

"*Stop.*" Voss had risen too. His hand hovered near the sheet as though it might protect him. "You can't possibly—"

"Remember?" Edith said. "Captain, I took every one of those statements myself, in kitchens and doorways and the back room of the chandler's shop, from mouths that trembled while they spoke, and I wrote them in a fair hand with dates and witnesses because a record without particulars is worth nothing. You have been reading me a butcher's list. I am giving you back the people. Every name you read, I can give you the street they live on, the number of their children, the day their trouble began, and the name of the man who caused it — and I will continue to do so, Captain, at whatever hour you choose to visit, for as long as you choose to visit, until this room contains more sworn testimony than your colonel's entire garrison has paperwork. You have not brought me a list to confirm." She laid her hand flat upon the table, upon the very page, with the deliberate weight of a seal pressed into wax. "You have brought me a congregation. And I am reading them aloud."

The bulb hummed. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed, softly, a guard deciding he had heard nothing.

Voss gathered his papers with hands that were not quite steady, and left, and did not lock the outer door properly behind him — she heard it, the latch failing to catch, whether from haste or conscience she could not say and did not need to.

Edith remained standing a moment in the empty room, heart hammering, fifty-one years old and more alive than she had been at twenty. Then she returned along the corridor, past the drowsy sentry who started and fumbled for words and settled for opening her cell door with the sheepish air of a footman late with the carriage.

Back on the cot, in the dark, she resumed the count where she had left it — sixty-one, the Tregenna boy, seventeen years old, warned off the pier by name — and she was smiling as she did it, a fierce, unsentimental smile, because somewhere beyond the fog, on a road she could not see, a motorcar was carrying her congregation toward the light.

And somewhere nearer — much nearer — she heard boots in the corridor, several pairs, moving with purpose toward her door.
