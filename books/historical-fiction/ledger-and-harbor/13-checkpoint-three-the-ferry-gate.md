## 13. Checkpoint Three: The Ferry Gate

The road to the ferry ran along the estuary like a sentence trailing off, and Jonah drove it with her coat buttoned to the throat despite the June warmth. The ledger rode against her ribs where she had sewn it three days ago at the garrison road — a rectangle of stitched canvas lining that made her silhouette slightly wrong, slightly thick on one side, so that she had spent ninety miles holding her left elbow out from her body like a woman guarding a bruise.

Tom was still behind her. That was the arithmetic of it now, the sum she could not stop recalculating: his name given to a corporal with a clipboard, his face turned once toward her over his shoulder — *go* — and then the barrier arm dropping between them like a guillotine that had missed its mark by exactly one person. She had driven four miles before her hands stopped shaking enough to hold the wheel. She had driven twenty before she let herself cry, and only then because the wind through the window took the tears sideways off her face as fast as they came.

Now the estuary opened grey-green to her right, wide as a held breath, and the ferry terminal rose out of it: a long shed of corrugated iron, a queue of motorcars and carts and one hearse, and above all of it the flag — the army's flag now, not the town's, snapping on a pole planted in what had been, six weeks ago, a flowerbed.

Checkpoint three. The last door.

She joined the line behind a fish cart and studied the machinery of it the way Edith had taught her to study anything: patiently, as if it might be persuaded. There were two tables set up at the head of the pier. At the first, an officer in spectacles checked papers. At the second, a second officer consulted a board of printed notices — she could see the white rectangles pinned in columns, wanted notices, photographs gone soft and grey with copying — and cross-referenced each traveler's name against them aloud, in the flat voice of a man reading a menu.

Passenger lists. They had the passenger lists for the morning sailing, which meant they knew who had bought tickets, which meant every alias she might have invented was already worthless before she opened her mouth.

Her ticket said *J. Blackwood*. She had bought it in her own name, two days ago, from a booking office in a market town where no one knew her face, because Tom had argued — before the garrison road, before everything — that the safest lie is always built on truth. *They'll be looking for a stranger,* he'd said. *Be nobody. Be yourself, going somewhere dull.*

But Edith Marlowe would have been asked about her driver. A woman in custody, questioned day after day, would eventually give up the name of the girl who carried her letters — and Jonah had no way of knowing whether Edith had held out or broken, and no way to blame her either way, and no way to know until she reached the second table and heard her own name read back to her in that flat, menu-reciting voice.

The fish cart ahead of her moved forward. The smell of it rolled back over the motorcar, brine and blood, and she breathed it in deliberately, because nausea would sharpen her and she needed to be dull. Dull as dishwater. Dull as laundry.

*You got lucky at the bridge,* she told herself. *Lucky is not a plan.*

The young sergeant had waved her through at the bridge. She thought of him now without wanting to — the freckles, the too-big collar, the way he had blushed when she'd lied about the laundry and then looked away first, ashamed, though ashamed of what she couldn't say. He had searched the car badly, or kindly; she had never decided which. Somewhere behind her, folded into the spare tire well where the ledger had lived for one night, there was probably still a stray button of hers or a hairpin, evidence of how nearly luck had failed.

He wouldn't be here. The army didn't post boys at ferry gates. Ferry gates got men with spectacles and boards full of names.

The fish cart cleared the tables. The officer beckoned her forward.

"Name?"

"Blackwood. Jonah Blackwood." Her voice came out level, and she marveled at it from a small distance, the way one marvels at a stranger's composure.

"Purpose of travel?"

"My employer's business correspondence. I'm delivering documents to a solicitor in the city." True, true, and true — Reeve kept his office behind a solicitor's front, and the lie had the particular smoothness of a fact wearing another fact's coat.

"Solicitor's name?"

"Mr. Hargreaves. Chancery Lane."

The spectacled officer wrote nothing down, which frightened her more than writing would have. He simply looked at her over the spectacles, taking inventory — hat, gloves, face, hands — and then jerked his thumb toward the second table.

Jonah drove the ten yards between the tables in what felt like the length of a funeral procession.

At the second table, the notice-board stood propped against a crate, and she could read the topmost sheet upside down from the driver's seat: *MISSING — PRESUMED DETAINED*, and beneath it a photograph of a dockworker whose face she knew from the ledger, entry forty-one, *beaten on the pier, warned off, family evicted.* The army did not call them disappeared. The army called them missing, presumed detained, and pinned them to boards beside runaway apprentices and deserters, as if a man beaten half to death for refusing to scab were a boy who'd skipped his regiment.

The officer at the table had a voice like gravel poured slowly. "Step down from the vehicle, miss."

"I'm expected in the city by—"

"Step down, please."

She stepped down. The coat swung heavy on her left side, and she let it swing, natural as weather, while a private walked around the motorcar with a bored expression and ran a hand under the running boards.

"Open the boot."

She opened the boot. Laundry — she had learned something from the bridge, at least — a basket of it, genuine, borrowed from Mrs. Penhale's sister-in-law in the last village, shirts and collars smelling of starch and lye. The private poked it with one finger and lost interest.

Meanwhile the officer with the gravel voice turned his pages. "Blackwood," he said. "J. Blackwood." His finger traveled down a column of the passenger list. Then across, to the board. Then down again.

Jonah watched his finger the way a condemned woman watches the warder's keys.

The finger stopped.

"Blackwood," he said again, and lifted his eyes to her face, and in that instant — in the half-second before whatever came next — Jonah understood with total clarity that this was the end of the road. Ninety miles, three checkpoints, a coat sewn in lamplight, Tom left standing at a barrier with his hat in his hands: all of it funneling down to this finger resting on a line of type, and a gravel voice about to say *that's the one, that's Marlowe's girl,* and then hands on her arms, and then the ledger taken off her like a coat, like luggage, like nothing.

And the worst of it — the thing she would remember afterward, lying awake in other towns in other years — was that her first feeling was not fear. It was fury. Cold, specific, itemized fury: *they will not get these names. Not this finger, not this board, not this grey morning.* Edith had written every entry in a hand so careful it looked typeset. People had been erased once already by the shipping company's clerks, their names struck from wage books and tenancy rolls as if they had never stood on the pier at all. The ledger existed so that erasure would fail. It was not going to fail at a ferry gate because a man with gravel in his throat could read.

"Is there a difficulty?" said a voice behind her.

*

Sergeant Wilfred Crane had been at the bridge checkpoint eleven days ago because Captain Hollis wanted him there and Captain Hollis wanted him there because Crane was nineteen and eager and had not yet learned that eagerness in the army was a currency spent on other men's errands. He had searched a motorcar containing a pretty driver and a basket of laundry, and found nothing, and waved her through, and spent the rest of that evening feeling vaguely swindled without being able to say by whom.

Then the postings changed. Ferry gate, two weeks, cross-reference duty. And on his second morning at the second table, working down the passenger list with the notices pinned at his elbow, he had seen a name that stopped his finger like a splinter.

*Marlowe, E.* — organizer, wanted for questioning, do not detain publicly, telegraph on sight.

Edith Marlowe. Everyone in the harbor district knew that name now; it was spoken in the mess like a curse and in the town like a prayer, sometimes by the same mouth in the same hour. And everyone in the harbor district also knew, because soldiers talked and soldiers' sisters listened, that Marlowe kept a book. Names in it. Every rough thing the company and the column had done since the strike began, written down fair and square. The colonel wanted that book the way a drowning man wants air. Half the sergeants in the garrison had been promised leave for it.

And a J. Blackwood, aged twenty-six, occupation listed as *secretary*, had just driven up to his table wearing a coat that hung wrong on the left side.

Crane had known it was wrong the way you know a note sung flat — instantly, bodily, before thought arrived. He had watched her come through the line with his heart hammering like a boy caught stealing apples, and he had done the arithmetic faster than he had ever done any arithmetic in his life:

If he searched the coat, he found the book, and got his leave, and went home to his mother in the valley town with a commendation, and a hundred people whose names he'd never learn stayed disappeared — including, said a voice in his head that sounded exactly like his sister Nell, *a girl called Alice Crane, domestic servant, dismissed without wages, blacklisted at every house in the parish for asking why.*

Because Nell's friend Alice was in that book. Nell had said so in her last letter, furious and frightened, the ink smeared where her pen had pressed too hard: *they put her name in Mrs. Marlowe's book, Will, so at least somebody will know what they did to her, because nobody else writes anything down.*

His sister had written that to him in an army envelope, not knowing she was posting it straight into the machine it described.

So now Sergeant Crane stood at his table and heard the gravel-voiced Lieutenant Dawes say "Blackwood" a second time, and saw the driver's face go white and set at once, like ice forming over moving water, and he stepped forward and said, "Is there a difficulty?" in the steadiest voice he owned.

Dawes glanced up. "List says Blackwood, J., secretary. Notices say Marlowe's circle includes a driver of that description. Woman, mid-twenties." He tapped the board. "Photograph's a poor copy."

It was. Jonah Blackwood's photograph — taken from her employer's house during the search, though Crane was not supposed to know that — had been copied and recopied until she looked like a smudge wearing a hat. He looked from the smudge to the living woman standing in the wind with her chin up and her coat hanging wrong, and he understood that she knew he knew, and that she was deciding, in real time, with nothing but his face to go on, whether he was a wall or a door.

He had never been anybody's door before. He hadn't known a person could see it in you.

"The photograph's useless, sir," Crane said. "I've seen the actual notice. This isn't her."

"You've seen the actual notice."

"At the bridge, sir. Eleven days ago. Full board, clear copies." This was even true; the bridge had received the first printing, before the copying blurred them. "This woman's too tall, for one. Marlowe's driver is short. Little dark thing, according to the description sheet." He had invented the little dark thing on the spot and hated himself for the relief of how easily invention came.

Dawes frowned at the smudge, at the woman, at the smudge again. The wind snapped the flag. Behind Jonah, the private finished his bored circuit of the motorcar and shrugged at nothing.

"Occupation secretary," Dawes said at last, grudgingly. "Delivering documents to a solicitor." He stamped the ticket with more force than the document deserved. "Move along. Next!"

*

Later — much later, in a city office that smelled of pipe smoke and cold tea — Samuel Reeve would ask her how she had known. How she had looked at a nineteen-year-old sergeant of the occupying army and decided, in the space of a heartbeat, to entrust him with the only copy of the only record that mattered.

*I didn't know,* she would tell him. *That's what trust is. It's a leap made in the dark, and the dark doesn't owe you a landing.*

But that was later. On the pier, in the wind, with the stamped ticket trembling in her hand, Jonah walked back to the motorcar on legs that belonged to someone else, started the engine, and drove the fifty feet onto the ferry deck with her heart going like a hammer in a barrel.

And then, as the deckhands chocked her wheels and the gulls wheeled screaming over the funnel smoke, she saw the sergeant again.

He had come down off the checkpoint platform. He was walking the line of waiting vehicles with a clipboard, ostensibly checking tie-downs, and he passed her car once, twice — and the third time he stooped, as if inspecting her rear axle, and said, very quietly, without looking up:

"There's a copy of the notice board in the chart room. Clear prints. Yours is on it. Don't sail on tomorrow's boat; they telegraph ahead. Today's crossing, or none."

She stared down at the top of his cap. The ferry horn blew, a long low note that seemed to come up through the deck itself.

"Why are you telling me this?" she said.

He straightened then, and for the first time she saw his face properly in daylight — freckles, and a jaw not yet decided whether to be a boy's or a man's, and eyes that had the look of someone who has jumped and not yet landed.

"Because my sister's friend is in your book," Sergeant Crane said. "Alice Crane. Domestic servant. Page — " he paused, and she realized he had memorized it — "page nine, third entry from the bottom. Dismissed without wages. Blacklisted at seventeen." His voice cracked on the number and he hated it and went on anyway. "Nell wrote me about her. I've been carrying that letter for a month wondering what a fellow does with a thing like that." He looked out at the water. "Now I know what I do with it."

The deckhands began pulling the gangway. Somewhere behind them, on the shore, a telegraph key was chattering — perhaps about her, perhaps about nothing — and every second it chattered was a second off her life.

"The coat," she said.

"What about it?"

"Take the coat." Her fingers were already at the buttons, working fast, the wind tearing at the fabric. "Not the book — the coat. You're army. You can carry anything aboard this boat and nobody looks twice at a sergeant. If they search me on deck, I'm clean. If they search you—" 

"They won't."

"That's not a reason. That's a hope."

"It's both." He took off his own jacket and held it out to her, absurdly formal, a gentleman at a dance. Underneath he wore shirt braces and the pale arms of a boy who had grown too fast. "Swap. Now, if you're doing it. And Miss Blackwood—" 

"How do you know my—"

"Nell knows everybody's business. It's a regional talent." The ghost of a grin crossed his face and vanished. "Miss Blackwood. When you give that book to your newspaper man. Make sure Alice's name gets read out. Not printed — *read out.* Printed names get skimmed. Spoken ones get remembered."

The gangway rattled up. The horn sounded again, longer this time, and the deck shuddered as the engines took the strain, and the gray water widened between the ferry and the shore — between her and the checkpoints, the vigils, Tom somewhere in a cell or a barracks or worse, Edith in a cell certainly, the whole drowned town falling away astern like something viewed from the far side of a life.

Jonah stood at the rail in a sergeant's jacket that smelled of pipe tobacco and gun oil, watching the harbor shrink, with the ledger warm against her ribs where it had always been, because at the last moment she had not been able to let go of it — and because Crane, taking the empty coat off her arms, had met her eyes and nodded once, as if to say: *I understand. I'd have done the same. Keep it till you can't.*

The town became a line, the line became a smudge, the smudge became sea.

She had the names. She had the city ahead of her, and a journalist's address sewn into her memory alongside ninety miles of road, and one ally aged nineteen whose whole family she intended, someday, to thank by name.

What she did not have — and would not discover until she pushed open the door of Samuel Reeve's narrow office the next morning, Ledger under her arm, hope under that — was the news waiting on his desk: a telegram from the harbor town, received at dawn, in a hand that shook.

*MARLOWE REMOVED. DEPORTATION ORDER SIGNED. TRANSPORT LEAVES THURSDAY.*
