## 14. Samuel Reeve's Narrow Office

The ferry crossing took eleven minutes. I counted them because counting was the only thing keeping me from standing at the rail and screaming Edith's name across the water like a madwoman.

I crossed alone. No coat with its sewn seams. No ledger. Just a woman in a travel-worn dress with a forged reference letter from a lady's maid who did not exist, presenting herself to a bored officer at the gangway as *Miss Blackwood, domestic staff, position sought in the city.* The officer looked through me the way men had looked through me my whole life — as furniture, as service, as nobody — and for the first time in twenty-six years I was grateful for it.

Invisibility, it turned out, was a skill I'd been training for since childhood. Every servant learns to be a wall with eyes. I had simply never known the talent would one day save ninety-one names.

The sergeant — Wrenley, he'd said, his hand shaking as he took the bundle from me behind the coal shed — had pressed a scrap of paper into my palm along with his promise. An address in ink gone brown with age: *S. Reeve, 4th floor, Garrick Lane, ask for the man who owes Mrs. Marlowe a debt.*

The city hit me like weather. I had forgotten what cities did to a person — the noise stacking on noise, the smell of coal smoke and horse and hot bread, ten thousand strangers each carrying their own private ledger of grievances. In the harbor town everything that happened to you happened in front of people who knew your grandmother. Here, a woman could carry the evidence of an army's crimes down a public street and no one would glance twice.

I walked from the ferry terminal because I didn't trust myself not to weep if I sat still. The address was in a district where the buildings leaned together like conspirators and the cobbles were slick with something better not identified. Garrick Lane was barely a lane at all, more a slot between a shuttered printworks and a chandlery, and number four was a door the color of old teeth with a bell pull that produced nothing audible.

I climbed four flights of stairs that smelled of ink and cold tea and other people's tobacco.

* * *

Samuel Reeve's office was exactly the width of a coffin stood on end, which struck me as appropriate, given what I'd brought him. One window, grimy, overlooking an airshaft. A desk buried under newspapers, three of them banned, their headlines still visible beneath the newer sheets like drowned men under ice. A gas lamp turned low. A cot in the corner with a blanket folded into a rectangle so precise it looked military, though nothing else about the man did.

He was forty-something and built like a question mark, all stoop and elbow, with spectacles mended at both hinges with wire and ink stains up both forearms to the elbow, as though he washed regularly and worked harder than he washed. When I opened the door he didn't startle. He looked up from a galley proof with the expression of a man who had been expecting trouble for years and was mildly disappointed it had taken this shape.

"You're not the compositor."

"No."

"Then either you're lost, or you're her." He set down the proof. "Marlowe's driver. The one Tom Quill wired about."

"Quill wired you?" My voice came out sharper than I intended. "When?"

"Three days ago. Before the garrison road, by the sound of your face." He studied me with eyes the pale gray of newsprint left in sun. "Sit down, Miss—"

"Blackwood. Jonah Blackwood." I sat, because my knees made the decision for me. "Mr. Reeve, there's a complication. I don't have it. The ledger."

Something flickered across his face — I watched hope leave it the way tide leaves a harbor — but to his credit he only said, "Go on."

"At the ferry gate they cross-reference every passenger against the wanted notices. They photograph everyone. If they'd found it on me—" I heard my own voice doing what it always did, arranging facts into tidy rows, detachment as armor. I hated it and leaned on it anyway. "There's a soldier. Sergeant Wrenley, bridge checkpoint originally. His sister is in the ledger — page thirty-one, the Ellery girl, blacklisted from every mill within forty miles because she spoke at a meeting. He took it. He's bringing it to you himself, tonight or tomorrow, by way of his aunt in Southwark. I couldn't carry it across the last gate and live, and dead women deliver nothing."

Reeve was quiet long enough for the airshaft to deliver someone's dinner smells and someone's argument, faintly, through the brickwork.

"You trusted a soldier," he said at last. "An armed agent of the government currently occupying your town."

"He read page thirty-one before I gave it to him. He asked me to write down his name too, in case something happened to him. He wanted to be *in* it." I swallowed. "Yes. I trusted him."

"And if he doesn't come?"

I hadn't let myself think that sentence in full. Now it arrived whole, wearing its consequences. Ninety-one names, sewn into a coat in a sergeant's kit bag, and between here and there: two railways, one aunt in Southwark, and the entire apparatus of Colonel Ashworth's courtesy.

"Then I've killed Edith Marlowe for nothing," I said, "and I'll go back and testify from memory, because I've read every entry twice, and then I'll probably hang, and none of it will have mattered."

For the first time, Reeve smiled. It wasn't a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man who has heard worse sentences spoken in flatter voices.

"Well," he said, rising with a crackle of joints. "You'll want tea. It's terrible. Everything here is terrible, it keeps expectations honest."

* * *

We waited the way people wait in wartime, which is to say badly. He worked; I pretended to. He let me read the banned newspapers, and reading them was like pressing my ear to a wall and hearing the whole country breathing — the strike at our docks was a paragraph, the martial law a sentence, Edith's detention not mentioned at all, which told me how thoroughly the telegraph office occupation was working.

It was near dark when boots came up the stairwell — two sets, unhurried — and Reeve blew out the lamp with the calm of long practice, and we stood in the black listening while the boots passed on upward, arguing about rent. My heart took several minutes to accept that we were not dead.

At half past nine, a knock. Three quick, one slow. Reeve opened the door on a boy of perhaps fifteen, coal dust to the eyebrows, holding a parcel wrapped in butcher paper the way boys hold things they've been paid to forget they carried.

"For the gentleman. From my aunt." A pause, perfectly deadpan. "My aunt in Southwark."

Reeve paid him and shut the door and unwrapped the butcher paper with hands that, I noticed, were not entirely steady, and there it was. The ledger. My coat's lining slit open with more care than skill, the stitching torn where Wrenley had been in a hurry, the oilcloth wrapper damp with sea fog. Edith's handwriting showing through the first page like a heartbeat through a wrist.

*Names, dates, what was done, and by whose order.* Ninety-one entries in a hand that never hurried and never trembled.

Reeve cleared his desk with one sweep of his arm — newspapers cascading to the floor, and I thought, wildly, that somewhere below us a neighbor was about to receive a rain of sedition through their ceiling — and laid the ledger in the lamplight. He put on a second pair of spectacles over the first, which should have been comical and instead was somehow the most frightening thing I'd seen all week, and began to read.

I watched him read. That's the truth of it. I sat across that narrow desk and watched a professional witness take in what I had carried, and I understood for the first time the difference between smuggling a thing and surrendering it. While the ledger had been mine — hidden, defended, ransomed across checkpoints — it had been a burden with a shape, a thing with weight and seams. Now, under his eyes, it became what it had always been: a list of what had been done to people, in order, by date, with initials in the margin where an officer had signed off on a broken arm or a burned boat or a girl of nineteen taken from her father's house at midnight.

Reeve read for an hour and forty minutes. I know because I counted, because counting is what I do when feeling arrives faster than I can build walls for it.

He closed the cover at last. Took off both pairs of spectacles. Rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands like a man coming up from deep water.

"This is the worst document I have held in nineteen years of journalism," he said quietly. "And I held the Zabern transcripts."

"It's real," I said, stupidly, as if he might suspect forgery. "Every entry. Edith verified names against parish records. She—"

"I believe it. God help me, I believe every line." He looked at me across the ledger, and his face had changed — not softer, exactly. Graver. The face of a man preparing to say something he wished he didn't have to say. "Miss Blackwood. Sit down properly. And don't interrupt, because what I'm about to tell you is going to feel like a betrayal, and it isn't, and you'll need the whole of it before you decide whom to hate."

* * *

"The obvious next step," Reeve began, "is that I publish. Front page, full text, names and dates. You smuggled it ninety miles past three checkpoints; I run it Monday; the nation rises in righteous fury; Ashworth swings from the nearest yardarm and Marlowe goes home a martyr-heroine. That's the story you've been carrying in your chest since the bridge. Am I close?"

"You're close," I said, warily.

"It's also a fantasy, and I need you to hate me for saying so before I explain why." He tapped the ledger's cover once, gently, like a doctor touching a wound. "Do you know what happens to the ninety-one people in this book, the morning after I print their names?"

Nothing. Silence. The airshaft breathed its rotten breath.

"They become *findable*," he said. "Right now they're scattered accusations in a locked ledger in a stolen coat. Diffuse. Deniable. Ashworth can't arrest a rumor. But the moment I print them — with addresses, because evidence requires addresses, that's what makes it evidence — I hand the colonel a complete, alphabetized, cross-referenced arrest schedule. Every dockworker who signed. Every woman who spoke. Every family that sheltered strikers. He won't even have to do the work of finding them. *We will have done it for him, in type, on good paper.*"

"That's—" My mouth was dry. "That's monstrous."

"It's arithmetic." He said it without apology, and I hated him, and I could see he knew I hated him and was prepared to survive it. "I've seen it happen twice. Manchester, '09. A printer ran a list of blacklisted weavers. Heroic piece of work. Within a fortnight, eleven of the fourteen families named had been evicted, three men imprisoned on charges manufactured overnight, and the printer himself was ruined — not arrested, mind, that would have made a martyr. Ruined. Sued, libeled, bankrupted. Evidence without power is just a target list, Miss Blackwood. The state does not read exposés and repent. The state reads exposés and takes notes."

"But the public—"

"The public is furious for six days and then needs bread," Reeve said. "Fury is weather. Power is climate. Ashworth understands this. It's why he occupies the telegraph office first and the town hall second — he isn't afraid of your ledger, woman. He's afraid of *witnesses.*"

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. My body simply rejected the chair, the room, the arithmetic.

"So we do nothing," I said. "Is that it? Edith rots in a cell, Tom bleeds somewhere on the garrison road, and the great Samuel Reeve files it all under 'premature'?"

"Sit. Down." He said it quietly, and something in the quietness made me sit. "No. We don't do nothing. But we do it in the correct order, and the correct order is this: publication comes *second*. Witnesses come first."

"Witnesses."

"Sworn ones. Named ones. People willing to stand in front of an inquiry — not an article, an *inquiry*, with counsel, with subpoena power, with the army legally obliged to sit there and listen — and say *this happened to me, this is my name, I sign it.*" He pulled a sheet toward him and began writing as he talked, fast, the pen scratching like something alive. "A newspaper can be denied. A stack of sworn affidavits from ninety living witnesses cannot be denied, because you cannot call ninety people liars without producing ninety people to contradict them, and Ashworth can't produce them, because half of them are in his cells already and the other half are the ones he disappeared. The ledger proves the record exists. Only the witnesses prove it *happened*. Do you see? Evidence saves no one. Witnesses save each other."

"And if no one will testify?" I asked. "If they're too frightened?"

"Then we publish anyway and bury the dead afterward," he said flatly. "But they will testify. Because—" and here he paused, and looked at me with those newsprint-gray eyes, and delivered the blow I would carry out of that office and through everything that came after—

"—because someone is going to have to show them how. Someone they know. Someone from the town itself, who carried the ledger, who can stand up first and make it safe to follow. Not Marlowe — she's in a cell, which is precisely why her testimony will be dismissed as coerced. Not me — I'm press, we're professionally unbelievable. It has to be one of them. Or near enough."

The floor went out from under me. Not gradually. All at once, the way a pier collapses — one plank, then the whole structure, then the cold water.

"No," I said. "No. I'm the driver. I'm the — I read letters, Mr. Reeve, I steal other people's words out of envelopes, that's the entire architecture of my character. I am not a witness. Witnesses are people history happens *to*. I've spent my whole life making sure it happens to other people where I can watch."

Reeve set down his pen.

"Miss Blackwood," he said, "you hid a seditious document in a spare tire well while soldiers searched the house above you. You talked your way past a checkpoint with laundry that didn't exist. You handed the most dangerous piece of paper on this coast to a teenage sergeant because you judged his courage correctly in under a minute. And you crossed ninety miles of occupied country knowing that every mile increased the price of failure." He took off the doubled spectacles and folded them, slowly. "You are the least peripheral person I have met this year. You've simply been rehearsing modesty so long you've mistaken it for identity."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth and found the argument had gone — dissolved somewhere between the ledger and the lamplight and the memory of Edith's face at the window as I drove away, getting smaller, getting taken.

"There's an inquiry convening," Reeve went on, gentler now, the reporter giving way to something older and wearier. "Government board, sitting in the city, three weeks. Ashworth will appear before it confident, reasonable, decorated. The testimony of one frightened driver won't topple him. But the testimony of one frightened driver *followed by ninety sworn statements* will bury him so deep they'll need a map to find his reputation." He slid the sheet he'd been writing across the desk. On it, in his cramped hand: a date, a chamber number, a name I didn't recognize — counsel, he said, the best radical barrister in England, who owed Mrs. Marlowe rather more than a debt. "You don't have to answer tonight. But understand what you're deciding. If you go back — if you walk into that town under your own name and stand up first — every person in that ledger will look at you and know it can be done. And if you don't—"

He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. If I didn't, the ledger stayed paper. Paper in an archive, paper in evidence bags, paper that lawyers would eventually wave at other lawyers while the ninety-one grew old around their missing fathers and brothers and daughters.

I looked at the ledger on the desk. Edith's steady hand. Page thirty-one, where a sergeant's sister lived in ink. The last page, blank, waiting — I wouldn't know yet for what, or whom, and I'm glad, now, that I didn't.

Outside, the city went on being loud and indifferent, and somewhere south of it Tom Quill was moving toward me through the dark with a plan and a forged wedding invitation, and somewhere nearer Edith was reciting names to men who thought repetition was weakness, not knowing they were being made into a witness themselves, entry by entry, night by night.

"Mr. Reeve," I said at last, "how soon do you need an answer?"

He glanced at the clock on the mantel — a cracked thing that had clearly stopped years ago and been kept anyway, out of some loyalty to its own history.

"I'd say by morning," he said. "Though between us, Miss Blackwood — you answered somewhere around the third checkpoint. The rest is just letting yourself know it."

And that was true, and I knew it was true, and I was still standing in the wreckage of my old life trying to find a plank that would hold, when boots sounded on the stairs again — running, this time, two at a time — and a fist hammered the door, and a voice I knew better than my own called through the wood, breathless and ragged:

"Jonah. Jonah, it's Tom. Open the door — they're behind me."
