## 15. Love Against Duty

### Jonah

The knock came at half past two in the morning, three soft raps and then a fourth, arrhythmic, like a man who had forgotten how to ask for entry.

Jonah had been sleeping in her clothes in the narrow room above Samuel Reeve's office, the ledger's absence under her coat heavier than its presence had ever been. She woke before the third rap finished. In the weeks since Requisition she had learned to wake that way — the body keeping watch even when the mind surrendered.

She crossed the cold floorboards and put her eye to the curtain gap. Below, in the alley where the lamplight gave out, stood a man with his collar up and his cap pulled low, shifting his weight the way a man does when he has been walking too long on a wound he won't name.

She knew the way he shifted. She'd watched it through a windscreen for two years.

"Tom," she said through the glass, though he couldn't hear her, and then she was down the stairs four at a time, undoing the bolts with hands that would not hurry properly.

He came in sideways, the way men come in when they expect to be turned away, and stood dripping harbor fog onto Reeve's threadbare runner. There was a cut above his eyebrow, scabbed over and reopened once. His knuckles were torn. He looked, she thought absurdly, as if he'd walked all ninety miles from the garrison stockade, and when she asked him — "How?" — he said only:

"Went over the wall during the latrine detail. Walked the rail line till my boots gave out. Rode a coal truck from Wexford Junction with my face in a coal sack." He managed something that was almost his old grin. "You should smell me, Miss Blackwood."

"You smell like a man I've been worried sick about," she said, and heard her own voice crack on *sick*, and hated it, and didn't take it back.

They stood there in the dark hall, two feet apart, not touching, because touching was a door they had both spent years pretending wasn't there.

"You shouldn't have come here," she said finally. "Reeve's office will be watched. They know someone brought the ledger in. They'll be looking for—"

"They'll be looking for a woman driving a motorcar," Tom said. "I'm not that. And I had to know." His voice dropped. "I had to know you got through. That's all I've thought about since they took my papers. Whether you made the ferry gate. Whether you were sitting in some cell next to mine, or worse."

"I got through."

"The names?"

"All of them. Reeve has them. Every entry."

He closed his eyes then, just for a moment, and Jonah saw how much the answer cost him and carried him at once — a man setting down a weight so heavy its absence hurt. When he opened them again he looked at her the way he had looked at her across the Marlowe kitchen table a hundred times, and never once let himself finish looking.

"There's a rooming house off Chandler Street," she said. "A widow named Mrs. Okafor takes cash and asks nothing. It's not the Ritz."

"It's the finest hotel in England," said Tom Quill, "if you're in it."

* * *

### Tom

He had rehearsed nothing on the walk from the coal yard, and that was unlike him. Tom Quill was a man who planned — plans were what kept forty strikers fed when the company shut the pay office, plans were why Edith Marlowe trusted him with the union's money. But standing in Mrs. Okafor's back room with a candle burning low and Jonah Blackwood three feet away, unrolling the bandage on his own hand because she told him to, he found he had no plan at all except *stay*.

"You've been shot at?" she asked, working salve into the torn knuckles with a nurse's briskness that fooled neither of them.

"Grazed. Running the wall." He watched her bent head, the lamp catching the brown of her hair where it escaped its pins. Two years of watching that head from behind a steering wheel, from across rooms, from the far side of every proper distance. "It's nothing."

"Don't say *nothing* to me. I've read enough letters to know what *nothing* means when a man says it."

He laughed despite himself, and the laugh hurt his ribs, and she noticed that too, of course she did, and pressed more gently.

"You read everything," he said. "That's your sin, isn't it? Edith told me once — she suspected for years. Said her post arrived already breathing."

"She knew?"

"Knew and never stopped you. I think—" He paused, choosing. "I think she decided a person who reads other people's hearts that hungrily must eventually get around to her own."

Jonah's hands went still on his. The candle fluttered in a draft from the ill-fitted window, and in its stutter of light and shadow her face was very close, and very grave.

"And did I?" she asked quietly.

"You tell me."

Outside, a patrol went by on Chandler Street — boots in rhythm, the dull clink of a rifle sling. They both went silent without moving apart, listening the way soldiers listen, and Tom understood that this was the shape their whole night would take: closeness interrupted by the war outside, closeness chosen anyway.

When the boots faded she said, "We need to talk about what happens now. Not—" a small helpless gesture between them, "—this. Yet. What happens now."

"Now," Tom said, "I go back."

* * *

### Jonah

"To the docks," she said. "Of course. To the docks, where Ashworth has doubled the pickets and where every man who ran the wall is being hunted by number."

"By name, mostly. Your ledger saw to that — God forgive me for complaining, it was the right thing, but the colonel knows exactly who slipped him now." He flexed his bandaged hand. "There are eleven hundred men on strike, Jonah. Eleven hundred families eating on credit and pride. If the organizers scatter, the company wins by Tuesday and every name in that book becomes a warning instead of a wrong being answered."

"And if you go back, you're a warning too. The one who escaped. They'll shoot you on sight and call it resisting arrest, and no inquiry will ever see the paperwork."

"Yes," he said simply. "Probably."

"*Probably.*"

"What do you want me to say? That I'll be careful?" He smiled, tired and crooked. "I'll be careful."

She got up and went to the window because if she stayed close to him she would say unforgivable things — *don't go, stay, let the docks burn, let the whole strike drown, I only just found you and I will not carry another name in my handwriting* — and those things were small and selfish and hers, and Tom Quill had never once been small, and she refused to make him smaller.

Below, Chandler Street lay empty under the gas lamps. Somewhere east, beyond the rooftops, the city slept on top of a newspaper that hadn't printed yet, and she thought about the article sitting in Reeve's safe, and about what Reeve had said with terrible kindness: *Evidence alone saves no one. Witnesses are needed.*

"I could run," she said to the glass. "Us. Tonight. There's a boat from Harwich — Tom, I've thought about it every hour since the ferry gate. Canada. He has cousins in Toronto who'd take us both. We could be gone before Ashworth knows his escapee isn't lying dead in a ditch somewhere. By the time the inquiry convenes we'd be untouchable, and the ledger would still print, and we'd read about it in a paper that smelled of maple and safety."

She hadn't meant to say *us*. It was out before she could weigh it, hanging in the room between them like a struck bell.

Tom rose slowly. "And Edith?"

The bell kept ringing.

"Edith," Jonah said, "is in a cell because I drove past a checkpoint and she stayed behind to draw the search off me. Every day I don't testify is a day she sits in that cell believing I chose correctly. And every day I *do* testify—" her voice failed and restarted, "—is a day I hand Ashworth my name on a plate. You know what happened to the last witness against him. They haven't found all of Mr. Calloway."

"So we're agreed," Tom said softly, "that running is the sane thing. The thing any sensible person would do."

"We're agreed it's the sane thing."

"And yet."

"And yet," she said, turning from the window at last, "neither of us is going to do it."

He crossed the room then. Not quickly — Tom Quill did nothing quickly that mattered — but steadily, the way tide comes in, until there was no distance left to pretend about, and his good hand came up and cupped her jaw as if she were something written down that he was afraid of smudging.

"I love you," he said. "I've loved you since the winter you drove me to the union meeting through the ice storm and complained the entire way that revolutionaries ought to live somewhere with better roads. I love you reading other people's letters. I love that you couldn't stop yourself from caring about strangers. I even love that you're going to stand up in front of God and Colonel Ashworth and ruin your life on principle, because I fell in love with a woman who would."

"Tremendous luck for you," she managed, wet-eyed, "since I'm ruining it anyway."

"Jonah." His thumb moved along her cheekbone. "Say it back. Before either of us does anything brave tomorrow. I want it said while we're both alive to hear it."

* * *

### Tom

She said it back. He would keep the exact sound of it — the slight break on the second word, the way she gripped his shirtfront as though he might be requisitioned out of her arms by dawn — for as long as he had a mind to keep anything, which he intended, stubbornly, to be a very long time.

Later, the candle burned to a stub and neither of them lit another. They sat on the narrow bed with the blanket around both their shoulders like conspirators, her head on his shoulder, planning in whispers.

"If I testify Thursday," she said, "and you're at the docks—"

"Then whatever happens to you, happens in front of reporters. That's armor, Jonah. Ashworth can't disappear a woman the whole press gallery is watching. It's the safest dangerous thing you can do."

"And you? What armor do the docks have?"

"Numbers." He felt her skepticism in the shift of her shoulder. "Real numbers. Since the article's coming — Reeve showed you the proofs? — the men aren't scattering. They're standing straighter. Mrs. Penhale's women are feeding the picket lines from church halls. You can't shoot eleven hundred people in front of their own wives." A pause. "You can shoot one man, though. I've done that arithmetic too. I'm not a fool."

"No," she said into his neck. "You're a man who keeps doing sums where he's the one subtracted."

"That's the trade, love. Somebody carries the flag and somebody carries the witness." He turned so he could see her face in the dying light. "But hear me. Whatever happens Thursday — whatever happens at the docks — you find a ship, or you find Reeve's cellar, or you find Mrs. Okafor, and you *live*. Promise me the living. I can lose everything else in this world as long as somebody I love is in it afterward, telling it true."

"That's a horrible bargain."

"It's the only one I'm offering."

She was quiet a long time. He counted her breaths, six of them, seven, and on the eighth she said: "Then promise me the same. And promise me something else. When it's over — the inquiry, the strike, all of it, however it ends — you come find me. Not a letter. Not word sent through Penhale. You. Walking up whatever road I'm on. Because I have spent my whole life reading other people's endings, Tom, and I am done. I want ours to happen in front of me, out loud, in my own name."

"In your own name," he repeated, and kissed her hair, and thought: *if I die at those docks, let this be the sentence they carve — she asked, and I promised, and I mean to keep it.*

* * *

### Jonah

Dawn came gray through Mrs. Okafor's lace curtains, and with it the end of the stolen night. They dressed in the same room, backs politely turned, laughing once — helplessly, giddily — at the absurd domesticity of it, two fugitives sharing a cracked mirror.

At the door Tom stopped with his hand on the latch.

"The docks first," he said. "Then, if the strike holds past the weekend, I'll come to the inquiry myself. Sworn statement, my own name, the works. Let Ashworth choke on a whole congregation of us."

"He'll have your description at every gate by now."

"He had my description at the garrison road too." The grin again, the real one. "Didn't stop me finding you, did it?"

She kissed him once, hard, at the threshold — not goodbye, she told herself, *not goodbye*, a deposit against Thursday — and then he was down the steps and into the blue morning fog, walking north toward the river and the picket lines, unhurried, a man going to work.

Jonah watched until the fog took him.

Then she went inside, sat down at Mrs. Okafor's kitchen table, borrowed pen and paper, and wrote the note she had been avoiding for a week:

*Mrs. Penhale — Tell Edith's counsel I will testify Thursday, under my own name, Jonah Blackwood, full statement. And tell her the eleventh man on page forty-one of the ledger is alive; he sends his love, and he means to keep it.*

She sealed it, and was reaching for her coat when the street door below banged open and footsteps came up the stairs two at a time — and Mrs. Okafor's voice, sharp with alarm: "*Miss! Miss, there's a soldier at my door asking after a young woman with a motorcar!*"
