## 16. The Town Remembers

Mrs. Penhale read the article three times before she allowed herself to believe it.

She stood in the doorway of the bakery with the *Herald* folded to page five, flour still on her apron from the morning's batch, and let the words pass over her again: *Names, dates, wages withheld, men taken from their beds in the night — a ledger kept by Edith Marlowe of Harbor's Reach documents forty-one disappearances since March.* Below it, in smaller type, the editor had printed a statement that no inquiry would be convened without sworn testimony from those named.

Sworn testimony. As though the army had been waiting all this time on a technicality.

"Agnes," said her husband behind her, low, "you shouldn't stand there reading that where folk can see."

She did not turn around. "Where else would I read it, Thomas? In the cellar? Under the floorboards with the rest of what we're not supposed to know?"

He came to the doorway and looked out past her at the street. It was seven o'clock and the fog had lifted for once, and the town was doing its ordinary morning business — carts, gulls, the smell of pitch off the water — except that it was not ordinary at all, because half the people passing the bakery were carrying newspapers, and the other half were looking at the ones who were.

"They've printed the names," Thomas said.

"All forty-one." She smoothed the page with her thumb. "And mine isn't one of them. My Walter was turned away from the pier in April with his card torn up in front of him, and nobody wrote that down but me, in my own hand, in my own book, because Mrs. Marlowe was already full up with worse." She folded the paper with great care, as if it were linen. "I've been waiting four months for somebody to ask me."

* * *

By ten o'clock there were eleven women in Penhale's front parlor, which had never in thirty years held more than six.

They came in ones and twos, the way you learn to come when soldiers are on the corners — Mrs. Tregowan first, whose son Daniel had been taken from the Fisherman's Rest in June and returned nine days later unable to say what had happened to him; then the Widow Curnow, who walked with a stick and had outlived two husbands and clearly intended to outlive a colonel as well; then younger women, dock wives, girls of seventeen with their shawls pulled up, each of them carrying something — a letter, a wage slip, a scrap of cloth cut from a uniform, a name written on the back of a laundry list.

Mrs. Penhale set out the tea things because that was what one did, and because the ritual of it steadied them. But when the cups were poured she did not sit down. She stood at the mantel beneath the photograph of her dead husband's fishing boat and said:

"You've all seen the paper."

Nods. One or two of the younger ones had not brought papers; they had heard.

"The journalist — Reeve, his name is — he says testimony has to be sworn. Written down, signed, witnessed, so it stands in law even if they burn everything else." She let that sit. "Now I'll tell you what signing means. It means your name goes in a book too. A different kind of book than Mrs. Marlowe's, but the army won't see much difference. Some of you have children. Some of you have shops the garrison buys from. I'm not going to pretend I don't know what you'd be risking, because I do, and I'll not think less of anyone who walks out that door right now and stays home."

Nobody moved.

The clock ticked. Somewhere down the street a soldier's boots rang on cobbles, passed, faded.

"Well," said Mrs. Penhale, and took off her apron and hung it on the peg by the door, deliberately, the way a woman takes off an apron when she is done with kitchen work and beginning other work. "Then we'd best get started, hadn't we."

* * *

They wrote through the afternoon.

It went slower than any of them expected, because it turned out that telling the truth properly was a skill, and none of them had been taught it. The Widow Curnow began with the weather on the night her lodger was taken, and got lost in the weather, and wept a little into her cuff, and started again. Mrs. Tregowan wrote her son's account in his voice, word for word as he'd finally told it to her in October, and when Mrs. Penhale gently suggested it should be in hers, she said, "His voice is the evidence. Mine's only the vessel," and Mrs. Penhale, who had run a household and a widow's purse for thirty years and knew an argument she couldn't win, let it stand.

The girl with the shawl — Bess Pascoe, seventeen, whose father had been blacklisted in May — wrote her statement in pencil first, then went over every word in ink, pressing hard enough to emboss the next three pages beneath.

"Why so heavy?" asked Mrs. Tregowan.

"So they can't rub it out," said Bess, not looking up. "Paper burns. Indentation doesn't. You can hold it over a flame and still read what was written under." She said it the way you recite something learned by heart, and later Mrs. Penhale understood she must have practiced it, alone, in her room, deciding — the way Jonah Blackwood had once decided, and Edith Marlowe before her, and every soul in that parlor that afternoon — that fear was a thing you could rehearse yourself past.

By dusk there were fourteen statements, signed, dated, witnessed twice each. Mrs. Penhale wrapped them in oilcloth and put them in the flour bin, and then, thinking better of it, took them out again and sewed them into the ticking of the spare mattress in the back bedroom, because if the army ever searched her house she wanted the search to take time. Time was a weapon. She was only beginning to learn how many ordinary things were.

* * *

Word traveled the way it always does in a harbor town — faster than the post, slower than rumor, arriving everywhere eventually.

The fishwives on the quay heard it from the baker's boy, who heard it from Bess Pascoe. The men in the strike committee heard it from their wives, which was how most true news reached them. And within three days there was a queue.

Not a mob — the ban on gatherings of more than three saw to that. A queue. Women spaced along Fore Street at lawful intervals, each waiting her turn to be invited into Penhale's parlor or the back room of the chandler's shop or, when both were watched, the vestry of St. Elwyn's, where the vicar — a mild man who had spent the whole occupation preaching carefully about Romans and rendering unto Caesar — suddenly discovered a passion for hearing private confessions that required him to take down detailed notes.

Old Mr. Trevanion, who had not left his house above twice since his son vanished from the *Marguerite*'s crew in July, walked a mile and a half on a bad hip to dictate his statement to Bess Pascoe, because his hands shook too badly to write. He dictated for two hours. He remembered the exact words the sergeant used. He remembered the number painted on the lorry. He remembered that his son had been wearing his good boots, the ones with the brass eyelets, and he made Bess write down the brass eyelets, because, he said, "You want them to know we noticed. We notice everything. That's what they never understood about the likes of us."

At the chandler's, a young able seaman named Kit Rosewarne signed a statement saying he had been ordered to help load a prisoner transport at midnight and had counted eleven men, and gave the date, and the tide, and the name of the officer who'd told him he'd be shot for desertion if he spoke of it. His mother stood beside him while he signed, holding his elbow, not because he needed steadying but because she wanted her hand in the picture of it, whatever picture there might someday be.

"We are making a record," Mrs. Penhale told them, evening after evening, until the sentence wore smooth as sea glass. "Not revenge. Not politics. A record. They can arrest a person. They cannot arrest a fact, and a fact written in forty hands is a very stubborn sort of fact."

* * *

The army noticed, of course. It noticed on the fourth day, when a corporal was seen copying down names outside the chandler's, and on the fifth, when two of the signatories were summoned to the town hall for "a friendly chat about public order."

Mrs. Penhale had prepared for this too. The summonsed women went — went cheerfully, went separately, as instructed — and gave the interrogating lieutenant nothing but weather and recipes and the health of their late husbands, while fourteen new statements were being sworn across town in the vestry. By the time the lieutenant understood the geometry of it, the arithmetic had already turned. There were twenty-nine statements. Then thirty-three. The army could arrest perhaps two people a day without provoking exactly the attention Ashworth could least afford now that a London newspaper had spelled his name in print beside the word *disappearances*.

"He can't arrest us fast enough," Bess Pascoe said, wonder in her voice, folding the day's pages. She had stopped wearing the shawl up. "Did you ever think it, Mrs. Penhale? All winter we counted ourselves lucky when they didn't notice us. Now we count how fast they can't."

"Don't get proud yet, child. Pride is for after." But Mrs. Penhale's mouth betrayed her at the corner, and everyone in the room saw it, and pretended not to.

On the sixth evening the vicar came to her door personally, hat in hand, and told her that a woman had come to him that afternoon wanting to swear a statement, and that he thought Mrs. Penhale should hear it herself before anyone wrote it down. He would say nothing further except that the woman was frightened in a way he had not seen before, and that what she knew concerned the interior of the town hall, and certain papers, and a clerk.

Mrs. Penhale put on her coat.

The woman was waiting in the vestry with her face turned to the wall — a small, tired woman in service clothes, wringing a handkerchief into a rope. She would not give her name aloud until the vicar had locked the vestry door and drawn the curtain.

"I clean for Colonel Ashworth," she said. "Twice a week. I've cleaned his office since the requisition." She swallowed. "This morning I emptied his wastepaper basket, like always, and there were orders in it. Burn instructions. For the records at the town hall — the intake books, the transport manifests, everything with names. Dated for Friday." Her eyes came up at last, and they were the eyes of someone who had carried a coal scuttle of dread for two days and could not set it down. "And there was a schedule underneath. For Thursday. A transport south. One passenger. A lady."

Mrs. Penhale felt the parlor, the statements, the whole careful architecture of the week go very still inside her.

"What lady?" she said, though she already knew. "What name was on the schedule?"

The cleaner's voice dropped to hardly anything at all.

"Marlowe," she said. "Edith Marlowe. Thursday morning, before the burning. They mean to move her before she can testify — and burn everything she'd testify *about*."

Outside, the fog was coming in off the water again, filling Fore Street, softening the corners of the world. Mrs. Penhale stood in the curtained vestry with the whole town's courage in the mattress ticking behind her and one name in her ears, and understood that everything they had built these six days had been practice — that history, having finally happened to them, by them, was now asking them to run with it, and fast.

Thursday. Two days.

She reached for her coat. "Fetch me Bess Pascoe," she told the vicar. "And find out where Jonah Blackwood is staying in the city. Tonight."
