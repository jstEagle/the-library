## 17. Ashworth's Reckoning

The telegram came at seven in the morning, folded into Jonah's hand by a boy who smelled of herring and didn't meet her eyes. She read it standing on the cobbles outside Mrs. Penhale's kitchen door, the harbor fog pressing close around her like a hand on each shoulder.

*Inquiry convened London 14th. Depositions required. Marlowe to be produced.*

She read it twice. Then a third time, because the words *to be produced* sat strangely on the page, like a phrase from a bill of lading. Produced. As though Edith were cargo.

Mrs. Penhale took the paper from her without asking, read it, and handed it back with the particular stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she did not like.

"They'll produce her," Jonah said. "That's what it says."

"That's what it says." Mrs. Penhale wiped her hands on her apron, though they were already clean. "And I've known men who promised to deliver goods and delivered crates of stones instead."

Colonel Reginald Ashworth stood at his office window in the requisitioned customs house and watched the fog burn off the harbor, and permitted himself — for exactly one minute, timed by the clock on his mantel — the luxury of despair.

It had been a disciplined career. Twenty-six years. He had served in three colonial postings where the word *insurgency* had been applied generously to men with machetes and grievances, and he had learned the first truth of command: that order was not the natural condition of human affairs but a garden, forever going to seed, forever requiring someone willing to pull things up by the roots. Someone had to be the gardener. The garden never thanked him. That was fine. Gardens did not have to thank you. They only had to hold.

Now there was an inquiry.

He had read the preliminary correspondence four times. He knew the shape of it the way he knew the shape of an enemy position: Reeve's article had run in a London weekly with a circulation the War Office could not ignore; three MPs had asked questions in the House; a judge — a *judge*, with the soft hands and long memory of that whole profession — would preside. And at the center of it, the ledger. Every name. The dockworkers threatened at the pier. The women blacklisted from the cannery. The two men taken from their beds on the fourth of March whose families had been told, in writing over Ashworth's own signature, that they had "left town voluntarily."

He had signed those letters because the alternative was admitting the men were dead, and admitting they were dead meant admitting the operation had exceeded its mandate, which meant the inquiry would not end with his retirement but with his ruin. So: plausible deniability, layered like sandbags. It had worked for twenty-six years.

Except now there was a woman in a cell who knew every name by heart.

Ashworth turned from the window. On his desk lay the transport authorization he had drafted the night before and redrafted twice, searching for the flaw, the sentence some clerk or lawyer could seize on. There wasn't one. That was the horror of it — there never was one. The paper was clean. *Detainee E. Marlowe to be transferred to metropolitan holding facility for deposition, escort of six, departure via military pier, 0400 hours, the 12th.* Two days before the inquiry opened. Once she was in London, in a facility administered by men who answered to the same undersecretaries Ashworth answered to, her testimony would be delayed, misfiled, rescheduled, forgotten. Not destroyed. *Misplaced*. The inquiry would convene, find its principal witness unavailable, express regret, and adjourn. A garden left to go to seed.

He was not destroying records. He would never destroy records; that was for panicked men. He was simply ensuring that the record and the witness should not, by unfortunate administrative accident, find themselves in the same room.

He rang the bell for his adjutant.

"Captain Voss. The transfer. I want the escort drawn from the garrison company — no local men, do you understand? No one with family in this town. And the manifest goes through my desk alone. No telegraph confirmations."

"Yes, sir." Voss hesitated, which Voss almost never did. "Sir — the civilian inquiries. There've been letters. About the schedule."

"Whose letters?"

"The journalist's people, sir. And the solicitor acting for the dock committee. They've formally requested notice of any movement of detainees."

Ashworth looked at him for a moment. "Then it is fortunate," he said pleasantly, "that we are not obliged to answer. Formal requests are received, Captain. They are not always granted. See to it."

Voss went. Ashworth sat down and began, again, to check the arithmetic of his survival. Four o'clock in the morning. Fog on the water. Six soldiers who owed him nothing to the town and everything to the crown, meaning to him.

It was, he told himself, the gentlest possible solution. No one died. The woman was merely carried out of history's earshot, the way one carries a lamp out of a room one does not wish examined.

Somewhere below his ribs, in the place where a man keeps the knowledge he refuses to read, he knew precisely what he was doing. He filed that knowledge with the other letters he had signed, and locked the drawer, and felt nothing at all, which was the entire art of his life.

Jonah found the informer at the fish market, because she had learned in three years of reading other people's letters that guilty men return to the scenes of small transactions. Albert Crane, Edith's clerk, stood among the stalls buying nothing, turning over a mackerel and putting it back, turning over another. His brother's debts had bought him — thirty pounds, Jonah had learned, spread across three payments, the last one made the week Edith was detained. Thirty pounds for a hundred names.

She walked up beside him and picked up a mackerel herself, weighing it as though considering dinner.

"You look poorly, Mr. Crane."

He flinched like a man struck. Up close his face had gone the color of the fish, gray and slick. "Miss Blackwood. I don't—"

"You told them about the ledger."

"I never—" His voice cracked and dropped to a whisper. "I never told them where it was. I swear before God. I only told them it existed. That she kept accounts. That's all I said. I'd have said nothing at all if—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "They had my brother's paper, Miss Blackwood. The loan sharks, and behind the loan sharks the company, and behind the company—"

"I know the chain," Jonah said quietly. "I've been reading other people's chains for three years."

She set the fish down. She had rehearsed fury on the walk over; she had composed speeches in which she called him coward, traitor, Judas with an ink-stained cuff. But looking at him now — a small man in a shabby coat, drowning slowly and calling it swimming — the fury would not come. What came instead was colder and more useful.

"Edith is being moved," she said. "Isn't she. Before the inquiry."

Crane's eyes went everywhere except to her face. "I don't know schedules. I'm not told schedules."

"But you hear things. You're still trusted enough to hear things." She leaned closer. "Albert. Listen to me. When this is over — when the names are read aloud in London, and they will be — the difference between you and every other man in this town will be one thing only: what you did next. Not what you sold. What you gave back."

"I can't give back what—"

"The schedule, Albert."

A long silence. Around them the market roared on — gulls, haggling, the slap of fish on marble — indifferent as weather. Then Crane spoke so softly she had to read his lips as much as hear him.

"Military pier. Four o'clock, morning after tomorrow. Escort from the garrison company. The launch takes her out to the destroyer — they won't even risk the train, the trains are watched." His voice shook. "They mean for her to arrive in London too late to matter. Or—" He stopped himself.

"Or not to arrive at all," Jonah finished.

Crane said nothing, which was itself a confession. She memorized his face then, deliberately, the way Edith had taught her to memorize entries: the wet eyes, the trembling mouth, the mole beneath the left ear. Not to forgive. Not yet to condemn. To keep.

"If you're lying to me," she said, "I'll know. I always know. It's the one talent I have."

She left him standing among the fish with his guilt and his thirty pounds, and she did not look back, because looking back was how you let a man believe you might soften.

By nightfall the plan had assembled itself in Mrs. Penhale's back kitchen, out of candle stubs and desperation. Tom Quill bent over a chart of the harbor with six men of the strike committee; Jonah sat apart with the transport details written fair in her notebook, watching Tom's hands — the hands that had sewn the ledger into her coat lining, the hands she had held for one stolen night while they chose, together, not to run.

"A destroyer won't wait past dawn," Tom was saying. "If we take her at the pier, we take her between the gate and the water. Ninety seconds, maybe less. After that she's on open sea and no one in this town has a boat the navy won't see."

"So we don't take her at the pier," Jonah said.

Every head turned.

"She's escorted by six garrison soldiers who expect trouble at the pier, because everyone plans trouble at piers. So we give them none. We stop the launch." She laid her pencil on the chart, on the black water beyond the breakwater. "Fog comes in off the point before five most mornings. If the pilot buoy light fails — if it *happened* to fail — the launch loses the channel. And there are fishing boats that know these waters blind, boats the navy doesn't bother stopping because they're only fishermen."

Tom stared at her. Then he smiled, slow and crooked, the smile she had driven past a hundred times before she ever saw it aimed at her. "You've been reading other people's mail too long. You think like a smuggler now."

"I *am* a smuggler," Jonah said. "I'm just not used to smuggling anything this heavy."

Mrs. Penhale, at the stove, spoke without turning. "And if the fog fails?"

No one answered. In the pause, Jonah heard the arithmetic of it plainly: ninety seconds of water, six rifles, one woman worth more alive than any of them. She thought of Edith in her cell reciting names to men who wanted her to feel insignificant, turning interrogation into testimony, memory into armor.

"Fog won't fail," Jonah said. "I've lived here all my life. The fog is the one thing in this town that's never once lied to me."

They worked until two in the morning — signals, positions, the old Kestrel brothers and their boat, the timing of the buoy light and the man who could make it fail. And near the end, when the others had gone, Tom caught Jonah's sleeve at the door.

"If this goes wrong tomorrow," he said, "you take the car and you drive. You don't stop for me. Promise me."

"No."

"Jonah—"

"I spent three years believing I was staff in other people's stories," she said. "I'm done promising to survive them politely. Come with me at four o'clock and we'll argue about it on the water."

He laughed once, low, and kissed her forehead like a vow, and went out into the dark.

Jonah stood alone in Mrs. Penhale's kitchen with the candle guttering and the plan lying on the table like something newborn and breakable. Forty-one hours. She thought of the colonel in his clean office, signing clean papers, certain that history was a garden he could weed. She thought of Edith, produced like cargo onto a military pier.

She pinched out the candle and whispered to the dark, to the fog gathering offshore, to every name in the lining of her coat:

"Hold on. We're coming by water."
