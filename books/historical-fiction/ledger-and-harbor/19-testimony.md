## 19. Testimony

The inquiry room had been a ballroom once, before the shipping company bought the land and turned it into offices, before the army requisitioned the offices, and now the parquet floor where dockworkers' daughters had danced was scuffed by the boots of men who took notes for a living. Jonah sat on a hard chair beneath tall windows and watched the light move across the floor and thought: this room has already survived us. It will survive Ashworth too.

She wore her good coat — the one with the torn lining she had never repaired, because the tear was a kind of signature now, a place where names had lived against her ribs for ninety miles. Under the table her hands were quiet. She had practiced that. For three days she had sat in Mrs. Penhale's kitchen practicing stillness, while Penhale drilled her: *They will try to make you small, girl. They will ask your name as if it doesn't matter. Let them learn otherwise.*

The presiding officer was a Major-General with a face like a folded map, all creases and no color. Beside him, two civilians from the Ministry of Labour who would not meet anyone's eyes. And at the far end of the long table, Colonel Ashworth, in full dress uniform, his medals arranged like an argument he expected to win.

He looked rested. That was the first obscenity of the morning. He had burned records and deported witnesses and shot a man on a harbor edge, and he had slept well, and here he sat with his hands folded, patient as a man waiting for tea.

Jonah looked away from him deliberately, and found Edith.

Edith Marlowe had been released from custody eleven days ago, and prison had taken things from her — flesh, color, the quick grace of her hands — but not the thing underneath. She sat upright in a borrowed coat, gray as November sea, and when Jonah caught her eye Edith did something no one else in the room did: she nodded, once, as if to say *I know why you're here. Say it anyway.*

"State your name for the record," said the major-general's clerk.

And here it was, the moment she had been walking toward since the fog-bound morning she first opened another woman's letter and read a stranger's grief by lamplight. Every name she carried had been spoken into rooms like this one by other people, under compulsion, in fear. Now it was her turn to give one freely.

"Jonah Blackwood," she said. "Of Harbor Lane."

"Not Miss Edith Marlowe's driver?"

"I drive for Miss Marlowe," Jonah said. "That is my employment. It is not my identity."

A rustle went through the clerks, the small scandal of a woman drawing a distinction. The major-general raised a hand and the rustle died.

"You are here to give evidence concerning the military administration of this district between March and September of this year."

"I am here to tell you what happened," Jonah said. "You may file it however you like."

Someone coughed. Somewhere behind her, among the townspeople packed onto the benches that remained, Mrs. Penhale made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled in wool.

* * *

They began, as she knew they would, with the ledger.

The ledger itself lay on the table between counsel and officers, wrapped in brown paper, handled like a relic or a bomb. Samuel Reeve's article had run six weeks ago — *NAMES OF THE DISAPPEARED HELD BY SUFFRAGIST ORGANIZER* across three columns — and the government had spent those weeks insisting the document was forgery, exaggeration, sedition, and finally, when none of that held, regrettable but necessary. This inquiry existed to decide which lie would be the official one.

"You state that you transported this document from this town to London."

"Yes."

"Under what instruction?"

"No instruction. I took it." She let that sit. "Miss Marlowe was prepared to carry it herself. I took it from her because I could pass where she could not. That is the whole of it."

"And you passed through three military checkpoints."

"I passed through two. At the third, the ledger traveled separately." She saw the young sergeant's name rise in her mind — Corporal Dennell now, promoted and transferred and subpoenaed, sitting somewhere behind her with his sister's name folded into his chest. "Corporal Dennell can speak to that."

"He will." The major-general turned a page. "Describe for the panel what this document contains."

So she did. Not the way Reeve had described it in print — statistics, patterns, the arithmetic of disappearance — but entry by entry, the way she had read it alone in the roadside inn with a candle dying and a maid's warning knocking softly at the door. She gave them Thomas Garrick, seized off the pier on the fourth of April, no charge recorded, no release recorded. She gave them the widow Meraud whose boarding house was burned as a warning and whose claim of compensation was denied twice. She gave them the boys from the trawler crews pressed into unpaid labor at gunpoint, the women blacklisted from the cannery for attending a vigil, the man whose only crime was lending his cellar to a union meeting.

And she gave them her father's name. She had known she would have to; she had decided on the train down that she would do it without pausing, because a pause would be a door and they would come through it.

"Entry forty-one," she said. "Josiah Blackwood, harbor pilot, retired. Detained nine days in the garrison cell for refusing to guide troop transports through the channel fog. Released when the weather cleared. Beaten on arrest. Age sixty-one." Her voice did not waver. She had wept over that entry months ago, alone, and there was nothing left in it that belonged to them. "My father does not speak of it. He is here today, third bench. You may ask him yourself."

For the first time, the major-general looked up from his papers.

* * *

Ashworth's counsel — a silk-gowned barrister imported from the city, whose voice had the oiled smoothness of a man paid by the hour — cross-examined her after lunch.

It was everything Mrs. Penhale had promised. He tried to make her small. Was she not merely a chauffeuse? Had she any legal training? Was she aware that reading correspondence entrusted to another was, technically, an offense? Did she expect the panel to believe she had memorized a ledger of hundreds of entries?

"I don't expect belief," Jonah said. "Belief is your department. I was asked what happened. I am telling you. You may write it down or not write it down; the events will remain identical either way."

"The Colonel's administration operated under extraordinary pressure—"

"Everyone in this room has operated under pressure, sir. The difference is what we did with it. My neighbors organized vigils. Your colonel organized disappearances."

"You call them disappearances. The Army calls them preventive detentions."

"I call them what the families call them." She turned, slowly, and let her gaze travel the benches — the faces she had driven past for years without seeing, the wives and mothers and brothers of every name in the book. "Ask them what they call it."

Nobody asked them anything. But nobody needed to. The silence in the ballroom had changed weight, and the barrister felt it, and called his next question too quickly.

Then, near four o'clock, with the light going amber in the tall windows, Ashworth himself rose. He had the right, as the subject of the inquiry, to put questions directly. He crossed the floor unhurried, and stood before her, and for a moment they simply regarded each other — the man who measured people for coffins and the woman who had served him tea with steady hands.

"Miss Blackwood," he said courteously. It was always courtesy, with him; politeness was his weapon and he drew it now. "In your testimony you've described yourself as a witness to these events. Yet by your own account you spent years in Miss Marlowe's employ reading her private letters, concealing documents, deceiving soldiers at checkpoints, and associating with strike agitators. Tell me — why should this panel regard you as anything other than a partisan?"

The room held still. Somewhere in her chest, old Jonah — the girl who belonged everywhere as staff and nowhere as herself — stirred and almost answered for her. Almost.

"Because a partisan defends a side, Colonel, and I spent most of my life defending none," Jonah said. "I read other people's letters because I thought history happened to other people. Then I found my father's name in a book of the disappeared, and I learned that neutrality is just a seat farther from the fire. You're right that I deceived your checkpoints. I'd deceive them again tomorrow. But everything I've told this panel I will swear to under my own name, and I will stand in whatever court remains after yours and say it again. Can you say the same about your orders?"

Ashworth smiled faintly, the smile of a chess player watching a promising pawn advance. "A stirring answer. Perhaps you missed your calling; you might have been a suffragist."

"She is one," said Edith Marlowe, from her chair, clearly, and the whole room heard it.

* * *

Edith testified the next morning.

She had refused counsel. She stood — insisted on standing, though the prison had left her thin as winter reeds — and when the clerk asked her to state her name she said, "Edith Marlowe. Organizer. Resident of this town for nineteen years. Keeper of the ledger you have before you, and author of every entry in it."

"Miss Marlowe, the panel notes that portions of the ledger cannot be independently verified. Records at the garrison have been—" the major-general consulted his paper with visible distaste "—lost in a fire of uncertain origin."

"Then verify them in me," Edith said.

And she recited.

She began at the first page and went forward, name by name, date by date, circumstance by circumstance — not the summary Jonah had given but the full entries, every one, delivered in a level voice with her eyes open and her hands folded, occasionally closing her eyes to consult some interior archive no fire could reach. Ten minutes. Twenty. The clerks stopped writing and started listening. A woman on the third bench began quietly weeping and was not asked to stop. When Edith reached the entries for the men taken from the *Maid of Carrick* she named their ages, their trades, the nicknames their mothers used, and the major-general — the folded map of a face softening into something human — said quietly, "Madam, how can you possibly hold all this?"

"Because they asked me to," Edith said. "Every one of them, at some point, said to me: *write it down, so someone knows.* Memory is the only vault they couldn't burn, General. I am simply the door of it."

She recited for fifty-three minutes. When she finished, the room did something Jonah had never seen a room do at an inquiry: it stayed silent, out of respect rather than procedure, until the major-general himself broke it.

* * *

The finding came on Friday.

Jonah heard it standing, wedged among the townspeople, with Tom's absence beside her like a chair left empty at a table. The major-general read aloud in a dry voice that could not quite flatten what he was reading: that the military administration of the district had exceeded its mandate; that preventive detentions had been conducted without lawful authority; that the destruction of garrison records constituted a serious offense; that Colonel Reginald Ashworth was relieved of command, stripped of his district authority, and remanded to the War Office for disciplinary proceedings.

Ashworth received it the way he received everything — with courtesy, with folded hands, with the faint smile of a man who believed all verdicts were provisional. As the officers rose, his gaze found Jonah across the room, and he inclined his head slightly, one gentleman acknowledging another across a lost game. She did not return it. She would remember his face, as she had memorized the informer's — filed away with all the others, in the ledger she kept now behind her eyes.

But it was not Ashworth she watched leave the room. It was the crowd outside.

Word had traveled faster than the major-general's voice. When Jonah stepped out into the pale afternoon, the square in front of the former ballroom was full — the whole town, it seemed, the fishing families and cannery girls and retired pilots, and old Josiah Blackwood standing at their head with his cap in his hands and a bruise-shaped memory in his posture. Someone had brought a fiddle. Someone had brought buns from Mrs. Tregowan's oven. And above them, run up the flagpole of the town hall that soldiers had occupied at dawn eight months ago, flew no official colors at all but a bedsheet, dyed purple, green and white by hands unknown.

Edith came to stand beside her. For a while neither spoke. Below, the crowd was beginning to sing — not an anthem, nothing martial, just an old harbor song about lights kept burning for boats coming home.

"They'll want speeches," Edith said at last. "Penhale's already drafting mine. And yours."

"Mine? I'm not—"

"You spoke under your own name yesterday, in front of the Crown. Whatever you were before, you aren't that anymore." Edith's eyes, when Jonah met them, held something that was not quite pride and was more than affection — the look of one keeper recognizing another. "There's a hall full of people down there who need to hear the names said aloud. Not stored. Spoken." She paused. "Starting, I think, with one that isn't yet in the book."

Jonah looked out at the square, at the faces of the living, and felt the weight and the warmth of the final page waiting at home in her drawer — the page where, in her own handwriting, she had written *Thomas Quill, striker, organizer, beloved,* and dated it, and closed the book gently as one closes a door on a sleeping child.

"All right," she said. "Hand me the list."

She descended the steps into the noise and the singing, toward the fiddle and the purple-green banner and the work ahead — because a verdict was only paper, and paper was only the beginning. The town had won a battle, but the ledger was not finished, and neither was she. There were names to be spoken, statements still to be sworn, and a national archive waiting for a book that two women intended to deliver together, under both their names.

And somewhere beyond the crowd, half-heard beneath the fiddle tune, a child's voice — some cannery girl's daughter, standing on a bench — was already repeating the first name on the list, learning it, keeping it, passing it on like a lit lamp down a dark road:

"Garrick. Thomas Garrick. Taken the fourth of April."
