## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The countinghouse floors were scrubbed before dawn, because that was when the ledgers slept and the clerks had not yet arrived to walk their dirt across the boards again. Juniper Dunmore had learned this in their first month of indenture, seven years past, and had never once been thanked for it. Gratitude did not appear anywhere in the Meridian Trading Company's accounts. Juniper had checked.

They knelt now at the end of the long gallery, wringing a rag into a bucket of grey water, watching Saltmere's earliest light come through the windows in slats of pearl and smoke. The harbor beyond was still dark enough that the masts stood up like reeds in ink, and the gulls were only beginning their argument with the morning. Somewhere below, in the cellars where the Company kept its paper the way other houses kept wine, the first clerks would be unlocking doors with keys on chains around their necks, so that no one could steal what everyone already owed.

Juniper scrubbed in slow, even strokes. There was a rhythm to it, learned the way sailors learn knots, and the rhythm made room for thinking—or for not thinking, which amounted to nearly the same thing and cost less.

* * *

The debt was written down in a book, and therefore it existed. That was the first law of Saltmere, older than the seawalls: if the ink said a thing, then the thing was true, and no amount of weeping or weather could make it untrue. The ink said that Elias Dunmore, master of the pearl sloop *Kindly Weather*, had drowned with all hands off the Serrat Reefs seven years ago, and that his estate—two rooms above a chandler's shop, a sea chest, a coil of good rope, and a daughter—passed to the Company against the costs of salvage and burial and the interest accruing thereon.

The ink said Juniper owed four hundred and eleven marks.

Juniper had done the arithmetic more times than they could count, kneeling exactly here, rag in hand. They earned two marks a month, minus board, minus clothing, minus the annual fee for the keeping of records, minus the fee for the fee. At this rate the debt would be paid in full in thirty-one years, provided nothing went wrong—provided no winter illness, no broken window charged to the household, no new levy, no clerk's error in their favor or against. Nothing ever went right either. The number did not move. It was not meant to move. It was meant to be a horizon, and horizons are beautiful precisely because you cannot arrive at them.

Other people had history. Juniper had understood this early, the way one understands tides—not bitterly, just accurately. The merchants' children in the square had grandmothers and grudges, portraits of men who had owned ships, stories told over and over until the telling wore grooves in them like water wearing stone. The divers who worked the oyster beds had songs about the sea taking their people, and the singing was grief, yes, but it was also possession: *this happened to us; we keep it.*

Juniper's history fit inside a single fact: *my father drowned.* Seven words, if you counted them generously. Everything before that fact belonged to a child too young to have been paying attention, and everything after belonged to the Company. There was nothing left over. Juniper had made peace with it the way one makes peace with weather—with resignation dressed carefully as acceptance, buttoned to the throat, worn daily until the seams stopped showing.

It was easier that way. Wanting things was a debt too, and debts compounded.

* * *

By mid-morning Juniper was at their proper post, third desk from the drafty window in the Hall of Entries, copying figures from loose slips into the great bound books. The work required a steady hand and an empty heart, and Juniper possessed both in abundance. Around them sat forty clerks in a silence broken only by nib-scratch, the coughing of old Master Pellwin, and the far-off cries of fishwives selling the day's catch in voices like gulls fighting over a spine.

Juniper was good at the work. This was not pride; it was observation. They knew the filing system the way a blind man knows a hallway—by its echoes, by the places where it caught. They knew that the Company recorded everything twice and trusted neither copy. They knew that entries from the harbor office arrived on blue slips and warehouse entries on white, and that when a slip went missing, the entry was reconstructed from memory and entered anyway, because the ledger must always be full. Emptiness frightened the Company. An empty line was a door left open, and doors left open let in questions.

"Entry," said Master Pellwin, without looking up, and slid a slip across the aisle.

Juniper took it. *Salvage recovery, vessel Kindly Weather, final accounting, ref. D-118 through D-124.* The name landed in their chest the way cold water lands—a shock that passes quickly into numbness, because the body has decided there is nothing to be done about it. Their father's ship appeared in the ledgers often. The Company liked to revisit its victories.

Juniper copied the figure, carried the book to the archive stacks at the rear of the hall, and began the cross-reference. The stacks rose to the ceiling, aisles of green boxes labeled by year, and the air smelled of dust and tallow and the faint salt rot that got into everything in Saltmere and was never entirely scrubbed out, not even from paper. Especially not from paper. Paper remembered.

D-118. The box for the year of the wreck was heavy, overfull, stuffed with the estates of drowned men. Juniper set it on the reading ledge and opened it, and the smell came up out of it like something exhaling—old glue, brine, and beneath it all a sweetness like flowers left too long in a closed room.

They found the folder. They found the salvage writ, the appraiser's note, the bill for the funeral that had contained no body, priced at the standard rate. And then, tucked behind it where it had no business being, a second folder with a torn corner, misfiled—the kind of thing Juniper noticed the way other people noticed a wrong note in a hymn.

The label read, in a junior clerk's careless hand:

*DUNMORE — deceased.*

Juniper's own surname. Of course it was. Half the harbor was named Dunmore or had been; it was a common name, like salt. But the folder was thin, and thin folders in the D-boxes meant small estates, and small estates meant—

They opened it.

A single record lay inside, and Juniper read it three times before the words agreed to mean anything. It was an indenture registration, the standard form, the one Juniper signed at eleven years old without understanding half the clauses. But the column marked STATUS OF PARTY—the column that should have read INDENTURED, ACTIVE—had been struck through with a single efficient line and corrected in another hand:

*Deceased. Estate closed. No further action.*

*No further action.* Four words that ended a person. Somewhere in the machinery of the Company, a pen had moved across Juniper's life and declared it finished, and the machine had gone on humming, satisfied. They were not a debtor in this book. They were a remainder—an entry balanced and forgotten, a rounding error with a heartbeat.

Juniper stood very still among the green boxes while the countinghouse murmured on behind them, nibs scratching, Pellwin coughing, the whole vast patient apparatus of the Meridian Trading Company digesting the lives of the harbor without pausing between courses.

Here was the strange thing, the thing Juniper would turn over for years afterward: the feeling was not anger. Not yet. It was smaller than anger and sharper—it was the feeling of a floor tilting a degree, barely perceptible, just enough that a body that thought itself standing still suddenly understands it has been falling all along. Resignation, Juniper discovered, is only peace until someone shows you the paperwork proves otherwise. Then it is just a word, and the seams start showing.

They looked at their own name, struck through. Deceased. Closed. No further action.

And underneath the numbness, in some cellar of the self Juniper had not visited in seven years, something knocked once—softly, the way a fist knocks on a door from inside a locked room—and went quiet again.

Juniper closed the folder. They did not correct the error. Correcting errors was a clerk's duty, and a dutiful clerk would have carried the folder to Pellwin and watched the pen move the other way, restoring them to the rolls of the living and the owing. Instead Juniper slid it back into the box, back behind the salvage writ, back into the dark where the Company filed its dead.

They told themselves it changed nothing. A mistake in a box in a room no one entered. Ink was ink; it could not drown you twice.

They almost believed it.

* * *

That evening the fog came off the harbor in earnest, swallowing the masts, muffling the fishwives' last calls. Juniper walked home along the seawall with their collar up, past the chandler's shop where they'd been born, past the spot where the two rooms had been sold out from under them and become a sailmaker's loft where strangers now laughed at supper. The lamp-lighter was making his rounds, and each lantern he lit bloomed gold for a moment before the fog ate it.

At the foot of the seawall stairs, Juniper stopped. It was habit, not ritual—they did not permit themselves rituals. Seven years ago, on the night the news came, a woman from the harbor office had stood roughly here, holding a paper she did not read aloud because Juniper was a child and the paper was long, and had said only: *Your father's ship is lost. The Company will see you're cared for.* Cared for. The phrase had a taste, like copper coins.

Juniper looked out at the black water where the *Kindly Weather* was supposed to be lying, and felt the usual nothing—the well-practiced nothing, smooth as river stone, worn by handling. My father drowned. History belongs to other people. The debt stands at four hundred and eleven marks.

Then, from up the fog-bound street, came footsteps running—uneven, desperate, the slap of soaked boots on wet cobbles—and a shape stumbled out of the murk toward the lamppost at the stair's foot, catching it with one arm to stay upright. A stranger, half-drowned by the look of him, coat streaming, chest heaving, eyes wild and fixed on Juniper with the terrible focus of a man who has swum through hell to reach one particular door.

"You," the stranger gasped, and fumbled inside his coat, dragging out an oilcloth packet clutched against his chest like a rescued child. "You're the Dunmore. I know your face—you have his face." He thrust the packet toward Juniper with a shaking hand. "He's alive. Your father wrote these. He's *alive*—"
