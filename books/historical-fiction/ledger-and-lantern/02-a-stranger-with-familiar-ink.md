## 2. A Stranger with Familiar Ink

The countinghouse smelled of tallow and wet stone, the way it always did after rain, when the river breathed through the foundation cracks and turned every ledger's corner soft. Juniper Dunmore had learned to read that smell as a kind of weather. Damp meant slow business. Slow business meant fewer clerks at the long desks, fewer eyes on the floor, and a longer stretch in which to scrub without being corrected for scrubbing wrong.

There was, Juniper had discovered, an infinite number of ways to scrub a floor incorrectly. Too fast suggested haste, and haste suggested guilt. Too slow suggested idleness, and idle hands were a debt against the soul, or so Under-Clerk Brannigan liked to say, tapping his quill against his teeth like a man chewing on someone else's bone.

Juniper knelt now beside the great oak counter, wringing the rag into a pail gone gray with salt-grime, and watched the water spiral down the drain. Seven years of gray water. Seven years of watching other people's fortunes pass across that counter in paper and coin—cargoes of indigo, barrels of whale oil, pearls strung like small cold moons—and none of it, not one farthing of it, belonging to anyone Juniper had ever loved.

*History belongs to other people,* their father used to say, back when he was alive enough to say things. *We're divers, little bird. We go under, we come up, we keep what fits in our fists.* He'd laughed when he said it, salt-crusted and sunburnt, his hands enormous around Juniper's small ones. Then the *Meridian Star* went down off the Teeth with forty-one souls aboard, and the Company sent a clerk with a black ribbon on his sleeve to inform the family that Captain Elias Dunmore's estate—such as it was, the boat, the diving rights, the narrow house on Lantern Row—was forfeit under salvage law, and would they care to settle their father's debts by entering indenture?

They had cared to. What else does a twelve-year-old do?

Juniper pressed the rag flat against the boards and pushed. The wood gleamed. Somewhere behind them, Brannigan cleared his throat in the particular way that meant *faster*, and Juniper obliged, and thought nothing, because thinking was another form of debt, and they had sworn off debts they could not pay.

It was near the change of the tide when the door banged open.

Not opened. *Banged*—shoulder-first, hard enough to rattle the brass fittings, and the man who came through it was streaming water onto the clean floor Juniper had just finished. Sea water, from the look of it, green-tinged and smelling of weed and bilge. His coat was torn along one arm. His face was gray as old rope, and he stood swaying in the doorway like a mast after the storm has passed through it.

"I need—" he began, and then his legs simply declined to continue the conversation, and he went down onto the countinghouse floor with a sound like a dropped sack of oysters.

The room went still. A junior clerk stopped mid-entry, quill hovering, a bead of ink trembling at its nib. Brannigan rose from his desk with the unhurried menace of a man who owned the floor everyone stood on.

"Now what," Brannigan said, "is this."

"Drowned," croaked the man from the floor. "Nearly. I need—I'm looking for—"

"You're looking for a physician, and you'll find one at your own expense, which I suspect you cannot meet." Brannigan snapped his fingers toward the door. "Harrow, fetch the watch. Driftwood washes up on the beach, not in my countinghouse."

Something in Juniper moved before Juniper did. Later they would turn it over and over—the strange autonomy of the body, how it acted out of some ledger older than indenture—but in the moment there was only the fact of kneeling beside the stranger, one hand finding his shoulder, the other pressing the rag into his grip.

"Here," Juniper heard themself say. "Hold this. Press here—you're bleeding through the coat."

The man's eyes found theirs. They were pale eyes, sea-scoured, ringed with the bruised violet of someone who had not slept in days, and they fixed on Juniper with an intensity that made the hair rise along their arms.

"Name," he rasped. "Your name. Tell me your name."

"Juniper," Brannigan answered for them, coldly amused, arriving at their elbow. "An indentured hand. You'll address the Company's property through me."

"Not yours." The man tried to rise, failed, gripped Juniper's wrist with fingers cold as mooring chain. "Dunmore. You're Dunmore. You have her hands. Her jaw. Gods, you have her—"

"Let go of them," Brannigan said sharply, "or I'll have you flogged for touching Company property—"

"Look in my coat." The words came out cracked, desperate, each one costing him something. "Inner pocket. Left side. *Please.* Before the watch comes. Please."

And Juniper—fool, traitor to every survival instinct they had spent seven years sharpening—reached into the ruined coat while Brannigan was still drawing breath for the next threat.

The letters were wrapped in oilskin, tied with tarred twine, sodden but sealed. There were perhaps ten of them. They were stained with salt. And on the face of the topmost one, in handwriting Juniper knew better than their own heartbeat—in loops and slashes they had traced a thousand times in memory, in the hand that had written *little bird* across seven years of birthday letters and harbor notes and one final, unfinished page—was written:

*For J. Dunmore.*

Juniper could not breathe.

That is not a figure of speech. They understood, distantly, that breathing was occurring somewhere in the world—the junior clerk was breathing, Brannigan was breathing, the rain outside the windows was the sky breathing—but Juniper's own chest had closed like a fist around a coal. Because Elias Dunmore had drowned seven years ago off the Teeth. The Company had said so. The Company had *filed* it so, in triplicate, and the Company did not make errors, the Company did not misfile, the Company's word was the bedrock under Saltmere—

*Dated three years after the wreck.*

Juniper saw the date the way a drowning person sees light through water. It sat there on the fold of the letter, plain as a toll stamp, undeniable: written two full years after the sea had supposedly taken their father.

"You're lying," Juniper whispered. Their voice sounded foreign, scraped. "These are forged. People forge dead men's hands. There are—there are tricks—"

"Do I look like a forger?" The man almost laughed; it came out as a cough, wet and terrible. "Oren Voss. I served under your father six years. I know his hand because he wrote my mother's letters for her, she couldn't hold a pen. Little bird—that's what he called you, isn't it? He told me if ever I got clear, if ever I found you—"

"Harrow's gone for the watch," Brannigan announced, and there was iron in it now, real alarm, because he was not a stupid man and he could see the shape of what stood in his countinghouse holding its breath. "Whatever fraud this is, it ends at the magistrate's—"

"The audit." Oren Voss hauled himself up Juniper's arm, and his whisper was urgent enough to cut through everything else. "Your father found the ledgers, Juniper. He found what they did—the wrecks, the estates, all of it, *all of it written down*. And they've been hunting those pages for seven years, and in nine days the auditors come to Saltmere, and when they come, every record that can burn will burn. Do you understand? Your father isn't dead. Or he wasn't. And the proof—"

"Enough." Brannigan's hand closed on Juniper's collar, hauling them upright, and the letters slipped from their fingers—no, no—they caught them against their chest, both arms folding around the oilskin packet like a diver closing around a pearl, and Brannigan froze, because Juniper had never, in seven years of perfect silence, held on to anything.

"Give those here," Brannigan said quietly.

"They're addressed to me."

"They are *paper in my house*, and you are *debt in my house*, and the watch is coming up the street as we speak." His voice dropped, silky. "Unless, of course, you'd like me to explain to them why an escaped piece of Company property is harboring stolen correspondence."

"I never ran," Juniper said. "I'm standing exactly where you put me."

"And yet."

Boots in the street. Torchlight beginning to smear yellow against the rain-dark windows. Oren Voss grabbed Juniper's sleeve, and his pale eyes were wild and certain and terrified all at once.

"There's a cellar," he breathed. "Under the chandlery on Lantern Row. Your father's people are there—divers who remember him, who've been waiting seven years for someone to carry this. Come with me, or stay and let them file you away too. But decide *now*, little bird, because that door opens in thirty seconds either way."

Juniper looked down at the letters crushed against their chest. At the topmost one, where their father's hand curved and looped, patient as tide, waiting seven years to be read.

*History belongs to other people.* That had been the whole of their creed. That had kept them safe, or whatever passed for safe in a life measured out in gray water and gray floors.

The watch knocked once, politely, the way the powerful knock.

Juniper ran.

* * *

Lantern Row had not changed, which was its own small cruelty. The same leaning houses, the same nets hung like sleeping bats between eaves, the same smell of pitch and brine that lived in Juniper's body somewhere below memory. They had not walked this street since they were twelve years old, and their feet remembered it anyway, carrying them past the narrow house that had been theirs—someone else's window lit there now, someone else's shadow moving behind the glass—down the alley, to the chandlery with its door standing open just wide enough for a thin slice of dark.

Inside was stairs, and below the stairs was lamplight, and below the lamplight was a cellar that smelled of wax and seawater and old arguments, where Oren Voss collapsed onto a crate and finally let himself shake, and where a woman with silver-braided hair looked up from a table covered in charts and took one long look at Juniper's face.

"So," she said. "He found you after all."

"He said my father—" Juniper's voice broke on the word. They steadied themselves against a beam crusted with barnacles, indoors, impossibly, barnacles above the tide line, as though the sea itself had been climbing toward this room for seven years. "He said these are in his hand. He said they're dated after—"

"Read one," the woman said simply.

Juniper untied the twine with fingers that would not stop trembling. Unwrapped the oilskin. Lifted the topmost letter into the lamplight, and there it was, the whole familiar country of it—the heavy downstroke, the way he crossed his *t*s like rigging under strain, the little bird he always drew in the margin, wings spread, rising.

*My dear little bird. If you are reading this, then Oren got through, which means I owe him a drink and an apology, in whichever order he prefers...*

Below, in the margin: the bird. Rising.

Juniper sat down on the cellar floor without deciding to, and held the letter the way you hold a live coal, and could not breathe, and did not need to, because for the first time in seven years something else was breathing for them—hope, terrible and enormous, filling their chest like floodwater.

Above them, muffled through the floorboards, the watch was still knocking at the countinghouse door. Below them, the woman called Sef began, calmly, to snuff lamps.

"We have nine days," Oren Voss said from his crate, his voice raw and steady and absolutely certain. "And your father's last letter tells us where he hid the rest."
