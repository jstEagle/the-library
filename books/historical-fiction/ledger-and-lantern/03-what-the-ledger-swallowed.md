## 3. What the Ledger Swallowed

The cellar smelled of brine and tallow, of rope gone soft with salt, of the particular sourness of a room where too many people had been afraid for too long. Oren Voss sat on an upturned oyster crate with his boots still dripping, and he watched the child—no, not a child, he corrected himself, though the instinct was older than the truth—watched Juniper Dunmore stand with their back against the far wall as if the wall might open and take them somewhere else.

Above them, through the floorboards, came the muffled sounds of Saltmere going about its evening: cart wheels, a gull screaming over the fish market, someone's off-key singing. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city that did not know yet what it was sitting on.

"You said you have letters," Juniper said. Their voice was flat, careful, a voice that had been trained by years of countinghouse floors to give away nothing. "Dated after the wreck. That's what you shouted in the street. That's why they're hunting you."

"That's why they'll kill us both, if we're careless." Oren reached inside his coat, slowly, letting them see every movement. He drew out the oilcloth packet and set it on the crate beside him without opening it. "I'm not going to hand these to you like a peddler selling miracles. You deserve to understand what they are first. What they cost."

"Then talk."

So he talked.

* * *

"I was nineteen when I signed aboard the *Kestrel's Grace*. Deckhand, third class. I'd never seen open water before that day—the captain laughed at how green I was, said I looked like a cabbage someone had planted on his quarterdeck." Oren's mouth did something that was almost a smile. It hurt to wear it. "Elias Dunmore. Sixteen years commanding Company ships, never lost cargo worth complaining about, never lost a man he could help losing. The divers trusted him. Do you understand how rare that was? The divers trust no one. They're the ones who go down into the dark and come up bleeding from the ears, and the Company pays them by the pearl and cheats them by the pound. But your father learned their hand-signs. Ate at their table. Carried Sef's youngest on his shoulders through the harbor festival like he had a right to."

Juniper's breath caught—a small sound, quickly smothered.

"He talked about you," Oren said quietly. "All the time. The way you'd memorized tide tables for pleasure. The way you corrected his spelling in his own letters. He kept one of your corrections folded up in his chart case like a relic."

"Don't." The word cracked down the middle. "Don't do that. Don't make him warm and real to me now, after seven years of—" Juniper stopped. Pressed the back of their hand against their mouth. When they spoke again, the flatness had returned, but Oren could see the labor of it, like watching someone hold a door shut against a flood. "He drowned. The Company sent the notice. Wreck of the *Grace*, all hands lost, estate assessed, debts transferred. I was twelve. I signed the indenture papers because there was nothing left to sign and no one to ask whether signing was right."

"The notice was true and false at once." Oren leaned forward, elbows on knees. "The *Kestrel's Grace* went down off the Serpent Reefs, that part is honest. What the notice didn't say is who scuttled her."

The cellar went very still. Even the house above seemed to stop moving.

"We were carrying pearl stock from the southern beds—two seasons' harvest, plus the private holdings the divers had entrusted to the Company for safe passage. Insurance writs on that cargo alone would have covered half the Meridian Company's debts that year. And the Company was drowning, though the ledgers in Saltmere would never say so. Bad investments in the northern fisheries. Bribes paid out to officials who hadn't delivered. So certain men in the countinghouse made a decision." Oren's hands curled into fists on his thighs. "They hired a salvage crew out of Corvane itself. Men who knew how to open a hull from the outside, quiet, below the waterline, while the ship lay at anchor waiting for a tide. Eleven of us were ashore procuring stores. The rest—the captain among them—went down with her."

"They murdered him for paper," Juniper whispered. "For insurance."

"For estates, too. That's the part that took Elias seven years to prove. Because the wreck wasn't the crime—it was the beginning of it. Every diver whose pearls went down with the *Grace* found themselves suddenly owing the Company sums they couldn't verify. Estates seized for 'salvage recovery.' Homes taken. Children indentured." Oren looked directly at Juniper then, and did not flinch from it. "You weren't collateral, Juniper. You were the harvest. One entry among hundreds, filed under losses the Company manufactured so it could collect."

Juniper slid down the wall until they were sitting, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around their shins. They didn't cry. Oren had expected crying. This was worse—this was arithmetic, he realized. They were recalculating seven years of their life, line by line, and finding the sum unbearable.

"Why do you have the letters?" they asked finally.

* * *

"Because I failed him."

Oren said it plainly, because dressing it up would be theft of another kind.

"I was one of the eleven ashore. We came back to empty water and floating debris and a Company clerk with a pre-written casualty list, dated three days before the ship sank. Dated, Juniper. Before. I didn't understand what I was holding until years later, and by then I'd signed my own statement attesting to the accident, because a nineteen-year-old deckhand doesn't argue with men who control whether he eats. I carried that signature like a stone in my chest ever since."

He rose, joints protesting, and crossed to the single high window—a slot barely wider than a hand, glowing with the last grey light. "Elias didn't drown. I don't know all of how—he won't say, even in ink—but he came up. And instead of running, instead of taking whatever broken life the sea offered him, he spent seven years becoming a ghost with a purpose. He worked the salvage ports under other names. He bought drinks for corrupt clerks and remembered what they bragged. He dove wrecks himself, at his age, in cold water, pulling up Company manifests that contradicted the official record. And when he had enough—when he had the ledger pages, the real ones, cross-referenced against the falsified ones—he started writing letters."

"To whom?"

"To everyone who mattered. To Sef, whose family lost two boats. To a magistrate inland named Corvane, who has a reputation for being unbuyable. And to you." Oren turned from the window. "Every year on your birthday. Seven letters. He wrote them not knowing if you were alive, if you still used his name, if the Company had buried you as thoroughly as it buried him. He wrote them anyway."

He picked up the oilcloth packet and held it out.

Juniper didn't move. "I can't."

"You can."

"If I touch them, they're real. If they're real, then everything I made peace with—the numbness, the floors, the name I stopped saying—I made peace with a lie. Seven years, Oren. I built a whole life on the foundation that grief was finished, that history happened to other people." Their voice shook. "I don't know how to be someone's child again."

Oren knelt—not easily; the sea had taken payment from his knees long ago—and set the packet on the floor between them, halfway across the space, so that reaching for it would be their own act and no one else's.

"Then don't be anyone's child tonight," he said. "Be a witness. These pages are evidence. The ledger entries your father copied prove the salvage crew was paid from Company funds six weeks *before* the wreck. Prove eleven estates were seized on the strength of a shipwreck the Company arranged. Prove that Captain Elias Dunmore, listed dead, was drawing on a hidden account in Corvane to fund his own investigation—which means the Company knows he lives, which means the hunt you saw tonight isn't about me." He held their gaze. "It's about making sure no Dunmore exists to contest anything."

Silence stretched between them, taut as rigging in a storm.

Then Juniper reached out and drew the packet into their lap, and untied the knot with fingers that trembled only a little, and unfolded the first page—and Oren watched them recognize the handwriting from across the whole distance of seven years. Their whole body changed. Shoulders that had learned to stoop beneath invisible debt straightened. Something kindled behind their eyes that had been banked coals since childhood.

"It's him," Juniper breathed. "The loops. He always looped his *d*s like waves. I used to tease him that he drew the sea into everything he wrote."

"He did draw the sea into everything," Oren said. "Including his escape plans. Read the third letter. There's a reference to 'the lantern account'—a cache of documents hidden where only a Dunmore would think to look. Your father hid the full ledger, Juniper. The original. And he built the hiding place around a memory only you would share."

Juniper looked up sharply. "What memory?"

"That's his to tell and yours to find. But here is what matters tonight." Oren stood, and the cellar's shadows gathered on him like an old coat. "In nine days, the Meridian Company begins its annual audit. Every questionable record gets burned—officially 'consolidated.' Every loose end tied off. Including, I suspect, any indentured Dunmore who might someday testify. We have nine days to find that ledger, or copy what your father sent, and get it past three Company checkpoints to Magistrate Corvane."

"And if we fail?"

"Then Elias spent seven years building a fire in the rain, and the rain wins."

Juniper looked down at the letters spread across their knees, at the looping sea-worn handwriting of a father the world had declared dead. When they spoke, their voice had changed—still quiet, still careful, but with iron laid along its spine now, the way a keel lies hidden beneath a hull.

"My shift at the countinghouse begins at dawn," they said. "And I know exactly which clerks keep the audit schedules, and which of them files his secrets under the wrong name." A ghost of something fierce crossed their face. "Tell your divers to get ready, Oren Voss. If the Company wants a Dunmore erased from its books, it should remember one thing."

"What's that?"

"I've been keeping their ledgers for seven years. I know where every body is filed."

Above them, through the floorboards, Saltmere's bells began to ring the hour—and somewhere out in the dark harbor, a lantern swung at the end of a diver's mast, signaling that the table was set, and the family Juniper had never known they had was waiting to feed them.
