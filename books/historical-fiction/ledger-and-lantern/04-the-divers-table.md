## 4. The Divers' Table

The cellar door was hidden behind a fishmonger's stall, and I only understood that we were meant to enter it when Sef, the matriarch with hands like knotted rope, shouldered through the hanging salt cod without a word. Oren followed her. After a moment in which my legs debated the matter seriously, so did I.

Down a stair slick with brine, past barrels that breathed of brack and tar, into a low room where candlelight pooled like spilled honey on long trestle tables. The air was warm and thick—fish stew, woodsmoke, wet wool, the mineral ghost of the sea that clung to divers even after they'd wrung out their clothes. Someone was fiddling with a lute whose strings had gone soft as old rope, plucking something half-remembered. And everywhere, voices. Dozens of them. Arguing, laughing, interrupting each other with the ease of people who have been interrupting each other for decades.

I stood at the bottom of the stairs with my Company-issue boots dripping on someone's clean floor, and felt like a coin dropped into the wrong purse.

"Sit," said Sef, not turning around. It wasn't an invitation. It was weather.

I sat.

* * *

They fed me before they asked me anything, which I would learn is the divers' law. A bowl of stew appeared in front of me—eel and potato and something green that grew where it shouldn't have, all of it swimming in fat that gleamed like the pearls they pulled from the dark. Bread came wrapped in a cloth still warm from a bake oven somewhere above us. Oren watched me eat from across the table with an expression I couldn't read, his bandaged hands curled around a mug he hadn't drunk from.

"You're thin," said an old woman to my left, as if reporting a crime. "Elias was thin too, but he ate like the tide coming in. Relentless." She refilled my bowl without asking whether I wanted it refilled. Her name, I gathered, was Marta, and she had once held a diving record for depth that three men at this table were currently disputing.

"I'm sorry," I said, "I don't—"

"Don't apologize to food," Marta said sternly. "It's had worse said over it."

Laughter rolled down the table like a wave finding its shore. I ducked my head and ate, and the stew was so good I nearly embarrassed myself.

Then a broad man with a beard braided into two grey ropes leaned across the table and squinted at me. "It's the eyes," he announced, to no one and everyone. "Look at the eyes. Elias Dunmore had exactly those eyes, always watching the ledger instead of the horizon."

"He watched everything," said another voice. "Watched the water like it owed him money."

"It did owe him money," said Marta.

"That's not how debt works, you old barnacle—"

And just like that they were off, six or seven conversations braided together, arguments about currents and captains and a card game played eleven years ago whose outcome was still under formal protest. Nobody looked at me except sideways, quick glances, taking my measure the way they might take the measure of a pearl—by some instinct that didn't require opening me up to do it.

A girl of maybe twelve slid onto the bench beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched. She smelled of salt and lamp oil. "You're the captain's child," she said. Not a question.

"I'm—" I started to say *an indentured clerk*, which is what I had been for seven years, the whole of my identity filed neatly in a Company drawer. But the word wouldn't come. "Yes."

She nodded gravely, as if confirming a fact already known throughout the room, then handed me a boiled crab on a plate. "You get the first one. That's the rule when someone comes home."

"I'm not—I've never been here before."

"Coming home isn't about the place," she said, with the crushing authority of childhood, and went back to her own dinner.

* * *

Later, when the tables had been pushed back and the lute had found two more strings' worth of courage, a man named Doss—huge, gentle-faced, with forearms scarred white from old line-burns—pounded the table twice and called for quiet in a voice that made the candles shiver.

"Tell them," he said to Oren. "Tell them what the letters say. They've earned more than rumors."

Oren stood. He looked older in the candlelight, the sea having carved him and then changed its mind halfway through. He told it plainly, the way sailors tell hard things: Captain Elias Dunmore survived the wreck of the *Meridian Star*. He had spent years gathering proof that the Company had scuttled its own ships—three of them—to drown their crews and claim the estates of every diver and officer lost aboard. The letters proved dates, cargo, orders signed above the captain's rank. And somewhere, if the letters could be believed, there was a ledger. The true one. The one that recorded what the Company actually took.

"The audit," Oren finished. "We heard it in the countinghouse. Nine days. When the auditors come, every record in Saltmere gets burned or rewritten. Whatever proof exists has to be out of the city before then."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down like deep water.

Then Doss said, "My brother drowned on the *Star*." His voice didn't shake. "They told my mother the sea took him. Turns out it was men in a countinghouse." He looked around the table. "I say we finish what the captain started."

"They'll call it piracy," someone objected. "Smuggling. They'll take the boats."

"They'll take the boats anyway," said Sef, from the shadows near the stair. She hadn't raised her voice; she never seemed to need to. "An audit is a net. You don't argue with a net. You cut it, or you drown in it quietly." She looked at Oren. "You'll have my oyster boat. And you'll have whatever else this table decides to give, because this table decided seven years ago, the night the news came, that Elias Dunmore's fight was ours. We just needed to know there was still a fight to be had."

Murmurs of assent moved around the table like current. I sat among them holding my second bowl of stew I hadn't asked for, and felt something in my chest crack open—a hairline fracture, painless, terrifying.

These people had known my father. Not the way I knew him—the smell of ink on his cuffs, the song he hummed while sharpening his razor, the way he'd lift me onto his shoulders at the harbor and point at ships and lie beautifully about where they were going. These people knew him as crew knew a captain: his temper, his fairness, the time he'd dove himself to cut a fouled line because he refused to order anyone into water he wouldn't enter. They carried pieces of him I had never seen, and suddenly my father was larger than the memory I'd kept sealed for seven years, and smaller too—human, argued-over, disputed, loved imperfectly by imperfect people who were now, without ceremony, loving me.

Nobody said *we are your family now*. Nobody made a speech. The girl gave me the first crab. Marta filled my bowl. Sef's boat became mine to ride in. That was all. That was somehow everything.

History, I thought, staring into the candleflame until it doubled and swam. All my life I'd believed history belonged to other people—magistrates, merchants, men with monuments. That people like me were what history happened *to*, silently, between entries. But here it was, alive and loud and smelling of eel stew: history sitting on a bench, arguing about a card game, deciding over bread and beer to walk into danger for a dead man's name and a living child's inheritance.

For the first time in my life, I belonged to it.

* * *

It was near midnight when the warmth turned cold.

Sef climbed onto a bench and the room hushed again, faster this time. She held up a folded paper, damp at the corners, the wax seal of the Meridian Trading Company glaring red even in the poor light.

"This came by way of a customs clerk who owes me," she said. "It's the audit proclamation, posted tonight at the harbor offices before the ink was dry." She unfolded it. "'By order of the Meridian Trading Company, a full inventory and reconciliation of all records, estates, and obligations shall commence in nine days' time.'" Her eyes lifted. "Nine days. Every ship searched. Every warehouse sealed. Every diver's account reviewed—and any diver found harboring fugitives or stolen documents forfeits everything. Boat, license, house. Everything."

The candlelight seemed to dim, though nothing had changed. Around me, faces I had just begun to learn set themselves into expressions I recognized from the countinghouse—the look of people doing arithmetic with their lives.

Doss spoke first. "Then we move in eight."

"The checkpoints—" someone began.

"Will be handled," Sef said, and looked at Oren, and something passed between them that I wasn't yet part of. "Tomorrow. This table plans best on full stomachs and short sleep. Go home. Kiss your children. And if anyone asks, you spent tonight gutting fish and complaining about me, which will be believable."

Uneven laughter, thinner than before. People began gathering cloaks and lanterns, and the room that had felt like a lungful of warm air began exhaling toward the stairs.

Oren found me by the wall. Up close I could see how tired he was, how the guilt he carried sat on him like ballast. "Juniper. About tomorrow—we'll talk routes, papers, all of it. But I want you to understand something first." He hesitated, and I saw him choose honesty over comfort. "What Sef said about forfeiting everything? She meant it. Everyone in this room is risking their lives for a name that isn't theirs. For you."

The words landed wrong and right at once. "That's not—"

"It is," he said gently. "And you should know it before you decide what you're willing to risk back."

He left me with that, climbing the stairs behind the others, and I stood alone in the guttering cellar amid the wreckage of the feast—bowls, crab shells, a lute lying silent on its back like a beached thing. Nine days. Somewhere across the city, in the countinghouse where I had scrubbed floors for seven years, clerks were already pulling drawers, already sharpening their knives of ink, and my name sat misfiled in one of them under *deceased*.

I picked up the lantern Sef had left burning for me, and its small flame steadied my hand.

Eight days, then. Tomorrow, a plan. Tonight, for the first time since my father's handwriting had walked back into my life, I fell asleep—not resigned, not numb, but afraid in a way that finally felt like living.
