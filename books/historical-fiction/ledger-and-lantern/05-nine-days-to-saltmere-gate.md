## 5. Nine Days to Saltmere Gate

Oren Voss had learned to measure time in the way drowning men measure air: not by the clock, but by how much of it was left before the water came in. Nine days. That was what Sef's grandson had counted off on his fingers, standing in the doorway of the diver's cellar with salt-crusted hair and a boy's careless cruelty for arithmetic. Nine days until the Company's audit clerks arrived at the Saltmere countinghouse, nine days until every record that could convict them was pulped and re-inked into innocence, nine days until the ledger pages Oren carried against his ribs became worth exactly nothing more than the rag they were written on.

He sat now at a table made from a ship's hatch cover, in a room that smelled of brine and lamp oil and the sweet rot of oyster shells piled in the corner for lime. The ledger pages lay between them, weighted at the corners with four smooth stones, as if the paper might otherwise take flight—or be taken.

Elias Dunmore's handwriting marched across them in columns: dates, hull numbers, insurance filings, payments to men whose names Oren remembered from the *Meridian Grace*'s own crew list. Men who had been paid to open her seams. Oren had read them so many times he could recite them like scripture, and like scripture they offered no comfort—only judgment.

"The assizes," Ilsa said.

She sat apart from the others, as she always did, a thin woman with ink-stained fingers and the gray, bleached look of someone who had spent eleven years indoors under Company lamps. She had been a scribe of the second rank once, before she corrected a director's figures in front of other directors. They had not forgiven her the accuracy.

"Magistrate Corvane sits at Harrow Fen," she went on, tapping the table with one stained nail. "Inland assizes, twice yearly. He is the only magistrate on this coast who has ever ruled against Meridian, and only because the case came to him wrapped in enough paperwork that denying it would have cost him more than courage. If we want these pages heard, they go to him. Not to the harbor court. Not to the port reeve. Corvane."

"How far?" asked Sef.

The matriarch of the divers was seventy years old and built like the keel of a boat, broad and scarred and utterly without hurry. She had listened to everything so far with her arms folded and her eyes half-closed, like a woman napping through a sermon she'd already heard. But Oren had seen her kind before, on ships and off them—the stiller they sat, the closer they were to striking.

"Three days' travel by river, if the river cooperates," Ilsa said. "Four checkpoints on the road route—we don't take the road. By water, there are three. The toll at Saltmere Gate, where they check every soul on every deck against the indenture rolls. The patrol station at the burning yards, past the tar works. And the assizes gate itself, which is the worst, because by then Corvane's clerks will know we're coming whether we want them to or not."

"Why will they know?"

Ilsa looked at Oren when she said it, and her voice went flat as slate. "Because the Company will have sent its lawyers ahead of us. They always do. A magistrate doesn't get warned off; he gets *prepared*. They'll arrive with affidavits, with warrants, with a tidy little story: a disgraced scribe forging documents, a deserter impersonating a dead officer's man. And—" she hesitated, the first crack in her composure— "they may have Juniper Dunmore's description already. An escaped indenture. There are notices printed within a day of anyone running. I've written them myself."

The word *escaped* settled over the room like cold fog. Oren's hand went, without his permission, to the place below his shoulder where a Company lash had left its signature twenty years ago.

Sef opened her eyes.

"My boat," she said.

Everyone turned. She said it again, slower, the way she presumably said everything that mattered. "My boat. The *Kestrel's Sister*. Oyster boat, licensed, boring, mine. I've carried pearls under Company seal for thirty years and never once been boarded below decks, because oyster boats smell so badly of mud and death that no clerk wants to go below." Her mouth bent—not quite a smile. "We put your papers under the shell racks. We put your people among my crew. My granddaughters dive for us, real cargo, real licenses. Nobody questions an old woman going upriver to sell stock before audit season empties everyone's purses."

"And you?" Oren asked. "You're known at the gate, Sef. Thirty years known."

"That is precisely why it will work." Now the smile came, and it had teeth in it. "They do not search the faithful, boy. They search the ones who flinch."

* * *

It was near midnight when the argument began, and Oren should have known it would come down to this, because it always came down to this, because Elias Dunmore had argued with him the same way across the same kind of table seven years and a lifetime ago.

"You are not coming."

Juniper stood on the far side of the cellar with their arms crossed, and Oren saw Elias in the set of those shoulders so plainly it hurt—the same stubborn architecture, the same chin lifted at the exact angle that meant the discussion was already over in the speaker's mind and merely awaiting ratification everywhere else.

"They have my name on a notice," Juniper said. "My face is being inked onto bills right now, probably by her—" a jerk of the head toward Ilsa, who did not deny it— "so tell me, Oren, where exactly am I safer? Here? In the city where every clerk knows me, where my bedroll sits empty in the Company barracks waiting for someone to ask why? At least on the river I'm moving."

"You're indentured. You run, you're not a witness anymore, you're a *fugitive*. Corvane can dismiss testimony from a fugitive with a wave of his hand. You testify, you testify clean—brought forward, surrendered formally—"

"Surrendered to whom?" Juniper's voice cracked and they pushed through it. "To the Company? You want me to walk back inside those walls and wait politely while nine days become eight and eight become none? I've spent seven years being patient. Patience got me a floor scrubber and a misfiled grave."

Oren looked at the others for help and found none. Sef was studying her knuckles. Ilsa was very interested in the ledger stones. Even the granddaughters had developed a sudden fascination with mending nets. He was alone in this, as he had perhaps always been alone in it, since the day he'd pulled Elias's letters from a drowned courier's satchel and understood what they would cost everyone who touched them.

"Your father made me promise," Oren said quietly, and hated himself even as the words left his mouth.

"Don't." Juniper's eyes went bright and hard. "Don't you dare. You didn't keep him alive either, and you've carried that like a stone ever since—I watched you carry it for two days and I barely know you. Don't hand me his promises like I'm supposed to hold them too. He wrote me letters, Oren. Seven years of letters I never got. Do you know what's in them? He says he's sorry. He says he's coming home. Every single one says he's coming home." Their voice dropped to something almost gentle, which was worse than shouting. "He never stopped trying to reach me. So don't you stand there and tell me his last wish was for me to sit still."

The lamp flame leaned in some draft Oren couldn't feel. Above them, through the cellar's low ceiling, he could hear the harbor breathing—rigging ticking against masts, water worrying the pilings, the vast indifferent city getting ready for another day of ledgers.

"He trained me," Juniper said. "Did he tell you that? Before the *Grace*, before any of it—he taught me knots, current-reading, how to talk to clerks. He used to say a Dunmore should never be somewhere they can't talk their way out of. Well." A short, bitter laugh. "Seven years of countinghouse floors. I can read a ledger faster than anyone in this room, including her. I know the filing codes. I know which clerks are lazy and which are frightened and which take bribes. If we're carrying proof made of *paper* past people who live in paper, then I'm not cargo, Oren. I'm the sharpest thing on this boat."

Silence. Sef hadn't moved, but somehow her stillness had changed quality, the way water changes before wind arrives.

"Well," the old woman said at last, to no one in particular. "That's the captain's mouth talking, right enough. Heard that tone across a supper table more nights than I can count." She unfolded her arms and looked at Oren directly. "You lost one Dunmore to your caution. How's keeping the second one in a cage working out for you?"

The words went in like a gaff hook. Oren closed his eyes and saw the *Grace* going down in the gray water, saw the boats that should have come, saw Elias's face the last time—no. He hadn't seen it. That was the whole wound. He'd been below decks checking the seals Elias had ordered him to check, and by the time he came up there was only wreckage and gulls and a horizon with no one waving from it.

He had failed one Dunmore by obeying orders. Perhaps that was the lesson the sea had been trying to teach him for seven years, and he'd been too slow a student.

"Below decks," he said finally. "When we're boarded, you stay below decks. You don't speak unless Ilsa tells you to speak. And if it goes wrong—if they take the boat—you swim for the north bank and you don't look back, and you let the pages burn with everything else rather than be taken alive with them." He held up a hand before Juniper could speak. "Those are my terms. Swear them, and you come."

Juniper crossed the cellar and spat in their palm, and held it out—an old diver's oath, older than the Company, older probably than Saltmere itself. Oren took it. The grip was young and strong and terrified, and it was Elias's grip exactly, and he held it one heartbeat longer than the oath required.

"It's done then," Sef said, rising with a groan of joints that had spent seventy years underwater. "Tide turns at dawn. We load shells tonight, papers under the racks, and Ilsa—" 

"I'm writing the manifests," Ilsa said. Already her fingers were moving, ghosting across the table as though it were a page. "Crew rolls, cargo declarations, transit passes. I'll need wax, a director's seal impression—I kept one, don't ask—and six hours." She paused, and for the first time all night something almost like fear moved behind her gray eyes. "And I need to say plainly what none of you seem to grasp. If any of us is taken with forged Company instruments on their person, the penalty isn't the workhouse. It's the gallows at Saltmere Gate, where the road leaves the city, so that everyone leaving can see. I have spent eleven years being careful. I want each of you to understand exactly what you are asking me to stop being."

No one answered her, because there was nothing to answer. Instead, Sef crossed the room and took Ilsa's ink-stained hands in her own rope-scarred ones, and held them, and said something low in the divers' old tongue that Oren didn't know but understood anyway.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, and the *Kestrel's Sister* sat low in the black water with her cargo of shells and secrets. Mist lay on the harbor like a sheet over a body. Somewhere behind them the countinghouse bells rang the hour, and Oren thought he could hear, beneath the bronze, the sound of audit clerks sharpening their pens.

He stood at the tiller while Sef's granddaughters cast off, and Juniper crouched at the rail watching Saltmere recede—the countinghouse roof, the warehouse cranes, the gray wall where they had spent seven years learning to be small. Their face was unreadable as weather.

"North bank," Oren reminded them. "If it goes wrong."

"I heard you."

"I mean it, Juniper."

"I know you mean it." They still didn't look at him. "That's what frightens me."

Ahead, where the harbor narrowed into the river, the first checkpoint rose out of the mist: Saltmere Gate, a stone arch straddling the channel, lanterns still burning in the gray light, and beneath it the toll barge with its desk, its ledger, its clerk waiting with a pen dipped and ready to write down every name that passed.

Oren tightened his hands on the tiller and steered for it.
