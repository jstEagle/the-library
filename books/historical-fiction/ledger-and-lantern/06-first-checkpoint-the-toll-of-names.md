## 6. First Checkpoint: The Toll of Names

The river at Vell's Crossing ran the color of old pewter that morning, flat and dull and patient, as if it had been waiting seven years for us to try to cross it.

I had never seen it before. Seven years in Saltmere, scrubbing the countinghouse floors of the Meridian Trading Company, and my world had been exactly the width of a mop handle: the countinghouse, the dormitory loft above the chandlery, the alley where the gulls fought over fish heads. The Company did not waste money moving indentures who had nowhere to go, and so I had nowhere to go, and I told myself this was peace.

Now I crouched behind a stack of salted herring barrels with Oren Voss's breath fogging the air beside me, and I understood that peace had only ever been another word for a small cage, well kept.

The toll station sat on a spit of gravel where the road narrowed to meet the ferry barge. It was nothing grand—a plank building with a slate roof gone soft with moss, two lanterns still burning pale against the morning, a rope strung between posts to funnel travelers into a line like beads on a string. But beside the clerk's window stood the thing I could not stop looking at: a chest, iron-banded, and out of it a guard was lifting bundles of paper tied in red cord.

Indenture rolls. I knew them the way you know your own pulse. The red cord meant current rolls; the Company used blue for discharged, black for dead, and there was a whole drawer—drawer nine, second cabinet from the window in the Saltmere countinghouse, because the window leaked and the clerk complained about damp—devoted entirely to people like me. People filed under *deceased* by clerical accident, or under *absconded* by clerical convenience, or not filed at all until someone remembered they existed and needed them to stop existing.

"Say nothing," Oren murmured. His hand hovered near his coat, where the ledger pages lay sewn into the lining, flat against his ribs like a second heartbeat. "Let Ilsa do the talking."

Ilsa stood apart from us, dressed in a widow's gray that made her look smaller than she was, holding our papers fanned out like a losing hand she intended to play as a winning one. She had worked on them through the night by candlelight—the ferry passes, the travel writ signed by a salt-merchant whose signature she reproduced better than he could have himself, the little wax seal she'd pressed with a button carved to imitate the Company's oyster-and-anchor. Her hands had been steady all night. Looking at her now, watching her jaw set as the guard waved forward a fishwife and her squalling children, I thought her hands were probably steady now too. It was the rest of us shaking.

Sef had taken the oyster boat downriver to be visible elsewhere, drawing eyes, being loudly Sef somewhere we were not. That left four of us to cross as ordinary travelers: Oren, Ilsa, Tam the youngest diver acting as a hired porter with our single trunk, and me.

Me. Juniper Dunmore, escaped indenture, wanted by no one yet—because no one yet knew I was gone—but written into that iron-banded chest all the same. *Dunmore, J.* Filed under deceased. I had found the entry myself, years ago, when misfiling my own name was the closest thing to freedom I possessed.

"Next," said the guard.

* * *

The clerk inside was young. That was the first thing I noticed, and the thing that frightened me most. Young men are eager. Eager men check everything twice. He had a long face, ink-stained fingers, and a copybook open before him with the careful, cramped lettering of someone still proud of his penmanship. A brass nameplate read **CLERK OF THE TOLL, MERIDIAN CHARTER**. Behind him, the guard with the red-corded bundles stood like a headstone.

Ilsa stepped up to the window. "Passage for three," she said, in the flat, tired voice of a woman who has done this crossing a hundred times and resents every one. She laid the papers down. "Writ of travel, merchant's authorization. My brother's boy is the porter. We're taking goods inland to Corvane for the assizes season—salt, cloth, the usual."

Corvane. The magistrate's name spoken aloud, casual as weather. Ilsa was good. Ilsa was terrifyingly good.

The clerk took up the writ. Turned it over. Held it near the lantern—and here my stomach dropped, because I knew what he was looking for. The watermark. The Company's oyster-shell watermark sat in the lower right corner of every genuine document, faint as breath on glass. Ilsa had simulated it with a thin wash of gum arabic and a stamp cut from a dried cap. It would pass a glance. It would not pass a lantern held close and tilted.

The clerk tilted the lantern close.

"The light's poor in here," Ilsa said pleasantly. "Mornings, this fog off the water gets into everything."

"Mm." He set the writ down. Picked up one of the red-corded bundles instead, thumbed it open, and began to run his finger down columns of names. "Names," he said. "For the roll. All persons crossing must be checked against the indenture registry. Charter law."

"We're free merchants," Ilsa said.

"Then it will take no time at all." He did not look up. "Name."

"Vess. Marta Vess."

His finger traveled. Stopped. Traveled again. My heart performed some slow, terrible arithmetic—I knew those columns. I knew their rhythm, the way names clustered by intake year, the way the scribes alphabetized sloppily within each year so that *V* names scattered across three pages depending on whether they'd been entered under *Ves*, *Vess*, or *Vesse*. I had spent seven years learning this registry the way other people learned psalms.

"I don't find—" the clerk began.

"There'll be a cross-entry," Ilsa said smoothly. "Intake 1189, the year they reorganized the ledgers. Half the year's names got double-entered under the supplemental rolls."

Something flickered in the clerk's face. Not suspicion—recognition. The particular misery of a man who knows his own office's filing is a swamp. He pulled a second bundle, untied it, searched, frowned.

Tam shifted behind us with the trunk. Oren's breathing had gone very quiet, which was worse than if he'd gasped.

And then the guard by the door said, "Check the porter too. Big lad, traveling with strangers. Check everyone," and pulled open the trunk's lid without asking, and I saw the corner of Oren's spare shirt, and beneath it—no. Beneath it only shirts. Ilsa had moved the last of the incriminating thread to her own bodice hours ago. But the guard lifted out the shirt anyway, shook it, and among the folds fell a scrap of paper, a packing list in Tam's looping hand—

—and on it, half-visible, a practice line of letters. *D-u-n-m-o-r-e.* Tam practiced his letters on anything. We'd all laughed about it two nights ago around Sef's table.

The clerk looked at the scrap. Looked at Tam. And something in the room changed temperature.

"Dunmore," the clerk repeated. "That's a Dunmore?"

"It's a packing list," Ilsa said. "The boy writes our marks."

But the guard had straightened, and the clerk was already pulling a third bundle from the chest—the black-corded ones, the deceased rolls—and I felt the floor tilt beneath me, because I knew, I knew with absolute certainty, what page his finger would land on. There was only one Dunmore in the black rolls of that district intake. I had put my own finger on it once, in the dark, after hours, when I should have been asleep.

*Dunmore, J. Indentured, Meridian Saltmere. Status: deceased, per wreck report of the* Sweet Adeline. *Cross-ref: estate claim, closed.*

My father's ship. My father's false grave. My name, buried in it.

"No match for Vess," the clerk said slowly, still hunting. "No Vess in either roll. And a Dunmore reference on a porter's effects." He looked up at Ilsa, and the eagerness in his face had sharpened into something like triumph. "Madam, I'll need to hold these papers while I send to—"

He never finished.

"Yes, you won't find Vess," I heard myself say.

Every head turned. Oren's hand closed on my sleeve—too late. I stepped forward to the window, into the lantern light, and my terror did not leave me; it simply changed shape, the way water changes shape when you pour it into a vessel. It became something with edges. Something I could hold.

"You won't find her," I said again, "because you're searching the primary rolls for an intake-year name, and the 1189 reorganization didn't just double-enter—it reassigned. Anyone whose surname carried a patronymic prefix got refiled under the consolidated index by given name first. Your own office flagged the error. There's a correction slip in the front of every affected bundle—red slip, quarter sheet, stamped with the assistant registrar's mark. Did anyone give you the correction slips?"

The clerk blinked. "I—there were slips in the chest, yes, but—"

"But they weren't bound to the bundles, because whoever prepped the chest this morning followed the old binding order, which puts slips in the chest lid pocket. Check the lid pocket." I kept my voice level, bored, the voice of a person who had said these words a hundred times to a hundred floundering clerks across a countinghouse counter. Because I had. "Your Ves entries from 1189 will be under *Essa, Marta*—the intake scribe misheard the name at registration and recorded it phonetically backward, patronymic-first. It happens constantly with river-family names. You'll also want to initial the correction yourself, because if the assizes auditors come through and the slip isn't initialed by the checking clerk, it goes back on *your* desk as an unresolved discrepancy. And unresolved discrepancies during audit season"—I let that sit—"go to the regional supervisor. Not you."

Silence. The lantern hissed. Somewhere outside, the ferry bell rang once, impatient.

The clerk opened the chest lid pocket. Found the red slips. Found Marta's true entry, misfiled exactly as I had known—invented? No. Remembered? I would never know which—it would be, because I had spent seven years inside the mind of the Company's filing system, and I knew how it erred the way a shepherd knows how his sheep stray.

His shoulders came down. He initialed the slip. Then, obedient to the trap I had built for him, he glanced at the guard and said, "If the auditors find discrepancies, that's on us," and passed the papers back to Ilsa without another glance at Tam's scrap of paper, because the scrap was no longer evidence; it was nothing; the moment had been resolved and filed.

"Safe crossing," he said, already reaching for the next traveler.

* * *

We walked onto the ferry barge on legs made of borrowed timber. The deckhand cast off the line, the pewter river took us, and Saltmere shrank behind us into smoke and gull-cries.

Only when the toll station was a brown smudge did Oren turn to me. He looked at me the way I imagined a man looks at a lantern he'd mistaken for a stone.

"How," he said. "Seven years scrubbing floors, and you talk like the Registrar General."

"I told you," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, and pride came pouring through the crack like light through split wood, terrifying and warm. "I know the ledgers better than anyone alive. They taught me every day I served. Every rule, every error, every place they bury people." I laughed—actually laughed, high and unsteady. "They built the weapon themselves. They just never imagined I'd pick it up."

Tam was staring at me with his mouth open. Ilsa squeezed my shoulder once, hard, which from her was a standing ovation.

Oren opened his mouth to answer—and stopped. His gaze had gone past me, upriver, to the far bank, where a Company patrol skiff had rounded the bend under full oar, its wake cutting white, its ensign snapping, running—not toward the toll station.

Toward the ferry.

Toward us.
