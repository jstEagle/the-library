## 7. Second Checkpoint: The Burning Barge

The river narrowed at Vell's Bend, and with it, Oren's hope.

He had told himself all morning that the water would be kind. That after the toll clerk at the first checkpoint—after Juniper stood there with terror sweating through their borrowed coat and talked a grown man into doubting his own filing system—the river owed them something. But rivers don't keep accounts. Only companies do, and the Meridian Trading Company kept theirs in blood and tar smoke.

Oren smelled it before he saw it. Tar and burnt hemp, riding downriver on a wind that had turned lazy in the afternoon heat. He was crouched at the stern of Sef's oyster boat, pretending to mend a net he had no skill for, when the bend opened up ahead of them and showed him what the smell belonged to.

A barge. A long, low barge crowded with divers—families, by the look of them, bundles of bedding and drying nets strung like laundry between poles. And moored against its flank, gunwale to gunwale, a Company patrol cutter with its lanterns already lit though the sun hadn't set, the way soldiers light lanterns early: not for seeing, but for being seen.

They had set fire to the barge's stern.

Not all of it. Not enough to kill, not yet. Just the piled nets and the tarred rail, burning slow and dirty, while the patrol's men walked the deck with buckets—not water, oil—and the divers pressed themselves against the bow rail, thirty souls maybe, watching their world catch flame one armful at a time.

"Down," Sef said. She said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. Her hand found Oren's shoulder and pushed, gentle as a closing door. "All of you. Down behind the crates."

Oren went down. It was instinct older than his service, older than his guilt. He crouched among the oyster crates with Ilsa beside him, her scribe's fingers white on the gunwale, and Juniper pressed flat against his other side, close enough that he could feel their breath going quick and shallow.

Through the gap between crates he watched.

* * *

The man running the patrol wore a Company sash and the particular patience of someone being paid by the hour. He did not scream. That was what made it terrible. He strolled the burning deck of the barge as if inspecting cargo, lifting a scorched net, dropping it, nodding to his men to feed more fuel to the fire. A woman near the bow—a diver, her hands scarred white from years of saltwater, the way Sef's were—stepped forward once, and two patrolmen simply turned to look at her, unhurried, and she stepped back.

"They're making an example," Ilsa whispered. "This is what they do before an audit. Burn a barge where every boat on the river can see it. Remind everyone what the ledgers cost."

"I know what they do," Oren said. His voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's.

He knew because he had been the man walking the deck. Not this deck, not this fire—but others. In his first years under Captain Dunmore, before Elias had begun to ask questions, before the wreck that wasn't a wreck, Oren had stood watch on patrol cutters while villages along the coast learned what Company law weighed. He had told himself then what he told himself now: *there is nothing you can do*. He had been twenty-two and afraid, and the telling had worked, and that was the wound under all his other wounds—that it had worked, that fear made a fine anesthetic, that he had slept.

Elias Dunmore had never slept well again after the first one. Oren remembered that now with awful clarity: the captain on the quarterdeck of the *Meridian Rose*, turning his ring around his finger, saying, *Voss, a man who watches and does nothing has still chosen.*

"Juniper." Oren kept his voice low. "Don't look."

But Juniper was already looking, and Oren understood—they could no more not look than they could stop breathing. Their father's child. Elias's eyes in a younger face, fixed on that burning stern with something that wasn't only horror. Recognition. Somewhere out there was a ledger with names in it, and some of those names were on this barge, and the fire was the Company's signature.

"We go past them," Sef murmured from the tiller. Her face was carved wood. "Slow, like any oyster boat. Eyes down. They'll wave us through or they won't. We have papers, we have cargo, we have a job to be doing. We are nobody. Nobody gets through."

The boat slid forward. The current helped; the current always helps the river carry you toward whatever's next, good or ill, without opinion.

Fifty yards. Thirty. The heat reached them first, a breath of it across the water, tasting of pitch. Oren could hear the fire now, the wet crackle of tar, and under it a sound he'd hoped not to hear: a child crying, high and exhausted, the cry of someone too tired to be properly frightened anymore.

Twenty yards.

The officer on the barge turned his head and looked directly at them.

* * *

"Papers," the officer called across the gap. Not hostile. Worse—bored. A man doing arithmetic with people.

Sef handed over the forged manifests without a tremor. Ilsa's work was beautiful; Oren had watched her age the paper with tea and fold the creases soft. The officer read. One of his men wandered to the gunwale above them, looking down into the oyster boat with the dull curiosity of a man whose day was mostly cruelty and needed variety.

Then the man looked past Oren. Past the crates.

At Juniper.

Juniper, who could not keep their eyes off the fire. Juniper, whose face was turned up and lit orange, full of a grief too large and too specific to belong to an oyster-hand. The patrolman saw it. Oren saw him see it, saw the small shift in his posture, interest sharpening into suspicion, his mouth opening—

And Oren was moving.

Later he would try to reconstruct the decision and find there was nothing to reconstruct. There was no weighing. There was a boy on the barge crying, a woman with burned-diver's hands standing helpless, and Elias's voice saying *a man who watches has still chosen*, seven years late and exactly on time. Oren came up off the deck, caught the patrolman's wrist as the man pointed, and drove him backward into the rail.

The shout went up like gulls off a fish cart.

What followed took less time than the memory of it ever would. The deck of the oyster boat became a wrestling of arms and rope; Sef swung the tiller hard and put the hull grinding against the barge's flank, whether by accident or judgment Oren never learned. He got one clear look at the officer—whose boredom had finally cracked into something alive and furious—and then a boat-hook came down and caught Oren across the shoulder and he went to his knees on boards that shuddered with the impact.

He heard Juniper scream his name. He heard Sef roaring at everyone, at God, at the river. He saw the patrolman he'd struck reaching for the whistle at his collar, and Oren did the only thing left in his body's account: he grabbed the man's boot and hauled, and both of them went half over the rail, and somewhere in the tangle the whistle blew anyway—one long shriek that went skimming downriver like a knife thrown flat.

Then hands pulled him back. Sef's hands, iron from forty years of hauling lines. The oyster boat lurched free, the gap widening, the burning barge falling astern with its thirty souls and its patient officer, who stood at the rail watching them go and did not fire the musket his man offered him.

Because he didn't need to. The whistle had done the work. Downriver, faint but unmistakable, another whistle answered.

* * *

They ran the boat into the reeds two miles on, where the channel braided into shallows no cutter could follow, and only then did anyone speak.

"You *fool.*" Juniper was kneeling over him in the mud of the bank, pressing a wad of sailcloth to Oren's shoulder, and their voice broke apart even as it shouted. "You absolute—you had the papers, we were *through*, we were going to make it—"

"We weren't." Oren sat against a fallen trunk and let them work. The shoulder was bad—deep, ugly, the kind of wound that festered if it got the chance—but the pain arrived strangely distant, as if it were happening to a man upstream. "He saw your face, Juniper. He saw you looking at that fire like—" He stopped.

"Like what?"

"Like a Dunmore looks at a ledger."

Juniper's hands stilled on the cloth. In the failing light their face was unreadable, and then it wasn't—rage and gratitude fighting openly, the way weather fights over a coastline. "I could have talked our way past him. I talked a toll clerk into apologizing to *me*. You didn't even give me the chance—"

"No," Oren said. "You're right. I didn't."

That stopped them colder than agreement would have. Oren watched them wrestle with it—the fury with nowhere to put itself, because the argument was true. He had spent their chance. He had spent it buying seconds for strangers, and now there was a whistle-print on the air and patrols converging and a wounded man slowing the boat, and Juniper had every right in the world to hate him for it.

"And the people on the barge?" Juniper asked finally, quieter.

"Burned or not burned. Alive or not. We couldn't have known."

"That's not what I asked." Juniper tied off the bandage, clumsy but firm. "Would you do it again? If the boat came around the bend tomorrow morning, everything the same?"

Oren thought about lying. He thought about how much simpler the next three days would be if he said *no, child, it was weakness, it won't happen again*, and how Juniper would know it for a lie, because they were Elias's child and lies sat badly on Dunmores.

"Yes," he said.

Juniper closed their eyes. When they opened them, they were shining, and furious about the shining, and they turned away to help Ilsa drag the boat deeper into the reeds, and that was answer enough.

Sef came back from the channel edge, low-voiced. "Two whistles answered. Maybe three. They'll sweep the banks by morning." She looked at Oren's shoulder, at the blood soaking through, and her expression held no blame—which was somehow heavier. "We can't run the main channel now. We can't run anything fast. And the assizes gate is four days off, if Corvane even sits that long."

"Then we walk," Ilsa said. She had the provenance chain wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and her hands shook only a little. "There are drover roads. Old ones. I know the forgeries will hold better on foot than on water."

"Four days," Sef said, "with a bleeding man, a hunted name, and every checkpoint between here and the assizes looking twice at everyone who passes." She spat into the reeds, an old diver's benediction. "Fine. I've buried better plans in worse mud."

They moved as the dark came down, single file along a drover path only Sef could find, Oren's shoulder screaming with every step, Juniper silently matching their pace to his so he wouldn't have to ask for slowness. Behind them, far off on the black river, a light moved—a patrol lantern, sweeping the water where their boat should have been.

Ahead, three days inland, Magistrate Corvane waited with a courtroom, a conscience, and no idea yet what was coming toward him through the dark.

Oren walked, and bled, and carried Elias's letters dry against his heart, and did not regret the barge. He suspected he never would. That, too, was inheritance of a kind—the captain's voice in him now, permanent as a scar: *chosen, Voss. You chose.*

The question was what the choosing would cost the ones walking behind him.
