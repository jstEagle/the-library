## 8. The Letters We Never Sent

The patrol's torches passed below the ridge twice before we dared light even a coal.

We had gone to ground in an abandoned charcoal burner's camp, a hollow cupped into the hillside where the trees grew thick and old enough that no Company surveyor had bothered to name them. Sef said the burners had left when the Meridian bought up the timber rights, and now even the squirrels kept better ledgers than the Company did. It was meant as a joke. Nobody laughed. Oren lay propped against a fallen log with Ilsa stitching the gash along his ribs by lantern-light, his breath coming in short, disciplined pulls—the sound of a man refusing to make noise about his pain because making noise was how men got found.

Juniper had counted the stitches. Eleven so far. Counting was what they knew how to do; it steadied their hands while everything else shook.

"You should sleep," Sef murmured, settling beside them on the cold earth. She had spread her oiled coat over Juniper's shoulders without asking, the way she seemed to do everything—without asking, as if belonging were simply assumed and refusal would be rude.

"I can't."

"Then do something with your hands." From inside her coat she produced the oilcloth packet—the letters, Elias Dunmore's letters, which had traveled from a wrecked ship's locker to a smuggler's cache to Oren Voss's desperate hands and finally here, to a hillside that smelled of char and wet moss. "You've been carrying these like they're cargo," she said. "Cargo is for selling. Letters are for reading."

Juniper's throat closed. "I'm afraid of what they'll say."

"You're afraid of what they won't say." Sef's eyes, in the lantern's low glow, were the grey-green of deep water. "I've buried two husbands and a daughter. The dead who leave nothing behind are the ones who haunt you. The dead who leave words—" she pressed the packet into Juniper's palms, "—those are just family who've gone ahead."

* * *

Juniper waited until the camp settled. Oren slept at last, feverish but breathing evenly, one arm flung out as though even unconscious he meant to shield someone. Ilsa sat sharpening her pen-knife with the grim tenderness of a woman maintaining the only tool she trusted. Somewhere downslope, the river muttered over its stones, keeping its own accounts.

By the coal-glow, wrapped in Sef's coat, Juniper opened the packet.

Seven years of letters. Some water-stained, some creased from being hidden in boot-soles and false bottoms and once—Oren admitted shamefacedly—inside a hollowed-out prayer book stolen from a Company chapel. The earliest was dated three months after the wreck of the *Meridian Star*.

*My dear Juniper.*

The handwriting hit first, harder than any of the words. That looping ascender on the J, the way Elias crossed his t's like small sails catching wind. Juniper had spent seven years believing those loops were at the bottom of the sea, and here they were, alive on cheap paper, writing:

*I know what you have been told. I am sorry for it—I could not send word through any honest channel, because there are none left. The Star went down because we made her go down. Not me, little one. Never me. But I saw the manifests. I saw what the Company gained from our drowning, and I could not stay silent and alive at the same time, so I chose alive, and silence, and shame.*

*Forgive me for letting you mourn me. It was a coward's kindness. Grief ends; hope only wounds. I told myself you were safer believing me dead than waiting for a father who might never come home. I have regretted it every day since, which I suppose is its own kind of penance.*

Juniper read it three times. The words swam and reassembled. Around them the night sounds continued—owl, river, the pop of cooling coals—as if the world had not just tilted on its axis.

He had chosen to let them grieve. He had sat somewhere, alive, whole, and decided that Juniper's sorrow was safer than Juniper's waiting.

And the terrible thing—the thing Juniper would carry like a stone in the chest—was that they understood. Hadn't they done the same? Hadn't they spent seven years telling themselves the numbness was peace, that not hoping was a kind of wisdom? They had built their resignation brick by brick and called it shelter. Their father had simply built his further away.

* * *

The second letter told stories. That was all—stories, as though a man hiding from Company killers had nothing more urgent than to describe the color of the water off the Cinder Shoals, or the diver named Brissa who could hold her breath long enough to mend a net underwater, or the taste of roasted chestnuts sold outside the Saltmere customs house in autumn.

*You were six when I taught you to float on your back in the harbor shallows [the letter read]. You screamed the entire time and then refused to stop floating when I said we were done. I think of that often. Stubbornness is a Dunmore inheritance; I only hope you spend yours better than I have spent mine.*

Juniper laughed—a broken, startled sound—and clapped a hand over their mouth. Across the camp, Ilsa glanced up, said nothing, returned to her knife.

But the stories kept coming, letter after letter, year after year. A man on the run, gathering proof that the Company had scuttled its own ships to seize the estates of drowned pearl-divers, pausing amid all that danger to write: *the moon over the shoals tonight looked like a coin too large for any purse*, and *Brissa's boy lost his first tooth and demanded it be buried at sea, which caused considerable theological debate among people who own nothing but boats*. And always, always, near the end, some version of: *I love you. I never stopped. If these pages reach you, know that everything I did, I did so that your name would still be yours.*

Your name would still be yours.

Juniper thought of the countinghouse floor, of scrubbing between the flagstones where spilled ink had soaked in years ago, of finding their own name misfiled under *deceased* and feeling—what had they felt? Relief, almost. A dead person owed nothing. A dead person's history belonged to other people.

They had mistaken surrender for peace. Their father had mistaken protection for absence. Between those two mistakes lay seven unanswerable years, and sitting in the dark with the letters spread across their knees like a fan of fallen leaves, Juniper finally wept—for the birthdays he'd missed, for the mourning that had been unnecessary, for the child who had floated in the harbor shallows screaming and stubborn, for the stranger they'd become who no longer knew how to scream at anything.

Sef appeared silently with a tin cup of chicory, pressed it into their hands, and disappeared again. Found family required no ceremony. It only required presence.

* * *

Near dawn, when the sky above the trees had gone the grey of oyster shell, Juniper took out the stub of pencil Ilsa kept for marking forgeries and a sheet of wrapping paper, and wrote a reply.

*Papa.*

The word looked strange, enormous, like a ship in a bottle. They had not written it in seven years. Their hand shook, then steadied, then shook again.

*I got your letters. All of them. Oren Voss brought them—he was half-drowned and full of guilt and I think you would like him, or maybe you already do, since you seem to collect loyal people the way other men collect debts.*

*I used to believe history belonged to other people. People with estates and surnames and marble markers. I scrubbed floors in your murderers' countinghouse and told myself it was peace. It wasn't. It was just grief wearing a clean apron.*

*I am angry at you. I want you to know that, because I think you already are angry at yourself, and one of us ought to get to be angry out loud. You let me mourn you. I let myself disappear. Neither of us gets to pretend anymore.*

*But Papa—I read every story. The chestnuts. The moon like a coin. Brissa's boy and the tooth. I know the color of the water off the Cinder Shoals now without ever having seen it, and I know you loved me in postscripts for seven years, and I have decided that counts. It has to count. Inheritance isn't houses or pearls or even names. It's this. It's memory carried forward by whoever agrees to carry it. I agree. I'm carrying it.*

*Come find me. Or don't, if it keeps you safe—but if you can't come, then keep writing, because I will keep reading until my hands give out. Your stubbornness was well spent after all. It floated.*

*—J*

They read it over twice. Then, instead of sealing it, Juniper folded it into the oilcloth packet with the others—letter and reply traveling together now, the conversation continuing in both directions whether or not it ever reached its destination. Some letters were prayers. You sent them anyway.

The lantern guttered. Downslope, the river kept muttering its endless arithmetic, and Juniper sat with the packet against their chest like something with a heartbeat, watching the sky lighten over the ridge.

It was Sef who broke the quiet, rising from the treeline with salt spray still on her boots and her face gone the color of ash.

"The tide brought news upriver," she said quietly. "Company riders at the assizes town since midnight. Corvane's clerks turned away at the gate—lawyers got there first." Her jaw tightened. "They're saying a Dunmore heir is wanted for escaping indenture. They're describing you, child. To the magistrate himself."

Juniper stood, the letters heavy against their ribs, heavier than any ledger.

"Then we'd best arrive," they said, "before my reputation does."
