## 9. Third Checkpoint: The Assizes Gate

The assizes town of Corvane's Hollow announced itself before we could see it: a smell of cut hay and horse dung and, beneath it, the iron tang of a garrison. Oren Voss had smelled that combination in a dozen ports across a dozen years, and it always meant the same thing—soldiers, and men who paid them, and paper that decided who lived free and who did not.

He stood at the treeline above the river road with Sef's oyster boat tied up behind him in the reeds and watched the town wake. Smoke climbed from a hundred chimneys into a sky the color of unpolished pewter. And there, on the far bank where the river bent toward the assizes hall, he saw what he had been afraid to see since Saltmere: Company colors. Not one banner. Six.

"They're already here," Ilsa said beside him. She had not slept. None of them had slept since the burning barge, since Oren's arm had been opened by a patrolman's hook and bound with sailcloth and Juniper's fury. His shoulder throbbed with his heartbeat now, a dull drum keeping time with his fear.

Sef spat into the reeds. "Of course they're here. A magistrate doesn't summon an audit without telling the Company first. Half these magistrates *are* the Company, retired." She squinted across the water, her face brown and creased as driftwood. "Question is whether Corvane is the other half."

"Half a chance," Ilsa murmured. "I've gambled ledgers on worse odds."

Oren looked for Juniper and found them standing apart, down the bank where the mist still clung to the grass, holding the oilcloth packet against their chest the way a diver holds a last breath. Seven days on the river had changed them. The stoop was gone from their shoulders—or rather, it had transformed; they carried themselves now like someone bearing something precious instead of something shameful. But their eyes when they turned were still a child's eyes under all that new iron.

"You should stay with the boat," Oren said. It came out wrong, too much like an order, and he hated himself for it even as he said it. "Juniper—"

"No." Quiet. Absolute. The same word they had used at every checkpoint, every crossroads, every moment in seven days when Oren had tried to stand between them and the danger. He was learning that no from Juniper Dunmore weighed more than any argument he could stack against it.

"The lawyers know your face," he said anyway. "They'll have descriptions. *Escaped indenture.* They'll have papers on you, warrants—"

"And you're a forger," Juniper said. "And Ilsa is a disgraced scribe, and Sef is a smuggler, and every diver at that table is a debt-thief by Company law. We're all of us criminals the moment we set foot in that town. The only difference is what we're carrying." They touched the packet. "I'm the last living Dunmore. If anyone walks through that door to testify, it has to be me. You told me yourself—Oren, you *told* me—the letters only matter if the blood that answers to them is standing in the room."

He had said that. Three nights ago, feverish, arm burning, trying to convince himself as much as the child. He had not expected to be held to it.

* * *

They spent the morning preparing the way soldiers prepare—not cleaning weapons but cleaning stories, because stories were the weapons this day would use. Ilsa sat cross-legged in the bottom of the boat with her scribe's kit open like a surgeon's tray, walking them through the provenance chain one final time.

"A ledger page proves nothing," she said, tapping the oilcloth. "A ledger page is ink. Any clerk can forge ink; I would know. What convicts is the *chain*—who held the pages, who witnessed them, how each hand passed to the next without a gap the Company's lawyers can drive through." She laid out her documents like cards. "Here: the divers' marks, sworn before witnesses at Sef's table—seven names, seven seals, dated the night of the supper. Here: my own attestation, with my old Company cipher, which will cost me whatever is left of my name. Here: Oren Voss, service record under Captain Elias Dunmore, *Meridian Trading Company, third fleet*—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes, some old grief of her own—"*declared drowned, seven years past.*"

"That line again," Oren said softly.

"That line again. It's the hinge of everything. You are legally dead, Oren. Which means either the Company's shipwreck records are false—and then so are the wreck records that seized the divers' estates—or *you* don't exist, and neither does your testimony. They cannot have both. That contradiction is the door, and Juniper is going to walk through it while the lawyers are busy arguing about which lie to defend."

"And if Corvane is bought?" Sef asked flatly.

Ilsa gathered her cards. "Then we lose beautifully, and history at least learns our names before it buries us."

Nobody laughed. But nobody argued, either, and Oren understood that this was the family's whole method: not hope exactly, but the stubborn refusal to let fear make the decision.

It was Sef who spoke the plan, and she spoke it looking at the water, not at them, the way she gave orders that cost people something. "The lawyers will have guards on the assizes gate and eyes on the river landing. So we give the eyes something to look at. My boat, my crew, me—we run the landing loud at midday. Cargo dispute, shouting, maybe a little fire if Brannick's feeling generous. Every guard on that quay turns to watch poor drowning Sef argue about oysters." She finally looked up, and her eyes found Juniper's. "You three go in overland, through the tannery lane, plain as pilgrims. You do not stop. You do not look back. Whatever happens at that dock—you do *not* come back for us."

"Sef—" Oren began.

"Don't you *Sef* me, boy. I'm seventy-one years old. I've buried a husband, two sons, and every dream I had before I was thirty. Everything left in me is spendable." She smiled then, quick and crooked as a fishhook. "Besides, I want to watch the Company's faces when their audit drowns in oysters."

* * *

Midday came gray and windless, the kind of light that makes everything look already remembered. Oren stood in the tannery lane with Juniper and Ilsa, close enough to the river landing to hear it, far enough to be invisible among the curing racks and the reek of lime. His arm screamed. His heart screamed louder.

At noon exactly, Sef's boat came around the bend flying every scrap of colored cloth she owned.

What followed, Oren would tell for the rest of his life, and never quite capture. Sef herself standing at the prow like a figurehead from some older, angrier religion, voice pitched to carry across three parishes, accusing a Company cargo-master of shorting her weigh-bill by forty pounds of shell. Brannick and the divers swarming the dock, oyster baskets overturning, pearls—their seed-pearls, their savings, their children's futures—rolling glittering across the cobbles for anyone to steal. And then smoke, a small orange bloom from the stern where Brannick's "accident" with a lantern had gone better than planned, and the garrison bell beginning to ring, and six Company banners' worth of guards peeling away from the assizes gate like scabs off a wound.

Every eye on the river. Every boot on the dock.

"Now," Ilsa breathed.

They went. Down the tannery lane, through a yard hung with hides that brushed their shoulders like dead men's hands, out into the assizes square. The gate stood ahead—a black arch in pale stone, two remaining guards, a queue of petitioners, and posted on a board beside the arch, fresh-inked notices that made Oren's stomach drop:

*WANTED FOR FORGERY AND FRAUD: ONE OREN VOSS.*
*ESCAPED INDENTURE: PERSON KNOWN AS JUNIPER, LATE OF THE MERIDIAN COUNTINGHOUSE.*

His own face, rendered badly in woodcut, stared back at him.

"They described Juniper," he whispered. "How—"

"The countinghouse clerk," Ilsa said grimly. "First checkpoint. Someone talked after all." She squared her shoulders. "New plan. Same plan, worse odds. I take the provenance chain to the clerks' entrance—I'm known here, I served this circuit five years, and shame travels faster than warrants. While they digest *me*, you two go through the front."

"You can't—"

"Ilsa Vane, disgraced scribe of the Meridian Company, presenting herself for judgment." She almost smiled. "Let them arrest the chain of custody. It makes the chain more interesting, not less. Every hour I sit in their cells is an hour the papers can't be called forged without explaining why the forger surrendered voluntarily." She pressed the oilcloth packet's twin—her certified copies—into Juniper's hands, then gripped Oren's good shoulder once, hard, which from Ilsa was a sonnet. "Walk through that door. Both of you. Don't you dare be interesting."

And she went, straight-backed as a mast, toward the clerks' entrance, where the guards took her by both arms within ten steps and she did not resist, did not cry out, only lifted her chin as though arriving exactly where she had always intended to arrive.

* * *

The queue at the gate moved like syrup. Oren kept his hood up, his bandaged arm hidden under a borrowed coat, Juniper's shoulder against his—the two of them a single hunched pilgrim, or that was the hope. Ahead, the guards checked faces against a board of woodcuts, lazy with heat and half their number gone to Sef's fire.

Three petitioners. Two. One.

The guard looked up. Young, sunburned, bored. His eyes slid past the hood, snagged on the woodcut board, slid back.

"Papers."

Juniper stepped forward before Oren could move, and pulled back the hood, and Oren's heart stopped dead in his chest.

"I'm expected," Juniper said, clear as a bell across water. "By Magistrate Corvane personally. Tell him the last living Dunmore is here about the shipwreck records—and that his afternoon just got very long."

The guard blinked. Behind him, from the direction of the river, the bell was still ringing, and somewhere a woman's voice—seventy-one years old and utterly unafraid—was still giving the Meridian Trading Company holy hell about forty pounds of shell.

The guard hesitated. Looked at the woodcut. Looked at Juniper's steady, terrible calm.

And stepped aside.
