## 10. Ledger and Lantern

The assizes hall smelled of cold stone and older ink, the way a church smells of candle smoke—history soaked into the walls until it had become part of them. Juniper stood in the witness box with their hands folded to keep them from shaking, and looked out at Magistrate Corvane: a narrow woman with grey threaded through her dark braid and eyes like the inside of an oyster shell, pearl-grey and patient.

Behind her, along one wall, sat six Company clerks in matching grey coats, their ledgers stacked before them like ammunition. One of them, a young man with ink-stained fingers, kept glancing at the door as though hoping the building might simply fall down and spare them all.

"State your name," Corvane said.

Juniper's throat was dry. Nine days of checkpoints and river fog and Oren's blood soaking through a borrowed coat—and now it came down to this, a name spoken aloud in a room full of strangers.

"Juniper Dunmore."

The word *Dunmore* moved through the clerks' bench like a draft under a door. Papers rustled. Someone whispered. Corvane raised one hand and the room went still.

"The last living Dunmore," she said. "That is what your advocate claims. The Company claims otherwise." She nodded toward the clerks. "Their records list Elias Dunmore as deceased seven years past, lost with the *Meridian Grace*. They list his child—" she consulted a page, "—as deceased also. Filed four years ago. Yet here you stand."

"I stand," Juniper said.

It was Ilsa who had coached them through the night before, sitting cross-legged on the floor of Sef's boat with her forgery kit spread around her like a surgeon's tools. *"Don't perform for them. Don't weep. Weep later. You are not asking for mercy—you are reading from a record they wrote themselves. Let their own hand convict them."*

So Juniper did not weep. They reached into the oilcloth satchel at their feet and drew out the ledger pages, wrapped now in sailcloth that still smelled faintly of brine and oyster shells, and unwrapped them on the rail of the witness box.

"These were recovered from the wreck site of the *Meridian Grace*," they said. "And from the countinghouse of Captain Elias Dunmore, my father. I would like to read from them."

A clerk stood. "Magistrate, those documents have not been authenticated—"

"They will be authenticated by the only means available to either of us," Corvane said mildly. "By comparison against your own records. Proceed, Dunmore."

Juniper opened the ledger to the first marked page. The lantern above the witness box threw warm light across the columns, and for a moment the numbers seemed to swim—the way numbers had always swum for them, back in the countinghouse, back when every figure was a weight chained to their ankles.

Then they began to read.

"*Meridian Grace*, cargo manifest, final voyage. Pearl stock, forty-one casks. Insured value, entered twice. Hull insurance, entered twice. Salvage claim filed by Meridian Trading Company, dated eleven days after the reported sinking." They paused. "Eleven days, Magistrate. Before any search vessel was dispatched. Before the divers who survived had even been questioned. The Company claimed salvage on its own ship while the bodies were still washing ashore."

The clerk who had objected was writing very fast. Corvane watched him do it, and something almost like a smile touched her mouth.

"Continue."

Juniper continued. Page after page. Estate seizures listed as *administrative windfall*. Divers' homes transferred to Company holdings within weeks of each man's drowning. A second ledger, hidden, that recorded what the first one concealed: payments to a harbormaster, to a shipwright who had weakened the *Grace*'s hull plates, to men whose names appeared nowhere else except in Elias Dunmore's careful, furious hand.

Reading those names aloud was strange. Some of them belonged to people standing in this room or ones like it—sons and daughters of the drowned, grown now, scattered through Saltmere like seeds from a split pod. Juniper read each name slowly, giving it its full weight, the way you lower something heavy into deep water so it does not splash.

When they reached the final page, their voice caught—not from grief, but from recognition. Their father's handwriting changed here. The neat merchant's script loosened, hurried, as though he had written it knowing time was short.

"*If I am found dead before this reaches a magistrate, let whoever finds it know: the sea did not take these people. We did. And a ledger is a kind of lantern—it shows what was done in the dark.*"

Silence held the hall for three long breaths.

Then the senior clerk rose, smoothing his coat, and began to speak about forgery, about a disgraced scribe named Ilsa Venn, about an escaped indentured worker with no legal standing whatsoever—and Corvane listened with her chin propped on her hand, and then said, "Bring me the Company's original indenture rolls. All of them. Now."

The rolls arrived under seal. Ilsa herself carried them, pale and steady, having volunteered for exactly this moment: she broke the seal publicly, before the whole court, so that no one could accuse her of tampering. She turned to the page for the *Meridian Grace* survivors. Turned to the page for Elias Dunmore. Turned, at last, to the misfiled entry Juniper had found on their knees scrubbing floors—a child's name typed into a column headed *deceased*, in the wrong year, in the wrong hand.

"This filing error," Ilsa said, "was made four years after the alleged death. Deceased persons are not re-filed, Magistrate. They are struck through once and closed. This entry was *created*. Someone added Juniper Dunmore to the dead to close out the estate permanently."

Corvane looked at Juniper over the rim of her spectacles.

"You worked in the Company countinghouse."

"Seven years, Magistrate. Floors first. Ledgers after."

"And do you swear, as the last living Dunmore, that these pages are your father's?"

Juniper thought of the letters in Oren's coat. Of the loops of the *E*, the way their father crossed his sevens. Of a voice they could barely remember saying *you'll have my hands, little sprout, whether you want them or not.*

"I swear it," they said. "His hand taught mine. I would know it blindfolded."

The gavel came down at midday. Not with thunder—with the quiet, terrible precision of a stamp pressed into wax. The audit collapsed; Corvane dissolved it on the spot, calling it *a concealment conducted in plain sight*. Estates seized under false wreck records were ordered restored to surviving kin, with interest reckoned from the date of seizure. Warrants were issued for the harbormaster, the shipwright, and three Company officers whose names Juniper had read aloud an hour earlier. The clerks gathered their grey ledgers and left the hall like men carrying sand away from a tide.

Outside, Saltmere was loud with ordinary evening—gulls, fish-carts, a bell buoy clanking in the swell. Sef stood waiting at the assizes steps with half the divers' quarter behind her, and when Juniper came down she did not cheer or clap. She simply took their face in both weathered hands, looked at them for a long moment, and said, "You look like him when he was angry. It's a good look on you."

Oren leaned against a post, arm still bound, and grinned like a man twenty years younger. "Your father argued with me about everything," he said. "But he'd have agreed with me on this: you never needed finding, sprout. You just needed handing the pen."

They ate that night at the divers' table—the real one, above ground now, lamps lit openly because there was nothing left to hide from. Ilsa drank too much wine and cried about a man none of them knew. Sef sang, badly, a song about the sea being a thief with excellent manners. Juniper laughed until their ribs hurt, and somewhere in the laughing understood that this was the inheritance: not land, not coin, but a table that made room for them without ceremony, and a history they finally carried instead of fled.

Later, alone, they walked down to the harbor with a lantern.

The water lay black and glassy, holding the lights of the town upside down. Juniper lit the wick, cupped the flame against the wind, and set the lantern on the seawall where the current could see it.

"For the record," they said quietly, to the dark water, to a man maybe alive and maybe not, "the estate is restored. The last Dunmore testified. You'd have argued with half of what I said." A breath. "I hope you're somewhere arguing with someone right now. I'll leave the lantern lit either way."

The flame steadied. Far out past the breakwater, where the channel markers blinked, something moved—a shape low against the water, a small boat running dark toward the harbor mouth, unhurried, unlit, as if its pilot knew these waters the way a hand knows its own lines.

Juniper watched it come, heart hammering a rhythm they hadn't felt since a stranger laid letters on a countinghouse floor, and did not move from the lantern's light.

The boat turned toward the seawall.
