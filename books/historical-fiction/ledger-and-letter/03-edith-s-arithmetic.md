## 3. Edith's Arithmetic

Edith Marlowe had never been a woman who flinched from numbers. She had spent fifteen years doing sums the newspapers refused to do: what a woman earned against what a man earned for the same hour at the same loom; how many arrests it took to fill Holloway's women's wing; how many column inches a chained suffragist bought compared to a dead one. She kept her accounts in a green notebook with a broken clasp, and she balanced them nightly, the way other women said their prayers.

But the boy asleep in her front room was a sum she could not make come out even.

He lay curled on the settee beneath Ansel's own coat, which the driver had draped over him without being asked—a small tenderness that told her more about Ansel Pemberton than six years of employment had. The boy's boots were soaked through and had left two dark crescents on her Turkish rug. His breathing had the ragged, whistling quality of a child who had run a long way in cold rain, or been carried. His face, even in sleep, held the particular tension of someone listening for a door.

Edith stood in the doorway with her lamp turned low and studied him as she would study an opponent's brief: thoroughly, without sentiment, because sentiment was a luxury the movement had taught her to postpone.

The note lay open in her hand. *Forget the face. Keep the promise.* She had read it four times, and each time the handwriting struck her afresh—the upright, economical script, the crossbars ruled straight as ledger lines, the German habit of capitalising every noun with a kind of stubborn formality. She knew this handwriting. She had seen it on letters that arrived quarterly between 1908 and 1911, letters containing postal orders drawn on a Hamburg bank, letters signed:

*Karl Brandt.*

She closed her eyes briefly. Of course. Of course it would be him, of all the men whose money had passed through the movement's coffers like water through gravel.

* * *

Brandt had come to her first in the autumn of 1908, when she was still a name known mostly to policemen and to the women who queued outside Holloway with parcels. He had arrived at a Manchester meeting unannounced—a tall, stooped man with spectacles that fogged when he spoke too quickly, which was always—and he had asked questions no Englishman in that hall had thought to ask. Not *why do the women want the vote*, but *what is the arithmetic of it*. How many households did a woman govern? What fraction of national labour went unpaid? What was the interest, compounded annually, on half a nation's disenfranchisement?

He had talked about economics the way some men talked about God, and Edith had recognised a useful ally when she heard one.

"He is not sentimental," she'd told her committee afterward. "That is precisely why he is valuable."

For three years his money had arrived—never large sums, never traceable to any German organisation, always modest, always regular, the contributions of a university lecturer stretching a salary across two countries' causes. He had written that he believed the enfranchisement of women in England would be "the proof-case," the demonstration that would drag the argument out of the salons and into the parliaments of Europe. He had written that ideas did not carry passports. He had written, once, in the letter Edith remembered best, that history was not made by the men in the photographs but by "the invisible ones who carry the papers"—and she had wondered, then, whether he meant himself.

Then, in 1911, the money had stopped, and so had the letters, except for one final note explaining that his position at the university had become—his phrase—"delicate." He wished her courage. He enclosed nothing. It was the last she heard from him until tonight, when his handwriting appeared on her doorstep attached to a sleeping child and an instruction to forget.

Forget the face.

Edith looked at the face in question. Even exhausted, even rain-pale, there was something of Brandt in it—the same long brow, the same serious mouth already settling into the lines of a man who would think too much. Eight years old, perhaps. Which would place his birth squarely within the years of the correspondence. A son conceived while his father was writing cheques to English radicals.

And now it was August 1914, and Germany was a word the newspapers were setting in black type, and the boy's father had chosen her doorstep—the doorstep of the most surveilled woman in London—as the one place in England where his son might be safe.

Karl Brandt had made a catastrophic error of judgement, or a desperate act of faith, and Edith could not yet determine which.

* * *

She went to her study and opened the green notebook, because numbers were the only language in which she trusted herself at one o'clock in the morning.

*Costs,* she wrote.

One: sheltering a German national's child in the home of a woman already under police observation. She had counted the constables herself these past months—one outside the tobacconist's on Tuesdays, one who pretended to repair the same section of pavement for three weeks running. If they found the boy, the headlines would write themselves. *SUFFRAGIST HARBOURS ENEMY CHILD.* The movement had spent years climbing out of the pit the arsonists had dug for it; the Pankhursts' militants had made *law-abiding* suffragists like Edith suspect in polite drawing rooms, and one photograph of a German boy in her hallway would bury the parliamentary bill she had spent two years nursing toward its second reading. There were forty-seven signatures on that bill's supporting memorandum. She had counted those too.

Two: the informers. She had long suspected one inside her own orbit—too many meetings raided, too many routes anticipated. A child in the house was a loose thread, and loose threads were how constables found their way into parlours.

Three: Ansel. She paused over this entry, pen hovering. What did she actually know about her driver? Six years of punctuality, of silence, of doors held open and umbrellas tilted precisely. He read—she had seen the books in the coach house, philosophy mostly, borrowed from Mudie's. He listened. And tonight he had stood in her hall holding a wet child and a stranger's note, and something in his face had been—not fear. Recognition, perhaps, though recognition of what she could not say. He had looked at that scrap of paper the way scholars look at manuscripts.

There was a mystery in her driver, she realised, and she had been paying his wages for six years without once wondering what he did with the hours she didn't see.

*Assets,* she wrote next.

One: a child's life, weighed against everything. Here her pen pressed hard enough to dent the page. She had built her whole public life on the proposition that principles must be priced—that comfort, reputation, even safety were currency to be spent rather than hoarded. *Deeds, not words*, the movement cried, and meant it. Well. Here was a deed lying asleep on her settee, and every argument she had ever made from a platform demanded she shelter him.

Two: Karl Brandt himself. A man who funded causes across borders in peacetime might prove useful again, if Europe survived its present lunacy. This was the coldest entry in the book, and she wrote it anyway, because honest accounting required ugly columns.

Three: the promise. *Keep the promise.* A promise to whom? To Ansel, evidently—the note had been addressed to him, or at least meant for his eyes. Her quiet driver had promises outstanding with a German economist. The night had grown considerably stranger.

She sat back. Outside, the rain continued its patient work on the glass, and somewhere down the street a newspaper boy called the late edition—*war crisis, cabinet sits all night*—in the flat, rehearsed voice of a child selling catastrophe by the penny.

Here was the truth she had arrived at, the sum that would not resolve: whatever she decided, she decided for everyone. For the boy, for the movement, for Ansel, for the forty-seven signatures, for a man she had met four times in her life and liked enormously. That was the burden she had chosen when she chose to lead—that her conscience was not entirely her own property anymore. It belonged also to every woman who would be judged by her actions.

And yet.

And yet she thought of the last line of Brandt's final letter, the one from 1911. *Ideas do not carry passports, Mrs. Marlowe. Neither, I hope, will children.* He had written it lightly, as a pleasantry. He had written it four years before anyone dreamed of August 1914. But he had written it, and now it stood in the ledger of her memory like a debt called due.

* * *

At dawn the boy woke, and Edith was waiting with bread and butter and milk warmed on the hob, because whatever else she was, she was not cruel, and hungry children answered questions badly.

"Tomas," he said, when she asked. His English was good—schoolroom English, faintly formal, the vowels of a well-taught foreigner. "Tomas Brandt." Then he stopped, and his hands went still around the mug, and she watched him understand, with the terrible speed of children, that the name was a confession.

"And your papa sent you," Edith said gently. "To England. To this house."

"To the driver," the boy said. "Papa said the driver would know. Papa said—" He frowned, retrieving the exact words, because he was his father's son and exactness mattered to them both. "Papa said, *the quiet man keeps everything.*"

Edith set down her teapot very carefully.

The quiet man. Ansel Pemberton, who had driven her through six years of London weather without a single unnecessary sentence, who stood outside doors at rallies and apparently heard everything and said nothing, who kept—what? Everything?

When Ansel came up from the coach house an hour later, wiping his hands on his rag, Edith was standing in the hall with the boy's damp coat over her arm and her account-book expression firmly in place.

"Mr. Pemberton," she said, "we are going to have a conversation I should have had six years ago. But first—" she nodded toward the study, where the note lay on her desk beside the green notebook, its columns unfinished— "I want you to look at this and tell me what you know. All of it. Because I have done my arithmetic, Ansel, and it comes to ruin either way unless there is a figure in the equation I haven't found."

She saw it cross his face then, plain as print: the look of a man whose private ledger has just been demanded for public audit. He glanced once toward the boy's room, once at the note, and when he raised his eyes to hers they held something she had never seen in six years of service—

Fear. And beneath the fear, unmistakably, resolve.

"You'd best sit down, ma'am," Ansel Pemberton said quietly. "It's a longer story than you'll like."
