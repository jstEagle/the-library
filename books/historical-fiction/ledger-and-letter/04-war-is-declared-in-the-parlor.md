## 4. War Is Declared in the Parlor

The news came the way most terrible things come—not as thunder but as a bell, small and ordinary, rung by a paperboy whose voice had not yet broken.

Ansel heard it from the coach house, where he was mending a strap on Edith's travelling trunk with his teeth and an awl, Tomas asleep in the loft above him among the hay. *War declared,* the boy cried, running down Marlowe Lane with his satchel of papers held aloft like a trophy. *Germany refuses ultimatum—war declared!* And then the bell of St. Aldric's began to toll, and Ansel sat very still in the straw-smelling dark with the awl in his hand and felt the world tilt beneath him without moving at all.

He had known it was coming, of course. Everyone had known, in the way one knows a storm is coming—the air goes strange, the birds go quiet, the light turns the colour of old pewter. For weeks the newspapers had been full of it, and for weeks Karl's letters had grown shorter, more careful, written as though the man were choosing each word the way a man chooses footholds crossing ice. The last letter, which Ansel still carried folded against his heart like a relic, had said only: *Whatever happens between our governments, remember that I am your friend, and that friendship is not a matter for parliaments.*

Ansel had believed that. He had believed it the way he believed in the sunrise—without argument, without examination.

Now the bells were ringing and somewhere in London men were already queueing outside recruiting offices, and upstairs in Edith Marlowe's house slept a German child.

* * *

By noon the street had changed its face.

Ansel saw it first in small things: Mrs. Calloway next door taking down the postcard of Baden-Baden she'd kept propped on her parlour mantel since her honeymoon thirty years ago—he watched her carry it out wrapped in newspaper and bury it in the ash bin with a kind of furious ceremony. He saw Mr. Hobbes the greengrocer chalk over the word *German prunes* on his slate and write *Imperial prunes* instead, pressing hard enough to snap the chalk. He saw two young men in Sunday clothes stop before Edith's house and look at it for a long moment, the way dogs look at a door they have been told not to scratch, and then walk on, and Ansel stood in the shadow of the coach house doorway with his heart going like a hammer until they turned the corner.

The boy woke at half past two and came down the ladder rubbing his eyes, hair standing up like a cockatoo's crest, and asked in his careful, accented English: "Is it today? Is it the day?"

"Yes," Ansel said. "It's today."

Tomas considered this with the grave seriousness of an eight-year-old being told something enormous. "My father said you would keep me safe until it is over."

"Your father said right," Ansel answered, and was surprised at how steady his own voice came out. Inside him there was nothing steady at all. There was only the image of those two young men looking at the house, and the sound of the bells, and the memory—sudden and sickening—of every letter from Karl he had ever read in doorways, every phrase he had copied into his ledger in his small precise hand. *Correspondence with an enemy national.* He had never thought of the words before. Now they arrived fully formed, like soldiers.

That evening Edith summoned him to the parlor, and it was there, in the room where she had planned rallies and drafted speeches and argued philosophy with visitors until midnight, that England's war truly arrived at Number 14.

* * *

She stood at the window when he entered, her back to him, watching the street. She had taken off her spectacles, which she only did when something had gone badly wrong; without them her face looked younger and harder, like a portrait of herself from ten years ago.

"You've heard the wireless reports," she said. It was not a question. "And seen the street."

"I've seen it."

"There were men at the corner for an hour this afternoon. Not the constable—men. One of them wrote in a notebook." She turned. "They are making lists, Ansel. Everyone is making lists now. Grocers, vicars, landladies. Anyone with a foreign name, a foreign accent, a foreign face." Her gaze went briefly upward, toward the loft above the coach house. "A foreign child."

"They won't touch a child."

"They touched children in the East End last week. A shopkeeper's daughters, because their mother was born in Frankfurt. Threw bricks through the window while they ate their supper." Edith put her spectacles back on, and the gesture was like armour going on. "I do not say this to frighten you. I say it so that you will understand that the arithmetic has changed. Everything we do from tonight must be weighed against what it costs, and the cost has gone up since breakfast."

Ansel stood with his hands behind his back, the posture of a servant waiting for orders, and hated himself a little for how natural it felt. "What would you have me do?"

Edith crossed to the writing desk, unlocked a drawer, and took out a sheet of paper—an ordinary sheet, cream-coloured, with nothing on it—and laid it flat. "I want you to tell me everything about the father. Everything you know. Not for the police—" she caught his flinch and something moved across her face, quickly, like a fish under ice— "for myself. If I am to shelter this boy, I must know precisely what I am sheltering him from, and whom I am sheltering him against. Names, Ansel. Addresses. Dates."

And there it was, the trap he had built for himself out of six years of silence.

Because here was the thing Ansel Pemberton had never told anyone, not even the ledger: he did not know where Karl Brandt lived.

Oh, he knew it once. It had arrived in the very first letter, six years ago, in a neat hand on thin paper: *Karl Brandt, 14 Lindenstrasse, Heidelberg.* Ansel had copied it into the ledger's front page the way he copied everything, faithfully, believing himself a clerk of other people's lives. But addresses change. Men move. Karl had written from Berlin by 1911, from Munich in 1912, and each time the old address grew a little less true, a little more ghost, until—

Until tonight, when Ansel went to the coach house after the household slept, took down the ledger from its hiding place beneath the loose floorboard, and turned to the page where he had burned the truth away.

* * *

It had seemed sensible at the time. That was the unbearable part—that it had seemed *sensible*, that he could reconstruct his own reasoning step by step like a proof, and watch it arrive at disaster.

Three nights ago, before the declaration, before the bells, when the boy had first appeared on the doorstep soaked through with rain and carrying nothing but a note in his father's hand—*forget the face, keep the promise*—Ansel had done what any careful man would do. He had gathered up every letter Karl Brandt had ever sent him. Forty-one letters. Six years of a friendship conducted entirely on paper, the only friendship Ansel had ever had that asked nothing of him but honesty. And he had looked at them, all of them, lying there in a bundle tied with string, and he had understood that if anyone ever found them—a constable, a neighbour, Edith's watchers, the men with notebooks—they would find not just correspondence with an enemy but evidence, dates, names, opinions, Karl's wry remarks about the Kaiser, Karl's careful analyses of European finance, Karl's signature at the bottom of every page like a confession.

So he had burned them. In the grate of the coach house, one by one, feeding them to the flames with hands that shook only a little. He had kept the last letter—the one against his heart—because a man may keep one letter, surely, one letter proves nothing, one letter is a keepsake and not an archive. And he had told himself, watching Karl's words curl and blacken and go up the chimney into the English night, that he was protecting the boy, protecting Edith, protecting Karl himself. Prudence. Prudence dressed up as sacrifice.

But the letters had contained the address. The recent ones. *Munich, 1912. Write to me here, my friend, for I am settled now.* And Ansel, faithful clerk of everything, had never copied that one into the ledger because it had seemed too private, too much like a treasure to be filed away among receipts.

He had burned the map along with the territory.

* * *

He sat on the hay-strewn floor of the coach house past three in the morning with the ledger open on his knees and the single surviving letter unfolded before him, reading it again and again as though the address might condense out of the words if he read long enough. It would not. Karl wrote of hope and of trust; he wrote *keep him warm and keep him reading*; he wrote *I know what it costs you and I will repay it however I can, when the world allows*. He did not write where he lived. Men about to be arrested do not write return addresses.

Above him, Tomas slept on, breathing slow and even, utterly unaware that the only thread connecting him to his father had just gone up in smoke.

Ansel pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes until he saw sparks. *Stupid. Stupid, cautious, cowardly fool.* Six years of keeping records of everyone else's words, and the one record that mattered—the one that would let him find a frightened man across a continent at war—he had destroyed with his own hands, in a grate, for safety. He had spent his whole life believing that the invisible man who watched history happen was also keeping history honest. And the first time history had demanded a receipt, he had burned the books himself.

Somewhere down the lane a drunk was singing *Rule Britannia* badly, and a woman shouted at him to shut up, and the night went on being a night, indifferent.

Then Ansel heard the boy stir above him, and a small voice came down through the dark, sleep-blurred: "Herr Pemberton? Are you crying?"

"No," Ansel said, and found that it was true. What he felt was beyond tears—colder and more useful than tears. "Go back to sleep, Tomas. We leave soon. Both of us. Before anyone makes us stay."

He closed the ledger. In the margin of the final page, by candlelight, in his small precise hand, he wrote the only address left in the world that mattered: *Cornwall. The aunt. Find the aunt.*

He did not yet know her name.
