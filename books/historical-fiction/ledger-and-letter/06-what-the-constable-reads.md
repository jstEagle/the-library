## 6. What the Constable Reads

Edith Marlowe had long ago learned to measure the temperature of a government by its silences. In the years since she had first stood on a crate in Trafalgar Square and made the mistake of having opinions while female, she had catalogued those silences the way a physician catalogues symptoms: the silence of withdrawn invitations, the silence of printers who suddenly could not be reached by telephone, the silence of old friends who crossed the street rather than pass her door. Each one meant something. Each one was a pulse taken at the wrist of the state.

This August, the silences had begun to arrive in clusters.

The printer on Fetter Lane who had produced her pamphlets for eleven years—through two arrests, one libel suit, and the whole ugly business of the Cat and Mouse Act's predecessors—had sent a note that morning written in a hand so unlike his own that Edith read it twice before understanding it was an apology. *Circumstances beyond my control. Regret infinitely.* Eleven years, and he could not even sign it properly. His wife did the accounts; perhaps she had held the pen.

She sat in her study with the note flat on the blotter and let herself feel the cold arithmetic of it. The rally was set for Saturday—the eleventh, if the eleventh still belonged to them. Hyde Park had been denied them, as had Trafalgar Square, as had every public ground within the metropolitan police district, each refusal arriving on Home Office letterhead with the same bland courtesy, as though they were declining an invitation to dinner rather than forbidding assembly. So the rally would happen anyway, somewhere the maps did not quite govern: a yard behind a chapel in Bethnal Green that belonged to a dissenting congregation who had never once asked her to moderate her language. Three hundred people, perhaps four hundred if the weather held. Women with banners rolled in umbrella stands. Speakers who would need to arrive separately, unremarked, like contraband.

And now her printer had gone quiet, which meant either fear or instruction, and both amounted to the same thing.

*They know,* she thought, not for the first time that week. *They have known for some time.*

What she could not yet calculate—and Edith Marlowe, who could price a printing run, a bail bond, and a year's rent on a safe house in her head before breakfast—was *how much* they knew, and whether the knowing had yet reached the coach house at the end of her garden, where a German boy slept under a driver's blanket and dreamed, presumably, whatever children dream when their fathers have vanished into another country's suspicion.

She heard the motorcar return at half past four—the particular cough of the engine as Ansel brought it round the mews, a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat these days—and she did not go down to meet it. That was unusual for her, and she noted her own cowardice with distaste. Lately she found herself avoiding the boy's eyes at supper, not because she regretted sheltering him but because looking at Tomas Brandt was like looking at a debt she had not agreed to incur, and debts made her sharp, and sharpness frightened children, and she had already done enough frightening for one lifetime.

*You are becoming a woman who flinches,* she told herself. *That is how they win. Not with arrests. With flinching.*

She went down anyway.

* * *

The kitchen smelled of coal and onions. Mrs. Quill, who had kept house for Edith through scandal and siege and had never once asked a question she could answer by observation instead, was cutting bread with the violence of a woman who disapproved of everything edible. Tomas sat at the scrubbed table with a pencil stub and a sheet of butcher's paper, drawing what appeared to be a train, or possibly a cathedral, rendered with the total conviction of eight-year-old architecture. He had learned three English words in ten days—*please*, *bread*, and *motorcar*—and deployed them with the strategic economy of a diplomat.

Ansel stood just inside the door, hat in hand, rain still beading on his shoulders. He had that quality of standing slightly outside rooms, Edith thought, as though he were perpetually waiting to be dismissed from his own life. She had employed him four years and could count their conversations on one hand with fingers to spare, and yet there was no one whose competence she trusted more absolutely. It was a strange thing, trust without intimacy. Like trusting a lock you'd never seen opened.

"Mrs. Marlowe." He inclined his head. "There was a man on the corner by the chandler's. Twice this week. Different men, I think, but the same posture."

"The same posture," Edith repeated.

"Standing where they can see the gate without seeming to. Reading nothing. Men who stand on corners reading nothing are always reading something." He said it without drama, the way he said everything, and Edith felt the cold slide down from her collar into her spine.

"You've been watching," she said.

"I watch everything, madam. It's the work."

Yes, she thought. It was the work, and he did it so well that she had stopped noticing it was being done. A driver who saw everything and reported nothing. She had assumed his silence was discretion. She had not, until recently, wondered what else it might contain.

"Tomas." She softened her voice deliberately, switching to the slow, clear English she used with him. "Would you take your picture to Mrs. Quill? Ask her—" she glanced at the drawing, "—ask her what that building is called."

Tomas looked up. He had Karl's face around the eyes—she had recognized it the moment she'd seen him, that particular gravity in a child's gaze, the look of someone listening for information rather than affection—and he looked at Ansel first, a small reflex of checking, before gathering his paper and going to Mrs. Quill, who received him with the grudging tenderness of a woman who claimed to dislike all children equally and fed this one seconds daily.

When the kitchen door swung shut, Edith turned to Ansel.

"Now. From the beginning. And don't arrange it to spare me."

So he told her. Plainly, chronologically, without ornament: the constable—a Sergeant Doyle, known to the household by sight for two years, formerly courteous—had stopped the car Tuesday near the chapel and engaged Ansel in conversation about the state of the roads, then about the state of the nation, then about the state of Edith Marlowe's calendar. Wednesday, the same sergeant, or his twin, had been observed speaking to the chandler across the street from the mews gate. This morning, delivering a parcel, Ansel had passed the sergeant again, and the man had smiled—he had smiled, Ansel said, and the word seemed to cost him—and remarked, apropos of nothing at all:

"Funny weather we're having, for August. Keeps folk indoors. Though I see your household keeps its own company regardless. The German woman's driver, and his little shadow." A pause. "Tell her the Commissioner sends his regards."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere above them a pipe ticked as it cooled. Edith became aware that her hands were flat on the table, that she had pressed them there without deciding to, as though steadying herself against a list the ship had begun to take.

"*His little shadow,*" she said.

"Yes, madam."

"They've seen him. Seen the boy. They've watched him come and go from my garden." Her voice came out level, which surprised her; underneath the levelness something was moving that had teeth. "How long?"

"I couldn't say. They're careful. But the sergeant has been on our streets too often to be coincidence, and the chandler is a talker when money changes hands. If they've followed Tomas to the shops—if anyone has marked his accent—" Ansel stopped himself, jaw tightening. He was not a man accustomed to unfinished sentences, and the unfinished ones seemed to hurt him physically.

Edith pushed back from the table and walked to the window over the sink, the little window that looked out on the yard, the wall, the gate, the world arranged in order of threat. Rain was starting again, fine as sifted flour.

Let her think clearly. Let her do what she did best, which was arithmetic under fire.

Item: the rally, Saturday, already illegal, now attended by surveillance that had graduated from watching *her* to cataloguing her household. Item: a German national's son sleeping forty feet from her kitchen, discoverable by any constable with a warrant and a lantern. Item: her own file at Scotland Yard, thick as a Bible by now, in which the entry *harbours enemy alien* would land not as a footnote but as a headline—the woman who defended Germans while British boys were being marched to the coast. The press would do the rest. They had done it to others; she had read the headlines over other women's ruins and told herself the difference was preparation.

Item, and this was the item that sat in her chest like swallowed ice: they had not arrested anyone yet. Which meant they were not collecting evidence. They were savoring position. A cat does not pounce while the mouse is still unaware of the shape of the room. Something was being timed—to the rally, most likely, to the moment of maximum spectacle. Arrest Edith Marlowe on a platform with a banner behind her and a German child discovered in her coach house the same week, and they would not need to write a single line themselves. The story would compose itself out of her own principles, inverted and hung round her neck.

"They're going to let us assemble," she said aloud, mostly to the window. "And then they're going to make an example. All of it at once. Me, the movement, and the boy as the proof of my treason." She turned. "Unless the boy isn't here when they open the coach house door."

Ansel had gone very still. Stillness, in Ansel Pemberton, was not emptiness; she had begun lately to suspect it was density—that behind his quiet face a whole correspondence of decisions was taking place at speed, none of it shown. She had caught him, twice now, looking at Tomas with an expression she could only call ownership—not possessive, exactly, but responsible in the way of a man guarding a ledger, accountable to entries no one else had read.

"Madam," he said carefully, "where would he go?"

"That is not your burden."

"With respect, madam, it became my burden the night he arrived at your door. I carried him inside. I don't set things down after I've picked them up."

It was the longest speech she had ever heard from him. She studied him—the rain-damp shoulders, the hands that had held a wheel through four years of her campaigns without one accident, one complaint, one visible opinion—and understood, with the small shock of a woman discovering a room in her own house, that she had misjudged the architecture of this man. She had thought him furniture. He was a foundation.

"There's a sister of Karl Brandt's," she said slowly, working it aloud because saying it made it real and real things could be planned against. "In Cornwall. Penzance way, I believe—I have it in the correspondence somewhere, I'll find the exact direction tonight. A fishing family, or nearly. Far from London, far from the files, and a woman with no politics worth arresting." She met his eyes. "It will mean roads, Ansel. Roads full of men like Sergeant Doyle, asking questions in a country that has just decided to hate the sound of German."

"I know the western roads," he said. "Better than the men who patrol them, I'd think. Delivery routes. Back ways." And then, quieter, almost to himself, in a tone she wasn't meant to hear: "I've been planning it since the second night."

Edith felt something turn over in her—fury, yes, at Doyle and his masters and the whole grey machine that had taught a nation to see a child's accent as a weapon—but beneath the fury, unexpectedly, a fierce and inconvenient pride. They had watched her house for weeks and understood nothing of what lived in it. They counted banners and pamphlets. They could not count loyalty, because loyalty left no receipts.

Except, she thought, glancing at Ansel again, that perhaps this one did.

"Then here is the arithmetic," she said, crossing back to the table and laying her palms flat, the general returning. "Saturday, the rally happens, and I am on that platform whatever they intend, because if I hide, they win by forfeit. You will drive me there and bring me home—or not bring me home, if it comes to that. And before Saturday, Tomas goes west, quietly, cleanly, with no thread connecting him to me that a constable can pull." She paused, and the last of it cost her more than she showed. "And Ansel—you will not be driving me home afterward. You'll be on the road with the boy. Whatever happens Saturday, I release you from my service until it's done."

He didn't move. Then: "You're expecting to be arrested."

"I'm expecting," Edith Marlowe said, "to give them exactly the evening they've been arranging for us. The only question is whether they get the ending they wrote." She allowed herself, briefly, the smile that had appeared in the illustrated papers beside accusations of hysteria. "We shall edit."

Above them, faintly, came the sound of Tomas laughing at something Mrs. Quill had said—a bright, unguarded sound, a sound that had no country—and Edith listened to it the way one listens to a clock one knows is ticking toward something.

Outside, at the corner by the chandler's, a man stood reading nothing at all.
