## 7. A Promise Made in Peacetime

The rally was to have been at Finsbury Green, but the authorities had banned it, and so it happened anyway, which was the whole point of Edith Marlowe.

Ansel stood at the fringe of the crowd with his cap low over his eyes, the motorcar parked three streets off where no constable would think to look for it. He had argued—quietly, in his way, which meant he had said four sentences and let her hear a fifth in them—that she ought not go. That the ground was watched. That the word *sedition* had begun appearing in newspapers beside names he had read aloud to Tomas as bedtime stories, and that hers would be next if she gave them the excuse.

She had buttoned her glove and said, "Ansel, I have spent ten years teaching women that silence is not safety. It would be a poor lesson to unlearn it now."

He could not argue with arithmetic like that. He never could. It was, he suspected, why she kept him: everyone else in her orbit argued loudly and lost, and Ansel argued softly and lost too, but she heard him lose.

Now the green was thick with bodies and banners and the smell of wet wool, because it had rained again, London's August refusing to be summer. Edith stood on the platform—a cart bed, really, borrowed from a coal merchant—and her voice carried the way it always did, not loud so much as *level*, a voice that made shouting seem like panic and calm seem like strength.

"They call this speech sedition," she was saying. "I call it arithmetic. Half of England is told it may not vote. Half of Europe is being told it may not live. And we are asked to believe that the dangerous people are the ones who point this out—"

The whistle went. Not one whistle; a chorus of them, from four corners of the green at once, and the crowd folded inward like paper in a fist. Ansel moved before he decided to move, the old driver's instinct, shoulder into the press of bodies, toward the platform, but two sergeants got there first and took her arms, and she did not struggle, which was worse somehow. She looked out over the heads of the crowd—not searching for anyone, he understood later; simply looking at them, all of them, as if to say *remember this*—and then her eyes found his, found him in the crush the way she always could find him, the invisible man made suddenly the only visible thing in London.

She did not call out. She inclined her head, very slightly, the way she did when handing him the day's letters. *Your task now,* the gesture said. *Drive.*

Then she was gone between the blue coats, and the crowd roared its fury and grief, and Ansel stood in it alone, a man with nowhere left to drive.

* * *

The house on Cavendish Row felt different empty. It had always been full of other people's words—Edith's letters, her visitors, the scratch of her pen through the study door—and Ansel had been content to move among them like a servant ghost. Now the silence had teeth.

Tomas was in the coach house, sitting on an upturned crate, hands folded in his lap the way children do when they have been taught stillness is survival. Eight years old and he already knew how to make himself small. Karl's boy. Karl's handwriting in miniature, if handwriting could inherit anything but ink.

"They took her," Tomas said. Not a question. He had seen them go past the coach-house window, or heard the name shouted in the street. Children absorbed London the way walls absorbed damp.

"Yes."

"Will they take me?"

Ansel crouched down so their eyes were level. He was not good at faces, his own least of all, but he arranged his into something steady. "Not tonight. Not while I'm here."

"You're only the driver." The boy said it without cruelty—the flat recitation of something an adult had said within his hearing, probably more than once. Perhaps Karl had said it once, apologetically, in a letter: *he is only my driver, but he is the only man I trust.* Ansel had kept that sentence in the ledger. He kept most sentences.

"I'm the man who knows the roads," Ansel said. "Tonight, that's worth more than Parliament."

Tomas considered this with his father's gravity, then nodded once, and went back to being small and quiet, and Ansel went inside to burn the house clean.

* * *

It took him an hour to decide what to destroy, and when he finished he hated himself for how little hesitation some of it required.

The letters were the danger. Six years of correspondence, hidden in plain sight in the coach house, filed in a tea chest among delivery manifests because no constable searches what bores him. But the constable who had spoken to Edith's housekeeper used words like *the German woman's driver and his little shadow*, which meant someone had been watching the comings and goings, and watchers eventually searched. A German correspondent's letters, in wartime, in the possession of the arrested woman's employee—it would be a confession written in another man's hand.

He burned Karl's letters one by one in the study grate, reading each a final time as the flames took it. This was the terrible arithmetic of fire: to save the living you must kill the words. He had built his ledger on the belief that ordinary people keep history honest by keeping receipts. Now he fed history's receipts to the grate to keep one small boy breathing, and understood, with a lurch, that both things were true at once. You keep the record. You break the record. You choose, every hour, which ordinary person gets to stay ordinary.

He saved three letters. The earliest, from 1908, in which Karl had written: *We are not citizens of our governments, Ansel. We are citizens of each other.* And the one containing the promise, the strange formal vow two men had made across the North Sea in peacetime, when promises cost nothing: *If ever you are asked—if ever there is no one else—you will know what is asked of you.* And the last, postmarked July, already carrying war in its margins like weather: *Tell Tomas that borders are lines drawn by tired men on maps that will be redrawn. Tell him kindness travels better than any of us will.*

He did not burn the ledger. He could not. He wrapped it in oilcloth instead and bound it under his coat, against his chest, where a heartbeat might convince any customs of its innocence.

And then he made the mistake he would carry longer than any other: he burned the envelope with Karl's last return address on it. The camp, the town, the postal district—all of it curling into black lace in the grate before he thought to rescue it. He had been so practiced at destruction, so fluent in forgetting, that his hands did the work before his mind objected. He knelt at the grate fishing charred fragments from the coals like a man trying to un-ring a bell, and came away with ash to the elbow and nothing else.

Somewhere in Germany—or somewhere in England, in some internment pen for enemy men—an address existed that Ansel would now never write to. He had delivered ten thousand letters. He had lost the only one that mattered back.

* * *

The Marlowe motorcar sat in the mews behind the house, a 1911 Daimler, dark green, polished by six years of Ansel's own hands until it looked like respectability itself. He had driven Edith in it to suffrage meetings and salons and once, memorably, to a prison gate where she collected a hunger-striker and held the woman's hand all the way home without a word.

Stealing it was not dramatic. That was the shameful part. He simply went down to the mews at half past eleven, checked the petrol gauge by matchlight, cranked the engine the way he had cranked it five hundred times, and drove out through streets that did not care. No alarm. No witness but a cat on a wall. The grandest betrayal in Ansel Pemberton's quiet life, and it sounded exactly like an engine turning over.

Except that it was not theft, he told himself, gripping the wheel through Islington's sleeping streets. Except that it was exactly theft, and he should not soften it. Edith Marlowe had trusted him with everything she owned that could not speak, and he was taking the thing that spoke loudest, her machine, her independence, her means of arriving anywhere on her own terms. When she came out of Holloway—when, he refused to consider *if*—there would be no car waiting, no driver, no explanation except the truth: *I took your trust and spent it on a promise made to a man your government calls the enemy, and I would do it again, madam, and you would tell me to, which is the worst of it.*

That was the part that steadied his hands. She would tell him to. If she were standing in the mews tonight, she would hold his sleeve and say, *Take the car, Ansel, don't be a fool about sentiment,* because Edith's principles had never once stopped at her own property line. He was not stealing from her. He was borrowing her convictions, which she had left to him in trust along with everything else.

Tomas climbed onto the seat beside him wearing a coat two sizes too large and Edith's traveling rug over his knees, and asked, in the small voice he used for questions he feared the answers to:

"Where are we going?"

"Cornwall," Ansel said. "There's an aunt. There's always an aunt, in the end. Families are like rivers—they all run somewhere."

"Is it far?"

"Three hundred miles." He engaged the gear and the Daimler gathered itself beneath them. "We'll drive by night and hide by day. We'll be nobody going nowhere, which is the safest thing a person can be in England just now."

Tomas nodded, accepting this the way he accepted everything, with that grave economy, and after a while he said, "My papa said you'd come. Before we even came to your door. He said, *if it goes wrong, there is a man who reads everything and forgets nothing.*"

Ansel's throat closed around whatever answer he had. He drove instead, out through the northern edge of London where the lamps thinned and the road opened toward Barnet and the dark country beyond, the ledger a hard rectangle against his ribs, three letters in oilcloth under the spare seat, and behind him the whole weight of Cavendish Row going dark in the mirrors—house, cause, woman, life.

At the top of the hill he stopped once and looked back. London lay below like a banked fire, and somewhere in its middle Edith Marlowe sat in a cell writing, no doubt, arguments in her head for judges who hadn't met her yet. *Remember this,* she had told the crowd. Very well. He was the remembering kind. It was the only trade he had ever practised.

He let in the clutch, and the night took them west.
