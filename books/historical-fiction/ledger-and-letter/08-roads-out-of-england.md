## 8. Roads Out of England

They left London in the blue hour before the milk carts, when the streets belonged to cats and lamplighters and men too tired to ask questions. Ansel had chosen the small Austin over Edith's Daimler for exactly this reason: it looked like nothing. A tradesman's car, a doctor's runabout, a nobody's machine. In a city that had begun to memorize faces, anonymity was a currency, and he had spent his whole life hoarding it.

Tomas sat beside him on the folded motor blanket, so still that Ansel kept glancing sideways to confirm the boy was real. Eight years old, with his mother's dark eyes and something of Karl around the mouth—a seriousness that sat strangely on a child's face, as though he were waiting for the grown-ups to finish and explain everything.

"Are we going to my aunt?" Tomas asked. It was the first thing he'd said in an hour.

"To Cornwall. To your Aunt Marta." Ansel had found the address in Karl's last letter, pressed flat between pages of argument about tariffs. *If anything should ever require it, my brother's widow keeps a house near Falmouth—she is English, Ansel, remember that above all things. She is English, and she will know what to do.*

"Why can't we tell people where we're going?"

Ansel considered lying, then discarded it. Lies required a confidence he didn't have with children.

"Because some people are frightened just now," he said. "And frightened people do arithmetic badly."

Tomas turned this over. "My father says fear makes poor bookkeepers."

Ansel laughed—a short, startled sound—and then felt his throat tighten. Six years of letters, and here was Karl distilled into a boy's borrowed proverb. He put the car into gear and pulled out of the mews without lights.

* * *

The first checkpoint was at Hounslow, where two constables stood beside a sawhorse with a hurricane lamp guttering between them. Behind them, a sergeant in khaki—army men on roads now, three days into the war and already the country had grown spines—waved down a cart of cabbages and made the farmer open every sack.

Ansel slowed the car and did what he had practiced all night in his head: he became boring.

He was a driver, he said, taking his employer's motor west for servicing, since the garages in town were choked with mobilization. He produced papers that were genuine enough—Edith Marlowe's registration, his own license, both bearing names that would mean nothing to a constable who read newspapers for the war news and skipped the society pages. The sergeant leaned through the window. His eyes went past Ansel, past the blanket-wrapped shape beside him, and snagged on nothing at all, because there was nothing at all to snag on. A boy asleep under a rug. Servants' children traveled with servants. It was the oldest camouflage in England.

"Business?" the sergeant asked.

"My employer's," Ansel said. "I only drive it."

The sergeant smiled at that—the universal smile of a man who also only drove things, or swept them, or carried them, whose whole life was other people's business—and waved them through.

Ansel drove a mile before his hands stopped shaking. Beside him, Tomas peeked out from the rug.

"You lied very well," the boy said gravely.

"I told almost no lies at all. That's the trick of it. Say less than the truth and no one catches you."

"My father says—" Tomas began, and stopped, because his father said many things and all of them were now in a camp somewhere, or a prison, or worse, and saying them aloud might break whatever spell kept the man alive across the water. Ansel reached over and squeezed the small knee under the rug.

"He sounds like a wise man, your father. Tell me one of his sayings later. I'm collecting them."

It was true. He was collecting them. Somewhere behind his breastbone, the ledger instinct had woken fully—*record this, record this, ordinary words are the only history that doesn't lie*—and he thought: someday this boy will want every scrap of his father back, and I am carrying some of them in my head like a man carrying embers.

* * *

They made Reading by midday and Swindon by late afternoon, avoiding the main arteries where military traffic thickened. Ansel had planned the route from memory—six years of delivery runs, of driving Edith to rallies in Bristol and Plymouth, had etched the western roads into him like a map drawn in ink. Every lane he took was a lane some organizer had once taken with pamphlets hidden under the spare tire. The suffragists' smuggling routes, repurposed. Edith would have appreciated the irony. Or perhaps not; her cause was in enough trouble without lending its infrastructure to a German boy.

The fever came on outside Bath.

Tomas had been quiet all afternoon in the way that quiet becomes wrong, and when Ansel pulled under an elm to check on him, the boy's cheeks were scarlet and his eyes too bright, and when Ansel laid a hand on his forehead the heat came off him like a road in August.

"No," Tomas said, pushing weakly at the blanket. "No stopping. You said we keep going."

"And you'll keep going better after rest and water." Ansel heard Edith in his own voice and was surprised by it. All those years in the front seat, silent, and apparently the woman had been teaching him the whole time.

He found the inn by accident—or told himself it was accident—in a village whose name he never learned, a low stone building with beehives behind it and a sign showing a fox and a bell. The woman who answered his knock was sixty if she was a day, built like a gatepost, with forearms that suggested a lifetime of lifting things heavier than opinions. She looked at the car. Looked at Ansel's face. Looked at the burning child bundled in the back.

"Fever," she said. Not a question.

"Yes, ma'am."

She stepped aside. "There's a room at the top of the stairs. Don't tell me anything else, because I've got a son in France as of Tuesday and I haven't the patience to decide whether to worry about you too. Tea's at six. Broth for the lad. That's all I want to say about it."

She charged him double for the room and half price for the broth, which Ansel understood, in some deep ledger-keeping part of himself, to be the exact mathematics of mercy.

* * *

The fever peaked near midnight.

Ansel sat on the edge of the narrow bed with the candle turned low, holding a cloth he had wrung in cold water, pressing it to Tomas's forehead, his neck, the thin wrists. The boy muttered in German—fragments, a word here and there that Ansel caught and treasured shamelessly: *Papa*. Once, terribly: *don't forget the face*, though Ansel could not tell if he was quoting his own memory of that rain-soaked doorstep or dreaming something new.

"Tomas." He leaned close. "Tomas. Listen to my voice. You're safe. We're going to your aunt's. Do you hear? Falmouth. Your aunt Marta."

The boy's eyes opened, unfocused, swimming. "Where?"

"On the road. In an inn. And while you rest, I'll tell you a story. Would you like a story?"

A nod, barely.

Ansel had never told a story aloud in his life. He had written ten thousand words in a private ledger and spoken perhaps a tenth as many in six years of employment. But the fever needed drowning out, and so he opened the leather book he had carried everywhere since the boy had arrived—had carried, in truth, for years—and began to read.

"'March the third, 1911,'" he said. "'Mrs. Pankhurst spoke at Caxton Hall. A woman named Ada Wright was thrown out by two policemen and came back in by the window.'" He paused. "That's true, Tomas. That actually happened. I wrote it down the same night."

The boy blinked at the ceiling.

"There was a mill girl in Lancashire," Ansel went on, leaving the page now, letting the ledger spill out of him, "who walked eleven miles to hear a speech and walked eleven miles home and was at the loom by six the next morning. Her name was Hannah Ollerenshaw. Nobody famous. Nobody wrote about her except me, because she told me about it while I waited with the motor, and she said, 'Tell someone, will you? Tell someone it happened.' So I did. Eleven miles each way, Hannah. At the loom by six."

Tomas's breathing had changed. Slower.

"There was a baker in Camden who left loaves on the movement's step every Friday for two years and never once came inside, because his wife didn't approve and he loved her more than the cause but not more than his conscience. There was a constable—yes, a policeman, don't look shocked—who looked the other way twice and told me, 'I've got daughters.' Two words and a whole life inside them."

The candle fluttered. Outside, an owl worked the hedgerows.

"And there was a man in Germany," Ansel said softly, "an economist, who wrote letters to a stranger in England about how ordinary people are the ones who keep history honest. Kings make it, he said, but ordinary people keep the receipts." He swallowed. "That man has a son with a fever in an inn in Wiltshire, and his son is braver tonight than most soldiers, because being small and sick and far from home and staying calm anyway—that's courage with nowhere to hide."

Tomas's eyes had closed. But before sleep took him properly, he murmured, in English carefully learned: "Keep the receipts."

"I always do," Ansel said. "Go to sleep. I'll keep watch."

He sat until the fever broke in the grey hours, sweat soaking the pillow, the boy's breathing going long and even. Then Ansel stood at the window watching the dawn come up over unfamiliar hills and understood, with a clarity that frightened him more than any checkpoint, that he would not be able to give this boy up cleanly in Cornwall. That some cargo changes the ship.

* * *

The innkeeper pressed a parcel of bread and cheese into his hands at departure and refused his extra coins with a look that ended the discussion. At the gate she paused.

"The lad," she said. "He called you Papa in the night. In German."

Heat climbed Ansel's neck. "No, ma'am. He was dreaming of someone else."

"Mm." She looked at him a long moment, her face like weather. "Well. Whoever taught you to drive gentle with a scared child, you tell them they did it right." And she went back inside, asking nothing, having decided everything.

West again, through Somerset, the hedges high and green and indifferent to empires. By afternoon Tomas was sitting up, color returning, eating bread with the ferocious appetite of the recovering. They passed a column of Territorials marching toward a station, singing, their faces young and excited, and Tomas watched them out the back window until they were gone.

"They think it will be an adventure," Ansel said quietly, to no one.

"Is it?" Tomas asked. "War?"

Ansel drove in silence for a full minute. Somewhere behind them were checkpoints and a household under watch, a woman in a cell for speaking her mind, a man behind wire for the crime of his birthplace. Ahead lay Cornwall, and after that—an empty passenger seat, a ledger with blank pages, a signature he'd never used.

"It's what happens," he said finally, "when everyone stops reading each other's letters."

Tomas nodded solemnly, as though this were one of his father's proverbs, and settled back against the seat.

They crested a hill as the light went gold, and far below, where the land fell away toward a glittering line of sea, Ansel saw a crossroads ahead—and standing at it, unmistakable even at distance, a policeman. Not waving down carts. Not bored. Looking directly up the hill at the approaching motorcar, one hand raised, the other holding something white that caught the sun.

A paper. A list. A description.

Ansel's foot found the brake, and beside him Tomas whispered, "Is that for us?"
