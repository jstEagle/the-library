## 9. The Tribunal

The room they had given her was not a courtroom, though it wished it were. Edith Marlowe sat at a plain table beneath a high window and understood, with the clarity that had never once deserted her in thirty years of agitation, that the architecture itself was the verdict. No dock. No jury box. Just a table, two men from the Home Office, a magistrate with a face like a folded newspaper, and a stenographer whose fingers hovered above her keys as though afraid to touch them.

Sedition. The word had followed Edith through the rally on Hounslow Heath like a dog she had once fed. *Words calculated to prejudice His Majesty's forces.* She had said what she had always said — that the war was made by men who would not fight it, paid for by women who could not vote against it, and sold to boys who did not know they were being sold. She had said it louder than usual, because the crowd had been large and the police cordon thin, and because loudness, in August 1914, was the only honesty left.

Now she smoothed her skirt and waited. Her hands were steady. She had learned long ago that the body could be trained to lie even when the cause could not.

The senior man from the Home Office — Verrall, his card had said, though no one had offered it twice — opened a folder with the reverence of a priest handling relics.

"Mrs. Marlowe. You will have the opportunity to respond to certain statements attributed to you."

"Attributed," Edith repeated. "By whom?"

"That is not—"

"Because I find," she continued, "that attribution has become rather the whole of English justice this month. Attributed by whom, Mr. Verrall?"

He looked down at his papers. "A witness of good standing."

*Of good standing.* Edith felt the phrase land somewhere below her ribs. In six weeks she had heard it four times now — from the constable outside her gate, from the woman who ran the tobacconist's and no longer met her eye, from the letter that had come unsigned to warn her she was watched. Someone close. Someone who knew the household. She had run the arithmetic every night on the narrow cot in Holloway and then here, in this borrowed room, and the sum always came out the same: there were only three people who could have told them about the motorcar's movements, the letters, the little shadows.

And one of them had vanished into the night with her Daimler and a German child, and had not sent word.

She did not blame him. That was the strange thing, the thing she kept having to explain to herself in the dark. Ansel Pemberton had driven her for eleven years and spoken perhaps four hundred words aloud in all that time, and she had thought him furniture — loyal, well-made furniture. Then a boy had appeared on her step in the rain, and the furniture had looked at the note in its hand and become a man with a face like a locked door opening.

She had let him decide. She still did not know whether that had been principle or cowardice dressed in principle's coat.

"Mrs. Marlowe?" Verrall again.

"I am listening," she said. "I am always listening. It is my profession."

* * *

Forty miles west of London, past Reading where a constable had waved them through because the motorcar bore a suffragist's plates and no one imagined a traitor would steal from a traitor, past Salisbury where Tomas had slept against Ansel's shoulder in the inn's kitchen, the roads gave out into lanes and the lanes gave out into hedges and the hedges finally gave way to sea.

Ansel had not slept properly in nine days. The boy's fever had broken at an inn near Crewkerne kept by a woman named Mrs. Tregenna who asked no questions and charged fairly and put cod-liver oil in everything; she had read the ledger aloud herself, one night, when Ansel's voice failed — the entries about the mill girls of 1908, the strike fund letters, Karl Brandt's own words copied out in Ansel's careful hand — and Tomas had listened as though hearing his father's voice come back through another man's throat.

Now the boy sat upright beside him on the seat, washed and feverless, watching the hedgerows flick past, and Ansel watched the road and thought about tribunals he would never attend.

He had left a letter under Edith's cell door's outer grate — or rather, he had bribed a wardress with two pounds and the truth, which was more persuasive — telling her only: *If they ask who knows your character, wait until the second day. I will come. I keep promises slowly but I keep them.*

He had written it in the ledger's own hand, so she would know it was true.

The aunt's cottage stood where Karl's last peacetime letter had described it: grey stone, a blue door, a garden gone wild with late roses, the sea beyond it flat and pewter-coloured under a sky the colour of dishwater. A woman came out wiping flour from her hands before the motorcar had fully stopped, as though she had been waiting at that window for nine days, which Ansel suspected she had.

Tomas got down before Ansel could open the door for him. He had insisted on doing doors himself since Salisbury. The woman — Frau Reinhardt, though the village called her Mrs. Renwick now, and Ansel saw her flinch at nothing and understand everything — knelt in the gravel and held out her arms, and Tomas walked into them the way children walk into weather, without ceremony, entirely.

Then he turned back and looked up at Ansel. "You'll write it down," he said. It was not a question. "What happened. You write everything down."

"Yes," Ansel said.

"And Papa will know?"

"Papa will know."

The boy nodded once, gravely, an eight-year-old closing an account, and went inside with his aunt, and the blue door closed, and Ansel Pemberton stood alone in a stranger's garden with the engine ticking and the sea breathing behind the wall, and allowed himself, for exactly one minute by his pocket watch, to lean against the warm bonnet and shake.

Then he wound the watch, checked the fuel, and turned the car back toward London, because tomorrow morning was the second day, and somewhere in a room that was not a courtroom, a woman who had let him decide was waiting to learn whether she had decided rightly.

* * *

The second day of the tribunal began badly, which Edith noted with professional interest, as bad beginnings were usually followed either by worse middles or by miracles.

Verrall produced his witness of good standing at eleven o'clock. He was a slight man with a moustache like a smudged signature — Mr. Cyril Fenn, lately secretary of the West London branch, lately keeper of her correspondence files. Edith looked at him for a long moment and felt something worse than anger, which was recognition. She had seen that particular servility before, in men who had wanted to be important to a movement and found themselves only useful to it.

Fenn testified to the speeches. Fenn testified to the letters. Fenn testified — and here Edith's hands, trained to steadiness, tightened once upon the table — to *the German connection*, to funds received years ago from foreign sympathizers, to a driver, to a coach house, to *a child of enemy blood sheltered while English mothers wept.*

"He kept records," Fenn said, and his voice took on a wet eagerness. "The driver. Obsessively. Everything she said, everything that passed through the house. I've seen his book."

"You've seen his book," the magistrate repeated.

"Everyone in the household knew of it, sir. He was — well. Not a servant one spoke to."

Edith almost laughed. Eleven years of not speaking to him, and this man had understood Ansel perfectly after all.

It was at half past eleven, while Verrall was establishing that the funds had been *significant in aggregate*, that the door at the back of the room opened without knocking, and a man came in whom half the officials present had seen a hundred times and never once looked at.

He wore his driving coat still, travel-stained, smelling faintly of petrol and salt air. He carried a book bound in green cloth, fat and swollen with inserted papers, tied with a bootlace because its original clasp had broken sometime around 1909. He walked to the front of the room as though walking to a car door, stopped, and waited to be recognized, because he was Ansel Pemberton and he did not presume.

"Name yourself," said the magistrate, unsettled.

"Ansel Pemberton. For eleven years employed as driver to Mrs. Marlowe." A pause. "Keeper of the record Mr. Fenn has mentioned."

Verrall leaned forward. "Mr. Pemberton. Your employer's household is under investigation. Your testimony regarding the German child—"

"My testimony," Ansel said, and his voice, so rarely used, filled the room the way cold water fills a glass, "is not about the child."

He untied the bootlace. He opened the book. And Edith Marlowe, who had prepared for martyrdom, for prison, for the slow ruin of her life's work, watched her invisible man become visible.

He read to them. That was the only word for it. He read from March 1907, a speech she had given to forty women in a draughted hall in Croydon, transcribed the same night in a doorway with frozen fingers: *"We are not asking to be trusted with the vote. We are informing you that trust is no longer required."* From 1909, a letter she had dictated to a striking laundress too sick to hold a pen. From 1911, the exact wording of a promise she had made to a widow in Poplar, and — this was the hinge of it, Edith saw the magistrate sit forward — the date on which the promise had been kept, and by how much, and witnessed by whom.

Page after page. Years of it. Speeches and letters and debts paid and debts forgiven, the whole unglamorous ledger of a movement that history would remember only through its martyrs, recorded faithfully by a man in a doorway whom nobody had ever thought to arrest.

"You have documented," the magistrate said slowly, "every public statement my witness has characterized as seditious. In full."

"In full context, sir. Context being the part that gets lost." Ansel turned a page. "Mr. Fenn testified to seeing this book. I'd like the record to show what else Mr. Fenn testified to knowing." He looked at Fenn then, and there was nothing cruel in it, which was somehow worse. "On the fourth of August, Mr. Fenn removed three letters from Mrs. Marlowe's correspondence tray and delivered them to Inspector Hale at Scotland Yard. Letters from a German economist, Karl Brandt, written in 1912 and 1913, opposing the war before there was a war to oppose — letters that would have helped her case. Mr. Fenn kept the ones that hurt and burned the ones that didn't." He glanced at the stenographer. "There are copies. There are always copies. That is rather the point of a ledger."

The silence afterward lasted long enough for Edith to hear the clock in the corridor, and the sea, absurdly, in her memory, forty miles away.

Fenn was escorted out at noon, protesting in a voice that rose and thinned like a kettle taken off its heat. Verrall's folder stayed closed. The magistrate adjourned for two hours and returned to dismiss the charge with language so dry Edith would treasure it for years — *no evidence of disloyalty, considerable evidence of the contrary* — and it was done, and the room emptied, and she was free in the ordinary terrible way that people are free.

She found Ansel outside on the steps, standing beside the Daimler as though he had never left it, holding his hat.

"You stole my car," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"And burned my correspondence."

"Some of it. To protect your correspondent's son."

"And disappeared for nine days without a word except a note in your own handwriting, which I confess frightened me more than Holloway did." She stopped before him. The afternoon light caught the green book under his arm. "Why did you come today? You were safe. The boy is safe. You owed me nothing but the driving."

Ansel Pemberton looked at her, and for the first time in eleven years she saw him decide to speak plainly.

"Because you let me decide," he said. "Nobody had ever let me decide anything. I came to pay it back." He hesitated. "And because there's a letter coming. From Germany — from the camp, rather, if the post runs. And when it comes, someone should be keeping the receipts."

Edith laughed — a real laugh, rusty with disuse — and put her hand briefly on the worn cover of the ledger.

"Then drive me home, Mr. Pemberton," she said. "And tell me everything. We have eleven years to discuss, and I find I have been living with a stranger who knows me better than anyone alive."

He opened the door for her, and as she settled onto the seat she noticed, tucked into the ledger's spine where it showed between pages like a pressed flower, the corner of an envelope bearing a Cornish postmark — and beneath the flap, just visible, handwriting she did not know, in ink that had crossed the sea and a war to arrive.
