## 1. The Question Too Many

The census taker came to the divers' barracks on a morning that smelled of brine and boiling tar, and Ivo Voss knew before he spoke that he did not belong there.

It was not his clothes, though they were wrong for Vellmark's salt quarter — a grey wool coat without a single patch, boots that had never stood in bilgewater, a leather satchel too clean to have carried anything heavier than paper. It was not even the brass badge on his lapel, stamped with the Halvard Company's three-tined crown, which half the dockhands wore and none of them trusted. It was the way he stood at the mouth of the barracks corridor with his notebook already open, as though the room were something to be *entered* rather than lived in.

"Name?" he said, to the first bunk.

"Piet," said Piet, who was mending a net and did not look up.

"Age?"

"Old enough."

The census taker wrote it down anyway. That was the first thing Ivo noticed about him: he wrote down everything, including the answers that could not possibly matter, including the silences. When Piet refused his age, the man's pen scratched a small mark in the margin — a dash, or a hook — and moved on. He worked down the row of bunks like a man counting coins into a purse: name, age, trade, years of service. Name, age, trade, years of service. The barracks answered him in the flat way the barracks answered everyone in Company colors, which was to say with just enough truth to be left alone.

Ivo sat on her bunk at the end of the row, wringing out her hair from the morning's first dive, and watched him work. She was seventeen and had been Company property since she was nine, since the day her mother went down off the Gullstone shoals and did not come up, and in eight years she had learned to sort men into three kinds: those who gave orders, those who took them, and those who counted. This one was the third kind. The third kind was the most dangerous, because they never raised their voices and never needed to. What they wrote became true.

He reached her last. Of course he did. She was the smallest thing in the room.

"Name?"

"Ivo Voss."

His pen paused — not long, barely a heartbeat, but she saw it. "Voss," he repeated. "Mother's name?"

"Maren Voss." She let the water drip. "Diver. Deceased."

"I'm sorry." He said it the way clerks say such things, smoothly, without looking up, but then he *did* look up, and his eyes were pale grey and unexpectedly kind, and that unsettled her more than the question had. "Eight years indentured?"

"Since I was nine."

"And your trade?"

"Diver. Like her."

He wrote. She watched the letters form — she could read, which was itself a small rebellion her mother had paid for in extra dives, trading pearls for a season of letters from a defrocked schoolteacher who drank — and she saw him enter her neatly into the column with all the others, one line among forty, a body accounted for.

*Trade: diver. Status: indentured. Term:* — here he paused again, flipping back through his pages — *term expires at majority.*

"I'm seventeen," Ivo said. "I'll be free in the spring."

"Yes." He smiled faintly. "That will be noted."

Something in the way he said *noted* made her skin prickle, though she could not have said why. She had spent eight years thinking of freedom the way divers think of the surface — as a place that existed, that she would reach eventually, that would look exactly like the stories said. She did not like hearing it spoken of as an entry in someone else's book.

He moved to the next question. "Registered dependents?"

"None."

"Unregistered dependents?"

She frowned. "What does that mean?"

"It means—" He stopped. And this was the moment, though Ivo would not understand its weight until much later, when everything in the room quietly changed. The census taker looked at his own page. His pen hovered above the line, and Ivo saw his hand — steady through forty names, steady through Piet's insolence and the old diver Sela's coughing and every flat answer the barracks had given him — begin, very slightly, to shake.

"How many children sleep in this barracks," he said, and his voice had dropped as if the walls might be listening, "who are not on any Company roll? Not apprenticed. Not registered. Born here, living here, fed from the Company stores — but never entered anywhere. How many?"

The tar kettle hissed on the stove. Someone laughed in the yard outside, the bright careless laugh of a boy who did not know he was being asked about.

Ivo opened her mouth to say *none*, because that was the correct answer, the answer the barracks always gave, the answer that kept the tally-men away and the rations coming and the world arranged as it had always been arranged. But the census taker was still looking at her, and his face had gone the color of the paper in his satchel, and she understood suddenly that he was not asking the question the Company had sent him to ask. He was asking it because he needed to hear the answer himself.

"There's Tam," she heard herself say slowly. "Tam Ossa. His mother dove until her lungs went. She's dead two years. Tam sleeps in the loft with the younger ones. He's not on any roll — the stewards said there wasn't a berth for him, no mother to sign for him, so he just... stayed."

The pen came down. One word. Two.

"And the little ones," Ivo went on, and now the words were coming faster, pulled out of her like thread from a hem, "the ones born in the barracks after their mothers' terms ended — some of the women stay on anyway, diving without contract, because where else would they go — their children aren't registered either. There's Sela's granddaughter. There's the twins Brin keeps, her sister's, after her sister went inland. There are—"

She stopped. She had begun, without meaning to, to count. And she found, to her horror, that she could not stop at five, or seven, or ten; that the number kept unfolding in her memory like a net coming up heavy, names attached to faces attached to bunks attached to years — children she had shared bread with, taught letters to, pulled from the water when they misjudged a breath — and not one of them, not one, had ever appeared on a list.

How many unregistered dependents sleep in the divers' barracks?

*I don't know,* she almost said. *I've never counted. No one counts us twice.*

The census taker closed his eyes for a moment. When he opened them, the clerkly smoothness was gone, and what remained underneath looked young and frightened and terribly awake.

"That will be all," he said quietly. "Thank you for your cooperation."

He gathered his satchel. At the door he turned, and for one strange moment he seemed about to speak — his lips parted, his hand rose halfway toward her — and then a steward's whistle shrilled from the yard, sharp as a gull, and whatever he had meant to say went back down his throat like a diver's last breath held past safety.

He left. The barracks exhaled. Piet spat into the tar kettle and said the Company was counting heads again, which meant cuts were coming, which meant dive quotas would rise by spring, and the room grumbled and settled and thought nothing more of it.

But Ivo sat on her bunk a long time, her hair drying stiff with salt, turning the question over in her mind like a stone with something alive under it.

*Unregistered dependents.* She had spent her whole life believing that history happened elsewhere — in the marble halls up the hill where the Company directors dined, in the guild archives across the canal, in the printed broadsheets the literate men read aloud in taverns. History was a country she had never been issued papers for. Her world was water and rope and the weight of a shell in the hand, and the ledgers were someone else's weather, distant thunder that never touched the barracks.

Except that a man with a clean coat had come into her world that morning and asked a question with teeth in it, and his hand had shaken, and he had written down the name of a boy whose own mother's name was already gone from every record in Vellmark.

And she had seen his face when he asked. Not the face of a man doing his duty.

The face of a man reading his own sentence.

* * *

He came back that night.

Ivo was awake because she was always awake at that hour — the hour her mother used to call the second tide, when the water gives back what it took — lying in her bunk listening to the barracks breathe. So she heard the door hinge, careful and slow, and saw the thin wedge of lantern light move along the floor, and watched the census taker kneel at the great roll-book chained to the steward's desk, the master roster of the divers' barracks, and open it against regulations.

He worked by shielded lamplight, pen moving in quick strokes, and Ivo lay perfectly still and understood, with a cold that started in her chest and spread outward like water filling a lung, that he was striking names out.

Line after line. Small, precise strokes of ink, each one erasing a person. She could not see the names from her bunk. She did not need to. She knew the rhythm of the pen, the pause between entries, the same rhythm it had held that morning over her own name — and somewhere in that ledger, she realized, was a line that read *Ivo Voss, diver, term expires at majority*, and she did not know yet whether it would still be there when he finished.

The pen stopped. The light lifted. He had felt her watching — divers know when they are seen, even in the dark, even underwater — and he turned, and neither of them moved for a long moment, two figures in a room full of sleeping people whose names were drying into ink or dissolving out of the world, she did not know which.

"You came back to fix it," Ivo whispered.

Anselm the census taker looked down at the open ledger, at his own handwriting, and said, in a voice like a man standing at the edge of deep water:

"No, child. I came back because I finally read what I'm writing *for*."
