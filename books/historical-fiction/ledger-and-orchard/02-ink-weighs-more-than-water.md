## 2. Ink Weighs More Than Water

The barracks held its breath around them. Forty sleepers breathing in the dark, and two people awake, and a ledger open on the census taker's knees like something dissected.

Ivo did not move. Divers learn stillness the way other children learn to walk — you do not thrash when the thing you fear is looking at you, because thrashing is what makes it certain. So she stood in the aisle between the sleeping pallets, barefoot on cold planking, and waited.

Anselm closed the ledger slowly, as though the pages might bruise.

"You shouldn't be awake," he said.

"Neither should you." She kept her voice low, pitched under the snoring of Old Marta two bunks down. "The Company pays you for daylight work."

"The Company pays me for accurate work." Something moved across his face — not quite a smile. "Daylight is when accuracy is easiest to believe."

He was older than she'd thought in the lamplight of the afternoon. In the dark he looked thinner, grayer, a man made of angles and ink stains. There was an ink stain on his knuckle now, fresh, and Ivo found herself staring at it with a hatred that startled her. That stain had touched names. Her neighbors' names.

"What were you striking out?" she asked.

"Ivo Voss." He said her name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "Sit down. No — don't sit. Stand if standing keeps you angry. You'll want to be angry for this."

* * *

They went out into the yard between the barracks and the drying sheds, where the night fog came off the harbor thick enough to chew. Anselm carried his ledger against his chest like a shield that had already failed him. He lit no lamp. He seemed to prefer the dark, or to have earned it.

"In the morning," he said, "I asked how many unregistered dependents slept here. Do you remember your answer?"

"You didn't like it."

"I liked it fine. It's the question I didn't like." He turned the ledger over in his hands. "Do you know what happens to my counts? Where they go?"

"To the Company historians. To be filed."

"To be *used*. There's a difference, child, and the difference is the whole shape of your life." He opened the ledger to a page near the front and angled it toward the faint harbor light. "Look at the column headers. Not the names — the headers."

She looked. *Registered souls. Unregistered dependents. Disposition.*

"*Disposition,*" she read aloud. The word felt wrong in her mouth, like a stone swallowed sideways.

"A word from property law," Anselm said. "Not from any census I was trained on. It appeared in my instruction packet three years ago, new printing, new binding, no explanation. A dependent with a disposition is not a person awaiting registration, Ivo. A dependent with a disposition is an asset awaiting assignment."

The fog pressed in. Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy knocked its slow knock, patient as a heartbeat.

"Explain it plainly," she said. "I'm a diver's daughter. Plain things only."

"Plainly, then." He drew a breath like a man about to go under. "Every year, the Halvard Trading Company certifies a register of its laborers. Every laborer on that register has an indenture — a legal chain, paper instead of iron, but it holds the same. Your mother was on it. You are on it. Now: a child born in these barracks to two registered divers is supposed to be entered on that register at birth. That entry is what makes her a ward of the Company rather than its *property*. Registration is the difference between an apprentice and an article of trade. Do you follow?"

"Follow."

"For eleven years, the census I take feeds a register that leaves those entries off. Not lost. Not misfiled. *Struck*. Deliberately, by instruction, by men whose names I am not permitted to know. And a child who appears on no register—" his voice caught, steadied— "a child who appears on no register is legally unclaimed. And unclaimed persons of Company blood, born on Company ground, are ruled *derelict assets*. They can be transferred. Sold. Moved inland to the pearl farms, the processing houses, wherever the ledgers say the shortage is. Their own mothers cannot object, because in the eyes of the law their mothers never bore them at all."

The bell buoy knocked. Knocked again.

Ivo stood very still. This was the second skill of divers, after stillness: knowing the exact moment the bottom drops away beneath you, when the safe shelf of sand ends and there is nothing under your feet but blue falling darkness.

"That's not true," she said. "That can't be true. People would know."

"People would have to *read*, child, and the reading is arranged so that no one does. The falsified register is certified by the Company and witnessed by clerks who are paid to witness nothing. The true count exists in exactly one place besides this book." He tapped his chest, where the ledger lay. "And until tonight, I told myself the true count was a formality. A redundancy. Ink weighing itself against ink. I told myself that whatever the register said, the children were still fed, still housed, still here." His hands were shaking now; he let them shake. "Then today I asked the question too many, and watched you answer it honestly, and I understood that I have spent eleven years taking the measurements for my own gallows."

"My mother," Ivo said. It came out flat, strange, a word with the air let out of it. "My mother drowned in '61. Before that she kept a list of every child born in this barracks since the flood year. She kept it in her head. She taught it to me. She said, *paper burns, but a diver carries her depths down with her.*" She heard her own voice crack and hated it. "There were thirty-one names. Thirty-one children born here in nine years. How many are on the register?"

Anselm was quiet for a long time.

"How many," Ivo said, louder, and Old Marta shifted in her sleep inside, and neither of them cared anymore.

"Nine," he said.

The number fell into her like cold water into a lung. Nine. Out of thirty-one. Twenty-two children, alive, breathing, sleeping forty feet behind her — twenty-two children who existed only in a dead woman's memory and a stranger's contraband ledger.

"Which ones?" she whispered. "Which ones got to stay?"

"That isn't—"

"Which. Ones."

He told her. Four names, five. Children she knew. Children she'd shared bread with, taught to hold their breath in the shallows, buried a pet crab for. And the others — gone, all of them, in ones and twos across years, always with the same story trailing behind: *went inland. Taken in by family upriver. The sea takes some, the land takes the rest.* She had mourned playmates who were, right now, possibly alive somewhere, owned, transferable, dispositioned.

"They're not dead," she said, and the vertigo came then, full force, the world tilting off its axis — because grief she knew how to carry, but this was worse. This was grief that could be *undone*. Twenty-two children might be alive, and their aliveness had been filed away in a column header. "They're not dead, they're *inventory*."

"Yes."

"And mine." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "My name. Am I on the register?"

Anselm looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him decide — watched the decision move through him like weather.

"No," he said. "You were born in the flood year, during the storm week, when the registrar's office was closed four days running. By rights you should have been entered late, with a note. Instead your entry was struck with the rest." He paused. "You survived the purge by accident, Ivo. A clerical error. The clerk who should have struck you was ill that week, and the correction never came. You are the only child in this barracks whose freedom is a mistake in the Company's favor — because an unregistered adult of working age can't be transferred quietly. You're too visible. Too old. They left you in place and marked the file *deferred*."

*Deferred.* The word rang in her skull. Her whole life — the hunger that was never quite starvation, the work that was never quite slavery, the narrow, precarious, stubborn survival of her — all of it balanced on one sick clerk's fever, one missed correction, one line of ink that failed to fall.

Her legs went soft. She sat down on the cold stones of the yard without deciding to.

"So they'll come back for me," she said. "When it's convenient. When the paperwork's tidier."

"Registers get corrected every certification cycle," Anselm said quietly. "Month's end. The historians certify the new register before guild witnesses, and everything deferred becomes... resolved."

The bell buoy knocked. Ivo realized she had been counting the knocks without meaning to — her mother's habit, keeping time against panic — and that she was at eleven.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "Why come back at night? Why show me the book? You could have finished your corrections and sailed home to wherever census takers sleep."

Anselm lowered himself onto the stones beside her, an old man's descent, joint by joint. For a while he said nothing. Then:

"I struck names tonight," he said. "Fourteen of them. I've been striking them for eleven years, and tonight, when I came to the fourteenth, I made myself look up from the page and *count the empty beds*. Do you know what I saw? Nothing. No one even notices the beds are empty, because the roster says they were never filled. I have been erasing children so thoroughly that their absence doesn't even leave a hole." His voice frayed. "A man can live with making marks, Ivo. What a man cannot live with is discovering the marks are teeth."

The fog thickened. Inside, the barracks breathed on, forty sleepers minus fourteen ghosts, and none the wiser.

"There's a copy," Anselm said suddenly, as if the words had escaped before he could stop them. "Of my true counts. Eleven years of them. Real numbers, real names, dates, districts, the ships the transfers left on. If that document reached the Kestrel Guild archive before month's end—"

He stopped himself. Closed his mouth. Looked out at the black water as though hoping the sentence would drown.

But Ivo had grown up among people who read currents for a living, and she had heard where that sentence was going before it broke off. She sat in the cold, on the stones, with her mother's thirty-one names rising in her memory one by one like bubbles from the deep, and she said:

"—before month's end. Then what?"

Anselm did not answer. But his hand drifted, almost without his knowing it, to the ledger against his chest — and held it there, the way a drowning man holds the last piece of his wrecked ship.
