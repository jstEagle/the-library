## 3. What the Barracks Know

I did not sleep. The barracks breathed around me in the dark — forty bodies, give or take, though I had never once thought to count them, the way you never count your own fingers. Anselm's words sat in my chest like a swallowed hook: *unregistered dependents*. Children who were not children. Entries that could be struck.

By lamplight's last grey hour I lay on my cot and made myself do the thing no one in Vellmark ever did. I counted backward through my own life, and I looked for the gaps.

* * *

There had been a girl named Sefi who shared my sleeping shelf when I was eight. I remembered her laugh — high and braying, like a gull being robbed — and I remembered her teaching me to hold my breath by lying flat on the boards with a wet cloth over our faces, counting the seconds together until one of us gasped. Sefi could go to ninety. I could go to sixty-one.

Then one morning her shelf was empty, her blanket folded into a square so neat it looked like a lesson. Old Marta, who kept the divers' roster, said Sefi's mother had taken her inland. To an aunt, she said. In Harrow Fen, she said. And I had believed it, because why would Marta lie, and because at eight years old you believe whatever fills the shape of a question fastest.

Except now, seventeen years old and wide-eyed on my cot, I made a list of everyone who had gone inland.

Sefi, and her mother Brekka, who dove the deep beds off Gullspit and once brought up a pearl the size of a thumbnail. Tam and his little brother Osk, whose father worked the tar sheds. Little Wren, who used to bite. Maren, who was not little at all but fourteen and strong as a winch cable, who had been promised — *promised*, they'd said, like a blessing — a place in the Company's weaving house upriver. And their mothers. Brekka. Odile. The two sisters from the salt flats whose names I suddenly realized I had never learned, only *the sisters*, as if even then something in me understood they would not need names for long.

Eleven of them. Twelve. I kept losing count because grief kept interrupting, shoving its face into the ledger of my memory like a creditor.

And here was the thing that made my hands go cold under the blanket: not one of them had ever written back. Not one had sent a word, a token, a scrap of paper with a Harrow Fen address scrawled in a child's hand. Inland, where letters supposedly traveled slow, where ink cost money — yes. But twelve people, over nine years? Not a single line?

At eight I had believed it. At eleven I had begun to wonder. At thirteen I had done what children do with unbearable things: I folded the wondering small and put it away, and told myself the inland was simply far, and people simply forget.

Anselm's pen hovering over his page had unfolded all of it again.

* * *

I went looking for proof the way my mother went looking for pearls — by knowing where the bed lay and dropping down into the dark without wasting breath.

The roster hung by the barracks door: a slate board, chalked and rechalked, listing every indentured soul assigned to the divers' quarters. I had walked past it ten thousand times. That morning I stood before it and read it like an enemy.

Forty-one names. I knew this board had held forty-one names when I was eight, because Old Marta used to make the new children recite it as a lesson — *forty-one souls, forty-one debts, learn them or be lost.* Forty-one names, and since then I could name thirty-two people who had arrived: born in the barracks, brought in as foundlings after wrecks, transferred from the salt works. Thirty-two arrivals. And the board still read forty-one.

I ran the arithmetic three times against the doorframe, my finger leaving damp tracks on the slate. Even allowing for deaths — and there had been deaths, the sea collected its rents — even allowing for the ones who finished their terms, there should have been sixty-some names on that board. There were forty-one. There were always forty-one. The number never moved, the way a pond never moves, no matter how many stones are dropped in, because somewhere underneath there is a drain.

"Reading practice?" said a voice behind me.

It was Halla, sixty years old and still diving, her shoulders built like mooring posts, her hands scarred white from cold water. She stood too close, the way she did, smelling of tar and brine.

"The numbers don't add," I said.

"No," said Halla. "They don't."

She said it plainly, without surprise, and that frightened me more than anything Anselm had confessed. She took my elbow and steered me out onto the drying racks' walkway, away from the door, away from ears.

"You've been talking to the census man," she said.

"I asked him a question."

"He came back at night. Last night. You saw." It wasn't a question either. Of course she'd seen; Halla slept four hours a night and spent the rest listening to the dark the way other people listen to music. "Girl, listen to me. When I was young there was a woman named Yrsa who counted. Not aloud, not on paper — just in her head, like you're doing now. She kept track of who went inland and who arrived, and one day she went to the Company house and asked why her sister's name had come off the wage rolls while the sister was still diving the beds. Do you know what happened?"

"She went inland," I said.

Halla's mouth twitched — almost a smile, nothing warm in it. "Yrsa drowned. In six feet of calm water, with her line cut clean. They called it misadventure. Her sister's name stayed off the rolls, and two years later the sister was sold upriver and nobody signed anything because nobody could prove she belonged to anybody." Halla gripped my wrist, hard, her diver's grip, the grip that pulls you up from the bottom whether you fight it or not. "The Company doesn't kill people, girl. It amends them. Understand the difference and you might live long enough to die of old age."

"And the children?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Sefi. Was there an aunt in Harrow Fen?"

Halla let go of my wrist. For a moment she looked out at the harbor, at the grey water going silver, and I watched sixty years settle onto her face.

"There is no inland," she said quietly. "Not the way they mean it. There are only ledgers, and ledgers have two columns." She turned back to me. "Stop counting, Ivo. Whatever he told you, whatever you think you know — stop. Counting is how they find you. Counting is how they *see* you."

But that was the whole point. That was exactly, precisely the whole point, and I couldn't explain it to her without explaining Anselm, and Anselm had begged me to tell no one. So I said, "Yes, Halla," the way you say yes to the sea, meaning nothing, promising nothing, and she looked at me a long moment with those pale eyes that had seen everything under the water and most things above it, and she said, "You have your mother's face when she's about to do something stupid," and left me standing among the drying nets.

* * *

I spent the day diving the shallows, because the body needs its work and because underwater is the only place in Vellmark where no one can watch you think.

And down there, in the green silence, working the shell beds off the breakwater, I finally let myself remember my mother's last morning. Everyone in the barracks remembered the drowning — the storm, the late return, the empty line. But before that morning there had been a week, and in that week she had been strange. Quiet. She had sat with me on the pier and taught me the long breath, the deep-water breath, the one divers keep secret from their employers because a diver who can hold air for four minutes instead of three can also hide, and slip away, and survive.

"Why are you teaching me this now?" I'd asked. "You said I was too young."

"Because paper is coming," she said. I remember it exactly, the way you remember a wound. "Paper is coming for everyone, and I want you able to go where paper can't follow."

I had thought she meant the census. Everyone grumbled about the census that year. But the census had come and gone, gentle as rain, and my mother had gone out into a storm she was too good a diver to drown in, and I had been seven years older and none the wiser until a pale man with a pen hovered it over my name and flinched.

My mother had known. She had known what was coming for the unregistered — for me, the dependent no record claimed, born of a diver's body and entered nowhere. She had tried to arm me against it the only way she could, and then the sea had taken her, or the Company had borrowed the sea's hands, and the armament had sat unused in my chest for nine years like a pearl in a closed shell.

I surfaced. The harbor master's bell rang the hour. Somewhere across the water, in the Company house, men were writing the world the way they wanted it to be, and the writing was binding, and the binding was law, and the law was coming for the shelves I'd slept on and the breath I'd drawn.

Twelve gone inland. Twenty names short on the board. One mother who taught me to breathe.

I climbed out of the water and did not stop counting.

That night, when the barracks lamps went dark, a shadow crossed the yard below my window — thin, hunched, carrying a case that clinked faintly of glass ink bottles. Anselm, returning. And behind him, at the gate, two figures in Company grey, waiting without lanterns, patient as arithmetic.
