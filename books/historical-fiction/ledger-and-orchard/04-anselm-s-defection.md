## 4. Anselm's Defection

The knock came at the third hour past midnight, three soft raps and then a pause, as if the knocker had lost his nerve between the second and third. Ivo knew the rhythm of a Company man's fist — brisk, proprietary, the knock of someone who believed the door owed him an opening. This was different. This was a knock that apologized.

She slid from her cot in the loft above the net-mender's shed, where she slept now that the divers' barracks had been sealed for the winter count, and pressed her eye to the gap in the boards.

Anselm stood in the alley with a satchel strapped across his chest and rain beading on the brim of his census office hat, which he still wore even though no one wore hats to a midnight meeting unless they wanted to be seen from a distance as someone harmless. His face was the color of the fog coming off the harbor.

She opened the door before he could knock again.

"You shouldn't be here," she whispered.

"No," he agreed. "I shouldn't." He did not move to come inside. He stood there with the rain running off his hat brim like the eaves of some small drowned house. "But I've been striking names for six years, Miss Voss, and tonight I struck one too many."

* * *

He talked fast once he started, standing in the doorway because sitting down meant settling in, and settling in meant this was real. The satchel came off his shoulder and landed on the net-mender's table with a sound heavier than leather should make.

"Private counts," he said. "Every year since I took the post. The census goes to the Company clean — the clean version, the one with the dependents struck out. But I kept my own rolls. Every diver's child. Every dockhand's daughter born after her mother signed her indenture papers. Names, birth dates, barracks assignments. Eleven years of them." His hand rested flat on the satchel like a man calming an animal. "Four hundred and six children the register says were never born."

Ivo stared at it. "Why keep them? If you were going to strike them anyway—"

"Because a clerk who erases without recording isn't a clerk anymore. He's just a hand. And I told myself the true roll would matter someday, when there was somewhere safe to put it." He laughed, short and joyless. "That was cowardice dressed as patience. There is never a someday. There's only the month before certification, or nothing."

The candle between them guttered. Outside, the tide was turning; she could hear the groan of the mooring posts, the old breathing of Vellmark harbor.

"The Company certifies its register at month's end," Anselm said. "Once the historians stamp it, the falsified count becomes legal truth. The indentures of four hundred and six children transfer to the Company's creditors — sold without consent, without names, without anyone ever knowing those children existed to object." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet, whether from the rain or something else she couldn't tell. "You asked me why my pen hovered. Because you were the first one who made me say the number out loud."

"What do you want from me?"

"To take it where I can't." He pushed the satchel toward her. "The Kestrel Guild archive. They certify counter-registers — it's the only law the Company can't buy. If the true count enters their archive before the Company stamps its own, the falsification becomes contestable. It becomes *evidence*. Not history — evidence. There's a difference. History is what they let survive. Evidence is what convicts them."

"Why me?"

"Because you're not in any ledger," he said simply. "I checked. You're the safest person in Vellmark, Ivo Voss, and you don't even know it. A girl the records insist doesn't exist can walk into places the rest of us can't leave."

* * *

Anselm walked home the long way, along the seawall, letting the rain hide the fact that he was crying, or possibly just being rained on; at fifty-three he had stopped distinguishing.

The census office was dark when he reached it, but not empty. That was the first wrong thing. The lamp in Historian Greve's window burned at this hour the way a trap lies open.

He entered through the front, because skulking was for the guilty and he wanted, desperately, for the last few hours of his innocence to be structurally sound. Greve sat behind his desk with two stewards flanking him, and on the desk lay a single sheet: the night's corrected entries, pulled from the copying queue before Anselm could file them.

"Master Anselm." Greve's voice was mild, which was worse than anger. Mildness meant procedure. "We've noticed a curiosity. Your correction entries run eleven percent above your field tallies. Struck dependents, mostly. Children." He tapped the sheet. "A diligent man strikes what the law tells him to strike. A curious man asks how many sleep in the barracks. Which are you?"

"A tired one," Anselm said.

"Mm." Greve folded the sheet in half, then in half again, small and precise, until it was a thing that fit in a fist. "Steward Brann will escort you home. Consider yourself asked after. People will continue asking after you until we're satisfied with the answers."

They let him walk. That frightened him more than irons would have. Men they irons are done with; men they follow are being weighed.

In his rooms, by the light of a shuttered candle, he began to copy. Not the satchel — that was gone, given away, the best decision of his life arriving exactly on time — but a duplicate index. Years of habit had taught him redundancy; tonight redundancy was a rope thrown backward. If the notes were taken, the index would tell whoever held it what had been lost, and where it might surface. If he was taken—

If he was taken, it would tell them nothing they didn't already intend to do.

He wrote until the sky over the harbor went the grey of unpolished pewter. Then he hid the index inside the spine of the office's oldest atlas, the one nobody consulted because everyone already knew where Vellmark was, and went to work looking like a man with nothing in his chest but routine.

* * *

Ivo read the true rolls by daylight, in fragments, hidden inside mending.

She'd sewn false bottoms into two herring crates by noon — a skill learned young, because the docks taught every child where things were worth hiding — and by dusk she had memorized the first forty names and burned the pages in the forge, one at a time, watching each name curl and go.

Not burn, she corrected herself. She wasn't burning them. She was moving them somewhere fire couldn't reach.

Four hundred and six. She said it under her breath while she worked the needle. She tried to match faces to entries. *Mira Sond, b. 1841, Barracks C.* Mira Sond used to dare the boys to jump off the coal pier. *Denna Osk, b. 1839, mother: pearl-diver (drowned).* Denna Osk had taught her which gulls meant weather. There were pages of mothers marked *drowned*, so many that the word stopped looking like a word.

Her own name wasn't there. Of course it wasn't. She'd been seven when the sea took her mother, and the Company had absorbed her indenture the way water absorbs a dropped stone — no entry required, because the debt transferred silently, child following contract like foam following hull. She was the gap in the ledger. She was what happened when the clerks got everything right.

And now she was about to become what happened when one of them got it wrong.

The older divers found her at the evening ration line. Old Sefa took her elbow, her grip still strong from thirty years of hauling lines, and steered her aside.

"You've been counting," Sefa said. Not a question. The barracks knew everything; that was its only wealth.

"I haven't."

"You have the look. Your mother had it, the last spring. Counting the boats that didn't come back." Sefa looked out at the harbor, where the Company's cranes stood against the sunset like gallows waiting for cargo. "Whatever you're carrying, carry it smaller. Stewards were asking after the census man today. Asking after *him* means asking after everyone who spoke to him. And everyone knows you argued with him in the yard like a fishwife."

"He gave me something."

Sefa went very still. "Then it's already heavier than you. Give it back."

"It's four hundred and six people, Auntie. It doesn't go back."

For a long moment the old woman said nothing. Then she pressed something into Ivo's palm — a pearl, small, grey-blue, imperfect, warm from her skin. "Your mother's last find. She left it with me for you. I was keeping it for a wedding." Her jaw tightened. "It'll buy passage better than it buys marriage. Take the flooded streets. Never the main gates."

Ivo closed her fingers around it. "Why give me this?"

"Because you have the look," Sefa said, "and the look always ends up doing what it's doing already. I'm only choosing whose fault it is." She turned back to the ration line. "Go before I change my mind and drown myself instead."

* * *

On the fourth day, the stewards came to the barracks asking after Anselm, and Ivo understood that whatever window had been open was beginning to close.

They moved through the yard in pairs, polite as tax assessors, showing his likeness — a charcoal sketch, stiff, making him look guiltier than any confession could. *Have you seen this man outside his duties? Has he spoken with you privately? Has he handled anything belonging to the Company?*

Ivo stood in the bread line and watched a steward hold the sketch up to a girl of maybe ten, watch the girl shake her head, watch the steward write something down anyway. Writing something down anyway. That was the whole machine, wasn't it. The pen moving regardless of the answer.

That night she met the smuggler.

He operated out of a chandlery near the salt gate, a broad man named Corvane with rings on every finger except the ones that mattered, and he listened to her request with the expression of a man appraising cargo.

"The Kestrel archive," he repeated. "Through the district checkpoints. With Company stewards sweeping every route because their little bird clerk flew crooked." He smiled. "Possible. Not cheap."

"I have this." She set Sefa's pearl on the barrel between them.

Corvane picked it up, turned it in the lamplight, and something flickered behind his face — recognition, or arithmetic. "Fine stone. Fine." He set it down but did not slide it back. "Passage I'll grant for this. But I deal in favors as well as coin, and I'll need one from you. Small thing. Later. When I ask."

Every instinct her mother had planted said *no*. Every circumstance she stood inside said *yes*.

"When you ask," Ivo said slowly, "how will I know the favor stays small?"

"You won't." He said it cheerfully, sliding the pearl into his vest. "That's what makes it a favor and not a price. Meet me at the salt gate tomorrow, low tide. Wear warehouse crew colors. And girl—" he leaned close, and she smelled brine and tobacco, "—walk like you belong to no one. You'll find it comes naturally."

* * *

Anselm spent his last free evening correcting entries.

Not the Company's — his own. In the census office, under Greve's renewed and increasingly attentive gaze, he filed the season's tallies with immaculate precision, every figure reconciled, every dependent struck in accordance with statute. He had decided that if they were going to hang a falsifier, they would at least have to manufacture the crime, because he refused to leave it lying around where they could pick it up honestly.

Greve appeared at his shoulder near closing. "You seem calm, Master Anselm."

"I'm a clerk. Calm is the trade."

"Hm. The Kestrels certified a curious acquisition request yesterday. Archive space reserved. Priority handling. One wonders what the guild expects to receive." Greve smiled with all the warmth of a filing cabinet. "One wonders what *we* should expect."

"Expect accuracy," Anselm said. "It's what I've given this office for twenty years."

"And yet." Greve straightened his cuffs. "Stewards will want a full accounting of your private papers tomorrow morning. All of them. Do sleep well."

That night Anselm sat at his window with the shutter cracked, watching the harbor, and permitted himself, for the first time in eleven years, to imagine the number spoken aloud in a room full of witnesses. Four hundred and six voices that had never been counted, entering history through the side door of a rival guild's archive, carried by a seventeen-year-old girl the ledgers insisted was nobody.

Let them come for his papers. Let them come for him. Papers could be copied faster than men could be broken, and he had made sure of the copies — and besides, they were already gone, already moving through the city in herring crates, already becoming someone else's burden and someone else's hope.

He thought of her face in the net-mender's doorway, furious and frightened and absolutely unwilling to look away. Good. Anger kept. Fear kept. Only indifference sank.

He blew out the candle and, in the dark, began composing in his head the statement he would give when they finally asked — rehearsing it the way he'd once rehearsed tallies, line by line, until every lie was load-bearing and every truth was hidden in plain sight.

Below in the streets, a Company bell began to ring the night watch, and somewhere between the salt gate and the harbor, a girl in borrowed crew colors was already moving.
