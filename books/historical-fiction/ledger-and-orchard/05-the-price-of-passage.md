## 5. The Price of Passage

The pearl had lived in a hollowed knot of wax at the bottom of Ivo's sea chest for nine years, wrapped first in oilcloth, then in a strip of her mother's diving sash, then in silence. It was not a beautiful pearl by the standards of the counting houses — too gray at the crown, too small for a signet — but it had come up from the deep in Maren Voss's cupped hand on the last dive she ever made whole, and Ivo had been told, once, in the plain voice divers use for important things: *This one is yours. Not the Company's. Yours.*

She unwrapped it now in the grey hour before dawn, turning it between finger and thumb until it caught the lamplight and held it, a small captive moon.

"You don't have to," Anselm's notes said, in his cramped clerk's hand, from the page open beside her on the floor. He hadn't written that. She had written it herself, days ago, and never crossed it out. The notes went on to other things — dates, districts, the names of guild archivists who might still be honest — but the sentence stayed where she'd put it, like a splinter she could neither work out nor ignore.

She had three days before the Company's stewards finished their sweep of the harbor district. Three days before the checkpoints on the Saltbridge went from lazy to lethal, before every face moving between districts got looked at twice, before Anselm — wherever he was now — stopped being a rumor and became an example. The Kestrel archive sat across the river in the Guild Quarter, behind doors that did not open for dockhands, divers' daughters, or girls with no registered name. To get there she needed passage, and passage in Vellmark belonged to exactly one kind of person: the kind who sold it.

* * *

Dessin Karo kept his office above a rope-walk that stank of tar and hemp, in the crooked seam of streets where the Harbor District forgot itself and became something older and less lawful. You reached him by buying a coil of rope you didn't need and asking if it came with *knots*. The boy at the counter had looked at her — a girl of seventeen in a diver's patched coat, salt-stiff hair, hands rough as wharf planking — and something in her stare had made him swallow his laughter and jerk his thumb toward the stairs.

Karo was smaller than the stories said. That was the first thing. The smugglers of Vellmark were spoken of in the barracks the way weather was spoken of — vast, inevitable, indifferent — and Dessin Karo sat behind a desk scarred with knife-marks, a narrow man with a clerk's fingers and a diver's eyes, flat and patient and always slightly amused. A lamp burned green glass beside him. On the wall hung no maps, which told Ivo he kept them all in his head.

"The census girl," he said, before she'd closed the door.

Her stomach dropped through six floors of dark water. "I don't know what you mean."

"Of course you don't." He gestured to a stool. She didn't take it. "Half the harbor knows a census taker went strange and handed his papers to somebody. Half is wrong about who. The half that's right isn't talking, because the Company pays worse than I do." He laced his fingers. "You want to cross the river. You're wearing everything you own and your eyes keep going to the window, which means you're counting exits, which means you've already learned the most useful lesson there is. What are you offering?"

She set the pearl on the desk.

Karo didn't touch it. He leaned over it the way a moneylender leans over a ledger, reading weight, nacre, provenance — reading, perhaps, the wax and oilcloth smell of a thing kept hidden nine years. "Halvard pearl," he said. "Deep-water. Old stock." His eyes came up. "This was a diver's."

"It's mine."

"They're all someone's, girl. The question the Company asks is different from mine." He turned it once with one fingernail, delicate as a surgeon. "It'll buy you passage. Rope crew, warehouse run, in and out of the Guild Quarter under my seal. Nobody looks at cargo twice when it moves under Kestrel-adjacent paper." A pause, precise as a blade laid down. "But passage isn't enough for this. Passage gets you across the river alive. It doesn't get you *forgotten*, and forgotten is what you need to be by month's end. For forgotten, I want a favor."

Ivo kept her face still. Her mother had taught her that too — how the water punishes panic, how a diver who thrashes tells the deep exactly where she is.

"What favor?"

"There's a warehouse on Cordage Row. Company property, sealed since before you were born, guarded like a reliquary. Every runner I've bought has a guess. Guesses rot." He slid the pearl back toward her, an inch, a courtesy, a demonstration that nothing was settled. "You were raised in the barracks. Barracks children see things grown men don't think to hide from them. Deliveries, rotations, which door opens for which cart at which dead hour of night." He smiled without warmth. "Tell me which warehouse holds the Company's original ledgers — the sealed ones, the true ones, the ones they'd burn rather than show — and we're square. Pearl returned, passage granted, memory wiped."

The room went very quiet. Even the tar-smell seemed to lean in.

"You want me to find it for you," Ivo said slowly, "so you can steal it."

"I want you to find it so I can know where it is. What happens afterward is a matter of appetite." Karo spread his small clerk's hands. "Consider what you're doing anyway, girl. You're carrying a dead man's numbers into a rival guild's vault. That's theft of a kind — the Company would hang you for it with great ceremony. All I'm offering is to make you better at the trade you've already chosen."

*It's not the same,* some part of her insisted, the part that still believed in clean hands and clear water. But another part — newer, harder, grown in the weeks since Anselm's pen had hovered over that terrible question — looked at the pearl on the desk and understood what it actually was: not treasure. Leverage. Everything was leverage. The Company had used her mother's death as an entry in a column; the guilds used hunger; the smugglers used desperation; and now here she stood, being offered her first chance to hold a piece of the board instead of being a piece upon it.

Betrayal, she thought, was a tool like any other. A boat hook. A breathing line. Ugly, necessary, owned.

"If I say yes," she said, "and then I lie — tell you the wrong warehouse —"

"Then I'll know eventually, and knowing will cost more than either of us wants to pay." He said it lightly, which made it worse. "Don't lie to me, Ivo Voss. Lie beautifully to everyone else. That's a professional courtesy."

He knew her name. Of course he did. She filed the fear away where she kept all the others, in the cold locker below thought.

"I need an answer tonight," Karo said, taking up his pen — a clerk's pen, absurdly domestic — as though she were one more line in a day's business. "Cordage Row. Which building. How it's guarded. Where the sealed ledgers sleep."

Ivo looked at the pearl. Gray-crowned, deep-water, her mother's hand around it in the last light she ever gave her daughter. *This one is yours.*

Everything hers had been taken by ink. Perhaps ink could be made to pay it back — even borrowed, even dirty, even spent into another man's pocket. She thought of Anselm's notes against her ribs, years of true counts written in a coward's careful hand, and of the number that would end them, the number that would name every erased child in the barracks, and she thought: *let the Company learn what it feels to be read.*

"Cordage Row," she said. "Third warehouse from the river gate. The one everyone thinks is empty."

Karo's pen stopped.

* * *

She walked back through the harbor district as the lamps came on, and the city performed its nightly magic trick: the same streets, the same faces, and underneath, invisible as current, the new knowledge moving through everything. The chandler's boy was a pair of ears. The woman selling fried eel outside the barracks gate watched who lingered. Ivo bought eel she didn't want and paid with a smile she'd practiced, and all the way home she felt the bargain sitting inside her like swallowed ballast — heavy, useful, hers.

In her room she unwrapped Anselm's notes again and read until the candle drowned in its own wax. Warehouse three, Cordage Row. She had known it since she was nine years old, though she'd never known she knew it: the building the cart drivers called the Quiet House, the one that took deliveries at the hour when even gulls slept, the one whose guards changed shift twice as often as any other post because, the drivers joked, *the boredom down there rots a man faster than the damp.* Empty warehouses didn't need fresh guards. Empty warehouses didn't need a door that opened only from the inside.

She had traded her mother's pearl for a thing she'd carried free all along, and given away in the trading a piece of ground she could never stand on again. That was the shape of it. That was the arithmetic. Karo now held a key she'd cut with her own memory, and whatever lock it opened — the Company's secrets, or the Company's throat — he would turn it without asking her leave.

*Every hand extended has been a ledger entry,* she wrote once in the margin of Anselm's notes, then scratched it out so hard the paper thinned. Some truths were safer unrecorded. She was learning that too.

Two days remained before certification. Somewhere across the city, Anselm was either hiding or caught, and she could do nothing for him except the thing he'd asked: carry the count. Somewhere in her chest, beneath the fear and the fury and the strange cold pride of having made her first corrupt bargain, a smaller voice asked the question she refused to answer yet:

When the moment came — when Karo's men moved on the Quiet House, or the Company's historians moved on her — which side of the ledger would she discover she'd written herself onto?

She banked the candle. In the dark, the pearl's absence in her sea chest felt exactly like a debt coming due.

Tomorrow, the rope crew. Tomorrow, the river. Tomorrow, the Guild Quarter — and the first move in a game where she had just learned, to her horror and her profit, that she was no longer a piece on the board.

She was beginning to play.
