## 6. A Man Made of Entries

They came for Anselm on the fourth morning after he handed over his life's work, which meant they had been looking since at least the third, and possibly since the first. He had expected it sooner. He had counted on it sooner. Every hour they failed to knock was an hour Ivo Voss might be wasting in some tavern, or worse, trusting someone she shouldn't, and every hour they did knock was an hour stolen from his own margin.

When the knock finally came — three measured raps, courteous as a summons to dinner — Anselm set down his pen mid-stroke. He had been drafting a letter of resignation he never intended to send, addressed to no one, a document whose only purpose was to occupy his hands. He blotted the page carefully. A man made of entries did not leave ink wet on the desk when strangers arrived; that was how stains happened, and stains were how questions began.

"Come in," he said, though the door was already opening.

Two stewards in Halvard gray, and behind them a woman he recognized from company portraits in the great hall: Historian Veyra Coll, keeper of the Company's register, third of her name to hold the office. She wore her authority the way other people wore jewelry — visibly, but with the confidence of someone who had never once needed it explained.

"Census taker Anselm Rook," she said. It was not a question. His name, spoken aloud in his own rooms, sounded like an entry being read back for verification. "You'll come with us. There are irregularities in the southern district returns."

"Of course," said Anselm. "I'll bring my field notes."

"No," said Veyra Coll, with the faint smile of a woman declining a gift she intended to take anyway. "You won't."

* * *

The historians' wing sat above the counting floors, where the air smelled of sealing wax and cold iron, and the windows — Anselm noted within his first ten minutes, because noting was what he was — looked out not on the harbor but inward, onto the archive courtyard, where nothing could be seen arriving or leaving except by those who already knew. They put him in a room with a table, two chairs, a lamp, and a barred window facing that courtyard. It was, he understood immediately, a stage set. Everything about it was arranged for his benefit. Even the comfort was a message: *you are valuable enough to be comfortable, and watched closely enough that comfort is all you'll get.*

Veyra Coll came alone, carrying a single folder. Thin. He tried not to stare at its thinness and failed. Thin folders meant little evidence, or little need for evidence.

"Do you know why we keep historians, Mr. Rook?" she asked, settling into the chair across from him as though it were her parlor. "Most people think it's to preserve the past. Sentimental nonsense. We keep historians so the past can be *corrected*. Memory is a draft. History is the fair copy. Our work is making sure the fair copy is the only copy anyone ever sees."

"I've entered my counts faithfully for eleven years," Anselm said.

"Faithfully." She turned the word over like a coin of doubtful mint. "Eleven years. Do you know what I admire about you, census taker? Your handwriting. Exemplary. Uniform pressure, no hesitations, no second thoughts visible in the stroke. A man who writes like that is either honest or extraordinarily disciplined. The Company has use for both."

She opened the folder. Inside lay three pages — his own pages, torn from his field ledger, the ones recording the true count of unregistered dependents in the divers' barracks. Forty-one children. He had written the number forty-one times in eleven years, and each time the number had been struck from the fair copy before certification, and each time he had told himself that keeping the record at all was a kind of victory.

"You took these from my quarters," he said.

"We took these from your *office*, which belongs to the Company, as does everything written in it, as do you, if we're precise about the indenture language governing salaried staff. But let's not be lawyers. Let's be honest men and women." She laid the pages flat, aligned their edges with a fingertip, a small compulsive gesture he found himself cataloguing despite everything. "Here is what happens next, and you may choose between two versions of it."

She spoke the first version plainly, without theater, which somehow made it worse. He would recant. He would sign a statement attesting that his private tallies were the product of a long illness — a melancholia of the eyes, a fatigue that had led him to invent dependents where none existed, to pad his counts out of a misguided zeal for thoroughness. In exchange: rehabilitation. Retention. Restoration of full salary, back pay, even a commendation. "The Company forgives sickness, Mr. Rook. It does not forgive treachery. But we are prepared to call your condition sickness, if you'll meet us halfway and agree to be ill."

"And the other version?"

"The other version has no paperwork," she said. "It happens below the counting floors, where there are no windows worth noting."

Anselm looked at the barred window. Through it, in the grey morning light, he could see the archive courtyard, and in the courtyard, porters were stacking crates by the furnace house door. He knew the crates' dimensions by heart. He had specified them, years ago, in the storage requisitions for the census bureau: long, shallow, lined against damp. Built to hold folio volumes flat. Built to hold his folios.

His stomach went very quiet and very cold.

"When?" he asked.

Veyra Coll followed his gaze without turning her head. She had seen the crates too. She had chosen this room deliberately, of course; she chose everything deliberately. "Certification is in nine days," she said. "You have until then to decide which version of yourself survives it."

* * *

They left him alone most of each day, which he understood was also part of the design — solitude was the whetstone they meant to grind him against. So he used the solitude the only way he knew how: he worked. He had no paper — they had taken his pen, his ink, even the pencil stub from his boot, and the thoroughness of that search had almost moved him — but he had memory, and memory was a ledger with infinite pages.

He reconstructed his years of true counts in his head, entry by entry, district by district, the way a diver's daughter might run through her mother's breathing patterns to calm herself. Forty-one children in the southern barracks, the year Ivo Voss's mother drowned. Forty-six the next year, when the Kess twins were born and nobody wrote them down. Fifty-three, fifty-one — two lost to fever, unrecorded even in grief — then fifty-eight, sixty, sixty-four. Sixty-four children sleeping in beds the Company insisted were empty. Sixty-four names that existed nowhere but in his hand, and now, if she had been quick and lucky, in the hands of a seventeen-year-old girl running through the city with a satchel full of his sins.

He tested the numbers against dates, against weather, against the year the pearl fleet lost three boats and the barracks swelled with widows' children. The totals held. They always held. That was the thing Veyra Coll would never understand about him, the thing no historian could rehabilitate: he had never wanted to falsify anything in his life. Falsification was their trade. His trade had been the slower, more stubborn one — writing down what was true, year after year, knowing the fair copies would erase it, keeping the drafts anyway, hoarding them like a miser hoards coins he never intends to spend.

Until he'd spent them. All at once. On a girl with salt-scoured hands who had looked at him across a barracks table and asked the question he'd been waiting eleven years for someone to ask: *why does your pen stop?*

On the third day they brought him meals through a slot, and on the fourth day Veyra Coll returned with the same thin folder and a new document — the recantation, drafted in beautiful clerical script, awaiting only his signature. She read it aloud to him as though he couldn't read, and perhaps that too was deliberate, a reminder of who controlled the text.

"'I, Anselm Rook, census taker of the southern district, being of unsound and melancholic mind—'"

"Say the number," Anselm interrupted.

She paused. "I beg your pardon?"

"The number of children. In your certified register, say the number of unregistered dependents in the southern barracks. Say it aloud, in this room, just once."

Something flickered behind her eyes — not guilt; he doubted the organ existed in her — but the professional irritation of a craftsman shown a flaw in her own work. "Zero," she said. "The register records zero, because the register is correct, and your illness—"

"There were sixty-four last winter," Anselm said. "Sixty-four children who eat Company bread, dive Company waters, and will be sold under Company seal before spring, because you are going to certify a book that says they don't exist. You don't need my signature, Historian. You have my pages. Forge the recantation if you want it — God knows the Company has the skill. Which tells me something, doesn't it? If you could finish this without me, I'd already be ash."

He watched her weigh it, the truth of it, and set it down among her other tools.

"Nine days," she said again, softer now, almost kind, which was the most frightening tone she had used yet. "A man made of entries, Mr. Rook. That's what you are. Every year of your life filed and cross-referenced. Do you imagine anyone will look for the drafts?"

* * *

On the fifth day, they began moving the crates.

He heard it before he saw it — the groan of the courtyard doors, the flat slap of porter's feet on stone — and crossed to the barred window with his heart hammering in a rhythm he refused to call fear. Below, four porters wheeled the first crate across the flagstones toward the furnace house. It rode low on its cart. Heavy. Full.

His folios. Eleven years of them. The true counts of every child the Company had ever erased, bound in his own exemplary hand, uniform pressure, no hesitations — carried now toward the fire that would make the fair copy the only copy, forever.

He pressed his forehead to the cold bars and did the arithmetic he could not stop doing. Nine days had become five. Certification was four days off. For the register to be corrected — for Anselm's numbers to surface anywhere the guilds could see them, anywhere they could not be quietly burned — the true counts had to reach the Kestrel archive before the Company's historians signed their beautiful lie into law. Four days. And the girl had the notes, and the city between her and the archive was full of Company men asking after a missing census taker, and every road she took was a road someone was paid to watch.

*Let her be careful,* he thought. *Let her be smarter than the ledgers.*

Below, the first crate disappeared through the furnace house door, and a smell of scorched vellum drifted up faintly on the harbor wind — or perhaps he imagined it; perhaps he would imagine it for the rest of his life, however much of that remained. The second crate started forward.

Anselm straightened, wiped his face with both hands, and stood very still in the grey light, resolving himself, hardening, the way wax hardens into a seal. He knew now exactly what he was worth in this transaction: time. Every hour he stalled — every meeting requested, every clause disputed, every melancholic silence he could stretch across Veyra Coll's patience — was an hour bought for Ivo Voss with the only currency he had left.

He sat down at the table, folded his hands, and raised his voice to the empty room, knowing they listened beyond the door.

"Historian Coll," he called. "Regarding the recantation. I find clause four ambiguous, and I should like it clarified in writing. Take all the time you need. I have nothing but time."

It was the first lie he had ever told in an official proceeding, and he found, examining it in the privacy of his own skull, that it weighed almost nothing at all.

Outside, the second crate reached the furnace door — and behind it, unhurried, came a third, and a fourth, and more carts than he had ever requisitioned, rolling in through the courtyard gates from streets he could not see.
