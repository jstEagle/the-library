## 7. The Smuggler's Ledger

The smuggler's name was Corvane, though everyone in the flooded district called him the Ferryman, and Ivo had learned by now that men who took names from their trade were men whose trade was taking.

He received her in a room above a shuttered chandlery, where the tide had long since claimed the ground floor and the smell of brine rose through the floorboards like a confession. A single lamp burned on a table between them. On it, wrapped in oilcloth, lay her mother's pearl — the last one, pulled from the deep the week before she drowned, still warm-looking somehow even now, as if it remembered the water.

Corvane turned it over with one blunt finger. He was older than she'd guessed when they'd first met at the fish market, gray threading his beard, but his hands were steady, and steady hands frightened her more than shaking ones. Shaking men were nervous. Steady men had already decided.

"Fine work," he said. "Halvard beds?"

"My mother's own bed. She dove outside their leases."

"Then it's contraband twice over." He smiled without warmth. "You've brought me stolen goods to buy stolen passage. You'll go far in this city, girl."

"I need to reach the Kestrel archive before month's end."

"I know what you need. I asked what you'd give." He set the pearl down and slid it back across the table toward her. The gesture was so unexpected that for a moment Ivo simply stared at it. "Keep that. It's not enough anyway, and I don't deal in keepsakes. I deal in information." His eyes came up, pale as harbor ice. "So here's my price. Not the pearl. Not coin. Tell me which warehouse holds the Company's sealed original ledgers."

The question landed like cold water down her collar. She thought of Anselm's notes sewn into the lining of her coat, heavy against her ribs. *He knows what I'm carrying. He's known since the fish market.*

"And if I tell you?"

"Then you walk through any door in Vellmark, free as gull-shit, and no Company steward will ever hear your name from my mouth."

Ivo made herself breathe slowly. Her mother's rhythm — in through the nose, four counts, hold, release. Diver's breathing. The trick wasn't holding your breath; anyone could do that. The trick was spending less of it than you seemed to be spending.

"The Saltworks Row annex," she said. "Underground vault, third cellar. The Company moved them there after the fire scare two years ago."

She watched him receive this. Watched something flicker behind the ice-pale eyes — satisfaction, or its counterfeit. He nodded once, slow, like a man confirming an entry.

"Good," he said. "That's good. And now, because I keep honest books, let me show you mine." He reached beneath the table and produced not a ledger but a folded paper, which he opened and turned to face her.

It was a bill of sale. Her own description — height, hair, the scar on her left forearm from the oyster knife — written in a clerk's careful hand. Below it, a price. Below that, the Halvard Trading Company's seal.

"You sold me?" The words came out flat, which surprised her. She'd expected them to come out screaming.

"I sold your *description*," Corvane said mildly. "There's a difference. The Company believes you're the informant who caught Anselm. They believe you're coming to me to arrange passage out of Vellmark. Tomorrow night, two of their stewards arrive at this address to collect you. They pay me for the delivery. You get a cell in the historian's wing and a very thorough conversation about who gave you the census notes." He folded the paper away. "And I get paid twice for one warehouse. That, girl, is what they call efficient bookkeeping."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the stairwell. Ivo sat very still, and inside her stillness something was rearranging itself — the same cold clarity that used to come over her mother before a dive, when the water went from being home to being enemy and every movement had to count.

Every hand extended has been a ledger entry. The thought arrived whole, finished, like a number read aloud. The Company kept books on children. Corvane kept books on her. Even Anselm — kind, doomed Anselm — had kept books, years of true counts written in a dead man's careful script. Everyone wrote her down somewhere. Everyone priced her.

Except herself. She had never once been the one holding the pen.

"You're wondering how you get out of this room," Corvane said. He sounded almost kind. "Don't bother planning anything clever. There are two of my boys downstairs, and the tide's up."

"I'm not planning anything clever," Ivo said truthfully.

* * *

They locked her in the chandlery's old rope store while they waited for the stewards — a stone room half-sunk below the street line, smelling of tar and rot, with a barred window set low near the ceiling. Through it she could see the black water of the flooded cellar district lapping at the foundations of the buildings across the lane.

Her mother had grown up in these cellars. Had told her about them the way other mothers told ghost stories — how the tide ran through the lower streets twice a day, how the old merchants had built connecting cisterns and culverts beneath the houses, how divers used them as roads when the law closed the streets above.

*"Water's only dangerous if you fight it,"* her mother had said once, braiding salt-stiff hair by lamplight. *"Fight it and it spends you. Spend nothing, take everything. That's the whole secret. Everything else is just breathing."*

Ivo tested the bars. Old iron, rusted at the mortar line. She worked them in silence, patient as erosion, and while she worked she spent her breath carefully — in four, hold, out six — slowing her heart until her body believed it was floating.

An hour past midnight, the tide began to turn. She heard it in the walls, a deep shifting groan, water finding new paths. One bar came loose with a sound no louder than a knuckle cracking. The second took longer, and her fingers bled freely by then, but blood was cheap and silence wasn't.

The window looked out over a channel perhaps eight feet deep at high water. Cold enough to stop a heart, her mother would have said, if you went in gasping. So Ivo didn't gasp. She sat on the sill, filled her lungs in four counts, held, and slipped into the dark like something returning home rather than escaping.

The cold hit like a blow, but she'd been raised in colder water. Halvard leases ran deep off the northern shoals, and her mother had trained her in them since she could walk — *breathe small, move slow, the sea takes from the wasteful.* She let herself sink, hung weightless in the black, and listened. Above her, muffled by stone and water, boots crossed the rope store floor. Voices. Then the door swinging open onto emptiness.

She swam.

The culverts of the flooded district were exactly as her mother had described: a drowned maze of merchant ambition, brick throats and cistern mouths, some collapsed, some clear, all of them mapped nowhere except in the memory of people the city had decided not to record. Ivo navigated by touch and by counting — thirty strokes along the left wall, surface, breathe, dive again. In four, hold, out six. Spend nothing. Take everything.

Once, passing beneath a grate, she saw lamplight and heard voices — stewards, arriving early, angry, asking after a girl who was already gone. Once, her hand found something soft in the dark and she jerked away before her mind supplied the word *rat*. Once, in a wide cistern where the current slackened, she surfaced under a broken sky-light and saw the stars, and allowed herself three full breaths of ordinary air, because even ledgers have margins.

She surfaced finally in the drainage basin behind the tannery row, hauled herself out on the slime-slick stones, and lay there dripping and shaking and laughing — a thin, wild sound she strangled immediately, because laughter carries.

They would look for a girl fleeing through the upper streets. They would watch the gates, the ferries, the fish market. No one watched the water. No one ever watched the water, because in Vellmark the water belonged to the people the books forgot.

Ivo pressed her hand flat against the wet stone and felt, absurdly, her mother's hand over hers. *Breathe small. Move slow.* The pearl was still in Corvane's keeping, lost with everything else — but Anselm's notes were dry against her ribs, sewn tight, and the month was turning toward its end.

Fury was a luxury; she filed it away to spend later, with interest. What remained was clarity, cold and clean as deep water. Every hand extended is a ledger entry. Fine. Then she would stop reaching for hands.

Certification Day was five days away. The Kestrel Guild convened its witnesses at dawn on the first, and between here and there stood a city full of people who had already sold her twice.

Ivo wrung out her coat, touched the lump of hidden pages one last time, and walked barefoot into the dark toward the guild quarter — unrecorded, unowned, and done with being bought.

Behind her, far off across the rooftops, a bell began to toll the alarm.
