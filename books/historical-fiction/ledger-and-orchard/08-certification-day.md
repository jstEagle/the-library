## 8. Certification Day

The morning of the certification, Vellmark's bells rang wrong. Not the usual nine strokes of the harbor bell marking the tide, but a slow, ceremonial tolling from the Company tower — twelve strokes, one for each district under Halvard seal, as if the city itself were being counted.

Ivo stood in the yard of Brannick's warehouse with forty dockhands dressed in Company gray, and she was one more body among them, face powdered with chalk dust to look like every other laborer who had ever been counted and never remembered. Her hands shook until she made fists of them.

"Rule one," Brannick said, walking the line. He was a broad man gone soft at the middle, with a smuggler's smile that never reached his eyes. "You lift nothing. You look at nothing. You are furniture." His gaze stopped on her a half-second too long, and she felt again the weight of what he thought he owned: the name she would give the Company when this was done, *Ivo Voss, informant*, wrapped and ready like goods for sale. He believed he held her leash. She let him believe it. A leash is only a rope until someone learns to tie knots.

"Rule two," he continued, "when the crates come down off the cart, you stack them where the stewards point and you do not open them."

The crates. Three of them, sealed with wax stamped by the Kestrel Guild itself — the archivists' own transport, or so the manifest claimed. Inside them, packed between false ledgers and rosin shavings, lay Anselm's true counts. Nine years of census pages in his cramped, patient hand. Every child the Company had struck from existence, restored in ink.

Ivo carried her own part of it against her ribs: a single folio, thin enough to hide beneath her shirt, containing the certified copy she was meant to remove and the page she was meant to leave behind. Anselm had taught her the trick of it weeks ago, in the flooded cellar dark, his voice steady even then, even knowing what it might cost him. *"A register is only paper until someone reads it aloud. Paper can be swapped. A voice, once heard, cannot be unspoken."*

The bells stopped. The gates of the Company hall opened.

* * *

The Hall of Reckoning had been built to make small people feel smaller. Its ceiling rose into painted clouds where merchant saints weighed gold against souls and found the gold heavier. Long tables ran its length, draped in green cloth, and upon them sat the register — six volumes bound in calfskin, each page ruled and countersigned, the falsified count rendered beautiful so that no one would think to question it.

Guild witnesses filled the gallery above: Kestrel archivists in their slate robes, a notary from the Saltworkers' Compact, two magistrates whose loyalties were purchased annually and openly. Below, the Company historians sat like crows on a fence, black-cowled, their chief — a woman named Odile Ferrant with ink-stained fingertips and eyes like a scale settling — presiding over the ceremony with the serenity of someone who has already read the ending.

Ivo moved through the hall with the other warehouse hands, hauling the Kestrel crates toward the side chamber where documents were staged for inspection. Each step took her deeper past the guards. There were more of them than Brannick had promised. They lined the walls in pairs, halberds grounded, and near the historian's table stood a man she recognized with a jolt that nearly buckled her knees: Anselm.

He sat in a plain chair, flanked by two stewards, his census satchel gone, his collar torn. He looked smaller than she remembered, grayer, but his spine was straight as a ledger rule. He did not look at her. He looked at nothing, at the middle distance, the way a diver looks before going under — conserving everything.

*He's still buying time,* she told herself. *They haven't broken him yet.* But the arrangement of the room told another story. Anselm was not here as a witness. He was here as evidence.

The crates went down in the staging chamber, and Ivo knelt to arrange them as instructed, sliding the third crate a hand's width out of alignment — the signal Brannick required, confirming delivery, confirming that his payment was secured. Her fingers trembled on the wax seal. Somewhere beyond the wall, the ceremony began: a herald's voice, the rustle of six hundred people rising.

She pressed her palm flat against the cool wood and breathed once, twice, the way her mother had taught her — four counts in, seven held, eight out — until the shaking stopped. Then she lifted the lid.

Anselm's pages lay inside like sleeping birds. She took the topmost bundle, slid the certified copy free from beneath her shirt, and set Anselm's true count in its place within her own folio. The swap took eleven seconds. It was the longest work she had ever done.

When she turned, a steward was standing in the doorway.

"You there. Gray shirt." He beckoned. "Hands are wanted for the presentation. Move."

* * *

They put her at the back of the hall, one of eight laborers holding the ceremonial canopy poles during the oath, which meant she stood thirty feet from the register table with a wooden pole in her grip and Anselm's truth hidden against her heart, and there was nowhere in the world she could run.

Chief Historian Ferrant rose. Her voice carried without effort, trained to fill rooms the way ballast fills a hull.

"Honored witnesses of the guilds. For three generations, the Halvard Trading Company has kept faith with this city through honest enumeration. Today we certify the General Register of Laborers, Dependents, and Indentures — complete, corrected, and true."

*Complete, corrected, and true.* Ivo counted the lies in the sentence the way Anselm had taught her to count breaths underwater.

The Kestrel delegation's senior archivist, an old woman with a chain of office and a face like weathered driftwood, stepped forward to receive the volumes. This was the moment. The archivist would inspect the register, verify the seals, and pronounce it accepted — after which no power in Vellmark could reopen it for a decade.

Ivo's plan had been simple and therefore doomed: get the folio onto the table, swap the final volume, withdraw. But the guards had thickened around the table like scar tissue, and the volume in question sat directly before Ferrant's folded hands.

Then Anselm stood up.

The stewards moved to force him down, but he spoke first, loud, cracked, unmistakable. "Before the seal is set — I have an objection to record."

The hall went silent in that particular way of crowds smelling blood. Ferrant's composure flickered, just once, like a lamp in wind. "The census taker Anselm Grell," she said carefully, "is present under examination. He has no standing—"

"I kept the counts." His voice filled the whole painted ceiling. "Every year. The true ones. And I say the register before you is false, and I will say so under any oath this hall can devise."

Chaos, controlled and immediate. Magistrates leaning together. The Kestrel archivist's hand rising for order. Guards converging on Anselm — and for ten seconds, ten glorious seconds, the table stood unwatched.

Ivo dropped the canopy pole.

It clattered, rolled, drew three pairs of eyes for exactly the time she needed. She crossed the distance low and fast, the way she'd crossed flooded cellars, and her hands did the work her fear had rehearsed a hundred times: the last volume lifted, the folio slid beneath it, the cover closed. Eleven seconds. Ten. She was turning away when Ferrant's gaze swept the commotion and snagged on her — a gray shirt moving wrong, a girl-shaped absence of furniture.

"Seize that one," Ferrant said, quietly, which was worse than shouting.

Ivo didn't run. Running was confession. She walked, shoulders bowed, one laborer among many, while behind her the old Kestrel archivist opened the final volume to certify it — and stopped.

"Madam Ferrant." Her voice was dry as old vellum, and it cut through everything. "This final count does not match your presented summary."

"It must," Ferrant said. "There is an error—"

"There is a number." The archivist adjusted her spectacles. In the hush, her voice reached every ear in the hall, and Ivo, frozen at the edge of the crowd, watched her lips shape it. "Four hundred and eleven unregistered dependents. Enumerated, dated, and signed across nine years by your own census officer."

The number fell into the hall like a stone into still water. Four hundred and eleven. Ivo had known the figure since the cellar; she had copied it herself. But hearing it aloud — hearing it spoken into the official air of the Hall of Reckoning, witnessed, irretrievable — was different. It could not be unspoken. Somewhere in Vellmark, four hundred and eleven children who officially did not exist had just been counted.

Guards closed around her from both sides. She saw them coming and understood, with terrible clarity, that the folio was done, its work finished, and whatever happened to the girl who carried it was now a separate matter entirely. She thought of her mother's pearl, traded. Of Brannick's rope around her name. Of Anselm standing straight-backed amid the stewards, watching her with something that was not surprise — something that looked almost like peace.

They took her arms. She did not struggle. Through the press of bodies and the rising roar of the hall, she heard Ferrant's voice, smooth and poisonous, spinning salvage: *"A clerical conspiracy — one man's falsification — we will produce the culprit and the correction—"*

And she heard Anselm answer, clear as a bell over water:

"Yes. Produce me. I alone altered the census. Every entry. Every erasure. On my head be all of it."

Her blood went cold. He was not defending himself. He was building the gallows a floor at a time, and laying the planks across everyone else's escape.

The guards dragged her toward the side door as the magistrates called for order, as the Kestrel archivist demanded the true count be entered into the guild record *verbatim*, as the word *execution* began its quiet journey through the crowd like a current under ice.

Ivo Voss, who did not exist, was carried out of the Hall of Reckoning in custody — victorious, terrified, and about to learn exactly what Anselm intended to pay.
