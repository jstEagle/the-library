## 9. What Anselm Paid

The proclamation went up on the salt-bleached boards by the customs house at dawn, and by the time the gulls had finished arguing over the harbor's scraps, half of Vellmark had read it.

*By order of the Halvard Trading Company: one Anselm Reik, census taker, having falsified the registers of this port through seven years of deliberate alteration, is sentenced to public execution. The register shall be corrected in full. Let all witnesses attend.*

Ivo Voss stood before the board with her hood up and her hands in her sleeves, reading the words until they stopped looking like words and started looking like water — something she was drowning in, letter by letter.

*Seven years of deliberate alteration.*

He had done it. He had actually done it. He had walked into the Company's tribunal with his own confession written out fair and square in his careful, cramped hand, every date supplied, every discrepancy owned, and he had told them the truth in such a way that it became a lie: *I altered the census alone. No diver coached me. No dockhand bribed me. No child ever spoke to me of anything but the weather.* The falsified register — the one Ivo had risked everything to expose — he claimed as his own work, a private madness, a clerk's corruption. And the true counts, the ones now sitting under Kestrel seal where no Company historian could burn them, became merely the evidence of what one man had done.

It was brilliant. It was monstrous. She could not stop seeing how neatly it fit.

Because that was the only version of events that let the Company certify the corrected register without admitting what the corrections meant. If Anselm alone had falsified the rolls — if no pattern of erasure ran back decades through the ledgers like rot through a hull — then the Company was guilty of nothing but hiring badly. The children restored to the count were clerical errors, not stolen lives. The indentures already sold were unfortunate, regrettable, *settled*. And the Kestrel Guild, holding the true archive, could accept the corrected register as a victory of guild oversight over one corrupt official — which meant accepting it at all, signing their names beside the Company's, letting the whole rotten machinery continue with its numbers slightly truer than before.

One dead clerk purchased all of it. Legal cover for everyone.

She turned away from the board and walked toward the execution ground without deciding to.

* * *

They held it on the shingle flats below the north quay, where the tide came in close enough that the Company would not have to dig far for the traditional burial at sea. Scaffolding had been raised overnight; Ivo recognized the Halvard carpenters who built it, men whose names she'd once helped carry lumber for, back when carrying lumber was the whole of her ambition. A platform. A post. A drum.

The crowd gathered the way crowds do — slowly, then all at once. Dockhands still smelling of tar. Pearl-wives in their good shawls. Children hoisted onto shoulders because children are always brought to executions, though nobody will say why. Ivo kept to the edge, near a stack of herring barrels, hood up, one of hundreds, none of them anyone.

Anselm was brought out on foot, no cart, wrists bound in front of him the way they bind a man they expect to walk willingly. He looked smaller than she remembered. In the barracks lamplight, in the flooded cellars, in the frantic hours of copying, he had seemed made entirely of entries — ink and intention, a man constructed line by line. Here on the shingle, stripped of his ledger and his pen, he was just an aging clerk in an unfashionable coat, squinting against the light the way he'd squinted at his columns for thirty years.

A Company steward read the charges. Ivo knew them by heart before he finished the first sentence, because she had spent three sleepless nights imagining them, and hearing them aloud changed nothing except that now they lived outside her skull too.

*Falsification of census records. Alteration of dependent counts. Conversion of registered persons into unregistered property through deliberate omission.*

Every crime listed was real. Every crime listed was his — except that he had not committed them alone, or rather, he had committed them first as an instrument and only afterward, in one impossible week, as their enemy. The steward's voice was flat and administrative, and that was the horror of it: justice performed as bookkeeping.

Then Anselm was permitted to speak, as condemned men sometimes are, and the crowd leaned in the way crowds do, hungry for a curse, a weeping, a confession with blood in it.

He gave them a clerk's speech instead.

"I kept the count," he said, and his voice carried oddly well across the shingle, thin but clear, the voice of a man who had spent his life being heard in silence. "For thirty years I kept the count, and for seven of those years I wrote down fewer people than existed. That is my crime, and mine alone. No one instructed me. No one benefited but me." His eyes moved across the crowd — past the stewards, past the guards, past the Kestrel observers standing stiffly at the perimeter in their grey guild-cloaks — and for one moment Ivo was certain those eyes found her hood, her barrels, her nothing-at-all, and rested there. "But I say this to whoever keeps the true record," he went on, "whoever it may be, wherever it may be kept: numbers do not lie. Only men lie about them. Count your children. Write them down. Make yourselves so heavy with names that no ledger can lift you."

The drum began.

Ivo did not look away. Her mother had taught her that much about the water — you do not look away from the thing that is killing you; you watch it, you learn its rhythm, you survive inside it. So she watched. She watched the whole of it, the ugly ordinary machinery of rope and gravity, and she watched the crowd, which gasped and then went quiet and then, terribly, began to disperse almost at once, because the tide was coming in and there was work at noon.

That was the last cruelty: how small a space his death occupied. Seven years of erased children, one week of smuggling, one pearl given away, one life traded, and the harbor absorbed all of it in the time it took the gulls to settle on the empty scaffolding.

* * *

She stayed until the flats emptied. She stayed until the Company men came with their stretcher and their boat, and she marked, without meaning to, which warehouse gate they carried him through — the third one, the one with the faded anchor painted on the planks — because some part of her had already begun keeping ledgers again, and this was the first entry: *where they take the bodies.*

The debt did not feel like grief. Grief she knew; grief was her mother's empty bunk, grief was the smell of brine in a room that no longer needed drying. This was colder and more exact. It sat in her chest like a figure in a column that refused to balance, and she understood, standing alone on the shingle with the water climbing toward her boots, that it would never balance. Not with mourning. Not with rage. Debts of this kind balanced only one way.

He had chosen her. That was the thing she kept returning to, turning over like a stone. In the tribunal, with rehabilitation offered and recantation demanded, he could have named her. One sentence — *a diver's daughter came to me, she took the notes* — and the Company would have had its conspiracy, its traitor, its seventeen-year-old girl to hang beside him or sell. Instead he built his confession into a wall around her, brick by brick of his own culpability, and left her standing outside it, free, unrecorded, alive.

*I am the entry,* she thought. *I am the last line he wrote.*

And the corrected register — the one bearing the Children of the Orchard, all four hundred and eleven of them restored to the count — was accepted by the Guild two days later, ratified with signatures and seals, legal cover purchased in full. Justice had arrived in Vellmark. It arrived wearing a dead man's name, and it cost exactly one dead man, and everyone agreed it was a fair price, and the warehouses opened at dawn the next morning same as always.

But Ivo had seen the third gate. And she had heard what the smugglers muttered in the taverns since certification day — that the Company's sealed original ledgers, the ones predating even Anselm, the ones that would show the erasure running back fifty years and more, had not been burned after all. They had been moved. Quietly, at night, in crates stamped with the anchor mark, out of the archive and into somewhere no guild archivist had jurisdiction.

Somewhere the truth could still be drowned.

She pulled her hood lower and turned toward the docks, toward the smugglers' quarter, toward the favor she owed a man with soft hands and hard eyes — and she understood that Anselm had bought her a beginning, not an ending, and that the ledger she carried in her head had room for many more lines yet.
