## 10. Signed With No Name

Winter came to Vellmark the way it always did — not as weather but as a color, draining the harbor from green to pewter, frosting the rigging until the ships looked like they had been dipped in sugar and left to set. Ivo Voss stood at the rail of the old fish-market pier, watching gulls argue over a herring head, and did not exist.

She had learned to hold that fact the way her mother once held breath: not as absence, but as a fullness carried inside. The Company's register listed four hundred and eleven souls indentured to the Halvard docks, certified, stamped, archived in triplicate. The Kestrel Guild's corrected count — accepted, after months of dispute, under the seal of an anonymous informant — listed six hundred and two. One hundred and ninety-one names the Company had struck from its rolls over thirty years, children born in the divers' barracks to mothers with no papers and fathers the census never troubled itself to ask about. One hundred and ninety-one debts the Company had converted into transferable property with nothing more than a pen stroke and a clerk's tired signature.

The Guild called the source *the Children of the Orchard*. It was printed now on the archive's index card, in the careful copperplate hand of a Kestrel archivist who would never know it was a phrase borrowed from a drowned woman, a word she'd used for the barracks kids because the courtyard behind the counting-house had one crooked apple tree and the children gathered beneath it like windfall. *My little orchard,* Mara Voss used to say, hauling her daughter out of cold water, wringing salt from her braids. *All of you. Sweetest crop this city ever grew.*

Ivo had submitted the alias with the true counts, wrapped around Anselm's notes like a shroud. She had written the cover letter herself, in a hand she practiced for three weeks until it looked like no hand at all — the neutral script of someone who existed nowhere, which was, by then, exactly what she was.

* * *

She went to the Guild hall on the day of acceptance, not to watch but because she could not stay away, and stood among the clerks and petitioners in the public gallery while the archivists read the findings aloud. The chamber smelled of lamp oil and old vellum. Sunlight came through high windows in dusty columns, and in those columns floated motes that rose and fell like something swimming.

A Kestrel archivist named Odelia Brandt — Ivo had learned her name from listening, from months of standing in queues and taverns where guild clerks drank and complained — read the corrected count into the record. Six hundred and two. The number rang against the stone walls and would not be explained away, though the Halvard representatives tried, their voices climbing through appeals and procedural objections like men wading into deepening water. But Anselm's notes were meticulous. Thirty years of true counts, cross-checked against baptismal records from the harbor chapel, against burial registries, against the barracks' own ration tallies, which the cooks had kept honestly even when the clerks did not. Children do not eat less because ink says they are fewer.

The Guild ruled the register falsified. The ruling did not free anyone — law is a slow animal, and property law slowest of all — but it made the falsification a matter of public record, and public record, Ivo had learned, is the one thing companies fear more than fire. A ledger can be rebalanced. A certified lie cannot be un-certified without admitting it was a lie.

And Anselm — Anselm had already confessed. Publicly, before witnesses, before the Company historians themselves: he alone, he swore, had altered the census, struck the names, built the false register out of private malice or madness, and the Company, magnanimous, had merely failed to detect his fraud. His execution had been scheduled and carried out with terrible efficiency in the autumn, on the customs green, before a crowd that came expecting spectacle and got instead a thin man in a clerk's coat who walked to the block as if walking to his desk, who looked out over the crowd once — only once — as if searching for a face he knew would not be there, because the face he was looking for officially did not exist.

Ivo had been in that crowd. She had worn a fishwife's shawl and a stranger's boots, and she had watched, and she had not looked away, because looking away would have been the second erasure and she had promised herself there would not be two.

He had taken the whole weight onto himself so that the truth could enter history clean. If the register's corruption was one man's crime, then the correction was simply the record repairing itself — no conspiracy to prosecute, no informants to hunt, no trail leading back to a seventeen-year-old girl who had traded a pearl for passage and a favor for betrayal and escaped through flooded cellars on lungs her mother had trained in her before she could swim. Justice arrived wearing a dead man's name, and Ivo understood, standing in the gallery now with the ruling ringing in her ears, that she was the debt Anselm had chosen to pay. There was no settling it. There was only carrying it forward.

* * *

Afterward she walked the long way home, past the counting-house, past the customs green where the scaffold had already been dismantled and the grass was growing back with the blind indifference of grass. The orchard tree stood bare in the winter courtyard, its one crooked trunk black against the sky, and someone had tied strips of cloth to its lower branches — red, blue, undyed gray — small rag ribbons that snapped in the harbor wind. She stopped at the fence and counted them. Nineteen. She wondered who had begun it. Perhaps a mother. Perhaps one of the older divers who had warned her to stop counting and then, when the counts became public, had gone out in the night with strips of their own clothing and tied them on, one for each child they remembered and could finally say they remembered.

She touched the fence rail. She did not go in. The barracks belonged to other people now, or to the same people wearing different expressions, and besides, there were things she could no longer afford — among them the luxury of being seen where she used to live.

Because that was the shape of her freedom: officially, Ivo Voss did not exist. No record, no owner, no name. The Company's certified register held four hundred and eleven souls and she was not among them; the Guild's corrected count held six hundred and two, and she was not among those either, because the alias she had chosen was not a person but a collective, an orchard, a hundred and ninety-one names none of which were hers. She had erased herself to save everyone else, and the erasure held. The smugglers she had double-crossed had sold her description to the Company stewards — young woman, dark hair, diver's shoulders, scar on the left palm — but descriptions require a registry to land in, and there was no registry that admitted she had ever been born. The stewards searched for a ghost and found nothing, because the Company's own falsified books were the best hiding place in Vellmark. They had taught her the trick themselves.

Free, then. Unrecorded. Able to walk any street, board any ship that would take an unnamed deckhand, slip any border where a face and a coin were the only documents required. And bound — bound more tightly than any indenture — to the ledger she kept in her head.

It was not written down anywhere. That was the point. Paper burns; furnaces eat crates of a dead man's life's work; archives can be bought, sealed, quietly amended by the next generation of careful men. But a ledger carried in memory, copied nightly from one living mind to another — she had begun teaching it to a girl named Petra, twelve years old, quick, sharp-eyed, one of the orchard's last windfalls, too young to have been struck from the rolls but old enough now to learn why — such a ledger could not be seized. It had no location. It weighed nothing and everything.

Every night, before sleep, Ivo recited it: names first, then mothers, then dates, then the small human details the official records never held — who whistled through the gap in his teeth, who was afraid of gulls, who could hold her breath four minutes in green water and laughed doing it. One hundred and ninety-one entries. She had never met most of them. Many were grown or gone or drowned by now, scattered inland with the mothers who 'went away,' dead in the same cold water that took her own mother. It didn't matter. A ledger does not require acquaintance. It requires only accuracy, and refusal.

* * *

On the pier, the gulls finished with the herring head and lifted off all at once, wheeling up into the pale sky like a handful of thrown coins. Ivo pulled her collar tighter and started toward the fish-market, where Petra would be waiting at the corner by the salt barrels, impatient, hungry, ready for the evening's recitation — entry forty through sixty tonight, the ones born in the year of the great storm, whose mothers' names Ivo had recovered from a chapel priest with a taste for schnapps and gossip.

Halfway down the pier she stopped.

At the far end, where the pilings went green with weed, a man stood looking out at the water — a narrow-shouldered man in a plain coat, and for one absurd heartbeat her whole body said *Anselm*, grief playing its old trick. But it wasn't him. He turned, and she saw he was a Kestrel archivist — she recognized the gray sleeve-band — and he was looking directly at her, and in his hand he held a single sheet of paper, folded once, extended slightly outward, the way one offers bread to a bird one does not wish to frighten.

"You're the one who ties the ribbons," he called across the distance, his voice carrying flat and clear over the cold water. "Odelia Brandt sent me. We have questions the archive can't answer — and a request." He paused, and the wind moved between them like something alive. "There are other registers, miss. Other cities. Other orchards."

Ivo Voss — nonexistent, unrecorded, free — stood at the end of the pier with the whole winter harbor behind her, and considered how heavy a nameless life could become, and how much heavier still the names of others.

She began to walk toward him.
