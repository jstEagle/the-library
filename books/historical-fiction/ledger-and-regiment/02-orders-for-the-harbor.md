## 2. Orders for the Harbor

The notice went up on the customs house door on a Tuesday, which I remember because Tuesday was the day I bought ink, and I had to stand in the rain reading it with a fresh bottle of oak-gall going cold in my pocket.

BY ORDER OF THE LORDS COMMISSIONERS OF THE ADMIRALTY, it began, in letters sized to be read from across the street by men who could not read. ALL ABLE-BODIED SEAMEN AND PERSONS ACCUSTOMED TO THE SEA ARE HEREBY IMPRESSED INTO HIS MAJESTY'S SERVICE.

There was more, but that was the heart of it. The harbor had been growing quieter for weeks — ships lingering at anchor, masters paying off crews early, the taverns along the Quay full of men suddenly careful about which door they used. I had noticed it the way you notice weather: as atmosphere, not event. Now here was the event, nailed to a door in wet ink, and Wexbridge was expected to make sense of it before supper.

I stood there long enough that the constable on duty gave me the look he gives women who loiter, and I moved along, and it wasn't until I was three streets away that I understood what I had actually been reading. Not the press notice itself. Beneath it, smaller, pasted up with less ceremony, was a second paper:

ALL CHARTS, SURVEYS, COASTAL PLATES, AND HYDROGRAPHIC MATERIALS WITHIN THE PORT DISTRICT ARE HEREBY SUBJECT TO REQUISITION FOR THE PUBLIC SERVICE. HOLDERS OF SUCH MATERIALS SHALL RENDER THEM TO THE NAVAL STOREKEEPER UPON DEMAND.

*Upon demand.* As though charts were turnips. As though two years of a dead man's patience could be gathered up like a harvest tax and carried off in a cart.

I walked home fast, and I will confess something here that shames me slightly: my first thought was not for the atlas. It was for the plates. There is a difference, if you have ever made either. An atlas is an argument; a plate is the proof. Aldous Hartwell had cut every copper himself in his last three years, when his hands were still steady enough and his eyesight failing in that particular way that makes a man work closer and closer to the metal until his nose nearly touches it. Twelve plates, each one the coast from Point Adery down to the Snare Rocks, engraved so fine you could count the soundings like sheep in a field. They sat in their cedar chest under the floor of my sleeping loft, wrapped in wool and tallow-cloth, and they were — there is no modest way to put this — worth more than the building they slept beneath.

And they were not mine. That was the other thing I carried home through the rain, along with the ink.

* * *

Mr. Quill came to the workshop that afternoon, which he did rarely enough that I knew it was serious before he'd removed his hat. Quill was my patron in the way that a dam is a patron of a river: he contained me, directed me, and occasionally let a little of me through for the benefit of the mills downstream. He kept the stationer's shop on Candle Street, sold our prints and instruments, advanced me credit against sales, and had known Aldous Hartwell for thirty years without once, to my knowledge, calling him by anything warmer than "Hartwell."

He set his hat on the bench, looked at the half-finished fair copy pinned to my drawing board, and said, "You've heard."

"I've heard."

"The storekeeper's clerks start their rounds Thursday." He picked up a loupe from my tray, examined it as if he suspected it of something, and set it down. "They'll come to the shop first. Then here. They have lists, Hazel. Lists drawn up before the notices even went up, which tells you how long this has been in preparation."

"What exactly do they take?"

"Everything hydrographic." He said it the way a physician says the name of a disease. "Charts, drafts, field books, plates. Anything showing depths, channels, anchorages, tides. Which is to say—" he gestured, with one hand, at the entire room— "that."

The room. The drafting table scarred by forty years of compass points. The shelves of journals in Aldous's cramped hand, tide tables going back to before I was born. The fair copy of the atlas itself, three-quarters finished, lying open on the board with its final plate still drying in the press room upstairs. Everything I had measured my life against for six years, itemized in advance on some clerk's list in London by someone who had never seen the water it described.

"They can't," I said. "It's private commission. Master Hartwell was engaged by the Trinity House brethren, not the Admiralty. There are letters—"

"There are letters." Quill sat down slowly on the stool by the window, which was an old man's way of sitting and told me he'd climbed the hill from Candle Street slower than usual. "Hazel, I'm going to say something to you, and I want you to hear all of it before you argue, because arguing is what Hartwell would do, and Hartwell is not here to suffer the consequences of it. When the Crown decides a thing is state property, it does not matter who paid for it or who owns it or what the letters say. What matters is whether anyone is willing to go to prison over the question. Do you understand?"

I understood. I hated understanding it.

"The plates," I said. "Would they know about the plates?"

Quill was quiet for a moment. Outside, a cart rattled past carrying — I could see it through the window — timber and rope, the harbor's commerce converting itself to war's materials in real time, as though the town had been waiting years for permission.

"Everyone knows about the plates," he said finally. "Hartwell talked about them for thirty years. He showed them to half the merchants in this port. If I know they exist, the man drawing up lists in London knows they exist." He rubbed his eyes. "What I don't know — what nobody outside this room knows — is where you keep them. That is the only advantage you have, and I would advise you to use it tonight."

"Tonight."

"Thursday is two days off. But rounds begin before dawn, and clerks with warrants are punctual in a way artists never are." He stood, took his hat, and paused at the door with his back to me. "Hartwell and I disagreed about many things, but we agreed on this much: the work outlives the politics. Whatever you decide, decide it as a mapmaker, not as a loyal subject. Loyal subjects lose everything and get a receipt."

He left. I stood in the middle of my inheritance — such as it was, nameless as it was — and listened to the rain start again on the skylight.

* * *

Here is what I did that night, and I record it plainly because I am proud of it and because pride was in short supply afterward.

The workshop occupied the ground floor of a narrow building whose back wall abutted the old ropewalk — that long shed where they spun cable, four hundred feet of dim interior smelling of tar and hemp dust, disused since the new walk opened nearer the quay. The back wall of the workshop, behind the shelf of bound journals, was stone below and lath above, and the lath section, I had discovered my first year of apprenticeship, hid a cavity. Aldous had built it. Of course he had. A man who spent his life recording every depth and shoal of a coastline does not leave his own house unmapped. There was a space between the inner lath and the outer stone wall, eighteen inches deep, running six feet, accessible through a panel disguised as the backing of the journal shelf, and inside it, when I first found it, I had found the things Aldous considered truly valuable: a strongbox of guineas, a pistol he never cleaned, and a letter from Trinity House thanking him for services rendered in 1791.

I moved the guineas to the flour crock. The pistol I left where it was; it seemed to belong to the wall somehow.

Then I brought up the cedar chest from under the loft floor — twelve plates, each weighing more than a cat, each wrapped in its wool — and carried them one at a time through the dark, holding my breath at the creak of every third floorboard as though the boards might inform on me.

It took until well past midnight. My shoulders burned. Twice I stopped in the blackness of the workshop and simply stood, listening to the harbor sounds — the bell buoy off the point, a ship's dog barking somewhere on the far side of the water — and felt the strangeness of what I was doing. Hiding maps from my own country. A girl who had never broken so much as the sabbath, now committing, in her nightgown, whatever crime it was called when you declined to hand over your dead master's life's work to men with receipts.

But here is the thought that kept my hands moving, and I offer it because it was the truest thing I knew that night: the plates were not information. Not yet. They were labor. Twelve coppers of patient engraving — soundings, leads, sandbars, the slow accretion of a decade of small boats and wet boots and Aldous Hartwell squinting into drizzle. The Admiralty wanted to seize knowledge. What I was hiding was not knowledge. It was craft. And craft, I reasoned, in the manner of the newly lawless reasoning everything through to suit themselves, belongs to the craftsman until it is published — and it had not been published. I was not concealing state property. I was protecting an unfinished book.

That the distinction would not survive contact with a naval clerk, I already suspected. But suspicion is not conviction, and the plates went behind the wall.

I replaced the panel, restacked the journals in front of it — Aldous's tide tables, twenty-two volumes, arranged by year exactly as he'd left them, because a rearranged shelf is the loudest confession in any room — and swept the floor of the telltale wool fibers and cedar shavings. Then I sat at the drawing board in the dark and worked by feel on the fair copy, ruling a border line I didn't need, just to have my hands doing honest work while my mind did dishonest arithmetic.

Twelve plates. One fair copy, unfinished. One set of field notes. One apprentice, unregistered, unnamed in law, whose connection to any of it existed nowhere except in her own stubborn memory and the habit of a town that called her "Hartwell's Hazel."

If they took the atlas, they took the proof that I had ever been anything at all.

I ruled the line straight anyway. It was, at least, one thing in Wexbridge that would be true by morning.

* * *

Thursday came grey and early, as promised. I was at the drawing board when the knock arrived — two knocks, courteous, evenly spaced, the knock of a man who expected the door to be answered rather than forced — and I opened it to find a clerk.

I will describe him properly, because he deserves it and because I misjudged him then in ways I would spend months correcting. He was perhaps five-and-thirty, dressed in good sober wool with nothing military about him but the precision, and he held his hat in both hands in front of him like a hymnal. His hands were remarkably clean. I noticed this because everyone else's hands in Wexbridge that week were dirty — tar, paint, rope, gunpowder — and this man's were pale and immaculate, as though he worked entirely in a medium that left no trace.

"Miss?" He consulted a card. "This is the late Mr. Aldous Hartwell's establishment? Formerly?"

"It is."

"My name is Fitch. Ambrose Fitch, clerk to the Naval Storekeeper's office." He produced papers, unfolded them with a crisp economy that suggested he had folded and unfolded them many times, and held them out. "I understand Mr. Hartwell is deceased. My condolences. I'm here regarding the requisition notice — you'll have seen it posted. Charts, surveys, plates, and related materials. I'm instructed to compile an inventory."

"An inventory," I repeated.

"Of holdings. Whether or not removal proves necessary is a separate determination." He smiled. It was a good smile, calibrated, the smile of a man who has learned that people confess to the pleasant. "May I come in?"

I stepped aside. He entered the workshop, and I watched him do what I would learn he always did: he read the room. His eyes went over the drawing board, the shelves, the press, the drying rack, the jars of pigment ranked like soldiers, and I felt each glance land somewhere on my skin.

"A working studio," he said approvingly. "You're the apprentice, I take it. The — Miss Hartwell?" He said the name lightly, a test balloon.

"Hazel," I said. "Just Hazel. Apprentice to the late master."

"Just Hazel," he echoed, writing something in a small notebook, and the scratch of his pencil was the most frightening sound I had heard all week. "And the master's papers? Field books, correspondence, draft charts? The inventory requires a full accounting."

"The master's effects are largely unsettled," I said. "He died without — arrangements being complete. What remains is family business, pending settlement."

Something flickered across his face — not disappointment, exactly. Interest. The interest of a man who has fished these waters before and knows what a vague answer weighs.

"Naturally," he said. "Settlement takes time. Though I should mention — purely for your planning — that the office distinguishes between materials under dispute and materials merely awaiting tidying. Only the former enjoy protection. The latter fall under the requisition." He tucked away his notebook, replaced his hat, and bowed slightly at the door. "I'll call again, Miss Just-Hazel. These inventories do tend to require several visits."

He went down the steps and along the quay, and I watched him until he turned at the corner, and I swear to you he did not hurry, and did not look back, and walked as though the entire harbor district were already inventoried and filed.

Behind the journal shelf, twelve copper plates sat in the dark, saying nothing, which is the great virtue of copper.

I barred the door, went back to the drawing board, and found my hand shaking too badly to rule a straight line.

Which was when I remembered the strongbox under the loft floor — emptied of its guineas the night before, but not empty. At the bottom of it, beneath the false bottom Aldous had surely built for exactly the purposes I was only beginning to imagine, lay the papers he had never shown me. And among them, I now recalled with a cold drop in my stomach, one letter, sealed in wax gone amber with age, addressed in his own hand to no one — or rather, addressed, in the fashion of dead men's secrets, to whoever came next.

To whoever came next. I fetched the lamp.
