## 3. A Letter Under Seal

My master kept his secrets the way he kept his instruments: in specific places, wrapped in specific cloths, with a strong opinion about who should never touch them. Aldous Hartwell had a box for his brass, a drawer for his correspondence, and a locked chest under the stairs that I was told, on my first day of apprenticeship, contained "nothing that concerns you." He said it without malice. That was the trouble with Aldous — he could make an entire life sound like nothing that concerned you.

Two years dead, and he was still doing it.

I had put off the sorting through the winter, telling myself there was no hurry, that grief deserved its own season like anything else. This was a lie dressed respectably, the way lies are when they wear mourning. The truth was simpler: I did not want to open that chest. Opening it meant admitting the workshop was mine to sort rather than his to return to. It meant being the one left holding everything.

But spring had come to Wexbridge with the smell of tar and new rope, and Mr. Quill had begun asking, in his gentle way, whether I intended to catalogue the master's effects for the probate clerk, and whether I understood that clerks ask harder questions than friends do. And the harbor — well. The harbor had opinions now. Press notices nailed to the Custom House door. Men walking the quays with ledgers instead of cargo. A world reordering itself around me, and me sitting on a chest full of paper.

So on a grey Tuesday, with rain ticking at the skylight like a patient creditor, I brought the chest up into the light.

* * *

The lock surrendered to the key that hung, all along, on the same nail as the apron. Of course it did. Aldous trusted locks the way he trusted weather — as a formality. Inside: survey journals going back thirty years, bound in oilcloth and labeled in his square carpenter's hand. A tin of drawing instruments I already knew by heart. A woman's miniature portrait, painted sometime before the wars, of a face I did not recognize and did not ask about while he lived. And beneath the journals, wrapped in sailcloth that smelled of the estuary mud we'd both loved him despite —

A letter. Sealed, but not posted. Wax unbroken, pressed with his signet: the Hartwell mark, a stylized lead line over water. On the face of it, in that same square hand:

*For my son, when he asks after me.*

I sat with it a long while. Rain, tick. Tick. Rain.

Elias Vane. The name arrived in my head wearing its old coat: the apprentice before me, the one whose letters from sea I had read aloud in this very room because Aldous's eyes were failing and his pride was not. I remembered those letters better than I remembered most scripture. Elias wrote the way sailors sing — half complaint, half love song. He'd written about the stars as if they were personal acquaintances. And then, six years ago, the letters stopped, and Aldous told me only that his son had taken a commission with the French, and that some doors close from the inside.

An enemy officer. That was what the Admiralty would call him now, if they called him anything at all. And here was his father, two years in his grave, still keeping a letter for a son who might never ask after him.

I should have set it aside. Marked it, catalogued it, done the honest workmanlike thing. But I have confessed already that I sat with it, and a sealed letter is a terrible companion for a person with ink-stained fingers and an idle hour. The wax cracked under my thumb before I had fully decided to break it, which is how most of my sins begin — halfway through before the decision arrives.

* * *

It was not, in the main, a father's letter. That was the first shock. I had braced myself for tenderness, for apology, for the raw unfinished sentences of a proud man trying to say sorry across an ocean. Instead Aldous had written like the surveyor he was — coordinates, bearings, a fix on something he wanted his son to know the position of precisely.

*Elias,* it began. *If you are reading this, you have asked after me, and someone has answered honestly, and I am past embarrassment.*

*I leave you the atlas. Not the plates — Quill will see to the printing, and printers have their ways — but the fair copy, finished in my hand or my apprentice's, whichever of us outlasts the other. You will understand why I say it that way. Hazel has more of my craft in her hands than I ever taught her, and less of my patience, which is as it should be.*

I read that sentence four times. My eyes kept snagging on it like a keel on a sandbar. *Less of my patience, which is as it should be.* Two years dead and the man could still make me sit down hard. I looked around the workshop as though he might be leaning in the doorway, enjoying the effect. Nothing. Just the rain, and the smell of linseed, and the little brass hygrometer he'd bought in Rotterdam that had never once agreed with the weather.

*There is a second matter,* the letter went on, *and I write it here because paper keeps what mouths cannot.*

*You will find among my papers a deed, old, concerning the tidal flats below Harrow Point — the saltings, the oyster beds, the strip of marsh where I drowned my boots every spring of your childhood. Your grandfather held it before me. It is signed by four men, and one of the names is ours. Hartwell. We hold a share in that ground, boy, and have these forty years, and I have said nothing of it because saying it would have made it real, and real things invite lawyers, and lawyers invite questions, and questions — you remember how I am with questions.*

*Now there is talk of war again, and the Navy has begun looking at coastlines the way misers look at purses. If they come asking, know what is yours. Do not let them take it for a formality and a receipt.*

*Your father, who was wrong about more things than he will admit, A.H.*

I read it twice more, then a third time standing up, because sitting felt suddenly unsafe, as though the floor itself had been resurveyed without my knowledge.

A deed. The saltings. Our name on it.

* * *

Let me explain what the tidal flats are, since the Navy would soon enough explain it their way. Below Harrow Point, where the estuary opens its throat to the channel, lie three miles of saltings and mudflat and oyster ground — worthless-looking, beautiful at low tide in the way only mud can be beautiful, gold and pewter and mirror. Every soul in Wexbridge knew them as common ground. Nobody owned the tide; that was the whole theology of the place. Except apparently somebody did, or a piece of somebody did, and that somebody's surname was stitched into my apron.

And now the press notices were on the Custom House door, and requisition officers walked the quay with their neat ledgers, and somewhere in London men were drawing lines on charts of exactly this coast, deciding where the guns would go and where the transports would land and which mudflats wanted fortifying. Land the Navy needs does not stay land long. It becomes *ground*, then *position*, then *property of the Crown*, and the people who thought they stood on it discover they were only ever permitted.

I did the arithmetic any sensible person would do. A deed naming Hartwell. A Navy wanting the flats. Those two facts were not neighbors; they were a fuse and a flame.

But underneath the dread — and I will be honest, because a mapmaker who fudges her soundings deserves whatever wrecks follow — underneath it, something else stirred. Something warm and ugly and hungry.

Hartwell. I had carried the name fourteen years without ever owning so much as a spoon of it. Aldous took me in off the charity rolls when I was eleven, taught me the trade, called me his apprentice, and never once — not once in fourteen years — signed the papers that would have made me his daughter. I asked him once, when I was sixteen, why not. He was cleaning his theodolite, and he said, "Paper doesn't make a family, Hazel," and went on cleaning, and I was too young and too grateful to answer that paper also protects one. I learned better later. Paper is often the only thing standing between a person and the world's opinion of them.

If there was a deed, and if Hartwell was on it, then there was a version of the world in which the name fit me legally, provably, in the way the Admiralty and the registry and every clerk with clean hands would have to acknowledge. Belonging, in ink. For a girl born nameless on the wrong side of respectable, that is not a small thing. That is the whole horizon.

Which is precisely why it frightened me. Gifts that arrive wrapped in wax seals and dead men's handwriting, addressed to sons across enemy lines, mentioning deeds nobody knew existed — such gifts do not come free. They come with the bill folded inside.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what I do when the world tilts: I worked. There is a plate of Harrow Point in progress, the third revision, and I inked its contour lines until my wrist ached, each line a fact I could trust. The flats appeared under my pen as they always did — low-water marks, oyster beds, the creek that winds through the saltings like a signature. I had surveyed that ground with Aldous himself, the last spring of his life. I knew its every mood.

He died there, in the end. Caught by the tide in the March fog, doing what he loved, which is either the best death available or the most stubborn, depending on the day I'm having. They found his theodolite standing upright in the mud like a monument to a man who wouldn't stop measuring.

Had he known about the deed then? Had he been surveying his own inheritance when the water came? Or was it coincidence, the way most catastrophes dress themselves as coincidence?

I put down the pen. The rain had stopped. Through the skylight, the evening was arranging itself in greys and rose-golds over the harbor, and the masts of the merchantmen stood up thin against it, fewer masts than there had been a month ago. Ships leaving. Men leaving. The harbor emptying itself toward whatever was coming.

I got the letter out again and read the middle section properly this time, the part about *among my papers*. Among his papers. Which were, as of that moment, spread across my workbench in various stages of sorted, boxed, and ignored. Somewhere in that drift of correspondence and journal pages, if Aldous hadn't lied — and he lied rarely, but thoroughly when he did — lay a deed to three miles of tidal flat with my name's future written on it.

Or it didn't lie anywhere, and the letter was an old man's wishful drafting, a fiction he'd sealed and never sent because even he couldn't quite believe it.

Only one way to find out, and the finding-out was the dangerous part. Because once I knew, I couldn't unknow. Once I touched that deed, I became a person connected to it, and persons connected to things the Navy wants develop a habit of appearing in other people's ledgers.

I sorted until dark. Correspondence, 1790s. Correspondence, 1801–03. Survey journals, northern coast. Receipts. More receipts. Aldous kept receipts the way other men keep grudges — exhaustively, and against all reason. No deed. No parchment, no ribbon, no great seal. By lamplight I went through the boxes twice, then a third time, faster, less honest, shaking folders like a woman hunting a lost glove.

Nothing.

I sat back on the stool and laughed — once, short, not amused. Of course. He'd hidden it. He hid everything worth having; it was practically his devotional practice. The chest under the stairs had been one hiding place, and this letter another, and the deed itself was doubtless behind a third door, waiting for whoever thought like Aldous Hartwell.

Which meant thinking like a man who distrusted lawyers, hated questions, and believed every important thing should be kept where only the deserving — or the desperate — would ever look.

I looked at the false wall behind the plan chest.

We had built it together, three years back, to store the master plates of the atlas away from damp and light. A panel of lath and plaster, painted to match, indistinguishable from the wall unless you knew where the seam ran — and I knew, because my hands had plastered it. Aldous had stood back afterward, dusted his palms, and pronounced it "adequate," which in his currency was a medal.

I hadn't opened it since the night I hid the atlas plates from the requisition notice. But that was charts — state business, arguably. This would be different. This would be me, choosing to go looking for a dead man's secret about land and names and inheritance, alone, at night, knowing that whatever I found would attach itself to me like a burr.

The lamp burned steady. Outside, a watchman's bell rang the hour, ten strokes, unhurried, indifferent to the dramas of apprentices.

"Well," I said aloud, to the workshop, to the chest, to the man two years gone who was still managing my affairs from beyond all reasonable jurisdiction, "nothing that concerns me. As usual."

I moved the plan chest.

Behind the false wall, in the oilcloth where the master plates slept, there was a space at the back that hadn't been there before. In it: a packet, flat, tied with the faded blue ribbon I recognized from his survey satchel, the ribbon his wife — Elias's mother, the one subject Aldous never once sounded — had used to bind her garden journals.

Inside the packet, stiff and yellowed and older than my whole acquaintance with this house: a deed of conveyance, four signatures along its foot, and the third one down, in ink gone brown as old seawater, unmistakable even to a stranger —

**Hartwell.**

Not Aldous's hand. His father's, I guessed, from the flourish. But the name. Our name. *A* name, anyway, and for the first time in my life it looked like it might be made out to me.

I stood there a long time, holding three miles of tidal flat in two hands, listening to the harbor breathe, and understanding with perfect clarity that I was no longer merely an orphaned apprentice finishing her master's atlas.

I was a holder of something the Navy wanted. And I had just become findable.
