## 4. The Clerk with Clean Hands

There is a particular pleasure in a well-kept ledger, and I will confess it plainly: I love the moment before an entry is made, when every column stands balanced and every account is honest because nothing has yet been written in it. Ambition, debt, treason — all of these are merely ink awaiting its line. My trade is to provide the line.

I had been three days in Wexbridge when I called upon the late Master Aldous Hartwell's workshop, and I had spent those three days doing what I always do first: reading. Not letters, not papers — those come later, when they have been properly surrendered — but the town itself. A harbor town is a ledger written in weather and rope. The press notices had gone up on Tuesday; by Thursday morning three taverns stood quieter than their landlords liked, and the chandler on Quay Street had sold out of sea-chests at prices that would have made a bishop blush. War does not arrive like thunder. It arrives like tide, and everyone pretends to be surprised by water.

The Hartwell workshop sat at the top of Foremast Lane, which is to say it sat where the mud gave up trying and the cobblestones began. I noted, as I climbed, that the step had been freshly scrubbed. That told me something. Grief lets a doorstep go; industry does not. Whoever lived there was still working.

I knocked — three measured raps, neither timid nor imperious. One learns early that a clerk who bangs on doors gets lies shouted through them, while a clerk who knocks politely gets invited into the room where the lies are kept warm.

She opened it herself. I had expected an old housekeeper or perhaps the master's widow, but it was a young woman with ink to her second knuckle and the particular stillness of someone who has already decided how this conversation will go. Her eyes did the arithmetic of me in one pass: my coat, my satchel, my boots too clean for the lane. I watched her arrive at *Admiralty* and then, a half-second later, at *trouble*.

"Miss Hartwell?" I asked.

"Miss Hartwell is dead," she said pleasantly. "Two years past. You've the wrong door."

It was a good lie — quick, unembarrassed, delivered with the mild courtesy of a woman correcting a stranger's directions. But it was also the wrong lie, and I made a note of that, the way one notes a chart drawn by a hand more confident than accurate. She had lied about the name rather than the papers. A person protecting documents says *there are no papers*. A person protecting a name says *there is no Hartwell*. Which meant the name mattered to her more than the papers did, and names, unlike papers, cannot be hidden behind a false panel. Names are held in registers, in parish books, in the memories of landladies and publicans. Names can be looked up.

"I beg your pardon," I said, and bowed slightly. "Then I have the honor of addressing his apprentice. Miss — forgive me, I wasn't given a Christian name."

"Hazel," she said. Just that. No surname offered, which was its own small confession. "And you are?"

"Ambrose Fitch," I said. "Clerk to the Commissioners of the Admiralty." I produced my warrant with both hands, as one presents a calling card rather than a weapon, though of course it is both. She read it with more patience than most magistrates bother to show, and handed it back with two fingers, as one returns a borrowed umbrella.

"You'd better come in, Mr. Fitch," she said. "It's cold on the step."

* * *

Her master's parlor was exactly what I had hoped it would be and exactly what I feared. I hoped for disorder — a grieving household lets things scatter — and instead found order so complete it had the quality of a statement. The drafting table stood squared to the window light. The instruments lay in their case, each in its velvet groove. On the walls hung proofs of coastal plates, and I confess I stopped before one — the Wexbridge roads themselves, soundings marked in a hand so fine it seemed the pen had been afraid to wake the paper.

"Do you know," I said, "that in thirty years' time, men will fight over these sheets the way their fathers fought over saints' bones?"

"They'll fight over them sooner than that," she said. "That's rather why you're here."

I turned. She had not sat down. She stood by the hearth with her hands folded — not defensively, I noted, but in the attitude of a woman keeping them clean, which struck me then as amusing and strikes me now, looking back, as prophetic.

"You have me at an advantage, Miss Hazel."

"I doubt that very much."

"Then let us be efficient, since efficiency flatters us both." I took from my satchel the requisition — fair-copied, sealed, correct in every particular, for I am nothing if not correct in particulars — and laid it on the drafting table, squaring its edges to the wood as she squared her instruments. "By order of the Lords Commissioners, all charts, surveys, field books, plates, and papers belonging to the late Aldous Hartwell, surveyor, are hereby required for the use of His Majesty's Navy. The war requires his coastlines. It is, I am sure you'll agree, a fine thing for a mapmaker's work to matter so much."

"It would be finer," she said, "if he were alive to be flattered."

"Dead men's work serves the living. That is the whole of civilization, when you compress it." I smiled to show the remark was philosophical rather than cruel. She did not smile back, but she inclined her head a fraction, acknowledging the point the way one acknowledges a well-played shot in a game one intends to win anyway.

"And the papers?" she asked.

"That is why I'm standing in your parlor."

"We have none." She said it easily. "Master Hartwell's effects were settled two years ago. What he left — his clothing, some books, his instruments — went to his family. His working materials were his own affair, and he was not a man who kept affairs. He kept *soundings*. Numbers go into the atlas; paper comes out again. There's the atlas, or the plates toward it, and those I'm finishing under commission to Mr. Quill, the stationer, who holds the copyright and will tell you the same."

Every sentence true. Every sentence arranged. I have heard a thousand lies in my career and I want to be clear about something: the lies that defeat me are never the elaborate ones. Elaborate lies tangle. It is the true sentences, selected and ordered like stones across a stream, that cost the Crown real money. She had not said *there are no other papers*. She had said *we have none*, present tense, attached to a definition of *papers* narrow enough to sail a frigate through.

"His family," I said. "You mentioned family. I understood Master Hartwell's relations to be — complicated."

"He had a son."

"Who is, I believe, presently serving elsewhere." I let *elsewhere* do its work. She let it pass without flinching, which was its own answer: she knew precisely where Elias Vane served, and had known when she chose the word.

"The son took nothing," she said. "They were estranged. Whatever papers existed, Elias wouldn't know, and wouldn't want."

"Perhaps. Estrangement is a form of inheritance too. Half the disputes I settle began with two people agreeing never to speak again and then speaking only through solicitors." I moved along the wall of proofs, unhurried, letting my shadow cross each sheet. "Tell me about the atlas, Miss Hazel. How far along?"

"Three plates unfinished. Perhaps four months' work."

"And after you? Who finishes it then?"

"No one. It finishes with me or not at all."

"There it is," I said warmly. "Do you see? That is precisely the sentence that brings me to your door. Work that ends with one person is work the state must collect, because states do not trust endings. We prefer continuities we administer ourselves." I tapped the requisition. "So. If you would be good enough to show me the plates as they stand — purely for inventory, purely for receipt — I can leave you a certified copy of this order, and matters proceed civilly."

"Civilly," she repeated.

"Miss Hazel, I am a clerk. Civility is not my manner; it is my method." And here I will admit what I admitted to no one in that room: I liked her. I liked the level way she weighed me, the absence of flutter, the craftsman's contempt for fuss. It is rare to meet an opponent who understands that the conversation is the contest. Most people think the contest begins when soldiers arrive.

She considered the requisition for a long moment. Then she said, "The plates aren't here."

"No?"

"They're at Quill's premises. Under lock, since the press notices went up. Mr. Quill thought — and I agreed — that with strangers asking questions in the town, commissioned work was safer under the stationer's seal than in a lodging above a workshop."

Another stone across the stream. True, probably — I had indeed been asking questions in the town, and word of my inquiries would have traveled faster than I did — and unfalsifiable, and neatly relocating the burden. If I wished to inspect the plates, I must call upon Quill, an old and well-connected tradesman, thereby announcing myself formally to half the merchants of Wexbridge. She had turned my politeness into her fortification. I could compel the inspection; compulsion would cost me the cooperation of every shopkeeper between here and the registry, and a clerk who spends his welcome in the first week has nothing left for the tenth.

"Quill keeps a careful shop," I said. "I'll call on him tomorrow, with his permission and yours."

"With mine, certainly," she said. "Mr. Quill answers to no one else's."

We regarded one another across the drafting table, and for a moment — I record it honestly, because my reports are meant to be honest even where my methods are not — I believe we understood each other completely. She knew I did not believe her. I knew she knew. Neither of us said so, because saying so would end the game, and the game was the only channel through which either of us might learn what the other actually wanted.

"I'll leave you the order," I said, sliding it across. "Receipt it when convenient. And Miss Hazel —" I paused at the door. "One small thing. Purely clerical. When you wrote *Miss Hartwell is dead*, you spoke of him in the present tense twice afterward. *He was not a man who kept affairs.* *His working materials were his own.* Grief does that. It keeps the dead among us in our grammar." I smiled. "It was a lovely tribute. I only note it because noting things is my trade."

The color left her face by a degree — a draftsman's increment, measurable only to someone who measures. Then she lifted her chin and said, with perfect composure, "Good day, Mr. Fitch. Do give Mr. Quill my regards tomorrow."

* * *

I walked down Foremast Lane in a mood I can only describe as professional joy, which civilians invariably mistake for cruelty. Let me set the record straight, since history will have its say regardless: I bear Hazel Hartwell — or whatever she is truly named — no malice whatever. Malice is inefficient. Malice makes clerks stupid; it makes them want outcomes rather than records, and wanting is the death of accuracy. What I felt walking away from that workshop was the feeling a surveyor must feel sighting a coastline that appears on no chart: delight at the discovery, and a calm certainty that it would be plotted before week's end.

Because here is what she does not understand, or understands and has decided to outrun: everything is recorded somewhere. The parish register that fails to show her birth shows its absence, and absences are entries too. The indenture binding apprentice to master lives in duplicate — one copy with the master, and one lodged, per statute, with the town. The deed she thinks I do not know about — and yes, I knew before I ever knocked on her door; the tidal flats are wanted by the Navy Board, and the Navy Board asks its clerks to trace titles the way a surgeon traces veins — that deed bears signatures, and signatures bear witnesses, and witnesses grow old and talkative and frightened in reliable proportion.

I do not need her to confess. I need only to keep asking, patiently, in rooms where her answers accumulate against her. Every polite visit adds a page. Every page narrows the water. In my experience the hunted begin by lying smoothly, as she did, and end by lying badly, and between those two conditions lies all the room a patient man requires.

That evening I dined alone at the Anchor and Chain and wrote up my notes in the fair book I carry for purposes no warrant mentions. Against her name I entered: *Intelligent. Composed. Protecting a name more than papers. Plates relocated within last 72 hours — check Quill's delivery book for cartage.* And beneath that, because a good clerk distinguishes observation from inference: *Inference: whatever she guards, she guards as hers, not his. Find what makes a fatherless apprentice an heiress, and you find the atlas.*

The fire in the inn's parlor had burned to orange. Through the window I could see the harbor lights, and beyond them, faint on the dark water, the riding lights of a frigate at anchor in the roads — foreign-built lines, flying no colors a customs officer would salute. I had noticed her that morning and assumed a smuggler. Smugglers are common. But smugglers do not hold station so precisely, nor show so little traffic with the shore.

I made another entry. *Vessel in roads, third night running. Inquire.*

A coastline, after all, is only the place where two great bodies meet — the land and the sea, the law and the appetite, the lie and the record. My whole profession consists of walking that line and writing down what I find. And I had just begun to suspect that Miss Hazel's coastline ran considerably further inland than any of us had charted.

Tomorrow, Quill. Tomorrow the delivery book, the carters, the dates. And somewhere behind it all, a false wall, or a false name, or a false calm — it hardly matters which. Paper remembers what people promise themselves they'll forget.

I blotted the page, squared it with the others, and slept extremely well.
