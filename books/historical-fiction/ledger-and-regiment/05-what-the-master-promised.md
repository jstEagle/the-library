## 5. What the Master Promised

There is a particular smell to a dead man's papers that no amount of lavender or camphor quite defeats. It is not decay — Aldous Hartwell had been two years in the churchyard by then, and whatever he'd been made of had gone about its business without him. It is something older than decay. It is the smell of ink dried slowly in a closed room, of sealing wax and pipe smoke soaked into paper until the paper itself became smoke. I had been sorting his effects for three days when I found it, and I will confess honestly: by the third day I was no longer sorting so much as rummaging, with the guilty pleasure of a woman who has lived in a house for eleven years and is only now permitted to open its drawers.

The letter sat at the bottom of the third drawer of his survey desk, beneath a folio of tide tables so obsolete they were useful only as kindling, wrapped in oilcloth against damp as though it were the one thing in the room he truly cared about. The seal had been broken once and pressed shut again with a thumbprint in the wax, careless, hurried — a man resealing his own letter after reading it, which is either vanity or doubt, and Aldous Hartwell had never shown me much of either.

I should have set it aside. There is a version of Hazel Hartwell who sets things aside, who files them under *not my business* and gets on with her work, and I have met that version of myself often enough to know she is a fiction, a flattering portrait drawn by someone who never had to live with me.

I broke the wax again. The thumbprint cracked neatly down the middle, as if the man himself were giving permission.

*My son,* it began, and my heart did something complicated in my chest, because in eleven years I had heard Aldous speak of his son exactly four times, and three of those times had been to say nothing at all with great precision.

*My son —*

*You will have heard by now that the atlas is nearly done. It will be done this winter if my eyes hold, and next summer at the latest if they do not. I am an old man's stubbornness in a young man's body, or possibly the reverse; the surgeon has opinions.*

*I have kept my word, though you will not thank me for how long it took. A finished copy of the coastal atlas, fair-drawn, bound in calf, delivered to your hand — this was promised to you at Portsmouth, the spring before you went to sea, when you were fourteen years old and thought the whole world was a chart waiting to be corrected. You wanted to see my work finished before you made your own. I said you would have it. I have been slow, but I have not forgotten.*

*It will be delivered into your hands, wherever the sea puts you, by whoever I trust to carry it. Do not come for it yourself. We would quarrel, and I have neither the time nor the spleen left for good quarrels, and ours were always excellent.*

*Your father,*

*A.H.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, standing in the middle of his study with dust settling around me like weather, while outside the window the harbor did its usual business of pretending to be eternal.

Here is what I knew about Elias Vane, which was nearly everything, because knowing things was my trade and I hoarded knowledge the way other people hoard spoons. Aldous Hartwell had married late and badly, to a woman named Constance Vane who had died within the year, leaving behind a son from some earlier arrangement of her own. The boy had taken her name — Vane — and Aldous had taken the boy, in the way a man takes on a cargo he cannot refuse but does not particularly want. They had been close once, I gathered. There were letters from the boy's naval school in a box under the stairs, addressed in a hand that grew more confident year by year, and I had read every one of them during a feverish week in my first winter in Wexbridge when I was fifteen and homesick for a home that had never existed. The early ones called him *sir*. The later ones called him *father*, briefly, hopefully. The last one in the box was dated eight years back and contained, between lines about prize money and gunnery drills, a single sentence of such naked hurt that I had put it away and not looked again: *You need not answer this. I see you have stopped.*

And then there was my own small part in it, which I had never mentioned to anyone because it seemed too absurd to be true and too precious to be doubted. In my second year of apprenticeship, Aldous had begun reading aloud to me of an evening — passages from the boy's old letters, chosen apparently at random, though nothing Aldous did was ever random. He read them the way a man reads proof sheets, hunting for errors, and sometimes he laughed, and once — only once — he stopped in the middle of a paragraph about a storm off Ushant and sat looking at the fire for so long I thought he'd fallen asleep. *He sounds like me,* he said finally, in a voice I pretended not to hear. *That was the trouble.* Then he blew out the candle and went to bed, and we never spoke of it, and that silence became one of the load-bearing silences of my life.

Now here was the letter, and here was the promise, and here was the arithmetic that ruined everything.

A finished copy of the coastal atlas. Delivered to his hand. Wherever the sea put him.

Wherever the sea put him was, as of eight months past, aboard a frigate flying French colors.

I want to be clear about the shape of this problem, because it is the sort of problem that looks simple and then turns over, like a fish showing its pale side. The war was nine months old and everyone still spoke of it in the future tense, as though it were weather expected rather than weather arrived. Press notices had gone up on the customs house door. Mr. Quill had already warned me — with the gentle tact of a man delivering a diagnosis — that the Admiralty considered all coastal surveys to be state property now, or soon would be, and that an unregistered atlas sitting in a workshop above a chandler's shop was less an inheritance than an indictment. And somewhere across the channel, wearing a commission I could not even look at without committing a technical offense, was the son of my master, holding — if he held anything at all — a promise made in peacetime, in a world where a father could tell a boy *you will have my life's work* without anyone asking whose life's work it would become.

If I gave him the atlas, I armed an enemy. Not with powder — I was no fool, the atlas carried soundings and anchorages, yes, but also corrections, shoal marks, the little honest annotations of a man who had spent forty years learning where the coast lied to sailors. That was exactly the information navies killed each other over. That was precisely why Fitch — no, not Fitch yet, but men like Fitch, men who would come — wanted it seized.

If I refused, I broke the last promise Aldous Hartwell ever made, sealed with his own careless thumbprint, and I broke it on behalf of a government that would burn the original the moment it suited them.

I sat down on his stool — his stool, which still bore the dent of him, which I had never once sat in before that morning because some furniture belongs to the dead — and did what any sensible mapmaker does with an impossible coastline.

I surveyed it.

* * *

The first sounding was legal. If I delivered the atlas to Elias Vane, was I aiding the enemy? Yes. Undeniably, prosecutably, hanging-ly yes. There was no version of the law — statute, admiralty court, or the rougher law of the mob at the dockside taverns — under which handing a completed coastal survey to a serving officer of France ended well for the hander. I wrote *TREASON* in my head in capital letters, the way Quill wrote *URGENT* on his bills, and observed that the word looked smaller written in my head than it sounded spoken aloud.

The second sounding was moral, which is a different instrument entirely and gives different readings. Had Aldous known, when he wrote the letter, that his son would take a French commission? No. Impossible. The letter predated the estrangement's final rupture — it was warm, or as warm as Aldous ever got, warm the way a banked fire is warm. It was a letter written by a man who believed he had years. It promised delivery *wherever the sea put you*, which in peacetime meant Portsmouth or Plymouth or some honest station where a copy of an atlas was a gift and not a weapon. The promise had been made by one world and was being called due by another. That is not treachery; that is timing. But the gallows does not distinguish, as Tamsin would say, and Tamsin says most things better than I do.

The third sounding was personal, and here I struck mud, because personal soundings always do.

Because the truth — the truth I had been circling since I broke the seal — was that I wanted to deliver it. Wanted it with a ferocity that frightened me. Not for Elias Vane, whom I had met perhaps six times in eleven years, a tall awkward youth who once corrected my hatching technique and was, infuriatingly, right about it. I wanted it for Aldous. Because the letter proved something I had spent eleven years refusing to believe: that underneath the flint and the silence and the terrible economy of his affection, the man had loved his son. Had remembered a promise made to a fourteen-year-old on a quay at Portsmouth. Had wrapped that promise in oilcloth against the damp, the way you protect the thing you mean to keep forever.

And I was the one he had trusted with it. Not named in the letter — the letter named *whoever I trust to carry it* — but I knew, the way you know a bearing is true, that whoever meant me. He had left me his plates, his pens, his debts, his unfinished life. He had left me this. And he had left me, in the same drawer, in the same oilcloth, that other paper — the deed, the name, the thing that could make Hartwell legally mine instead of merely factually — and the two papers lay together now on the survey desk like a pair of weights, and I understood with terrible clarity that he had left me both because he could not choose between them, and had decided, in the manner of fathers, to make the choosing someone else's problem.

Well. It was my problem now. I had inherited it along with everything else, and inheritance, I was beginning to learn, is mostly a polite word for burden.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing honest work, which is the traditional remedy for dishonest thoughts. The final plate — Cape Merrow to the estuary mouth, the survey Aldous had been walking when the marsh took him — needed its last corrections inked, and I inked them, and the work steadied me the way it always did. There is no anxiety that survives contact with a ruling pen and steady light. By dusk the plate was done, and I stood looking at it with the peculiar grief-and-pride mixture that has been the flavor of my life these two years, thinking: *he would change the shading on the eastern spit, and he would be wrong, and I am glad it is my shading that stands.*

Then I lit a candle, went back to the desk, and read the letter a fourth time, because the fourth reading was for details, and details are my trade.

*Delivered to your hand, wherever the sea puts you, by whoever I trust to carry it.*

Not sent. Carried. He had not intended to post it through channels, through agents, through the indifferent machinery of commerce. He had intended a person — a particular person, with a face and a voice, who could stand in front of Elias Vane and say *your father kept his word*. He had intended the promise to arrive wearing a human being.

And where was the sea putting Elias Vane? I went to the window. The harbor lay below in its evening grays, masts and rigging and the slow traffic of a port emptying itself toward war. And there — I knew before I raised the glass, knew by the way the fishing boats gave it room, by the way it sat at anchor with the insolent patience of a thing that owns the water it floats on — there was the frigate. The one that had appeared three days ago, flying merchant colors at distance and something else at close inspection, taking on water, careening nothing, buying nothing, selling nothing, a ship doing absolutely none of the things ships come to harbors to do. Quill had muttered about it. The harbor master had muttered about it. Everyone muttered; nobody acted; war makes mutterers of us all.

*A survey errand*, the harbor master's office had been told, and everyone had smiled politely at the fiction, because a French frigate conducting a hydrographic survey of an English anchorage in wartime was a joke told with a straight face and loaded guns.

But I stood at the window with the glass, and I watched that ship sit in the roads, and I thought about a boy who had wanted to correct the whole world's charts, and I felt the cold slide of a suspicion going home.

He hadn't come for water.

* * *

I did not sleep, which surprised me not at all. I spent the night the way I spend most difficult nights: drawing. There is a plate in progress — the estuary, the unmapped one, the project Aldous and I had argued about cheerfully for a year before the marsh settled the argument — and I worked on it by candlelight until the candle drowned, and then by the grey beginnings of dawn, and what I was really doing was drawing a map of the decision, laying out the coasts of it, marking the shoals. On one shore: the deed, the registry, the name. Legitimate. Legal. Mine, potentially, in the way that mattered to clerks and courts and gravestones. On the other shore: the letter, the promise, a fair-copy atlas bound in calf, delivered to the hand of a man in enemy colors. Treason, possibly. Fidelity, certainly. And the channel between them narrowing every day as the Admiralty requisitioned and the press notices multiplied and the world reorganized itself around the principle that everything belongs to someone with a warrant.

By full light I had reached the only conclusion available to a person built the way I am built, which is that I could not yet choose, and that refusing to choose was itself a choice with a schedule attached.

But I knew one thing with the certainty of a fixed point on a chart. Whatever else happened — whatever Fitch came, whatever Quill feared, whatever the registry held — the promise came first. Not because Elias Vane deserved it. Not because France deserved it. But because Aldous Hartwell had wrapped it in oilcloth against the damp, and a promise a dead man bothered to waterproof is not a promise you let drown.

I folded the letter along its original creases, returned it to the oilcloth, and put it in the satchel. The satchel that measured my life, that held what fit and nothing more. It seemed suddenly important that the letter travel with the instruments, with the pens and the parallel rule, among the tools of the trade that had made me. If I was to be a courier of treason, I would at least be a competent one.

Below my window, the tide was turning, and the frigate in the roads swung slowly on its anchor to meet it, and I caught myself raising the glass again — catching myself watching that ship the way you watch a letter you've posted, willing it onward, wondering what it carries.

Three days it had sat there. Ships do not sit three days without reason.

And a thought arrived, unwelcome as weather: if I could read that ship's purpose from my window, then so could the Admiralty. And if the son had come looking for his father's work, then sooner or later, someone with clean hands and a warrant would come looking for it too — and they would start, as I had started, with the drawers.

I picked up the satchel. It was time to stop rummaging, and start deciding what to hide.
