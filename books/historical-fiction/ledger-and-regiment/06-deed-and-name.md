## 6. Deed and Name

The Wexbridge Registry of Deeds occupied a narrow house on Cordwainer Lane that had once been somebody's residence and had never quite stopped pretending otherwise. A brass plate by the door read *REGISTRY* in letters so small you had to be looking for them, which I supposed was the point. The men who ran the place seemed to feel that land itself was slightly vulgar — all that mud and tide — and that their office existed to keep it at arm's length, behind wax seals and clerical handwriting.

I had walked past it perhaps four hundred times in my life. I had never once gone in.

I stood across the lane for a good ten minutes, telling myself I was waiting for the rain to slacken. It was not raining. A dray cart went by; two apprentices from the sailmakers' walk passed me with a roll of canvas over their shoulders and did not see me at all, which is a particular talent of apprentices everywhere.

Here is what I was afraid of, if I am honest, and this chapter of my life obliges me to be honest more often than I like: I was afraid the paper would not say my name.

Not Hazel Hartwell — I had written that name on charts and title blocks and the flyleaves of borrowed books since I could hold a pen, and it had always been a kind of fiction, a costume worn daily until it fit like skin. No. I was afraid the deed would list signatories and heirs and devisees in careful clerk's Latin, and that somewhere among them Aldous Hartwell's daughter would appear — some legitimate child, some nephew, some arrangement made before I ever came shivering into his workshop at nine years old with salt-cracked hands and no surname anyone would write down — and that the letter under seal, which I had read eleven times by candlelight, would turn out to mean nothing to me at all.

It is a strange thing, to hope and dread the same document. But then, most inheritances arrive that way, I think. We simply don't say so out loud.

I crossed the lane.

* * *

Inside, the registry smelled of dust, tallow, and the particular staleness of paper that has been important for a very long time. A clerk sat behind a counter of blackened oak, copying something with the deliberate slowness of a man paid by the hour and determined to prove the wage just. He looked up when my shadow fell across his page.

"Madam?"

"Hazel Hartwell." The name came out steady, to my surprise. "I'm enquiring after a deed of conveyance. Tidal flats and saltings east of the harbor — the Marshlands grant, I believe it's called. Dated 1771."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not alarm, exactly. Recognition of a category. "And your interest in the grant, madam?"

"I believe I may be named in it."

He set down his pen with great care, as though it might go off. "May I ask who directed you here?"

"My late master's papers." Which was true. "Aldous Hartwell, surveyor, of Chandler's Row." Also true. It is remarkable how much truth a person can carry without once setting down the whole of it.

The clerk — Mr. Pellowe, according to the little card angled toward the light on his desk — studied me for a moment longer than courtesy strictly required. I met his look with the expression I had learned from Aldous: the flat, patient stare of a man who has measured a thousand coastlines and cannot be hurried by weather. It works on clerks as well as it works on harbormasters.

"One moment," he said, and disappeared into the back rooms.

I heard drawers. I heard the particular rustle of a folio being drawn out and consulted. And then — and this I noted even through the drumming of my own heart — I heard him stop. A pause too long to be accident. When he returned, his manner had shifted by one degree, the way water shifts before it decides to be a wave.

"The Marshlands grant," he said. "Conveyance dated the fourth of October, 1771. Grantors: the Commissioners of the Harbor Trust, to certain parties. Holders of record—" he glanced down at a slip he'd copied, or pretended to copy, "—include Josiah Vane, merchant; Thomas Quill, stationer; and Aldous Hartwell, surveyor. Joint tenants, with survivorship, and provision for devise to heirs of the body."

"Heirs of the body," I repeated.

"Meaning blood kin, madam. Lawful issue." He said it kindly enough, but kindness in a clerk's mouth can be the cruelest instrument there is, because it comes without apology. "Are you— forgive me — are you a daughter of Mr. Hartwell?"

The room was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a shutter knocked against its frame in the wind off the harbor.

"I was his apprentice," I said. "For fourteen years. I lived in his household from the age of nine. He left me everything he had that wasn't nailed down, and half of what was."

Mr. Pellowe's face did the thing faces do when they are being polite about a fact everyone already knows: a small settling, a sympathy arranged like furniture. "Then I'm sorry, madam, but apprenticeship conveys no estate. Unless there was a formal adoption — an enrollment before a magistrate, an act of Common Council, something entered in the parish register — the name Hartwell descends only through blood or lawful wedlock." He paused, and then, because clerks cannot help themselves, added the detail that undid me: "Though I will note that the deed names its holders jointly, and provides that any holder may designate an heir by codicil, enrolled here, provided it is done while the holder lives."

"Did he enroll a codicil?"

Pellowe drew out the folio again — I watched his thumb move down a column of marginal entries — and shook his head slowly. "Nothing enrolled against the Marshlands grant after 1789. I'm sorry."

* * *

I want to be fair to Aldous Hartwell. I spent fourteen years learning to be fair to Aldous Hartwell, mostly because fairness toward that man required the patience of a woman surveying a coastline in fog.

He had taken me in when nobody else would have. He had taught me the trade entire — the plane table and the theodolite, the sounding lead and the arithmetic of tides, the discipline of drawing a coastline true when every instinct screams to make it prettier than it is. He had called me *Hartwell* in front of clients and magistrates and the customs men, let them assume what they liked, corrected no one. Once, when a visiting hydrographer referred to me as "your daughter," Aldous had said only, "She has my eye for soundings," which is not a denial and is not a claim, and is precisely the sort of sentence that man built his whole life from.

He had given me the name the way he gave me everything: by usage, by habit, by never quite putting it in writing where the law could reach it.

And now I understood why. Or I began to. Because a deed signed by Aldous Hartwell alongside Josiah Vane — Elias's grandfather, or great-uncle; the Vane family tree was a thing of thorns — and old Quill, holding tidal flats the Navy suddenly needed, was not an inheritance. It was a target. Whatever promise or sentiment might have moved Aldous to enroll a codicil naming me heir, he had weighed it against what such a paper would do — paint a target on a nameless girl with no family to speak for her — and he had chosen silence. His silences were always protection, of himself first and everyone else a distant second, but they were protection nonetheless.

Except that now the war had come, and the Admiralty wanted the flats, and the deed existed whether I was named in it or not. My namelessness protected nothing anymore. It only meant that when the taking began, I would have nothing to show for fourteen years of being a Hartwell except the habit of the name in my own mouth.

"You'll understand, madam," Pellowe was saying, "that enquiries touching the coastal grants are presently subject to requisition by the Crown. If the Admiralty moves to extinguish the holders' interest — purchase, compulsory transfer, whatever instrument they choose — claims will need to be established quickly, and proved thoroughly." He said it delicately, the way one mentions weather to a farmer whose crops are visibly dying. "Blood kin with papers stand a chance. Others..." He spread his clean hands.

"How quickly?"

"I couldn't say. But there was a gentleman here Tuesday. Naval clerk, very civil, very thorough. Asking after exactly this grant, and after the Hartwell papers generally." Pellowe lowered his voice as if Fitch might be standing behind the folio shelves. "He took notes. He'll be back, I shouldn't wonder. They're always back."

Of course he had been here first. Of course Ambrose Fitch, with his clean hands and his unhurried smile, had already visited the registry, already seen the deed, already begun the slow work of mapping my family the way I mapped coastlines — feature by feature, until nothing was left unmarked. The difference between us was that his maps ended in seizure and mine, God willing, in publication.

"Thank you, Mr. Pellowe," I said. "You've been most informative."

"If you should establish a claim," he said, as I turned to go, "come to me directly. Don't send letters. Letters get read." And then, softer: "My father kept a chandlery on the east quay, madam. I remember your master surveying the flats when I was a boy. He was a hard man, but he was straight with the water." A pause. "So far as I know, no one ever proved otherwise about you either."

It was the closest thing to a blessing the law had ever offered me, and it came wrapped in a warning.

* * *

I did not go home. I walked instead out along the east quay, past the ropewalk, to the sea wall, where the Marshlands themselves lay spread out below me at low tide: a thousand acres of mud and samphire and winding creeks, silver-brown in the afternoon light, breathing with the tide like something alive. Our flats. Or not ours. Or mine, if a word could be made to stretch that far.

Aldous had surveyed these flats in '71, a young man with a new theodolite and a head full of ambition. I had tramped them with him a hundred times since, driving stakes, measuring the creep and retreat of the channels, learning that a coastline is not a line at all but a negotiation. He used to say — standing hip-deep in the mud with his breeches rolled, entirely happy in a way he was never happy anywhere else — *"The flats don't lie, girl. Land above the tideline lies all day long. Down here everything is exactly what it is."*

I had thought he meant it as instruction in surveying. Standing there now, I wondered if he'd been talking about himself. About the difference between the man above the tideline — respectable, silent, unclaimed fatherhood — and whatever he actually was, down where things could not lie.

Heirs of the body. Blood kin. Lawful issue.

I had none of it. What I had was fourteen years of his teaching in my hands, a name worn smooth by use, and a deed in which I appeared nowhere — but which, if it survived, would put ground under the name of Hartwell that no clerk could argue with. Ground makes names real. That is nearly the whole of English law.

And Fitch could take it. That was the other nearly-whole of English law. One requisition order, one quiet afternoon, and the Marshlands grant would pass to the Crown, and the name Hartwell would be a sound I made with my mouth and nothing else. He didn't even need to be cruel about it. He'd be civil. He'd offer terms. He'd probably offer *me* terms, compensation for services rendered, a modest annuity, a pat on the head for the loyal apprentice. He would erase me the way an eraser removes a penciled annotation: cleanly, without malice, improving the document.

Unless I got there first. Unless the claim was established, enrolled, proved — blood or no blood — before his instruments closed around it.

But how? A codicil never enrolled. A name nowhere written. An apprentice with no standing, whose only evidence of belonging was the muscle memory of her own hands.

There was one path I could see, and it ran straight through the thing I least wanted to touch: the Vanes. Josiah Vane's name stood on that deed beside my master's, and Elias Vane — the estranged son, the officer on the enemy side of the channel, the man to whom Aldous had promised a finished atlas in peacetime — was anchored off Wexbridge roads at this very hour under cover of a survey errand. If the Vane interest in the grant could be proved, if the family acknowledged the connection, if—

If. The word I had built my whole life on.

I stood at the sea wall until the light went long and coppery, and the tide, which does not care about deeds or names or naval clerks, began to come in over the flats — covering, acre by acre, the ground I might have owned, erasing it exactly as thoroughly as Fitch would have, and then giving it back, because that is the arrangement the water keeps and we do not.

On the walk home, past the darkening windows of Cordwainer Lane, I made myself a surveyor's decision. I would not choose yet between the deed and the promise; both threads still ran through my hands. But I would follow them to their ends, and quickly, because the tide was coming in on both.

When I reached Chandler's Row, there was a folded paper wedged in the crack of the workshop door, unsigned, in a hand I almost recognized from years of reading aloud the master's correspondence:

*You are watched. The loft on Rope Walk is not safe. Come to the flats at low water tomorrow, if you want to know what your master promised me.*

E.V.

I stood a long time in the doorway, the note trembling slightly in my hand, listening to the harbor breathe.
