## 7. Fitch Returns

I have found, in eleven years of service to His Majesty's paperwork, that the most dangerous people in any port are not smugglers, press-gangs, or French agents, but clerks who have grown comfortable in their lies. A smuggler lies with his fists and his feet; a clerk lies sitting down, which means he has had time to practice.

Hazel Hartwell had had time to practice.

I returned to the Hartwell workshop on a Thursday, because Thursdays are when respectable tradesmen do their accounts and are least inclined to theatricals. I brought with me a second warrant — broader in its language than the first — a list of questions I had composed the night before in my neat, upright hand, and a small cake from the confectioner on Cordwainer Lane. The cake was not bribery. It is simply that people find it difficult to be hostile toward a man holding cake. I have watched hardened quartermasters soften over a slice of seed loaf. It is one of the great unrecorded technologies of my profession.

The bell above the workshop door announced me with more enthusiasm than the occasion warranted. She looked up from her desk — she was always at her desk, that girl, as though the chair had grown around her like the wood of a ship around its keel — and her face did exactly what it had done at our first meeting: nothing at all.

"Mr. Fitch," she said. "You've brought weather with you."

It had, in fact, begun to rain. "It follows me," I said. "Or perhaps I follow it. One loses track." I set the cake on the edge of her desk, beside an inkstone worn to a shallow valley by years of some other man's pen. "From Cordwainer Lane. The almond one. I'm told it doesn't keep."

"Then you'd best stay while I eat it," she said, "so it doesn't go to waste."

You see what I mean? Most apprentices would have stammered. Most apprentices would have taken the cake and thanked me for it, thereby accepting, however symbolically, a gift from the Admiralty. This one accepted my presence the way she apparently accepted tides: as a fact of the coast, to be worked around rather than argued with.

* * *

Let me set down, for the record of my own conscience, what I actually knew that Thursday morning, because Fitch works from knowledge or he is nothing:

Item: Aldous Hartwell, hydrographer, dead two years, left behind him a coastal atlas of the southern approaches — a work, by every report, of such accuracy that the Navy would pay handsomely for it and the enemy would pay better.

Item: The Atlas Office requisitioned all Hartwell papers by order dated the ninth. What arrived at the Exchequer Quay was a crate of correspondence so dull I have read sermons with more seditious content. Bills for paper. Letters about tide-tables. A touching note from a chandler regarding the price of lamp oil. Not one fair-copy plate. Not one working drawing. Not even a sketch of a gull.

Item: No man completes an atlas of forty plates without producing forty plates' worth of drafts, corrections, spoiled impressions, and false starts. Paperwork does not vanish. It accretes, like coral. Someone had cleaned this reef, and recently.

Item: The girl. Apprentice, unadopted, nameless by law though she signed herself Hartwell in the manner of someone trying the word on for size. She had received me once with perfect courtesy and answered every question with the truth arranged so carefully it functioned as a lie. *All papers were surrendered, sir. Everything of the master's went in the crate.* Everything. A word that covers a multitude.

And item, though this I kept to myself: the name Hartwell had appeared, three weeks prior, on a schedule of interests relating to certain tidal lands east of the harbor — land the Navy suddenly required, land encumbered by an old deed bearing old signatures, among them an *A. Hartwell*. I did not yet know what the deed meant to her. I suspected she did not fully know either. But names on schedules are like fish on hooks; they do not struggle yet, but they are no longer entirely their own.

So I came back with cake, and patience, and a proposition.

* * *

She poured tea because refusing to pour tea would have been an admission, and Hazel Hartwell did not admit things before noon. We sat across the workbench from each other with the rain ticking against the south window, and between us lay the tools of her trade — the pens, the burnisher, a half-finished chart of some estuary I did not recognize, drawn in a hand that was almost the dead man's and almost not.

"You'll forgive the second visit," I began. "Bureaucracy is a tide too. It goes out and comes back, and it never comes back smaller."

"I've noticed," she said. "The harbor master says the same of you."

"The harbor master flatters himself. I am far more predictable." I let that sit, then unfolded my warrant and laid it flat, squaring its corners to the bench edge out of habit. "A supplementary order. It widens the definition of 'papers' to include plates, drawings, tracings, and any material prepared in furtherance of the atlas. You'll want to read it."

"I can read," she said, and did, at a pace that told me she was reading it properly, which annoyed me more than evasion would have. Evasion I could work with. Competence was going to make this long.

When she finished, she set the warrant down and folded her hands. "We've covered this ground, Mr. Fitch."

"We've covered it shallowly." I took out my list of questions, then, deliberately, did not look at it. There is a moment in every examination where the examiner must choose between his notes and his instincts, and I chose my instincts. "Miss Hartwell. Hazel. May I call you Hazel?"

"You may attempt it."

"Your master worked on that atlas for nineteen years. I have seen the correspondence. Nineteen years, four months, and — if the man's letters are to be believed — roughly six thousand quarrels with the sea. Do you know what a surveyor's loft looks like at the end of nineteen years?"

"Like mine," she said, gesturing at the room, which was immaculate.

"Precisely my point." I smiled at her. She did not smile back, which was correct of her; I have rarely learned anything useful from a person who smiled back too soon. "Nineteen years leaves sediment. Draft upon draft upon draft. And yet the crate your office sent contained correspondence only. Clean, sorted, tied in bundles with string cut to even lengths." I leaned back. "Someone in this room organized that sediment into something presentable. Someone decided what the Admiralty needed to see."

"And you think that someone hid the rest."

"I think," I said gently, "that someone protected it. Which is a kinder word, and I prefer kind words. They get better results."

The rain filled the pause. She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a cartographer's gesture if ever I saw one — adjusting a bearing by degrees.

"If there were plates," she said slowly, "and I'm not saying there were—"

"Naturally."

"—what would the Crown do with them? Frame them? Admire them?"

"What the Crown would do," I said, "is keep them. And what the Crown would give, in exchange, is the thing you cannot buy, borrow, or forge." I let the silence do a little work, then continued, quieter. "Protection."

That word landed. I watched it land. Her chin came up perhaps a quarter-inch — the smallest defiance, the kind that costs nothing and signals everything — but she didn't speak, so I went on, because I am at my best when I am uninterrupted.

"You are twenty-two years old, unmarried, un apprenticed now that your master is dead, and legally — I say this with real regret — nobody. Your name exists by habit, not by record. If the wrong clerk in London takes an interest in the Hartwell estate, you could be put out of this workshop in a fortnight, and there is no document in this kingdom that would stop it, because documents, Miss Hartwell, are precisely what you lack."

"You've given this a great deal of thought."

"It's my trade." I spread my hands. "Now consider the alternative. The plates come to the Atlas Office. In return, your apprenticeship is confirmed retroactively under the terms of the late master's will — I can arrange that; I have read the will, and it is generous, whoever executes it. Your tenure of these premises is secured. And the name Hartwell ceases to be a costume and becomes a coat of arms, so to speak. Legitimate. Recorded. Safe."

*Safe.* I have offered that word perhaps forty times in my career, and I will tell you what I have learned about it: everyone wants it, and nearly everyone believes they don't, and the ones who believe it loudest are the ones who want it most. I watched her want it. I saw it move behind her eyes like a fish under ice — there and gone, there and gone. She wanted safety the way the drowning want air and pretend, while gasping, to be swimming for pleasure.

And then she wanted something else instead. I could see the moment of exchange, the internal transaction, though I could not see its terms.

* * *

"There's something you're not telling me," she said.

"There usually is."

"About the name." She set down her cup with a click that was very slightly too firm. "You keep saying *Hartwell* as though it were a case file. As though it were already open."

Ah. She was quicker than I'd credited. I had intended to hold that card until the third visit — one never plays everything on the second; the second visit is for making the third visit necessary — but there was no help for it now. Withhold a fact she has already deduced and you lose credibility forever. Credibility is the only currency a clerk carries, and unlike coin, it devalues in the rain.

"The name Hartwell," I said, "appears on a schedule. A routine schedule of interests concerning certain lands along the eastern flats. Lands the Admiralty requires for — anchorage, fortification, matters beyond my station. Where a name appears on such a schedule, an inquiry follows. Inquiries are slow, thorough, and indifferent to sentiment. They would look at the deed. They would look at who holds rights under the deed. And they would ask, as I am asking you, in this gentler way, who exactly is entitled to call herself a Hartwell."

The color left her face in a most orderly fashion — first the cheeks, then the lips, then the knuckles where her hands gripped each other. But her voice, when it came, was level. Leveler than mine would have been, if I am honest, and I make a point of being honest in this ledger of mine, since nowhere else is it required.

"Entitled," she repeated. "As one is entitled by a ticket, or a deed."

"As one is entitled by anything, in the eyes of the law. The law is not sentimental. That is its single virtue and its entire cruelty." I softened my tone, because the moment called for softness the way a wound calls for a clean bandage. "Which is why my offer stands, and why it is generous, whatever you may think of the man offering it. Give up the plates, and I will see to it that this inquiry finds a settled household, a recognized apprentice, and nothing worth digging beneath. Refuse, and I cannot shield you from what comes next. I am not threatening you, Hazel. I am describing the weather."

"The weather," she said, "seems to be following you."

* * *

I made one final appeal, and I confess it was not entirely professional. I have a weakness — well catalogued, much regretted — for people who are alone in rooms full of evidence. She sat there amid the ghost of a dead man's life, guarding his work as though guarding him, and I thought: *she does not know what she is carrying. She thinks it is an atlas. It is an anchor.*

"Think of it practically," I said. "Paper burns. Warrants don't. Whatever you're keeping — and we both know by now that something is being kept — it cannot protect you. It can only incriminate you. Whereas I—" I tapped the warrant, once, lightly, the way one taps a sleeping animal. "I can convert danger into safety. That is all I do. All day, every day. I am, if you like, a money-changer of trouble."

For the first time since I had known her, Hazel Hartwell laughed. It was a short laugh, dry as old vellum, and it frightened me more than shouting would have.

"A money-changer," she said. "And what's your commission, Mr. Fitch?"

"The Crown's gratitude. Modest lodgings. The satisfaction of order." I rose, gathered my hat, and squared the warrant's corners once more out of pure nerves, which I was annoyed to discover I had. "You have until Monday."

"Generous."

"The Admiralty is at war. Generosity is measured in days now, not months." At the door I paused, because a good exit line deserves a good doorway, and said: "Miss Hartwell. When the inquiry comes — and it will come whether you give me the plates or not — the only question that will matter is whose side of the ledger you stood on when it arrived. I am offering you the chance to be, on paper, precisely what you appear to be: an orphan apprentice who cooperated. That is not nothing. I have seen men hang for less defensible positions."

She stood. She was not tall, but standing, with the gray light behind her and the rain running down the window like ruled lines, she managed to look as though she were the one leaving.

"Thank you for the cake," she said. "You can tell the confectioner it kept after all."

* * *

I walked back through the wet streets composing my report in my head, which is how I compose everything, and I will record here the finding I did not put in it:

I had expected refusal to sound like defiance. Hers had not. It had sounded like arithmetic — the quiet click of a woman weighing two sums and choosing the heavier one, knowing it was heavier, choosing it anyway. Defiance I know how to break; it breaks like anyone else, only louder. Arithmetic is another matter. You cannot out-patience a sum.

And beneath the arithmetic, something else, which troubled me all the way to the Exchequer Quay and past it. She had not asked me the obvious question. She had not asked *what happens to the atlas if I refuse*, nor *will I be arrested*, nor any of the questions a frightened person asks. She had asked about the name. About entitlement. About who may call herself what.

Which told me the plates were, in some sense I did not yet understand, secondary to her. And a subject whose treasure is not the treasure you are seizing is a subject you do not understand. And a subject you do not understand is a subject who wins.

I filed my interim report that evening — *no plates located; further inquiries proceeding; recommend continued surveillance of premises and associates* — and then, because thoroughness is a form of prayer, I wrote a second memorandum requesting the original deed records from the registry office, and the full signatory history of the eastern tidal lands.

If she would not trade me her inheritance for her safety, then I would learn what her inheritance actually was.

By Monday, I told myself, I would hold every card in the deck. I was, as it happened, wrong in a manner I could not have predicted — for by Monday, someone else had been to the registry office before me, and what I found there rearranged the entire game.
