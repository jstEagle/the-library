## 8. The False Wall

There is a particular arithmetic to fear that no surveyor ever puts in a manual. You learn it the way you learn the weight of your own head — by carrying it. By the third hour past midnight I had learned this: forty-one copper plates, wrapped in oilcloth and wool, weigh something close to a grown man's grief, which is to say more than you think and less than you can admit.

I had spent the evening doing sums at the workbench with the shutter closed and one candle lit, the way my master used to do when he didn't want the harbor knowing his business. The candle was mostly for my hands. My eyes had memorized the room years ago.

Here is what fits in a satchel: two shirts, a comb, the letter under seal (which I had taken to wearing against my ribs like a second, colder heart), a knife for scraping plate edges that would serve, in a pinch, for other purposes. Here is what does not fit in a satchel: an atlas of the entire Wexbridge coast in master plates, each one a season of Aldous Hartwell's life pressed into copper, each one two years of mine laid over it like a second skin of ink.

The Admiralty wanted charts. Mr. Quill had said it gently, the way you tell a woman her house is on fire by remarking that the weather's turned warm. *Requisitioned, my dear. State property, once war is declared. Best decide what is yours and what is theirs before someone decides for you.* And then he had looked at me over his spectacles with an expression I chose not to interpret, because interpreting it would have meant admitting he knew about the false wall, and if Quill knew, then the whole harbor knew, and if the whole harbor knew, then Fitch knew, and Fitch — Clerk Ambrose Fitch, with his clean hands and his voice like a well-bred file — did not strike me as a man who forgot anything except, deliberately, how to be frightening.

So. The false wall.

Aldous built it the year I came to him, which tells you something about the man. Behind the map press there was a stretch of stone that rang wrong when knocked, and behind that stone a cavity deep enough for a coffin or a fortune, depending on your trade. He kept his best plates there during the last war scares, and after he died I kept them there out of habit, then out of superstition, then — these past weeks — out of the very practical conviction that the wall was the only thing between Ambrose Fitch and everything my master ever made.

But walls keep nothing. They only wait. A false wall is a confession with masonry around it; anyone who finds it knows exactly what you thought worth hiding. Better to make the plates walk. Better to carry the world out of the house in pieces on my back and let the wall stand empty, ringing true at last, guilty of nothing but bad acoustics.

* * *

The ropewalk runs from the old sail yard down toward the mudflats, three hundred yards of covered passage where they once spun cable, now mostly a home for cats, spiders, and the particular silence that collects in long thin buildings nobody uses. At midnight it is a corridor of grey dark with slots of moonlight falling through gaps in the roof like coins dropped by a careless God.

I went out the workshop door with the first bundle strapped across my back under my cloak and walked the way a woman walks when she is going nowhere interesting: not too fast, not too slow, shoulders rounded, mind visibly occupied with bread or a sweetheart or nothing at all. Wexbridge at midnight is not empty — it is merely honest. The watchman outside the King's Arms nodded at me and asked if I was lost, and I said I'd been sitting up with Mrs. Pellowe's fever, which had the advantage of being plausible and unverifiable, since Mrs. Pellowe was asleep and would swear to anything I liked come morning out of sheer delight at being involved.

The second trip was harder. The third, hardest of all, because by then the exhilaration had arrived.

I should explain the exhilaration, because it shames me slightly. There is a moment in every illicit act — and I maintain that carrying your dead master's life's work through the night streets is illicit only in the way breathing is illicit when someone has told you to stop — there is a moment when fear tips over into something else entirely. Fear is heavy. Exhilaration is the same weight, suddenly given wings. On the fourth crossing of the ropewalk, with the moon coming through the roof-slats in bars and my breath smoking and thirty-odd pounds of coastline riding my spine, I felt — I will say it plainly — magnificent. I felt like the heroine of one of Tamsin's scandalous novels, all of which she reads aloud badly and remembers perfectly. I felt like a person in a story, moving through the dark with purpose, and the feeling was so warm and so foolish that I let it carry me two trips further than caution advised.

That is the thing about exhilaration. It is fear wearing a better coat, and it lies just as fluently.

Tamsin's sail loft sits at the landward end of the ropewalk like a full stop at the end of a long sentence. Her father had been a sailmaker and her grandfather before him, and Tamsin had inherited the loft, the debts, the needle calluses, and a talent for reading cloth the way I read water — instantly, totally, and with opinions. We had been friends since we were eleven, when she caught me drawing the harbor from the breakwater and informed me that my tide-marks were wrong and then showed me, with a length of string and a fish hook, why. She has never once let me forget it. This is what friendship is: someone who corrects your soundings and loves you anyway.

I knocked the pattern on her door — two, pause, three — and she opened it holding a candle and a marlinspike, in that order of intent.

"You look," she said, "like a burglar who's decided to take up cartography."

"Move back. Mind the corner."

She moved back. I set the bundle down on her loom table and unwrapped it, and even Tamsin — who thinks maps are pictures of where to get lost — went quiet. Moonlight through the loft's high windows lay along the copper, and the engraved lines woke up, the coast assembling itself in negative: Wexbridge roads, the shoals off Gull Point, the deep channel Aldous had sounded forty times because he trusted nothing he'd measured fewer than ten.

"That's us," Tamsin said finally. "That little squiggle. That's my loft."

"That's everyone. That's the whole shore from the Head to the marshes."

"And the Navy wants it."

"The Navy wants to own it. Wanting is different." I began rewrapping. "They'll go in the sail canvas. The consignment for the Vanterpool trader — can they pass?"

She considered, chewing the inside of her cheek the way she did when calculating. "Bolt canvas, double-folded. Nobody inspects sailcloth for copper. They'd want to know why it weighs so much, though. Forty plates—"

"Thirty-eight. Two stay with me."

"Which two?"

"The fair copy goes nowhere without me. And—" I touched the plate beneath it, the last one I'd inked, the estuary mouth, the final page of a dead man's sentence. "This one."

Tamsin looked at me for a long moment. In the candlelight her face did the thing it always did before she said something kind, which was go sharp and unimpressed, armor for the softness underneath.

"You've got the look my father had," she said, "the winter the revenue men came asking about his ledgers."

"What look?"

"Like someone who's already counted the cost and decided the arithmetic is acceptable." She blew out the candle, which was her way of ending conversations that were becoming emotional. "Bring the rest tomorrow night. And Hazel — don't use the ropewalk again. Once is luck. Twice is habit. Habit is what they catch you in."

* * *

It was on the seventh trip — I know because I had begun counting them the way I count everything, compulsively, in fives — that the night changed its mind about me.

The ropewalk again, near its middle, where the roof sags lowest and the dark pools deepest. I had the last four plates strapped high between my shoulder blades, and I remember thinking, absurdly, about how Aldous had described carrying his instruments along this same walk thirty years before, when the rope-makers still worked it and the air had been thick with hemp and tar. *You learn a coast with your spine before your eyes,* he'd written in one of the field books I'd since burned. *The body takes the bearings first.*

And then I heard the click.

Small. Behind me. The particular small sound of a boot finding loose gravel where the paving had gone to ruin, and — here is the detail that turned my blood to seawater — the sound *stopping*. Not continuing, as it would from carelessness. Stopping, as it does when the person making it realizes the night is listening.

I did not turn around. Turning around is what people do in stories, and stories end badly for the ones who turn around. Instead I stopped too, bent as if adjusting my bootlace, and used the posture to look back along the walk the way you check a horizon: sidelong, low, patient.

Moonlight fell in those coin-slots, twenty feet apart. Between them, darkness stacked itself like unplumbed water. In the third pool of shadow — or perhaps the fourth; I will be honest, my hands were shaking — there was a shape that was not a shape. Something upright. Something that breathed.

Or something that was a stack of old rope bales and my own terrified invention. That was the argument my sensible mind made, and it was a good argument, and I did not believe a word of it, because sensible minds are exactly what fear recruits first.

I finished tying a lace that did not need tying. I straightened. I walked on toward the sail loft at the pace of a woman with sore feet and a dull evening behind her, and every nerve I owned stood up inside my skin and screamed *run*, and I did not run, because running confirms, and confirmation is a gift you do not give a man like Ambrose Fitch.

At the loft door I knocked my pattern — two, pause, three — and heard, faintly, far back down the covered dark, the gravel click again. Closer. Or farther. Or nothing. The trouble with a sound is that once you've heard it, you hear it forever, the way once you've seen a reef marked on a chart you can never again believe the water above it is innocent.

Tamsin opened the door. I stepped through. She took one look at my face and reached past me to shut it softly, without the bang, without the noise, and stood with her hand flat against the wood listening the way she listened to wind in rigging.

"Well?" she whispered.

"There's someone in the ropewalk. Maybe."

"Maybe."

"Maybe." I set the plates down with hands that would not quite obey. "Maybe it's been there since the third trip. Maybe it followed me from the workshop. Maybe it's been counting my trips better than I have."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. Then she went to the window that faced the ropewalk, the little round one her father had cut to watch for weather, and put her eye to it, and stayed there so long I could hear my own heart filing objections.

"Hazel," she said at last, in a voice I had never heard her use — flat, and cold, and certain, "there's a man standing at the far end of your street. Under the bracket lamp. He's not hiding."

I came to the glass. He stood in the cone of yellow light with his hat brim low and his hands folded, and he made no attempt at all to be invisible, because invisibility, for a man like that, would be admitting doubt, and men like that never doubt.

I couldn't see his face. I didn't need to. I knew the stillness of him, the patience of him, the terrible tidy way he held himself — like a ledger awaiting its entry.

"He's not following me," I heard myself say. "He's letting me finish."

"Finish what?"

"Counting."
