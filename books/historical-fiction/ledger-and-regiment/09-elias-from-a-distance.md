## 9. Elias, From a Distance

A frigate at anchor is a lie told in oak and canvas, and I have become fluent in the telling of it.

The *Cerberus* sat off Wexbridge roads for the third morning running, riding a swell gentle as a sleeping animal's breathing, and every man aboard believed we were here to sound the approaches and correct the harbor chart — which was true, in the way that a coat is true when what you mean is the body inside it. My orders did say survey. They said it in the Admiralty's flat hand, with a clerk's initials at the foot, and they gave me leave to take a boat's crew ashore for soundings as required. What they did not say — what no order ever says, because orders are written by men who have learned never to write down the actual reason for anything — was that Lieutenant Elias Vane had requested this posting with a persistence that should have been noted in his file as either sentiment or madness.

I had dressed it up well enough in my letter of application. The elder Hartwell's coastal survey, I wrote, remains the finest work of its kind on this stretch of channel; its completion would be of material value to navigation in wartime. Every word of that was honest. That is the trick I have learned since taking the King's commission: never lie, merely arrange the truth like furniture until the room looks the way you need it to.

From the quarterdeck, Wexbridge arranged itself pleasingly against the grey morning: the church tower, the masts crowded along the quay like winter reeds, the smoke standing up straight in the windless air. Somewhere in that tangle of roofs was a workshop with a south-facing window where a man had bent over copper plates for thirty years, and where, presumably, he no longer bent over anything at all.

Two years now. Two years, and I had learned of it eleven months late, from a letter forwarded through three ports and a purser who could not resist adding his own postscript in a hand like a spider drowned in ink: *Sorry for your loss, sir, if it was a loss, being as how you never spoke kind of him.*

The men thought I came from nowhere. This is not difficult to arrange. A naval officer's past is whatever he says it was over the first bottle, and I had said very little, so the wardroom had invented the rest: a father somewhere respectable, a childhood of parsonage Sundays, no mother worth mentioning. I let them keep it. It was easier than explaining that my father had chosen copper plate and graver over son, and had done it so thoroughly, so early, that by the time I left home at seventeen I could not tell you whether I hated him or merely wanted, with the whole force of my being, to be looked at by him just once the way he looked at a coastline.

Both, probably. Both is the usual answer.

* * *

"Soundings party's ready whenever you are, Mr. Vane." Mr. Cobb, the sailing master, a man built like a bollard and nearly as talkative. He squinted at the town. "Odd weather for it. Glass is steady enough, but there's a smell off the land."

"There's always a smell off Wexbridge," I said. "Tar, herring, and ambition."

He grunted, which from Cobb constituted laughter, and went forward to curse the boat crew into some semblance of order. I stood a moment longer watching the harbor mouth, because there was something wrong with it that had taken me two days to name, and naming things is the one inheritance my father left me that I have never managed to refuse.

The harbor was emptying.

Not empty — a port is never entirely empty, any more than a heart is — but thin. Where there should have been forty sail waiting on cargoes there were perhaps twenty-five, and half the fishing boats were tied up rather than out, though the weather was fair enough for it. And on the customs house wall, visible even from this distance as a pale rectangle among older paler rectangles, something new had been posted. A notice. I knew the format without being able to read a word of it: government paper has a posture all its own.

Press notices. Requisitions. The war reaching down into the channel towns like a hand into a drawer, feeling for whatever could be taken.

My father had hated exactly this. He used to say — I can hear the voice still, dry as a scraped plate — that a coastline belonged to whoever measured it honestly, and that the moment governments started measuring, the measurements began to flatter themselves. *A chart made by committee,* he told me once, when I was young enough to be standing beside him instead of against him, *will drown you politely.* I had argued, because I was seventeen and the King's uniform was new and I believed then that order was a thing worth serving. He had looked at me the way he looked at an inaccurate sounding — not angry. Disappointed in the data.

I had walked out that night and had not walked back, and then there had been years of silence broken only by letters that were really invoices of grievance, and then there was no silence at all, because silence requires two people keeping it and death is a poor correspondent.

And yet.

And yet he had been working on the atlas. I knew this the way I knew the tide tables, from a letter written four years ago in his cramped surveyor's hand, the last letter he ever sent me: *The Wexbridge plates are done through the outer roads. Another season and the whole coast will stand. You may see it someday, if the Navy ever lets you off your leash.* No affection in it. No apology. But also — and this is what I had read aloud to myself more times than I will confess under oath — no closing of the door. He had written *you may see it*. Not *they* may see it. *You.*

I had read that letter aloud, too, in another context entirely, but that comes later in this account and I find I am not ready to arrive there yet.

* * *

The shore party went in with the lead-line and the log and the general air of men doing arithmetic they'd rather not. We worked the eastern approach all morning, the leadsman chanting depths in that rhythm that is half prayer and half lullaby — *by the mark five, and-a-quarter five* — while I stood in the stern sheets with a notebook and tried to conduct a survey of my father's last work without anyone noticing I was grieving.

It is strange, the things grief chooses. I could look at the church tower without trouble. The quay, fine. But when our course took us close along the mudflats below the town — the tidal flats, wide and brown and glistening, stitched with channels that shifted with every storm — I had to take out the notebook and pretend an urgent interest in the bearing of a beacon that did not require my attention.

Because I knew these flats. Not from memory alone. From the plates. As a boy I had inked proofs of them sitting on a stool too low for the table, my father correcting my line weights without ever once saying my lines were good, because they weren't, mostly, except sometimes, and he never said when they were. These flats were the first thing I ever drew that he did not wipe clean and redraw. I remembered it. He had taken the proof, held it at arm's length, and put it in the drying rack without a word, and I had lived on that wordless minute for a month.

Now the flats were going to belong to the Navy. That was why we were here, whatever my orders said about soundings; everyone in the wardroom knew it and nobody said it. Tidal flats, deep mud, a channel that could be closed with pilings — a natural anchorage for gunboats, or a place to run a boom across, depending which way the strategists were leaning that week. The old deed-holders would be bought out or brushed aside, the charts would be redrawn by committee, and my father's careful lines would be traced over by clerks until the coast flattered itself into drowning people.

I had come, under cover of a survey errand, to find out whether his work had survived him.

The workshop was easy to find. I had not intended to go near it — the plan, such as it was, involved loitering near the harbor with a notebook like a man with nothing better to do, which is a disguise that works better than it should — but the feet decide these things before the officer does. A narrow street climbing away from the water, a sign with a pair of dividers painted on it, faded almost to ghosthood. The window was lit. There was smoke from the chimney, which meant a fire, which meant someone.

I stopped across the lane and did not go in, and I want it recorded that this restraint cost me something.

Through the glass, at an angle, I could see a figure bent over a table near the window. Small — smaller than my father, who had been built like a dock piling himself. Dark hair pinned up carelessly, a smudge of ink on one cheek that she did not know about, sleeves rolled to the elbow. Her hands moved over the table with a steadiness I recognized before I could name it: the particular economy of a hand that has been trained to draw a line once and trust it. My father's hands had moved that way. Nobody else's I have ever seen, except —

Except hers.

Because I knew who she was. Of course I knew. Hazel Hartwell — Hartwell, though whether by baptism or theft of the name I had never determined, and my father had never deigned to explain — the apprentice. The girl who had appeared in the workshop when I was nineteen and already at daggers-drawing with the old man, so that I had left before I properly knew her, and thereafter had known her only in the medium we shared, which is to say paper.

She wrote to him. That was the thing. After I left, after the silence set in like frost, she wrote — short, practical letters about plates and deadlines and the state of the roof — and she always added a line at the end: *Your son asks after you.* Which was a lie, because I didn't; pride forbade it. And my father, who must have known it was a lie, who must have known that a girl of sixteen does not invent filial concern out of whole cloth unless someone has let slip that he wants to, answered those letters. Answered them, I later gathered, at length, in a tone he used with no other living creature.

And she sent copies. Or extracts. However it was arranged — I never asked, and the arrangement died with the correspondence — certain passages reached me, forwarded through ports, unsigned. Passages in which the old man, writing to his apprentice about tide-races and plate-copper, occasionally said things that were unmistakably meant for a son he could not bring himself to address directly. *Tell him the coast holds its shape better than men do.* *Tell him the atlas will bear his name nowhere, and that this is as it should be, and that I think of him when I rule a true line.*

I read those aloud. To myself, in a cabin, at anchor, in the dark — that much I'll admit. Read them aloud because silent reading let me skim past the parts that undid me, and some nights I needed to be undone slowly, sentence by sentence, like a rope paying out.

So: Hazel Hartwell. Keeper of the letters. Presumably keeper now of the plates. The figure at the window, inking something with my father's economy of line, two years after his death, in a workshop the war was about to reach into.

I should have knocked. Any reasonable account of myself would have me knock — introduce myself as the surveying officer, express professional interest in the late master's papers, arrange everything aboveboard and legal and safe. Instead I stepped back into the shadow of the eaves like a burglar in my own history, because I understood, with the sudden clarity of a man reading a chart by lightning, that if the Admiralty was posting notices on the customs house, then the Admiralty's clerks would soon be interested in exactly the papers I wanted to see. And anything I touched officially would be touched officially. Filed. Requisitioned. Perhaps buried.

There were men whose business was burying things. I had met them. They had clean hands and excellent manners and they could make a lifetime of work vanish between one polite sentence and the next.

So I did not knock. I stood in the lane like a coward with a commission, memorizing the angle of her shoulders, the tilt of the lamp, the fact that she paused — once, twice — to press the heel of her hand against her eyes the way a person does when they have been working too long at something that matters, and then resumed.

Then a door banged somewhere up the street and I remembered I was a King's officer in plain clothes loitering outside a shop, which is a hanging matter if you do it badly and a scandal if you do it well, and I withdrew. Down the hill, along the quay, past the customs house where I permitted myself, in passing, to actually read the notice.

Requisition of charts, plans, and hydrographic papers. All holders to render such documents to the Naval Commissioner's office within fourteen days. Signed, in a small precise hand: *A. Fitch, Clerk.*

Fitch. I turned the name over and found nothing under it, which was itself informative; the dangerous ones are always the names you've never heard.

* * *

Back aboard by evening boat. Cobb met me at the entry port with the tide book and one raised eyebrow, which is the sailing-master equivalent of a full interrogation.

"Well?"

"The approaches are as charted," I said. "Within the old man's tolerances, which were finer than ours."

"He was a tyrant about his numbers," Cobb said, unexpectedly. "Met him once, year of the mutiny scares. Came out to correct our chart unasked, told the captain his anchorage was wrong in a crosswind, and was right." He handed over the tide book. "Shame about him dying."

"You knew?"

"Everyone in the trade knew Aldous Hartwell, sir." He looked at me a moment longer than comfort allowed. "Everyone knows his apprentice is finishing the atlas, too. Talk of the quays. Girl's completing it alone."

Alone. Two years, alone, with the war arriving like weather. I climbed to the quarterdeck and watched Wexbridge put on its evening lights one window at a time, and I located, without difficulty, the one light that would be the workshop's south-facing lamp, and I stood there long enough that the midshipman began giving me that particular mixture of curiosity and alarm the young reserve for officers caught feeling things.

Here is what I had come to learn, and here is what I learned: the work survived. She was keeping it alive with her own hands, line by line, in a town whose harbor was draining away around her, under notices signed by men named Fitch. Whether she could keep it alive *past* the war, past the requisitions, past whatever quiet machinery was already turning — that I could not know from a lane and a lamplit window.

But I knew one more thing now, something the day had handed me unasked. In the notebook, among my soundings, I had written down — in the column where I should have recorded the bearing of the outer beacon — the words: *she draws his lines.*

Which meant the atlas was real, and near finished, and in that workshop.

Which meant that whatever the Admiralty wanted buried, she was sitting in the middle of it, alone, with a lamp burning in the window like an invitation to every wolf on the coast.

I went below, took out pen and paper, and began — with the fluency of a man lying to himself about which part is duty — to draft a letter that might plausibly be delivered to a mapmaker's apprentice in Wexbridge without bringing the wolves with it. I got as far as *Madam —* and then sat looking at the word, hearing my father's voice say *tell him the coast holds its shape better than men do*, wondering what shape I held, and whether any line I ruled toward that lit window could be trusted to come true.

Outside, the tide turned. I felt the ship swing gently at her cable, nose coming round toward the open sea, like a dog remembering which way home lies — and refusing, for one more night, to face it.
