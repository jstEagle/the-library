## 10. A Meeting on the Mudflats

The trouble with meeting a man in secret on a tidal flat is that the tide keeps its own appointments and cares nothing for yours. I had calculated it the way I'd calculate any sounding — two hours before low water, moonless, the flats exposed like the harbor's own ledger opened for inspection. What I had not calculated was how long two hours could feel when spent standing in mud to the ankles, waiting for an enemy officer who might not come, or might come with marines behind him, or might come at all only because his father's ghost had better manners than either of us.

I told myself I was there for the atlas. That was the story I'd rehearsed on the walk down from Wexbridge, past the ropewalk where the tar smell hung thick enough to chew, past the gibbet-post that hadn't held anything in years but still stood as a reminder of the harbor's opinions about treason. The atlas. Deliver it, keep the promise, come home before dawn, tell no one. Simple as a chart legend.

I did not believe my own legend. I never have been able to draw one convincingly.

* * *

He came out of the dark the way the master used to say good coastline does — all at once, after you'd convinced yourself nothing was there. One moment the flat was empty mud and the whisper of ebbing water; the next there was a man standing ten yards off, no lantern, wearing a plain boat-cloak over what I would have sworn was a naval uniform even if he'd wrapped himself in sacking. Some things you can't disguise. The way he stood was one of them: feet planted, weight even, a man accustomed to decks that move under him.

"Hazel Hartwell," he said. Not a question. He pronounced it carefully, the way you'd sound a channel the first time.

"That depends who's asking."

"Elias Vane." A pause. "Though I suppose you knew that, or you wouldn't be standing in a swamp at midnight."

"Technically it's a tidal flat. A swamp holds water year-round. This drains twice a day. My master would have corrected you, so consider it done by proxy."

Something moved across his face — I couldn't read it in that light, but I felt it land. The proxy correction. Of course it would hurt him; everything about his father was a wound dressed as a lesson. I'd known that since I was twelve years old and first understood why Aldous Hartwell never spoke his son's name above a certain volume, the way you don't speak loudly near a sleeping animal.

"You got my letter," I said.

"I got your letter." He came closer, boots making the obscene sucking sound mud makes of dignity. "Four sentences. Unsigned except for your initials. 'Your father's atlas is finished. I can deliver it or destroy it. Tell me which, and quickly.' I've been at anchor off these roads for six days pretending to take soundings for the Admiralty of a country I'm currently at war with, so that I could stand here and ask you in person which sentence is true."

"And what did your soundings tell you?"

"That the channel has shifted two fathoms eastward since my father surveyed it," he said quietly. "And that whoever finished his charts knew it before I did."

* * *

We walked while we talked, because standing still on a flat at night feels too much like waiting to be arrested. He asked where, and I led him — God help me, I led him — to the place. There's no marker. Aldous drowned at low water on a rising tide, caught in the quicksand off the old oyster beds, and they found him the next morning with his survey book strapped to his chest, dry, the pages still legible, as though the sea had taken the man and declined the paperwork. There should be a stone somewhere. There isn't. The flats are the stone. Every time I survey them I am walking on his grave and measuring it.

"He died here?" Elias said, stopping.

"A hundred yards that way, near the beds. The tide came up faster than—" I stopped. I had rehearsed this part too and found, as with the chart legend, that rehearsal was worthless. "Faster than an old man could walk through it. He was sixty-eight and he refused to hire a boy to carry his instruments. He said a surveyor who couldn't manage his own chain deserved what the coast gave him."

"That sounds like him."

"It doesn't sound like anyone," I said. "It sounds like a man talking to himself, which is what he mostly did."

Elias laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly strangled, but real. And I understood something then that I had been circling for weeks without landing on: I had loved the man, and hated him, and grieved him, and this stranger had done all three from four hundred miles away, blind, on rumor and memory. We were two readers of the same illegible text.

"The atlas," he said. "Is it truly finished?"

"Every plate. Inked, proofed, fair-copied. The last one two days ago. It took me two years because he left half of it in pencil and the rest in a hand that would embarrass a spider."

"And your name isn't on it."

"My name isn't anywhere," I said. "That's rather the theme."

* * *

Here is where I must confess to a piece of strategy, because I went into that mud armed, and not only with the atlas. I had decided — walking down, heart hammering, telling myself stories about duty and promises — that I would give him the truth about the deed. All of it. Not because he'd earned it, but because I wanted to watch his face when he heard it, and a face in the dark tells you more than a face by lamplight. Lamplight flatters. Darkness audits.

So I told him. About the sealed letter among the master's papers, the one naming Aldous Hartwell among the signatories of a deed to the tidal flats — these very flats, or near enough, the marsh and saltings running up toward the creek — land the Admiralty now needed for fortifications and careening wharves now that the war had made the harbor worth defending instead of merely using. I told him about Fitch, the clerk with the clean hands and the endless patience, who had called twice and smiled both times and made my skin crawl both times. I told him the registry record had gone missing, torn out, and that I suspected the Crown's fingers in it, though I couldn't prove whose.

"And you're named in the deed," Elias said. It wasn't quite a question either. He was quicker than I'd credited.

"'Hartwell' is named. Which would be a fine inheritance if I were legally a Hartwell. But my master never adopted me. He taught me, housed me, fed me, worked me like a press-gang mule, and never once put pen to paper to make me his. So the deed names a family that exists only in ink I mixed myself."

"And the Admiralty wants the deed buried."

"Wants the land, and wants no tangles. A clerk named Fitch has been collecting Hartwell papers with the enthusiasm of a man gathering firewood in a drought. If he finds the deed's existence, he'll void it or bury it or both, and if he finds the atlas he'll requisition it as state property and I'll have spent two years building a gift for the government."

Elias was quiet for a long moment. The water whispered along the channels, doing its patient arithmetic.

"My father owned land," he finally said, "and never told me."

"Did he tell you anything?"

"He told me the sea was the only honest employer." His voice had gone strange. "He wrote to me exactly eleven times in nine years. I kept every letter. They were mostly instructions. How to hold a sextant. Why the French charts of the Breton coast are superior to ours and what that says about us as a nation. Once, three pages on the moral character of barnacles. I answered all eleven letters. He answered perhaps four of mine, briefly, and once enclosed five pounds with no explanation, which was somehow worse than nothing."

"He talked about you," I said.

He turned. In the dark I felt it more than saw it.

"What did he say?"

I had to be careful here, and I knew it, because the temptation was enormous — the temptation to give this man a warm version of his father, a balm, a fiction. I've seen what happens to coastlines when people redraw them to suit their wishes. They wreck ships.

"He said you argued," I said. "About your commission. He said you chose the wrong side of a question he thought wasn't a question. He never said the word 'enemy' about you. He said 'my son, who is presently inconveniently employed.' Then he'd drink tea and say nothing for an hour, and I learned to do the surveying during those hours, because silence was when he thought best."

"Inconveniently employed," Elias repeated. I heard him turn the phrase over like a coin bitten for gold. "That's the most affectionate thing anyone's ever reported to me secondhand."

"He kept your letters too," I said. "In the drawer with the deed. I found them when I was sorting his effects. He kept all eleven."

The silence that followed was long enough that I could hear my own pulse in my ears, and beneath it the flats ticking away their slow clock. When Elias spoke again his voice was level in the way that costs a man something.

"Why are you telling me all this? You owe me nothing. Less than nothing — I'm the enemy officer, you're the loyal subject with contraband charts. You could sell me to Fitch tomorrow and buy yourself a great deal of safety."

"Because Fitch would sell me just as fast, and because—" I stopped. Honesty, then. The flats were as good a confessional as any; the mud absolves nothing but hears everything. "Because I spent two years finishing a dead man's work and signing nothing, and I have begun to suspect that the people who sign nothing get erased, and I don't intend to let it happen to him. Or to you. Or to me. Whatever else is true, Mr. Vane, you are in that atlas whether the title page admits it or not. Half the soundings south of the point are corrections he made the summer you were fourteen, when you sailed with him. I've seen the dates. He annotated them. Little notes in the margins — *E. took this bearing, steady hand.* I copied them faithfully onto the plates and then removed the initials, because initials invite questions."

I heard him breathe. Once, unsteadily.

"Steady hand," he said.

"That's what I said."

* * *

I gave him the atlas there, on the flats where his father drowned. It was wrapped in oilcloth and waxed canvas, tied with tarred twine, and I had carried it against my chest the whole walk down like a second heartbeat. He took it the way you take a christening child — awkwardly, too carefully, aware of dropping it.

"This will hang me," I said. "You understand that. If anyone learns I gave finished coastal charts to a foreign officer, it isn't a fine. It's the gallows, or at best transportation, and Fitch already suspects the plates exist. So don't be taken with it. Burn it rather than surrender it. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Swear it properly. On something."

There was a pause, and then, wryly: "On my father's memory. It's the only thing I own outright."

"On that, then."

He tucked the parcel inside his cloak. And then he did something I hadn't expected: he reached into his coat and brought out a paper, folded small, soft at the creases from handling.

"I took a coach through Wexbridge yesterday," he said. "Officially to inspect the harbor defenses, unofficially because I wanted to see his workshop from the street and couldn't make myself knock. While I was there I passed the registry office. It was open late — past any honest hour — and there was a clerk inside I recognized. Not your Fitch. Another one, a junior man, and he was not filing documents. He was destroying them. I watched through the window while he razored pages out of a register and burned them in the grate. When he left I went in, and the register for the old land grants was open, and this page had been cut out but not yet burned. It was in the grate. Still legible."

He pressed it into my hands. I unfolded it with fingers that did not entirely obey me, and even in the dark I knew what I was holding — I knew the format, the secretary's italic hand, the layout, because I'd stood in that registry a week ago reading this exact record with my own eyes. The deed. Or its heart: the parties, the parcels, the metes and bounds of the tidal flats, and the name *Aldous Hartwell*, and beneath the signatures the memorandum of entailment — the words I had memorized and recited to myself like a psalm for a week straight.

"...to the heirs and assigns of the said parties, their claim to stand good in perpetuity..."

"They missed one," I said stupidly.

"Fires are inefficient," Elias said. "Someone was careless. Or someone wanted it found. I couldn't tell you which, and I've stopped trusting my ability to distinguish accident from design where my father's affairs are concerned."

I stood there on the mud with my inheritance in my hands — the actual paper, the thing that could make *Hartwell* legally, provably mine — and discovered that it weighed almost nothing. A single page. All that hunger, all that wanting to belong to someone, reduced to an ounce of rag paper and iron-gall ink. I had imagined it heavier. Everything imaginary is heavier; that's the whole trick of imagination.

"Burn it," Elias said quietly.

"No."

"Hazel—"

"If I burn it, it never existed, and Fitch wins by default. If I keep it, it's evidence — of what, I'm not sure yet, but evidence cuts both ways, and I have learned this month that evidence is the only weapon a person like me is issued." I folded it small again and pushed it deep into an inner pocket, next to my body's heat. "Besides. You didn't keep it to give me. You kept it because tearing it out of the register was the first inheritance you've ever taken for yourself, and giving it away is how you finish refusing yours. Am I wrong?"

He didn't answer for a moment. The tide had begun its return; far out in the blackness the water was coming back for its property, patient as a creditor.

"You're not wrong," he said finally. "God help me, you're not wrong. Two days I've had it. Two days of thinking: this is what he actually left me, a scrap proving he belonged to a piece of mud, and I could burn it and be free of him at last. And then I thought: free of him is not something I want to be. Free of him is just another kind of orphaned." A rough laugh. "So yes. I stole our father's deed out of a fire and carried it around like a relic until I could find someone it belonged to more. Congratulations. You're the heir apparent to a salt marsh."

"Our father," I repeated.

"I said what I said."

* * *

The water was coming in earnest now, finding the channels first, filling them like veins. We had minutes, not hours. He needed to be back aboard the Cerberus before the watch changed; I needed to be home before Fitch's clerk made his morning rounds of gossip.

"There's a copy of the deed in my head," I said, as we started back across the flats, faster now. "Every line. If the paper goes, the knowledge stays. Remember that."

"And the plates?"

"Hidden where no warrant will look. Don't ask where."

"I wouldn't dream of it." He hesitated. "When this war ends — if we both—"

"Don't," I said. "Don't make promises shaped like that. Promises need room to live in, and right now there's a clerk in Wexbridge with clean hands and a warrant growing in his desk drawer like a tumor. Make me a smaller one."

"Name it."

"Come back. However long it takes. Come back and survey the estuary with me — nobody's ever mapped it properly, and it'll take two people twenty years, and I refuse to do it alone and unsigned."

He stopped walking. Out in the dark, the water touched his boot heels.

"Twenty years," he said.

"At least. Estuaries are difficult. Shifting ground, double tides, sediment. A coastline is never finished — that's rather the point of me."

For one absurd moment, standing in rising water on a drowning flat, with an act of treason inside his cloak and my whole legal future in my pocket, Elias Vane laughed — really laughed, helpless, the sound carrying flat and far over the water the way sound does at night on open ground. And I laughed too, because the alternative was weeping, and a mapmaker learns to choose the response she can survey afterward.

Then he gripped my hand once, hard, mud-slick, and said, "Keep safe, Hazel Hartwell. Whoever you decide that name belongs to," and turned and waded out toward where his boat waited beyond the shallows, and became darkness, and was gone.

I stood until the water reached my knees, because my legs had opinions of their own and none of them involved moving. And it was there, calf-deep in my master's grave with a stolen deed against my ribs, that I heard it: a sound from the seawall path behind me. A footfall. Deliberate. Unhurried. The tread of a man who has found exactly what he came looking for and sees no reason to rush.

I did not turn around. Some part of me already knew the shape of the silhouette against the last grey of the sky, the neat hat, the hands folded at rest because they had never once had to hurry in their lives.

"Clerk Fitch," I said to the water, "if that's you, you've chosen a remarkably inconvenient place for a conversation."

From the seawall, mild as morning, came the answer:

"I find the inconvenience is usually instructive, Miss Hartwell. Do come out of the tide. We should talk about your friend in the boat."
