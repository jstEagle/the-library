## 11. Listed

There is a particular satisfaction in a well-kept ledger, and I will confess it freely, because confession costs me nothing: I have never once committed a crime. Everything I have ever taken was taken lawfully. That is the whole art of my profession — to arrange the world so that what you want and what is owed to the Crown become the same object, and then to wait, politely, with clean hands.

The Hartwell file had grown pleasingly thick by the third week of November. I sat in my office above the Custom House with a small coal fire at my knee and the harbor fog pressing against the glass like something that wanted in, and I did what I do best, which is nothing dramatic at all. I wrote.

*Hartwell, Aldous, deceased, mapmaker, Wexbridge.* Beneath it: *Hartwell (apprentice), given name Hazel, parentage unrecorded.* Beneath that, in the column reserved for disposition: *Papers, plates, and instruments — seizure warranted. Deed re: tidal flats — voidance proceedings initiated.*

I have been accused — never to my face; people are rarely that honest — of enjoying this work. Enjoyment implies appetite, and appetite implies disorder, and disorder is precisely what I am employed to prevent. What I feel is closer to the pleasure of a mason watching a wall come plumb. Every stone in its place. Every name in its place. A coastline surveyed, a harbor charted, a family inventoried.

Hartwell was not even properly a family. It was an old man dead two years and a young woman with no surname she could defend in court, and between them they were holding a piece of paper that said the Navy's new breakwater would stand on ground belonging to somebody else. The tidal flats east of the point — four hundred acres of mud that would become, within the decade, a victualing yard, a powder magazine, and a careening wharf. The Admiralty had already spent the land in its ledgers. It simply had not yet told the land.

My task was to make the telling unnecessary.

* * *

The clerk who brought me the morning post — a boy called Petch with ink on his chin and ambition in his eyes, which is the standard configuration for boys in government service — set down three items and stood waiting, the way boys do, hoping to be noticed.

"Anything from the registry?" I asked.

"Registry sent round a runner yesterday evening, sir. Said the copy room'd be open late." He hesitated. "Said someone come asking after the old deeds twice this month."

I looked up. "Asking after which deeds?"

"Tidal flats, sir. The Hartwell grant."

Now, here is a lesson I learned early and have never had cause to revise: when two people want the same document, one of them is about to be disappointed, and your entire career consists of ensuring it is not you. Miss Hartwell had been to the registry. Of course she had. She was a mapmaker's apprentice; her instinct upon learning of a deed would be to go and look at it the way another woman might go and look at a mirror. And Fitch knows, said the small voice of professional caution, that a document consulted is a document that can be copied, and a document copied is a document that exists in two places, and a Crown that seizes only one of them has seized half a victory.

I dismissed Petch and wrote a note to the registrar requesting — the word *requesting* doing quiet work there — that all papers touching the Hartwell grant be held under seal pending Admiralty review. Then I took up my coat, because there are things better done in person, and because the fog was lifting into that thin gray light that makes Wexbridge look like an engraving of itself.

* * *

The registry office smelled of dust, tallow, and the particular despair of public record-keeping. The registrar, a Mr. Loame, received me with the cringing courtesy men reserve for anyone carrying a Crown stamp, and showed me to the grant books with the air of a man opening his own veins.

"The Hartwell grant," he said, running a finger along spines. "1801 enrollment. Here we — "

Here we nothing. The slot where the document should have been contained a docket stub and a paper band, snapped.

Loame went the color of tallow himself. "It was here," he said. "I'll swear it on anything you like, Mr. Fitch, it was here Thursday last —"

"When was the copy room last open late?"

He told me. Two nights past. One attendant, one fire, one door with a lock older than either of us.

I did not raise my voice. Raising one's voice is an admission that the situation exceeds one's methods, and no situation has ever exceeded mine. I thanked him. I noted the date, the hour, the attendant's name. I observed aloud that a missing document was a matter for inquiry, and inquiry required witnesses, and witnesses required names, and I would return for all three. Then I stood a moment by the empty shelf, and I confess the sensation I felt there was not anger but something colder and more durable: the pleasure of confirmation.

She had gone for it. Good. A person who moves is a person who can be tracked. A person who wants a thing tells you everything by the shape of her wanting. Miss Hartwell had revealed herself as exactly what I had assessed her to be — careful, loyal, and possessed of a claim she believed worth defending — and now she had revealed something more valuable still: urgency. Urgent people make mistakes, and mistakes, unlike crimes, require no proof. They merely require recording.

"You look pleased, sir," said Loame, with the timidity of a man hoping to be wrong.

"I am never pleased, Mr. Loame," I said. "I am satisfied. There is a difference. Pleasure passes; satisfaction accumulates." I tapped the empty shelf. "And this accumulates nicely. Whoever removed that page has committed an offense far easier to punish than any offense involving keeping it. Theft leaves traces. Custody leaves none. You have done the Navy a service today, though I doubt you feel it."

He did not feel it. Men seldom appreciate the architecture until the building stands.

* * *

That afternoon I made my second call of the day, unannounced, as is my custom. Unannounced calls are the honest man's espionage: everything gained by surprise is gained without a single lie being told.

The workshop on Chandler's Lane was shut, shuttered, and — I noted with approval — swept. But sweeps tell their own stories. There were drag-marks faint in the dust of the alley door, and the false wall behind the survey cabinet — yes, I had found the false wall on my first visit; a man who hides things builds them lightly enough to move again — stood open a hand's width, and empty. Empty walls, dragged floors, a loft across town owned by a sailmaker named Tamsin Cowper whose consignment book I had read over a customs officer's shoulder a fortnight ago. I had not needed any of it confirmed. Confirmation, like dessert, is pleasant but not nourishing.

What I wanted from Chandler's Lane was smaller and sweeter. I wanted the post.

Miss Hartwell's letters came and went through the harbor delivery, and the harbor delivery passed through a sorting house in which one desk, near the window, belonged to a widower named Cobb who supplemented his wages in ways he believed were invisible. I had bought Cobb's discretion eighteen months ago over a matter of smuggled brandy, and he had repaid the investment with the punctuality of a tide table. He handed me the day's take without being asked: a bill from a paper merchant, a printed notice concerning press rates —

— and a folded note, cheap paper, sealed with plain wax, addressed in a small upright hand: *For Mr. E. Vane, to be left at the Sign of the Anchor, to go aboard.*

Elias Vane. Aldous Hartwell's estranged son, now wearing His Majesty's — no. Not His Majesty's anymore, and there was the poison in the cup. Elias Vane wore the commission of a power across the channel, and a frigate flying those colors lay at anchor off Wexbridge roads under the thin fiction of a survey errand, and here was a letter in the apprentice's hand traveling toward it by way of a waterfront tavern.

I held the note up to the window light. Sealed paper conceals nothing from a patient man; patience is a kind of X-ray. I could feel the weight of it — one leaf, perhaps two. Short, then. A time, most likely. A place. People arranging meetings write almost nothing, because they have learned — or believe they have learned — that brevity is safety. They are wrong. Brevity is merely guilt in a smaller envelope.

Cobb watched me from the doorway with the expression of a man who has decided not to have decided anything.

"You'll want it back unbroken," he said.

"I'll want it back improved," I said, and steamed it open over my kettle there at his own desk, because I have found that performing such small surgeries in the presence of a witness keeps the witness honest. Fear of complicity is the strongest adhesive known to administration.

One leaf. Her hand, neat as a fair-copy legend:

*The atlas is finished. All of it, as he wished. Thursday, low water, the east flats — you will know the place. Come alone; the harbor watch has been doubled and I am followed. Burn this. — H.*

I read it twice. Once as evidence, once as text, and I will admit — privately, here, in the ledger of my own head where no one audits — that the second reading gave me pause. *You will know the place.* The place where Aldous Hartwell died, if the parish record and the drowned-boot story were to be credited. A son summoned to the mudflat that killed his father, by the apprentice who finished the father's life's work. There was something almost literary in it. I have no taste for literature. Literature is disorder with rhythm.

Then I dried the page, resealed it with a candle-end and a steady thumb, and considered my position, which was excellent.

Collusion between a resident of Wexbridge and an officer of a hostile power, evidenced in writing. Removal of a Crown-relevant deed from the registry. Concealment of Admiralty-seizable charts and plates. Any one of these, alone, was manageable — a warning, a fine, a quiet arrangement. Together they formed a case, and cases, unlike documents, cannot be torn out of shelves. You may steal a page. You cannot steal a pattern.

I should say, because history may one day ask, that I bore Miss Hartwell no malice. I bear no one malice; malice is expenditure without return. If anything, I admired the construction of her resistance — the false wall, the sail-loft, the letter routed through a tavern rather than the post. She had learned craft from her master, and craft is craft whether it makes charts or evades clerks. But admiration is not jurisdiction. The Admiralty had drawn its lines, the war had drawn its lines, and a woman standing on the wrong side of both with a satchel full of coastal soundings was not a tragedy. She was a discrepancy. And discrepancies exist to be reconciled.

Thursday, low water, the east flats.

I put the note back into Cobb's outgoing tray and instructed him, mildly, that it should reach the Anchor by the evening boat as though nothing had intervened. Let it travel. Let the meeting happen. Meetings are gifts to men in my trade, because a meeting gathers everyone I need into one place at one hour, wearing their intentions openly, on a mudflat with no cover and no witnesses friendly to either party.

I walked back to the Custom House through the failing light, and at the corner by the ropewalk I paused, as I sometimes do, to look at the harbor. Masts, fog, the dark line of the Cerberus at her anchorage, riding lights beginning to show. Out there somewhere was Elias Vane, wanting his father's atlas the way Miss Hartwell wanted her deed — each of them reaching for the same dead man's legacy from opposite ends of it, like heirs pulling a single sheet between them until it tears.

They thought they were conducting a private exchange. Everyone believes that, right up until the moment they learn that privacy is simply a file the Crown has not yet opened.

Back at my desk, by lamplight, I opened the master ledger and turned to the H's, and beneath the entry for the deceased I wrote the apprentice's name in full — *Hartwell, Hazel, alias, of Chandler's Lane* — and beside it the word I reserve for cases that have stopped being inquiries and become certainties:

*Listed.*

The ink dried quickly in the heat of the fire. I blotted it, squared the pages, closed the book, and sat for a moment with my hands flat on the cover, feeling the particular quiet that descends when a thing long suspected becomes a thing recorded.

Tomorrow I would draft the warrant. Tonight I allowed myself one small luxury: I poured a finger of brandy and drank it looking at the fog, and I thought of the east flats at low water, all that glistening mud laid bare, every channel and creek exposed, nothing hidden anywhere — and how much the world improves, I reflected, whenever the tide goes out.

Thursday could not come soon enough.
