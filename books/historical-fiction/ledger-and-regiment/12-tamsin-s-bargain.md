## 12. Tamsin's Bargain

Tamsin Cole could thread a sail needle in the dark, by feel alone, and she claimed she could smell a lie the same way. So when I came into her loft that evening smelling of lamp oil and fear, I did not bother to lie to her. I only stood in the doorway with my satchel heavy on my shoulder and said, "I need a favor that could hang us both."

She was working late — the Navy's orders had tripled her business even as they emptied the harbor, which was its own kind of obscenity. Canvas everywhere, bolted and folded and stacked to the rafters like snowdrifts, and Tamsin in the middle of it with her sleeves rolled and her forearms dusted white, looking up at me with the flat patience of a woman who had been expecting this visit for a week.

"Shut the door," she said. "And put that down before you tell me anything. If you're carrying it while you confess, I'll want it, and then we'll both be fools."

I set the satchel on her cutting table. She didn't open it. That was Tamsin all over — she never took hold of a thing until she'd decided whether she was willing to carry it.

"Master plates," I said. "The atlas. All of it. Aldous's last survey, finished in my hand. Fitch wants it, and he wants whatever papers came with it, and if he gets them I don't think he burns them. I think he files them."

"Files them," she repeated, tasting the word.

"Puts them somewhere they can be produced later. Against someone. Against me, probably. Against anyone whose name is written inside." I heard my own voice go thin and stopped it. "There's a consignment of yours going out soon. Neutral trader, Dutch flag, isn't there? The *Vryheid*?"

"You've been reading my ledger."

"I've been reading everyone's ledger. It's what I was trained for."

Tamsin wiped her hands slowly on her apron, taking her time about it, and I understood she was taking her time because whatever she said next would cost her, and she wanted to arrive at the price honestly rather than discover it afterward.

"The *Vryheid* sails Friday's tide," she said. "Bundled canvas, spars, tar. Nobody counts bundles of sailcloth going to a neutral port; they count guns and powder and men. Plates wrapped in oilcloth, packed flat between bolts of duck, they'd pass a customs man's eye easy enough. He'd have to want to find them." She looked at me. "Does this clerk want to find them?"

"He wants them more than he wants anything else in Wexbridge. And he's patient. That's the worst of him. A thief you can run from. Fitch just waits for the world to hand things to him, and the world keeps doing it."

"And if they're found aboard — whose name is on the wrapping?"

"No one's. I'd see to that."

"Then it's mine," she said simply. "My loft, my consignment, my manifest. You'd be gone by then, or hidden, or hanged, but the paper trail ends at Tamsin Cole, sailmaker, who shipped canvas to Amsterdam and somehow shipped an Admiralty atlas along with it." She let that sit between us the way she'd let wet canvas sit before cutting — no rushing it. "I'll do it."

The relief hit me so hard I nearly sat down on a bolt of duck. I kept my feet, barely, because I knew Tamsin, and I knew she hadn't finished.

"But," she said.

"There it is."

"You knew there'd be a but. Sit down anyway." She kicked a stool toward me and I took it, and she leaned against her cutting table across from me, arms folded, and looked at me the way she looked at cloth that had been sold to her as first quality and wasn't.

"Three days," she said. "That's how long until Friday's tide, and that's how long you have to decide something you've been not-deciding since the day that clerk first knocked on your door. Because I've watched you, Hazel Hartwell, these two years, and you are a woman trying to carry two cargoes in one boat, and any fool who works the water knows how that ends."

"I don't know what you mean."

"You know exactly what I mean, and your knowing it is written all over your face, which is why I'm saying it out loud where you can't pretend otherwise." She held up two fingers. "The deed. The promise. The land that makes your name real, and the atlas that has to reach Elias Vane before his ship sails. You've been telling yourself you can do both. You cannot. Every hour you spend copying registry pages is an hour you're not packing plates. Every risk you take for one is a risk shared by the other. And now Fitch has made it simple for you: he's hunting both, so you must save one and let the other go, and you must choose *now*, not on Friday morning when the tide decides for you."

"Tamsin—"

"I'm not finished." Her voice didn't rise. It never rose. It just got heavier, like ballast. "You asked me to hide the atlas. Fine. Done. But hear the terms, because I'm a tradeswoman and I don't work on credit. If you choose the deed — if you decide your name matters more than his promise — then the plates stay here, and they go nowhere, and when Fitch comes with his warrant I will swear I never saw them, and I may hang for the swearing, and you'll be a landed woman named Hartwell with a clean record and a freehold on tidal mud. That's one future."

She raised the second finger higher.

"If you choose the promise, then everything goes aboard the *Vryheid* — the plates, the fair copy, all of it — and you deliver it to Elias Vane yourself, tonight or tomorrow night, whichever the tide allows, and you come back here with empty hands and no deed and no proof of who you are except what people remember of you. And I will help you do it, gladly, and call it the best work of my life. But you don't get to stand at the harbor with one foot in each boat. Not anymore. The water doesn't allow it, and neither do I."

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the quay, because I couldn't bear the workshop yet and couldn't bear anywhere else either. The harbor was half-empty, ships gone or going, and the masts that remained stood up against the gray evening like a forest that had been felled from above. Somewhere out in the roads, invisible in the dusk, the *Cerberus* rode at anchor, and somewhere aboard her a man was waiting for a book his father had promised him eleven years ago, and did not know it existed, and might hang for wanting it.

Two cargoes. One boat.

I tried to argue with Tamsin in my head, walking. I tried the arguments I'd been nursing for weeks, the ones that let me sleep. *The deed is only paper,* I told myself. *Identity isn't paper.* But then: if identity weren't paper, why had I spent two years signing nothing? Why did the absence of a name under every plate I inked ache like a splinter too deep to draw? Aldous had taught me that a map was a claim — that to chart a coast was to say *this exists, I measured it, I stake my skill upon its truth.* What was a name but the same claim made of a self instead of a shoreline?

And then the counter-argument, the colder one: *Elias chose his side. He wears the enemy's uniform. Delivering the atlas to him isn't keeping a promise, it's treason with good penmanship.* But I had read his letters — the old ones, the ones Aldous kept in the bottom drawer and never spoke of, that I had found while sorting effects and read standing in the dark like a trespasser. A boy of seventeen writing to a father who never wrote back, asking questions about soundings, about currents, about whether the old lead line still sang when it hit the shingle. You don't stop being someone's child because you put on a different coat. I knew that better than anyone. I had spent my whole life being no one's child and learning how much it mattered anyway.

The truth, which I admitted to myself somewhere near the fish market with the gulls screaming their opinions overhead, was that I wanted both because I was afraid of both. The deed frightened me because it could be taken — Fitch had already shown me how easily, how legally, how politely. The promise frightened me because it could not be taken, only abandoned, and abandonment leaves a mark no clerk can file. I had been circling the choice for weeks, telling myself I was being careful, when in fact I was being cowardly in two directions at once, which is a feat of navigation no chart can render.

Aldous would have seen through me in a moment. He'd had no patience for indecision dressed as diligence. *Measure twice, cut once, girl, but for God's sake cut.* He used to say the worst error on a chart wasn't a wrong sounding but a blank space — the place where the surveyor, uncertain, had simply written nothing, leaving some future sailor to find the rocks by breaking on them.

What was I leaving blank?

* * *

I got back to the workshop near midnight and lit one candle, the stub I allowed myself now, and sat at the bench where Aldous had sat for thirty years. His tools were still laid out the way he'd left them, or the way I'd left them after him — I no longer knew which, and it occurred to me that this was the whole question in miniature. Whose marks were these? Whose life was I living, and under whose name?

I pulled the false wall panel and took out the letter — the sealed one, the one that had started all of this — and read it again by the stub of candle. Aldous's hand, older and shakier than I remembered it being, though of course it hadn't changed; I had.

*I have signed the deed, and I am glad of it, though I doubt the law will ever make much of me or mine. Let the flats go to whoever holds the name after me. I never made Hazel legitimate on paper, God forgive me, because paper seemed a poor thing to give a girl who draws coastlines truer than any clerk in the kingdom. But if the name should ever be worth having, she should have it, and I set my hand to that now.*

He had known. He had known and chosen the map over the document, the same choice Tamsin was now forcing on me, made twenty years early by a dying man who thought he was being generous. Maybe he was. Maybe generosity and evasion look identical from the inside.

And beneath the deed, the other paper — the promise. *To my son Elias, a finished copy of the coastal atlas, delivered by my own hand or by the hand I trust most in this world.* By the hand I trust most. He had meant me. He had trusted me with his son and his life's work and his name, all three at once, and had never once said so aloud while alive, because Aldous Hartwell loved the way he surveyed: thoroughly, silently, and only visible in the finished chart.

I sat there until the candle drowned in its own wax, and I understood, finally, what Tamsin was really asking. Not *which do you want.* She knew I wanted both. She was asking *which are you willing to lose* — because that was the only honest measure of either. A thing you won't surrender isn't a treasure; it's a chain.

The deed could be torn out of a registry book. It already had been, or something like it — I still didn't know who had gotten there first, and the not-knowing gnawed at me. Paper burned. Paper was seized. Paper was filed away in government offices to be produced against you at the state's convenience. Everything I wanted from the deed — the name, the belonging, the legitimacy — was stored in the most flammable, most confiscatable medium mankind had ever devised.

But the atlas — the atlas was true whether or not anyone owned it. The soundings were true. The coastline was true. If it reached Elias, it existed in the world, carried in oilcloth on a Dutch ship, copied in a hundred hands eventually, useful to sailors who would never know my name. And if it reached Elias, then Aldous's promise was kept, and I was — not on paper, not in any registry, but in fact, in the only ledger that had ever mattered to either of us — his daughter. His apprentice. His heir in craft if not in law.

The name could be printed later. Under my own hand. On a title page, in a published book, where no clerk could tear it out without tearing out the whole world's copy.

It came to me with the simplicity of a correct measurement: I had been trying to inherit a name. But names aren't inherited. They're earned, or drawn, or published. Aldous had known it. Tamsin had known it. Even Fitch, in his terrible orderly way, knew it — which was why he hunted paper instead of people. Paper is easier to kill.

I would keep the promise. I would let the deed go.

* * *

Friday was two days off, but the tide wouldn't wait for my epiphany to ripen, and neither would Fitch. I went to the sail loft at first light, before the harbor was fully awake, and found Tamsin already at work, and I said, "The promise," and she nodded once, the way she nodded when a seam came right the first time.

"Then we pack today," she said. "Not Friday. Today. The *Vryheid*'s master owes me a favor and takes early cargo without questions, but every extra day that atlas sits in my loft is a day Fitch has to find it. When does Vane sail?"

"Morning tide. Tomorrow, if the wind holds."

"Then you have tonight." She was already pulling bolts of duck from the stack, measuring with her eye, no tape needed. "One night to get to him, one night to say whatever it is you're going to say, and one morning to watch his ship go. After that, Hazel Hartwell, you start the rest of it — whatever the rest of it turns out to be."

"What if Fitch stops the ship?"

"Then we're both hanged and it won't matter what we chose." She glanced up, and for a moment the flat tradeswoman's face broke into something warmer, crooked and quick. "But he won't. Bundles of canvas, Hazel. Nobody counts canvas."

I helped her wrap the plates in oilcloth, my hands steadier than they had any right to be, and as I worked I felt lighter than I had in months — pared down, like a chart reduced to essentials, every line load-bearing. Somewhere behind the false wall in my workshop, the deed's memory sat in my head and nowhere else, and I found I could live with that. Some inheritances you carry in paper. Others you carry in your hands.

It was Tamsin herself, at the door at noon, squinting down the street, who went rigid and said quietly:

"Hazel. Don't turn around. There's a man across the lane, outside the chandler's, and he's been reading the same broadsheet for the last quarter hour — and he's holding it upside down."
