## 13. The Registry Burns Late

The man with the upside-down broadsheet was gone by evening, which was worse than if he'd stayed. A watcher who stays is a wall you can plan around. A watcher who leaves is a question that follows you into your sleep.

I spent the night at Tamsin's loft, sleeping badly on a sailmaker's bench with a bolt of canvas for a pillow, listening to the harbor breathe. Somewhere past midnight I made my decision, and it had the clean simplicity of all desperate decisions: I would go to the registry office and copy out the deed in full — every name, every signature, every seal — in my own hand, before Ambrose Fitch could find some clerkly instrument to void it. Paper could be seized. Paper could be burned. But paper copied into memory, into muscle, into ink held ready to be rewritten — that was harder to confiscate.

"Copy it," Tamsin said in the grey morning, not looking up from her needles as she patched a jib. "And then what? You'll hold it up to Fitch like a talisman? He'll laugh at you with those pleasant little teeth."

"I'll know what it says."

"You already know what it says. You read it four times."

"I know what I remember," I said. "That's not the same thing. Memory smooths things. Memory makes the words prettier than they were. I want the ugly exactness of it — the metes and bounds, the parcel numbers, the spelling of Hartwell with its two l's. If it comes down to my word against the Admiralty's, I want to be able to say it *precisely*, and mean it."

She bit her thread and finally looked at me. There was something in her face I couldn't read — not doubt exactly. More like a person watching another person walk toward the edge of a pier in weather she doesn't trust.

"The registry burns late," she said.

I blinked. "*What*?"

"It's a saying among the sailmakers' wives. The registry office keeps its lamps lit long after the customs house goes dark. The clerks there are paid by the page, so they copy and copy until their eyes give out. If you mean to do this quietly, go after the customs house closes but while the registry still burns. The street will be empty, and old Mr. Latch will be too busy squinting to notice much." She turned back to her needlework. "Just don't be seen coming out of my loft at dawn again. Once was luck. Twice is a habit."

So I waited through the day the way one waits for a tide — uselessly, impatiently, counting minutes that refused to move. I mended pens. I trimmed wicks. I ate half a roll and forgot about the other half. And when the customs house shutter came down with its final bang, I took the long way around by the fish market, where the gutting tables were being scraped and the gulls were conducting their usual riotous audit, and I came to Registry Lane from the north, hood up, walking at the pace of a woman with nothing more urgent on her mind than a headache.

Registry Lane is the narrowest street in Wexbridge, a slot of shadow between two warehouses, and the registry office sits at the bottom of it like a stone dropped in a well. It has one window, barred, and one door, oak, older than anyone who uses it. Above the door a lamp burned yellow, exactly as Tamsin promised, and beneath the lamp the door stood —

— open.

Not ajar. Not unlatched. Open, six inches, into darkness, and the darkness inside did not have the shape of lamplight in it.

I stood in the mouth of the lane for the better part of a minute, doing the arithmetic every sensible person does: *turn around, go home, write to no one, touch nothing.* And then I did the other arithmetic, the kind that has been ruining me since Master Aldous died: *if someone else reached the deed first, every hour I stand here is an hour they're ahead.*

I went in.

The lamp above the door gave me just enough light to see that the room beyond was wrong. Registries have a particular smell — dust, tallow, leather, the faint vinegary tang of old iron-gall ink — and underneath all of that, tonight, was another smell: disturbed paper, the dry scent of documents handled roughly, fanned, thrown. The great ledger chests along the east wall stood with their lids up like a row of crows with open beaks. Drawers hung out at every angle. A chair lay on its side. And Mr. Latch, the registrar, a man of seventy-odd years who had been copying deeds since before I was born, sat in the corner on a stool with his hands folded in his lap, white as his own paper.

"They weren't soldiers," he said, before I'd said anything at all. His voice was steady, which frightened me more than shaking would have. Steady meant rehearsed. "They wore no uniform. Three of them. Polite as Sunday. One held the lamp. One talked. One went through the chests."

"Mr. Latch," I said carefully, "I've come to—"

"To copy something. Yes. They always come to copy something." He looked up at me then, and his eyes were wet, but his voice stayed level. "Miss, whoever you are, whatever you came for, it's gone."

My mouth had gone dry. "The tidal flats. The parcel under the old saltmarsh grant. The deed signed—"

"—in 'sixty-one. Four signatories. One of them Hartwell." He nodded slowly. "I knew it was that one the moment they asked. They didn't have to say. They went to that chest first, like men who'd been told precisely where to look." He unfolded his hands and showed me his palms, as if presenting evidence. "They took the record entire. Cut it out with a knife, clean, right along the binding. Left the stub. They were very particular about leaving the stub — the talker said it was so the ledger would still show a folio number, so nothing would seem amiss to a casual eye." His mouth twisted. "Casual eyes. That's what we are to them now. Casual."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a deck does. I put my hand on the edge of a chest to steady myself and felt the rough grain of the wood and thought, absurdly, of Aldous's hands teaching me to hold a rule steady against wind.

"Who were they?" I asked. "Did they leave names?"

"They left payment," said Mr. Latch. He nodded toward the registrar's desk, and there, laid square and neat in the middle of it, sat a small stack of coin, counted and wrapped. "For my trouble, they said. For my discretion." He laughed once, without any humor in it whatsoever. "Seventy years I've kept these records. Do you know what a registry is, girl? It's the only place in England where a poor man's word becomes true. A field, a house, a grave plot — none of it exists except in here, in ink. Take away the ink and a family can live somewhere three generations and own nothing at all. That's what's been done tonight. Not theft of land. Theft of *truth*. And they paid me to hold the lamp while they did it."

"Did you take the money?"

"No." He looked at the coins with an expression I can only call loathing. "But I didn't refuse it either, because refusing would have told them I understood whose deed it was. Let them think me a coward who can be bought. Cowards live longer." He rose stiffly from his stool. "Now I'm going to ask you a question, and I advise you not to answer it. Did you ever see that deed? With your own eyes?"

"Yes," I said.

"Then here is what I advise, and it costs you nothing, because I never said it." He came close, and up close his eyes were fierce under the watery surface, a clerk's ferocity, the ferocity of a man whose whole life is exactness being robbed of exactness. "You saw it. You remember it. So make yourself into the registry. Every line. Every bound. Every signature and its date. Because paper can be cut out, miss, but a trained mind is a chest they haven't found the key to yet. Write it down tonight, while it's fresh, and then hide what you've written, and then — this is the important part — *forget where you hid it* until you need it. Can you do that?"

"Yes," I said, and discovered I was crying, quietly, without drama, the way rain comes to a coast — simply because conditions required it.

"Good." He patted my shoulder once, awkwardly, the way men of his generation pat anything younger than themselves. "Now go out the way you came, and don't come back. If they return and find the same visitor twice, even cowards get questions."

I walked home through streets gone soft with fog, and the world had the tilted, unreal quality of a coastline drawn from a bad bearing. Everything I had believed solid twenty-four hours ago had come loose. The deed existed — I had *read* it, I had traced the letters of my own name-to-be with my fingertip — and the deed did not exist. My inheritance was real, and my inheritance was rumor. Somewhere between those two states lived a naval clerk with clean hands, and I began, walking, to suspect that the polite man across the lane with his upside-down broadsheet had not been watching the sail loft at all.

He'd been waiting for dark too.

* * *

Tamsin found me at dawn at the workbench in the back of the loft, surrounded by candle stubs, writing. My hand ached. Ink blackened the creases of my fingers. Before me lay four pages of close, cramped script — the deed entire, or as near entire as grief and memory could make it: the metes and bounds beginning at the channel marker, running thence along the mean high-water line; the parcels numbered; the rents reserved; the signatures dated the ninth of June, 1761; Aldous Hartwell, his hand firm and young, thirty years before I was born.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"I slept between lines."

She read over my shoulder for a while, saying nothing. Sailmakers can be quiet for hours; it's one of the reasons I love them. At last she set down her needle and said, "There's a thing I don't understand. Say it plain for me."

"Ask."

"Fitch wants the atlas because charts are war-matter. That I follow. But why does the Admiralty care about an old marsh deed? Land's land. What's flats to a fleet?"

I set down my pen. And because she had hidden my master's plates behind her bolts of canvas at real risk to herself, I gave her the truth.

"Look at a chart of Wexbridge roads," I said. "What do you see at low water?"

"Mudflats."

"What do I see?" I tapped the edge of the bench. "I see forty acres that flood at spring tide and drain again — deep enough at high water to float a frigate over, shallow enough at low water to beach and repair her hull below the tide line, out of reach of any enemy's guns. I see a careening ground the Crown doesn't own. And I see that whoever holds the deed to it holds the legal power to tell the Navy *no* — to refuse access, to demand rent, to tie an anchorage in chancery for ten years while a war gets fought without it. Aldous and three other men bought worthless mud sixty years ago for the price of a horse. And now there's a war, and the mud is worth a squadron."

Tamsin whistled low. "So it's not the paper they want buried. It's the *word* no."

"That's why they took the record instead of buying it," I said. "A purchase leaves a trail. A purchase means someone said yes. Cutting the page out means the land simply... reverts to silence. And silence belongs to whoever speaks loudest next."

"And the loudest voice in Wexbridge," Tamsin said slowly, "belongs to a clerk with clean hands."

We looked at each other across the workbench, and I saw her arrive at the same conclusion I had arrived at somewhere in the fog-walking hours: the ransackers hadn't needed to search for the deed. They had known the chest, the folio, the year. Someone had given them the bearings. And the list of people who knew those bearings was short enough to count on one hand, and every finger of that hand pointed somewhere I did not want to look — toward the workshop, toward the papers of a dead man, toward the possibility that Aldous Hartwell's effects had been read by more eyes than mine long before I ever sorted them.

"There's one more thing," Tamsin said. She hesitated, which was unlike her, and then reached beneath the cutting table and brought up a scrap of paper, sea-damp at one corner, the kind of cheap sheet used for wrapping butter. "Boy brought it round yesterday evening, just after your friend with the broadsheet cleared off. No name on it. He said a sailor gave him a penny to see it delivered."

I took it. Inside, in a hand I did not know — quick, slanted, pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

*If the record is lost, the memory is not. Ask the woman at the sail loft what the master promised, and whether promises survive a change of government. There is still one copy of everything, and it floats. — E.V.*

I read it three times. Around us the loft filled gradually with morning light and the hammering of the yard next door, and Tamsin watched my face do whatever it was doing, and did not comment.

"He knows," I said finally. "Elias knows the registry's been hit. How does a man on an enemy frigate at anchor in the roads know what happened last night in Registry Lane?"

"Maybe he doesn't know," Tamsin said. "Maybe he guessed. Maybe men who tear pages out of ledgers always guess alike." She crossed her arms. "Or maybe, Hazel, the question isn't how he knows. The question is the one he wrote. What did the master promise — and who else knows about the promise besides you and him and me?"

I looked down at the note, and at my four handwritten pages beside it, my inheritance rebuilt in ink at the cost of a night's sleep, and I understood with a cold settling certainty that the game had changed shape entirely. Fitch was hunting paper. Elias was speaking of copies. And somewhere in the middle of it, I had stopped being the keeper of a dead man's atlas and become a piece on the board myself.

"One copy of everything," I murmured, "and it floats."

"A ship," said Tamsin.

"The Cerberus," I said. And outside, far down the harbor, the morning fog was lifting off the water exactly as it does before a tide turns.
