## 15. What Can Be Carried

There is an arithmetic to flight, and I had learned it late and unwillingly, the way one learns most arithmetic that matters. A satchel holds a certain number of pounds. A body can carry a certain number of pounds for a certain number of miles before it lies down in the road and refuses all further negotiation. Everything else — everything you love, everything you are, every scrap of paper that proves you were ever anybody at all — must fit inside that arithmetic or be left to the fire.

I had two days left of Tamsin's three. She had not repeated the terms. Tamsin never repeated terms; repetition, she said, was for people who expected to be argued with, and she did not extend me that courtesy. But I could feel the days ticking down the way you feel a tide turning under a boat — not by watching the water, but by watching what it does to everything moored to it.

So on the second evening I locked the workshop door, drew the shutter over the window that faced the ropewalk, and set about the business of deciding which parts of my life were worth the carrying.

* * *

The workshop of Aldous Hartwell had always been a museum of almosts. Drafts upon drafts, each one a coastline nearly right, each one superseded by a coastline actually right. He had kept everything — the failed soundings, the charts drawn in a hand so young and cramped it could only have been mine at fourteen, the practice plates I'd scratched and spoiled learning how to hold a burin against copper without letting it skate off and ruin a week's work. He had kept my first attempt at the Wexbridge roads, which showed the sandbar a full cable's length east of where God had actually put it, a cartographical error that had, to my certain knowledge, grounded at least one coal barge and given old Captain Merrick a story he still told at the Anchor for the price of a pint.

I laid it all out on the long table under the lamp. It looked like a life. That was the trouble. Spread out, it looked exactly like a life, and lives are not portable.

The fair-copy atlas went first into the satchel — no decision there, no deliberation required. Bound in grey boards, two hundred and eleven pages, every chart inked fair, every soundings table ruled and checked twice. Two years of my hands. It weighed rather more than a Bible and meant rather more, though I supposed the Admiralty would have disputed the ordering. Then the master plate of the final chart — the one I had finished inking two years after his death, the estuary mouth with its treacherous shifting bar rendered in lines fine as eyelashes. Copper, fourteen by twenty inches, wrapped in flannel. It went in like a sleeping child, if children weighed eleven pounds and could be ruined by a single scratch.

That left the rest.

I had made a list, because I am my master's apprentice and my master made lists of everything, including, on one memorable occasion, the contents of his own pockets, annotated with his opinion of each item. The list had two columns. *Keep.* *Burn.* The keep column was short. The burn column was everything else, and I found I could not begin.

It is a strange thing, burning paper. You would think paper burns easily, and it does, but it burns *reluctantly*, the way a cat leaves a warm chair. It curls and blackens and holds its shape a moment after it should have surrendered it, and in that moment you can still read what you wrote, and you read it, because you cannot help yourself, and then it is gone.

I started with the drafts. That was supposed to be the easy part — they were superseded, they were wrong, the fair copy had made them obsolete. I fed the first one to the stove and watched my fourteen-year-old self's crooked coastlines curl up orange, and I felt nothing, and then I felt everything, which is the usual order of such things.

Because it was not the paper I was burning. It was the evidence of becoming. Every bad chart was a day I had been bad at this and kept at it anyway. Every spoiled plate was a week of Aldous standing behind my shoulder saying *again, Hazel, the line must be confident even where the coast is not,* in that voice like dry gravel, and me hating him, and loving him, in proportions that shifted daily and settled, over the years, into something I had never had a word for and still don't, though *father* was the word I did not use, because he had never used it either, and we were both too proud, or too frightened, or too something, to be the first.

The letters were harder.

His letters to me — no, that's not right. He never wrote me letters. He wrote *marginalia*, which is a kind of letter if you're the sort of person who reads other people's working notes, which I am, having been raised on them. *Check this bearing — H. is careless after supper.* *Tide tables for Froward Point suspect; resurvey in spring.* *The girl improves. — A.H.* That last one, in the corner of a soundings sheet from my sixteenth year, three words in his gravel hand, and I sat on the floor with it for a quarter of an hour like a fool, and then I burned it, because *the girl* could be anyone, and that was rather the point.

The other letters were worse. The correspondence with the Hydrographic Office, with Mr. Quill, with the two surveyors who had wintered with us in '01 and drunk all the brandy. Innocent, nearly all of it — but *nearly* is a word that has no standing before a clerk like Ambrose Fitch, who could make a man hang from the word *nearly* if he set his mind to it. There was the letter from Aldous's cousin in Bristol mentioning, in passing, *your boy Elias's commission — a strange road for a surveyor's son,* which tied my household by name to an officer now serving on the enemy side of the Channel. There were three letters from Elias himself, written before the war, when he had still written to his father, stiff and formal and aching underneath the formality, letters Aldous had never answered but had never thrown away either, and which I had read perhaps forty times and never told a soul.

I held those three a long while. They were not mine to burn. They were not mine to keep, either, and the arithmetic of the satchel does not concern itself with whose a thing is, only what it weighs.

I burned them. I stood at the stove and burned them one at a time and read each one through as it died, because that seemed the only honest funeral — to let the words be witnessed once more before the dark took them. *I hope the survey of the eastern approaches goes well. I remember the eastern approaches. I remember everything.* He had been twenty. He had signed it *your obedient son,* which is a phrase that tells you everything about a family by everything it fails to say.

The ash went into the grate with all the rest. I did not cry. I am told some people can burn letters without crying, and I have decided to believe them, the way one believes in mermaids.

* * *

What remained was the deed.

Or what remained of the deed, which by then was a single page — the torn leaf Elias had pressed into my hand on the mudflats, the record Fitch's men had ripped from the registry book before I could reach it, the page Elias had stolen back from the thieves, which is a sentence that would have made his father laugh, or weep, or both, quietly, at his desk, in the manner to which we were accustomed.

I sat with it under the lamp and turned it over and over. The vellum was soft at the edges from two hundred years of hands. *Hartwell* appeared among the signatories in ink gone the brown of old tea. The tidal flats. The flats where Aldous had died with his theodolite in the mud and the tide coming in around him, because he was stubborn and the light was good and he wanted one more bearing.

The deed was worth a name. That was the whole of it, laid plain. I had been Hazel Hartwell the way a boat is called a ship — by custom, by affection, by everyone agreeing not to look too closely at the paperwork. The deed, properly entered, properly witnessed, would make it true in the only way the world recognizes: in ink, in a book, in a registry office with a clerk who could find it again. It would make me a landholder. It would make me *somebody's* — his, legally, at last, posthumously, the way he had made me everything else: indirectly, silently, and too late to thank him.

And Fitch wanted it destroyed. Fitch, with his clean hands and his endless patience, wanted the flats for the Navy and the Hartwell name struck from the record, and he had already torn the page out of the book, and the only copy left in all the world was in my lap, and it was the size of a page, and a page is very light, and the satchel had room.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the one luxury I have never been able to afford and am therefore always overdrawn on: I almost kept it.

I sat there and I planned it. Fold it small, sew it into the lining of the satchel, carry it to France or Falmouth or wherever the tides took me, find a lawyer, make the claim, become Hazel Hartwell *de jure* instead of Hazel Hartwell *de facto*, walk into rooms and have doors open that had only ever been ajar for me. I planned it the way I plot a chart — carefully, in pencil, with corrections. And then I did the surveying that matters, the kind Aldous taught me, the kind where you measure not the coast but yourself, and I found the flaw in the chart.

The flaw was Elias.

The deed in my keeping was a page torn from a book. A page without its book is a curiosity; a page *returned* to its book, or at least to the hands that could argue for it, might yet be a deed. And Elias had torn it out at risk of his commission, his neck, and whatever the two of us had become on the mudflats in the dark, which we had not named and had no time to name, and he had given it to me not as an heirloom but as a *trust* — I could hear the word in his voice, though he hadn't used it — *it's yours by every law but the one they're using, and I'd rather you have it than the mud.*

But the flats were his inheritance too. The deed named Hartwell, and Elias was a Hartwell's son, whatever name he sailed under, and if I carried that page across the Channel in my satchel it would be nothing but a souvenir of a country that had erased itself. If it stayed — if it reached the right hands, the neutral trader's hands, the hands Tamsin was arranging — it might yet be argued. Might yet be *entered*. Might yet make true, on paper, in a book, what had only ever been true by custom.

The promise was the atlas. The deed was the name. And I had promised Aldous — in the marginalia of my own heart, in the only contract we ever executed — that I would finish the work and put it in his son's hands.

You cannot carry both. That was the whole lesson of the evening, the lesson the satchel had been trying to teach me since sundown. The atlas or the deed. The work or the name. The thing I made or the thing I was made by.

I chose the way he would have chosen, which is to say I chose the work, and hated myself for it, and knew the hatred was a form of vanity, and chose anyway.

* * *

I did not burn the deed. Let that be recorded, wherever records are kept for women who are nobody's daughter. I wrapped it in oilcloth, and I sewed it into the hem of my oldest cloak — the grey one, the one no searcher looks at twice because it looks like poverty, which in my case it mostly was — and I told myself I was not keeping it, I was *routing* it, the way you route a chart around a hazard. A lie, possibly. Cartographers learn to live with small inaccuracies at the margins.

Then I burned the rest.

The drafts went first, all of them, a decade of my hands going up in orange. Then the correspondence, the innocent and the nearly-innocent alike, Quill's careful commercial letters, the surveyors' receipts, the brandy inventory. Then the practice plates — I could not carry copper I did not need, and every plate bore my hand, and my hand was the thing being hunted. The stove ate for two hours. I fed it until the grate glowed and the room smelled like a library drowning, and I sat on the floor with my back against the workbench and watched ten years of becoming turn to ash, and I will not pretend it was clean or noble or bearable. It was arithmetic. That is all it was. The satchel holds so many pounds, and I had measured, and the measure had spoken, and I had obeyed it the way sailors obey the tide — not because it is kind, but because it is true.

Near the end I found one thing I had forgotten: a chart of the harbor, drawn small, on a half-sheet, in two hands. The coast in Aldous's gravel line-work. The soundings in mine, added years later, when his eyes had begun to fail him and he had handed me the numbers and said *you do the water, girl, I'll do the land.* Two hands on one sheet. The only document in the workshop that was entirely, equally, both of us.

I held it over the stove for a long time.

Then I folded it into eighths and put it inside my bodice, against my heart, where it weighed nothing at all, which is the only measurement that had ever made sense to me, and I let the stove have everything else, and I banked the fire, and I sat in the dark of a workshop that no longer contained a single word of proof that I had ever existed in it, and I felt — lighter. That was the obscene part. That was the part I will not apologize for. Stripped down to the atlas and the plate and the page sewn in my hem and the chart against my heart, I was lighter than I had been in two years, and I sat there in the ash-smelling dark and discovered that I could breathe.

Identity pared to essentials. That was what Aldous would have called it, and then he would have made a list.

* * *

I was wrapping the plate for the second time — flannel, then oilcloth, then sacking, a cocoon of precautions — when I heard the knock.

Not the knock. Not Fitch's knock, which I had learned the way you learn a weather sign: three measured raps, evenly spaced, the knock of a man who has never in his life had to ask twice for anything. This was the other knock. The quick double-rap, thumb-side of the fist, that Tamsin used because she said knuckles were for people with something to prove.

I opened the door on the chain, and she slipped in sideways like weather, and she took one look at the stove, at the ash, at the satchel, at me, and she said, "You've done it, then."

"I've done it."

"Good." She set a covered lantern on the bench and did not look at the fire, which was a kindness, because Tamsin's kindnesses were always the kind that pretended not to be looking. "Then you'll want to hear what I heard at the loft an hour ago. A runner from the customs house, asking after sailcloth for a government consignment. Asking *which* sailcloth. Asking which loft, which bales, which nights."

The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere down the ropewalk a dog began, quietly, to be worried about something.

"Tamsin," I said, "when does the *Merrow* take her consignment aboard?"

"Tomorrow night, on the dark of the tide. Which means the plates go aboard tomorrow night. Which means, Hazel—" and here she looked at me at last, straight on, with the expression she reserved for storms and other unavoidable facts, "—tomorrow you stop being a woman who owns a workshop and become a woman who owns a satchel. Are you ready?"

I looked at the ash in the grate. I looked at the bare walls, the nail-holes where ten years of charts had hung, the ghost-outlines in the dust where the master plates had stood behind their false wall, now open and empty as a held breath.

"No," I said. "But I'm measured."

She snorted — Tamsin's highest form of praise — and turned up her lantern. "Then we'd best get you and your eleven pounds of copper down to the loft before the customs man's runner finds his tongue. And Hazel —" she paused at the door, and her voice dropped into the register she used for the things that mattered, which was nearly the same as her register for storms, "— there's a watch boat sitting off the harbor stairs tonight. Two lanterns. They weren't there yesterday."

Outside, the dog had stopped barking, which was somehow worse.
