## 16. Fitch Closes In

I had always assumed that if the crown ever came for me, it would come with trumpets. That was the trouble with a childhood spent reading naval dispatches for their marginalia: I had learned war as a pageant, drums and colors and ships heeling grandly into the wind. Nobody had told me it arrived instead on a Tuesday morning, in the person of a customs runner with bad boots, followed by six marines and a warrant.

Tamsin heard them before I did. She was up in the loft's high gallery, stitching a jib, and her needle stopped mid-stroke — which, in Tamsin, was equivalent to another woman screaming.

"Back room," she said. "Now."

I had slept in the sail loft three nights running by then, on a cot among the bolts of canvas, and the master plates were folded into the consignment bales like letters tucked into envelopes: eleven sheets of copper, each one engraved with a stretch of coast that His Majesty's Navy very much wished to own and did not yet know existed in private hands. They were wrapped in oiled sacking and packed inside two finished sails bound for the *Meridian*, a neutral trader out of Flushing whose captain owed Tamsin more favors than he could comfortably count.

I went through the back room door as the knock came at the front, and the two sounds — my latch clicking shut, his fist meeting oak — landed almost together, like a cannon's flash and its thunder.

Through the seam between the boards I watched her go down the stairs. I will say this for fear: it sharpens the eyes. In that one glance I took in more of Ambrose Fitch than I had managed in all our previous meetings combined. He had removed his hat — courtesy again, always courtesy — and stood holding a paper at arm's length as though it were something he'd found on a shoe. The marines fanned out behind him with the bored precision of men who had done this forty times and would do it forty more.

"Mrs. Tamsin Crowe," Fitch said. It was not a question; clerks do not ask questions they have already answered in triplicate. "Proprietor of this establishment."

"That's me." Tamsin leaned against the doorframe with her arms crossed, and I swear to God she looked *bored*. "And you've brought soldiers to buy canvas. That's either flattery or an insult, sir, and I haven't decided which."

"A requisition inspection." He held up the warrant. "Under authority of the Admiralty. All stock, stores, and goods held on these premises are subject to examination."

"All stock." Her eyebrows went up. "Every bolt? Every thimble?"

"If need be."

"Well." She stepped aside with the air of a woman opening her pantry to a beggar she intended to make feel guilty about it. "Mind the tar on the stair. And mind the dog — he bites officers on principle. I've never had the heart to correct him."

The dog in question was asleep under the cutting table and did not so much as lift his head, which was fortunate, because principle or no principle, he was a coward, and cowards talk.

* * *

What follows is the longest hour of my life, and I include in that reckoning the night my master died on the mudflats with his sounding line still in his hand.

They went through the loft the way water goes through a wreck: patiently, thoroughly, without malice and without mercy. I sat in the back room with my back against the wall and counted my breathing, the way Aldous had taught me to steady a hand over a penning knife. In through four. Out through four. The plates are hidden. The plates are hidden. The plates —

One marine lifted one of the consignment bales.

I heard the sacking shift. I heard the canvas within it give that particular soft slither that finished sails give when they're moved — a sound like nothing else in the world, a sound I had been listening to since I was twelve years old, and I understood in that instant exactly how loud it was, how loud it must seem to a man who had been sent to find precisely such a sound.

"Careful," Tamsin called from across the loft, not hurrying, not rising from her stool. "That's the *Meridian* order. You bend a bolt-rope there, Mr. Soldier, and Captain Verhoeven'll have your pay for a year, and I'll let him."

The marine set the bale down. He did not open it. He moved on to the next shelf, and I put my forehead against my knees and discovered that my hands were shaking so badly I could hear the cot frame rattling behind me — no, that was my teeth. Both.

Because here is the thing I had not properly understood until that moment: it was not my life I was afraid for. My life, I had already quietly signed away somewhere around the false wall, the way you sign away a coat you've decided not to wear into the rain. It was hers. Tamsin had done nothing. Tamsin had sold sails to neutral traders for fifteen years and paid her taxes and fed the harbor watch pie at Midsummer, and she was about to lose all of it — the loft, the trade, possibly her liberty — for a girl who wasn't even family, who was nobody's daughter, who was, legally speaking, a smudge on the parish register.

She didn't have to do any of this. She had said so herself, the night I'd arrived with eleven pounds of copper on my back: "You know I could refuse you." And then she'd made up the cot.

* * *

Fitch worked differently from his marines. Where they ransacked, he read. I watched him through the board-seam as he moved along the walls reading the labels on the sail bins, reading the chalk tallies on the slate, reading — and this made my blood stop — the ledger that lay open on the counter, the one in which Tamsin recorded every commission, every consignment, every name.

He read it for a long time. Then he closed it, gently, the way one closes a book one has memorized, and carried it with him to where Tamsin sat.

"You do work for the *Cerberus*," he said.

"The frigate? Aye, when her boatswain can be bothered to row ashore. Canvas for the awnings, mostly. Officers want shade; sailors want dry feet; the Admiralty wants both and pays late."

"Mm." He laid the ledger down in front of her and tapped one entry with one clean finger. "This consignment here. Bound aboard the *Cerberus*, marked received by Lieutenant Vane."

"Elias Vane," Tamsin said. "Aye."

"You knew him before the war."

"I knew his father before the war, same as half this harbor. Master Hartwell bought his duck from me for twenty years. Boy used to carry it home on his shoulder when his shoulders were big enough." She shrugged. "War's a long way off, Mr. Clerk. Sails is just sails."

"Just sails." Fitch smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, evenly proportioned, and utterly empty, like a chart of a coast with no soundings on it. "You'll forgive me, Mrs. Crowe, but I am paid to notice that nothing is ever just anything. A sail is cloth, and cloth is flax, and flax is a field in Norfolk, and a field is rent, and rent is a man's livelihood, and a livelihood is a reason a man does what he does. Everything connects. That is rather the whole of my profession." He turned the ledger toward himself again. "So when I see the name Vane entered in a sailmaker's book in a port the *Cerberus* has visited twice — twice, Mrs. Crowe, in a war where her station should keep her fifty leagues east — I begin to wonder what the sails are carrying."

"Fear," said Tamsin. "Same as everything else in this harbor. You'd know that better than me; you're what they're afraid of."

For a moment — I measured it, I was measuring everything by then, breaths and heartbeats and the width of board-seams — for a moment the smile fell off him entirely, and I saw underneath it something that frightened me worse than the marines had: not anger, but *certainty*. The look of a man who has never once doubted that the ledger he keeps is the truest thing in England.

"I am going to ask you a plain question," he said. "Is Hazel Hartwell on these premises?"

The back room was dark. The window was shuttered. There was a bolt of canvas stacked in front of the door that had not been there yesterday — Tamsin had seen to that at dawn, saying it wanted re-rolling, and now I understood it had wanted nothing of the kind. But a marine was standing four feet from that door, and if Fitch said the word, four feet was nothing.

"No," said Tamsin.

"Hm." Fitch took out a pencil — a pencil, God help us all, a little silver-cased pencil — and made a note in a pocketbook. "You understand that sheltering a person named in an Admiralty inquiry constitutes—"

"I know what it constitutes. I can read." She stood up. She was not a tall woman, but she had spent thirty years hauling canvas in a harbor wind, and when she planted herself in front of that small neat clerk, something in the room rearranged itself around her, some old law older than warrants, the law that says the woman in her own loft outranks the king in his own navy. "Mr. Fitch. I've buried a husband the press gang took and a brother the fever took, and I've buried neither of them any deader for having loved them. If Hazel Hartwell walks into this loft tomorrow, I'll feed her dinner, same as I would you, and I won't inform on her for doing it, same as I wouldn't expect you to inform on your mother for setting your place at table. That's not sedition. That's being a person. Now — are you buying canvas today or aren't you?"

The silence went on so long I could hear the harbor gulls outside, wheeling and crying over the mudflats as though the world below them were still ordinary.

Then Fitch pocketed his pencil, picked up his hat, and inclined his head with perfect courtesy.

"Not today, Mrs. Crowe. Thank you for your time." At the door he paused, and did not turn around. "Tell Miss Hartwell — if she calls — that her master's workshop was searched this morning as well. The false wall behind the plan chest was quite empty. Someone got there before us." His voice stayed mild as milk. "Whoever it was has perhaps a week. Perhaps less. Good day."

The door closed. The marines' boots went down the stairs. The dog woke up, yawned, and went back to sleep.

* * *

I don't remember crossing the back room. I remember the light changing, and then Tamsin was holding me up by both arms, and my face was wet, and I hadn't noticed crying start, which is the worst way to find out you've been doing it.

"He knows," I said. "Tamsin, he knows about the wall, he knows about the *Cerberus*, he —"

"He suspects. Knowing and suspecting are different beasts, and only one of them bites." She steered me onto a stool and pushed a cup of something into my hands. It was tea gone cold, and I drank it anyway. "Sit. Breathe. You did fine."

"I did *nothing*. I hid in a room while you —" My voice cracked straight down the middle. "You lied to a crown officer. To his face. With a warrant in his hand. Do you know what happens to you if he comes back and finds me here? They hang people for less, Tamsin. They hang people for *less*."

"They also hang people for nothing at all, which is why I've stopped arranging my life around hanging." She sat down opposite me, took the cup back, drank the rest of my cold tea herself, and looked at me with those grey eyes that had sized up weather and customers and me, all with the same unsentimental accuracy. "Listen to me. Fifteen years I've run this loft alone. No husband, no children, no sister, no one. I told myself it was freedom." She turned the empty cup slowly on its saucer, round and round, a small tide of its own. "It was just emptiness with good light. Then you come stumbling in here at midnight with a sack full of the whole coastline on your back, scared out of your wits, too stubborn to cry — and I thought, ah. There she is. The family I forgot to have."

"Tamsin —"

"So don't you dare thank me," she said, "and don't you dare apologize, and above all don't you dare get caught. Because I lied beautifully in there and I'd hate to waste it." She stood, brisk again, the storm passed, the practical woman restored — but her hand, when it touched my hair on the way past, stayed a moment longer than briskness required. "Now. He gave you a week. Clerks always overestimate their own patience and underestimate other people's legs. What's the leg-work left to do?"

I looked at my hands. They had stopped shaking.

The atlas was in the bales. The fair copy — Aldous's own fair copy, the dedication written in his hand years ago, naming me — that was still at the workshop, or wherever the searchers hadn't reached it. And Elias was at anchor off the roads with a promise waiting on him that had already cost one deed, one registry record, one old man's peace, and nearly one sailmaker's neck.

"The tide," I said. "There's a night tide tomorrow. High water a little after two."

Tamsin looked at me for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way she nodded at a wind that meant business.

"The *Meridian* sails Friday," she said. "Which means the bales go out to the boat Thursday morning. Whatever's leaving this harbor leaves with them, or it doesn't leave at all." She was already moving, pulling charts of her own from under the counter — harbor charts, boat tracks, the small secret geography of a working waterfront. "You'll want a boat that doesn't answer hail. Old Cobb's wherry, the one with the patched gunwale — he owes me since the jib job. Two hours out, two hours back, if you row like your supper depends on it."

"It might," I said.

"Don't joke about it. Or do." She glanced up, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not the storm-register, not the praise-register, but something quieter, that I think was simply love wearing work clothes. "Hazel. Whatever's out on that ship — whoever he is to you — you bring yourself back. The atlas I can replace. Copper's copper. You I cannot."

Outside, the gulls wheeled over the flats, and the tide began to turn, and somewhere off the harbor stairs, I knew, a frigate rode at anchor with my master's son aboard and my master's promise in the balance — and somewhere across town a small neat man with a silver pencil was writing my name in a book, patient as ink.

Two days. One tide. Eleven pounds of copper, one fair copy, one borrowed wherry, and every friend I had in the world standing between me and a warrant.

I picked up the oiled sacking and started packing.
