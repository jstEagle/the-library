## 17. The Night Tide

There is a particular arithmetic to rowing a small boat out of a guarded harbor in the dark, and it goes like this: every stroke you take is a coin spent, and the harbor watch is the moneylender, and he does not extend credit.

I had done the sums three times over before I ever touched the oars. The Cerberus lay at anchor in the roads, past the mole, past the chain that would be raised come full dark — but chains are for ships, not for a twelve-foot skiff with a woman in it who has spent nine years learning to read water the way other people read scripture. Tamsin's cousin had left the boat for me behind the salt-works, and I had paid for it in silence and a promise to return it by dawn, which was, when you counted honestly, the shortest lease of my life.

The atlas rode wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, lashed with sailmaker's twine, riding where a heart would be if my heart hadn't climbed up into my throat somewhere around the second stroke.

I want to say I was afraid. I was. But fear is a strange cargo; it weighs nothing going out and everything coming back, and there were moments between the salt-works and the mole when the fear went quiet and what remained was only craft — the pull of the tide running east, the smell of tar and cold weed, the shape of the harbor at night, which no chart in Master Hartwell's atlas could render because no surveyor worth his lead line would ever be fool enough to need it.

The watch boat sat off the custom-house quay with its lantern hooded, two men aboard playing cards by feel, if their murmuring meant anything. I gave them a wide berth along the shadowed side of the mole, shipping oars and paddling with one hand like a lady out for an illicit midnight dip, which in fairness was not far from the truth of it. A dog barked on the quay. A man coughed in some loft above the fish-stalls. Wexbridge at night is a town holding its breath, and everyone in it pretending they aren't.

Past the mole the water opened and the wind found me, and I let myself breathe for exactly as long as it took to find the frigate's silhouette against the last grey of the sky.

She was bigger than I'd imagined, though I'd drawn her kind a hundred times — the masts like two bare winters, the anchor lights burning low and orange at her stern. Somewhere aboard her slept Elias Vane, who had been a boy shouting on these same mudflats, who had been a young man quarreling with his father loud enough to shake the shingles, who had been a name on letters his father never answered and then a name his father never spoke at all. And now he was the reason I was committing, by any honest reading of the law, treason.

I had rehearsed the speech the whole way out. *Your father promised you this. I made him a promise about you. Here it is. Good luck, God keep you, don't come back.* Clean, brisk, a delivery of goods and nothing more.

Then I came alongside under her quarter, and a face leaned over the rail with a shuttered lantern in it, and every word I owned went out of me like air from a pricked bladder.

"You're late," Elias Vane said quietly. "You said the turn of the tide."

"The tide turned early." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Tides do that. It's why we map them."

A rope came down. I tied off the satchel first — priority, always priority — and then my wrist, and went up the ship's side hand over hand like something hauled aboard by accident, which was nearly accurate.

* * *

His cabin was barely a cabin — an officer's berth on a warship is a coffin that's been talked into standing upright — but there was a lamp, a chart table, and a decanter, and after nine days of sail lofts and false walls and looking over my shoulder until my neck ached, it felt like a palace. He shut the door. We stood a yard apart, two people who had known each other entirely through other people, and did not speak.

He looked like his father. That was the cruelty of it. Aldous Hartwell had been eleven years dead, and here was his jaw, his brow, his way of standing with his weight back on his heels as though the ground might present an invoice. But the eyes were different — softer set, and watching me the way I imagine a man watches a coastline he has only ever known from charts.

"You're smaller than your letters," he said at last.

"Letters exaggerate." Then, because honesty is a habit I can't break even at gunpoint: "They weren't my letters. They were his reports, and I copied the fair hands. You knew that."

"I knew he dictated them to someone whose spelling improved year over year." Something moved through his face — grief arriving late, as grief does, like weather from a long way off. "I used to read them aloud to my messmates. *The apprentice again notes that the master is stubborn regarding soundings off the point.* Eleven years old and already insubordinate on paper."

"I was accurate on paper. There's a difference."

"There is," he said, "and it's the whole difference between us, isn't it."

I unwound the twine. My fingers shook, and I hated them for it. "Hold still. This isn't a gift I can give twice."

The oilcloth came away, and the fair-copy atlas lay in the lamplight — every plate, every sounding, every careful lettered name along ninety miles of coast that Aldous Hartwell had walked, rowed, measured, and argued with for thirty years, finished at last in ink mixed the week before, by me, in a locked room, with my heart going like a drum. Elias put his hands flat on the cover and did not pick it up. I understood that. Some things you have to let settle before you lift them, like stones from a riverbed.

"He promised me this," he said. "The last summer. Before—" His thumb moved along the edge of the boards. "Before I told him I'd taken the commission. Before I said the things you say when you're twenty-two and certain that conviction costs nothing." He looked up. "Did he finish it knowing I'd be on the other side?"

"He finished it knowing nothing except that it wasn't finished. He died with the pen in his hand, Mr. Vane. Whatever he thought of you, he didn't leave it in the margins."

"Elias," he said. "If you're handing me my inheritance on a stolen night tide, we might stand on Christian names. Hazel."

It was the first time anyone had said my name in that tone — not as an apprentice, not as a question mark where a daughter should be, but simply as itself. I discovered that I had been waiting for it for rather longer than nine years, which was inconvenient information to receive at sea, in wartime, in a room the size of a cupboard.

"I kept the promise," I said, gruffer than I felt, because feeling is a thing you batten down or it takes the helm. "That's all tonight is. A promise kept. Don't make it into more than it can carry."

"No," he agreed softly. "We wouldn't want to overload the boat."

* * *

He poured wine neither of us drank. He asked about the plates — whether copies existed, where they were, and I lied to him without effort, which surprised me; apparently treachery is a craft like any other and I have an aptitude. He told me how he'd come to be anchored in the roads under a surveying errand so thin that even his own captain had seen through it and granted it anyway, out of what sailors call humanity and officers call inconvenience tolerated.

"And Fitch?" I asked. "Do you know him?"

"I know of him. Ambrose Fitch does not exist below the level of rumor, and the rumor is that he has never once raised his voice and has ruined forty men." Elias turned the atlas toward himself, studying the title plate. "If he's hunting this, Hazel—"

"He's hunting it. He offered me protection in exchange for it, which in his language means a cell with better light." I took a breath. "And my name is listed. For investigation. Which in his language means worse."

Something hardened in him then, the family jaw setting at the angle I'd seen in Aldous's death-mask portrait that Quill kept hidden in a drawer. "Then you shouldn't go back."

"I have a boat and a tide and a workshop full of evidence, and I'm expected at dawn by a friend who is hiding contraband on my behalf. Running is how people get caught, Elias. Staying is how I stay legible." I almost smiled. "Besides. If I vanish, everything I've done becomes confession. If I stay and answer plainly, I'm only a mapmaker who returned borrowed property to its owner."

"Whose owner is a declared enemy officer."

"Yes, well." I lifted my chin. "Nobody's perfect."

He laughed — once, briefly, the laugh of a man who has been at war too long to spend it freely — and then he reached inside his coat, and what he brought out stopped my breath.

A single page, folded twice, soft at the creases from handling, the registry's watermark showing pale in the lamplight. Even before he opened it I knew what it was. I had dreamed of that page for a fortnight: the deed to the tidal flats, the signatures along its foot, *Aldous Hartwell* among them in his narrow, impatient hand — the paper that would have made Hartwell legally mine, that would have converted nineteen years of being nobody's daughter into a name with land attached to it, a self with foundations.

"It was torn out," I said stupidly. "Quill told me the record was gone. Ripped from the book."

"It was." He laid it flat between us on the chart table. "By me. Three nights ago, from the ash bucket behind the registry office, where a clerk of mine watched a naval courier burn a stack of papers and assumed the wind would finish the job. The wind was careless." His eyes came up to mine. "Hazel. This is yours. By blood if not by law, by work if not by blood, and by every measure I trust. Take it."

For a moment I could not move. Every hungry thing in me leaned toward that page — the name, the legitimacy, the ending to the sentence *she is no one's daughter* — and I let it lean, because refusing too quickly is just another kind of lying.

"Why do you have it?" I asked finally. "Why give it to me and not use it yourself? It's your father's name too. Your inheritance."

"Because I watched my father spend thirty years measuring a coast he loved and die owning nothing but the measurements." Elias's voice stayed level, but underneath it something was bleeding. "Because I took a commission to spite him, and he answered my letters with silence, and we both of us died to each other years before either of us died at all. Because if I carry that page across the channel it buys me nothing but a lawyer, and if you carry it, it buys you a name." He pushed it an inch toward me. "One of us ought to inherit something from that stubborn old man besides his silences. I've already had my portion. I got his jaw and his temper and his handwriting, God help me. Take the rest."

"And what will you tell Fitch's people when they ask how the page survived?"

"That the wind was careless." The ghost of the laugh again. "Let them audit the weather."

I picked up the page. It weighed nothing. Paper always weighs nothing; it's the meaning that has mass, and this had tons of it, and my hands knew it even as the scales insisted otherwise. I thought of the registry at night, of Quill's confession still sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone — *I tipped him off, girl, I thought cooperation would keep us safe* — of everyone in this story bargaining with the same machine and paying in the same coin.

"If I'm caught with this," I said, "it hangs me."

"If you're caught with nothing," he answered, "it drowns you slower. I know which I'd choose."

So I folded it along its soft old creases and put it inside my shirt, against the skin, where it beat faintly with my pulse like a second heart — a colder one, an older one. And that was the moment, absurdly, that I nearly wept: not over the deed, not over the atlas, but because Elias Vane watched me hide it and did not look away, and there was in his face such careful, disciplined tenderness that I understood we had run out of night before either of us had run out of things to say.

"We can't name it," I said. It came out before I decided to say it, the way truth does. "Whatever this is. There's a channel between us wider than the one you're sailing home across, and tides won't fix it, and I won't pretend otherwise at four in the morning on an enemy deck."

"No," he said. "But I'd like it noted in the log."

"Noted," I managed, "and unsigned."

"For now." He stepped closer — close enough that the lamplight put us both in shadow, which seemed right — and pressed his palm flat over the place where the page lay against my heart, and held it there for three seconds, or possibly three years. "Deliver your boat. Answer him plainly. Be legible, Hazel Hartwell. And write to me the way you used to — accurate, insubordinate, and in a fine fair hand. I'll read it aloud."

"To whom? Your messmates are the enemy."

"They're my messmates," he said. "It's a distinction I've learned to live inside."

* * *

Going down the side was easier than coming up; partings usually are, the body understanding before the mind that the hard part is over. He lowered the satchel — empty now, the atlas staying aboard, lashed where he'd keep it, he swore, closer than his sword — and then his hand lingered on the gunwale a moment above mine.

"The tide turns in twenty minutes," he said. "Row east of the watch boat. They shift position at the half-hour; it's the laziest crew in the channel and I mean that as a compliment to your timing."

"You've been watching them."

"I've been watching for you since dusk. Everything else was reconnaissance." And then, quieter, the words almost lost under the slap of water on the hull: "Thank you for finishing it. He couldn't have given it to me himself. So you did. Both of you, I think."

I pushed off. I did not look back immediately, because I am a professional, and professionals check their heading first — and because I knew if I looked back at the lantern-lit square of his face at that rail, I would say something that could hang us both, and I had already carried quite enough hanging-weight for one night.

The tide took me east of the watch boat, exactly as promised. The two lazy sentinels never stirred. Wexbridge assembled itself out of the dark in its familiar pieces — mole, custom-house, the black teeth of the harbor chain, the grey smudge of the salt-works — and I rowed in under the last of the ebb with the deed against my heart and the satchel empty and the whole sky going the color of pewter, which meant dawn was perhaps two hours off.

Two hours to stow the boat. Two hours to sleep, or attempt it. Two hours before I walked into my own workshop and answered whatever the day intended to ask me.

I shipped the oars behind the salt-works and stood a moment in the reeds, letting the cold come up through my boots, doing the arithmetic one final time. Deed: delivered to me. Atlas: delivered to him. Promise: kept entire. Name: still mine to earn, and no paper in England going to do it for me.

And yet, as I climbed the path toward the sleeping town, I could not shake the sensation — the mapmaker's sense of contour, of pressure building along a fault — that the night had been too easy by exactly one piece. The watch boat had been idle. The chain had been slack. The harbor had let me pass both ways like a gate left open on purpose.

Ambrose Fitch never raised his voice, and he never wasted a tide.

I walked faster.
