## 19. The Examination

They did not put me in a cell. I want that recorded, because I had imagined a cell — I had, in fact, rehearsed for a cell the way an actress rehearses for a role she hopes never to play — and what I got instead was a room above the customs house with a good fire, a decent chair, and a pot of tea that had gone lukewarm out of what I can only assume was deliberate policy. Comfort, I was learning, is an instrument too. A cell tells you what you are. A comfortable chair tells you what they want you to believe you might still be.

Ambrose Fitch sat across the table from me with a leather folio open before him and his hands folded on top of it, and I will grant him this: he had the best hands for stillness of any man I have ever met. Sailors fidget. Mapmakers fidget — we are always wanting to measure something. But Fitch's hands lay on that folio like two sleeping animals, and I understood that the stillness was the whole argument. *I have nowhere to be,* the hands said. *I have all the time the Admiralty can print.*

"Miss Hartwell," he said. "Or shall we begin with the question of the name?"

"You may begin wherever you like," I said. "I'm told the tea is included."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not a smile, but the memory of one, filed away for later use. He opened the folio.

What followed was three hours of the most careful conversation of my life, and I include in that count every argument I ever had with Aldous Hartwell over the proper weight of a coastline, and I once watched him argue a point of hydrography for a full afternoon against a man who turned out to be deaf, which he did not discover until the end, and which he afterward called the most satisfying debate of his career.

Fitch laid out his evidence the way I would lay out a chart: north at the top, everything in its measured place, nothing included that did not serve the reader. He did not accuse. He *displayed*. It was, I confess, masterly. If the man had been born twenty years earlier and into poverty, he might have made a fine cartographer, and the country would have been the worse for it.

* * *

"Item the first," he said. "On the fourteenth of March, a requisition was posted for all hydrographic materials in the port of Wexbridge. You were seen by the harbormaster's clerk removing a satchel from the Hartwell workshop at half past nine in the evening. The satchel was heavy. You walked with your weight forward, which is the posture of a person carrying a burden against the chest."

"I carry everything against the chest," I said. "It's how I was taught. A burden carried on the back shifts your center of gravity and you cannot feel the road."

"And where is the satchel now?"

"Carried against my chest, presumably, wherever it is."

He wrote that down. I have often wondered what he wrote. I imagine it was something like *subject evasive but composed*, and I imagine he underlined *composed*, because I believe my composure offended him more than my evasions did. A frightened person confirms things by accident. A composed person gives you nothing, and Fitch had built a career on the interest paid by frightened people.

"Item the second," he said. "The registry office on Cooper's Lane was entered on the night of the ninth. The page recording a certain deed of conveyance, dated 1771, concerning tidal flats below the Hartwell holding, was removed. Not destroyed — removed. The knife-work was clean. A person in a hurry tears. A person with a purpose cuts." He looked up. "You have steady hands, Miss Hartwell. I have seen you hold a pen."

"You have seen me hold a pen," I agreed. "You have not seen me hold a knife. The two are different trades. Ask any cook."

"Item the third. A note was intercepted — I will not say how, as the how reflects no credit on anyone — addressed to an officer aboard His Majesty's— " he paused, delicately, "—aboard a certain frigate presently in these roads, and signed with your initial. The note made reference to *the morning tide*, to *what was promised*, and to *the flats where he died*. Do those phrases mean anything to you?"

"They mean," I said, "that whoever wrote that note has a poor sense of what can be proved by a phrase. *The morning tide* occurs daily. *What was promised* might be a loaf of bread. And *the flats where he died* — well. Half of Wexbridge died on those flats, one way or another. The mud keeps accounts for everyone."

He wrote again. The pen scratched. Outside the window a gull screamed at something, or at nothing — gulls being the only creatures in this port with a fully honest relationship to their own opinions.

"Item the fourth," Fitch said, and here he set down his pen, which I had learned by then to read as a change of instrument, the way a surgeon sets down the probe and takes up the knife. "The name Hartwell appears on a list. I am not at liberty to show you the list. I will tell you that the list is not a good list to be on, and that names come off it in one of two ways: by cooperation, or by exhaustion. The second way takes longer, and the person whose name it is does not always enjoy the removal."

I looked at my tea. It had gone fully cold now, which at least meant the policy had achieved its natural conclusion.

"Mr. Fitch," I said, "may I ask you a question?"

"You may ask. Whether I answer depends on the question."

"When you were a boy — and you were a boy once; I have checked, everyone was — did you ever make anything? Not a ledger. Not a list. Something that did not exist until you made it exist. A model boat. A drawing. Anything."

He regarded me for a long moment, and I saw the question go into him like a sounding line into deep water, down and down, touching nothing, coming back clean. That, I thought, is the answer. He made nothing. He *ordered* things. There is a difference, and the difference is that a man who makes things knows what they cost, and a man who only orders them knows only what they fetch.

"I fail to see the relevance," he said.

"No," I said. "I don't suppose you would."

* * *

He returned to the items. There were, in the end, eleven of them, and I will not rehearse them all here — some were trivial, some were invented, and one or two were true in ways that made my stomach tighten even as my face did nothing, which is a skill I did not know I had until that afternoon taught it to me. The true ones were the dangerous ones. He knew about the ropewalk. He knew about Tamsin's loft — though not, I judged from his phrasing, what was in it, or he would not have been sitting so comfortably. He knew that Aldous Hartwell had never filed adoption papers, and here he paused, and here, for the first time all afternoon, he leaned forward.

"Let us speak plainly," he said. "You are not his daughter. Not in law. Not in blood. The name you wear is a courtesy, extended by a dead man who never made it legal, and I can make it not exist with the stroke of a pen. Do you understand? There is no Hazel Hartwell. There is a Hazel — something — apprentice to a dead mapmaker, in unlawful possession of materials the Crown has requisitioned, in correspondence with an enemy officer, and connected by thread after thread after thread to a deed the Admiralty has an interest in burying." He folded his hands again. "Or — and I offer this sincerely — there is a young woman of evident intelligence who cooperates, and who wakes tomorrow still free, still employed, and still, if she wishes it, Hazel Hartwell, because I am the only man in this room with the power to make that name real, and I am prepared to use it."

And there it was. The whole trap, sprung not with iron but with ink. He was not offering to spare me. He was offering to *make me* — to conjure my name out of his folio the way a conjuror pulls a ribbon from his fist, on condition that I become his ribbon. I thought of the deed, burned. I thought of the atlas, gone out on the night tide to a man on an enemy deck. I thought of Tamsin, who had stood in front of a warrant with her arms crossed and lied to a clerk of the Admiralty with the serenity of a woman describing the weather, and who had never once in ten years asked me what my name legally was, because to her it had never been a legal question.

"You are quite right," I said.

Fitch's eyebrows rose a fraction. I had surprised him, and I resolved to do it as often as possible, since surprise was the one commodity he could not requisition.

"I am not his daughter," I said. "There are no papers. There never were. He taught me to hold a chain and read a theodolite and ink a coastline so fine you'd swear the sea itself had drawn it, and he never once thought to write down that I was his, because — and here I am guessing, but I have had two years to guess — because he believed the work was the proof, and papers were for men who needed something other than the work." I leaned forward now, matching him. "So you may strike the name from your list, Mr. Fitch. You may strike it from every list in the Admiralty. You may rule that no such person exists. And I will still be the one who finished the atlas. I will still be the one who knows every sounding on that coast better than any officer in your service, because I drew half of them and checked the rest. You cannot make me not exist. You can only make me not *yours*. And I was never yours to begin with."

The fire ticked. The gull outside had found a new grievance.

"That is a very fine speech," Fitch said quietly.

"It is not a speech," I said. "It is a survey. I have been taking soundings of myself all afternoon, same as you have, and I am reporting my findings. You asked what I am. I am no one's daughter. I am a mapmaker. Everything else is annotation, and annotations can be burned."

He looked at me for a long time — long enough that I began, for the first time, to feel the cold tea in my stomach and the ache in my shoulders from a night spent rowing — and then he did the last thing I expected. He closed the folio.

"You understand," he said, "that I am obliged to hold you until this is resolved."

"I assumed as much. Is there a cell after all? I've been rather looking forward to comparing it with the chair."

"There is a room," he said, "with a window that does not open, and I would caution you against regarding it as a stage." He stood, and gathered his folio, and paused with his hand on the door. "One more item, Miss Hartwell. Item the twelfth. It came in an hour ago, and I have not yet had time to examine it."

"What is it?"

"A fair-copy atlas," he said. "Recovered this morning from a consignment of sails bound for a neutral trader. The loft was searched. The sails were opened. And this—" he tapped the folio, "—was found sewn into the lining of a jib, wrapped in oilcloth, with no name on it anywhere. No name at all." His stillness came back, the two sleeping animals of his hands, and for a moment I saw something almost like regret cross his face, or perhaps it was only the lamplight moving. "A document with no name is a curious thing. It wants an owner. And I find, sitting across from you all afternoon, that I have begun to wonder whose name belongs in it."

He left. The door closed. The fire ticked, and the gull screamed, and I sat alone in the comfortable chair in the comfortable room and did the arithmetic of ruin: Tamsin taken or fled, the atlas seized, the plates — the plates I did not know, the plates might still be at the bottom of a neutral hold bound for anywhere, or might be in this building, item the thirteenth, waiting its turn.

And then I heard his boots on the stair, unhurried, patient, going down — and stopping, I realized, one floor below, where the lamplight of some clerk's desk would be good and the table would be clear, and where a man with clean hands would open a fair-copy atlas to its front matter, as a careful man always does, to see what the title page had to say.

I had not written the title page.

I had never been permitted to write the title page.

I closed my eyes and tried to remember — truly remember, letter by letter, in my master's hand, which I knew better than my own — what Aldous Hartwell had written there, two years and more ago, in ink now dry as bone, in a book I had sewn into a sail and sent out into the world to speak for me.

I could not remember. And it would speak anyway.
