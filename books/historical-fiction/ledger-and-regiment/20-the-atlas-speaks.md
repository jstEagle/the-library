## 20. The Atlas Speaks

Ambrose Fitch had a theory about paper.

He had held the theory for eleven years, through four postings and one demotion he preferred to call a reassignment, and the theory was this: that all human wickedness eventually committed itself to paper, because men could not bear for their sins to go unrecorded. Liars kept ledgers. Traitors kept letters. Smugglers kept lists of ports they swore on their mothers' graves they had never visited. Paper was where vanity outlived caution. A man might burn his correspondence in a panic, but he would keep his accounts, because the accounts were *his*, and pride was the one informant that never required payment.

So when the soldiers brought him the contents of the Hartwell workshop — three crates, two locked chests, and a quantity of loose charts that looked as though a cartographer had exploded — Fitch did not begin with the obvious things. He began with the inventory, and then with the letters, and only then, when he had established the shape of the household's dishonesty in his own mind, did he permit himself the fair-copy atlas.

It had been found, according to Sergeant Bray's report, sewn inside a sail. This detail had already begun to irritate him. Sewn inside a sail implied forethought, and forethought implied an accomplice at the sail loft, and an accomplice at the sail loft implied that Miss Hartwell had not acted alone, and Fitch disliked cases that grew new limbs faster than he could amputate them. He wanted the case tidy. He had been promised a tidy case: a dead surveyor's papers, a disputed deed, an apprentice with no legal name worth the wax to seal it, and an enemy officer who had made the fatal error of writing things down.

Instead he had spent two days among crates that smelled of linseed and salt, and every document he confiscated seemed to have been selected by someone with a better understanding of his methods than he had.

The atlas itself was a beautiful object, which put him immediately on his guard. Beauty in evidence was like charm in a witness — not proof of guilt, but never innocent. It was bound in grey boards, hand-sewn, the sewing so even it might have been machine work if machines had existed for such things. On the spine, in small gilt letters: *A Survey of the Coasts from Wexbridge Roads to the Skerries.*

No author. No title page attribution beyond that. Fitch noted the absence with satisfaction. Here was the flaw in the whole sentimental edifice: a book without a name on it belonged to whoever held it, and he held it.

He cleared a space on his desk, which took some doing; the desk was currently defending itself behind barricades of the master's correspondence. He drew the lamp closer. Outside, Wexbridge was being rained upon in the indifferent manner of port towns everywhere, and somewhere below a soldier was arguing with a cat about jurisdiction. Fitch opened the atlas to the first leaf.

And there, written across the top of the dedication page in a hand he recognized from two years of hunting its smaller descendants through registers and letter-books — the strong downstrokes, the peculiar way the old man crossed his *t*s like a man fencing — Aldous Hartwell had written:

*To my apprentice, Hazel Hartwell, who has earned her name twice over: once by birth, which was no fault of hers, and once by this work, which was entirely to her credit. The coasts herein were surveyed by me, but they are drawn in her hand, corrected by her judgment, and finished after my death by her patience, of which I had too little and she, God help her, will need more than she knows. Let no clerk or commissioner mistake whose book this is. It is hers, given freely, in my own hand and hers together, this fourth day of October, in the year 1802.*

*A.H.*

Below it, in a second hand — finer, quicker, the letters leaning forward as though impatient to be done — the words: *and accepted, H.H.*

Fitch read it twice.

Then he sat very still, which those who knew him would have identified as his most dangerous state, and considered the fact that the dead man had beaten him.

Not defeated him — Fitch was careful with words; precision was the whole of his religion — but *beaten* him. Outdrawn him. There was a duelist's term for it and Fitch, who had never fought a duel and never intended to, nevertheless felt the cold specific shame of the man standing in the morning mist watching smoke rise from his opponent's pistol while his own ball had gone wide.

Because here was the difficulty, laid out in iron-gall ink by a man two years drowned or buried or however Aldous Hartwell had contrived to leave the world: the apprentice's name was not something she had claimed. It was something she had been *given*, dated, witnessed by the page itself, in the master's own hand, years before any of this business with the deed had surfaced. Fitch could seize papers. He could void a registry entry, intimidate a registrar, list a family for investigation, and he had done all three with the clean conscience of a man tidying a room. But he could not unwrite a dead man's dedication. There was no warrant for that. There was no *office* for that.

He turned pages with fingers that wanted to hurry and forced them not to. The plates were magnificent, which was beside the point and yet entirely the point: soundings in neat columns, bearings annotated in that same quick forward-leaning hand, corrections in red where the apprentice had disagreed with the master and, judging by the marginal notes, won. *Aldous insists 4½ fathoms; I measured thrice and find 5. Trust the lead, not the memory.* In another margin: *E.V. — see how the shoal has marched eastward since your last summer here. Come back and we shall argue properly.*

Fitch closed the atlas gently, which surprised him. Gentleness with objects was a habit he permitted himself precisely because he extended it to nothing else.

So. The girl had told him the truth. That was the intolerable part. She had stood in her workshop with the rain coming through the door behind her and said, in that level voice he had spent two interviews trying to crack like a walnut, that she was no one's daughter and nothing but a mapmaker — and he had filed the statement under *defiance* when it belonged under *testimony*. She had not been claiming the name. She had been declining to claim anything else. And the entire time, the proof of what she actually was sat bound in grey boards in her keeping, speaking on her behalf in her dead master's voice, saying everything she was too proud or too frightened to say herself.

She had sent it away rather than surrender it. To Vane. To the enemy. Fitch turned this over and found, to his considerable annoyance, that he could not make it monstrous. If a dying father-figure had asked him — no. Fitch had no dying father-figures. He had the Admiralty, which asked nothing and simply took, and had taken from him once already, in the matter of the reassignment. He knew exactly what it was to hold something of value and watch it requisitioned by men who called theft *procedure*. He had merely decided, long ago, that it was better to be the procedure than the thing procured.

He was aware, distantly, that this was the thought of a man losing an argument with himself, and he set it aside with the rest of the day's inconvenient inventory.

Sergeant Bray knocked, entered, and stood at attention in the doorway dripping with the conviction that he was not wanted, which was accurate.

"The sail loft woman, sir. Tamsin Crowe. She's asking — politely, mind — whether she's charged with anything, on account of she'd like to know whether to open the loft tomorrow."

"On what grounds would I charge her?"

"Sewing, sir," said Bray, with the delicacy of a man handling someone else's grenade. "Or not sewing. She says she can't recall what went into which sail, sails being much alike, and she'd want it noted that she said so."

Fitch looked at him. Bray had served under him for fourteen months and had learned to translate. What the sailmaker was telling them, in the language of canvas, was that she would swear blind to ignorance, and that her ignorance would be unimpeachable, and that she was informing him of this *in advance*, as one professional courtesy to another.

"Tell Mrs. Crowe she is charged with nothing," Fitch said. "Tell her the Crown thanks her for her cooperation."

"She'll like that, sir," said Bray. "She'll laugh."

"I imagine she will."

Bray withdrew. Fitch remained at his desk with the atlas before him, and the rain came down, and he did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since he opened the cover.

The case against Hazel Hartwell now consisted of: possession of papers she had legally inherited, in a name she had legally been given, by a man who had documented both gifts in advance. The deed was another matter — the deed still smelled of evasion, and the torn registry page still smelled of something worse, and Elias Vane was still an officer of a hostile power who had anchored off Wexbridge under a survey errand thin as communion wine. That thread Fitch could still pull. That thread he *would* pull, because a man needed something to pull or he was not a man but a fixture.

But the girl herself — the girl had become, between one page and the next, the wrong kind of defendant. The kind whose examination made the examiner look foolish. He had seen it happen in courtrooms: the moment when the accused's plain truthfulness made the prosecution's elaborate architecture appear to be what it secretly always was — a story, built by men, about a person who had merely lived. Juries forgave smuggling. They forgave even treason, sometimes, if the traitor's grandmother wept convincingly. What they never forgave was being made to feel that their indignation had been borrowed against bad security.

Fitch had no intention of standing in a courtroom holding a dead man's dedication page like a fisherman displaying an eel that had slipped his grip.

There remained one avenue, and it was narrow, and it embarrassed him to look down it. Vane. The note. The frigate that had weighed anchor in the night like a thief leaving a party early. If collusion could be established — actual correspondence, actual intelligence passed — then the atlas became evidence rather than inheritance, and evidence could be held regardless of dedications. It would require finding where the girl had sent her letters, whom she had paid, which window in the harbor watch had been bribed or charmed. It would take weeks. And it would mean pursuing, across a closing sea, a book that had already told everyone whose it was.

Fitch took up his pen, and wrote a memorandum to his superior recommending that the seizure of the Hartwell papers be *regularized* — the word doing quiet heroic labor — and that the question of the deed be settled administratively, "the said Hazel Hartwell appearing to hold a colorable interest which it would be neither economical nor seemly to contest." He wrote the sentence and stared at it. It was, he realized, a surrender wearing a uniform. Somewhere in the last hour he had stopped building a case against her and started negotiating terms with a dead man.

He signed it anyway. Procedure, whatever else it was, had the great virtue of providing a door out of any room.

Then he sat back, and the lamplight lay along the grey boards of the atlas, and Fitch — who did not believe in ghosts, believing instead in paper, which was worse — understood that the room contained three people: himself, and the living mapmaker who had out-told him, and the dead one who had out-written him, and that he was outnumbered in his own office.

He blew out the lamp.

* * *

I heard about it the way Wexbridge hears about everything, which is wrongly, quickly, and in four versions before breakfast.

Tamsin arrived at Quill's back room before the fish carts, shaking rain off her shawl and her dignity in equal measure, and announced that the soldiers had struck their tent outside her loft at dawn and that the young lieutenant — Bray, the polite one, the one who blushed — had touched his hat and said the Crown thanked her for her cooperation.

"Thanked me," she repeated, dropping onto the bench as though the word had physical weight. "For cooperation. Me. I lied to a naval officer's face for six hours about the location of a seam."

"He believed you?" Quill asked, over the kettle.

"He *thanked* me. That's what frightens me, old man. Men who arrest you believe you're guilty. Men who thank you know something you don't."

Quill poured tea for three, which was his manner of deciding matters before they were raised, and looked at me across the steam with an expression I had learned to distrust, being composed half of apology and half of news.

"There was a visitor yesterday evening," he said. "After you'd gone to bed. A clerk. Not Fitch — a lesser creature, one of Fitch's ink-stained jackals, come round the offices collecting signatures on a receipt." He slid a paper across the table. "For the returned effects of the Hartwell workshop. Inventory attached."

I read the inventory with the particular slowness of a woman checking whether her own funeral has been correctly itemized. Three crates. Two chests. Loose charts, sundry. And there, third from the bottom: *One atlas, fair copy, bound in boards, grey — returned to claimant upon settlement.*

"Settlement," I said.

"Of the deed," Quill said gently. "They've dropped it, Hazel. Or folded it up small enough to fit in an envelope. There's a pension in it, and a freehold — the tidal flats themselves, or the greater part, minus whatever the Navy wants for its anchorages, which is to say the parts with water near them. It's not the victory. It's the draw. But it's drawn in your name." He hesitated, and the hesitation was the loudest thing in the room. "*Hazel Hartwell,*" he read aloud, from the receipt. "Legally. In an Admiralty hand."

Tamsin was looking at me. Quill was looking at me. The kettle ticked as it cooled, marking time the way kettles do, as if even boiling water wished to remind me that everything measured must eventually pour.

I should have felt triumphant. I had rehearsed triumph often enough, in the nights when the alternative was despair: myself standing straight-backed while the clerks slunk home, the name mine at last, the land mine, the whole rotten ledger balanced. Instead what I felt was closer to the sensation on the mudflats when the tide turns — that vast unhurried shift beneath your feet, the water beginning to come for places that were dry ground an hour ago, and no single wave to point to as the cause.

Because I knew what had turned it. I had not argued Fitch into retreat; I had not bested him; I had not even seen him since the raid. Something had spoken for me. Something bound in grey boards, sewn into sailcloth, carried out on a night tide by my own hands — something I had shipped into the dark like a letter to a drowned man, trusting the address.

"Aldous," I said. Not to either of them. To the teapot, possibly.

"Eh?" said Quill.

"Nothing." I smoothed the receipt flat on the table, and my finger stopped on the line beneath the inventory, the line I had read past once already because hope makes a careless reader of us all. There was a notation there, in the clerk's cramped official script, appended like an afterthought:

*N.B. — Dedication page examined. Authorship confirmed per deceased's declaration. Claimant advised that title passes accordingly.*

*Per deceased's declaration.*

He had known. Two years dead, and he had known — had sat at this same table, probably, in this same chair, with the rain doing this same thing to the window, and written me into existence in the only court that mattered, dating it, signing it, sealing it against exactly this season of clerks. All those years I had signed nothing. All those years I had believed the silence meant I was nothing, when in truth he was holding his fire, saving his one shot for the man who would come to take me apart.

"You're crying on the receipt," Tamsin observed. "That's government property now, I expect."

"I am not."

"You are. Blink, then."

I blinked. I looked at the paper, at the small dry words doing the largest work any words had ever done for me, and understood that I had been granted everything I had starved for — and that it had arrived the wrong way round, backwards, like a reflection in water. The deed was settled. The name was mine. The atlas had spoken.

And none of it was the thing I wanted, which was out past the harbor bar, lashed to the chest of a man on a ship I could no longer see from any hill in Wexbridge, carrying the only other copy of my life's work into a war.

Quill refilled my cup without asking. "There's post," he said, too casually. "Came with the tide this morning. Neutral trader's bag — Tamsin's contact owed me a kindness." He produced a letter, worn soft at the corners from a long voyage, addressed in a hand I knew almost as well as my master's: the quick forward-leaning letters, eager even at rest.

It was addressed to *Miss Hazel Hartwell, Mapmaker.*

Not apprentice. Not *the Hartwell woman*. Not care-of anyone.

Mapmaker.

Tamsin pushed it across the table before I could decide whether my hands were steady enough. "Well?" she said. "Are you going to read it, or are we going to sit here all morning watching you memorize the envelope?"
