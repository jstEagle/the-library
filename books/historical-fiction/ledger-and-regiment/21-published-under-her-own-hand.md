## 21. Published Under Her Own Hand

The first copy arrived in March, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string that had been knotted by someone who clearly hated knots. I cut it free with the same knife Aldous Hartwell had used to trim his pens for twenty years, and then I sat very still at the workbench and did not open the parcel for the better part of an hour.

There is a particular cowardice reserved for moments like that one. I have faced down a naval clerk with a warrant, rowed across black water toward an enemy frigate with a stolen page of a deed sewn into my bodice, and lied to His Majesty's government with such fluency that I half believe my own inventions. But I could not bring myself to cut the string on a book. Because a book is a verdict. Everything before it is testimony; the printed thing is what the world decides you were.

When I finally opened it, the smell came first — ink and size and fresh paper, the smell of a thing newly alive — and then the title page, and there it was, set in type that some compositor in London had handled without ever knowing what it cost:

*The Coastal Atlas of Wexbridge and the Southern Approaches. Surveyed by Aldous Hartwell. Completed and Engraved by Hazel Hartwell.*

*In her own hand.*

I had asked them to strike the last line. I had written three letters about it. The publisher — a patient, shrewd man named Corben who had survived forty years in the trade by agreeing with everyone and doing precisely as he pleased — had replied each time with the same sentiment dressed in different words: *Miss Hartwell, the line stays. It is the best advertisement a book has had since the Flood.* Apparently there was no arguing with a dead man's dedication and a living woman's notoriety combined. Fitch's examination, the raid on Tamsin's loft, the whole absurd theatre of the Crown versus a mapmaker's apprentice — it had traveled, as scandal does, faster than any post rider, arriving in London parlors embellished past recognition. By the time the atlas was ready for sale, people were buying it less to consult the coastline than to see what all the fuss had been about. Corben said this shamelessly, rubbing his hands together like a man warming them at someone else's fire. "Notoriety sells, Miss Hartwell. Virtue merely sits on the shelf."

So the line stayed. And sitting there in the quiet workshop, with March rain ticking against the south window, I found I could no longer remember why I had wanted it struck. Perhaps because *in her own hand* sounded like something written about a forger. Or perhaps because I still flinched, somewhere old and unexamined, from claiming things outright. I had spent twenty-two years learning to hold a pen steady over other men's names. The habit of effacement dies slowly, and it does not go quietly; it goes the way a tide goes, in small retreats, leaving weed on stones to mark how far it once reached.

I carried the atlas to the window and looked at the plate I knew better than my own face: the southern approaches, Wexbridge Roads, the mudflats where Aldous had died with his boots full of water and his notebook held above the tide like a man surrendering his sword hilt-first. Two years of my life were drawn into those lines so finely that you would need a lens to find the seam where his surveying ended and mine began. That was the point. That was always the point. A map should not show its mending. But the title page showed it plainly, and the world, for once, approved of the seam.

* * *

Corben sent twelve copies ahead of the trade consignment, "for distribution among interested parties," which is bookseller language for *give these to people who will praise us loudly.* I distributed them with more care than he intended.

One went to Tamsin Cade, who received it in her sail loft with her hands still chalky from the cutting table and read the title page twice, and then said, "Huh," which from Tamsin was a eulogy, a coronation, and a round of applause compressed into a single syllable.

"You could manage a little more," I said.

"I've just read your name in print, haven't I. What more d'you want, a parade?" She turned the atlas over, examining the binding with a professional eye, then handed it back. "No. Keep it. First copy off your own press-run ought to stay with you." When I protested she fixed me with the look she used on apprentices who had stitched a seam crooked and hoped nobody would notice. "Keep it, Hazel. You'll want it someday when you've forgotten what today felt like. People do forget. It's the great flaw in the design."

Another went to Mr. Quill, and giving it to him was harder than giving it to anyone, because Quill was the man who had betrayed me to Fitch — years ago, quietly, believing cooperation would keep the workshop safe, and confessing it later with tears in his eyes and his hands shaking around a cup of tea he never drank. Forgiveness, I have learned, is not a door you walk through once. It is a room you keep re-entering, finding different corners of it every time.

He took the parcel from me in the shop among the ledgers and the smell of leather and dust, and he unwrapped it with the deliberation of a man defusing something. He read the title page. He took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, put them back on, and read it again.

"Hazel Hartwell," he said aloud, testing it. Then, in a smaller voice, as though the shop might overhear and disapprove: "It suits you."

"It's only a name."

"It has never once been only a name, child. Not in this town, not in any town. Names are the only property the poor are permitted, and we guard them like dragons." He closed the atlas gently. "Aldous would have—" He stopped. Started again. "Aldous *did*. That's what the dedication means. He wrote it years ago. He simply lacked the courage to say it while breathing." Quill smiled, and it was a sad, creased thing. "Most of us lack it. We leave our truths to be delivered posthumously. Very tidy. No risk of argument."

"Are you asking forgiveness again, Mr. Quill?"

"No. I'm telling you the atlas is good. The rest is between me and my conscience, and we've come to an arrangement: it stops shouting and I stop pretending I can't hear it." He set the book down and squared its edges with the desk edge, twice, a precise little ceremony. "Now. There's a matter I'd raise while you're here, if you'll permit it."

And that is how I learned, before anyone thought to tell me officially, that the deed had been settled.

* * *

Settled is a generous word for what actually happened, which was that the Admiralty, having been embarrassed in front of its own lawyers, decided to make the entire question evaporate. The tidal flats were indeed needed — a new breakwater, powder magazines, the usual appetite of war — but a public seizure of land belonging to a young woman whose name appeared in a celebrated atlas, defended by a dead surveyor's own hand, was the sort of spectacle ministries pay to avoid. So they purchased. Quietly. Through intermediaries who spoke of *arrangements* and *considerations* and never once of deeds or names or the word *legitimate*, which had hung over my whole existence like weather.

What it came to was this: a pension to the estate of Aldous Hartwell, administered to his lawful heir-at-law — a phrase the clerks had evidently choked on before settling on it — and a small freehold, upriver, two acres with a cottage on it that had once belonged to a pilot and smelled permanently of tar. The flats themselves passed to the Crown. The name passed to me. Everyone got what they could live with, which is the definition of a settlement, and possibly of most marriages.

"The cottage is yours outright," Quill said. "Deed registered, witnessed, filed in triplicate. I checked. Twice. I intend to check a third time next week, out of principle."

"Fitch let it stand?"

Quill's mouth thinned. "Mr. Fitch has been reassigned. Portsmouth, I'm told. Somewhere his talents for order are appreciated and there are fewer mapmakers to trouble him." He paused, and something flickered behind the spectacles — not quite sympathy; Quill's sympathies were always rationed. "You made him look foolish without ever once making him wrong, Miss Hartwell. Men forgive being resisted. They do not forgive being *outmaneuvered politely*. He'll remember you."

"Let him. I'll be easy to find. My name will be on a book."

"That," said Quill, "is rather the point of it."

* * *

We held the gathering in April, when the harbor had filled again with merchant shipping and the war had settled into its long grinding middle age, too busy elsewhere to bother a port like ours. Tamsin organized it, because Tamsin organized everything that involved more than four people, and she organized it in her characteristic fashion: by informing everyone it was happening and daring them to object.

They came anyway. Half the harbor came. Ropemakers and pilots, the chandlers' wives, old Brannock the mudlark who had taught me to read the tide-tables when I was nine and still called me "the little surveyor" though I stood a head taller than him now. The sail loft was emptied of sails and filled instead with tables, and Tamsin's apprentice boys were deployed as waiters and performed about as well as you would expect, dropping a tray within the first hour and blaming the tray.

Someone — I suspected Brannock, who denied it with a grin that convicted him — had nailed up a copy of the title page above the great doors, torn from a proof sheet, with HAZEL HARTWELL picked out in red thread by some sailmaker's needle. It swayed in the draught from the loft doors all evening, gently, like a pennant.

Tamsin gave a speech. It consisted of the following, in its entirety: "She kept her promises, she paid her debts, and she drew a better coast than any of you deserve to sail along. To Hazel Hartwell." Then she drank, and the room drank, and I stood there holding my own cup and understood, with a clarity that nearly undid me in front of eighty people, that this was the inheritance. Not the deed. Not the pension, though God knew I would not refuse it. This: a room full of people who had known me nameless for twenty years and had come anyway, because belonging was never a document. It was attendance. It was showing up, season after season, until your absence would be noticed and your presence assumed.

Aldous had never given me his surname in law. He had given me something better and slower: his craft, his standards, his stubbornness about true bearings. And when the moment came, he had reached out from beyond death with a line of type — *in her own hand* — and done formally what he'd been too proud or too frightened to do in person. The dead are generous exactly once, and at their convenience.

"Eh," said Brannock, appearing at my elbow with the tactless timing of the very old, "your master's son. He write?"

"He's at sea."

"He write when he can?"

"When he can." Which meant: when the blockade permits, when a neutral trader can carry a letter, when the war allows two people on opposite sides of a channel the luxury of paper. Elias's letters came perhaps once in six weeks, sometimes folded around a chart he'd annotated, sometimes containing nothing but a pressed flower from a French hedgerow and a single line — *Still here. Still reading your coasts.* We had acknowledged everything aboard the Cerberus that night in the dark between the oars, and had had no opportunity since to give it a name, which may be why neither of us rushed to find one. Some things keep better unlabeled, like charts marked *unsurveyed — approach with caution.*

"He'll see the atlas, then," Brannock said. "Good. Man ought to know his father finished strong." He patted my arm with a hand like cured leather and wandered off toward the punch, which Tamsin had fortified in a manner consistent with naval tradition and inconsistent with safety.

Late, when the lamps had burned low and most guests had gone home weaving slightly along the quay, Tamsin and I sat on the cutting table with our legs dangling like girls, looking at the empty loft.

"Well," she said. "Belonging earned rather than inherited. That's what you'd call tonight, wouldn't you. Being literary about it."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say something with the word 'belonging' in it. I know you. You've got a face for it." She nudged my shoulder with hers. "For what it's worth, you didn't earn it tonight. Tonight they just told you. You earned it years ago, one sounding at a time, out on the mud in all weathers, doing the work nobody watched. That's the only kind of earning there is. The rest is ceremony."

"And what did you earn? Standing watch over a hidden wall of copper plates while a clerk with clean hands searched your rafters?"

"Terror, mostly. And afterward, the right to be insufferable about it forever." She slid off the table. "Come on. You're staying at mine tonight; your cottage has no furniture and smells of tar, and I refuse to let you sleep on the floor of your own inheritance like a saint in a ballad."

At the door I stopped and looked back once at the proof-sheet title page swaying on its nail, red thread glinting, and thought: *there. That's done.* The ledger closed. The regiment sailed. Whatever came next would be new work.

Which is, of course, precisely when new work arrives — it has a nose for the moment a person feels finished.

Because three days later, opening the morning's post at the workshop bench, I found a letter with a foreign postmark and a seal I didn't recognize, and inside it, in a clerk's careful hand, an offer that would take me further from Wexbridge than I had ever been — and a second enclosure, unsigned, in a hand I knew intimately, that began: *My dear Miss Hartwell. The estuary has never been surveyed properly. Someone ought to begin.*

I read it twice. Then I reached for a fresh sheet of paper, and a pen, and began to measure what such a journey would require — what could be carried, and what must be left behind.

The answer, as always, was: less than I feared, and more than I wished.
