## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The city smelled of herring and rain, which Elias Vann had decided was the most honest thing about it. Other cities lied with perfume. This one told you plainly what it lived on, and how little of it there was to go around.

He stood across the street from a narrow gray house on Verkstraat and rehearsed for the fourth time.

*Good afternoon. My name is Elias Vann. I am a translator by trade. I have come about your brother.*

No. Too much like an undertaker.

*Madam Yarrow — you don't know me, but I knew someone you loved.*

Worse. That sounded like a man selling insurance or worse things.

He had been standing in the drizzle long enough that a fishwife had looked at him twice, then once more with the particular suspicion reserved for men who loiter near doors without buying anything. He tipped his hat to her. She did not tip anything back.

Inside his coat, in an oilcloth packet sewn against his ribs where a heart ought to be, were seven letters. He had read them so many times he could have recited them in three languages, and in one language — the old alphabet, the one they'd both been children under — he could have recited them *sideways*, because that was how Tomas Yarrow had written the important parts: right to left, letter by letter reversed, the way twins and siblings invent when the world gets too full of listeners.

Elias had never had a sibling. He'd learned this fact about himself somewhere over the mountains, reading another man's letters by candlelight, feeling like a burglar who had broken into a house only to find it warm and occupied and better than his own.

He crossed the street before his courage could file for emigration like everything else he owned.

* * *

The door was blue. That surprised him. Everything else on the street was gray or brown or the color of giving up, but Ada Yarrow's door was painted a stubborn maritime blue, and someone had recently oiled the hinges, because when it opened it made no sound at all, which struck Elias as exactly the sort of detail a woman keeping twenty years of silence would attend to.

The woman herself was smaller than the letters had led him to expect. Letters always made people enormous; grief has no sense of scale. Her hair was going silver at the temples in a way that looked less like age than like weather, and her eyes went over him the way a customs officer goes over luggage — quickly, thoroughly, finding nothing declared and suspecting everything anyway.

"Madam Yarrow." He removed his hat. Rain ran off the brim and he was careful not to let it fall on her step, because first impressions were the only currency he had left and he intended to spend every coin carefully. "My name is Elias Vann. Forgive the hour, and forgive the — " he gestured at himself, damp and road-worn and honest-looking in the way he worked hardest to be — "the general condition."

"You're the third stranger this year," she said. Her accent had been sanded down until only the vowels remembered the old country. "The first wanted money for a church roof. The second wanted money for a church that didn't exist. What do you want?"

"Nothing," he said, and watched the word land like a counterfeit coin, because nobody wanted nothing, and she knew it, and he knew she knew it. "That is — I want ten minutes. I'll pay for them with something you've been owed for twenty years."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not hope — he knew hope when he saw it; hope was loud, hopeful people breathed differently. This was quieter. This was the sound a house makes at night when it settles.

"I don't buy things from strangers on doorsteps," she said.

"No," he agreed. "But you might take them free of charge, if they're letters."

He said the word *letters* and watched her hand tighten on the doorframe, just slightly, the knuckles going pale as paper. So. The bookkeeper's hands betrayed her too. Good. He was not alone.

"Whose?" she asked.

And here was the moment he had rehearsed forty ways across two borders and one very uncomfortable ferry, and all forty versions abandoned him at once, so he simply said the true thing, plainly, the way you'd say a name at a graveside:

"Tomas Yarrow's."

* * *

She didn't slam the door. He had expected the slam, had budgeted for it emotionally the way he budgeted for everything now — carefully, with margins. Instead she stood very still, and the stillness had a quality he recognized from interrogations he'd translated, the stillness of a person deciding whether the thing they'd heard was survivable.

"My brother is dead," she said. "He died at the evacuation. Everyone knows this. It was confirmed."

"It was reported," Elias said gently. "I've found those are different animals. Reports die easily. People are harder to kill."

"That's a clever sentence."

"I have a lot of clever sentences. I'm a translator. It's mostly what we are — clever sentences standing between people who should be talking directly." He held up his hands, palms out, empty. "I'm not selling anything, madam. There are seven letters. They're in his handwriting. Some of them are in a code I suspect you can read and I cannot, though God knows I've tried, and I say that as a man who makes his living at codes."

"The third stranger wanted a church that didn't exist," she said slowly. "What does the fourth want?"

"To give you the letters and leave," he said. "Eventually. After coffee, if it's offered. I've been traveling eleven days and my charm is nearly spent and I'd like to sit down somewhere that isn't a train station."

For the first time, something flickered at the corner of her mouth that in a warmer climate might have been called amusement. "You admit your charm is spent?"

"Madam, I admit it freely. Whatever remains of it is on loan, and the interest is ruinous."

She looked at him a moment longer. He let her. He had learned that the best thing a man carrying other people's secrets could do was stand still and be examined; fidgeting was for the guilty and the salesmen, and he was — well. He adjusted the thought. He was *mostly* neither.

Then she stepped back from the doorway, and the blue door opened onto a dim hall that smelled of typewriter ribbon and strong tea, and Elias Vann understood that he had just done the hardest thing, and also that he had done almost nothing at all, because the hard part hadn't even put its boots on yet.

"You'll wipe your feet," she said. It wasn't a question.

"On my life if necessary."

"Don't be dramatic. Just the mat will do."

* * *

The front room held a desk, a lamp, a typewriter under a cloth cover like a sleeping bird, and shelves of ledgers in perfect order, their spines aligned with a discipline that made Elias feel briefly guilty about the state of his suitcase, which opened like a startled mouth whenever anyone touched it.

She sat behind the desk. She did not offer him a chair. He respected this enormously and remained standing, hat in hand, dripping politely onto the mat.

"Now," she said. "Slowly. From the beginning. Where did you get these letters?"

"A courier died in Trieste." He gave her the version he'd prepared — the clean version, the version with the edges filed smooth. "A courier whose effects came through a man I do translation work for. Among the papers were letters addressed to you, care of various offices, none of which ever delivered them. The last postmark is nine years old."

"Nine years." She said it the way other people swore. "He wrote them nine years ago and they sat in a bag?"

"They sat in several bags. Letters travel badly when everyone handling them is afraid of what's written inside." He reached toward his coat and stopped, hand hovering, asking permission with the gesture. At her nod — a small, grudging nod, the nod of a woman granting a visa — he drew out the oilcloth packet and laid it on the desk between them like a card he wasn't sure won the trick.

She didn't touch it at first. She looked at it the way you look at a dog that might bite or might belong to you; the whole question was in the approach.

"You understand," she said quietly, "that if this is a cruelty — if someone has paid you to bring a grieving woman a forged name — there is no punishment I could arrange that would be adequate, but I would spend the rest of my life arranging it."

"Madam Yarrow," Elias said, "I promise you I have thought about exactly that possibility every night since Trieste. Which is why I brought them unopened past the point where opening them would have profited me most."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "You could read them and sell the contents."

"I could. And then I'd be a man who'd sold a dead man's voice, and I'd have to live with him, and I already live with enough people." He tapped the packet once, lightly. "Open it or don't. But open it yourself. I've carried it this far; the last inch should be yours."

She picked up the packet then, and her hands — steady hands, ledger hands, hands that had clearly never once entered a figure wrong on purpose — trembled just at the edges, like flags in wind too small to name.

She broke the seal on the oilcloth. Inside were the letters, tied with string gone soft with age, and on top, folded separately, a single page in a different, shakier hand.

Elias watched her unfold it. Watched her face do something complicated and private — the shutters going down and then, before they finished closing, a crack of light underneath, a whole burning house visible through the gap.

"This isn't his writing," she whispered. "This is — this is Mama's. This is Mama's hand."

"There's a note on the back," Elias said softly. "I couldn't read the language. I assumed — "

Ada turned the page over. Her lips moved. Then she sat down — actually sat, abruptly, in the chair behind her desk as though the floor had resigned — and pressed the paper flat against her chest with both hands.

"She carried them," Ada said, to no one, to the room, possibly to the dead. "All this time, she carried them and never told me. She kept them from me. Why would she keep them from — "

She stopped. Elias watched her arrive at the answer the way you watch someone walk into a room where bad news lives: she got there, and wished she hadn't.

"Because I would have gone after him," Ada said flatly. "Twenty years ago. Or nine years ago. Whenever. Because I would have burned my life down and walked into whatever trap was waiting, and she knew it, because mothers know it." She laughed, a terrible small laugh. "I cataloged her effects last month. Every button, every receipt. I threw away nothing. Do you know why? Because some part of me was waiting for exactly this. A piece of paper I wasn't supposed to find."

Elias said nothing. There were moments in his trade where translation meant silence — where the words existed in one heart and needed no passport into another.

Ada looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and, beneath it all, doing arithmetic.

"You said seven letters," she said. "In his hand."

"In his hand. And parts in a code — a childhood cipher, I think, though I only guess that because the shapes look like homework rather than diplomacy."

She untied the string with fingers that had steadied themselves by force of will. She unfolded the topmost letter. And Elias, standing in the lamplight of a bookkeeper's front room with rain drying on his shoulders, watched a woman read her own handwriting backwards — because that was what it was, he realized, watching her lips move left-to-right along lines that ran right-to-left: she and her brother had written mirror-wise, two children facing each other across a table, each one's script legible only to the other.

She read for a long time. The clock on the mantel ticked like a patient witness. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked — someone else in the house, listening at the top of the stairs, though Elias didn't know that yet.

When Ada finally looked up, her face was the face of a woman who had spent twenty years standing at a grave, and had just been informed the grave was empty, and did not know whether to rejoice or demand a refund on two decades of mourning.

"He describes the harbor," she said hoarsely. "Our harbor. The fish crates with the green paint. Nobody alive knows about the green paint except me and — " Her voice failed and restarted. "It's him. It's either him or someone who tortured it out of him, and I'll know which, because there's a question in here that only I can answer, and the wrong answer proves forgery, and the right answer proves — "

She stopped again. Pressed the letter flat on the desk. Looked at Elias with an expression he would remember for the rest of his life, in whichever country he ended up having one.

"Who else has seen these letters?" she asked.

And Elias Vann, translator, carrier, debtor, liar-by-omission, felt the first cold thread of the truth he'd been not-thinking about since Trieste slide down his spine — because there was a second name on the courier's delivery list, and it was not hers, and it belonged to a colonel.

"No one," he said, and heard his own charm fail him for the first time, the word arriving a half-second too late and an octave too high. "No one important."

Ada Yarrow looked at him the way she must have looked at a ledger column that refused to balance.

"Mister Vann," she said pleasantly, "you are going to tell me the truth now, or you are going to leave through that blue door considerably faster than you entered it. And I warn you — I am a bookkeeper. I notice when the numbers don't add up. You, sir, have not added up since the doorstep."

Outside, the rain began again in earnest, knocking at the windows like a late guest, and Elias understood that the past hadn't merely knocked at this house.

It had been let in. And it was starting to make demands.
