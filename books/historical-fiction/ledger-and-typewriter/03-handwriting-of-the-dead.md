## 3. Handwriting of the Dead

Ada Yarrow had spent twenty years teaching herself not to flinch.

It was a discipline like any other, she told her bookkeeping clients when they marveled at her composure during audits — the same as keeping a ledger balanced. You did not look directly at the frightening column. You worked around it, line by line, until the arithmetic of the day was done and you could afford your fear in private, at home, after dark, where it belonged.

So when Elias Vann set the letters on her kitchen table that evening and said, "Take your time," she did not flinch. She poured tea instead. She arranged three cups though only two were wanted, because her hands needed employment, and then she sat down across from a stranger in her aunt's kitchen and looked at the envelope on the oilcloth as though it were an invoice from a supplier she distrusted.

The envelope was soft at the corners, the way paper goes when it has been carried a long way against a body. Postmarks meant nothing to her anymore; borders had been redrawn twice since the year it was stamped. What mattered was the address, written in faded brown-black ink:

*To A. Yarrow, if living. If not, burn unopened.*

Her own initial. Her own name. And beneath it, in smaller script, almost apologetic:

*You'll know it's really me by the third word.*

Ada's tea went cold in her cup before she touched the flap.

"May I?" Vann asked gently. He had the voice of a man who had learned to be gentle in rooms full of frightened people. "Or would you rather I waited outside? Some letters are better opened alone."

"No." The word came out harder than she intended. She softened it. "No, stay. If I open this alone, I'll convince myself by morning I dreamed it. I'm very good at that. Twenty years of practice."

She said it lightly, the way she said everything that mattered, and saw him register the lightness for what it was. He was perceptive. She disliked that about him already, and would continue disliking it, off and on, for years.

She broke the seal.

* * *

The handwriting hit her before the words did.

That was the thing no one warned you about grief — that it lived in the smallest places. Not in anniversaries or photographs or the empty chair, which you could fortify yourself against, but in the *loop of a letter*. Tomas had written his capital T the way their father had, with a crossbar that drifted leftward like a man glancing over his shoulder. He crossed his sevens. He dotted his i's slightly right of center, always, so that every sentence he ever wrote looked faintly hurried even when he'd been sitting perfectly still, chewing his pen, taking an hour over a paragraph.

Ada had seen that handwriting last on a laundry list. March 1938, the week before everything ended. Tomas, nineteen years old and solemn about it, had been delegated to write down what the household needed from Frau Brenner's shop, and he had produced a list so elaborately organized — soap grouped with candles under *household*, flour grouped with salt under *kitchen*, and a separate category labeled simply *matters of importance*, containing one item, *honey* — that their mother had laughed until she had to sit down.

Ada had kept that list folded in her shoe through the evacuation, through the border, through two years of rooms that weren't hers. It had crumbled to lint somewhere around 1941, and she had grieved its loss more violently, more privately, than she could have explained to anyone without sounding mad. You cannot tell people you are mourning a scrap of paper. They will start watching how much you drink.

Now here was that same hand, older, the letters narrower, the dots still right of center. Here was Tomas's writing on paper that had outlived him, addressed to her, asking her to know it was really him by the third word.

She read the first line. Then she read it again, and her breath did something complicated.

*"Dear Addie, I hope the pigeons have forgiven you by now—"*

"Oh," she said. It came out very small.

Vann leaned forward slightly. "The third word?"

"The third word is *pigeons*." Ada pressed the flat of her hand against the table as though checking it for level. "When I was nine, I convinced Tomas that I could speak to pigeons. I charged him two buttons a lesson. When he found out I couldn't — when he caught me making up pigeon vocabulary out of whole cloth — he didn't tell our parents. He made me swear an oath instead, sealed with honey, that I would never again sell false knowledge. And for years afterward, any time either of us wanted to say *I trust this is really you and not someone pretending*, we referenced the pigeons."

"An oath sealed with honey," Vann repeated. He had an accent she couldn't place and a way of repeating things that suggested he was filing them somewhere permanent.

"It was my mother's good honey. We were in enormous trouble." Ada heard her own voice doing the thing it did — staying level, staying dry, narrating instead of feeling. "The point is, nobody knows about the pigeons. Nobody alive. I never told Marta. I never told anyone. So whoever wrote this letter—"

She stopped, because her voice had developed a crack in it, and Ada Yarrow did not permit cracks. She looked at the window instead, at the gray harbor light coming through the lace curtain Marta insisted on calling *the good curtain* despite its having no remaining original holes.

"You needn't perform composure on my account," Vann said quietly. "I've carried these letters for eleven months. I've imagined this moment perhaps four hundred times, and in none of those versions did the recipient pour me tea first. You're allowed to drop things. I'll pick them up."

"That's very kind," said Ada, dropping nothing, "but I assure you I am entirely composed," and then she unfolded the second page and read the rest, and was not composed at all, though outwardly you would never have known, unless you counted the fact that she read the same paragraph five times and at the end of it her thumb had gone white pressing the paper flat.

The letter was long. It was dated — the date meant little, but the season meant something; it was written in high summer by someone who mentioned the heat twice. It talked about ordinary things first, deliberately, she could see the deliberate-ness, the way a swimmer wades in slowly: the food where he was (bad), the other men (good company, mostly), a chess problem he'd been working ("White to move and win, I promise you it's solvable, think about the pawn"). He asked after Marta. He asked whether Nell still bit her nails — which meant he knew about Nell, which meant he had known things after the evacuation that Ada had believed died with everyone else who knew them.

And then, near the end, the letter turned.

*I don't know if this reaches you, Addie, and I don't know what state things will be in if it does. So I'll say the plain thing, which we were always bad at saying aloud. I know what you gave up at the dock. I've known since the third day. You put me in that berth and you stayed on the stones and you let them believe you were nobody worth searching, and I want you to hear it from me: that was not foolishness. Whatever it cost — and I can guess some of what it cost — it bought twenty minutes that got thirty-one people onto that ship. I've done the arithmetic too. We are the same kind of accountant, you and I.*

*Don't carry it alone anymore. That's all. That's the whole letter, underneath the chess problems. Don't carry it alone anymore.*

There was a postscript: *P.S. The pigeons send their regards. They never forgave you either.*

Ada set the pages down. She stood, walked to the sink, ran the tap, and held her hands under cold water for a while, looking out the small window at the yard where the washing line creaked. From behind her, Vann had the grace to study the ceiling.

When she came back to the table her eyes were dry and her voice worked fine, and both of them knew exactly what had happened at the sink, and neither of them acknowledged it, which was, she would reflect later, the most Yarrow interaction possible between two strangers.

"He mentions Nell," she said. "He shouldn't know Nell exists. Nell was six months old when the evacuation happened. He was already—" She stopped. Recalculated. "Unless he learned afterward. Unless someone reached him. Unless—"

"Read the second letter," Vann said. "Then decide what's possible."

* * *

The second letter was shorter, and different in texture — less careful, written faster, the dots of the i's now smudged sideways as though the pen had been moving quickly on poor paper. Dated later. Much later; there was a reference inside it to a winter that placed it years after the first.

*Addie — they moved us again. Don't try to find the place; I won't put it in writing, and anyway you'd come, and you mustn't come. Promise me you won't come. I can feel you deciding to come from here, across however many miles this is, and I forbid it. Forbid is a strong word between siblings, I know. Use the honey oath against me if you like, I deserve it.*

*Something has changed here. Someone new has been asking questions — not about escape routes, they're past caring about those, but about names. About who knew whom, back before. I think someone sold a list once, a long time ago, and the buyer has never stopped being afraid of it. If you still have anything — anything at all from that night, from the route, from the ledger — I want you to think very carefully about who you would trust with it, and then trust no one, except possibly a man named Vann if he finds you, because I paid a courier I trusted to find him, and Vann is the only honest intermediary I could think of who owes nothing to anybody, which in our old world is the closest thing to clean hands a person can have.*

*I'm tired, Addie. I'd like to see the sea. Isn't that stupid. There's a prison wall between me and the water and all I want is to see the sea.*

*Burn this after reading. I mean it.*

*T.*

P.S. *The pigeons have founded a government in exile. They await your report.*

Ada read it twice. On the second pass her lips moved slightly, translating not the language but the tone, matching each sentence against her memory of her brother's actual voice, testing it the way a bank clerk tests a coin — the bite, the ring, the weight. Sentence by sentence it rang true. The jokes were his jokes: self-deprecating, precise, deployed exactly where another man would put despair. The chess problem was his chess problem. The tenderness was armored in instructions, which was the family disease entire — *don't come, don't search, don't trust, burn this* — every Yarrow love letter arriving wrapped in orders to stay safe and keep quiet.

And the third layer — because there was always a third layer in anything Tomas wrote, she should have remembered that, she had invented the pigeons and he had invented the oaths, between them they had built secrecy into a family art form — the third layer was in the margins.

She saw it when she tilted the page toward the lamp. Faint pinpricks along the left margin, tiny punctures in groups, almost invisible. Her breath stopped properly this time.

"Tomas," she whispered, "you ridiculous, careful man."

"What is it?" Vann was on his feet.

"Our father taught us this one." Her finger hovered above the punctures without touching. "We called it the needle alphabet. Papa used it in his ledgers — real ledgers, the boring ones — to mark certain entries. A pinprick beside a number meant the number lied. Two pricks meant the entry was watched. We thought it was a game. He'd hide a prick-mark in our schoolwork and if we found it, we got honey." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle, because she had just remembered — with twenty years' delay, the way her worst memories always arrived — that her father had taught them a traitor's cipher at the dinner table, dressed up as a game, with honey as the prize, and none of them had ever asked themselves what a respectable customs officer needed a lie-marking system *for*.

"There's a message in the margin," she managed. "In the pinpricks. It's short — the needle alphabet is slow — but it's there."

"Can you read it?"

"Yes." She took the lamp from the shelf and held it low and went prick by prick, counting groups, her lips moving, using the finger-counting method Tomas had devised when they were children, the one where the left hand counted fives and the right counted ones, and it came out slowly, letter by letter, like water dripping into a bucket:

*B. SOLD THE ROUTE. B = BRECHT. HE IS ALIVE. DO NOT MEET HIM ALONE.*

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere below, the harbor bell rang the half hour, patient as ever, indifferent to the contents of kitchens.

"Brecht," Vann repeated, and something crossed his face — quick, quickly buried, but Ada had spent twenty years reading faces across audit tables, and she caught it. Not surprise. Recognition.

"You know the name," she said.

"I've heard it." Too smooth. Too fast. "Everyone in certain circles has heard it."

"Mm." Ada gathered the letters, squared their edges, and looked at this stranger with the gentle voice and the unplaceable accent who had appeared at her door carrying her dead brother's handwriting like a lantern. "Mr. Vann, I'm going to ask you a question, and I'd like you to consider that I have just discovered my brother may be alive after twenty years, which means my capacity for being shocked is currently occupied elsewhere, and I will detect a lie. Where exactly did a courier get these letters? Who gave them to you? And why you?"

Vann looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the washing line creaked. Down in the harbor, the water did what water does.

"I think," he said at last, "that you should make fresh tea."

Which, in Ada's considerable experience, was what people said when the answer was going to take a while and reflect no particular credit on anyone — and she rose to put the kettle on, because whatever was coming, she intended to hear all of it sitting down.
