## 4. What the Ledger Carried

The trouble with lying to Ada Yarrow, Elias Vann discovered over the second cup of tea she poured him without asking whether he wanted it, was that she listened the way accountants read numbers — patiently, completely, and with a small pencil of attention hovering behind her eyes, waiting for something not to add up.

So he told her almost everything true. It was the only strategy he had left.

* * *

He had found the letters in Trieste, in March, in a room that smelled of camphor and river damp. He did not tell her about the room yet. Rooms were dangerous things to mention; rooms implied addresses, and addresses implied how he'd come by them. He started further back, where the story could be made clean.

"I worked for a shipping concern," he said. "Freight manifests, bills of lading, the poetry of crates. I translated documents out of four languages into two others and back again when somebody made an error." He turned his teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a habit he'd picked up from watching diplomats do it and deciding it looked like a man with nothing to hide. "Last autumn the concern took on private work. A courier service. Documents carried between cities, no questions printed on the waybill."

Ada sat across from him in her narrow parlor with her hands folded on her knees, and he had the distinct impression she was balancing him against some internal column and finding the figures interesting but provisional.

"Whose courier service?" she asked.

"That's rather the heart of it," Vann admitted. "I don't know. The man who hired me went by Herr Brandt, which I have since concluded is roughly as real as his smile."

"And the letters?"

"Found among another man's effects." He let a beat pass, because honesty arranged well was still honesty, more or less. "A courier named Ostrer. He carried mail along the coastal route — Trieste up through the ports. He died in February. Fever, they said at the time. There were questions afterward about whether fever is what you call it when a man has been drinking with the wrong sailors."

Ada's pencil-of-attention moved visibly behind her eyes. "You were asked to finish his route."

"I was asked to inventory his effects and deliver what could be delivered." This was true. This was so nearly true that it deserved to be said aloud in a warm room with tea in it, where it might become true by residence. What he omitted was the envelope of banknotes, half paid already, that waited for confirmation of delivery. What he omitted was that Brandt had named her street before Vann had ever seen this city, this house, this woman with her mother's china and her brother's ghost.

Omission, Vann reflected, was just translation with the inconvenient words left out. He was very good at his work.

"The effects were mostly dull," he went on. "Invoices. A spare shirt that I declined to inventory. And a oilcloth packet sewn into the lining of his coat, which is where couriers put things they intend to survive the trip even if they don't." He reached into his breast pocket and produced the letters — three of them, tied with a length of plain string, worn soft at the folds. He set them on the table between them as carefully as if they were live shells, which in a sense they were.

Ada did not reach for them immediately. That was the first thing that unsettled him. Most people, offered letters from the dead, grabbed. She looked at the handwriting visible through the topmost fold for a long moment, the way you look at water before deciding whether to go in.

"Where was Ostrer taking these?" she asked finally.

"He never got to say, or I never got told. But one was addressed to a name in this city. Yours."

"And the others?"

"One has no address at all. Just a number — 14, underlined twice. The third—" He hesitated. Here was a fact he had turned over all night in his lodging house, examining it from every angle like a coin he suspected of being foreign currency. "The third is addressed to Colonel Brecht."

He watched the name land. It landed hard. Ada Yarrow went very still, in the particular way of people who have practiced stillness until it became a load-bearing wall of their character.

"I don't know a Colonel Brecht," she said.

"No," Vann agreed mildly. "I imagine you don't."

* * *

He gave her the afternoon with the letters and took himself out to walk the harbor, because there are conversations a man cannot watch from inside the room without becoming part of the evidence.

The harbor was doing what harbors do in early spring: pretending industriousness. Gulls argued over fish heads. A crane swung its long neck with the bored elegance of a heron. Vann walked the length of the quay with his collar up and his thoughts down, and what he thought about was Herr Brandt's office, and the envelope, and the specific sentence Brandt had used when handing over Ostrer's coat.

*"Deliver everything intact. The lady reads first. Then we discuss the rest."*

Then we discuss the rest. Vann had been translating other people's sentences for fifteen years and had developed an ear for what lived underneath them. Underneath *then we discuss the rest* lived: *the letters are the hook, the woman is the cargo.* He had told himself a dozen comfortable stories on the train north — that Brandt was a lawyer settling an estate, that the colonel was a creditor, that Vann himself was merely a postman and postmen are blameless as rain. Every story had come with fine print, and he had signed anyway, because the alternative to signing was the lodging-house arithmetic he knew by heart: three days of credit left, one coat with a fraying cuff, no country that would stamp his passport without laughing.

Complicity, he thought, watching a tug nudge an ocean liner sideways with what looked like insolence and was probably skill, complicity arrives in installments. First you carry a letter. Then you carry a lie about the letter. By the time the payments come due you've forgotten there was ever a moment when your hands were empty.

He bought roasted chestnuts from a vendor with a voice like a foghorn and ate them standing up, burning his fingers on principle. A boy selling newspapers shouted something about a repatriation ship — rumors, schedules, hope priced at a kopeck. Vann bought a paper he couldn't afford and didn't read it. He was thinking about the second letter, the one addressed to 14.

Because here was what he hadn't told Ada, and what he wasn't sure he ever would: he had opened it. Not out of villainy. Out of the translator's oldest sin, curiosity wearing the costume of professionalism. The seal had been broken already when he found it — Ostrer, perhaps, or whoever had gone through the dead man's pockets before the effects reached Vann — and a broken seal is an invitation shaped like an accident. Inside, in that same urgent handwriting, was a single line:

*If she will not come, tell her the ledger remembers what we burned.*

Vann had copied the line out three times in his own hand, trying to make it mean something smaller than it meant. It refused. Somewhere in this city or beyond it there was a ledger, and somewhere in the past there had been a burning, and a dead-alive man named Tomas Yarrow believed his sister was the only person alive who could read the difference.

And Colonel Brecht wanted whatever Ada read first.

* * *

When he returned at dusk, the parlor window was lit amber and Ada opened the door before he knocked, which meant she had been watching the street, which meant she trusted him exactly as far as the width of a window.

"You came back," she said. It wasn't gratitude. It was data.

"You expected otherwise?"

"I expected a man carrying letters from my dead brother to be either a fraud or a fool. Frauds leave after the money. Fools stay for the tea." She stood aside to let him in. "I haven't decided which you are. Come in while I deliberate."

He liked her enormously. This was going to be a problem.

Inside, she had arranged the three letters in a row on the table, squared like documents awaiting audit. She had also, he noticed, laid a sheet of blank paper beside them and a pencil sharpened to a weapon's point.

"I want to know how Ostrer died," she said without preamble. "Not the official version. The version you believe."

Vann sat down slowly. "Fever," he said, "is a very convenient word. He was found in a boarding house near the fish market. No wounds. His papers were untouched, his money untouched, his boots untouched — and couriers' boots, Miss Yarrow, are the first thing anyone with sense takes. Whatever killed him, it wasn't robbery." He paused. "But nothing else was taken either. Including, apparently, the most valuable thing he carried."

"So whoever searched him wasn't looking for the letters."

"Or didn't know they existed." He met her eyes. "Or already had copies."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked — the aunt, he guessed, or the girl, conducting surveillance with all the subtlety of their trade, which appeared to be none.

"And Brecht," Ada said. Her voice was level but he heard the cost of the levelness, the way you hear the strain in a bridge. "Why would my brother write to that man?"

"That, Miss Yarrow, is the question I crossed four borders to ask you."

"Don't flatter yourself. You crossed four borders because someone paid your fare."

The sentence dropped into the room like a coin into a well, and Vann felt it falling all the way down. She couldn't know. She was guessing — fishing with a very straight line. But his face, he was certain, had done something involuntary; he had watched better liars than himself ruined by less.

"Someone always pays the fare," he said, recovering, and hating the smoothness of it. "That's what fares are. What matters is who chooses the destination. I chose yours."

"Did you?" She picked up the unaddressed letter — the one numbered 14 — and held it without opening it further, though he could see it had been read. "Because this morning a stranger arrived at my door with my family's ghosts in his pocket, knowing precisely which door to knock on. Men don't find doors like that by accident, Mr. Vann. Doors like that are given."

"Would you believe me if I said I'd prefer to be a fool?"

"No." But something at the corner of her mouth moved — not quite a smile, more the place where a smile kept its winter clothes. "Fools don't notice which questions frighten people. You noticed mine."

* * *

She fed him supper. This undid him more than any interrogation could have. It was a modest meal — barley soup, dark bread, pickled something-or-other that Marta (introduced briefly, glaringly, from the doorway) declared was "the last of the good jar, so appreciate it, stranger" — and it was served in a kitchen that smelled of twenty years of the same stove, and Ada Yarrow ladled soup for a man she did not trust because leaving a guest hungry was apparently a sin graver than espionage.

He ate and told her the rest of the story, the safe remainder of it. How he had traced the address on her letter through a shipping clerk who owed him a favor. How he had spent his last coin on the train north. How he had rehearsed, on the platform, seventeen different ways to begin and abandoned all of them upon seeing her face, because she looked like a woman who had been waiting for bad news so long that good news would require translation too.

All of which was true. All of which was a moat around the castle of what he didn't say.

After supper, Nell appeared. She was perhaps fourteen, sharp-faced, with the unnerving habit of looking at a person as though reading the label on the back of them. She cleared the dishes with efficient hostility and then stopped directly beside Vann's chair.

"You're the one from the letters," she said.

"I'm the one who carried the letters."

"That's what I said." She stacked a bowl on a plate with a click. "Tomas Yarrow's been dead twenty years. Aunt Ada keeps his photograph face-down in a drawer, which everyone pretends not to know. So either you're a liar, or a cruel joke, or—" she squinted at him with frank scientific interest, "—something else. I haven't decided. Marta says men who arrive with debts attached. She says it loudly, on purpose, so you'll hear."

"Nell," Ada said, in the tone of a woman who has said it ten thousand times.

"I'm going." The girl went, pausing at the door to deliver a final look at Vann that contained, compressed into a single glance, suspicion, pity, and a grudging curiosity that he suspected was the most dangerous of the three.

The kitchen clock ticked. Ada gathered the letters, squared them again, and looked at him across the table with the lamplight making copper of the grey in her hair.

"You'll sleep in the back room tonight," she said. "Marta will object. Let her. In the morning I want to know everything about Herr Brandt — his hands, his teeth, how he counted money, what he smelled like. Liars are identified by details, Mr. Vann, and I collect them." She rose, then stopped, her hand flat on the letters as if holding down something that wanted to fly. "And tomorrow you'll explain to me why a courier dies of convenient fever, why a colonel gets a letter, and why my brother writes about a ledger I have spent twenty years pretending doesn't exist."

She swept the letters off the table and was gone, and Vann sat alone in the yellow light with the cold remains of the soup and the warm remains of his conscience, doing the arithmetic he was best at: what he owed, to whom, and when the payment would be called.

Brandt's voice came back to him across the miles and months, pleasant as a knife being oiled. *Deliver everything intact. The lady reads first. Then we discuss the rest.*

Tomorrow, Vann thought grimly, finishing the pickles out of pure defiance, tomorrow she'll ask about Brandt's hands, and I'll describe them perfectly, and she'll know within a minute that I've left out the price.

He got up to find the back room, and paused at the drawer where, earlier, he had glimpsed the corner of a photograph lying face-down.

Some silences, he was beginning to understand, ran in the family.
