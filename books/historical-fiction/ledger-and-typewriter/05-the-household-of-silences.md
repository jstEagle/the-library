## 5. The Household of Silences

There were three ways to bring a stranger into the house on Vondelstraat, and Ada Yarrow had catalogued all of them the way she catalogued everything: by risk.

The first was to bring him in openly, announce him at dinner, and let Marta interrogate him until he confessed to something—anything; Marta could extract a confession from a lamppost if she set her mind to it. The second was to keep him outside, which meant keeping him in the street, in the rain, in view of neighbors who noticed everything and reported half of it. The third was the way she had actually chosen, standing in the doorway with the letters pressed flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat: bring him in sideways, without announcement, and let the household discover him the way it discovered everything else—gradually, suspiciously, and with commentary.

"Take off your shoes," she said, because it was the first thing her mother had ever said to a guest, and because saying it made the house feel like hers for one more hour.

Vann looked down at his shoes as though they had personally betrayed him. They were good shoes, she noted—the kind that had been resoled twice by someone who cared about the work—and wet through at the seams.

"They're ruining your floor," he said.

"The floor has survived worse." She stepped aside. "Come in before the street decides you live here."

He came in the way a cat comes into a room it hasn't been invited to: low, quiet, reading every exit. His eyes went up the staircase, along the coat hooks, to the kitchen door where the light was yellow and someone was doing something loud with a pot. He catalogued her house the way she catalogued ledgers. She found she didn't entirely mind. It was honest, at least. Most visitors pretended not to look.

"You'll sleep in the back room," she said. "It's small. The bed is narrower than you are tall."

"I've slept in worse."

"I don't doubt it. But here we sleep in beds, and we eat at the table, and we knock before entering rooms with closed doors. Whatever you were before this week, Mr. Vann, you are a lodger now, and lodgers have manners."

Something moved across his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if it ever decided to move in. "Yes, ma'am."

"And stop calling me ma'am. I'm thirty-nine, not a magistrate."

"What should I call you?"

"Miss Yarrow until I decide otherwise."

* * *

Marta was in the kitchen, which was where Marta was always, cosmically, structurally, inescapably. She stood at the stove stirring something that smelled of onions and long grievance, and she did not turn around when Ada came in with Vann behind her, because Marta never turned around for anything less than fire or royalty, and even then she'd want to know what they intended to do about dinner first.

"Aunt Marta," Ada said. "This is Elias Vann. He'll be staying in the back room."

The spoon stopped.

It stopped the way a clock stops—one moment ticking, the next suspended—and the silence that followed had texture to it, weight, a temperature. Then Marta turned, slowly, wiping her hands on her apron as though preparing them for work, and looked Vann up and down with the unhurried thoroughness of a customs officer.

"A lodger," Marta said.

"A lodger," Ada confirmed.

"For how long?"

"That depends," said Vann.

"On what?" said Marta.

"On whether the soup is as good as it smells."

Ada closed her eyes briefly. Twenty years she had managed this house, and this man had been in it ninety seconds and had already said the one thing guaranteed to either endear him to Marta forever or get him thrown into the canal.

Marta's chin lifted a quarter inch. "The soup," she said, "is better than it smells. Sit down before you drip on my floor."

So that was settled, then. Ada exhaled and pulled out chairs.

Nell arrived the way Nell always arrived—soundlessly and already present. One moment the kitchen held three people; the next it held four, and a girl of fourteen was sitting on the counter by the window with an apple in her hand, watching Vann with the frank, unblinking interest of a scientist observing a new species of beetle.

"Nell," Ada said, "counters are for pots."

"Mm," said Nell, not moving. "Who's he?"

"Elias Vann. A translator. He'll be staying with us."

"Why?"

"He needed a room."

"People with rooms don't need rooms," Nell said, and bit her apple. "People with rooms need explanations."

"Nell."

"I'm just asking. You always say questions are free."

"I also say apples are eaten at the table."

Nell slid off the counter, landed lightly, took her apple to the table, and sat directly across from Vann, arranging herself with the deliberate casualness of a chess player setting out pieces. Up close she had Marta's dark eyes and nobody's patience whatsoever.

"So," Nell said. "What do you translate?"

"Contracts, mostly. Letters, sometimes."

"Whose letters?"

"Nell," Ada said again, more sharply than she intended, and saw Vann's glance flick toward her—quick, noting the sharpness, filing it somewhere. She softened her voice. "Introductions over dinner. Both of you. Marta, is there enough?"

"There's always enough," Marta said, which was true and also a rebuke, because there was only ever exactly enough, calculated nightly by a woman who had lived through two shortages and a famine and considered abundance a form of carelessness. She ladled four bowls and set them down hard enough to make a point about something, though Ada couldn't have said what.

They ate. Vann ate carefully, appreciatively, complimenting the soup once and then, wisely, never again. Nell watched him eat the way she watched everything, which was completely. Marta ate with her eyes down and her ears wide open, and Ada knew—knew with the certainty of twenty years—that every word spoken at this table tonight would be re-examined later, alone, in the dark, by a woman who missed nothing and forgot less.

"So you're from the old country too," Marta said at last. Not a question. Marta didn't ask questions when she could issue verdicts instead.

"Born there," Vann said. "Stateless now, technically. My papers are—" he paused, a fraction too long, "—complicated."

"Complicated papers," Marta repeated. "In my experience, complicated papers belong to complicated men."

"Then it's fortunate," said Vann, "that I've come to a house that clearly enjoys complications."

Nell snorted into her soup. Marta's mouth did something at the corner that in a younger woman might have been called a smile.

Ada sat among them and felt the strangest sensation, like wearing a coat that fit after years of borrowing other people's. There were four people at her table. There was noise. There was a stranger telling a story about a border crossing and a bribed inspector, and Nell interrupting twice to ask how much the bribe was, and Marta declaring that inspectors had no shame in any country, and the whole ridiculous warm racket of a household forming itself around a man she had known for six hours and trusted approximately none percent.

She should have been afraid. She was, a little. But underneath the fear, quieter and more alarming, was something that felt almost like relief.

* * *

After dinner, Marta washed and Ada dried, which was the arrangement and had been since 1939, and which permitted them to speak in the low register reserved for matters of state.

"Well?" Marta said, handing over a bowl.

"He brought letters."

"From whom?"

Ada dried the bowl slowly. "Tomas."

The dish towel in Marta's hands went still. For a moment the only sound was the tap dripping, one drop at a time, patient as a metronome. Then Marta resumed washing with double vigor, scrubbing a pot that was already clean.

"Tomas is dead," Marta said.

"I know what we told everyone."

"We told everyone because it was true as far as any of us knows, Ada. And now a wet stranger arrives with paper in a dead boy's handwriting, and you put him in the back room." She thrust the pot at Ada. "I taught you arithmetic. Use it."

"You taught me that sums can be wrong if the figures are wrong."

"I taught you that men who arrive with stories arrive with debts attached. Every one of them. The charming ones especially." Marta turned off the tap and faced her, arms folded, and in the lamplight Ada could see the fine map of lines around her aunt's eyes, each one earned. "What did the letters say?"

"Nothing yet. I haven't read past the first page."

"You haven't—" Marta pressed her lips together. "Twenty years, and you haven't read past the first page."

"It's his handwriting, Aunt Marta. Reading it isn't a chore. It's a wound." Ada set the bowl in the rack with exaggerated care, because her hands wanted to shake and she refused to let them. "Tomorrow. Tomorrow I'll sit down with all of them, properly, and I'll find out what my brother wants from me across twenty years and however many borders. Tonight I fed the messenger, because that's what this house does. That's what Mother built it to do."

Marta was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again her voice had lost its edge, which from Marta was nearly tenderness. "Your mother built this house to be a harbor. Harbors take in wrecks. But harbors also get boarded, Ada. Remember whose flag you're flying before you let just anyone tie up at the dock."

"I remember."

"Do you? Because I watched you look at that man at dinner. You looked at him the way you used to look at Tomas's friends. Like family you hadn't met yet." She picked up the dish towel and snapped it flat. "Be careful with that. Hope is the most expensive thing we stock in this house."

* * *

Nell caught her on the stairs.

This was Nell's preferred method of conducting diplomacy—ambush on neutral ground, no witnesses, no retreat. She materialized on the landing with her braid coming loose and her arms crossed, and Ada thought, not for the first time, that the child had the tactical instincts of a general and would use them exclusively for getting dessert and asking forbidden questions.

"You knew him before today," Nell said.

"I met him this afternoon."

"But you knew *of* him. Before. Because when he said 'complicated papers' you flinched. Just a little. Right here." Nell touched her own cheekbone. "You only flinch there when something's about the old country. Or Papa."

Ada kept her voice level. "You watch very closely for a girl who's supposed to be doing her multiplication tables."

"I finished them. They're boring. Numbers only say one thing each." Nell uncrossed her arms. "Words are better. Words can hide things. That's why I like finding out what they're hiding." She tilted her head. "Is he hiding something?"

Everyone in this house was hiding something; that was practically the architectural plan. Ada crouched to Nell's level, which was getting harder every year—soon the girl would be looking down at her, and God help them all then.

"Nell. Some questions take longer than others to answer. This is one of them."

"You said questions are free."

"They are. Answers cost extra."

Nell considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. "How much extra?"

"Ask me again in a few days. If the answer gets cheaper, I'll tell you."

"That's not fair."

"No," Ada agreed, rising, kissing the top of the loosened braid. "It's Tuesday. Fairness is closed until Thursday."

Nell let her pass, but at the top of the stairs Ada heard her call softly down: "Miss Yarrow? He sits like Uncle Tomas used to sit. Left ankle on the knee, like he's about to argue with somebody."

Ada stood on the stairs in the dark and did not flinch, because Nell was watching, and because if she started flinching now she wasn't sure she'd stop.

* * *

The back room had been a storeroom, then a sewing room, then nothing in particular. Ada had made the bed that morning—had made it before she'd even decided to offer it, which told her something about herself she chose not to examine—and now she stood in the doorway watching Vann set down his one bag as though it were made of glass and full of bees.

"Blankets are in the chest," she said. "Water in the hall. If Marta knocks, answer immediately. If Nell knocks, don't answer anything at all."

"Noted." He sat on the edge of the narrow bed, which complained under him, and looked around the little room—at the faded wallpaper, the water stain shaped like a country that no longer existed, the window overlooking the yard. "Thank you. For the room. For the soup. For—" he gestured vaguely at the whole evening, the whole improbable fact of himself indoors, dry, fed. "You didn't have to."

"No," Ada agreed. "But my mother had a rule about strangers in the rain."

"What was the rule?"

"She never mentioned one. She simply never left anyone in it." Ada folded her arms. "Which brings us to tomorrow. Breakfast is at seven. After breakfast, you and I are going to sit at this table and you are going to tell me, precisely and completely, how a dead courier's letters came to be in your pocket, and why a man with complicated papers walked halfway across a city to deliver them to me personally. No omissions, Mr. Vann. I keep accounts. I will know."

For a moment—just a moment—something crossed his face that was not charm at all. Something tired and young and frightened, quickly shuttered. "And if I omit anyway?"

"Then I'll notice," Ada said, "the way my aunt notices, the way that child notices. This house runs on noticing. It's the only thing we've never run short of."

She wished him good night and closed the door, and stood a while in the hall with her hand still on the knob, listening to the small sounds of a stranger settling into her house: the creak of the bed, the scratch of a match, the long exhale of a man who had been holding his breath, perhaps, for years.

Below her, Marta was banking the stove. Above her, Nell was supposed to be asleep and was certainly not. And in her own room, on her own desk, wrapped in oilcloth inside a hatbox, lay four letters in her dead brother's handwriting, waiting for morning the way wounds wait for salt.

Ada went to her room, locked the door, and did not open the hatbox. Discipline, she had learned, was mostly a matter of doors.

But she slept badly, and near dawn she dreamed of a kitchen much larger than theirs, full of people at a long table, all of them talking at once, and every single one of them lying—and woke with the certain, cold knowledge that tomorrow, when Elias Vann began to tell her his story, she would hear the exact moment he decided not to tell all of it.

She had been raised in a household of silences. She knew their sound the way sailors know weather.
