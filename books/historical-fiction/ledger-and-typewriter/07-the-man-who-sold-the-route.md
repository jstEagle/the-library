## 7. The Man Who Sold the Route

Elias Vann had learned, in the course of a life spent translating other people's secrets, that the most dangerous documents were never the ones that looked dangerous. The incendiary pamphlets, the coded telegrams, the maps with little red pins — those announced themselves. They practically cleared their throats. No, the truly lethal papers were the ones that looked like nothing at all: a laundry list, a love letter, an invoice for forty crates of agricultural lime.

He was thinking about this on the third morning of his stay at Ada Yarrow's house, sitting at the kitchen table with the dead courier's effects spread out before him like a modest funeral, while Marta watched him from the stove with the expression of a woman deciding whether the soup needed more salt or the guest needed less air.

"Take another roll," she said, in the tone of a woman offering a weapon.

"Thank you," Vann said, and took one, because refusing Marta was a category of mistake he had already made once and did not intend to repeat.

The effects were these: a canvas satchel, water-stained; a pocket watch that had stopped at 4:17 and, judging by the crack in its face, would never be persuaded otherwise; a tin of peppermints, half gone; a leather wallet containing forged identity papers for a man named Anton Reisz, who had presumably existed only long enough to die; and the letters.

The letters. Four of them, written in the unmistakable hand of a man named Tomas Yarrow, who had been dead for twenty years and was apparently taking the news badly.

Vann turned the third letter over in his fingers and looked, again, at the address on the back. This was the part he had not yet told Ada. Not the whole part, anyway. He had told her about the letters, and he had told her about the courier — a story involving a boarding house in Trieste, a landlady with a heart condition, and a locked trunk that had taken him three days and one bribed locksmith to open. All of it true. He had simply failed to mention that the letters had two addresses on them, and that he had already delivered copies to one of the recipients.

The first address was Ada's. That had been the assignment.

The second address was a post box in the commercial district, rented under the name of a shipping concern that did not, as far as Vann could determine, own a single ship.

He knew the name behind the concern, though. He had known it since Vienna, since the bad winter, since the night a man in an excellent coat had bought him dinner and asked him, with the casualness of a man asking after someone's mother, whether he'd ever heard of a courier network that ran south through the mountains in '38.

Colonel Brecht.

Not colonel anymore, of course. Brecht had discovered, as many officers did, that peace paid better than war if you were willing to sell to both sides, and he had reinvented himself as a broker — of shipping contracts, of import licenses, of information, though the invoices for the last item were always, always disguised as invoices for agricultural lime. Vann had spent one memorable evening in Brecht's company watching him negotiate the price of a freighter with a Greek shipping magnate, and had come away with two conclusions: that Brecht never raised his voice, and that everyone in the room who thought they were winning was mistaken.

"Your soup is getting cold," Marta said, "and your face is doing arithmetic."

Vann looked up. "Does it show?"

"Everything shows on your face. It's why I don't trust you." She refilled his bowl anyway, which was Marta's way of saying the arithmetic was forgiven but not forgotten. "Ada's at the office until six. Nell's at school. So it's just us, translator. Talk."

* * *

He considered lying to her. It was worth noting, in fairness to himself, that he considered lying to everyone, the way other men considered smoking — a habit acknowledged, disapproved of, and indulged regardless. But Marta had the particular quality of a person who had survived something, and people who had survived something could smell a constructed sentence the way dogs smelled fear.

"The letters had a second address," he said.

Marta's ladle stopped moving. "Whose?"

"A man named Brecht. Colonel Brecht, once. He ran the eastern sector of the evacuation network." Vann turned the letter face-down on the table, as if the name might be visible through the paper. He knew it wasn't. He checked anyway. "He's the one who sold the route."

The kitchen went very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked twice, as if the house itself were clearing its throat.

"You're certain," Marta said. It was not a question, but he answered it as one.

"I worked for him. Briefly. Translation work, document work. The kind of employment where you're paid well and never asked what the documents are for, which is how you know exactly what they're for." He rubbed the bridge of his nose. "In '46, a network of couriers was moving people out — papers, berths, the whole apparatus. Someone sold the schedule. Three boats intercepted, forty-one people taken at the border, and the network folded inside a week. The man who sold it was Brecht. I know because I was the one who translated the manifests he was selling. I didn't know what they were at the time. That's the sentence I've been repeating to myself for twelve years, and it has never once made me feel better."

Marta set the ladle down with great care, the way one sets down something that might otherwise become a weapon.

"And these letters." She nodded at the table. "Tomas Yarrow's letters. He sent them to Brecht?"

"No. He sent them to a dead drop. Brecht intercepted them — or had them intercepted — and then hired me to deliver one set of copies to Ada." Vann heard the sentence leave his mouth and winced at it. "Copies. I had the originals copied before delivery. It's a professional habit."

"A professional habit," Marta repeated.

"When you work for a man like Brecht, you keep copies of everything. Not for leverage." He paused. "Well. Also for leverage. But mostly because the day Brecht decides you're a liability, the only thing that saves you is knowing where his bodies are buried. Metaphorically. Mostly metaphorically."

Marta sat down across from him. Up close, Vann could see the geography of her face — the lines that came from squinting, the ones that came from laughing, and a deep vertical crease between the brows that came, he suspected, from twenty years of listening at doors.

"Ada believes her brother died in the evacuation," Marta said quietly. "She has believed it for twenty years. She built her entire life on top of that grave, the way you build a house on a foundation. If you knew — if you suspected — that Tomas Yarrow was alive when these letters were written, and you delivered them anyway as part of a job for the man who—" She stopped. Started again, in a voice like a drawn wire. "What exactly did Brecht want with Ada Yarrow?"

Vann looked at the letter. The handwriting on the front was neat, unhurried, the hand of a man who had all the time in the world, which was either a comfort or an obscenity depending on where the writing had been done.

"He wants what she's carrying," Vann said. "Or what he believes she's carrying. The ledger. The other half of it."

"And do you know what's in this ledger?"

"I know it's a codebook. I know it's the key to every name in the network — every courier, every safe house, every route. And I know that half of it names the people who betrayed the other half." He met Marta's eyes. "I know that Brecht's name is in it. And I know that a man like Brecht would burn down a city to keep a document like that from surfacing, and then send an invoice for the lime."

* * *

Marta was silent for a long time. The soup simmered. The pipe knocked again, and Vann found himself absurdly grateful for the interruption, because the silence had begun to acquire weight.

"You said you were hired to deliver," Marta said at last. "Hired by whom? Brecht directly?"

"Through an intermediary. A lawyer's office. It's always a lawyer's office." Vann turned his coffee cup, a habit he'd picked up from watching nervous diplomats. "The instructions were simple. Deliver the letters to Ada Yarrow. Observe her response. Report back. I was told she'd be expecting them."

"Was she?"

"No. Which means either Brecht lied to me, or someone lied to Brecht, and I genuinely don't know which possibility frightens me more."

"And what were you meant to observe?"

Vann hesitated. This was the part he had been circling for three days, the part that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

"Whether she still has the ledger," he said. "And whether she can be persuaded to part with it. And if persuasion fails—" He made a small gesture, a shrug that wanted to be casual and came out apologetic. "Brecht is not a patient man. And I am not the only person he employs. There will be others. There are always others. Men who do the work I won't do, and who are paid better for it, which tells you something about the economics of the profession."

Marta looked at him for a long moment. Then she stood, went to the dresser, and poured two glasses of a plum spirit that Vann suspected had been made during a war and had the character to prove it.

"Twenty years ago," she said, handing him a glass, "I watched a girl walk out of a burning country carrying a book that was worth more than her life, and I watched her never speak of it again. Not once. Not to me, not to anyone. She folded that whole year up and put it away, and she has lived on top of the folded thing ever since, and I let her, because I thought the folded thing was safer shut." She drank. Vann drank. The spirit was less a beverage than a decision. "And now you're telling me the folded thing is exactly what a man like Brecht wants, and that you are the string he's using to pull it open."

"I'm telling you that, yes."

"And are you? Pulling?"

Vann turned his glass. The plum spirit caught the light and held it, amber and unrepentant.

"I don't know," he said, and was surprised to find that it was true. "I came here to do a job. I told myself it was just delivery — papers, observation, report. But then I watched her open that first letter, and I watched her read her brother's handwriting, and I watched a woman come apart and reassemble herself in the space of four seconds, and I thought: this is what Brecht is buying. This. He wants me to sell him the moment a woman finds out her dead brother is alive, and then he wants me to help him take the one thing she has left of him." He set the glass down. "I've done ugly work before, Marta. I've done it for money, for survival, for reasons I've dressed up in better clothes afterward. But I find I don't want to do this one."

"Because of Ada?"

"Because of the letters. Because someone wrote those letters believing they'd reach her, and I keep thinking about what it cost to write them — where he was, what he was hoping. And because—" Vann stopped. He was aware, suddenly and acutely, that he was about to say something honest, and honesty was a language he spoke with a bad accent. "Because when she looks at me, I feel like a forged document. Like something that says one thing on the front and has the truth bleeding through the back."

Marta almost smiled. It was a small, dry, dangerous smile, the kind that preceded either warmth or a verdict.

"You should know," she said, "that Brecht's name is not unfamiliar to me either. I knew men who worked the eastern sector, back in the old days. I knew what happened to the boats. And I have spent twenty years watching Ada carry the cost of that man's treachery without knowing his face." She stood, collected his empty glass, and looked down at him with the expression of a woman who had just finished a very long calculation. "So here is what happens now, translator. You are going to finish your soup. And then you are going to tell me everything — everything — about Brecht. Where he drinks, where he banks, who he fears. Because if he is coming for this family, he will find that we have been rehearsing for twenty years."

* * *

They talked until the light in the kitchen went from gold to gray to the blue of early evening. Vann told her about Brecht's offices above the shipping concern, about the colonel's habit of arriving everywhere twenty minutes early, about the two men who shadowed him — one built like a wardrobe, one built like an apology. He told her about the lime invoices, about the post box, about the lawyer's office with the brass plate that had outlasted three governments. Marta listened the way stone listens, and asked questions in the places where his story wore thin, and twice caught him softening a detail and made him say it again, properly.

It was nearly six when the front door opened, and Ada's footsteps came down the hall — unhurried, even, the footsteps of a woman who had spent twenty years teaching herself to walk as though nothing were wrong.

Vann stood up so quickly he knocked his chair into the table.

Ada appeared in the doorway with her hat still on and a paper bag from the grocer's in her hand, and she looked at the two of them — Vann on his feet, Marta by the stove, the table between them swept clean of documents but not, he suspected, of evidence — with the particular stillness of a bookkeeper who has found a column that doesn't add.

"You've told her," Ada said. Not to Vann. To Marta.

"Some of it," Marta said.

Ada set the paper bag down. She looked at Vann, and her eyes were calm in the way that deep water is calm, and Vann understood, with the clarity of a man watching the tide go out, that whatever she was about to ask him, he was going to answer honestly, because lying to Ada Yarrow was about to stop being a habit and start being an impossibility.

"Then you can tell me the rest," she said. "Starting with why a dead man's letters were addressed to two people — and why one of them was the man who killed my brother."

Before Vann could open his mouth, the front door banged, and Nell's voice came down the hallway, bright and breathless and eleven years old and far too early: "I'm home! Why does it smell like secrets in here?"
