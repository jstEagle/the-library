## 8. Nell Asks Questions

Elias Vann had been interrogated before. Properly interrogated, once, in a room in Trieste with bad lighting and worse intentions, by two men who took turns asking the same question in different languages until the question itself began to feel like weather. He had survived that by understanding one thing: the people asking wanted something from him, and anything a person wants can be traded for.

Nell Yarrow wanted nothing. That was the problem.

She stood in the doorway of Ada's sitting room with her coat still on and one shoe half off, having apparently kicked the other somewhere in the hall, and she looked at him the way an accountant looks at a column that doesn't add up. Eleven years old. A smudge of ink on her chin — she'd been at the harbor office again, he guessed, watching the clerks, memorizing everything, filing it all away in whatever tireless little archive she kept behind her eyes.

"You're sitting in Mr. Aldous's chair," she said.

"Is that his chair?"

"It's where Mr. Aldous sat when he came about the rent, and Aunt Marta made him leave because he smelled of herring." She removed her second shoe and set it beside its twin with the care of someone defusing something. "You don't smell of herring. So either you're more important than Mr. Aldous or you've come about something worse than rent."

"I could be a herring enthusiast who bathes."

She considered this with complete seriousness. "Nobody bathes before talking about rent."

* * *

He would like to report, later, that he held his ground. That he deployed some portion of the charm that had gotten him fed in Lisbon and lodged in Marseille and forgiven, twice, in Genoa. But there is a particular kind of child who has grown up inside a house of silences, and such a child develops an ear for them the way a deaf man's neighbor develops an ear for music — secondhand, obsessive, precise. Nell had heard the raised voices through the floor. He knew it the moment her gaze flicked to the ceiling, then back down to him, weighing.

"Aunt Ada's upset," Nell said. It was not quite a question, which was how he knew it was exactly one.

"She had some difficult news."

"About Tomas." The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. She didn't look away from him. "Everyone always says his name quietly. Even Aunt Marta, who isn't quiet about anything. You said it loudly. I heard you from the stairs."

"Nell—"

"You knew him." She came further into the room, barefoot now on the worn rug, and sat down across from him uninvited, knees together, hands folded, a small tribunal of one. "Or you know something about him. Because strangers don't make Aunt Ada look like that. Strangers make her polite, and polite is worse, but this was different. This was—" She searched for it, frowning. "This was like when the doctor told us about Grandmother, but before anyone said any words out loud. Just the face."

Vann set down the teacup Ada had given him twenty minutes and one lifetime ago. It rattled against the saucer, betraying him.

"You have very sharp eyes," he said.

"Everyone says that like it's a compliment," Nell said. "It's mostly just that nobody tells children true things, so you learn to read faces instead. It's like learning French. Except useful." A beat, and then, with devastating mildness: "Why did you really come here?"

* * *

There were, he reflected, several answers available to him.

The true answer: I was paid. A dead courier's satchel, a name written on a scrap of paper in a border town café, a sum of money that would cover forged papers and a berth and six months of not being anyone in particular — all of it contingent on delivering certain letters to a certain address, and on reporting what he found there to a certain man whose name he still hadn't spoken aloud in this house, because speaking it felt like opening a window in a sickroom.

The almost-true answer: I came because the letters deserved to reach their address, and because a man who translates other people's words for a living eventually gets tired of carrying them and never saying them himself.

The safe answer: I'm a translator. Your aunt can explain.

He opened his mouth to produce the safe answer, and found, to his genuine surprise, that it wouldn't come. Not under those eyes. There are audiences that flatter a lie and audiences that punish it, and then there is the rarest kind — the audience that simply waits, without malice, with infinite patience, while the liar discovers that lying to them would be like kicking a dog that has already decided to love you.

"I was paid to bring them," he said.

The words came out quieter than he intended. Nell didn't gloat. That was the worst part. She just nodded slowly, as if confirming a figure in a ledger.

"By whom?"

"That's the part I can't tell you yet."

"Why not?"

"Because telling it makes it more real, and I haven't decided what to do about it being real."

Nell absorbed this. He watched her do it — the small crease between her brows, the way her thumb pressed into the back of her hand. Then she said, with the terrible fairness of children: "That's honest, anyway. Aunt Marta would say it's weasel-talk, but it's honest weasel-talk."

"Thank you," he said gravely. "I'll put it on my card."

* * *

She didn't leave. He'd hoped, briefly, she would — children in books always ask their one piercing question and then run off to feed pigeons. Nell settled in instead, pulling her feet up onto the chair, making it clear that this was now a sitting and he was the exhibit.

"How did you get the letters?" she asked.

"From a man who couldn't deliver them himself anymore."

"Dead?"

"Yes."

"Were you friends?"

"No. I bought his satchel from a landlady who wanted three months' rent and got one."

Nell frowned. "That's stealing."

"It's negotiation. Stealing would have been if I'd only paid two."

The corner of her mouth twitched, fought itself, lost. She laughed — a short, startled bark, quickly suppressed, as though laughter in this house required a permit — and he understood something in that moment that he would carry with him for the rest of what remained of this story: that Nell Yarrow was funny the way people in hard houses are funny, sideways and fast, humor kept in a drawer like contraband, brought out only when no one official was listening. And that she had decided, somewhere between *why did you really come* and the herring, to like him. Which meant she would also, when the time came, judge him more harshly than any stranger could. Strangers got politeness. Family got truth.

"Was the dead man a friend of Tomas?" she asked.

"I don't know. Possibly. The letters passed through a lot of hands."

"Like a relay." Her eyes brightened with the particular hunger of a child assembling a map. "Like the smugglers in the stories Aunt Marta pretends not to remember. Each person carries it a bit and doesn't know the whole route, so no one can give everyone away."

Something cold walked down Vann's spine. Because that was precisely correct, and because she had arrived at it in eleven years where it had taken him four countries and a bribe.

"Where did the letters start?" Nell asked.

"I don't know."

"You do know." No anger in it. Just arithmetic. "You looked at Aunt Ada when you said it. People look at things they're protecting."

"Nell." He leaned forward. For once he wasn't performing; the performance had simply left the building, and what sat in the Aldous-herring-chair was a tired man holding a cup of cold tea. "Some of what I know could be dangerous for you to know. Not dangerous the way grown-ups mean it — dangerous the way your aunt means it, which is realer. If I tell you a thing, you carry it. Do you understand? Carrying things is heavier than it looks. I've been carrying these letters for three months and I'd trade my good coat to put them down."

Nell regarded him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she reached over and patted his knee — twice, briskly, the exact gesture of an aunt comforting a nephew, learned no doubt from watching Marta practice it on Ada.

"You don't have to tell me," she said. "I'm going to find out anyway. I always do. But you don't have to be the one who tells me." She paused, and added, with the gentleness adults reserve for the dying: "That must be tiring, being the one who knows things."

* * *

Ada found them there twenty minutes later.

Vann heard her on the stairs — she moved quietly for a woman who carried so much weight — and watched her stop in the doorway, take in the scene: the translator in the herring chair, the ward curled opposite like a magistrate, the cold tea, the long silence that had somehow stopped feeling empty and started feeling companionable.

"He asked me why I really came," Vann said.

"And?" Ada's voice was careful. He saw now what Nell had described: the face before the words, the grief held the way one holds a full cup across a crowded room.

"I told him the first part," Nell said. "He's saving the rest for when it won't fall on me." She unfolded herself, retrieved her shoes, and stood. At the door she turned, and delivered her verdict with judicial calm:

"He lies badly and honestly. I think you should keep him."

Then she was gone, thumping upstairs, and the house rearranged itself around her absence.

* * *

Ada sat down heavily in the chair Nell had vacated. Up close he could see she had been crying, recently and efficiently, the way people cry who learned young that tears have a schedule.

"She does that," Ada said. "Asks questions like a customs officer and then forgives you before you've finished declaring anything."

"Does she know about the letters?"

"Not the content. She knows Tomas existed; I never managed to erase him from the house, only from conversation." Ada rubbed her eyes. "Marta says I should send her to a convent school where they teach ignorance properly. Marta also says that about most schools."

"Your niece thinks I lie badly and honestly."

"Marta would call that an accurate character reference." But there was something loosened in her voice, a fraction less frost, and Vann realized the child had done in forty minutes what he hadn't managed in two days: she had made Ada laugh somewhere behind her eyes.

Then Ada's expression changed. The loosening closed, gently, like a hand folding shut. She looked at him with a new attention — not suspicion exactly, but inventory.

"Nell didn't ask who paid you," Ada said.

Vann went very still.

"I noticed that too," Ada continued softly. "A girl who notices everything, and she skipped the most important question. Either she already suspects the answer, or she was protecting you from answering it in front of me." She folded her hands in her lap, the bookkeeper's posture, the posture of a woman preparing to reconcile accounts. "So let me ask the question she was kind enough to skip, Mr. Vann. Who paid you to bring my brother's letters to my door?"

Downstairs, faintly, the front gate creaked. Footsteps on the path — unhurried, deliberate, the walk of someone who had been here before and expected to be admitted. Ada's head turned toward the sound, and Vann watched every drop of color leave her face.

"That," she whispered, "will be Marta. And if she's home early, then she's seen something." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "And if Marta's seen something, Mr. Vann, then whoever paid you may already know exactly where we live."
