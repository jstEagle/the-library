## 9. Marta's Warning

Marta Yarrow had survived three governments, two revolutions, and one marriage, and she had learned that the secret to all three was the same: know when a man was telling you the truth, and know when he was telling you what he needed you to believe. Sometimes those were the same thing. Usually they were not.

She was thinking about this on Thursday morning while she salted the cod in the kitchen of the house on Verity Lane, because the stranger—Vann, the translator with the careful smile and the suitcase that never seemed to open—had been under their roof for six days now, and Marta had not yet decided whether he was a blessing or an invoice.

Ada was at the ledger office. Nell was at school, or pretending to be at school, which at fourteen amounted to nearly the same thing with better weather. The house was quiet except for the salt hissing against fish and the clock in the hall that ran four minutes fast and had run four minutes fast since before the war that had unmade them, because Marta refused to have it fixed. A woman who lost everything once learned to distrust punctuality.

She heard the front gate before she heard footsteps. Vann walked like a man who had learned to be quiet in places where noise cost money, but he hadn't learned that the third board on the path announced visitors the way a town crier announces executions. Everyone in this house came and went through the kitchen door anyway; nobody used the front gate except strangers and salesmen and, once, twenty years ago, a boy carrying a telegram about Tomas.

Marta did not go still. She had stopped going still decades ago. She simply kept salting the cod and listened.

The third board creaked. Then silence—the particular silence of a man standing in a garden deciding whether he wanted to be caught looking at something. Then the creak again, closer, and the kitchen door opened, and there was Vann with his hat in his hands like a man arriving at confession rather than breakfast.

"Good morning, Mrs. Yarrow."

"Marta," she said, as she said every morning. "Mrs. Yarrow is my mother-in-law, may she rest, and even dead she wouldn't answer to you." She nodded at the table. "Sit. There's coffee, if you don't mind it tasting like punishment."

"I've had coffee in five countries," Vann said, sitting. "Punishment is familiar territory."

That was the thing about him that unsettled her. Most men lied clumsily, with their whole faces, and you could read them like a laundry list. This one lied well—but more than that, he told the truth in ways that were designed to make you stop asking questions. *I found the letters among a dead courier's effects.* Fine. Whose courier? Dead how? And why does a translator with no country left cross two borders to deliver letters to a bookkeeper he'd never met, out of what—sentiment? Men crossed borders for money, for fear, for orders. Sentiment was the story they used to dress up one of the other three.

He ate his bread and talked pleasantly about the weather, the harbor, the scandalous price of coal, and Marta answered pleasantly back, and underneath it they were doing arithmetic about each other, and both of them knew it, and neither of them minded being known to be doing it. There was almost a comfort in that. Dishonesty offended her; honest dishonesty she could work with.

When he left for the shipping offices—he claimed to be chasing translation work, and perhaps he was, and perhaps the work was chasing him—Marta stood at the window and watched him go down Verity Lane, and noticed what she had noticed every morning this week: he glanced back at the house exactly once, at the exact spot where Ada's bedroom window faced the street. Not checking whether anyone watched him. Checking whether anyone watched *her*.

Men in debt always checked on the creditor's behalf.

* * *

She waited until evening, when Ada came home with her collar damp from sea fog and her eyes carrying the particular tiredness she'd worn for twenty years—the tiredness of a woman who kept her grief in a filing system. Nell was upstairs doing homework, or performing the sound of homework, which involved considerable sighing audible through the ceiling.

"Come into the parlor," Marta said. "Bring your mending. It gives your hands something stupid to do while your head works."

"My hands are always doing something stupid."

"That's why I married your uncle," Marta said. "His hands were the stupidest I ever saw."

Ada sat across from her by the lamp, a stocking in her lap she had no intention of mending, and waited. That was another thing about her niece: she had inherited the family patience, which was less a virtue than a weapon with the safety on.

Marta took off her spectacles, polished them on her apron though they didn't need it—a habit she permitted herself precisely because everyone believed it meant she was gathering her thoughts, when actually it meant she had already gathered them—and put them back on.

"I recognized the name," she said. "The one in the second letter. Brecht."

She watched it land. Ada's needle stopped moving through empty air. The girl had been carrying that name around for days now like a stone in her shoe, saying nothing, because saying nothing was Ada's native language, and Marta loved her for it and was tired of it in equal measure.

"You didn't say anything," Ada said.

"I'm saying something now."

"Where from? The old days?"

Marta looked at the fire. Twenty years ago, in another country with another name on its stamps, she had sat in a kitchen very like this one and listened to men talk in low voices about routes and berths and papers, and she had poured the coffee and kept her mouth shut, because her husband—Ada's father, God rest him and God forgive him, in whichever order heaven preferred—had been one of the men talking.

"Brecht was a name that came through the network," she said carefully. "Before the end. An officer who could be bought—not for money, mind, that would have been simple. He bought things with information. You gave him a name, he gave you a favor. He collected people the way other men collect stamps, and he was just as tidy about it." She turned the stocking over in her lap, though it wasn't hers. "When the route went wrong—when the boats stopped coming—I heard men say the name Brecht the way you'd say a word for a disease."

Ada said nothing. Her face did the thing it always did, closing like a shop shutter, polite and final.

"You knew," Marta said. "You already suspected."

"I suspected nothing. I am a bookkeeper. I notice discrepancies." Ada's voice was level. "A stranger arrives with my dead brother's handwriting and a name attached that makes your face do something I've only seen twice in twenty years. Once at Father's funeral and once when the ship left without us. I notice discrepancies."

For a moment the two women regarded each other across the lamp, and Marta thought: we are the same animal, this girl and I. We keep accounts. We balance them nightly. We believe that if the columns add up, the past will hold still.

"There's more," Marta said.

"There's always more. That's what 'more' means."

"The man in our kitchen. Vann." She said the name flatly, testing its weight. "I've been watching him for six days, and I'll tell you what I see. He eats like a guest and moves like a lodger. He asks questions sideways, so politely you don't feel them going in. He watches Ada—" she corrected herself, "he watches *you*—the way a man watches weather he's been paid to predict. And this morning, before you were home, he stood at the gate for two full minutes reading the notice board on the corner. Shipping announcements. Repatriation notices." She let that sit. "Nobody reads repatriation notices for pleasure, Ada. You read them because you want to leave, or because someone wants to make you."

Ada set down the stocking entirely. "You think he's here for the berth."

"I think men like Vann arrive with debts attached. Maybe he owes money. Maybe he owes a favor. Maybe he owes a man named Brecht his forged papers and his skin, and the price is a woman with a ledger-shaped hole where her family used to be." Marta leaned forward. "I'm not telling you to throw him out. He might be exactly what he says. But I'm telling you that when the route fell, it didn't fall because of bad luck or bad weather. It fell because somebody we fed at our own table sold it. So forgive me if hospitality has started to look like a door left unlocked."

The fire ticked. Upstairs, Nell's sighing paused, then resumed—she was listening, of course, that child heard everything short of thoughts, and give her a year.

"And what do we do?" Ada asked quietly. "Ask him directly? He'd tell us a truth so smooth we'd thank him for it."

"No. You don't ask a fisherman if there are hooks in the water. You watch where he casts." Marta reached over and took her niece's hand, the one with the ink stain on the middle finger that never washed out. "You watch, and I'll watch, and between us we'll be smarter than one suspicious old woman ought to be. And Ada—if it comes to choosing between him and us, you choose us. Do you hear me? You've spent twenty years paying for loyalty to people who didn't deserve the invoice. Don't start a new account."

Ada's jaw tightened. For a moment she looked nineteen again, standing on a quay with her hand around a ticket she was about to give away.

"Tomas trusted someone," she said. "And it got him killed. Or it didn't, apparently, which raises questions I can't ask a dead man. Either way—" she exhaled, "—either way, yes. I hear you."

"Good." Marta squeezed her hand and released it, and picked up her own mending, and for a few minutes the room was warm and ordinary and full of the small sounds of two women pretending the conversation was over.

It was Nell who interrupted, thundering down the stairs with the gracelessness of youth and the timing of a born journalist, waving an envelope.

"This came for Aunt Ada. The post boy was whistling, which I thought was cheerful, but then I saw the stamp and it's foreign, and also—" she held it out, eyes bright with the terrible delight of a girl who smelled a story, "—it's addressed in the same handwriting as the other ones. I compared."

Ada took the envelope as if it were hot. Marta watched her turn it over once, twice, thumb finding the seal without breaking it.

"Well?" Nell demanded. "Aren't you going to open it?"

"Not tonight," Marta said firmly, before Ada could answer. "Tonight you'll eat supper, and tomorrow is Saturday, and whatever's in that letter has waited twenty years and can wait twelve hours while we sleep on it. Letters written by the dead should be read in daylight. That's not superstition, that's accounting."

But she saw Ada's fingers resting on the sealed flap, light as a pulse, and she saw the hunger underneath the discipline, and Marta thought, with a sinking that no amount of salted cod could cure:

Daylight won't help, girl. Whatever's in there, it's already been opened somewhere else first—and whoever opened it knows exactly what bait to use.
