## 10. The Second Letter

The envelope came on a Tuesday, which Ada would later think was fitting, because Tuesdays were when she balanced the books at Halloway & Sons and was therefore already primed for bad news dressed in tidy columns.

It did not come by post. That was the first wrong thing about it.

Ada noticed the details the way she noticed everything—compulsively, in order, the way her mother had taught her to count silverware. The letter lay on the hall table of Marta's narrow house at eleven minutes past eight in the morning, propped against the umbrella stand like a guest who had let himself in. No postmark. No stamp. Her name written across the front in ink so fresh it still smelled faintly of iron gall: *Mrs. A. Yarrow.* Not Miss. Not Comrade. Mrs., though she had never been anyone's wife. Someone had guessed at respectability and missed by a decade.

She stood very still in the hallway, coat half-buttoned, one glove on, and listened to the house breathe above her. Nell's clock-radio muttering through the ceiling. The pipes knocking their familiar morning argument. Somewhere downstairs in the kitchen, Marta was murdering an egg with the particular violence of a woman who believed breakfast was a moral obligation.

No footsteps retreating down the front steps. Whoever had delivered it had done it quietly, hours ago, before the milk cart even. Which meant they had stood on this step in the dark, in the rain that had come in off the harbor around four, and pressed an envelope through a slot that hadn't been oiled since the war.

Ada picked it up with two fingers, as if it might be hot.

* * *

She didn't open it in the hall. She didn't open it in the kitchen either, where Marta had laid three plates out of habit and then, catching herself, snapped "sit down, all of you, we're not animals" at the empty chairs until Vann appeared from the sitting room looking rumpled and grateful, and Nell thundered down the stairs two steps at a time in the manner of fifteen-year-olds who have discovered gravity is free.

Breakfast proceeded under conditions of total surveillance. Ada watched Vann butter his toast. Vann watched the window. Marta watched everyone, and Nell watched Ada watch the envelope, which Ada had placed on the sideboard between the sugar bowl and the photograph of her mother, face down, casual as a losing hand.

"You've got a letter," Nell said, not a question.

"I have several letters."

"It's face down."

"So are yours, most mornings. I've seen you hide report cards behind the marmalade."

"That was *one time*, and it was a fair grade, they just don't like me." Nell reached; Ada moved the sugar bowl an inch to the left, a meaningless gesture that fooled no one, and Nell sat back with the satisfied expression of a girl filing evidence.

Marta said nothing at all, which from Marta was a shout. She refilled coffee cups nobody had finished, scraped the egg pan harder than eggs deserve, and once, only once, her eyes met Ada's over the teapot—a long, dry look that contained, translated roughly from Marta into English: *I know, and we will speak of it when the child is elsewhere, and you will tell me everything, and I will pretend I didn't already guess.*

Twenty years of practice, and the household could hold an entire argument without a word spoken aloud. It was almost a comfort.

* * *

She opened it upstairs, in her room, with the door locked and the window cracked—not for air, but because she had learned in another life that a cracked window gave you somewhere to throw things you needed gone quickly.

Inside: a single sheet, good stock, typed. Not handwritten. Tomas's letters—the ones Vann had brought, the ones she had checked and rechecked against twenty years of memory until her eyes burned—had been handwritten, slanted left, the *t*s crossed with little impatient slashes like a boy rushing toward something. This was typewritten, formal, correct.

But the signature—

The signature was Tomas's. And beneath it, where a man might write a date, there was instead a small figure: a circle with a tail, like a balloon on a string. Their childhood cipher's opening mark. The one he'd invented when they were nine and ten, hiding notes inside library books, convinced that the whole apparatus of adults and governesses and God Himself was reading over their shoulders.

Nobody alive knew that mark. Nobody except Tomas, and Ada, and now whoever had written this letter—which meant either Tomas had written it, or someone had made him write it, or someone had taken it from him along with whatever else they'd taken.

She read the body of the letter standing up, because she distrusted what her hands did when she sat down with bad news.

*Mrs. Yarrow —*

*Your brother asks that you bring the remainder to the Harbor Office, Pier District, any weekday between ten and four. He asks it himself, in his own words, below.*

*He also wishes you to know he thinks of the summer at the lake, and the boat, and understands at last why you laughed.*

Then the handwritten paragraph. Then the balloon-on-a-string.

Ada read it four times. On the fourth reading, her legs informed her they were no longer interested in standing, and she sat on the edge of her bed with the letter trembling in a way she refused to dignify by naming.

Because here was the thing. Here was the unbearable, impossible thing: *the summer at the lake, and the boat.* There had been a summer at a lake. There had been a boat, a rotten green rowboat Tomas had been forbidden to take out, and had taken out anyway, and Ada—ten years old, solemn, constitutionally incapable of mischief—had watched him push off from the reeds and had laughed. Not tattled. Laughed. And Tomas, halfway across the water, had turned around and shouted, "You're the only one who ever laughs, Addie!" and capsized on purpose to make her do it again.

It was a true memory. It was a *deep* memory. It was not the kind of thing a man could torture out of another man, because it wasn't a secret—it was nothing, it was a green rowboat and a sister's laugh—and that was precisely what made it perfect. You couldn't guard against a question about a thing you'd never thought to hide.

Hope arrived first. Hope always arrived first with her, barging in like Nell, all elbows and certainty. *Tomas is alive. Tomas is asking for me. Twenty years, and he's asking for me.*

Hope got exactly four minutes.

Then trap-sense came up the stairs after it, quiet as a landlady checking on unpaid rent, and began its inventory.

Item: the letter asked for "the remainder"—a phrase meaning nothing to a stranger and everything to her, since only last week she had decoded enough of the second letter's cipher to understand that the ledger she'd carried out of the republic in 1938 had been half of a whole, and that the other half—the names, the traitors' names, the half that mattered—had traveled inside her own head all these years, memorized and burned and carried like a stone behind her breastbone.

Item: no one should know the phrase "the remainder" existed. She had said it aloud once, to Vann, three nights ago, in this room, with the window shut.

Item: Tomas, if alive, if writing, if begging—would not route his plea through a typed intermediary and a hand-delivered envelope. He would write it himself, whole, in his slashing impatient hand. Unless his hands were no longer permitted pens. Unless someone was holding the pen for him.

Item: the harbor office kept hours convenient for soldiers.

Ada set the letter on her knee and looked at the ceiling and did the arithmetic she hated most, which was the arithmetic of hope divided by fear, and got a number she couldn't reduce.

* * *

Downstairs, the house had arranged itself into a tribunal without anyone calling a vote.

Vann was at the sitting-room window with a cup of coffee he wasn't drinking, wearing the expression of a man rehearsing innocence. Marta occupied the good chair with her mending—socks, always socks, as if the family were in danger of barefootedness rather than ruin—and Nell sat cross-legged on the rug doing homework with such theatrical diligence that the geography textbook was quite clearly upside down.

"The post came early today," Ada said, mildly, to the room in general.

"Did it," said Marta, to a sock.

"Before eight. Hand-delivered."

The needle stopped. Started again.

"For me," Ada continued. She took the letter from her apron pocket and held it up. "From my brother. Asking me to visit the harbor office and bring him 'the remainder.'"

Silence. The specific silence of four people each deciding whether to be the first to say something stupid.

Nell broke it, naturally, because Nell had never met a silence she couldn't improve. "What's the remainder?"

"Homework," Ada said.

"I'm holding my book upside down, Ada, I know how homework works."

Vann crossed the room and held out his hand for the letter, and Ada—this was the interesting part, the part she turned over later—hesitated. Just a half-second. Just long enough for him to see it. His face did something complicated and then settled into careful blankness, and he read the page twice, and when he looked up, his eyes went first to the window and then to the door, cataloguing exits, the way a man does when the walls have started talking about him.

"Harbor office," he said. "Pier district."

"Yes."

"That's—" He stopped himself. Ada watched him stop himself, watched the sentence he swallowed go down hard, and thought: *he knows whose office that is.* Or whose it might as well be. She filed it away with the other things she was filing away lately; the drawer was getting full.

"It's a trap," Marta announced to the sock, conversationally, as though remarking on the weather. "Of course it's a trap. Men don't survive to be old by walking into buildings where soldiers keep office hours. Sit down, Ada. Drink your tea. We'll think."

"We'll think," Ada agreed, and sat, and drank tea that tasted of nothing, while Nell righted her textbook with elaborate unconcern and Vann stood too still by the window, and outside the rain came back off the harbor like something returning for a forgotten debt.

* * *

That night she decoded it properly.

Not the typed part—there was nothing in the typed part but furniture, polite words built to sound official. The real letter was in the handwriting, compressed the way Tomas used to compress things when they were children and every word cost them: the balloon-and-string, and then beneath it, worked into the descenders and flourishes where no censor would think to look, a string of small marks that resolved, once she dug the old system out of the cellar of herself, into six words.

*COME ALONE. THEY READ EVERYTHING ELSE.*

She sat with it for a long time. Rain on the glass. The house ticking down toward sleep—Marta's floorboards, Nell's radio clicking off mid-sentence, Vann's light burning past midnight the way it did, the way a man's does when sleep has become a negotiation he keeps losing.

Come alone. They read everything else. Which meant: the handwriting was Tomas's, or close enough that it didn't matter. Which meant: Tomas was alive and aware enough to build a message inside a message, trusting his sister to find it the way she'd found library-book notes forty years ago—or thirty; time had gone soft at the edges—which meant hope again, hope climbing the stairs on its elbows.

And it meant, underneath, cold and clear as harbor water in February: *they*. There was a *they*. And *they* wanted the remainder, and *they* were willing to spend a living brother to get it, and they had chosen the harbor office because the harbor office was theirs.

Ada put the letter back in its envelope, and the envelope inside the lining of her suitcase, and the suitcase under the loose floorboard where her mother's effects had lived for a year, and lay in the dark doing sums.

Ten days, the newspapers said, until the repatriation ship. Berths for verified former citizens—three berths, as far as anyone could determine, and four people in this house who might claim one, and a fifth waiting at a pier somewhere with a pen he wasn't allowed.

Her brother was alive. Her brother was bait. Both statements were equally true, and she could not yet tell which one the world intended to use.

In the morning, she decided, watching the ceiling instead of sleeping, she would go to the harbor district—not the office, not yet, never the office—but near it. To look. A woman can walk past a building. A woman can buy fish. A woman can count windows and doors and men in coats who stand too long in doorways pretending to read shop signs.

And if she saw what she expected to see—if the trap showed its teeth—then at least she would know the shape of the cage before she decided whether to walk into it.

Beside her bed, the old typewriter sat under its dust cloth like a patient animal. She put her hand on it briefly, the way you'd touch a horse's neck before a long ride, and turned off the lamp.

Outside, far off toward the water, a ship's horn sounded once and quit, as if it had only been practicing.
