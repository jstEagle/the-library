## 11. Brecht at the Consulate

Elias Vann had stolen three suits in his life, and he could tell you honestly that the third was the best of them—a charcoal wool number lifted from a tailor's shop in Trieste with a story so clean he'd almost believed it himself. The shop owner had been selling papers on the side, and Vann had paid for the suit with a favor: a letter of introduction to a man in Vienna who owed the tailor money. Everyone owed someone. That was the whole architecture of the world, as far as Vann could tell, and tonight it had gotten him past a consular doorman who looked at forged invitations the way a customs officer looks at honest faces—with relief, and a little boredom.

The consulate occupied a former banking house, all marble and bad acoustics, and the reception for the anniversary of some treaty nobody believed in anymore was exactly the kind of event where a translator with no country left could move like a fish through reeds. Vann took two glasses of wine from a passing tray—one to hold, one to hold slightly differently—and positioned himself near the tall windows where the light was kindest and conversation came to die.

He told himself he was there for information. He was not there because Ada Yarrow had looked at him over her kitchen table three days ago and said, *You know more than you're saying,* and he had felt the sentence land somewhere under his ribs like a swallowed coin. He was not there because Nell—thirteen years old and sharper than any blade in the house—had asked him at breakfast whether lying was a profession or a hobby. He was certainly not there because Marta had watched him leave that morning and said nothing at all, which from Marta was a full paragraph.

He was there for information. The letters named a second recipient. A Colonel Brecht. And men like Brecht did not stay hidden; they surfaced, the way drowned things eventually surface, usually at parties.

* * *

The first hour taught him nothing except that diplomatic wine had not improved since the war, and that a vice-consul's wife named Ilse could talk about orchids with a passion that made him briefly forget he was afraid. He liked Ilse. He hoped she never learned what he was.

It was during his fourth circuit of the room—he had counted doors by then, an old habit, two exits besides the main one, one behind the band dais—that the temperature of the room changed. Not literally. But Vann had spent enough years in rooms where dangerous men were present to know how a crowd rearranges itself around power, unconsciously, the way iron filings find their pattern. Conversations bent toward the entrance and away again. A cluster of military attachés straightened without seeming to.

And there he was.

Colonel Brecht was not what Vann expected, which surprised him, because Vann prided himself on expecting everything. He was not tall. He was not scarred or cold-eyed or any of the things the word *colonel* conjures. He was a comfortable-looking man of about sixty, silver-haired, with the ruddy complexion of someone who enjoyed his health and a laugh that seemed genuinely warm. He wore civilian evening dress and wore it well. He stood with one hand resting on the shoulder of a young lieutenant, and he was telling a story—the lieutenant laughed, then a captain, then a small orbit of listeners—and Vann thought, with a lurch of professional admiration, *that is a man people trust.*

That was the horror of him. Not cruelty. Trustworthiness. Twenty years ago this man had sold out an escape route—Ada's route, Tomas's route, forty-one names on a list Vann had only begun to imagine—and he had done it well enough to be decorated, retired comfortably, and welcomed into rooms like this one as a pillar of whatever passed for stability now. He hadn't even needed to change sides. Men like Brecht never changed sides; they changed *clients*, and the furniture stayed the same.

Vann turned back to the window and drank his wine and did not look again for a full ninety seconds. He used the time to compose his face into the expression of a bored translator thinking about his supper.

Then he let himself glance, casually, the way one glances at anyone.

Brecht was looking at him.

Not idly. Not the sweep of a host taking attendance. The look was held a half-second too long, weighted, and then—this was the part that turned Vann's mouth dry—it ended in the faintest tilt of the head. Recognition. Or the performance of recognition. With a man like Brecht you could never be sure which was worse.

* * *

Vann did the sensible thing. He moved.

Not quickly—quickly was a confession—but with the unhurried drift of a man seeking better canapés. He circled toward the far wall, collected a plate of something involving pastry and regret, and inserted himself into a conversation between two trade attachés arguing about shipping tariffs. He contributed a remark about harbor fees that was technically true and completely irrelevant, and for four minutes he was merely a background figure, forgettable, one of those men whose name you lose before you've finished shaking his hand.

When he glanced up again, Brecht was being introduced to the consul himself, laughing warmly at something, one hand pressed to his own chest in modest delight. And yet.

A waiter drifted past Vann's elbow. "The colonel asks if you enjoyed Trieste," the waiter said quietly, not looking at him, already moving on.

Vann set down his plate. His hands were steady, which pleased him; inside, something was doing arithmetic very fast and not liking the totals. *Trieste.* The tailor. Which meant either Brecht's people had been following Vann since before he ever reached Ada's door—which meant the letters were bait from the start—or Brecht simply had eyes everywhere and had catalogued Vann's face the way a lepidopterist catalogs moths. Both possibilities ended in the same place: the predator knew the prey's face, and had known it longer than the prey knew the predator's.

He considered leaving. He actually considered it, standing there with the orchestra sawing through a waltz and Ilse waving at him from across the room with genuine friendliness. Leaving meant running, and running confirmed everything, and besides—there was Ada, and Nell, and an old woman who couldn't sail, and a dead brother who wasn't dead. Running had stopped being available to him somewhere around the moment he'd sat down at Ada's table and lied to her face while she poured him tea.

So instead Elias Vann did the stupid thing, which in his long experience was sometimes indistinguishable from the brave thing.

He walked over and introduced himself.

* * *

"Colonel Brecht. Elias Vann." He offered his hand with the smile he kept for border officials—warm, faintly self-deprecating, the smile of a man with nothing to hide because he had nothing worth finding. "I translate. Badly, according to my clients."

"Ah! The translator." Brecht's grip was dry and firm and lasted exactly one beat too long. Up close, the warmth in his face was real and also entirely decorative, the way wallpaper is real. "I'm told you have a gift for languages. How many?"

"Enough to get into trouble in six of them."

Brecht laughed, and the orbit of listeners laughed, and Vann understood that he was being permitted to exist in this orbit for exactly as long as it amused the center of it. "Trouble," Brecht repeated pleasantly. "You know, I spent twenty years in military intelligence, and I'll tell you what I learned. It's never the liars you have to worry about. Liars are honest work. It's the men who tell the truth selectively." He sipped his wine, eyes mild over the rim. "Truth is like currency, Herr Vann. Its value depends entirely on what you withhold."

"And what do you recommend withholding, Colonel?"

"Debts." The word dropped lightly as a coin into a well, but Vann heard it hit bottom. "Men who owe debts spend their lives looking over their shoulders. Very inefficient. Far better to settle accounts early." Brecht smiled at the lieutenant beside him. "Isn't that right, Andreas? Pay your tailor."

The lieutenant laughed obediently. Somewhere in Vann's chest, his heart was conducting its own quieter transaction: *he knows about the payment. He knows I took money to deliver those letters. He knows I haven't delivered the rest of what I was paid to deliver—her, the ledger, the whole cargo of it.*

"I'll keep that in mind," Vann said. "Though my tailors tend to forgive me. I'm charming when cornered."

"I don't doubt it." Brecht turned to say something to the consul's aide, giving Vann the exquisite courtesy of being dismissed without ceremony—and then, just before fully turning away, added, as if it were an afterthought, a small kindness: "Give my regards to the Yarrow household. The aunt especially. Marta, isn't it? We knew each other, years ago. Before the unpleasantness." And then he was gone into the crowd, silver head bowing graciously over someone's hand, laughter trailing behind him like a cologne.

* * *

Vann stood very still among the waltzing couples and let the room spin gently around him without his participation.

*Marta.* Not Ada—Marta. Which meant Brecht knew the household's interior geography. Which meant someone had given him that geography recently, precisely, and Vann ran down the short ugly list of candidates and arrived nowhere comforting, because the truth was the simplest one: Brecht had been the officer who broke the network. He had known these names for twenty years. He had been waiting, patient as arithmetic, for the ledger's remainder to surface—and now it had, in the shape of a translator carrying letters written in a dead boy's handwriting.

Or a living boy's handwriting. God, he still didn't know which lie the letters were telling.

Ilse appeared at his elbow, flushed from dancing, holding out a fresh glass of wine. "You look pale, Herr Vann. Are the canapés disagreeing with you?"

"They're plotting against me," Vann said, and managed to make it sound funny, and she laughed, and he thought: *this is the trick of it. You can stand in a bright room full of kind people and be utterly alone, and the danger doesn't announce itself, it laughs at jokes and remembers your debts.* He stayed another twenty minutes out of discipline, spoke to no one of consequence, and left by the main entrance at half past eleven, nodding goodnight to the doorman who had waved his forgery through hours earlier.

Outside, the night smelled of rain and coal smoke, and the consulate's windows blazed warm behind him. Halfway down the steps he stopped.

Under the lamplight at the street's edge, a man in a plain dark coat stood smoking, unhurried, watching the door of the consulate with the patience of furniture. As Vann descended, the smoker didn't turn—but he raised his cigarette slightly, a salute, or a receipt of delivery, and exhaled smoke into the cold air like punctuation.

Vann walked home the long way, twice around the fountain square, once through the market arcade with its shuttered stalls, listening to his own footsteps the entire way.

There was a lamp burning in the Yarrow kitchen when he got in. Ada sat at the table with the burned-corner ledger page flat before her, and she looked up when he came in, and her eyes went over his face the way she read columns of figures—looking for the error.

"You're late," she said.

"Yes," said Vann, hanging up his stolen coat. "And we need to talk about your aunt's old friends."
