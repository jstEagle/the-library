## 12. What Remainder Means

The letter sat on Ada's kitchen table for three days before she touched it again.

Not because she was afraid. She told herself it was because the bakery order needed doing, and the quarterly accounts for Herr Lindqvist's shipping office would not reconcile themselves, and Nell had worn through the heel of her only good stocking, which meant a trip to the cobbler on Fiskargatan, which meant haggling with old Persson, who charged by the argument rather than by the repair.

But Marta knew better. Marta had watched her niece carry that envelope from the hall table to the kitchen like a live coal held in a folded cloth, set it down in the exact center of the table — not near the window where damp might reach it, not near the stove where grease might find it — and then proceed to orbit it for seventy-two hours as though it were a piece of furniture she'd only just noticed and couldn't decide whether to keep.

"You're going to wear a groove in my floor," Marta said on the third evening, setting down a pot of coffee with more force than the pot deserved. "Either open it or bury it in the garden with the rest of your secrets."

"I'm thinking."

"You've been thinking since Tuesday. In my experience, thinking past Thursday is just fear wearing a respectable coat."

Ada almost smiled at that. Almost. "There's a right way to read this letter, Marta, and a wrong way, and I don't yet know which one it is."

"There's only one way to read anything. With your eyes."

"With *his* eyes," Ada said quietly. "That's what worries me."

Marta poured two cups, pushed one across the table until it nudged the envelope's corner, an act of provocation so deliberate that Ada could have laughed if her chest weren't so tight. Then Marta took her cup and her knitting into the parlor and closed the door — not all the way, never all the way, because Marta believed a closed door was an invitation to eavesdrop and an open door was an invitation to be useful, and she'd settled long ago on leaving them half-open so she could do both.

Ada sat alone with the letter and the coffee going cold and the house settling around her in its evening sounds: the tick of the water pipes, the groan of the stair's fourth step when nobody was on it, the gulls out over the harbor complaining about something eternal.

She turned the envelope over. Her name on the front, in Tomas's hand. Not the careful schoolroom hand he'd been taught, but his real hand — the one that slanted left when he was hurried and went round-bellied when he was calm, the one that always crossed his t's with a little downward flick, like a bird landing. Twenty years of official documents said this hand belonged to a dead man. Twenty years of official documents had also said her father was an honest customs officer, so Ada had developed a certain professional skepticism about paper.

*Bring the remainder to the harbor office.*

That was all the letter said. Five words plus a signature. She had read them a hundred times since Vann delivered it, and each reading had produced a different weather system in her chest. The remainder of what? Of Tomas's life? Of some debt? Of the escape route's story, the part that ended at the pier with soldiers and shouting and her mother's hand going slack in hers?

But no. That wasn't how Tomas wrote. Even at eleven years old, even in the middle of the games they played while their father's colleagues drank schnapps downstairs, Tomas had been precise. He didn't say things. He *encoded* them, and the encoding was never decoration. It was mercy. It was how you told someone the truth without making them hold it in their bare hands.

So there would be a cipher. There was always a cipher.

Ada finished the cold coffee, washed the cup, dried it, put it away — the small liturgy of delay complete — and finally took the letter upstairs to her room, where she kept the one object in the world she had never shown another living soul.

It lived at the back of her wardrobe, inside a hatbox that had once held her mother's Sunday hat, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. It was a child's notebook, its cover soft with handling, its pages filled in two hands: hers and Tomas's, alternating, arguing, correcting. They had invented it the winter she was thirteen and he was eleven, the winter the schnapps parties downstairs had started including men whose names were spoken too softly, and their father had begun locking the study, and their mother had begun humming hymns at odd hours like a woman trying to drown out a radio only she could hear.

They called it the Magpie Book, because magpies collected shiny things, and they had decided that they were collecting the shiny dangerous things adults said when they thought children weren't listening. And because even then Ada understood that a magpie's hoard must be hidden, they had built the cipher together out of the raw materials of their childhood: the tide tables from the harbor wall, the names of their grandmother's cats, the counting rhyme Nell would later learn in a different country without knowing it carried secrets in its bones.

The key was simple, which was the whole genius of it. Simple enough for a frightened child to remember under pressure. The first letters of every line of the counting rhyme gave you the substitution order; the cats' names, in order of seniority, gave you the transposition blocks; and the tide table gave you the day-key, because Tomas had insisted — eleven years old and already a cryptographer of real instinct — that a code without a changing day-key was a code waiting to be read.

*What date did you write it?* she asked the envelope. No postmark worth trusting. But the paper itself — she held it to the lamp — was the cheap pulpy stock used by the prison administrative offices in the north. She knew because she had spent twenty years learning everything about the places Tomas might have died, the way other people learned fishing or flowers. Prison paper. Which meant the date on the letter was likely true, because prisons kept honest calendars even when they told dishonest stories.

The fourteenth of October.

She opened the Magpie Book to the tide tables copied in the back, found the fourteenth of October, and began.

* * *

Decoding, for Ada, had always felt less like reading and more like listening to someone through a wall. You caught the shape of the voice before the words. First came the structure — the rhythm of the numbers, the way the transposition blocks fell — and you could tell from the structure alone whether the writer was calm or afraid. Tomas's letters home from school had decoded with a bouncy, showing-off rhythm. His last letter before the evacuation, the one she'd memorized and burned on the boat, had decoded with a rhythm like a man walking fast in the dark.

This one walked fast in the dark.

She worked by lamplight with a pencil she never let touch the paper itself, copying the plaintext onto a separate sheet she would burn afterward, because twenty years of habit does not loosen its grip just because the war is over and the borders have redrawn themselves twice. The first sentences came through in fragments, and her heart performed a slow, painful gymnastics routine:

*...if this reaches you, then Vann found you, which means either he is honest or he is very good, and I have had twenty years to consider the difference between those two things...*

Alive. He was alive when he wrote this. Her brother, who had a grave in a municipal cemetery with his name spelled wrong on it — she had checked, once, from a distance, and wept with rage at the misspelling — had sat somewhere with prison paper and a borrowed pen and written to her in the cipher they invented between the ages of nine and fourteen, and the cipher still worked, still fit him like his own coat.

*...I know what they told you. I know what the lists said. Lists are how they kill people twice, little magpie, once in the body and once in the record...*

Her eyes blurred and she made herself stop, made herself stand at the window and count harbor lights until her vision cleared, because a decoder who cries onto the plaintext makes errors, and errors in this kind of message could cost more than tears.

*Little magpie.* Nobody else alive knew that name. Vann had carried letters in a dead man's handwriting, but no stranger could carry a dead man's childhood.

She sat back down and worked until midnight, and what emerged, sentence by sentence, was this:

Tomas had survived the evacuation night. He had been taken north, interrogated, imprisoned — the details were thin, thinned deliberately, because Tomas knew that even encoded letters could be seized, and he had written this one to survive capture. What he needed her to know was not his suffering. It was the ledger.

*You remember what Father carried out of the customs house that last week. You remember because you helped him carry it, though you were too young to understand and he was too frightened to explain. Half of it burned with Mother's things, or so everyone believes. The other half — the half with the names — did not burn. It could not burn, because I made sure of it, because I am my father's son and my sister's brother and I have never in my life destroyed a document that could hang a traitor.*

Ada's pencil stopped.

She read the passage again. Then a third time. Then she got up, walked to the washstand, splashed water on her face, and read it a fourth time, because the words refused to stay fixed, shifting meaning like the harbor light shifting color.

The ledger. Their father's ledger — the thing she had spent twenty years believing was ash, the thing whose destruction she had witnessed, or thought she had witnessed, on a night when the world was ending and a girl of nineteen was carrying more than any nineteen-year-old should carry. She remembered the burning. She remembered feeding pages into the stove in the safehouse while soldiers' boots sounded in the street below, remembering nothing else about those pages except that they were heavy and coded and that her mother had said *burn it all, burn everything, we are done paying for your father's honesty.*

But Tomas said half of it hadn't burned. Couldn't have. Because—

Because she had separated it. That was the thing rising in her memory now like a body coming up from deep water. On the last night, with the smoke alarm in her throat and her hands shaking, she had split the ledger. The operational half — routes, contacts, timetables, everything that could get living people killed — had gone into the fire. But the other half, the half her father had kept separate, the half that recorded not the network but the *betrayals* — payments received, favors granted, names sold — that half she had not burned. She had told herself she burned it. She had told herself for twenty years that it was ash, because the alternative was admitting that she had carried it out of a collapsing republic, across two borders, sewn into the lining of a coat she later gave away to a refugee family in a moment of desperate charity, and had never once opened it since.

She stood up so fast the chair went over backward.

The coat. The gray wool coat with the torn lining, the one she'd pressed on the Ohlsson woman at the border camp because the woman's children were shivering and Ada's guilt needed somewhere to go. She had given away a coat. Had she given away—

No. No, wait. Breathe. Think like a bookkeeper, Ada. Debits and credits. Assets and liabilities.

She had not given away the coat. She had given away *a* coat — but the ledger half, the names, she had transferred it. When? The second winter here, when she'd bought the new valise. She'd moved everything that mattered from the old luggage into the new, and the packet of heavy paper sewn in oilcloth — she had assumed, all these years, that it was her mother's letters, worthless, painful, kept out of duty — had gone into the false bottom of the sewing basket in the parlor. The sewing basket Marta used every week. The sewing basket that sat three meters from where Marta was currently knitting, guarding, unknowing, the most dangerous document in the port city.

Ada laughed. It came out of her like something breaking, half sob and half the wild hilarity of a woman discovering she has been sitting on a bomb for two decades while dusting around it.

* * *

Marta appeared in the doorway with a candle, hair down, face creased from sleep. "It's nearly one o'clock. If you're being murdered, do it quieter."

"Marta." Ada's voice sounded strange to her own ears. "Where is the sewing basket?"

"In the parlor, where it has lived since the Flood. Why?"

"And you use it. Weekly."

"I mend, yes. It's a sewing basket. That is generally what happens near it." Marta's eyes narrowed, which in Marta took approximately half a second and covered considerable ground. "What's in my sewing basket, Ada?"

"Nothing. Something. Possibly the reason Colonel Brecht's name has been following me around all week like a bad smell."

Marta went very still. Candle flames don't move, but Marta did — a single degree of stillness that Ada had learned to read the way sailors read cloud banks. "Say that name again."

"Brecht. The letter named him. Or will, once I finish decoding the last page." Ada looked at her aunt, really looked, and saw something she had never seen in forty years of knowing her: fear with nowhere to hide. "You know him."

"I knew *of* him," Marta said carefully, "which in the old days was the same as knowing him, if you wanted to stay alive." She set the candle down. "Open your letter, finish your arithmetic, and then come tell me everything, because I have been keeping my own ledger in this house, girl, and mine is older than yours."

She left. Ada stood in the wreckage of her room — chair on its side, pencil shavings, the Magpie Book open to the tide tables — and understood that the house she had built out of silences was about to undergo structural inspection.

But first. The last page.

She sat back down and decoded the final paragraph, and Tomas's voice came through the wall of years, tired and steady and unbearably young:

*So now you know what the remainder means. It is the half that names them — Brecht, and the others, all the others, every man who sold us by the kilo. I spent eight years in a cell deciding whether to forgive you for surviving, little magpie, and I managed it, mostly, so do not waste my forgiveness on guilt. Spend it on the truth. Bring the remainder to the harbor office, or send it, or burn it if you must — but know that whatever you choose, you chose it carrying it all along. You were never empty-handed. None of us ever were.*

Ada folded the plaintext sheet into quarters and held a match to it over the sink, watching her brother's words curl and brighten and go to gray flakes, the words themselves already printed somewhere behind her ribs where no fire could reach.

Then she went down to the parlor, quiet as a burglar in her own house, and lifted the lid of the sewing basket.

Under the buttons, under the spools, under twenty years of Marta's mending, the false bottom sat exactly where she had fitted it in a colder winter, in a younger life. She lifted it out with both hands, the way you lift something you suddenly realize has weight.

Heavy paper. Oilcloth. A packet the size of a psalm book.

She had been carrying it all along.
