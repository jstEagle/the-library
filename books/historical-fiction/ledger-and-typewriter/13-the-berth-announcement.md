## 13. The Berth Announcement

Nell Whitlock-Yarrow—she had added the Yarrow herself, at eleven, without asking anyone's permission, and nobody had ever taken it back off her—learned the news the way she learned most things: by standing where she wasn't needed and listening.

It was a Tuesday. Tuesdays were bread days, which meant Marta was in the kitchen with flour on her forearms and her temper set to simmer, and Aunt Ada was at the counting-house until six, and the radio sat on the parlor sideboard like a cat that only spoke when it felt like it. Nell was supposed to be doing her geography. She was, technically, doing her geography. The atlas was open to South America, which she had chosen because it was far from everything and shaped like a question mark, and because if you held the book flat you could see straight through the parlor doorway to the wireless without moving your eyes.

The music stopped. The announcer cleared his throat with the particular ceremony he saved for announcements that mattered, and Nell's pencil went still over Patagonia.

"...the Ministry of Transport has confirmed that the repatriation vessel *Corinthian* will make berth at this harbor on the first of the month, departing ten days thereafter. Passengers holding verified documentation of former citizenship may apply for passage at the harbor office beginning tomorrow morning. The Ministry wishes to remind applicants that berths are limited and allocation is not guaranteed..."

The announcer went on about weather and shipping lanes and a festival in some town Nell would never see, but she had stopped hearing him. She was already doing the arithmetic. It was what she did instead of crying, though she hadn't figured out yet that it was the same thing.

One ship. Ten days. Berths limited.

Three people in this house who had come from *there*.

She closed the atlas on South America, which had done nothing wrong, and went to the kitchen.

* * *

"You'll burn the bread," Nell said, "if you keep stabbing it like that."

"I am not stabbing it. I am testing it for doneness." Marta stabbed it again. The loaf gave up with a sigh of steam. "And you are not wanted in this kitchen."

"I'm never wanted in this kitchen. I'm always in this kitchen." Nell hopped onto the stool by the icebox, the one whose wobbly leg she'd shimmed with a folded playing card two winters ago, so that sitting on it was technically an act of maintenance. "Marta. Did you hear the wireless?"

Marta's knife paused over the butter.

"The *Corinthian*," Nell said. "Repatriation. Ten days. Verified former citizens."

The kitchen got very quiet, the way kitchens do when they are being asked to hold something too big for them. Outside, a cart rattled past on the cobbles, and someone was arguing cheerfully with a horse, and the whole ordinary world went on as if it hadn't just been rearranged.

"Radio sets say many things," Marta said at last, and resumed buttering, but her hands had changed. Nell knew hands. She had been reading hands since she was four years old, when she'd worked out that grown-ups' faces lied fluently but their hands were terrible at it. Marta's hands said: *I heard. I have known since before you did, or I feared it, and now it has teeth.*

"Ada qualifies," Nell said. "Obviously. And you."

"I do not obviously anything," Marta said. "Sit properly. You'll fall off that stool one day and I shall be expected to feel something about it."

"Marta." Nell leaned forward, elbows on the scrubbed table, which was another crime. "Ten days. This isn't a thing we can leave in the icebox."

"And what would you know of iceboxes and what goes in them?" But Marta said it softly, and she put the heel of the fresh loaf in front of Nell, which was how Marta said *I love you* when she thought no one was translating.

Nell tore the bread and thought about the arithmetic while she chewed. It was a household habit. Everything in this house eventually came down to numbers: how much flour, how much rent, how many years since, how many names never spoken aloud. She'd grown up inside sums she wasn't given all the figures for.

Ada. Born there, fled from there, papers verified twice over by the consulate—Nell had seen the file once when Ada was drunk on cough syrup and couldn't stop her. Marta. Also born there, also fled, though her file, Nell suspected, was thinner and stranger. And Elias Vann, who claimed to be from everywhere and therefore nowhere, and whose papers Nell had looked at very carefully indeed while tidying the hall table, thank you very much, and which had struck her even at fourteen-going-on-sharp as documents that had been *handled*. Not forged exactly. Forged things were confident. His were nervous.

Four people. Three possible claims. One ship.

And somewhere underneath all of it, the packet under the sewing basket false bottom that Nell was absolutely not supposed to know about, and which she thought about approximately every forty minutes including in church.

The front door opened at half past five instead of six. That alone told Nell more than the radio had.

* * *

Ada came into the kitchen still wearing her coat, which she never did, carrying a newspaper she didn't read, which she also never did, and stood in the doorway looking at the two of them as if checking they were real.

"You heard," she said.

"We heard," Marta said.

"The *Corinthian*." Ada took her coat off finally, folding it over her arm with those precise bookkeeper movements, each crease defeated individually. "Verified citizens may apply. There will be interviews. Documentation checks. Medical inspection, probably." She said it all evenly, like reading out a ledger column, and then she sat down at her own chair at her own table in her own kitchen and put her face briefly, briefly, into her hands—and lifted it again so fast that anyone less trained than Nell would have missed it entirely.

Nobody said *home*. Nobody ever said it. It hung around the room anyway, fat and impossible, like weather.

"There'll be three berths at most," Ada said. "Possibly fewer. They'll want proof for each applicant. Witnesses, records, the whole machinery." A pause, thin as paper. "Elias should hear this too."

"He's walking Nell's route home from the library these days," Marta said dryly. "He calls it exercise. I call it a man learning our streets a little too well."

"He's kind to me," Nell said.

"He is kind to everyone," Marta said. "That is precisely what worries me. Kindness is cheap. Ask any shopkeeper."

But she got a fourth cup down from the shelf anyway.

* * *

Elias arrived twenty minutes later, breathless, hatless, with the particular brightness of a man carrying news he thinks is his to give. He burst through the door saying, "You've heard? The wireless—the *Corinthian*—" and stopped dead when he saw their faces.

"We heard at noon," Nell said sweetly. "You're four hours behind the times, Mr. Vann. In this house that's practically a week."

"Nell," Ada said, in the voice that meant *enough*, and Nell subsided, mostly.

Elias sat down heavily, accepted the tea, wrapped both hands around the cup like a man warming himself at something smaller than a fire. Up close, Nell noticed—he always noticed—his charm had seams in it today. His eyes kept going to Ada and away again, the way you look at a door you're not sure is locked.

"Ten days," he said. "It could be the last ship for years. Possibly forever, the way things are sliding everywhere else." He laughed, a small bad laugh. "Listen to me. I sound like a timetable."

"How many berths?" Marta asked, blunt as a hammer.

"They're saying twelve hundred aboard, and applications in the tens of thousands across the whole coast." He turned the cup. "For this harbor, perhaps sixty. Perhaps forty. Verified former citizens get priority, but verification—" he made a gesture like smoothing a page flat, "—is its own country, with its own borders."

Silence again. Nell counted it: everyone in this room was doing the same sum and nobody would show their working.

Then Ada said, quietly, "We should be clear about what we're hoping for," and Nell watched the arithmetic come out into the light at last, ugly and glowing, like a coin pried up from under floorboards.

"I qualify," Ada said. "On paper, cleanly. Marta—"

"My papers are complicated," Marta said, in a tone that ended that sentence's life immediately.

"Elias—"

"Mine are *creative*," Elias said, with a crooked smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes, and Nell filed that word away in the drawer where she kept things like *handled* and *nervous documents* and *he flinched when the knock came yesterday and pretended it was his knee*.

"And me?" Nell asked.

The silence that followed had a texture. It was the silence of adults who had all, independently, decided something about her without telling her, and had now been caught in the same room with the decision.

"I was born here," Nell said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "This city. This street. That bed upstairs with the spring that gets you in the hip." She made herself keep going. "I don't have papers from *there*. I'm not from there. So when you all sit here doing your sums—" she looked around the table, at Ada's careful blankness, Marta's stony face, Elias studying his tea like it might confess first, "—I'm the remainder. Aren't I. The number left over."

"Nell—" Ada started.

"It's fine," Nell said, which was the biggest lie she had ever told in that kitchen, and she had told some beauties, including a whole fortnight of pretending she hadn't broken the blue vase. "It's arithmetic. I understand arithmetic."

She got off the stool—the good stool, the fixed stool, and even her betrayal of it felt symbolic somehow—and went upstairs to do her geography, and did not cry until she was safely behind her door with South America open in front of her, all that empty space, all that ocean between one shore and another.

* * *

Except she couldn't stay behind the door. Staying behind doors had never once, in her entire life, been among her talents.

So at nine o'clock, when the house had gone to its creaks and murmurs, Nell sat on the top stair in the dark, where the whole ground floor performed itself upward through the banister like a play staged for an audience of one, and listened.

They were in the parlor. The wireless was off. She could picture it exactly: Marta in the good chair, Ada upright on the settee with her mending she never actually mended, Elias standing near the window because he was one of those people who couldn't talk sitting down.

"—should go regardless," Elias was saying. "Both of you. Whatever the berth count, you apply. If it comes to choosing—"

"If it comes to choosing," Marta said, "it will come to shouting, and I intend to win."

"Marta—"

"Don't *Marta* me, translator. Twenty years I have cooked in that woman's kitchen and buried that family's dead and raised their foundling, and I will tell you what choosing looks like. Choosing looks like a night in November with rain coming sideways and a boat that holds thirty where forty stood. I have already done my choosing. It is done. It stays done." A pause, and when she went on her voice had dropped into something older. "What I will not do is stand in a queue at a harbor office and let some clerk decide which of us is a person. I have been a line item before."

Nell pressed her forehead against the banister post.

"Then we find another way," Elias said. "There are always other ways. Fixers, brokers—"

"Brokers cost money we don't have and buy trouble we can't afford."

"Then the queue," Elias said. "The honest queue. God help us."

A longer quiet. Then Ada, very low, so low that Nell had to hold her breath to catch it:

"There's something I need to tell you both. About the application. About what verification means, for someone like me."

The stair was cold. Nell's legs were numb. She stayed anyway, because whatever Ada was about to say, she could hear in the shape of the silence before it—that it was heavy, that it had been carried a long way, that it was connected to oilcloth packets and burned corners and a brother named Tomas whom nobody ever explained and everybody always almost mentioned.

But downstairs, footsteps crossed to the parlor door, checked the latch, and drew it shut, and the rest of the conversation folded itself away behind wood and wallpaper, addressed to an audience that did not include a girl on the stairs.

Nell sat in the dark a while longer, doing sums.

One ship. Ten days. Forty berths, maybe sixty. Two clean claims, one complicated, one creative, one remainder. One packet under a false bottom that weighed the same as a psalm book and had started showing up in her dreams, opened, pages fluttering, names flying out of it like startled birds.

And one new figure entering the column, small but stubborn, underlined twice: whatever was in that packet, whatever Ada had carried out of a burning country twenty years ago—it was going to matter now. Because nothing in that house had ever mattered *this* much unless it was dangerous, and nothing had ever been hidden that well unless somebody, someday, would come asking.

Downstairs, the parlor door opened again. Voices, softer now, dispersing. Then—footsteps on the stairs themselves, and Nell flattened against the wall with her heart going like a telegraph key as Elias Vann climbed past her in the dark toward the spare room, close enough that she could smell pipe smoke and cheap soap and fear, actual fear, worn smooth from long use like a coin rubbed faceless.

He paused on the landing. Looked back down the dark stairs. And Nell, who had survived fourteen years in a house built of silences by knowing exactly when to be furniture, did not breathe until his door clicked shut.

Ten days, she thought. Forty berths. Four of us, plus one dead man who apparently wasn't, plus one packet full of psalms that weren't.

She went to bed and lay awake doing the only sum that mattered, over and over, getting a different answer every time:

*Who gets to leave, and what does it cost the ones who stay?*

By midnight she had filled three pages of her school notebook with figures that weren't numbers at all. By one o'clock she had torn them out and burned them in the candle flame, watching the curl of the paper, thinking: *that's what Ada does. That's what they all do.*

By two o'clock she had made herself a promise instead, which was harder to burn: before this ship sailed, Nell Whitlock-Yarrow was going to find out what was in that sewing basket—and who else in the world already knew.
