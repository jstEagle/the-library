## 14. Vann's Debt Comes Due

The trouble with being a charming man, Elias Vann had long ago concluded, was that charm worked on everyone except the people you owed money to.

He was thinking this while pretending to read a newspaper outside the fish market, a newspaper he had bought for the express purpose of not looking like a man waiting for someone. The fish market was the one place in the port city where nobody asked questions, because everybody there had something to hide — usually the true weight of a herring. He'd chosen it for the meeting precisely because it was loud, crowded, and smelled bad enough that no conversation could be overheard without heroic effort.

It had not occurred to him until that morning, standing in front of his cracked mirror tying his tie for the third time, that he might be the herring.

They found him anyway. Of course they did. Brecht's men always found you; it was practically their national sport.

"Vann." The voice came from behind his left shoulder, which was rude of it. "You're blocking the view."

Elias folded his newspaper with great care, as though it contained state secrets rather than an article about drainage improvements, and turned.

There were two of them. There were always two of them; Brecht apparently believed in symmetry the way other men believed in God. The tall one — Kessler, though Elias only knew him as *the tall one* — had a face like a closed ledger. The short one, whose name Elias had never learned and did not want to learn, because names made things real, stood slightly forward in the manner of a man who enjoyed his work.

"Gentlemen," Elias said, and managed to make it sound almost pleased. Twenty years of translation work taught a man to say *gentlemen* to anyone. He'd once said it to a customs officer mid-arrest. "What a coincidence. I was just—"

"No, you weren't," said the short one.

"All right, I wasn't. But I was about to enjoy myself, and you've arrived just in time to spoil it. That's practically punctual."

The tall one didn't smile. The short one smiled too much. Between them, they achieved a kind of average human expression that was somehow worse than either extreme.

"Walk," said the short one, nodding toward the mouth of the market street.

"I was rather hoping to buy some mackerel."

"You can buy mackerel walking."

"It's very difficult to inspect mackerel while walking. One ends up with inferior mackerel. It's the whole tragedy of the modern age, if you ask me."

Nobody asked him. They fell into step on either side of him, and the three of them moved through the market like a small unpleasant parade. A woman selling eels looked at Elias with something between sympathy and contempt, and he tipped his hat to her, because if a man was going to be marched through a fish market by thugs, he might as well do it beautifully.

* * *

They took him not to an office but to the back room of a shipping agency, which told Elias everything: Brecht didn't consider him important enough for an office. He was cargo. Cargo got warehouses; correspondence got offices. The room contained a desk, two chairs, a calendar from 1935 that nobody had bothered to update, and a window with a view of a wall.

The short one shut the door. The tall one leaned against it, which was either casual or strategic, and with these men it was never possible to know which, which was the entire art of it.

"So," Elias said, taking the chair before he was offered it. If they were going to interrogate him, he would at least sit like a guest. "To what do I owe the pleasure? And please don't say pleasure unless you mean it. My feelings bruise easily."

The short one sat across from him. He produced a folder — a thin one, mercifully, though Elias had learned that thin folders could hold more ruin than thick ones, since ruin compressed remarkably well — and laid it on the desk without opening it.

"Do you know what I like about you, Vann?"

"My cologne?"

"Your mouth." The short one leaned back. "You talk like a man who has nothing to lose. It's very restful. Most people we talk to, their hands shake, their eyes go everywhere. You just... chat. Like we're old friends at a funeral."

"We may yet be," Elias said. "Whose funeral is still under negotiation."

Something flickered across the short one's face that was not quite annoyance. Respect, maybe, insofar as men like him were capable of it, which was to say the way a dog respects a snake.

The tall one spoke from the door, his first words since the market. "Open the folder."

The short one opened the folder. Inside were papers. Elias knew them instantly, the way a man knows his own handwriting, because they *were* his own handwriting, or close enough — three documents, each one a small masterpiece, each one forged by a forger in Trieste whose name Elias had sworn on several saints never to reveal, and whose prices had gone up accordingly since 1938.

His travel papers. The ones that said he was a citizen of a country that no longer existed, issued by a consulate that had burned down, stamped with a seal he had personally watched being carved in a basement workshop while drinking wine of frankly criminal quality.

"Ah," said Elias.

"Yes," said the short one. "*Ah*."

"In my defense," Elias began, "those are extremely good forgeries."

"That's the point."

"Well. Yes. I suppose it is."

He sat with it a moment. There was no version of denial that survived those papers. He'd known this day would come from the moment he'd signed on with Brecht's intermediary — a soft-spoken man in Vienna who had explained the arrangement with the gentle clarity of a banker explaining interest. *You deliver the woman and her ledger. In return, your past stays where it belongs: nowhere.*

At the time, in that Viennese café, it had seemed almost reasonable. That was the trick of debts; they always seemed reasonable when you were hungry. Nobody signs away their future on a full stomach.

"Here's what happens now," the short one said, in the tone of a man reading train times. "The colonel wants the Yarrow woman. Not hurt — he was very clear about that, which should tell you how much she's worth. She comes to him, she brings the remainder of whatever she's carrying, and she does it believing it's her own idea. Your letters opened the door. Good work, by the way. We weren't sure a dead brother's handwriting would do it."

"She's grieving," Elias said, and heard his own voice come out flatter than he intended. "Twenty years she's carried it. You sent me in with her dead brother's hand and called it good work."

"I did call it good work. Because it is." The short one tapped the folder. "And here's where the good work finishes. Ten days. There's a ship coming — you'll have heard, the whole city's heard, every former citizen in the district is doing arithmetic at their kitchen tables. The colonel will have certain berths on that ship. The woman will want them. She'll trade. Everyone trades, eventually. All you have to do is keep her pointed at the harbor office and make sure the ledger arrives with her."

"And if I don't?"

The tall one answered from the door, and his voice had the terrible quality of a man who had rehearsed nothing because he needed to rehearse nothing. "Then the papers go to the port authority. Forged documents, attempted entry under a false nationality. That's a cell, Vann, pending deportation. And deportation means going back to—" He paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses over the worst part of the chart. "Well. You know where. You've spent twenty years making sure you don't go back there. We checked. You've been very thorough. You've been *admirable*, honestly. It made finding the pressure easy."

Elias looked out the window at the wall. The wall had a crack in it shaped, if you squinted, like a coastline — some drowned country's outline. He thought: *I have been drawing that coastline on walls my whole life.*

"There's a third option," he said.

"There really isn't."

"There's always a third option. It's the first rule of translation. Every sentence has a meaning the author didn't intend, and your job is to find it." He smiled at them both, and it cost him more than they would ever know, which was the point of smiles. "For instance: the woman could simply refuse. Have you considered that? She's kept secrets from armies for twenty years. What exactly do you imagine I'm going to do — push her?"

"You're going to do what paid men do," the short one said. "You're going to earn."

"And what am I earning, precisely? Beyond the continued existence of Elias Vann? Because I'll tell you, gentlemen, as salaries go, *not being destroyed* is generous the first month and wears thin after that."

"You're earning passage," the tall one said quietly.

That landed. That was the shot placed exactly where the armor wasn't. Passage. A berth. A country, even a borrowed one, even one reached by a ship he'd have to share with the memory of everything he'd done to get aboard. Elias felt the wanting rise in him like water in a lock, steady and mechanical, and hated himself for it with a fluency that had also become, over the years, nearly effortless.

"How much does the colonel know about her?" Elias asked, because deflection was a muscle and he kept it exercised. "The household. The aunt. The girl."

"The girl?" The short one's eyebrows went up, mildly interested. "There's a girl?"

"There's a ward. Sixteen. Sharp as broken glass. If any of your people go near that house with their faces on, she'll have your descriptions written down in a notebook inside ten minutes, with spelling corrections."

"Noted." The short one closed the folder and slid it across the desk toward Elias, who looked at his own forged life lying flat on the wood and did not touch it. "Keep those. Consider them a reminder. Ten days, Vann. The woman comes to the harbor office on her own feet, believing her own thoughts, carrying her own inheritance. After that, you're a free man. Well." A thin smile. "A man, anyway."

* * *

They let him walk out alone, which was its own kind of message: *we're not worried about you running.* And they were right not to be, which was the humiliating part. Where would he run? His profession was languages and his capital was a name that belonged to no flag. Men like Elias Vann did not flee; they merely relocated their compromises.

He walked for an hour. He walked past the tram depot, past the shuttered café where he'd taken coffee his first week in the city and pretended to read a paper in a language he hadn't learned yet, past the secondhand bookshop where the owner let him loiter on rainy afternoons because he actually bought things. He walked until his feet ached in the specific way that meant his brain was working, because his brain and his feet had always shared a lease.

The arithmetic was simple and it was monstrous.

Ten days. Deliver Ada Yarrow — grieving Ada, guarded Ada, Ada who fed him soup on his second night in her house and pretended it was leftover, Ada who had looked at her brother's handwriting and made a sound so small he'd pretended not to hear it out of mercy — deliver her to the man who had sold her family's escape route and filled the graves she'd spent twenty years mourning.

Or: lose everything. The papers, the name, the fragile scaffolding of a life he'd built out of other countries' scraps. The cell. The deportation. The place he did not think about, which meant he thought about it constantly, the way you don't think about an elephant by keeping it in every room.

*Everyone trades,* the short one had said, with the confidence of a man quoting scripture.

And the worst of it — the part Elias turned over and over as he walked, like a stone with a sharp edge he couldn't stop thumbing — was that the short one was probably right. Not about Ada trading. About everyone. About him. Hadn't he already traded? He'd taken the job in Vienna. He'd knocked on her door with letters like a bouquet of knives. He'd sat at Marta's table eating stew while Nell quizzed him about his fictional homeland with the thoroughness of a border guard, and he had lied to all of them, warmly, fluently, *charmingly*, and told himself the lie was smaller than the truth it protected.

Except the lie had grown. That was the thing nobody warned you about lies: they compounded. Daily. Silently. Like the interest in the banker's speech in Vienna.

He stopped at the harbor rail, because the city's streets all eventually spill you at the harbor whether you mean to go there or not, and looked at the water. Somewhere beyond the breakwater, in ten days, a ship would come. Three berths, maybe four, for people with verified histories — and his history verified to nothing, his nationality a work of fiction he'd typed himself, his whole self a document awaiting the right stamp.

Ada had a history. It was terrible, but it was real, and it was hers, and it was stitched into the bones of that strange, guarded household — the aunt with her radar-sharp silences, the girl with her notebook of suspicions, the brother who might be alive, the father whose shadow fell over all of it. Ada belonged somewhere, even in exile. Especially in exile.

And his job was to sell her belonging back to her, minus commission.

"Oh, Tomas," Elias said to the water, using the dead man's name the way you'd use a curse word if you were too polite to swear. "What did you write in those letters? Did you know what they'd be used for? Were you writing to save your sister or to spend her?"

The water said nothing. Water was excellent at saying nothing; it had had practice.

He turned up his collar and started back toward the house. There was no plan in his steps yet — or rather, there were four plans, rotating, none of them load-bearing, which is how his plans always began. But underneath the plans, in the place where a man keeps the thing he'll actually do when every clever option dies, something had begun to settle, quiet and heavy, like ballast shifting in a hull.

He had watched Ada examine those letters. He had seen her finger trace the childhood cipher — the little row of dots and dashes they'd invented as children, the code only siblings would know — and he had seen her face when understanding came, grief and recognition arriving in the same instant, twins.

She had trusted him with that moment. Unknowingly, unwillingly, but genuinely. And trust, Elias had discovered somewhere in the last few weeks without noticing the exact date, turns out to be the one currency a man can't forge.

The house lights were on when he reached the street. Through the parlor window he could see Marta's silhouette at the table and Nell's bent head over homework, and above them both the lamp glow that meant Ada was upstairs with the letters again, reading her brother back to life one line at a time.

Ten days, the short one had said.

Elias stood in the cold a moment longer, then went up the steps and rang the bell, wearing his best smile like a coat over a wound — and wondering, as the door opened, how much longer the coat would hold.

Inside, faintly, from upstairs, he could hear the typewriter. Ada was already typing something. And Elias Vann, professional knower-of-documents, felt the first cold drop of a suspicion he couldn't yet prove:

That whatever she was typing, it wasn't for him.
