## 15. Ada Confronts Vann

The forgery was good. That was what bothered her most.

Ada had spent three mornings telling herself she wasn't going to look at Elias Vann's papers again, and three evenings losing that argument with herself. She was a bookkeeper; the habit of checking figures twice ran through her like a grain through wood. A man arrives at your door carrying your dead brother's handwriting, and you check his papers the way you'd check an invoice. It wasn't suspicion. It was arithmetic.

Except it was suspicion, and the papers were wrong.

She had them now on the kitchen table under the good lamp, laid out flat the way Marta laid out linen for pressing: the travel permit, the transit card, the little stamped booklet of entry visas that had carried him across three borders and one sea to her doorstep. The paper stock was right. The ink had been aged convincingly — tea, most likely, or something cleverer. The stamps were excellent copies.

But the dates didn't reconcile.

Entry at the northern crossing: the ninth. Exit from the coastal district: the seventh. A man cannot leave a place two days before he enters it, unless he is very special indeed, and nothing about Elias Vann suggested special. He suggested tired. He suggested hungry. He suggested a man who had memorized a legend so thoroughly that he'd forgotten to make the legend obey the calendar.

Ada sat with her hands folded over the papers for a long moment, listening to the house. Upstairs, Nell's radio murmured dance music, turned down to the volume Nell believed was inaudible and which carried perfectly through the floorboards. Marta was at the market, arguing with a fishmonger about the definition of *fresh*, which could take upward of an hour. And somewhere in the front room, Elias Vann was reading one of Tomas's letters for perhaps the fortieth time, because she had seen him do it every evening since he arrived, holding the pages as though they might warm him.

She thought about how he'd said *your brother* that first night, standing dripping on her step, and how his voice had cracked on the possessive — not like a messenger's crack, but like a man handing someone else a parcel that weighed more than he'd been told.

She thought about the way he never ate until she did.

She thought about the dates.

"Mr. Vann," she called, not loudly. She never needed to call loudly; the house was small enough that a raised eyebrow carried.

His footsteps came down the hall at once, too promptly, the promptness itself a kind of confession. He appeared in the doorway with the letter still in his hand, folded into his breast pocket, and looked at the table, and looked at the papers laid out under the lamp, and Ada watched the exact moment his face performed honesty and then gave up the attempt.

"Ah," he said.

"Yes," said Ada. "Ah."

* * *

He sat where she pointed, which was the chair nearest the stove, the least comfortable one, chosen deliberately. He put his hands on his knees like a schoolboy summoned about a broken window. Upstairs the dance music changed records. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past toward the harbor, and Ada found herself cataloguing its sounds out of habit — wheels, four; load, heavy; direction, east — before she caught herself and brought her attention back to the man in front of her.

"I'm going to ask you questions," she said, "and I would prefer, this time, if you answered the question I actually asked rather than the one you wish I'd asked. You're very good at the second kind. I've watched you do it with Marta all week. She thinks you're charming."

"And you don't?"

"I think charm is a tool," said Ada, "and tools have purposes, and I'd like to know yours." She turned the transit card around so the dates faced him. "You entered the northern crossing on the ninth. You exited the coastal district on the seventh. Explain."

He looked at the card for a long moment. Then he did something unexpected: he laughed. Not much of a laugh — a short, defeated exhale through the nose — but real.

"The clerk," he said. "At the coastal office. He wrote it backwards. Day-month instead of month-day. I noticed when he handed it back, and I decided that noticing and correcting were two different activities, and only one of them would get me out of the room alive."

"So it's a clerical error."

"It's a clerical error sitting on top of approximately eleven other things that are not clerical errors," Vann admitted. "Yes. All right. Yes." He rubbed his face with both hands, and when they came down, the charm had drained out of him entirely, leaving something younger and considerably more frightened. "Ask me properly, Miss Yarrow. Ask me the big one. Because I've been waiting five days for you to ask it, and I confess my nerve is failing."

"Who paid you?"

"There it is," he said softly.

"Who paid you to bring those letters to my door?"

"No one paid me to bring the letters to your door." He held up a hand before she could speak. "That's true, and it matters, but it isn't the whole truth, and I promised myself — I actually stood in front of a mirror in a hotel in March and promised myself — that if you ever asked directly, I would not lie to you. So. The whole truth, as far as I can carry it."

Ada waited. The kettle ticked on the stove. Upstairs, Nell had given up pretending and turned the radio up two degrees.

"A courier died in a boarding house in Trieste," Vann said. "Heart, they said, though nobody checked, because checking costs money and dead couriers don't complain. I shared a wall with him for six weeks. His name — his real name, I think — was Anton. He talked too much when he drank, and he drank often enough that I knew he was carrying something, and when he died, the landlady went through his room looking for rent money, and I went through it looking for whatever made a man drink himself stupid in a town where he spoke no language and trusted no one."

"The letters."

"The letters. Wrapped in oilcloth inside the lining of a coat that was worth less than the oilcloth. And a set of instructions in a hand I didn't recognize, naming two recipients. One was a Colonel Brecht, care of a consulate I won't insult us both by describing. The other was a name I'd never heard: Yarrow. This address. Your address." He paused. "There was also money. Enough money that a man with no country, no papers worth the stamping, and no prospects might reasonably decide that delivering the second letter was worth the walk, and that the first letter could quietly find a drawer."

"You took the payment."

"I took the payment," Vann said steadily. "I'm not going to dress it as anything nobler than it was. Someone — I never learned who, and I've stopped pretending I'll sleep better once I do — wanted those letters delivered, and was willing to fund the delivery, and I was the nearest empty-handed man. I delivered yours. As for Brecht's—" He stopped.

Ada felt the cold arrive in her chest ahead of the understanding, the way winter comes through a door left open in another room. "Where is Brecht's letter, Mr. Vann?"

"Burned. In Trieste, the same night. I read it first." His jaw worked. "I read it because I told myself a translator reads everything, it's practically a professional obligation, and then I burned it because I couldn't make myself deliver it. It was instructions, Miss Yarrow. Confirmation of receipt, a schedule, and a request for verification of — of assets. I didn't know then what assets meant. I know now. I've seen the man. I sat forty feet from him at the consulate and watched him count the room the way a butcher counts stock."

"So you are a courier."

"I am a courier," he said. "Hired to move paper. Not hired to hunt. Whatever Brecht believes I am, whoever sent me believes I'm a delivery service with legs, and the difference between those two things is currently the entire distance between me and a shallow grave, and I am aware it is not a large distance."

The kettle began, lowly, to sing. Ada rose, moved it off the heat without pouring, and stood a moment with her back to him, hands flat on the counter, letting the warmth of the iron come up through her palms. Twenty years of practice said: *say nothing, learn everything, file it, burn it.* Twenty years of practice had kept Nell fed and Marta unarrested and her own name small enough to hide behind a ledger column.

But twenty years ago she had also stood in a harbor office and let a lie pass her lips to save a life, and she had been paying interest on that lie ever since, compounding quarterly.

"Look at me," she said, and turned around.

He looked. He had kind eyes; she'd noted it the first night and distrusted it immediately, kindness being the most commonly counterfeited currency in the world. But counterfeit or not, it was there now, along with fear, along with — and this was the part that undid her composure somewhat — guilt worn openly, like a shirt buttoned wrong on purpose.

"You lied to me in my own kitchen," she said. "Five days. Under my roof. Eating Marta's stew, which she makes for family and lodgers and no one else, and letting Nell follow you around the garden asking questions you deflected beautifully, and reading my brother's letters every evening like a man saying prayers. And all of it built on a story we could have tested in ten seconds with a calendar."

"Yes."

"Why tell me now? I asked about the dates. I asked about the payment. I haven't yet asked the question underneath, and you know there is one underneath, because you just told me yourself there's a question underneath. Why hand me the rope?"

Vann was quiet for a while. From upstairs, faintly, Nell's record ended and the needle hissed in the runout groove, a sound like distant surf, until Nell's footsteps crossed to lift it.

"Because of how you checked the dates," he said finally.

"What does that mean?"

"It means that a woman who catches a two-day discrepancy in a forged transit card is a woman who has been checking documents her whole life, and I don't mean professionally. I mean that somewhere, long ago, somebody taught you that paper lies and you mustn't, and you've kept that rule ever since even when keeping it cost you." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for once he did not look away. "I've spent five days watching you decide whether to trust me, and I realized this morning — watching you pretend not to watch me at breakfast — that you were never deciding whether I was dangerous. You'd already priced that in. You were deciding whether I was worth breaking your rule for. Whether to let one more person past the wall. And I thought — well. Either I'm honest with her now, or I prove I was never worth it. Those were the options. I picked the one that keeps me able to look at myself in mirrors, which, as habits go, is a recent acquisition, but I recommend it."

Ada discovered that her throat had gone tight. She disapproved of this intensely.

"You should understand what you've done," she said, when she could trust her voice. "By burning that letter. By coming here. You've walked into the middle of something that ended twenty years ago for everyone except the people it never ended for. Brecht doesn't send couriers after old letters out of sentiment. If he wants what came with mine, then what came with mine is dangerous to him, and anything dangerous to him is dangerous to everyone standing within arm's reach of it. That's Nell. That's Marta. That's you, Mr. Vann, most especially you, because you're the loose thread he can see."

"I know."

"Do you?" She crossed back to the table and stood over him, and heard her own voice drop into the register she used for junior clerks who fudged columns. "A man followed you home last Thursday. Two streets behind, gray hat, changed sides at the corner by the bakery. You pretended not to see him. Very smooth. But I saw, because I've been followed before, and I know what it looks like when someone pretends not to notice, and I will tell you what I concluded: I concluded that either you didn't see him, which means you're careless, or you saw him and led him here, which means you're worse than careless. Which was it?"

Something flickered across his face — surprise first, then a rueful respect that she suspected he showed few people.

"Saw him," he said. "Led him past here twice, on the far side, walking, not stopping, before I doubled back through the arcade. He lost me at the tram terminus. I checked."

"He'll be back."

"He'll be back."

"And when he comes back, he won't lose you, because next time you won't be alone, because I have a household full of people who will insist on being near you precisely because they're fond of you, and fondness is a tactical liability." Ada pressed her palms flat against the table and found, to her mild astonishment, that they were trembling slightly, and that beneath the anger in her chest — real anger, justified anger — there was something warmer and considerably more inconvenient, which was that he had told the truth when lying would have been easier, and that no one had done that in her presence in twenty years. "Do you understand what you've done to us? We were quiet. We were *quiet*, Mr. Vann. Twenty years of quiet. And you knocked on the door with my brother's handwriting in your pocket and a colonel's errand half-burned behind you, and now there's a man in a gray hat learning our street, and the quiet is over, and I want you to say you understand that, out loud, in words, because I need to hear whether you comprehend the size of it."

"I comprehend it," Vann said quietly. "I've comprehended it since the consulate. When I saw Brecht's face, I understood exactly what I'd carried to your door, and I lay awake that night composing the speech in which I confessed everything and offered to leave, and I got as far as your hallway every morning and lost my nerve, because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because the letters are all he has, Miss Yarrow. Tomas Yarrow is all he has. Whoever Anton was carrying them for, whatever chain they came down — those pages are twenty years of a man writing to a sister he believed was alive, and I was not going to be the second person to fail him. Anton failed him already, dying in Trieste with them in his coat. I could not. So yes, I lied about the money, and I lied about the instructions, and I let you believe I was a wandering translator with a sentimental streak, and every day I let it stand I liked myself less, and today you caught the dates, and thank God for the dates, because I was running out of ways to be a coward gracefully."

The silence afterward was long. The kettle, neglected, had gone quiet. Upstairs, Nell had put on the record again — the same one, always the same one — and the dance music filtered down through the joists like weather.

Ada looked at this man who had lied to her for five days and confessed in nine minutes, and she thought about her brother, whose handwriting she knew better than her own, who had pressed a code into her palm on a dock twenty years ago and said *memorize it and burn it, Addie, paper can be taken from you but memory can't,* and who had then walked up a gangplank and out of the world.

And she thought about what a man must feel, carrying such letters, reading them nightly, knowing the sender believed the recipient might never come.

"Sit down," she said, realizing she had risen. "Properly. Not the penitent posture. Sit like a guest, because in about a minute I'm going to do something foolish and I'd rather do it sitting down."

Warily, he resettled himself.

"I'm angry with you," Ada said. "I want that on the record, filed, dated, and initialed. I am angry for at least a week, possibly a fortnight, and Marta will be angrier, and Marta's anger has a longer half-life than radium. But." She exhaled. "But you burned the letter. You could have collected double — one payment from the sender, one from Brecht — and instead you chose the side that pays nothing. That's not nothing, Mr. Vann. In my experience it's the rarest entry in any ledger. So. You'll stay. You'll tell Marta yourself, tonight, before supper, because I refuse to let her discover it from Nell, who has known since Tuesday, by the way, and has been enjoying the secret enormously—"

"She has *not*," said Vann, stricken.

"—she absolutely has, she told me at breakfast Wednesday with the smugness of a cat presenting a bird, and I let her enjoy it because I was still deciding about you. Well. I've decided." She gathered the forged papers, squared their edges out of pure habit, and held them a moment. "These stay in the tin with my mother's deeds. Not because I trust you — give it time — but because a household shouldn't keep its worst documents scattered about where Nell can alphabetize them."

He almost smiled. Then his gaze dropped to the table, to the space where the papers had been, and something in his expression shifted — the look of a man arriving at a door he'd been circling for days.

"Miss Yarrow," he said. "Since we're doing truth. There's something in the letters I haven't shown you. Something I found on the third reading, in the margins, in the childhood cipher you told me about — the one only the two of you knew. It isn't more code." His voice had gone careful, the way voices go around fragile cargo. "It's dates. Places. It's — Miss Yarrow, Tomas didn't write these letters from wherever he died. He wrote the last one fourteen months ago. And in the margin of the third page, in your cipher, there's a line I've translated six times hoping I mistranslated it."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a ship leans before it answers its helm.

"He isn't where I told you he was," Vann said. "Ada — I think your brother is alive. And I think I know where they're keeping him."
