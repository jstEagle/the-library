## 16. Tomas's Last Night, Retold

Vann had told lies in six languages, and he had a theory about them: every lie had a weight, and you could carry only so many before your spine began to bow. He'd been carrying his since the day he took Brecht's money, and now, sitting at Ada Yarrow's kitchen table with her brother's letters spread between them like cards in a game nobody wanted to win, he felt the whole stack shift.

She had made tea. Of course she had. There was something about Ada that turned crisis into ceremony—she dealt with catastrophe the way other people dealt with weather, by putting a kettle on and waiting for it to pass or not pass. The cups were mismatched. One had a chip in the rim shaped, if you squinted, like a small bird in flight. She gave him the chipped one without comment, which he chose to interpret as either trust or contempt, and with Ada he was learning there was often very little daylight between the two.

"Read them," she said. "All of them. In order."

"You've already—"

"I've read what I can bear to read." Her voice was level, but her hands were folded on the table in the particular way of a woman holding herself down. "I want them read *to* me. Out loud. If they're going to be true, I want them in the air."

So Vann picked up the first letter, and the dead brother breathed.

---

The letters were written in pencil, on paper that had been folded and refolded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. Tomas Yarrow's handwriting was slanting and urgent, the handwriting of a man writing fast because he did not trust tomorrow. Vann cleared his throat, felt absurd, and began.

*"Ada. If this reaches you, then Kessler kept his promise after all, which will astonish both of us, as I have never known him to keep one."*

A sound came out of Ada—not quite a laugh, not quite anything else—and Vann understood he had just witnessed twenty years of grief change shape.

*"I am alive,"* the letter continued. *"Write that part twice. I am alive. I know what they told you. I know what the lists said. The lists were wrong, or rather, the lists were convenient, which in my experience is the same thing."*

Vann read on. The letter laid out the night of the evacuation from the other side—the side Ada had never seen. Tomas had made it to the harbor. He had seen the boat. He had even, for one impossible moment, seen Ada on the gangway, and had shouted her name into wind that swallowed it whole.

"He saw me?" Ada whispered. "He was *there?*"

"The third letter explains," Vann said gently. "May I?"

She nodded. She had gone very still, the stillness of a woman standing at the edge of something deep.

---

The second letter was shorter, and angrier. Tomas wrote of being taken at the docks—not caught, *taken*, someone had pointed him out, someone who knew his face—and of the months afterward in a cell where the ceiling had a crack shaped like a river. He wrote of learning, piece by piece, who had sold the route. But here the letter broke off mid-sentence, and the next page was missing, torn away.

"Somebody removed it," Ada said flatly.

"Or Tomas did himself," Vann admitted. "There are burn marks on the courier's folder. Whoever carried these wanted certain pages to survive transit, and others not to."

"Convenient," she echoed, and gave him a look that suggested she was filing the word away for later use against him personally.

But it was the last letter—the last night—that undid them both.

Tomas had written it, according to its own dating, on the eve of a transfer. He did not say where. He wrote instead about small things, the way men do when the large things would crack the page open. He wrote about the bread in the prison, which was terrible, and how he had learned to toast it against the pipe near his bunk, and how this small act of engineering had made him briefly popular among the other prisoners. He wrote about a guard named Piotr who pretended not to see contraband letters pass through his hands and who once, memorably, had whistled an opera tune badly enough to constitute its own crime.

And then, near the end, the handwriting slowed. You could feel the pen pressing harder, the way it does when a man stops performing courage and starts telling truth.

*"Ada, I need to tell you what I never told you, because if these letters die somewhere in a courier's coat, then let them at least die honest. The code—the ledger code—we didn't invent it. Father helped us build it. He thought we were making a game for children. He gave us the substitution wheels as a puzzle one winter, and we built our secret language on top of them, and neither of us ever asked ourselves why a customs clerk of his modest imagination happened to design cipher wheels at all.*

*"I've had two years in a cell to think about that winter. I think Father knew what was coming. I think he built us the tools to survive it, and I think he also built the tools that were used against us, and I don't know yet which hand held which. When you find the remainder—and you'll know it when you hold it, because it's heavier than paper has any right to be—look at the third layer. Not the trade codes. Not the route marks. The layer underneath. That's where the names live.*

*"Don't hate him until you've read all of it. And then, if you must hate him, hate him accurately."*

Vann stopped reading. The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked—Nell, listening, obviously—and the kettle ticked as it cooled.

"'Hate him accurately,'" Ada repeated. Her voice had a strange, careful quality, like glass under strain. "That is exactly the sort of thing he would say. He once told me grief should be itemized. *Itemized,* Elias. Like inventory."

"He sounds—" Vann searched for it. "He sounds like a man who learned to joke in self-defense."

"He sounds alive." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Twenty years, Elias. Twenty years I lit a candle for him every year on the anniversary, and Marta always said it was morbid, and I said no, it's bookkeeping, it's balancing an account that hasn't closed. And the whole time the account wasn't closed at all. He was *alive*. He was eating terrible bread and whistling bad opera and writing to me, believing—" her breath caught, "—believing the letters would arrive."

"They arrived late."

"They arrived via *you.*" She lowered her hand and looked at him, and Vann felt the full weight of every lie in his inventory settle onto his spine at once. "Via a stranger with forged papers, paid by whom, sent by whom, arriving precisely when—"

"Ada."

"No. No, you're right, this isn't the hour for it." She stood, walked to the window, stood with her back to him watching the street below, where ordinary people were doing ordinary things in the last of the afternoon light. "Tell me the rest. What does the letter say at the end? The actual end."

Vann looked down at the final lines. He had read them a dozen times on the journey here, on trains, in doorways, in the grey hours of ferries, and they had not gotten easier.

*"They're moving me tomorrow. I don't know where. But I know this much: they haven't killed me, and men aren't kept alive this long unless they're worth more breathing than dead. Which means someone still wants what's in my head. So I'll do what we always did, little sister. I'll keep the account open. If you can reach me, reach me. If you can't—then burn nothing. Keep everything. The truth keeps better than we do.*

*"Your brother, T."*

Silence. Then Ada said, without turning around, "He called me little sister. I'm eleven months younger. He never let me forget it. He used to say eleven months was the exact difference between wisdom and stubbornness, and he knew which one I'd been allotted."

"What did you tell him?"

"That he'd mixed up which of us got which." She turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed but dry, and behind the grief something new had kindled—a hard bright thing, banked like a stove fire. "Elias. They're keeping him alive because of the ledger. Because of what's in his head. Which means he's still leverage, still currency, still—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening.

"Still an entry in someone's books," Vann finished quietly.

"Yes." She came back to the table and gathered the letters with both hands, smoothing each page before stacking it, the gesture of a woman putting papers in order because she could not put the world in order. "Which means whoever holds him will want the remainder. And the remainder—"

She stopped. Vann watched her stop, watched the thought arrive and then get locked behind her eyes like a drawer sliding shut.

"—the remainder is a conversation for another day," she said.

"There you go again," he said, before he could stop himself. "Closing accounts early."

Something flickered across her face—anger, maybe, or gratitude, or the place where those two things share a border. "You've known me four days."

"I've read your brother's letters. I feel I've known the family considerably longer." He meant it lightly. It landed heavily, and he watched it land, and for once he didn't try to take it back.

Ada set the letters down. For a long moment she studied him with that bookkeeper's gaze of hers, the one that audited everything it touched, and Vann braced for the question he deserved—*who paid you, Elias, and what exactly were you hired to deliver?*

Instead she said: "Tomorrow. After breakfast. We'll go through the third letter line by line. There are marginal marks I want to check against the old cipher. And Elias—" she paused at the doorway, one hand on the frame, "—the candle I light every year. I'm going to keep lighting it. But starting this year, it's a birthday candle, not a memorial one. He'd find that funny. He'd find it unbearably sentimental and he'd laugh at me for a week."

"And you'd let him."

"I'd let him," she said, "because he's earned the right to be unbearable. Alive people get to be unbearable. That's practically the definition."

She went out. Vann sat alone with the cold tea and the ghost-warm letters and realized his hands were shaking slightly. Not fear—or not only fear. Something worse. He had come here a hired man carrying a dead man's words, and somewhere between the first line and the last, the dead man had stopped being cargo and become a person, and now Vann was going to have to stand in front of Colonel Brecht and explain why the merchandise had developed a heartbeat.

Upstairs, the floorboard creaked again. Nell, still listening. He raised his voice just enough:

"He's alive, Nell."

A pause. Then, muffled, delighted, entirely too loud: "I *knew* it. I'm telling Aunt Marta."

"Don't—"

Footsteps, already thundering down the hall.

Vann sighed, gathered the letters, and slid them back into their folder—where his fingers found, wedged behind the false backing, the corner of a page he had never noticed before. Torn edges. Burned at one side.

And in Tomas's unmistakable hand, a single unfinished line: *"If Brecht offers you berths, count them twice, because—"*

The rest was gone.
