## 17. Marta Makes a Call

Marta Yarrow—born Marta Vosk, though she hadn't answered to that name since before either of the children in her keeping had been born—had a system for lying to her family, and the system was this: she told the truth as often as possible, so that when it mattered, no one thought to check.

It was Tuesday, which meant fish, which meant she could walk to the market on Kessel Street without anyone asking why. Ada would assume herring. Nell would assume gossip, because Nell assumed Marta ran on gossip the way lamps ran on oil, and there were days—many days, most days—when Nell wasn't wrong. The girl had a talent for being right about people that was going to get her either married well or drowned in a canal, and Marta had not yet decided which outcome to encourage.

The truth was that Marta needed to make a telephone call, and telephone calls of a certain kind could not be made from a house where a bookkeeper counted every coin twice and a ward noticed everything, and where the third member of the household—that charming, evasive translator with his too-smooth hands and his too-careful answers—might very well be reporting everything he saw to someone worse than himself.

She bought the herring first. Two, wrapped in paper, because a woman carrying two herrings is invisible. A woman carrying nothing walks like a woman with a purpose, and purpose draws eyes.

* * *

The fishmonger's name was Oskar Pell, and he had been a bad smuggler and a worse resistance man and an excellent fishmonger, which was why he was still alive. He'd run messages along the coast for six years during the bad business, until the network folded in on itself like wet cardboard, and then he'd done what survivors do: he'd changed trades, changed his posture, and learned to shout about prices.

He shouted about prices beautifully now. You would never know.

"Two herring," Marta said, laying coins on the marble slab. "And they'd better be fresh, Oskar, or I'll tell everyone at Saint Anne's you've been selling them last week's catch."

"They're fresh," Oskar said, wrapping them with the solemnity of a priest handling relics. "Everything I have is fresh."

"That's a lie and we both know it." She leaned in slightly, over the smell of brine and ice. "I need to use your back room."

Oskar's hands did not stop moving. That was the mark of him; panic happened somewhere behind his eyes and never made it past the wrists. "The back room is for crates," he said loudly, for the benefit of the woman choosing eels beside her. "Smelly place. Nothing for a respectable lady."

"I've been in your back room before, Oskar."

"And I've been in yours, Mrs. Yarrow, and neither of us has ever once mentioned the other to a soul, so let's keep the streak alive." But he jerked his chin toward the curtain, and that was that. Twenty years of silence between them, and the silence still worked better than any codebook ever had.

* * *

The back room smelled of ice, salt, and the particular sweetness of fish left too long in August heat—a smell that, Marta reflected as she waited, had probably kept more secrets than any confessional in the city. No one lingered in a room like this unless they had to. Whatever was said here stayed here because nobody wanted to stay long enough to hear it twice.

There was a telephone on a shelf between a ledger (of course a ledger; everyone kept ledgers; the whole world was ledgers) and a tin of pipe tobacco that had held something other than tobacco in another decade. Oskar came in after a minute, wiped his hands on his apron, and looked at her the way he'd looked at her the night in '18 when she'd asked him to move a crate he was better off not asking about.

"Brecht," she said.

Oskar closed his eyes briefly, as if she'd named a disease. "You want me to ask around about Brecht."

"I want you to find out where he sleeps, who he eats with, and how many men come when he whistles."

"Marta." He sat down heavily on a crate marked FROZEN—AUGUST, which had been marked FROZEN—AUGUST since the war and was almost certainly a lie. "Do you know what I do now? I sell fish. I sell fish and I complain about my knees and I go to Mass on Sunday and I am *boring*, Marta. Boring is armor. I spent ten years building this armor one dull day at a time, and you walk in with two herrings and a dead man's name on your tongue."

"He's not dead."

"That's the problem!" Oskar spread his hands. "Dead men, fine. Dead men are safe. Dead men stay buried and their names are just names. A living Brecht is—" he lowered his voice to nearly nothing, which for Oskar was a genuine feat, "—is a live wire in a harbor full of water. Why? Why now? Who's asking?"

Marta considered how much truth to spend. Truth was currency, and she'd been hoarding it for twenty years; but hoarded currency only devalues.

"My niece found letters," she said. "In her dead brother's hand. And the man who brought them has a second recipient's name on his papers, and that name is Brecht, and I recognized it before he said it aloud, because I heard it first across a table in a café in '18 while men decided who lived and who got put on a list."

Oskar was quiet for a long moment. Outside, through the wall, his shop boy was yelling the price of mackerel at a woman who was clearly winning the argument.

"The café on Verstraat," Oskar said finally. "The green awning."

"You remember."

"I remember everything about that year except how to sleep afterward." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Brecht. Colonel Brecht, if we're being formal, which he always insisted on even when he was buying rounds. Charming man. Laughed easily. Kept a little notebook—he used to joke about it, tap the pocket and say, *everyone's story goes in the book eventually.* We all laughed." Oskar's mouth twisted. "We laughed because he was paying for the drinks."

"He sold the route, Oskar. The evacuation route. Eleven people on a list that was supposed to be four, and every one of them walked into a checkpoint that knew they were coming."

"I know what he sold. I helped carry two of them out by the skin of our teeth, and one of them didn't make it anyway, so yes, Marta, I have some idea of the bill." He stood up, went to the shelf, and took down the tobacco tin, weighing it in his palm as if checking whether the years had emptied it. "Why does your niece matter to Brecht after twenty years?"

"Because of what she carried out. Because of what her family carried out. And because—" Marta hesitated, and hated herself for hesitating, because hesitation was a door and doors invite people through them. "Because there may be more than one piece of it. There may be a piece that names names. His name among them."

Oskar set the tin down very carefully, the way a man sets down something that might be a bomb or might just be a tin, and both possibilities require care.

"If there's a document naming Brecht," he said slowly, "then half the old network would burn their own houses down to get it, and the other half would burn yours. And Brecht himself would burn everything, starting with witnesses."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me this in a room that smells like rotting herring, instead of taking your niece and running."

"She won't run." Marta heard her own voice go dry and flat, the way it did when feeling was too large for the container. "I raised her, Oskar. Her mother was my sister, God keep her, and Ada got all the stubbornness in the family plus a double portion. That girl keeps accounts the way monks keep prayers. She will not run while there's a column unfinished. So the question isn't whether she walks toward Brecht. The question is whether she walks toward him blindfolded or knowing where the knife is."

* * *

They argued for forty minutes. It was, Marta thought afterward, almost pleasant—the way certain arguments are pleasant, arguments with old friends where the friendship isn't in danger, only everything else. Oskar laid out reasons like cards: he was out, he was old, his knees were real now, the boy who ran messages for him back then was dead or turned or both. Marta countered each one, and somewhere in the middle of countering she realized she was enjoying herself, and felt ashamed, and enjoyed herself anyway.

In the end he gave in the way he always gave in: sideways, complaining, with conditions.

"I'll make calls," he said. "Not from here. From the tobacconist on Delph Street, old Sander, he owes me since '19 and he owes Brecht nothing, which in this city is rarer than fresh fish. Three days. Maybe four. I can tell you where Brecht eats, who guards him, and whether he's moved his office since spring." He pointed a thick finger at her. "But you owe me something."

"Name it."

"When this is over—one way or the other—you come to Sunday dinner. My wife has been asking after you for eleven years and I've been telling her you're imaginary."

Marta snorted despite herself. "What does she think of the imaginary friend?"

"That I invented a woman to have someone to argue with, since arguing with him was getting lonely." He nodded toward the front of the shop. "Go out through the yard. The alley connects to the lane behind Saint Anne's, and nobody watches church lanes on a Tuesday."

At the curtain she stopped. Old habit—old, old habit, older than the children, older than the exile.

"Oskar. If it goes wrong—if someone follows you, or asks you questions—"

"Then I'm a fishmonger who lent his phone to a lady from his parish, and you're a widow who buys too much herring, and we've never discussed anything but prices in our lives." He smiled, and it was the old smile from the green-awning café, the one that had made young men trust him with their lives, and Marta understood suddenly why he'd been such a bad smuggler and such a good survivor: because he could make you believe the lie even while you watched him build it.

"Prices," she agreed. "You gouge me terribly."

"It's the economy, Mrs. Yarrow. Everything's gone up."

* * *

She walked home the long way, because the long way passed the harbor, and she wanted—with a specific, professional curiosity—to look at Pier Nine.

The repatriation ship was already there, riding high and rust-streaked, cranes swinging over it like patient birds. Men with clipboards. A queue forming outside a temporary office, people holding documents the way pilgrims hold relics. Hope, Marta thought, always looks the same from the outside: a line of tired people clutching paper, believing paper can save them. She had believed it once. Paper had saved her, technically. It had also buried her, under a name that wasn't hers and a history that wasn't true, and she had been living inside the forgery so long she sometimes caught herself praying to a saint under the wrong name.

Brecht's office—she remembered it from the old days, from before, from the years when he'd been merely ambitious rather than monstrous—was the gray stone building with the arched windows near the customs house. She didn't slow as she passed it. Slowing is a signal; she'd taught that lesson to younger people once, and some of those lessons had stuck to her like burrs. But she let her eyes do the work while her feet kept their dull, shopping-day rhythm.

Two men smoking by the side entrance, wearing coats too good for clerks. A car idling in the shade with its engine running—nobody idles an engine for a clerk. And on the steps, checking a pocket watch with theatrical precision, a heavyset man whose posture she knew.

Colonel Brecht had gotten fat. Time had done to him what it refuses to do to the guilty in novels—it had simply softened him, given him jowls and a comfortable stride, turned the wolf into something more like a well-fed dog that still remembers biting. He laughed at something one of the smokers said, and the laugh carried across the street, big and easy, the same laugh from the café on Verstraat.

Everyone's story goes in the book eventually.

Marta kept walking. Past the pier. Past the ship. Past a flower seller who called out to her and whom she ignored with the courtesy of the deaf. Her heart was doing something complicated—not racing, exactly; she was too old for racing—but moving in a way it hadn't moved in years, a slow heavy turn like a millstone remembering its work.

Because here was the thing she had carried longest, longer even than the sister, longer than the guilt: she had known. In '18, across that table, watching Brecht tap his little notebook, she had known what kind of man he was, and she had said nothing, because saying something would have meant explaining how she knew, and explaining would have burned the route faster than any traitor. She had chosen silence to protect the children, and the silence had worked, and it had also cost—she'd lost track over the years of exactly what it had cost, the way you lose track of a debt compounding quietly in a drawer.

Twenty years of quiet. And now the quiet had a price on its head, and so did the girl she'd raised, and the boy who wasn't dead after all, and Marta found that at her age, with her knees, with her imaginary-friend reputation at Saint Anne's, she was not willing to pay it again.

Loyalty, she thought, climbing the hill toward home with her two herrings, is a muscle. You think it's gone. You think it atrophied decades ago. Then someone you love stands in a doorway looking like her mother did, and the muscle wakes up sore and furious and ready.

* * *

The house smelled of coffee and pencil shavings when she let herself in. Ada was at the kitchen table with the typewriter pushed aside, papers fanned out in her accounting fan, lips moving faintly as she checked figures against figures. Nell was supposed to be doing homework and was visibly memorizing the contents of Ada's papers instead, with her chin propped on her fist and her eyes flicking up every time Ada turned a page. And Vann—

Vann was at the window, ostensibly reading, actually watching the street. Watching it the way a man watches a street when he's counting exits.

"Well?" Ada said, not looking up. "Was the herring worth the walk?"

"The herring was adequate and the fishmonger is a criminal." Marta hung her coat. "Also, I ran into Mrs. Brandt, who tells me the Delacroix girl is engaged again, to a different man, and if you want details you'll have to ask, because I promised discretion."

Nell lit up like a struck match. "Which Delacroix girl? The tall one or the one with the—"

"*Homework,*" said Ada and Marta together, in a harmony twenty years deep, and Nell subsided with the expression of a girl filing the question away for later, which was precisely what Marta loved and feared about her.

Vann glanced over from the window, polite and mild. "Good morning, Mrs. Yarrow. Successful errands?"

"Very successful," Marta said warmly, meeting his eyes and giving him nothing whatsoever, the way she'd been giving people nothing for twenty years. "I'll start lunch. Ada, come taste the herring, I need a second opinion and Nell's palate is ruined by sweets."

Ada rose, puzzled—Marta never asked for second opinions; Marta considered her own opinion final—and followed her to the stove.

Marta kept her voice below the sound of the pan, below the sound of the street, pitched for one listener only. "The man at the window," she murmured, scraping the pan for the noise of it. "Watch him today. Not suspiciously—lovingly. Like a mother. Because either he's ours, in which case he needs feeding and protecting, or he's theirs, in which case he needs feeding and watching. Either way, Ada, he gets fed. That's how you learn which one it is."

Ada stared at her. "Marta. What did you do this morning?"

"I bought herring," Marta said, "and I made a call." She handed her niece a fork. "Now eat, and then tell me everything you've figured out that you haven't told me, because I know that face, and it's the face your mother made right before she did something reckless."

Outside the window, unnoticed by anyone in the warm kitchen, a man in a good coat crossed the street, paused opposite the house long enough to light a cigarette he did not smoke, and wrote something small in a small notebook before walking on toward the harbor.

Everyone's story goes in the book eventually.
