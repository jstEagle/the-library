## 18. The Harbor Office Trap

The harbor office smelled of tar and old decisions.

Ada Yarrow stood across the quay from it at a quarter past nine in the morning, pretending to study a fishmonger's display of herring that she had no intention of buying. The fishmonger had noticed this. He was the sort of man who noticed everything about everyone and said nothing to anyone, which made him, in Ada's professional assessment, the most dangerous kind of neighbor. She bought two herring she didn't want just to give her standing there a commercial explanation.

*There,* she thought. *A woman who buys fish is a woman with a kitchen. A woman with a kitchen is harmless.*

It was the same logic she had used for twenty years: make yourself legible, make yourself boring, make yourself the sort of person whose file, if anyone ever bothered to open one, would close again out of disappointment. It had worked through three cities and one war scare and an entire decade of Marta's complaints about the rent. It should have worked now.

Except that the letter in her handbag — Tomas's handwriting, Tomas's cipher, folded into eighths like something shameful — kept whispering its single instruction at her. *Bring the remainder to the harbor office.* And except that the harbor office, when she finally let herself look at it directly instead of through the flattering veil of herring, was wrong.

She had been in that office twice, years ago, on shipping paperwork for the import firm. She remembered it as a narrow building squeezed between a chandler's and a customs shed, with a green door that stuck, a clerk named Ostrowski who chewed his pencil, and windows so salt-fogged they might have been frosted glass. Bureaucracy at its most somnolent. The kind of place where time went to be filed.

The green door was still there. The fogged windows were still there. But this morning, two men in plain coats stood near the chandler's side of the building doing absolutely nothing, which was how soldiers pretending not to be soldiers always gave themselves away. They didn't smoke. Men who are genuinely waiting for someone smoke. Men who are waiting for something to *happen* stand with their weight forward, like dogs who have been told to stay.

And there was a third man on the roofline of the customs shed, or what Ada's eyes insisted was a third man — a shape against the chimney that resolved into a cap and shoulders whenever she looked slightly away from it and dissolved whenever she looked straight on. She had learned that trick from a cat.

Ada turned back to the herring.

"Fresh this morning," the fishmonger offered.

"Everything is fresh this morning," Ada said. "That's the trouble with mornings."

He laughed as if she'd said something charming rather than something true, and wrapped her unwanted herring in newspaper, and Ada paid him and walked away at the pace of a woman with nothing whatsoever on her mind, while her heart performed a drumroll that would have embarrassed a circus band.

*So. He expects me.*

She should have felt only fear. What she actually felt, somewhere beneath the fear, was a cold, clarifying anger — the feeling of a ledger that has finally balanced. Because Brecht had done her the courtesy of confirming every suspicion she'd been trying to talk herself out of. A letter asking her to come to a public office, in daylight, by ordinary means? No kidnapper works that way. But a man who wants a document surrendered *witnessed* — surrendered properly, with paper trails, with clerks, with the machinery of legitimacy wrapped around it like butcher's string — a man like that needs an office. Needs soldiers dressed as loafers. Needs the whole pantomime of procedure, because Colonel Brecht had never stolen anything in his life; he had only ever *acquired* things administratively.

Ada knew the type. She had married into nothing and buried everyone, but she had spent her girlhood watching men like Brecht sign things.

She walked home the long way, along the seawall, where the wind came off the water honest and cold and blew all the pretense out of a person. She held the wrapped herring under her arm like a baton and let herself think, properly think, for the first time since the letter had arrived.

They expected her today or tomorrow. They did not know when. Which meant they would wait, and waiting men get bored, and bored men relax their angles. Also — and here was the small, sharp gift inside the large, ugly parcel — they were watching for a woman arriving alone with a parcel. They were *not* watching, yet, for what she might do before she ever came near them.

Fear, Ada reflected, was a great deal like arithmetic. It looked like chaos from the outside, all those numbers tumbling, but if you sat down and did the work, it resolved into columns. And once you had columns, you had options. And once you had options, you were no longer prey. You were simply a bookkeeper with a difficult account.

By the time she reached the corner of her own street, she had stopped shaking. She noted this with interest, the way she noted everything now, and went inside to lie to her family with a straight face, which was, after all, her oldest professional skill.

* * *

"You've got herring," Marta said, in the tone other women reserved for discovering infidelity.

"I've got herring."

"We don't eat herring."

"Nell eats herring."

"Nell eats herring *grudgingly*, the way she does everything, and she'll eat it tonight and announce it was a punishment." Marta took the package, unwrapped it, sniffed it, and frowned — not at the fish but at her niece, in the particular frown she kept for discrepancies. "You're up early."

"I couldn't sleep."

"Since when? You sleep like the dead. You slept through the fire on Kessler Street. I had to carry the photograph album out myself while you dreamed on." Marta laid the fish in the basin and ran water over it, still watching her sideways. "Where did you walk?"

"The seawall."

"In those shoes?"

Ada looked down at her shoes, which were indeed the wrong ones, the good ones, the ones she wore to the bank. A lesser liar would have improvised. Ada simply said, "I wanted to look at the harbor," and let the sentence sit there, plain as bread, until Marta had to decide whether to swallow it or make a scene over breakfast, and Marta — who had been making her own calculations for twenty years longer than Ada — chose the herring.

But that night, after Nell had gone up to bed with the lamp and the household had gone quiet in its particular way, the way of people listening to each other not-sleep, Marta appeared in the doorway of Ada's room without knocking, which meant business.

"If you're going to do something foolish," Marta said, "do it after Thursday. The fish man extends credit until Thursday."

"Marta—"

"I'm not asking what it is." She came in and sat on the edge of the bed, hands folded, a woman settling in for a long wait at a station platform. "I'm telling you what I know. That office on the quay — Ostrowski retired two years ago. There's a new man. Young. Polite. He asked our neighbor Piotr, very politely, whether the Yarrow household received much post." Marta paused. "Piotr told him we receive bills. Piotr is a better actor than either of us gives him credit for, and he charges less."

Ada felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way it did on ships she hadn't boarded. "You went and asked around."

"I went and bought onions. Onions take you everywhere in this city." Marta's voice was dry as old rope, but her hand, when it found Ada's wrist in the dark, was warm and steady and not entirely steady at all. "Twenty years ago I watched your mother pack you onto a boat with a number written on your arm in ink because there wasn't time to explain. I promised her I'd keep you dull and alive. Dull I've managed. Alive is looking like more of an administrative challenge these days."

"Marta, I'm not going to do anything—"

"Don't insult us both." Marta stood, patted the quilt flat where she'd wrinkled it, restoring order to a bed nobody would see. "Whatever it is, you won't do it alone. Not because I approve. Because I'm contrary, and because your mother would come back from wherever she is specifically to haunt me, and she was insufferable enough alive."

At the door she stopped. "Eat something. Fear burns fuel like a stove with the damper open. Your father never learned that. It's why he was always so easy to read."

She left before Ada could ask what she meant by that — Marta was fluent in leaving before questions — and Ada lay in the dark with the letter under her pillow, beneath her head, like a stone she was learning to sleep on, and turned over the new column in her mental ledger: *they are already asking about our post.*

Which meant the trap was not waiting for her at the harbor office. The trap had already moved in upstairs.

* * *

She went back on Wednesday, because strategy demanded confirmation and fear demanded motion, and she went differently this time: in her work clothes, with the firm's canvas satchel and a genuine errand — invoices for a client whose warehouse stood four streets inland, a route she had chosen precisely because it passed the quay. If anyone asked, and she rehearsed the answer until it came out smooth, she was delivering paperwork and taking the sea air, because clerks were allowed sea air, weren't they, it was practically in the employment contract.

The morning was gray and busy, gulls conducting their usual racket, a tramp steamer taking on crates with the groaning patience of all ships everywhere. Ada walked her route, delivered her invoices, accepted a cup of terrible coffee she didn't want from the warehouse foreman because refusing things drew attention, and came back along the quay with her collar up.

The loitering men were still there. Both of them. One had acquired a newspaper he was holding upside down, which Ada found almost touching — somewhere, a colonel had given these men a briefing on blending in, and this was the result. The roof shape was gone, replaced, she guessed, by the man mending a net thirty yards down the quay who had not actually mended any net in the forty minutes she could observe him, his fingers moving with the purposeful rhythm of a man thinking very hard about something else entirely.

And then, as she passed the harbor office itself — not slowing, *not slowing*, counting her own footsteps like a metronome — the green door opened and a clerk stepped out to post a notice on the board beside it, and Ada, obeying some instinct older than caution, glanced at the board the way any citizen glances at notices, because that is what boards are *for*.

Sailing schedules. A customs regulation in language designed to be unread. And pinned at an angle, fresh, the announcement she had already read in the papers: the repatriation ship, ten days, limited berths, verification of documents required *at the harbor office, second floor, between nine and four.*

Second floor.

Ada had been in that office twice, and both times everything had happened on the ground floor, because there was hardly anything else. She remembered now — memory unspooling with the slow horror of a film run backward — the stairs behind the counter, narrow, uncarpeted, going up to a records loft that Ostrowski had once described as "full of damp and dead pigeons, madam, don't go up there unless you enjoy disappointment." Nobody conducted berth verification in a pigeon loft. Verification would happen in a hall, at tables, in sight of witnesses.

Unless the point was not verification. Unless the point was a room with one stair, one door, thick walls, and a window overlooking the water — a window over the water being convenient for a great many things that happened in ports to people who were never seen again.

She kept walking. She did not break stride. She passed the net-mender, who did not look up; she passed the man with the inverted newspaper, who was reading an advertisement for corsets with fierce concentration; she walked all the way to the end of the quay, turned at the bollard as though she'd only come out for the view, and allowed herself one long look back at the harbor office, letting her eyes do quickly and deliberately what they had done all week in glimpses.

Green door. Notice board. Second-floor window, curtains drawn — drawn, in a building where every other curtain hung open to the gray light, because harbor offices ran on light and cheapness, and closed curtains cost nothing and hid everything.

Behind those curtains, someone was watching the quay. She felt it the way you feel weather coming, in the scar tissue.

*They expect a woman with a parcel,* she thought, turning for home. *They expect her frightened, grateful, punctual between nine and four. They expect the remainder wrapped and ready, because men like Brecht always believe the thing they want is the thing that will be handed over.*

Ada climbed the hill toward home with the wind pushing at her back like a hand between the shoulder blades, and somewhere on the second street she realized she was smiling, a small grim smile she was glad no one could see, because it was not a kind smile and she had a reputation to maintain.

Let them watch the door. Let them watch the post. Let them count the days down to their tidy appointment between nine and four.

They were watching the front of the house, so to speak. And Ada Yarrow had spent twenty years learning that every house has a back way, and every ledger has a second set of books, and the whole art of survival — the entire discipline of it, the whole strange, lonely craft — consisted in knowing which numbers to show and which to keep written in a hand only you could read.

She had a typewriter at home. She had ink, paper, and ten days. She had a brother's cipher in her head and a colonel's name in her mouth and, now, the exact shape of the trap laid out in her mind like a floor plan.

What she did not have — and this was the entry she carried up the hill with her, the line item she couldn't balance, the figure that refused to sit still in its column — was any certainty at all about Elias Vann, whose story changed in the details the way stories do when they're mostly true, and whose eyes, when he talked about Tomas's letters, went somewhere she couldn't follow.

She let herself in quietly. From the kitchen came the smell of herring frying and Nell's voice saying, with tremendous dignity, "*I* didn't ask for it," and Marta's voice replying, "Nobody asks for herring, child, herring is assigned."

Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. Ada stood in the hallway with her hand still on the latch and understood, with the clarity that comes after fear has finished sharpening itself into a blade, that whatever she decided next, she would decide alone — and that Vann, whether he was a courier, a hunter, or merely a tired man caught in someone else's arithmetic, would have to choose his side within the week.

Because she had seen the second-floor window. And she had counted, on her long look back, exactly how many steps it was from the green door to the alley behind the chandler's.

Nine. She'd need nine seconds' head start, and one ally who could hold a door.

She hung up her coat, put on her face, and went in to dinner.
