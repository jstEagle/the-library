## 19. Nell Follows

Nell had three rules for following someone, and she'd invented all of them before breakfast.

Rule one: never look directly at the person you're following. Rule two: carry something. Nobody suspects a person carrying something—a loaf of bread, a library book, a paper sack of chestnuts. A person carrying nothing is a person with intent. Rule three, the most important: have somewhere to be if you get caught.

She had borrowed the bread from Marta without asking, which was not stealing exactly, because Marta always said waste was a sin and there was no sin in a mission. She had also taken two coppers from the jar on the mantel for the tram, which she fully intended to repay out of her allowance, assuming she survived long enough to receive an allowance again.

Ada had left the house at nine o'clock wearing the gray coat she only wore when she wanted to be invisible, which was itself suspicious, because Ada's whole life was an exercise in being invisible and she didn't usually need a special coat for it. She'd said she was going to the fishmonger's.

They did not keep fish at the harbor office.

Nell had known that since Tuesday, when she'd gone down to fetch Marta's spectacles from the repair shop near the docks and seen the sign—HARBOR MASTER'S OFFICE, ENQUIRIES, PERMITS—in letters so official they practically saluted. And she had known Ada hadn't been herself for days. There were the burned papers in the kitchen stove, still warm when Nell came down at dawn. There was the way Ada had started locking her ledger drawer, which she had never once done in the four years Nell had lived under her roof. There was Vann, who arrived like weather and stayed like furniture and watched Ada across the dinner table as though memorizing her face against some future loss.

Nell was eleven years old and everyone treated this as a kind of blindness. It was, in fact, the opposite. Children saw everything; they simply weren't believed, which was the great advantage of childhood and the reason nobody ever suspected them of anything worth suspecting.

So: rule one. Never look directly. She trailed Ada down the Esplanade by watching her reflection in the shop windows—the chemist's glass, the milliner's, the window of Herr Ostrow's bookshop where the owner arranged his volumes by color because he claimed customers remembered books better that way. In the glass, Ada moved like a woman going to an appointment she dreaded.

At the corner of Quay Street, Ada stopped. Nell ducked behind a barrel of herring brine, pressed the bread to her chest like a shield, and breathed through her mouth.

Ada looked back. Not searching—checking. The way a person checks whether they've been followed, which meant Ada expected to be followed, which meant whatever she was doing, she knew it was dangerous enough to warrant checking.

Then she went on toward the water, and Nell counted to fifty, and followed.

* * *

The harbor district smelled of tar and diesel and old rope, and it was full of men who had nowhere particular to be, which made it both the easiest and hardest place in the world to tail someone. Easy, because loitering was the local religion. Hard, because Ada kept stopping to look at things—the notice board outside the shipping agent's, the queue outside the consular annex, the gray water itself—and every time she stopped, Nell had to invent a reason to stop too.

She examined the notice board with tremendous interest. SAILINGS AND CLEARANCES. TIDE TABLES. A poster about typhoid inoculations featuring a drawing of a germ so cheerful it looked proud of itself. Then she bought a twist of salted plums from a cart with her two coppers, ate one, decided she hated salted plums, and kept them anyway, because a girl holding a twist of paper was a girl with errands, not intentions.

Ada crossed the yard toward the harbor office. She stood at the edge of it for a long moment, just looking, the way you might look at a dog you suspected of biting. Then she walked past the front steps—past them, Nell noted—and went around the side, where there was a door marked STAFF ONLY and a window with frosted glass and, beside the window, a bench where a man sat sorting fishing tackle.

Ada didn't go in. She stood where she could see the staff door and pretended to read a mooring schedule pinned to the wall, and Nell, from behind a stack of lobster pots, understood suddenly what she was watching for.

She was counting the men going in and out. Or waiting for one man in particular.

Nell's heart did something complicated. She wasn't afraid, exactly—or she was, but it was the good kind, the kind that came with knowing you were finally about to find out what the grown-ups were keeping behind their eyes at dinner. For weeks now the house had hummed with things unsaid. Marta's silences had gotten heavier, like furniture moved up another floor. Vann's charm had developed hairline cracks where you could see something frightened underneath. And Ada—

Ada had begun to look at Nell sometimes the way she looked at breakable things.

The staff door opened. A man came out. He was small and neat and wore a clerk's visor even though he was outdoors, which struck Nell as the silliest thing she'd ever seen, a man indoors on his own head while standing in the wind. He carried a leather folder and he stopped on the steps, and he looked—not at Ada, past her, scanning the yard with little quick movements like a bird deciding whether the crumbs were safe.

And then his eyes found Ada, and stopped moving entirely.

Nell saw it happen: the moment the bird decides the crumbs are a trap. The clerk smiled. It was a terrible smile, a smile built in an office, and he descended the steps and began walking toward Ada with the folder held against his chest like a hymnal.

Ada turned away from him. Too late. Too obvious. She began walking toward the far gate, quickly, then faster, and the clerk lengthened his stride, and Nell's stomach dropped through the bottom of the morning.

* * *

Here is what Nell would tell herself later, in the dark, going over it: that she hadn't thought. That following was one thing and intervening was another, and the line between them was supposed to hold. But the truth was simpler and worse. She saw Ada walking fast and the clerk walking faster, and her feet simply decided, without consulting her at all, that nobody chased Aunt Ada. Not today. Not in Nell's harbor.

She cut between the lobster pots and the fuel drums, planning to cross the clerk's path, trip maybe, drop the plums, cause a diversion of some kind—she hadn't got as far as the details. She was halfway across the open ground when a hand closed on her collar and swung her behind a stack of crates so smoothly it felt almost polite.

Vann.

He had a finger against his lips before she could speak, and his other hand held a coffee cup, steaming, absurdly domestic, as though he'd been standing here drinking coffee all morning, which, she realized with a jolt, he probably had.

"Hello, Nell," he whispered. "Nice bread."

"You're following her too," she whispered back, outraged.

"I'm following her professionally. There's a difference."

"There isn't."

"There's a salary," he said, "and I'll explain the joke later. Right now—" His eyes flicked over her head, tracking the clerk, and everything loose and joking in his face packed itself away like tools into a kit. What remained was somebody Nell hadn't met before. Somebody competent. "Right now, your aunt is about thirty seconds from being introduced to a very bad man's errand boy, and we are going to prevent that, and you are going to do exactly what I say."

"Or what?"

"Or I pick you up and put you in a coal shed, and I know you'd get out eventually, but eventually won't help Ada." He said it kindly, which somehow made it more convincing.

"What do I do?"

"Walk to the fish stall. Loudly. Ask about herring. Be eleven as hard as you can."

"I'm always eleven."

"Not like this," said Vann, and gave her a small push toward the stalls.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and Nell saw it sideways, in pieces, between herring questions she barely heard herself ask.

The clerk caught up with Ada at the gate. She turned, and Nell heard the exchange begin—low voices, the clerk's smile still nailed on, the folder coming up slightly, the way a man raises a document when he wants you to understand it's not a request. Words reached Nell in fragments. "...the lady from the Esplanade..." and "...an invitation..." and once, chillingly clear, "...he knows you've been here twice already, madam."

Twice. So Ada had come before. This was the second time, or the third, and none of the household had been told, and Nell filed that away in the room in her head where she kept the other evidence, which was getting crowded.

Ada said something Nell couldn't hear, level and cold, and took a half-step back, and the clerk took a half-step forward, and that was when Vann entered the scene.

He did it beautifully. That was the thing about Vann that Nell both admired and distrusted: he could enter any scene as though he belonged to it, as though the world had been waiting for him. He stumbled. He actually stumbled, into the clerk, spilling an entire cup of coffee down the man's neat front and over the leather folder, apologizing in three languages at once, in a voice pitched perfectly to draw every eye in the yard.

"My God—sir—I am so sorry—the cobblestones here, they're criminal, in Trieste they'd have been sued—" He produced a handkerchief from nowhere and dabbed at the clerk's chest with the enthusiasm of a man polishing a museum piece. "Your folder—is it ruined? Papers? Oh no. Here, let me—"

"Don't touch it." The clerk's voice had gone thin.

"Of course, of course, I wouldn't dream—" Vann touched it. Just briefly, just to steady it, just long enough, and though Nell was forty feet away she saw the clerk stiffen, saw something pass between the two men that had nothing to do with coffee. Recognition, maybe. Or warning. Vann's face stayed open and foolish and apologetic, but his eyes, for one bare instant, were flat as coins.

Then the fool came back, all stammer and contrition, and he steered the clerk bodily toward the office—"I insist on buying you a fresh cup, there's a kiosk, my treat, it's the least"—and the clerk went, because a man soaked in coffee cannot make threats with dignity, and dignity was clearly the only weapon the clerk had been issued.

Ada stood at the gate, alone, watching them go. Her hands were steady. Her face was not. For one second—one unguarded second before she smoothed it away—she looked like a woman standing on ice and hearing it crack.

Then Vann peeled off from the clerk at the kiosk with a final flourish of apologies, ambled back across the yard, collected Nell from the fish stall with a nod as casual as if they'd arranged the whole thing, and walked her out the far gate while Ada circled around to meet them on the quay beyond.

* * *

Nobody spoke until the harbor was two streets behind them and the noise of the city had folded back in around them like water closing over a stone.

Then Ada stopped, turned, and looked at Nell, and Nell prepared herself for thunder.

"You were supposed to be at Marta's mending lesson."

"Marta fell asleep," Nell said, which was true. "I finished the hem myself. It's crooked, but it's finished."

"That's not the point."

"No," Nell admitted. "But it's a good detail. It makes me sound diligent."

Something moved at the corner of Ada's mouth—fought its way up, lost, retreated. She pressed her lips together and looked instead at Vann, and her voice changed entirely, dropped all its warmth like a coat shrugged off.

"How long have you been watching me?"

"Since you locked your ledger drawer," Vann said. "Which, incidentally, is the first honest answer I've given anyone in this house, and I'd appreciate it being noted."

"And you—" Ada turned back to Nell, and Nell watched her aunt try to assemble a scolding and fail, piece by piece, because underneath the anger was something else, something that looked horribly like fear wearing anger's clothes. "Do you have any idea what that man was?"

"A clerk," said Nell.

"He works for Brecht." Ada said the name quietly, but the quiet made it worse, the way a whisper is louder than shouting in certain rooms. "Do you understand what that means? If he'd looked at you properly—if he'd seen your face yesterday, or tomorrow, anywhere near me—"

"He didn't see my face," Nell said. "That was the entire point of the bread."

"The bread."

"Rule two. Always carry something. Nobody suspects—"

"Nell." Ada closed her eyes. When she opened them she crouched down to Nell's level, which she hadn't done since Nell was nine, and took her by the shoulders, and her hands were cold through Nell's coat. "Listen to me. Whatever you think is happening—whatever you've worked out, and I know you've worked out a great deal, you always do—it is not a game, and it is not a mystery novel, and the people involved would hurt you without losing any sleep over it. Do you hear me?"

Nell heard her. She also heard what Ada didn't say, which was: I am terrified, and I have been terrified alone for weeks, and the loneliest thing in the world is being protected by people who won't tell you what from.

"Yes," Nell said.

Ada searched her face, looking for the lie, and found it, and knew she'd found it, and let it go anyway, which told Nell more than the whole speech had.

They walked home. Vann drifted a few paces behind, playing rear guard, whistling something tuneless. At the corner of their street he caught up and said, low, to Ada, not to Nell:

"He described you. To his people. Which means Brecht has your face now, or will by tonight." A pause. "It also means I'm blown. That clerk knows me. Not as Vann—he knows me as the man who was asking questions in the consular office last month, and now he knows I drink coffee in harbors and spill it on demand. My cover story has approximately one day left to live."

"Then stop spending it on us," Ada said.

"No," said Vann, pleasantly.

They reached the house. Marta was awake, furious, holding the crooked hemmed handkerchief like evidence at a trial, and the ordinary noise of the household swallowed them—Marta's voice rising, Vann's charm deploying itself, the kettle going on—and Nell slipped upstairs to the room she shared with the wardrobe that stuck.

Under cover of the argument downstairs, she opened her tin box and added today's findings to her collection: *harbor office, staff door, second visit minimum, clerk named nothing yet, works for B., coffee man knows him, cover one day.*

At the top of the page, after a moment's thought, she wrote one more line:

*Ada lied about how scared she is. It's worse than she says. Someone should do something.*

She looked at the line for a long time. Then she underlined *someone*, and shut the tin, and pushed it back beneath the loose floorboard, next to the plums she'd saved for evidence and now couldn't bring herself to eat.
