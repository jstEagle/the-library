## 20. Divided House

The argument began, as most arguments in that house did, with Marta setting down a teacup too firmly.

"Run," she said. "That is my entire contribution to the evening's deliberations. Run. It has worked for me since 1919 and I see no reason to abandon a method with such an excellent record."

Ada had arranged them around the kitchen table because the parlor felt too formal for what needed deciding, and the bedroom felt too intimate, and the stairwell — where she had done her best thinking for twenty years — could only hold one person at a time. The kitchen table was scarred, honest, and large enough that no one had to sit beside anyone they were currently furious with. She had not anticipated how quickly everyone would need that feature.

Vann sat with his elbows on the wood, looking like a man who had rehearsed this speech on a train platform and was now discovering the audience had changed. "You can't run from Brecht. I've watched men try. He doesn't chase; he waits. He owns clerks in every port office between here and the border. You'd be running through his filing cabinet."

"How poetic," said Marta.

"It's not poetry. It's paperwork. He wins by paperwork." Vann turned his hands over, palms up, as if presenting the argument to a jury of one. "Hiding is worse. Hiding means waiting for him to find you, and he will find you, because finding people is the only thing he's ever been good at besides betrayal."

"So we meet him," Ada said.

The words came out steadier than she felt. Across the table, Nell went very still — the stillness of a girl who had been told all evening to stay out of adult conversations and had instead been listening with the focused appetite of a customs inspector.

"No," said Marta.

"Yes," said Vann.

"Not both at once," said Marta. "It confuses the child."

"I'm sixteen," said Nell.

"You're a child with excellent hearing," said Marta, "which is worse."

* * *

Ada let them go around twice more before she spoke again, because twenty years of bookkeeping had taught her that the first round of any argument was only the warm-up act, and the real numbers never appeared until someone got tired.

Marta wanted to run. That was simple enough, and it wasn't cowardice, whatever Nell thought. Marta had run from three countries and two marriages, and each time she had carried the household on her back like a smuggler carrying lace — folded small, hidden well, delivered intact. Running was how Marta loved people. She loved them by keeping them moving, ahead of the thing that meant them harm.

Vann wanted to hide. That surprised her less than it should have. A man with no country learns that the world is full of closets and that he fits in most of them. He talked about hiding the way other men talked about weather — practically, without shame. *We go dark for six weeks. I know a woman in the fishing quarter who rents rooms and asks nothing.* He had addresses the way Marta had exits. Ada wondered, not for the first time, what kind of life produced a man whose instinct in crisis was to become unfindable, and whether that life had been chosen or simply happened to him, the way weather happens.

And Nell — Nell, who had been ordered to bed twice and had ignored the order once and negotiated a compromise involving "just sitting here quietly," which in practice meant sitting there not quietly at all — Nell wanted none of it.

"We meet him," Nell said now, chin up, arms crossed in an imitation of Marta that would have been funnier if it weren't so accurate. "Because if we run, he follows. If we hide, he finds us. But if we meet him, then at least we're facing forward when it goes wrong."

"When did you start talking like a general?" Marta demanded.

"When everyone started talking about me like luggage." Nell's voice cracked on the last word, and Ada saw the girl hate herself for it, saw her jaw set against the crack, saw her push through anyway. "Everyone keeps saying *we'll decide what's safest for Nell*, and *Nell can't sail alone*, and *the berth should go to whoever needs it most* — but nobody has asked me anything except whether I want tea. Nobody has said my name and meant a person by it."

The kitchen went quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a shutter knocked against its frame in the wind off the harbor, patient as a metronome.

"That's not fair," Vann began gently.

"No," Ada said. "Let her finish. She's the only one at this table who hasn't been lying to us tonight, including me."

She hadn't planned to say that. It came out the way truth does — sideways, unwilled, wearing the face of an accident. Vann looked at her sharply. Marta's teacup paused halfway to her mouth. And Nell, who deserved better than to have an adult confession land in her lap like a dropped plate, blinked and said, with devastating fairness:

"Well. Obviously. Everyone in this house lies. Aunt Marta lies about being French. Mr. Vann lies about why he came. You lie about—" she gestured vaguely at Ada's whole self, "—everything, but politely."

"And what do you lie about?" Marta asked.

"Nothing yet," said Nell. "I'm saving up."

Despite everything — despite Brecht and berths and the ledger burning a hole in the floorboard under her bed — Ada laughed. It escaped before she could stop it, short and startled, and she saw it break something loose in the room: Vann's mouth twitching, Marta pressing her lips flat to keep hers from doing the same.

"There," Marta said. "Now we are all criminals together. This is what family means."

* * *

Vann watched them and thought: *this is what I don't have, and what I'd burn a country down to keep.*

He had sat in rooms where men argued over escape routes before. Those rooms smelled of cigarettes and fear, and the arguments were arithmetic — weight limits, fuel, bribes per head. This room smelled of stewed tea and burnt toast, and the argument was something else entirely. They weren't arguing about survival. They were arguing about each other. Every position anyone took was secretly a gift wrapped in stubbornness.

Marta wanted to run because running meant taking all of them, together, and she'd rather die tired than leave anyone behind — though she'd have bitten him if he said so aloud.

The girl wanted to meet Brecht because meeting Brecht meant staying near the two women who had raised her, and she'd rather face a colonel than be sent away like a parcel — though she dressed the wish up in strategy, because she was clever enough to know adults only listened to strategy.

And Ada —

Ada sat at the head of the table with her hands folded, listening to everyone with the terrible patience of a woman doing sums, and Vann realized with a cold slide in his stomach that she was already calculating. He knew that look. He'd worn it himself, once, in another life, when a man with money had explained exactly what Vann's debts would cost and exactly how Vann might pay them. It was the look of someone pricing things that shouldn't have prices. Berths. People. Herself.

"The berth," Ada said at last. "One berth. Let's say it plainly, since we're all confessing tonight. One verified former citizen sails in ten days. There are four of us, and possibly five if Tomas lives, which I am choosing to believe until someone shows me a body." Her voice didn't shake. Vann admired it and feared it in equal measure. "So the question isn't run, hide, or meet. The question is: who do we spend?"

"Don't say spend," said Nell.

"It's the accurate verb."

"It's a horrible verb."

"She's right, it's a horrible verb," said Vann, "and also it's the accurate one, which is the trouble with accuracy." He leaned forward. "Then let me spend mine first, since mine costs least. I'm not sailing. I have no papers that survive scrutiny, no citizenship anywhere, and a debt following me with Brecht's cologne on it. Put me in front of the door and consider me a rug."

"You're not a rug," said Nell.

"He's a very useful rug," said Marta. "But she's right, he isn't one. And neither is he expendable, whatever his self-esteem tells him." She turned to Vann, and her voice lost its armor for just a moment — long enough for him to see the old woman underneath, the one who had buried friends in three languages. "Young man. I have watched you for two weeks. You eat like a man apologizing for the food. You sleep with your shoes on. And every time Ada says something kind to you, you flinch as though kindness is a bill you haven't budgeted for. No one spends you. Not while I run this kitchen."

Vann found, to his considerable embarrassment, that his throat had closed. He covered it by reaching for the teapot. Marta let him.

"Then it's between Ada and Nell," he managed. "And Tomas, God willing."

"No," said Nell again — quieter now, the fight going out of her voice and leaving something rawer. "No, it's not. Because I've heard you all at night. I hear everything, I told you. And I know Aunt Ada thinks the berth is hers. I know she's already decided it. She does the dishes differently when she's decided something — faster, like she's punishing the plates."

Ada stared at her ward. "I beg your pardon."

"You wash angry when you've made a decision you think will hurt us," Nell said. "You've been washing angry for three days."

Marta snorted into her tea. Vann bit the inside of his cheek. Even Ada's stern composure wobbled, betrayed by dishwater testimony.

"This household," Ada said finally, "has entirely too much surveillance and not nearly enough discretion."

"We learned from the best," said Marta.

* * *

Later — after Nell had been escorted upstairs over her loud objections and her louder silence, after the lamps were trimmed to blue coals of light — Ada stood at the sink washing dishes that were already clean, because apparently she punished plates even when nobody was watching, and Vann dried beside her in a silence that had gotten comfortable somewhere along the way without either of them signing for it.

"You're giving her the berth," he said. Not a question.

"I'm considering all options."

"You're giving her the berth, and you'll dress it up as logistics, and you won't tell her until the gangway, because telling her sooner means watching her argue, and you'd rather carry the hurt yourself than watch her share it."

Ada's hands stilled in the water. For a moment the only sound was the harbor wind worrying the shutters.

"You've known me three weeks," she said.

"Long enough."

"Then you know the next thing I'm going to say."

"That you haven't decided anything," he said.

"And?"

"And that you're lying, but politely." He held out his hand for the plate. She gave it to him. Their fingers touched over the porcelain and neither of them mentioned it, which was its own kind of honesty.

At the doorway, Marta appeared in her nightgown and shawl like a verdict arriving early. "One more thing before we all pretend to sleep," she said. "I've been thinking about the ledger. About what else it might be carrying that we haven't read properly."

Ada frowned. "We've been through every page."

"Have we? Or have we been through every page *once*, quickly, the way frightened people read?" Marta's eyes moved from Ada to Vann and back, and Vann saw that the old woman had been thinking for hours — that beneath the running jokes and the tea-fury, some older machinery had been turning. "Codes are like houses, girl. You lived in yours your whole childhood and I promise you there are rooms you never opened. Your brother was cleverer than any of us. If I were Tomas, and I were writing a message meant to outlive prisons and censors and twenty years of silence—" she tapped the table once, softly, "—I would not put everything in the first layer."

Ada had gone pale in the lamplight. Vann watched her set down the plate with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might detonate.

"Marta," she said slowly, "what did you just say?"

"I said," Marta replied, pulling her shawl tighter, "that tomorrow morning, before anyone runs or hides or meets anyone, you are going to sit down at that typewriter and read your brother's cipher a second time. Slowly. Like a person expecting company."

Upstairs, faintly, a floorboard creaked — Nell, listening, always listening. And in the quiet that followed, Ada reached beneath the edge of the sink and drew out the old ledger half, holding it as if for the first time, her thumb resting on the margin where a few faint marks clustered like birds on a wire that no one, in twenty years, had thought to count.
