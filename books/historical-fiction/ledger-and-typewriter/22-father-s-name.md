## 22. Father's Name

Marta Yarrow had survived two wars, one revolution, a bad marriage, and forty years of her sister-in-law's cooking, and she had done it all by understanding one thing: a house is held up not by its beams but by the things nobody says in it.

She was thinking about this on Thursday morning while shelling peas at the kitchen table, because Nell had asked her a question at breakfast that had been rattling around inside her like a marble in an empty jar.

"Aunt Marta," Nell had said, spooning porridge with the solemnity of a magistrate, "who was Grandfather? Nobody ever talks about him. There aren't even any photographs."

And Ada had said, "Pass the salt, love," which was Ada's way of building a wall out of crockery, and Nell had passed the salt, and that should have been the end of it. But Marta had caught the girl's eye over the rim of her cup, and Nell had looked back at her with that flat, patient stare she'd inherited from no one — or possibly from everyone — and Marta had known: this wasn't finished. Nothing in this house was ever finished. It only went underground and waited, like bulbs.

Now she sat with her peas and her colander and listened to the sounds of the house. Upstairs, Ada's typewriter clattered and stopped, clattered and stopped — working, but badly. Vann was in the parlor pretending to read yesterday's paper, which he had already read twice; Marta could tell because he kept turning pages backward and frowning at them as though they'd changed. And Nell was supposed to be at her arithmetic lesson with Mrs. Pell next door, which meant Nell was wherever Nell wanted to be.

The house was full to bursting with things unsaid. Twenty years of them, stacked floor to ceiling like crates in a warehouse, and lately — since the letters arrived, since Vann came through the door with his charming ruined face and his pockets full of other people's dead — the whole stack had started to lean.

Marta shelled a pea, dropped it in the bowl, and thought: *I am seventy-one years old. I have buried a husband, a sister, and most of my illusions. I am too old to keep carrying other people's secrets up four flights of stairs every day.*

The trouble was, they weren't other people's secrets anymore. They were hers. She'd been holding them so long they had grown roots into her hands.

* * *

It had been raining the night she decided to tell, which was fitting, because rain always made her honest. Rain made everyone honest; that was half the trouble with coastal cities. The barometric pressure came down and took people's discretion with it.

Vann found her on the back step under the awning, where she'd gone with a cigarette she wasn't really smoking. She didn't smoke. She'd taken it up for three weeks in 1919 out of spite toward her late husband and never quite put it down as a habit of the hands.

"You're hiding from something," Vann said, settling onto the step beside her without asking, which would have annoyed her from anyone else and didn't from him. That was the thing about Elias Vann that she resented most: he had manners like a good coat — you could see they'd come off someone else, but they fit him anyway.

"I'm shelling peas," Marta said. "Peas don't shell themselves, whatever the modern world thinks."

"Ada's typing."

"She's always typing."

"No," Vann said gently. "She's *translating*. I heard the cipher rhythm. Two taps, pause, three taps." He looked at his own hands. "I know what that means now. I wish I didn't."

Marta studied him sideways. He was younger than he pretended — thirty-five, maybe, wearing forty the way a man wears a borrowed overcoat — and he was frightened, and he was decent underneath the fright, and both of those facts together made him dangerous in the ordinary way that decent frightened men are dangerous: they tell the truth at exactly the wrong moment.

"Mr. Vann," she said, "do you know why my niece keeps a household the way she does? Everything labeled. Everything locked. Supper at six-thirty whether there's a war on or not?"

"Because the war never ended for her."

"Don't flatter yourself with poetry. It ended. That's the tragedy of it — it *ended*, and she missed the ending, and so she's still standing at attention twenty years after the armistice." Marta snapped a pod and let the peas roll into her palm. "You want to know what she's guarding. Everyone who comes near her wants to know, and she can smell the wanting, and that's why she'll die alone in that upstairs room with her ledgers if somebody doesn't say some things out loud first."

"And you're the somebody?"

"I'm the somebody," Marta said, "because I'm the only one left who was there, and because I'm old enough now that the secret weighs more than the shame does. Do you understand that arithmetic? When you're young, the shame is heavier. You can't set it down anywhere without everyone seeing you do it. When you're old—" she gestured at the rain, the yard, the sagging line where the washing hung even now, in November, because Ada insisted laundry must be seen to be believed— "when you're old, the secret gets heavier every year and the shame stays the same weight, and eventually you just want to be able to stand up straight again."

Vann said nothing. He was good at that, she'd noticed. Most men filled silence the way children fill a pail at the sea — endlessly, uselessly. This one could hold a silence open like a door.

So Marta walked through it.

* * *

"My brother Josef — Ada's father — was not a brave man," she said. "I want that understood before anything else. People will tell you he was a coward, and they'll be wrong, and they'll also be right, and neither word will do him justice. He was a careful man. Careful the way a man is careful when he has something to lose and knows precisely how much of it there is. He counted everything. His money, his steps, his words. Ada counts everything too. You've seen it. Where do you imagine she got it?"

"The ledger habit."

"The ledger habit," Marta agreed. "He used to say a life unrecorded is a life that can be stolen. Well. Someone stole his anyway."

She told it plainly, because plainness was all she had left to give him and all she'd ever had, really.

In the second winter of the occupation, when the escape network was moving people south in ones and twos — forged papers, coded manifests, the whole apparatus that Tomas would later carry in his head and Ada would carry in her bones — the network needed safe houses. And Josef Yarrow had a house with a cellar, and a reputation for neutrality, and a wife (Marta's sister Lena, God rest her, stubborn as a bootjack) who believed absolutely in doing the right thing.

Josef did not believe absolutely in anything except keeping his family breathing.

"They came to him in January," Marta said. "Two officers. Not soldiers — officers, which matters, because soldiers take, but officers *trade*. They knew about the route — not all of it, just enough to make the offer poisonous. Keep your books clean, they said, and we'll keep our eyes dirty elsewhere. Look away from certain shipments. Let certain names pass through your cellar without being written down. In exchange: your family is invisible. Your name never appears on any list. You are a man who was never asked anything."

"And he said yes," Vann said quietly.

"He said nothing. That was worse and it was better. He simply began to look away. And here's the part that has lived in my chest for twenty years like a swallowed stone, Mr. Vann, so listen carefully." She turned to face him fully now, rain dripping off the awning edge between them. "One of the shipments he looked away from was the one that carried the route's real map. The names. The whole architecture. It went south through his district unremarked, and it went straight into Brecht's hands, and Brecht sold it, and eleven people died at the border and Tomas spent twenty years paying for the difference."

Vann had gone very still. Stiller than a man ought to be. "He knew? Josef knew what he'd looked away from?"

"Not then. Later. After the betrayal, when the survivors scattered and the story came north piece by piece — yes, he worked it out. I watched him work it out. I watched him sit at this very table—" she pointed through the window at the kitchen, at the table where Nell did her sums and Ada balanced her books— "and do the arithmetic, because he couldn't help doing arithmetic, it was the whole disease of him. And he understood that his carefulness had a body count."

"What happened to him?"

"He died in '41. Heart, officially." Marta allowed herself one dry note of humor, because humor was the load-bearing wall of her entire personality. "Unofficially, a man can die of a heart long before it stops beating. He spent his last two years waiting for the knock at the door. It never came — Brecht kept his bargain, you see, Brecht is scrupulous, Brecht always pays his debts — and so Josef wasted away in perfect safety, which is its own kind of horror. Lena died the year after. Grief, or shame, or the damp. Pick whichever comforts you."

"And Ada?"

"Ada doesn't know." Marta said it flatly, and watched it land. "She knows her father was cautious. She knows the family lost its standing after the war. What she doesn't know — what I have carried for twenty years, alone, because Lena made me swear it on her deathbed and because I loved those children more than I loved the truth — is that the route wasn't betrayed from outside. It was betrayed from inside their own kitchen. By their own father. With a shrug instead of a signature."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere down the street a tram complained its way around the corner.

"That's why the berths matter so much," Vann said slowly, and Marta saw him assembling it, quick mind, quicker than he liked to show. "That's why Ada gave hers up. At the evacuation. She—"

"She'd found a page. One page, in her father's effects, half-burned — he'd tried to destroy the evidence of his looking-away and botched it, careful man, careless once. She was nineteen. She read enough of it to understand the shape of what he'd done, and then she burned the rest, and she never told a living soul, and she has spent twenty years paying his debt in installments. Every silence. Every berth surrendered. Every name she refuses to say. She thinks she's protecting Tomas's memory, or the network's ghosts, or Nell. She isn't. She's serving out her father's sentence, and she doesn't even have the mercy of knowing what crime she's serving it for."

Marta realized her hands were shaking, just slightly, around the colander. Seventy-one years old and the secret still had teeth coming out.

* * *

"Why tell me?" Vann asked. "Why not her?"

Because, Marta thought, if I tell Ada, she will either collapse or go to Brecht tomorrow with the truth in her teeth and get herself killed for the privilege of honesty, and I have watched this woman carry enough.

What she said was: "Because she's going to find out soon regardless. That letter business, the harbor office, the ship — the past has stopped knocking at this door, Mr. Vann. It's coming through the walls. And when it arrives, someone in this house needs to have already said the hard part out loud, so she isn't hearing it fresh in front of enemies. You're leaving eventually — men like you always leave, it's practically your job description — so you'll be a witness that walks away instead of a wound that stays at the table." She paused. "Also, frankly, because you look like a man who understands owing debts to the dead."

Vann laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered. "Is it that obvious?"

"To a bookkeeper? You've been balancing an account since the day you walked in. Guilt is just arithmetic with worse posture."

They sat a while longer. The rain thinned. Upstairs, the typing had stopped altogether, which meant Ada had either solved something or broken something, and with Ada it was usually both.

"There's one more thing," Marta said, and here her voice did something she hadn't authorized — it softened. "You'll think less of me. Don't bother; I got there decades ago. I knew. From the beginning. Lena told me in the first spring, when the officers started appearing at market days and nodding to Josef like parishioners. I knew what my brother-in-law was, and I said nothing, because there were two children in that house who needed feeding and a roof, and the truth fed no one. I have told myself for twenty years that silence was protection." She looked out at the gray yard. "It was. It protected everyone except the people it was supposedly for. That's the trick of silences, Mr. Vann. They're always for us. We wrap them up and hand them over and call it a gift."

"And now?"

"Now I'm taking it back." She stood, joints announcing themselves in their usual order, hip, knee, dignity last. "Tomorrow, or the day after — when the moment comes, and it's coming, you can smell it in this house the way you smell snow — I'll tell her myself. All of it. Her father, my silence, the whole rotten inheritance. She deserves to hear it from someone who loved him and failed him in the same breath, not discover it in some officer's file."

"She may not forgive you."

"Oh, she won't forgive me," Marta said comfortably. "Ada forgives nothing; it's how she loves. But she'll *know*, and knowing is the only currency this family has ever actually traded in. We just keep forgetting to spend it until the shop's closing."

She gathered her colander and turned to go inside, and then stopped, because the kitchen window was dark and the door was open a hand's width, and standing in the gap, in her coat and school shoes, soaked to the shoulders and white as milk, was Nell.

Nell, who was supposed to be at Mrs. Pell's learning long division.

Nell, whose eyes went from Marta to Vann and back, and whose face did the terrible adult thing of arranging itself into blankness a half-second too late.

"How long," Marta said, very calmly, "have you been standing there?"

"Long enough," Nell said, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, fourteen years old and splitting down the seam, "to hear you say Grandfather betrayed everyone, and nobody ever told us, and Aunt Ada's been paying for it her whole life." She wiped her face with the back of her sleeve, furious at her own tears. "Was it true? Is any of it true? Because I've lived in this house my whole life and apparently I don't know *anything* about anybody in it."

Behind Marta, Vann rose slowly to his feet like a man watching the tide come in over sandcastles.

And down the hall, the stairs creaked.

Ada was coming down.
