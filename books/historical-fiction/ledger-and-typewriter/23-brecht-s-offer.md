## 23. Brecht's Offer

The summons came the way bad weather comes to a port city: first a change in the air, then everyone pretending not to notice.

For Ada Yarrow it arrived as an envelope of heavy cream stock, hand-delivered by a boy who looked about fourteen and terrified, who pressed it into her hands outside the counting-house at half past noon and said, "From the Colonel's office, ma'am," in the tone other boys used for "your house is on fire." Then he ran, which she thought showed excellent judgment.

She did not open it on the street. She had learned that much in twenty years — never let a street see your face change. She walked back into the counting-house, sat at her desk among the columns of other people's money, squared the envelope against the edge of the blotter until its edges were true, and only then broke the seal.

*Madam Yarrow,* it said, in a clerk's careful copperplate. *Colonel Brecht requests the pleasure of your company tomorrow at four o'clock, at the Hotel Meridian, Room 411. He believes you have matters of mutual interest to discuss, and he wishes to be of service regarding passage on the *Corinthian*. No questions will be asked. Respectfully, etc.*

*Respectfully, etc.* Even the clerk's pen had flinched.

Ada read it three times. The first time for content. The second time for what wasn't said. The third time because her hands were not quite steady and reading was better than noticing that.

He knew her name. He knew about the *Corinthian*, which meant he knew about the berths, which meant he had been watching the household long enough to know they'd been doing arithmetic at the kitchen table like a family planning a holiday instead of an escape. And he wanted to be *of service*. In Ada's experience — and her experience on this particular subject was deep, expensive, and twenty years old — men like Brecht were never of service. They extended credit. The interest came later, and it was always denominated in something you couldn't spare.

She put the letter in her handbag, behind her compact, and finished the afternoon's postings with a steadiness she was quietly proud of. Old Mr. Havel, whose eyes were failing and whose accounts were a scandal, thanked her twice for catching an error in his favor against a shipping firm. "You're an honest woman, Miss Yarrow," he said. "They're rarer than you'd think."

"No, Mr. Havel," she said. "We're exactly as common as you'd think. We just keep quieter company."

He laughed. She smiled. Inside, some small cold part of her was already packing a bag.

* * *

Marta heard the name before Ada had finished pouring the tea.

"Brecht." Her aunt set down the teapot with a click that was, for Marta, the equivalent of another woman throwing it through a window. "He sent you a letter. On cream paper. With his own seal."

"A clerk's seal, probably."

"On cream paper," Marta repeated, as though the stationery itself were an act of war. "Your mother used to say you could judge a man by how he asked for things. A good man asks. A bad man *invites*."

"And what does a man do when he summons?"

"He doesn't ask at all," Marta said. "He assumes the answer is yes and merely informs you of the time." She took her cup, drank, and looked out the window at the gray water beyond the rooftops. "I knew a Brecht once. Younger. Softer around the jaw. He wore his uniform like it was borrowed and returned everything he took with interest, and everyone said, what a reasonable man, what a civilized officer. Twenty years later I hear the name attached to a colonel who trades in secrets, and I think: the jaw hardened, but the reasonableness stayed. That's the worst kind. The ones who are still reasonable while they ruin you."

"Marta—"

"I'm not finished." She never raised her voice; she simply continued, and the room arranged itself around her. "You are not going alone. If you go at all."

"If I go at all," Ada agreed, which was not the same thing, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said so, because this household ran on the fuel of things politely unsaid, and it had gotten them remarkably far.

From the doorway, Nell said, "Go where?"

"Nell." Marta didn't turn. "How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough to know we're going somewhere and nobody wants to tell me." Nell came in and helped herself to a biscuit with the entitlement of youth and the watchfulness of someone who had grown up in this house and learned early that biscuits were safest taken during arguments, when the adults' attention was elsewhere. She was sixteen now, all elbows and questions, with Tomas's dark hair and — though no one ever said it aloud — Tomas's way of looking at people as if they owed her an explanation and she intended to collect.

"We're not going anywhere," Ada said.

"You got a letter today. Cream paper. You held it differently than bills." Nell bit the biscuit. "Also, a boy delivered it and then ran like the envelope bit him."

Ada exchanged a glance with Marta over the teapot. It lasted less than a second and contained an entire parliamentary session.

"The hotel," Nell said. "Room 411. Someone important. Someone who makes boys run." She swallowed. "It's about the ship, isn't it. The *Corinthian*. It's about the berths."

"How do you—" Marta began.

"I can count," Nell said. "There are three berths and four of us and Aunt Marta's papers are wrong and Vann goes pale whenever anyone says the word *passage*, and last night I heard Ada retyping pages at two in the morning when she thinks we're asleep." She brushed crumbs from her skirt with terrible calm. "I'm not a child. I'm the only person in this house who isn't pretending to be one."

The clock ticked. Somewhere below, the landlady's dog barked at a gull, as it had every day of its life, without success or hope.

"Tomorrow at four," Ada said finally, because lying to Nell had become, in the past month, less a protection than an insult. "Colonel Brecht has invited me to discuss the ship."

"Invited," Nell said.

"Summoned," Marta corrected.

"Same thing," said Nell, "when the man has soldiers."

* * *

Elias Vann read the letter that evening by the light of the kitchen lamp, and Ada watched his face do something complicated and quick, like a man shuffling cards behind his back.

"Room 411," he said. "The Meridian."

"You know it."

"I know the room." He set the letter down carefully, as if it might go off. "I've been in it. Last autumn. He receives people there because it's neutral ground — consular district, foreign staff, too many witnesses with diplomatic immunity to make anything messy. He'll have tea brought up. He always has tea brought up. He'll offer you a chair before he offers you terms, and he'll ask after your health, and he will mean none of it and be genuinely warm while meaning none of it. That's the trick of him. He's not cruel in the room. He saves cruelty for later, itemized."

"You speak as if you know him well."

Vann's jaw tightened. "I know his employments." He looked at her then, and whatever he was going to say next he decided against, and Ada let it go — not from kindness, but from strategy. She had learned that a man confesses in layers, like an onion, and if you pull too hard at any single layer you get nothing but stung eyes. Better to wait. Better to let him weep it out himself, in his own order.

"He'll offer me three berths," she said.

"He'll offer you three berths," Vann agreed. "No questions. That's the phrase he uses. *No questions asked.* It sounds like mercy. It's actually a receipt. It means: whatever you give me, I now own, and you'll never be able to prove otherwise."

"And if I refuse?"

"He doesn't waste refusals. He prices things exactly right, Ada. That's his genius. Not the threats — anyone can threaten. He finds the number you can't say no to and he offers precisely that, not a coin more, and lets you tell yourself it was your idea." Vann turned the letter face-down on the table, as though even the clerk's copperplate were watching. "Three berths. For a woman with a dead brother, a ward, and an aunt who can't sail. That's not a bribe. That's a mirror."

Ada stood at the window a long moment. Below, the harbor lay under its usual skin of fog, and somewhere out in that fog rode the *Corinthian*, white and certain, holding three berths with their names — or names enough like their names — already penciled onto a manifest.

Temptation, she thought, was supposed to arrive looking like sin. Nobody warned you it would come dressed as rescue, carrying tea, asking after your health.

* * *

She spent the evening doing what she always did with unbearable things: she filed them.

This was the discipline of her life, the one art she had mastered while everything else burned. Grief went in the drawer marked *later*. Fear went in the drawer marked *useful, pending*. Hope — hope was the dangerous one, the misfiled document, the thing that kept turning up in the wrong drawer and corrupting the whole system. She had buried hope once, on a pier, in 1938, and she had sworn never to let it back into the general correspondence.

And yet.

Three berths. Tomas — if the letters were true, if the handwriting was true, if the childhood cipher that only two children in a drowned country had ever known was true — Tomas alive, and reachable, and possibly within a day's walk of this very harbor. Three berths and four souls and a colonel offering to make the arithmetic kind.

*No questions asked.*

But that was the whole trouble, wasn't it. The ledger was not paper. It was questions — hundreds of them, typed in triplicate in a dead language of numbers, each line a name, each name a debt, and the largest question of all sitting in the third layer where she had found it three nights ago with her heart going like a trapped bird: her father's name, in her brother's hand, in the confession Tomas had written believing no one would live to read it.

Brecht wanted the remainder. Brecht wanted the questions closed forever.

And he was willing to pay in the only currency that had ever mattered to her.

Ada sat down at the typewriter — old Havel's castoff, temperamental, the *e* sticking — and rolled in a blank sheet, and did not type. She just sat, hands in her lap, listening to the foghorn out in the roads, patient and mournful, saying the same thing over and over to ships that could not answer.

*Here. Here. Here. Mind the rocks.*

* * *

At four o'clock the next afternoon, Room 411 of the Hotel Meridian smelled of lilies and strong coffee, and Colonel Brecht was exactly as advertised: silver at the temples, reasonable at the jaw, warm as a banked fire.

"Miss Yarrow." He rose, offered his hand, and held the shake one beat longer than courtesy required — long enough to communicate that he knew precisely who she was, and wished her to know that he knew. "You'll take coffee? The hotel's is terrible but the cups are lovely. Sit, please. You've had a long walk and I imagine a longer twenty years."

Everything about him was calibrated. His accent was the old country's, softened just enough to seem nostalgic rather than loyal. His uniform was nowhere in evidence — civilian tweed, the wolf in weekend clothes. On the table between them lay a leather folder, squared to the table edge, and Ada recognized the gesture because it was her own: a man who squared his papers was a man who feared disorder more than death.

"You received my note," he said.

"I received your note."

"And yet you came. That interests me. Most people in your position would have burned it." He poured the coffee himself, waving off the bellhop, and handed her the lovely cup. "Most people in your position are afraid. But you walked here through the consular district in broad daylight, alone, having told — I'm guessing — exactly one person where you were going, and probably not even that. You came because you wanted to look at me. To price me, the way I've priced you." He smiled, and it was almost charming. "So. What's your assessment?"

"That you use cream paper for threats," Ada said, "and that your clerk is more frightened than your enemies, which tells me everything about how you run an office."

Brecht laughed — a real laugh, briefly, before the shutters came down again. "Oh, you're your mother's daughter. Everyone said so. Marta said so especially, in the old days, before she stopped speaking to me." He let that sit in the air like smoke. "Shall we talk about the ship?"

"If you like."

"The *Corinthian* sails in eight days. There are berths — verified berths, stamped papers, the kind that survive inspection — for three former citizens of your unfortunate republic. I control the list. This is not a boast; it is simply my job, as it has been my job for twenty years, to know who leaves and who stays." He opened the leather folder, turned it toward her, and inside, neat as a hymnal, lay three documents with photographs waiting to be affixed. Blank photographs. Waiting. "Three berths. No questions asked. For the ledger."

"Not for the ledger," Ada said. "Say it properly. For the *remainder*. You already have half of it — you've had half of it since the evacuation, or you'd never have known to want the rest. You want the half that names people. You want the half that names *you*."

Something flickered behind his eyes — respect, she thought, horribly, and hated herself for recognizing it.

"Tomas wrote it," Brecht said quietly. "In prison. Did you know that? He was very thorough. Your brother had a gift for thoroughness. It runs in the family, apparently." He folded his hands. "Here is what I admire about you, Miss Yarrow, and I say this as a professional: you carried those codes out of a collapsing country at nineteen years old, you memorized what couldn't be carried, you burned what couldn't be memorized, and you kept silent for twenty years in a city full of people who talk for a living. That is discipline. That is *character*." He leaned forward. "So I'm not going to insult you with threats. I'm going to offer you exactly what you want, priced exactly right, and I'm going to let you decide like the adult you've been forced to be since you were a girl."

He slid the folder across the table until it touched her fingertips.

"Three berths. Your aunt's difficulties with her papers — solved. The young translator's debts to certain parties — dissolved; consider him paid in full and off my books. The girl travels as your ward, no scrutiny. And your brother—" he paused, delicately, "—is closer than you think, and in my custody, and his comfort over the next eight days will depend entirely on the smoothness of our transaction. No questions asked, Miss Yarrow. Not of you. Not ever. You sail, your family sails, the past stays sunk where it belongs, and everyone lives."

The coffee cup trembled, very slightly, in her lovely saucer.

Because that was the devilry of it. He hadn't threatened. He hadn't gloated. He had laid out her own heart on the table and priced it fairly — three berths, one brother, one aunt, one child, one cowardly translator forgiven — and the price was only this: that the truth stay buried one more generation. That the names in the third layer rot unspoken. That her father's collaboration, her brother's confession, Brecht's own tidy treachery, all of it sink with the ledger into the same gray water that had taken everything else.

Silence, purchased at last, at retail.

"You'll want time to consider," Brecht said, rising, signaling the end of the audience with the ease of a man who had never once been refused. "Take the folder. Show them. Discuss it as a family — I understand you're fond of discussion." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and looked back with something almost like kindness. "One thing, Miss Yarrow, professionally to professionally. Whatever else you're considering — and you are, I can hear the typewriter from here — don't mistake my reasonableness for patience. The list closes Friday. And Tomas is aging poorly in custody. Men like your brother don't do well in rooms without windows."

The door clicked shut behind him.

Ada sat alone in Room 411 among the lilies, the folder square and heavy in her lap, three blank photographs waiting like empty frames for faces she loved. Through the window, far below, the harbor fog was lifting, and the *Corinthian* stood out white against the gray water, close enough to touch, impossibly far.

She thought: *he has given me exactly what I cannot refuse.*

She thought: *therefore it is a trap, and therefore I must find the flaw in the pricing, because there is always a flaw, and finding it is the only thing I have ever been good at.*

Then she picked up her handbag, walked out past the frightened clerk, and went home to tell her family the price of their salvation — knowing all the while that somewhere across the city, Elias Vann was due at Room 411 himself, to collect the payment he had never told her about.
