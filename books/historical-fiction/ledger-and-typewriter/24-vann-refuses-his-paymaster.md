## 24. Vann Refuses His Paymaster

The Hotel Meridian had once been grand, in the way that certain men are once handsome — you could see the bones of it under the sag, the marble lobby still pretending it hadn't been subdivided into a card room and a dentist's office. Elias Vann stood across the street from it for eleven minutes by his own count, which was ten minutes longer than any sensible man would have stood, and he spent those minutes rehearsing.

He was good at rehearsals. It was, if he were honest — and he was trying very hard these days to be honest, at least in the privacy of his own skull — the only talent he had ever cultivated with real devotion. He could rehearse a greeting until it sounded spontaneous. He could rehearse an apology until it sounded sincere. He could rehearse a betrayal until it sounded like a favor, which was precisely what he had done for Colonel Brecht these past months, and what he intended to stop doing in approximately four minutes' time.

The trouble with rehearsals, he thought, watching a bellhop wrestle a trunk through revolving doors that had no business containing it, is that they never survive contact with the other person. The other person always brings lines of their own.

He crossed the street. His heart did something undignified that he chose not to narrate even to himself.

* * *

Room 411 smelled of cold coffee and cigar smoke that had given up on being luxurious and settled for being present. Brecht sat by the window in shirtsleeves, reading a newspaper as though the world's events required his personal supervision, and gestured Vann to a chair without looking up.

Vann sat. He had learned, in the army of a country that no longer existed, that the man who stays standing in another man's room has already conceded something. So he sat, and folded his hands, and waited out the newspaper with the patience of a man who had once waited out a border inspection while carrying forged papers in his boot lining and a ham sandwich in his coat pocket, and had eaten the sandwich during the wait, which he still considered his finest hour.

"You look tired, Vann." Brecht folded the paper. His voice was pleasant in the way that well-kept weapons are pleasant: oiled, balanced, made for one purpose only. "Sit in the sun. Take a walk along the harbor. You have ten days of freedom left; I'd hate for you to spend them all indoors."

"I've come about the arrangement."

"Good. Is she compliant?"

"She is not compliant." Vann heard his own voice and was mildly surprised by it — steady, unhurried, almost bored. Rehearsal, then, holding after all. "She is frightened, and she is clever, and she will come to your harbor office on her own terms or not at all. Which is why the arrangement is off."

Brecht's expression did not change. That was the terrible thing about him, Vann thought. Other men's faces performed. Brecht's face simply *was*, like furniture.

"Off," Brecht repeated.

"Off. Finished. I'm withdrawing."

"You are withdrawing." Brecht set the paper down on the table between them with great care, aligning its edge with the edge of the table, the way a man aligns things when he wants you to notice how calm he is. "May I ask what changed? A month ago you stood in this room and told me you could deliver the woman, the ledger, and a family history going back three generations. You were charming. You were specific. Specificity is rare in this trade; I valued it."

"A month ago I was paid to carry letters," Vann said. "I've since read them."

"Ah." Something moved behind Brecht's eyes — not warmth, but the recognition of warmth, the way a customs officer recognizes contraband without approving of it. "The brother. Tomas Yarrow. Alive, as far as anyone knows, in a detention barracks two kilometers from Pier Nine. Yes. I know what the letters say. I have known for some time. It's why I'm letting him stay alive, Vann. He is worth more breathing than dead. A living brother is a lever. A dead one is merely a grievance, and grievances don't open safes."

"There it is," Vann said quietly. "You see? That's the whole speech. That's the entire architecture of you, laid out on a table like a watchmaker's bench. And I sat across from it for a month and told myself I was only carrying mail."

"You were carrying mail. Well compensated mail." Brecht leaned back. "Your debts haven't vanished because your conscience has woken up. Your papers remain forged. Your name remains attached to a border incident in March that I can produce witnesses for. When I say your warrant exists, Vann, I am not describing a mood. There is a file. It has a number. Men with numbered files do not emigrate on repatriation ships; they disembark at the first port and meet gentlemen who ask questions in small rooms."

"I know how files work."

"Do you?" Brecht smiled — actually smiled, briefly, and it was worse than the furniture face. "Then let me tell you how this one works. You deliver Ada Yarrow and the ledger to my office before the ship sails. In exchange, your file loses its number and gains a clean page. Or you withdraw — your word — and the file proceeds, and I find another courier, because couriers are like taxis, Vann. There is always another one idling around the corner. The only person your little act of principle inconveniences is you."

"That's more or less what I'd calculated."

"Then why?"

And there it was. The question Vann had rehearsed seventeen answers to, standing under the awning across the street while the bellhop fought the trunk, and every answer had sounded like a speech, and speeches were exactly the currency he was trying to stop counterfeiting.

So he said the unrehearsed thing.

"Because she made me soup."

* * *

He hadn't meant to say it. He saw immediately that he was going to have to explain it, and explaining it was going to be either humiliating or true, possibly both, so he pressed on before his sense of dignity could reassert itself.

"It sounds absurd. It was absurd. Tuesday evening. Marta had gone out, Nell was doing homework upside down off the arm of the sofa, and Ada came home late from the office looking like a woman who had been carrying the twentieth century on her back, and she didn't say anything dramatic. She just went to the kitchen and made soup, four bowls, and put one down in front of me without asking whether I wanted it. As if I were — " He stopped. Started again. "As if I were someone who belonged at that table. And I sat there eating soup that I had no right to, knowing I was being paid to hand her over to a man who keeps her brother alive the way a farmer keeps a hen alive, and I thought: this is the most dishonest meal of my life, and I have eaten meals with ambassadors."

Brecht listened with his head slightly tilted, the way one listens to a foreign language one almost speaks.

"You're in love with her," he said at last. "That's the whole confession. How tedious. Love is the most common motive in my files, Vann. It doesn't make men brave; it makes them stupid in predictable directions."

"It isn't love. Or — " Vann waved a hand. "It may be. I'm forty-one years old and stateless; I've misplaced the manual. But it doesn't matter what we call it. What matters is that I sat at her table and took her bread, and I am not going to stand in your office and sell her the way I sold everyone else who ever fed me. That's the whole withdrawal. You can write it in the file. *Courier declined, citing soup.*"

For a long moment the only sound was the radiator ticking, counting down to nothing.

Then Brecht laughed. Not loudly — a short, genuine bark of amusement, quickly folded away, but real, and somehow that was the most frightening sound in the room yet.

"You know what I admire about you, Vann? Nothing. But I recognize you. I was you, thirty years ago — a clever young man with no country and too much charm, deciding each morning which lie would keep him alive longest. The difference between us is that I stopped apologizing for it and prospered." He rose, straightening his cuffs. "So. Let us be practical, since you've decided to be noble and nobility loves practicality. You have ten days. If the ledger reaches my office, all is forgiven — I mean that literally; forgiveness is simply bookkeeping done promptly. If it does not, your file opens on the eleventh day, and I should warn you: I've already sent word to the port authority that a translator named Vann may attempt to board under false documents. They'll be watching for you regardless of what happens here."

"You've burned me either way."

"I've insured myself either way. Burning is just insurance with better posture." Brecht extended his hand, an old habit of courtesy, and when Vann didn't take it, withdrew it without offense. "Go home, translator. Eat some more soup. Ten days is a long time. Men change their minds about principles at roughly the same rate they change them about women, which is to say: whenever the arithmetic turns against them."

Vann stood. At the door he paused, because there was one more thing, and he found he wanted to say it — not as a rehearsal, not as a performance, but plainly, the way Nell asked questions and Marta gave warnings: people who meant what they said.

"He's two kilometers away," he said. "Tomas Yarrow. You're keeping him in a barracks near the pier like a deposit against a debt, and you call yourself a businessman, and maybe you are. But I've met his sister now, and I've read his letters, and I want you to understand that the family you're trading in writes everything down. Codes, confessions, ledgers. Somewhere in this city there is a piece of paper with your name on it, Colonel, and paper is patient. Paper waits."

"Is that a threat?"

"No. Threats are transactions. That's just weather." He opened the door. "Forecast says storm."

* * *

The corridor of the Hotel Meridian was dim and carpeted in a red so old it had gone the color of dried wine, and Vann walked down it feeling lighter than a man in his position had any right to feel, which worried him, because lightness in his experience was usually the sensation of something essential falling away.

He counted the cost on the stairs, itemized, like Ada would have done — he caught himself thinking *like Ada would have done* and let the thought stand, unchallenged, which was itself new.

Item: one career as a deniable courier, deceased as of 4:40 that afternoon. Item: whatever clean pages Brecht had promised, revoked. Item: a file with a number, opening in ten days unless a miracle occurred, and Vann had stopped believing in miracles somewhere around the fall of his second country and started believing instead in typewriters, stubborn women, and soup. Item, largest of all: he had just informed a colonel of military intelligence that paper with the man's name on it existed, thereby telling Brecht — the one thing Vann had sworn to himself he would not reveal — that the remainder of the ledger named him. He had walked in intending to refuse the deal and had walked out having handed the enemy a reason to move faster.

*Paper is patient,* he'd said. *Paper waits.*

Brecht would not wait. Brecht would accelerate. Every timetable Vann knew — the exchange, the ship, the careful ten-day arithmetic the household had been performing at the kitchen table with pencils and hope — was now wrong, and he was the one who had shortened it, and the only people who could be warned were a bookkeeper, a sharp-tongued aunt, a girl who noticed everything, and a brother in a barracks who couldn't be reached at all.

He pushed out through the revolving doors into the evening air, where the gulls were conducting their eternal audit of the fish market, and stood on the steps a moment, doing sums.

Ten days had become fewer. Perhaps five. Perhaps three.

He turned toward Ada's street and began to walk, fast, rehearsing nothing at all — just the plain sentence he would say at her door, the truth, unpolished, arriving late: *I took their money, I lied to you, and today I refused them, and it has cost us time we may not have.*

Behind him, on the fourth floor of the Hotel Meridian, a telephone was already ringing.

Across town, in a house where a girl was supposed to be doing her homework, Nell was not doing her homework.
