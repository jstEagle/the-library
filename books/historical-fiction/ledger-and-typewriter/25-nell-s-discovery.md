## 25. Nell's Discovery

Nell had three theories about the locked drawer in Ada's desk, and she ranked them the way a scientist ranks hypotheses: by elegance.

Theory one: love letters. Elegant, but unlikely — Ada Yarrow did not blush, and Nell had spent nine years conducting a rigorous study of when and whether she did. The answer was never. Not once. Even the time Aunt Marta had told the story about the bishop and the mule at Christmas dinner, which had made even *Marta* pause for effect, Ada's face had remained a closed ledger of its own.

Theory two: money. Practical, but boring. If Ada were hiding money, it would be in the flour tin, because Marta checked the flour tin weekly with the devotion of a woman who believed all sin entered the house through baking supplies.

Theory three: papers. Important ones. This was the least elegant theory and therefore, Nell suspected, the true one. The universe, in her experience, preferred the ugly answer every time.

The drawer itself was unremarkable to look at — same scarred oak as the rest of the desk, same brass pull gone dull as an old coin. What made it remarkable was the keyhole, and what made the keyhole remarkable was that Nell had once, at age eleven, watched Ada open it with a small flat key kept not in any pocket or jewelry box but taped inside the false bottom of her sewing basket, beneath the pincushion, because nobody on earth voluntarily looked inside a sewing basket. It was, Nell thought now, the cleverest thing Ada had ever done, and also the saddest, because a person only hid things that well if she had spent her whole life being hunted by someone patient.

She had never opened the drawer. She wanted to be clear about that, later, when everything came out. She had *never* opened it. She had merely, over four years, assembled a complete map of its contents based on shadows through the keyhole and the sounds the paper made when Ada lifted the bundle out — usually late at night, usually after a letter arrived, usually followed by the particular silence Ada made instead of crying. Nell knew that silence the way other girls knew their mothers' footsteps on the stairs.

But tonight the drawer was already unlocked.

That was the thing she would keep returning to afterward. She hadn't picked it, hadn't coaxed it, hadn't done anything clever at all. She had come into the parlor after supper to fetch her library book, and there was Ada standing at the window with her back turned, and the desk drawer hanging open like a mouth mid-sentence, and Ada so far away in whatever country her thoughts had emigrated to that she might as well have been on the moon.

On top of the papers lay a telegram, face up, and Nell read exactly two words before Ada turned around and Nell became extremely interested in her library book, which was about lighthouses and which she could not have summarized under threat of death just then.

*Alive. Held.*

Two words. She carried them out of the parlor like contraband, pressed flat against the inside of her ribs.

* * *

She didn't sleep. This was not unusual — Marta said she'd been born with her eyes already asking questions and they simply hadn't stopped since — but tonight the insomnia had a job to do, and the job was arithmetic.

Tomas Yarrow was dead. Everyone agreed on this the way everyone agrees on weather. Dead twenty years. There had been a funeral without a body, which even at six Nell had understood was the saddest kind of arithmetic: one brother, minus one evacuation, equals a stone with a name and nothing underneath. Ada had raised her from that year onward, and in all those years Nell could count on one hand the times Ada had spoken Tomas's name aloud, and on zero fingers the times she'd spoken it without first checking the room the way you check for wasps.

And yet. *Alive. Held.*

Held meant held *by someone*. Someone was holding a man for twenty years the way you hold a debt or a weapon, and the someone, according to the shape of the last week — Vann arriving with his beautiful ruined accent and his letters, Marta going gray around the mouth whenever a certain colonel's name surfaced, Ada typing past midnight with the windows shuttered — the someone was connected to all of it, and all of it was connected to the drawer.

At half past one, Nell got up.

The house at night had its own geography. The third stair creaked; she skipped it out of habit though no one was listening. Ada's door was shut, lamplight dead beneath it. Marta snored downstairs in the rhythm of a woman who slept like a fortress garrisoned by one very tired soldier. And Vann — Vann slept at the end of the hall in the little room that had been Nell's before she'd been promoted to the attic, and he slept badly; she could hear him turning over, turning over, a man trying to find a position in which his conscience didn't dig into his hip.

The sewing basket sat where it always sat, beside Ada's chair. Nell lifted the pincushion. The tape peeled back with a sound like a small apology.

The key was cold and lighter than she expected. That was her first surprise: that the object guarding twenty years of secrets should weigh nothing. Her second surprise came thirty seconds later, kneeling in the dark with the drawer open, when she found that the bundle on top — the letters in the dead man's handwriting, the telegram — was not the secret at all. It was the *wrapper*. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth tied with string, was something older, heavier, bound in a cracked leather cover that had been scorched along one corner, burned black, the way paper burns when you hold it to a flame long enough to make a point.

She untied the string. She wants it recorded that her hands shook, but that they did it anyway.

* * *

It was a ledger. Half of one — the pages began somewhere in the middle, numbers running down in columns, and between the columns, letters. Not business letters. A code. Groups of figures, each one standing for a letter of something else, the whole page looking like a ledger that had dreamed of being a different book entirely.

Nell was not stupid about codes. She and her father — before, in the before-time she was not supposed to remember but did — used to play cipher games at the kitchen table while her mother laughed at them both. He'd taught her the simple substitution trick: find the most common symbol, guess it's E, work outward like unraveling a sweater. So she sat cross-legged on the parlor floor with the lamp turned low and the ledger open across her knees, and she worked.

It took her an hour to get the first sentence, and when she got it, she wished she hadn't.

*If this reaches no one, let it at least be written,* the decoded line said. *Names are heavy. I have carried mine too long in silence.*

She looked at the name signed below — a name she knew from the headstone, from the photograph on the mantel of a laughing young man with Ada's exact eyebrows — and her throat went tight. Because it wasn't the dead man's hand. She'd seen the letters Vann brought; she'd peeked, shamelessly, days ago, and she knew that handwriting, slanted and impatient, crossing its t's like fence posts. This was different. Rounder. Older. Careful where the letters were careless.

This was Ada's hand. Ada, writing in cipher. Ada, copying something out — no, not copying. *Continuing.* The entries changed character partway through, and Nell, who had read enough of Ada's account books to know her habits, recognized the seam. Someone else had begun this record. Ada was keeping it alive.

She read on. She should not have read on. She read on the way water reads downhill.

There were names. Dozens of them. Names with dates, names with routes — safe houses, couriers, crossings — and beside some of the names, in the newer hand, single devastating words: *lost. taken. sold.* One column tracked money paid to a man whose designation was only ever *B*, and the payments stopped the same week, according to the dates, that a whole network of people stopped existing. Nell did the arithmetic she was best at and felt it go through her like cold water: B had been paid to keep the road open, and had instead sold the map of it.

And then, near the end, in the newest ink, the freshest entries, she found a passage that was not cipher at all but plain words, written small and pressed hard, the way a person writes when they expect to burn the page:

*I was on the list for the ship. One berth. My brother put me on it himself and stayed behind to carry the ledger out by the longer road. He trusted the route. He trusted the man who ran it. I have spent twenty years deciding which betrayal is worse — theirs, for selling us, or my father's, for helping them buy the list of names in the first place. I chose silence because the truth had too many floors and I could not decide which one to fall from.*

Nell read the word *father* three times. Her grandfather — the stern man in the other photograph, the one Marta dusted but never spoke to — her grandfather had helped? Had helped *them*, whoever they were, the men whose payments filled column B?

The clock in the hall struck two. Somewhere upstairs, Vann turned over again, and the bedframe groaned, and Nell sat very still in the dark parlor with a dead man's ledger open on her knees and understood, all at once and completely, why Ada checked rooms for wasps, why she kept a key in a sewing basket, why her silences had walls you could lean against and never hear anything on the other side.

Childhood, it turned out, did not end loudly. Nobody slammed a door. It ended the way a lamp dims — you're sitting in the same chair, reading the same book, and gradually you notice you can't see the page anymore, and the dark has been coming on for years, and only tonight did you finally look up.

She was still sitting there when the floorboard behind her creaked.

* * *

Ada stood in the doorway in her nightdress with her cardigan over her arm, and for a moment neither of them moved, and Nell thought absurdly of the time she'd been caught stealing jam at seven and how much simpler the punishments of childhood had been.

Ada crossed the room. She didn't snatch the ledger — Nell had braced for that — she simply knelt down beside her, took it gently from her hands, and looked at the open page, at the paragraph in plain ink, at the word *father*.

"You read it," Ada said. It wasn't a question, and her voice wasn't angry. It was tired in a way Nell had never heard, tired all the way down to the foundations.

"The drawer was open," Nell said. "I'm sorry. I'm not sorry. Both."

"Yes." Ada almost smiled. "Both is usually correct." She closed the ledger and held it against her chest, and for a while they sat together on the parlor floor like two people sheltering from weather that was indoors.

"Is it true?" Nell asked. "About Grandfather?"

Ada was quiet long enough for the clock to tick eleven times. "It's true," she said. "Most of it. Some of it isn't mine to tell."

"Marta's part?"

"Marta's part." Ada reached out and tucked a strand of hair behind Nell's ear, an old gesture, one Nell had privately outgrown years ago and secretly never wanted discontinued. "You understand now why we don't talk about certain things."

"I understand you've been keeping a bomb in your desk," Nell said, "and wrapping it in my brother's letters—" She stopped. Corrected herself. "In *Tomas's* letters. Which aren't from a dead man, are they. *Alive. Held.* I saw the telegram."

Something moved through Ada's face then, fast, like a bird crossing a window — hope, fear, both wearing each other's coats. "You saw—"

"I see everything," Nell said. "You've known this for years. You named me a witness at Marta's dinner table when I was eight."

"I named you a menace at Marta's dinner table when you were eight."

"Witness-menance. Either way." Nell pulled her knees up. The cold of the floorboards had gotten into her, but she wasn't going anywhere. "Ada. Who's holding him?"

Ada looked at her for a long moment — measuring, Nell realized, not whether to lie but how much truth a girl of fourteen could carry. And then Ada seemed to take stock of the girl on the floor, the one holding a cipher-solved ledger's worth of evidence that she could carry quite a lot, and something in her posture changed, some wall quietly decommissioned.

"A colonel," Ada said. "A man who sells people's histories. And tomorrow, or the day after, he is going to try to buy ours." She rose, and offered her hand, and pulled Nell up off the floor. "Go to bed, Nell."

"Will you tell me the rest? All of it? Not the nursery version?"

"When there's a rest worth telling," Ada said. "Sleep."

Nell went. But at the bottom of the stairs she paused, because the front door latch — which she had locked herself at ten o'clock, a habit Marta had drilled into her — was turning, softly, from outside.

The door opened on Vann, hatless, coat collar up, breathing hard, with the look of a man who has run the last three streets and lost the argument he left the house to win. He shut the door behind him, leaned his forehead briefly against it, and only then saw them standing there in the dark hallway, Ada with the ledger clutched to her chest, Nell in her nightgown, both of them lit by the streetlamp through the frosted glass.

"They've moved up the exchange," Vann said. His voice was steady; his hands weren't. "Brecht knows. Everything. And Ada — the ship sails in four days. Not ten."
