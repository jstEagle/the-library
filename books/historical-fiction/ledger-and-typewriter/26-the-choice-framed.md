## 26. The Choice Framed

The kitchen clock said half past eleven when Ada Yarrow sat down at the table with a fresh sheet of paper, a pencil sharpened to a point that could have served a surgical purpose, and the particular expression of a woman about to do arithmetic she already knew would not come out even.

Three berths. Four people. Possibly five, if Tomas lived — and the telegram had said *held near the harbor*, which was a phrase built out of hope and barbed wire in equal measure.

She wrote at the top of the page, in her small bookkeeper's hand: **Assets**.

Under it: *One ledger (original, surrendered value: leverage). One ledger (copy, retyped, portable). One set of verified citizen's papers — mine. One set of forged papers — Vann's, good enough to fool a tired clerk and no one else. One aunt who cannot sail. One ward who does not know yet that she is a line item.*

She looked at that last entry for a long moment and then crossed out *line item* and wrote *person*, because Nell would have known, somehow, across the city, if Ada had written otherwise. The girl had a talent for it. She could sense being counted the way other people sensed weather coming in their knees.

*Persons,* then. Four of them. Three berths.

Ada turned the pencil over and began, as she always did when the numbers refused to behave, with the plan that was easiest to write down and hardest to live inside.

---

**Plan One**, she titled it. *The Honest Distribution.*

It went like this: Tomas took the first berth, because he had spent twenty years paying for a betrayal he hadn't committed, and because a man held near a harbor as leverage against a ledger deserved to be the first thing removed from the scales. Vann took the second, because his forged papers would eventually be his death warrant if Brecht's men came looking, and they would come looking; Vann had told her as much with the cheerful fatalism of a man describing someone else's funeral. Nell took the third, because she was seventeen and the whole point of everything — the silences, the burned pages, the twenty years of quiet — had been to buy exactly one person an unburdened life.

And Ada stayed.

She wrote it cleanly. No flourishes. It read like an invoice, which was appropriate, because that was what it was: the settling of a twenty-year account. She had given up a berth once before, on the night of the evacuation, and look how well the world had repaid the gesture. But there was a difference, she told herself, between a berth given up in panic and one given up in arithmetic. The first was fear. The second was bookkeeping. And Ada Yarrow trusted bookkeeping the way other people trusted God.

There was only one flaw in Plan One, and it was the same flaw that had ruined every honest distribution she'd ever attempted: Marta.

Marta could not sail — her papers named her a collaborator's widow, and the word *collaborator* sat on those documents like a stain that no amount of scrubbing would lift. But Marta would not be left behind gracefully. Marta did not do anything gracefully. If Ada stayed ashore, Marta would stay too, out of pure spiteful love, and then the household would be two women in a port city with a colonel's grudge against them and no ship on the horizon, and Nell would spend the rest of her life reading letters from strangers and wondering.

Ada read Plan One through twice. Then she held its corner to the stove burner and watched the arithmetic curl into black lace and go out.

The smoke smelled like the old days. She opened the window anyway.

---

Nell's light was still on across the hall. Ada could see the seam of it under the door, thin as a blade. The girl was awake, then — had been awake all evening, since the telegram came, sitting at dinner with her fork parked beside her plate like a soldier's rifle while the adults talked around her in the coded language families develop when the truth is too heavy to lift at the table.

*Held near the harbor.* Nell had heard it. Nell heard everything. Ada had once found her own missing thimble in Nell's room, along with a notebook in which the girl had recorded, in a hand disconcertingly like Tomas's, the comings and goings of every visitor to the house for the past four months, including — underlined twice — *Mr. V lies about where he gets his shoes polished.*

Ada knocked softly. "You should sleep."

"I'm reading." A pause. "I'm not reading."

"Then you're thinking, which is worse for the complexion."

A small laugh through the door, and then, quieter: "Are we really getting him back? Uncle Tomas?"

*Uncle.* Twenty years dead and the girl had made him an uncle anyway, out of photographs and Ada's accidental slips and the family resemblance in Nell's own stubborn chin. Ada put her palm flat against the door, the way she used to when Nell was small and afraid of thunder.

"We are going to try," she said. "That's what I'm doing right now. Trying is loud work. It needs the whole night."

Another pause. "Don't burn any more paper," Nell said. "It smells like funerals."

Ada stood in the hallway until the light went out, and then she went back downstairs and took out a second sheet.

---

**Plan Two** she called *The Bargain*, though privately she thought of it as *The Coward's Ledger*, because it was the plan that let everyone live and cost only the thing she'd been protecting for twenty years: the secret itself.

In Plan Two, Ada walked into Brecht's office at the harbor and gave him the original ledger — the true one, the one naming every traitor including, God forgive them both, her father — in exchange for three berths and Tomas walking free. No newspapers. No exposure. No truth. Brecht got his secrets buried, the family got their ship, and the past stayed past, which was, after all, the arrangement the twentieth century seemed to prefer.

It was a good plan. It was the plan a sensible woman would choose. It kept everyone alive, and it kept Marta's name from ever being spoken aloud in a courtroom, and it kept Nell from learning what her grandfather had done, and it kept Ada from having to stand in front of strangers and say the words *my father sold the route* in a voice that carried.

It also meant Brecht walked away whole. It meant the courier who died carrying Tomas's letters stayed a nameless dead man. It meant every silence Ada had ever kept got to keep itself forever, growing fat and comfortable in the dark.

She caught herself, halfway through writing it, doing the thing she despised most in others: dressing surrender up as prudence. *Discretion,* she had written. She crossed it out. *Silence bought at full price,* she wrote instead, and the honesty of it stung so much that she almost stopped there.

But she finished the plan. She owed it that much. A woman who burned a plan without reading it properly was just a woman burning paper.

Plan Two went into the stove. This time she watched it longer, all the way down, because some things deserved a witness.

---

The kettle had gone cold twice by now. Ada made tea anyway, standing at the counter while it steeped, looking out at the harbor lights through the gap in the curtains. Somewhere down there, in a warehouse or a barracks or a locked room above a chandler's office, Tomas was alive. Twenty years she had mourned him with the specific grief of the certain, and it turned out certainty was just ignorance wearing a black armband.

She thought about the letters. *If my sister can still be reached.* He had written them believing she might be reached, which meant he had never believed she was dead, which meant that somewhere in a cell all these years, Tomas had carried a faith in her that she had never once carried in him. That was the debt that didn't fit in any column. You couldn't balance it. You could only try to be worth it.

Vann had said something earlier that week, in his offhand translator's way, that had lodged in her like a splinter. They'd been arguing about the copy ledger — whether retyping it was madness or insurance — and he'd said, waving his cigarette, "You know what your trouble is, Mrs. Yarrow? You think sacrifice is a currency. You keep trying to pay for things with yourself." And then he'd laughed at his own cleverness and asked for more tea, but Ada had gone very still inside, the way one does when a stranger reads your private correspondence aloud in public.

He wasn't wrong. That was the humiliating part. Her whole life was a ledger of self-payment: the berth given up, the code memorized and burned, the truth swallowed year after year *to keep people safe* — and look where keeping people safe had gotten them. A brother in a cage. An aunt stained by a name. A girl raised in a house where the silences were load-bearing walls.

So.

---

**Plan Three** was the one she wrote last, and slowest, and with the pencil pressing hard enough to emboss the next three pages beneath.

In Plan Three, nobody paid with themselves except the people who actually owed the debt.

Tomas took a berth — non-negotiable, written first, underlined. Nell took a berth — also non-negotiable, though Ada suspected, with a sinking feeling, that Nell had opinions about negotiability that were about to become audible. That left one berth and two claimants: herself and Vann.

And here was the arithmetic that Plan Three demanded, the sum she had been circling all night without landing on: Vann's papers were forged. Hers were real. A forged paper discovered at the gangway meant a cell, or worse. A real paper, verified, meant passage — but only for the name on it, and the name on hers was a name Brecht wanted ashore, within reach, attached to a ledger.

Unless the ledger left without her.

Unless the true copy — the retyped pages, the ones naming Brecht in her own careful hand — sailed in a pocket, and the original — the forgery she was preparing, the poisoned gift — stayed behind to detonate in Brecht's desk, and the newspapers received their envelopes by morning post, and the name on the berth was Elias Vann, translator, stateless, alive.

Give him her papers. Give him the copy. Let him carry the truth home, because he was the one who had carried the letters to her door, and there was a symmetry in that which her bookkeeper's soul found almost restful.

And she would stay. With Nell — no. Without Nell. With Marta, then, two stubborn women in a city where Brecht's world was about to come down around his ears, hidden in the crowd, in the noise, in the chaos of a scandal breaking over the harbor district like weather. People vanished easily in scandals. People survived them, sometimes. Ada had survived twenty years of worse.

She wrote it all out. She checked the figures twice, the way she checked every ledger, and for the first time all night the columns balanced — or appeared to. There was a residue at the bottom, a remainder that wouldn't resolve: the image of Nell's face when she learned. The image of Tomas stepping off a gangway onto solid ground and turning around to look for a sister who wasn't there.

Ada held Plan Three over the burner.

Her hand hesitated. Not from doubt — doubt had been dealt with, itemized, closed. From something older and less reasonable: the superstition that a plan burned might, in burning, become inevitable. As if the fire were signing it.

She lit the corner and watched this one go too, all three plans now ash in the grate, and understood that she had not destroyed them at all. She had filed them. Every figure was still in her head, arranged and cross-referenced, waiting for the morning to tell her which column to open first.

The clock said ten past three. Upstairs, the house breathed — Marta's snore, steady as surf; Nell's silence, which was somehow louder. Down the hill, the harbor lights burned, and somewhere among them a man with her brother's face waited to learn whether his sister was the kind of woman who paid her debts or the kind who made others pay theirs.

Ada washed the teacup, dried it, and set it back on the shelf precisely where it lived. Then she took out one final sheet of paper — not a plan, just a list — and wrote at the top, in the hand that had fooled a colonel once and would fool him again:

*Things needed before Pier Nine:*

*1. Envelopes. Good ones.*
*2. The address of a newspaper that isn't afraid.*
*3. A way to get a message to Tomas's guards that doesn't pass through Brecht's hands.*
*4. One more pair of eyes I can trust —*

She stopped. Read the fourth item back. Heard, in memory, a seventeen-year-old voice saying *Mr. V lies about where he gets his shoes polished*, and felt the peculiar dread of a bookkeeper who has just realized the sharpest instrument in the house has been listening at doors all along.

From upstairs, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of Nell's floorboard creaking — the third stair from the top, the one that announced visitors, eavesdroppers, and daughters of the house who had decided the night's arithmetic concerned them too.

Ada blew out the lamp and stood in the dark, listening to footsteps descend.
