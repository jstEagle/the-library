## 27. Marta Stays Behind

Marta had always believed that a house told you things before its people did.

The Yarrow house — her sister-in-law's house now, though Marta had kept it standing through the lean years and would fight anyone who called it anything but hers — had been telling her things for a week. The way Ada's typewriter went silent at midnight instead of eleven. The way Nell set four cups on the tray out of habit and then, catching herself, put one back with the exaggerated care of a girl pretending she hadn't counted. The way Elias Vann stood in the hall each evening as though waiting to be evicted from a building he'd only just been allowed into.

Houses kept arithmetic too. Marta had learned that from the war, when the house had counted its occupants down from seven to three and back up again by ones: herself, Ada, Nell, and now the translator who polished his shoes too often and looked at the harbor like a man looking at a door.

She was seventy-one years old, her hands ached at the knuckles in weather, and she had decided, sometime around the second pot of tea that morning, that it was time to tell them about the papers.

Not because she wanted to. She had carried this particular item in her private ledger for twenty years, filed under the same heading as everything else from those days: *Do not open unless the house is burning.* But the house was burning now, quietly, in the way houses burned when everyone in them was doing sums they wouldn't say aloud — sums about berths, about ten days, about who could be spared and who could not — and Marta Yarrow had never in her life let other people do her arithmetic for her.

* * *

She found Ada in the study, of course. Ada lived in the study the way a crab lives in its shell; you could set your watch by the sound of her keys. The morning light came through the window in that grey, apologetic way the port city managed even in summer, and it fell across the desk where Ada was entering figures into a book so neat it might have been printed.

"Your aunt is here," Marta announced from the doorway, "to waste an hour of your valuable time."

Ada looked up. There were shadows under her eyes that hadn't been there in spring. "You're never a waste of an hour."

"Flatterer." Marta lowered herself into the visitor's chair — the good one, the one she pretended not to prefer — and folded her hands on her stick. "I've come to discuss the ship."

Something crossed Ada's face, quick as a fish under ice. "There isn't much news yet. Vann's checking the consulate lists again today."

"I don't want news. News is for people who still believe the world will surprise them pleasantly. I want to talk about lists." Marta held up one finger. "Specifically, the list of verified former citizens eligible for repatriation. I want to know whose name is on it."

"You know whose names are on it. Yours, mine, Nell's —"

"No." Marta said it gently, which for her constituted an event on the order of a solar eclipse, and she saw Ada register it immediately. Her niece had inherited her father's talent for hearing the thing beneath the thing. "No, child. Mine is not on it. And it will not be going on it. And I have come to tell you why, because you will find out anyway — Nell will probably find out first, knowing her — and I would rather you heard it from me sitting down than from a clerk standing up."

The clock on the mantel did its work. Tick, tick, tick, faithful as a heartbeat and twice as indifferent.

"The papers," Marta continued, before courage could reconsider, "that I used to enter this country in 'twenty-one. They are not forged, precisely. That would almost have been better. Forged papers can be replaced; a man in a certain office can always be found to sell you a new name if you have money or leverage or a pretty daughter. No — my papers are quite genuine. They say exactly who I am." She smiled, and was aware that the smile was the kind she wore to funerals. "They say I am the widow of Anton Yarrow, listed collaborator, deceased 1919, file reference B-114. And the repatriation board does not issue berths to the widows of men in category B. Not verified citizens, you see. Verified *collaborators*. The distinction matters very much to people who have never lost anything."

Ada had gone very still. It was a family trait; Marta had watched her brother go still in exactly that way the day the gendarmes came for the ledgers, stillness like an animal deciding whether to freeze or bolt.

"Marta," Ada said slowly, "I knew Father worked in the supply ministry during the occupation. Everyone's fathers worked somewhere during the occupation."

"Yes."

"And I knew there were rumors afterward. Whispers. Mother never spoke of it, and neither did you, and we were taught — you taught us — that some doors stayed shut."

"I did teach you that," Marta agreed. "It was the most useful thing I ever gave you, and someday you'll understand what it cost me to hand it over."

"But you're telling me the whispers were true."

Marta looked at her hands on the stick. Seventy-one years old, and they still wanted to wring themselves together like a girl caught out at something.

"I'm telling you," she said, "that in 1918, your father signed a requisition schedule that routed grain away from the mountain villages and into the garrison stores. Three times. Possibly four; the fourth entry is disputed, and I have spent twenty years disputing it with myself at four in the morning, which is a poor use of anyone's insomnia." Her voice held level. She was rather proud of that. "He was not a monster, Ada. He was a frightened clerk in a ministry full of frightened clerks, and a colonel he could not refuse put a form in front of him and said *sign*, and he signed, because refusal meant his wife and son and baby daughter went into the same room the resisters went into, and no one came out of that room smiling. He signed three times and hated himself thoroughly and completely, and then he spent the rest of his short life trying to buy it back — hiding families in the cellar, forging ration cards until his eyes gave out, and finally carrying messages for the network until the messages killed him." She paused. "None of which appears anywhere in file B-114, because redemption is a terrible bookkeeper. It never balances the column."

Ada's eyes had gone bright and hard. "You knew. All these years. You knew what the whispers meant, and you never said."

"I kept a silence," Marta said. "Yes. One more, in a house full of them. You kept yours about the evacuation. Tomas kept his about whatever he kept. Your mother kept hers about your father, God rest her tactful soul. We are not a family, dear, we are an archive of sealed envelopes, and I am simply the first one tired enough to break the wax."

"And the berth —"

"There is no berth. There has been no berth since the day the board pulled my file." Marta waved a hand, briskly, before pity could get a foothold in the room. "Oh, don't make that face. I have known since March. I applied in February like a fool, half-hoping the bureaucracy had mislaid my husband along with everything else, and I received in April a beautifully typed letter informing me that my application could not proceed due to the status of my late spouse, and wishing me, I quote, *continued good fortune in your current place of residence*. The current place of residence being, presumably, anywhere at all, so long as it wasn't home."

"Marta —"

"Don't." She raised the finger again. "I have rehearsed this speech in the bath for two weeks and I will not be derailed by sympathy, which is a fine seasoning but a poor meal. Here is the part that matters, and then I will allow you to hug me, briefly, because you look as though you need it more than I do." She leaned forward. "Three berths. Four people in this house who qualify — well, three now, plus whatever miracle Vann has arranged for himself. And I know — oh, I *know*, Ada, don't insult me — I know you have been lying awake dividing three by four and arriving at some answer involving yourself staying behind, because you are your father's daughter and you both share the fatal belief that guilt is payable in installments of self-sacrifice."

"That's not —"

"It is exactly so, and I watched you draft it in your face over breakfast. So hear me: I release you. Formally. As the senior member of this household, the keeper of its oldest silences, and the person who taught every one of you how to lie convincingly about jam supplies, I hereby release all of you from any obligation to spend your berths on a woman of seventy-one with bad knees and a category-B file. My life is here. My life has been here for twenty years. I have buried a sister-in-law, raised a grand-niece, and outlived every gossip in this district, and I intend to go on doing it. A person needs a project."

For a long moment Ada didn't speak. Then, in the small voice she saved for the truest things: "Why tell us now? Why not let us sail believing you'd follow?"

"Because belief deferred curdles into resentment, and I refuse to have you cursing my memory at forty knots somewhere in the middle of the sea." Marta's voice cracked, just slightly, and she pressed on through it. "And because — because if I am to stay, I should like to be remembered honestly. That is all. That is the entire request. Not remembered kindly — kindness smooths the edges off a person until you're mourning a stranger. Remembered *honestly*. Marta Yarrow, who lied fluently, loved fiercely, kept her brother's secret longer than was perhaps wise, and stayed behind because the paperwork of the past is the one enemy she couldn't out-talk." She sniffed. "Put that on my stone. In small letters. Stone masons charge by the letter, and I've left you nothing in the will but the good teapot."

Ada came around the desk and hugged her, brief and fierce, as predicted, and Marta permitted it while looking pointedly at the ceiling so that neither of them had to acknowledge the wetness involved.

"You could still try," Ada said into her shoulder. "Appeals. Petitions. Vann knows people at the consulate —"

"I know exactly the sort of people Vann knows, and so do you, and we will return to that subject presently." Marta disengaged, straightened her cardigan, and reassembled herself into a person who had not recently been embraced. "One more thing, and then I'll take my leave, because I have a casserole to build and a reputation for unflappability to maintain."

"Anything."

"The telegram office." Marta fixed her niece with the eye she'd once used on black-market butter vendors. "Vann has been down there twice this week. He thinks nobody notices him because he wears that coat and affects a limp that comes and goes depending on whether anyone official is watching. I notice everything. It is my sole remaining talent and I hoard it like sugar." She paused at the door. "Whatever he's waiting for, Ada — whoever he's expecting word from — when it comes, it will come to that office, and it will come soon, and you should decide *now* whether you want to be the sort of household that hears the truth last."

She let herself out before Ada could answer, which was, after all, the family style.

* * *

In the kitchen, Nell was supposed to be peeling potatoes and was instead reading the back of a seed packet with the intensity of a scholar deciphering Linear B.

"Nell," Marta said, taking up her own paring knife, "your great-uncle Anton was a collaborator."

Nell looked up. Potato juice dripped off her knife onto the newspaper. "Was he the one in the photograph in the tin? With the mustache?"

"The very one."

"Oh." Nell considered this with the terrifying economy of the young. "Aunt Ada cried at that photograph once. When she thought I was asleep. So did you, but you pretend you had something in your eye." She resumed peeling. "Does this mean you're not coming on the ship?"

"It means I'm not coming on the ship."

"Then someone else gets your berth." Nell's knife stopped. "That means the numbers work. Aunt Ada, Mr. Vann, me, and —" she counted on her fingers, lips moving, and arrived somewhere Marta suspected she'd already been many times, "— and there's still one left over. If he's alive."

Marta's paring knife paused against the potato skin.

"What makes you think there's a *he*?" she asked, as lightly as a woman of seventy-one can ask anything.

Nell shrugged, fifteen years old and transparent as glass. "Mr. V stands at the window every evening facing the harbor, and nobody faces a harbor that long unless they're waiting for somebody to come *out* of it." She dropped the peeled potato into the bowl with a small, decisive splash. "Also, he asked me last week what Tomas was like. Present tense."

The kitchen clock ticked. Outside, a gull argued with the sky. Marta looked down at her hands — her father's hands, her brother's hands, hands that had kept a hundred silences and built a casserole on top of every one — and felt, unexpectedly, something that was not dread. Something closer to the moment before rain after a long dry spell.

"Well," she said. "Then I suppose we'd best peel enough potatoes for four after all."

The floorboard on the third stair from the top creaked — descending, this time, quickly — and Nell's head came up like a pointer's, and from the hallway came the sound of the front door opening, and Elias Vann's voice calling up the stairs, breathless, holding something in his hand that crackled like a telegram.
