## 28. Tomas Is Alive

The telegram came at eleven minutes past seven on a Tuesday morning, which Elias Vann would later consider the most absurd possible hour for a man's entire understanding of the world to invert itself.

He was in the kitchen, ostensibly drying dishes but actually conducting his usual early negotiation with Marta over whether he was allowed to know where Ada had gone the night before. He was losing the negotiation. Marta had the advantage of twenty additional years of practice at not telling people things, and she wielded it the way other women of her generation wielded rolling pins—routinely, and without apology.

"You could simply tell me," Vann said, setting a plate down with exaggerated care. "It would save us both time."

"It would save you time," Marta said. "It would cost me a great deal." She was shelling peas into a bowl with the brisk efficiency of someone who had once disarmed men for a living and now found pulses a more satisfying target. "Ada went where Ada needed to go. That is all you require."

"And if what I require is more than that?"

"Then you require less than you think. Men always do." She flicked a pod into the bowl. "You have the look of a man building a case, Mr. Vann. I would advise against it. Cases built before breakfast are always flimsy."

The bell at the door rang then—a flat, official double-ring that belonged to no friend or neighbor—and Nell beat them all to it, as she beat them all to everything, skidding into the hall in her stockings with the particular velocity of a girl who had been told twice already that week not to run indoors.

"There's a man," she reported, breathless, appearing back in the kitchen doorway. "A telegraph man. He's got one of those caps. He says it's urgent and he won't leave until somebody signs."

"Did you sign?" Marta asked.

"I'm twelve, Aunt Marta."

"That has never stopped you before."

Vann got there first, mostly because Nell let him. The telegraph boy stood on the step with the studied boredom of youth employed by institutions, holding out his clipboard like a man offering proof of something unpleasant. Vann signed. The boy took the pen back, examined the signature with the air of a critic, said "Cheers," and departed down the street whistling something jaunty and off-key, entirely unaware that he had just delivered an earthquake wrapped in eight words.

Vann read it standing in the doorway with the cold morning air coming in around his ankles.

*CONFIRMED ALIVE YARROW TOMAS HELD CUSTODY HARBOR DISTRICT ADVISE DISCRETION STOP REPLY SOONEST.*

He read it again. Then a third time, because the first two readings had produced no sensation whatsoever, as though the words were written in a language he almost knew—a language he'd spent years translating and still occasionally mistranslated at cost.

Alive.

Held custody.

Harbor district.

Advise discretion.

His hands were shaking. He noticed this with detached interest, the way one notices weather happening to someone else. Behind him, Nell had gone very quiet, which from Nell was louder than screaming.

"What is it?" she asked. "Who's Tomas?"

And that was how Vann learned that Ada had kept even that from the child—that in twenty years of silences, this household had managed to keep a dead brother so thoroughly buried that Nell did not know he had ever lived.

"I think," Vann said, and heard his own voice come out strange, "I should wait for Ada."

"You're going to do the thing where everyone stands around being mysterious until I lose my mind," Nell said. It was not a question.

"Yes," Vann admitted. "Probably."

"Well. At least you're honest about it."

* * *

Ada came home at half past nine, smelling of river fog and cigarette smoke that was not her own, carrying bread she had bought as camouflage for whatever errand the bread had covered. Vann watched her come through the door and understood, with the clarity of a man watching a lamp about to be lit, that he was about to change her life with a piece of paper, and that no amount of rehearsal made such a moment kind.

She saw his face before she saw the telegram in his hand. He knew this because she stopped halfway through unbuttoning her coat, and because her eyes went first to him, then to Marta, then to the telegram itself, reading its shape rather than its contents, the way soldiers learn to read terrain.

"Elias," she said. Her voice was level. Twenty years of practice, he thought. Twenty years of keeping her voice level while the ground moved. "What is it?"

He held it out. He did not trust himself to summarize. A summary would be mercy, and mercy here would be cruelty dressed up—she deserved the plain words, the flat official words, the bureaucratic indifference of the state announcing that her brother had been alive all along.

She read it standing in the hall, coat half-buttoned, bread under one arm.

Then she sat down. Not collapsed—sat, deliberately, lowering herself onto the hall chair with the care of a woman handling something breakable, which perhaps she was. The bread rolled gently off her lap and was caught by Nell, who clutched it to her chest like a rescued infant and looked between the adults with rising alarm.

"Aunt Ada," Nell said. "What does it say?"

Ada didn't answer. She was looking at the paper the way one looks at a ghost—not with fear exactly, but with the vertigo of having been certain, for two decades, about the fundamental architecture of reality, and finding a door in a wall that had always been solid.

"He's alive," Vann said quietly. Because someone had to say it aloud. Because the words needed air. "Tomas is alive, Ada. They have him. Near the harbor."

"They've *had* him," Ada said. Her voice came out very small. "For twenty years."

"We don't know that. We don't know anything except—"

"We know enough." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "We know they kept him alive because he was worth more breathing than dead. We know they've been sitting on him like a bank holds collateral. And we know—" her hand dropped, and her eyes when they opened were dry and terrible, "—we know why they sent this now. They sent it now because of the ledger. Because Brecht is running out of time, and so are we, and he wanted us to know he still holds the thing we love most."

Nell made a small sound. Everyone turned. She was standing in the kitchen doorway with the bread still hugged to her chest, and her face had gone through confusion into something older.

"Tomas is your brother," she said. "The one in the stories Aunt Marta tells wrong on purpose. He isn't dead. He was never dead."

"No, sweetheart," Ada said.

"You *knew*? You knew all this time he might be—"

"I knew nothing. I believed—I was told—he died in the evacuation. Everyone believed it. That was the point of believing it." Ada rose from the chair, and Vann watched her assemble herself piece by piece, spine first, then shoulders, then the face she wore for the world: composed, competent, a bookkeeper's face, arithmetic where feeling used to be. But her hands, taking the telegram back from him, were not steady. "Which means every year since, every year I mourned him, he was somewhere being kept. Fed. Questioned, probably. Used." She folded the telegram along its existing creases, then unfolded it, then folded it again. "Elias, sit down. All of you. We are going to think about this properly, because the first thing a trap wants you to do is feel, and the second thing it wants you to do is run, and we are going to do neither until we've counted everything twice."

"She does this," Nell whispered to Vann, awed. "When she's most frightened, she gets organized. Marta says it's like watching a lighthouse pretend it isn't on fire."

"Nell," said Marta and Ada together, in identical tones.

* * *

They sat around the kitchen table, which had hosted meals and arguments and, last month, Nell's disastrous attempt at a chemistry experiment involving vinegar and the good silverware, and which now hosted the dismantling of twenty years of grief over a surface still dusted with flour.

Vann laid out what he knew, and found, laying it out, how little it amounted to. The courier who had died with the letters in his effects. The payment Vann had accepted and half-confessed and fully regretted. Brecht's name surfacing from the past like a body from a lake. The harbor office. The soldiers Ada had scouted through rain-streaked glass. And now this telegram, arriving with suspicious punctuality after their reconnaissance—as though someone had been waiting for them to look, and wished to reward the looking with a hook.

"The timing is the message," Ada said. She had pulled a sheet of paper toward her and was writing as she spoke, dates and intervals in her neat bookkeeper's column. "We visit the harbor office on Thursday night. The telegram arrives Tuesday morning. That is not coincidence; that is a reply. Someone saw us—or heard of it—and decided we needed encouragement. Or discouragement. With Brecht it's often both."

"They want you to come back," Vann said. "With the ledger."

"Of course they want me to come back. That is what leverage is *for*, Elias. Leverage sits in a drawer doing nothing until you need the drawer opened." She set the pencil down. "But here is what they may not have calculated." Her voice changed—dropped its composure for one unguarded syllable, and Vann saw beneath it the girl of nineteen who had given up a berth on a boat and memorized a code and burned the evidence. "He's alive. My brother is alive. Everything else—Brecht, the ledger, the berths, the whole rotten arithmetic of it—is secondary to that sentence, and I will not pretend otherwise, because pretending otherwise is how our family got into this profession of silence in the first place."

Marta, who had been silent throughout, set down her pea pod. When she spoke, her voice had a rasp Vann hadn't heard before.

"I told you once, Ada, that men like Vann arrive with debts attached. Let me tell you something else I know. In the old days, when a network was blown, they never killed the couriers outright. Not the valuable ones. A courier who knows a cipher is worth ten dead ones. They kept them. Moved them. Showed them to each other across tables to prove the network was broken." Her jaw tightened. "If Tomas survived the betrayal, he survived because someone decided he was inventory. And inventory is only released in a sale."

"Then we'll buy him back," Nell said.

Everyone looked at her. She had planted both elbows on the table, chin in hands, in the posture she adopted for mathematics homework and moral certainty alike.

"With what?" Vann asked gently. "That's rather the question, isn't it. Brecht doesn't want money. Money can be traced, taxed, explained. He wants the ledger. The real one. The half that names names—including, if Ada's right about the third layer, his name in Tomas's own handwriting."

"Then it's simple," Nell said. "We give him the ledger, he gives us Tomas, and everyone walks away."

"It is never simple," said Marta.

"It's a lie," said Ada, at the same moment, and then paused, and Vann watched the two truths collide behind her eyes. "Or—it would be a lie if we gave him the true ledger. Which raises a question I haven't yet said aloud, and I'd like us all to be adults about it." She looked around the table. "How many of you have wondered why I haven't destroyed the ledger? Burned it, sunk it, done any of the hundred sensible things that would end this? Because I could have, weeks ago. Instead I've kept it hidden and retyped and ready. Why?"

No one answered. The kettle ticked on the stove, gathering heat toward a whistle nobody wanted.

"Because a weapon you refuse to aim is just weight," Vann said slowly. "You've been carrying it to aim it. At him. Someday."

"Because truth that stays hidden protects no one," Ada said. "I learned that too late and at terrible cost, and I am not learning it twice." She gathered her papers. "But that is tomorrow's problem. Today's problem is smaller and larger at once: we now have confirmation that Tomas lives, we have approximately—" she glanced at the calendar on the wall, at the circled date of the ship's sailing, "—eight days, and we have one telegram that tells us where he is but not how to reach him. So. First: we verify. Marta, your contact—the one you risked yourself to call. Can they confirm a prisoner in the harbor district? Names, faces, anything?"

Marta was quiet a moment. "I can ask. Asking costs. But yes."

"Second: nobody goes anywhere alone, including me, including especially me, and Nell—"

"I know," Nell said. "Stay away from the harbor. Stay away from strange men. Don't follow you places." She sighed the sigh of the professionally supervised. "You always list the things I've already done."

"Third," Ada said, and her voice gentled in a way that startled Vann more than shouting would have, "we eat breakfast. All of this will keep until we've eaten. Grief and strategy both go better with eggs."

* * *

It was later—mid-afternoon, with Marta out on her dangerous errand and Nell dispatched to a neighbor's on the transparent pretext of returning a cake tin—that Vann found Ada at the typewriter.

She wasn't typing. She was sitting before it with a blank page loaded, her fingers resting on the keys without pressing, and the afternoon light came through the window and lay across the machine like something laid there on purpose.

"He used to sit like that," she said, without turning. Vann realized she had heard him in the doorway and chosen to speak anyway—to invite him in without turning around, which was, he suspected, as close to an embrace as Ada Yarrow offered before lunchtime. "Tomas. When he was learning the trade. He'd sit at Papa's typewriter with his hands hovering and make up letters to imaginary people. Dear Ambassador, I regret to inform you that my sister has eaten all the plums. Official correspondence about plums. He thought he was hilarious."

"Was he?"

"Worse. He was funny *and* he knew it." She laughed—a small, cracked sound, half sob, quickly governed. "Twenty years, Elias. I built my whole life on top of his grave. Every careful silence, every year I didn't write to anyone from home, every time Marta started a story and I changed the subject—I did it because I was keeping faith with a dead man. And he was—" Her hands came down flat on the desk. "He was sitting in some room near the water, alive, maybe thinking I'd given up on him. Maybe thinking I forgot."

"You didn't forget. You couldn't have. You're not built for forgetting—you're built for ledgers. Nothing gets lost in a ledger; it just waits to be reconciled."

She turned then, and looked at him with an expression he couldn't fully parse—something between gratitude and accusation and the beginning of an idea.

"Reconciled," she repeated. "Yes. That's exactly the word." She pulled the blank page out and fed in a fresh one. "And here is what reconciliation requires, Elias, in case you were wondering why I'm calm. It requires knowing the true figure on each side. What Brecht thinks he's getting. What we actually hold. What Tomas costs, and what he's worth, and to whom." The carriage rattled as she tested the keys. "Brecht believes he's trading three berths for a ledger. Fine. But a man who keeps a hostage for twenty years is a man who expects his merchandise to appreciate. He'll want more than the ledger. He'll want the *silence*. He'll want us gone and grateful and unable to testify."

"And you intend to disappoint him."

"I intend," Ada said, and began to type, each letter striking with the deliberate force of a decision, "to reconcile the account. Completely. Down to the last line."

Vann stayed in the doorway a while longer, watching her type, and let himself feel—for the first time since the telegram—something underneath the fear. Hope, maybe. Or its rough draft.

It lasted until the knock came at the door. Three slow raps, evenly spaced, the knock of a man with time and authority and no need to hurry. Through the frosted glass, a silhouette in a peaked cap.

Ada's fingers stilled over the keys. In the sudden quiet, the unfinished line of type sat glaring on the page.

"That," Vann said quietly, moving toward the hall, "is not a telegraph boy."
