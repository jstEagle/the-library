## 29. The Exchange Planned

Ada Yarrow had spent twenty years learning that love, properly administered, looked exactly like bookkeeping.

It was a lesson she'd absorbed early and against her will. Love as most people practiced it was a disorderly thing—impulse dressed up as instinct, sentiment mistaken for sense. But real love, the kind that held a household together through exile and rationing and her aunt Marta's opinions about the proper way to boil potatoes (there was one way; all others were treason), required columns. Entries. A careful accounting of what you had, what you owed, and what you were prepared to lose.

So on the morning after the telegram confirmed Tomas was alive—alive, held in a warehouse three streets from the water, alive like a coin someone had dropped into her palm after two decades of empty pockets—Ada sat down at the kitchen table with a fresh sheet of paper, ruled it into four columns, and began planning how to give away her life.

She titled the page, because she was who she was: *Disposition of Assets.*

*Asset one,* she wrote. *The ledger.*

The original—the half she'd carried out of the republic sewn into the lining of a coat that no longer existed, hidden since in a place she would not write down even on a private page, because Marta read everything left flat on tables and Nell read everything, full stop—was worth exactly one brother. That was its exchange rate. Brecht wanted it; Brecht had Tomas; therefore ledger minus Ada equaled Tomas plus freedom. The arithmetic was ugly but it balanced.

*Asset two.* Herself. Also worth one brother, if the ledger weren't enough on its own. Brecht would want her regardless—a loose end, a witness, a woman who could recite from memory things he'd prefer burned. She entered herself under *liabilities* first, crossed it out, and moved it to *assets*, because if she was going to be spent, she refused to be spent at a loss.

*Assets three and four.* Vann's papers. Nell's berth eligibility.

She stared at those last two entries for a long time, the pencil hovering, while outside the window the gulls argued over something dead on the pier and the harbor bells rang the half hour in their usual key of mild complaint.

Here was the difficulty, and it was not a small one: the ship sailing in six days had berths for three verified former citizens. Three. And there were, depending on how you counted—and Ada counted the way an accountant counts, which is to say hopefully until the numbers refuse—four people who might sail, five if you included Marta, who couldn't, and six if you counted Tomas, who must.

Four berths' worth of need, three berths of supply. Someone stayed. Everyone had spent the last week pretending this wasn't so, the way families pretend the leak in the roof is cosmetic, and the pretense had curdled every dinner conversation into a tournament of polite martyrdom. Vann kept suggesting, with elaborate casualness, that his papers were "probably forged well enough to pass" and that really Nell should have his spot. Nell announced nightly that she was staying and would hear no arguments, in the tone of a girl who has recently discovered principles and intends to wear them indoors and out. Marta said nothing, which from Marta was the loudest statement available.

And Tomas—who might already be aboard, who might be waiting at the warehouse, whose existence was still a rumor wearing a telegram's clothes—Tomas would take a berth or die before he let anyone else surrender theirs. Ada knew him. He'd been giving away seats since he was nine years old, when he'd traded his own place in the school concert line so a stammering boy wouldn't have to sing alone in front of rows of parents. It had been sweet then. Now it was a logistical nightmare.

Which meant the only person in the household without a stubborn streak about self-sacrifice was Ada herself, and that was convenient, because Ada had already decided.

She had decided somewhere around three o'clock that morning, lying awake listening to the house breathe—Marta's floorboards above, Vann turning over in the parlor below, Nell's clockwork breathing through the wall—and the decision had arrived not as a thunderclap but as an entry in a ledger. Clean. Final. *One life, exchanged. Two lives, preserved. One life, recovered.* The books balanced. The books always balanced, if you were honest about the figures.

The trick—the only trick—was execution. Because Vann would object. Nell would revolt. Marta would see through any plan more complicated than buying flour, which meant the plan had to survive Marta, and Marta had once uncovered a neighbor's smuggling operation by noticing he'd started buying cinnamon.

Ada turned to a fresh page and began, in smaller handwriting, *Phase One.*

* *

Phase One was breakfast.

This sounds trivial. It was not. In the Yarrow-Vann-Nell economy, information moved at meals, and whoever controlled the meal controlled the flow. Marta controlled most meals through sheer force of kippers. So Ada rose early, got to the kitchen before Marta did—an achievement roughly equivalent to arriving at a bank before the vault opens—and had bread frying in fat and the good jam set out when her aunt came down in her gray dressing gown with her gray expression and paused, visibly recalibrating.

"You're cooking," Marta said.

"I'm capable of cooking."

"Capability and occurrence are different animals." Marta hung her gown on its hook with the precision of a woman racking a rifle. "You want something."

"I want us to have a pleasant breakfast."

"No one wants a pleasant breakfast. People want breakfast. Pleasantness is what people say when they're about to ask for money." Marta poured her coffee, black, and drank it standing, watching Ada over the rim with eyes that had missed nothing since 1911.

Ada slid the fried bread onto plates and smiled the smile she used with tax assessors. "I thought we might all go down to the harbor offices today. Together. To look at the berth registry. As a family outing."

"A family outing to a government office."

"People pay good money for cruises, Marta. This is nearly the same."

Marta set down her cup. For a moment Ada feared the whole enterprise would founder right there, wrecked on the rocks of her aunt's suspicion—but Marta only said, "You've made a plan," in a flat voice that was somehow both accusation and blessing, "and you're not going to tell me what it is, because you think I'll disapprove."

"I haven't made a plan."

"You've made a plan and you're ashamed of it, which is worse." Marta took her plate to the table and sat, unfolding her napkin as though preparing to review the meal for a newspaper. "Fine. I'll come to your harbor outing. I'll watch whatever you do. And I'll say nothing, because I promised your mother I'd keep my mouth shut about family matters, and look how well that's gone."

"Marta—"

"Eat your bread. It's burning your reputation even faster than it's burning itself."

Ada ate her bread. Phase One, she noted privately, had gone approximately as well as a phase could go when its primary objective was deception and its secondary objective was not being murdered with a fish fork. She'd call it a qualified success. Accountants lived on qualified successes.

* *

Phase Two required Vann, and Vann was the problem, because Vann had developed the inconvenient habit of looking at her as though she were a puzzle he intended to solve out of pure affection, and puzzles being solved by affection tended to notice things.

She found him in the parlor mid-morning, ostensibly reading yesterday's newspaper but actually holding it upside down, which told her either that his mind was elsewhere or that his command of the local language was worse than he pretended, and given that he translated professionally, she chose to believe his mind was elsewhere.

"We're going to the harbor offices," she said. "All of us. This afternoon."

He lowered the inverted paper. "Is that wise? Brecht has men at the offices. I saw two of them Tuesday, loitering by the notice board with the subtlety of furniture."

"Then we'll fit right in. Families loiter constantly. It's practically our national sport now." She sat down across from him, folding her hands, arranging her face into the mild bureaucratic pleasantness that had gotten her through two decades of consular interviews. "I want to check the berth verification requirements. Nell's documents need a countersignature, and I want to know which office provides it."

Vann watched her. He had a way of listening with his whole face that would have been charming if it weren't so dangerous—he actually heard things, which put him several rungs above most of humanity. "That's reasonable," he said slowly. "That's almost too reasonable."

"It's paperwork, Elias. Nothing about paperwork is too anything."

"And yet." He folded the newspaper, gave up the pretense entirely. "Ada. If you're planning what I think you're planning—"

"You think I'm planning paperwork."

"I think you're planning to hand yourself to Brecht, and you're wrapping it in errands the way you wrap pills in cheese for the cat."

"The cat is a very suspicious creature. The method works."

"Ada."

"Elias." She matched his tone, gentle as a receipt. "Whatever I'm doing, it isn't settled. I'm gathering figures. You don't sign off on a ledger before you've checked the sums."

"That's exactly what worries me." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the charm fell away entirely and she saw underneath it the tired man with no country, the courier who'd carried other people's letters because he couldn't carry his own name anywhere. "I've seen what happens when people trade themselves. In the camps, in the border towns, everywhere. The trade never comes out even. There's always a clerk somewhere rounding down."

"Then I'll be the clerk," Ada said. "I'm very good at making sure things come out even."

He looked at her a long moment, and she saw him decide—not to believe her, exactly, but to grant her the dignity of pretending to. "Afternoon, then," he said. "Harbor offices. As a family."

"As a family," she agreed, and stood, and felt the weight of what she hadn't said settle onto her shoulders like a coat lined with stones. Twenty years of practice at silence, and here it was again, serving her faithfully: the plan she could not tell him, because if she told him, he would break it, kindly, gently, out of the same stubborn arithmetic that made him offer Nell his berth every night at dinner.

Love, she thought, climbing the stairs to check on Nell, was a great many columns that refused to add up in public.

* *

Nell was on her bed with her shoes off and a library book propped on her knees, and she looked up when Ada came in with the particular alertness of a fifteen-year-old who has learned that adults entering rooms unannounced are carrying something.

"We're going to the harbor offices this afternoon," Ada said. "All of us."

"To look at the berth registry." Nell said it evenly, but her thumb had stopped moving on the page.

"To check the countersignature requirements for your documents. Yours specifically. You can't sail without them in order, and I intend for you to sail."

"Do you." Nell closed the book on one finger, keeping her place, a habit she'd learned from Ada and deployed against her now like a small polished knife. "Because here's what I've noticed, Aunt Ada. Everyone in this house has suddenly become extremely interested in everyone else's documents. Mr. Vann keeps offering me his berth. Marta keeps saying nothing, which from Marta is basically a foghorn. And you—" she tilted her head, and there was Tomas in the gesture, Tomas at nine, working out why the dog had gone quiet, "—you've started writing lists. I found one under the blotter. It had columns."

Ada sat down on the edge of the bed. Outside, a steamer whistled, long and low, the sound that meant departures, and they both listened to it the way you listen to weather.

"Nell," she said. "If I asked you to do something for me—something difficult, something you'd have to trust me about without explanations—could you?"

"That depends."

"On what?"

"On whether you'll tell me eventually." Nell's chin came up. She had Marta's spine and her mother's eyes and none of anybody's patience. "Everyone keeps things from me 'for my own safety.' I've been safe my entire life and it's the most boring possible condition. So yes—I'll do the difficult thing. But I want it in writing that someday, somebody tells me the whole story. Not the tidy version. The one with the burned corners."

Ada felt something crack gently in her chest, the sound a lock makes when the right key finally turns. "Someday," she said. "I'll write it down myself. Every figure. Even the ones that don't balance."

"Especially those." Nell reopened her book, conversation apparently concluded, and then added, without looking up: "The harbor offices close at four. And the side entrance by the coal merchant doesn't have a guard. In case your paperwork errand needs options."

Ada left the room before her face could betray her, and on the stairs she permitted herself, for the first time in days, a small private laugh—because the child had just out-planned her, and because somewhere in the last two weeks the household of silences had quietly become a conspiracy, and conspiracies, whatever else you said about them, at least talked to each other.

* *

Phase Three was the hardest part, and it could not be done in the house, and it could barely be done at all.

She waited until evening, until Marta was at her sister-in-law's arguing about an inheritance and Vann had gone out—she suspected to watch Brecht's office, though he claimed a walk—and Nell was ostentatiously doing homework at the kitchen table. Then Ada took her coat, said she was going for thread, and walked out into the blue-gray dusk toward the harbor, where the cranes stood against the last light like patient iron herons.

At the shipping agent's annex, behind a counter staffed by a young man with ink-stained cuffs and the enthusiasm of a man counting hours, she filled out the form. *Berth transfer authorization. Requesting party:* she printed her own name in careful capitals. *Berths transferred:* two. *Recipients:* she hesitated one heartbeat over the blank lines, then wrote Elias Vann and Nell Yarrow. *Reason for transfer:* she wrote *voluntary relinquishment*, and the phrase tasted like iron and like freedom at once, and she wrote it firmly enough that the pen nearly went through the paper.

The young man stamped it without reading it. Bureaucracy's one mercy: it never looks closely at the things that matter most.

Two berths secured. Notarized, dated, filed. If she surrendered herself at Pier Nine—if she walked into Brecht's trap with the original ledger under her arm and her brother's name in her mouth—then Vann and Nell would board that ship whether they consented or not, because the authorization would stand, and ships keep schedules, and grief keeps no schedule at all but arithmetic does.

They would hate her. Vann would understand eventually, in the way he understood everything, slowly and completely. Nell would collect on her written promise: someday, the whole story, every figure. Ada would owe a debt payable across whatever distance remained between them, and she would pay it, in letters, in installments, for the rest of a life that might be shorter than she'd prefer but that would at least balance.

She stepped back out into the dusk. The tide was coming in, slapping the pilings in its patient rhythm, and far down the quay the warehouse where they said Tomas was held showed a single lit window, high up, like an eye.

"One brother recovered," she murmured to the arithmetic in her head. "Two children sailed. Books balanced." Her breath made a small cloud and vanished. "God forgive the rounding."

She was halfway home, rehearsing tomorrow's innocent face, when she saw the figure detach itself from the shadow of the customs shed ahead—broad-shouldered, unhurried, wearing the particular stillness of a man who has been waiting and is pleased not to have wasted the wait—and Ada's stomach dropped through the cobbles, because the man stepping into the lamplight was not Brecht and not a soldier but the clerk from the consulate, the one with the soft voice, and he was smiling at her the way people smile at a package they've been paid to intercept.

"Frau Yarrow," he said pleasantly. "What a coincidence. We were just discussing you at the harbor office. Colonel Brecht wonders—are you quite sure the ledger you're planning to bring him is the genuine article? He asks, you understand, purely out of administrative thoroughness."

Behind her, faint across the water, the harbor bell rang the hour—and Ada realized, with the terrible clarity of a woman whose ledgers have just been audited, that someone had read her columns.
