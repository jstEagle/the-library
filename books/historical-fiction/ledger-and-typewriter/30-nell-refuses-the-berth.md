## 30. Nell Refuses the Berth

Nell had learned to read a household the way other children learned to read books — by the light under doors, by which floorboards creaked and when, by the particular quality of silence that meant someone was keeping one.

So when she woke on Thursday morning and heard the typewriter going in the back room at half past five — an hour before Aunt Marta's kettle, an hour even before the gulls started their arguments with the fish carts — she knew something had finished happening overnight.

Ada typed at night sometimes. Nell knew that too. But this was different. This was fast, and steady, and it had the sound of pages that were not going into a drawer. Pages that were going *somewhere*.

She lay in her bed and did arithmetic, because that was what you did when you wanted to be brave about something: count the facts until they stopped being frightening and started being useful.

Fact one: Ada hadn't slept. The lamp oil in the back room had gone down two fingers since Tuesday, and nobody refilled it who wasn't sitting beside it.

Fact two: Elias Vann had come home last night smelling of the harbor — actual harbor, salt and rope and diesel, not the polite harbor of the promenade — and he had lied about where he'd been, which Nell knew because he'd said he'd been at the consulate, and the consulate was uphill, and uphill men came home smelling of cigar smoke, not fish.

Fact three: there were three tickets folded inside the tin where Marta kept the sugar. Nell had counted them yesterday while pretending to look for the strainer. Three third-class berths on the *Corvina*, sailing Sunday. And three was a number that left somebody out, and everybody in that house had spent twenty years being very careful never to say out loud who got left out.

The typewriter stopped.

Nell got up.

---

She found Ada at the little desk with her back to the door, her gray braid coming loose, a page rolled halfway out of the platen like a tongue sticking out. On the desk around her: the old ledger, its burned corner wrapped now in oilcloth; a fresh stack of typing paper; and a list written in pencil, the kind of list that had been rewritten so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth.

"Nell." Ada didn't turn around. "It's early."

"I know."

"There's bread on the board."

"There are three tickets in the sugar tin," Nell said, "and four of us, and you've been typing all night, and Vann came home from the wrong direction, and you've rewritten that list so many times it's falling apart, and I want to know what it says."

Ada turned then. She looked tired in a way that went past her face and into her hands, which were stained faintly with carbon paper black. For a moment Nell thought she would do it — the thing adults always did, the gentle folding shut, the *not now, when you're older*, the whole architecture of silence that this house was built on like pilings.

Instead Ada pushed the pencil list across the desk.

"Sit down," she said. "And close the door. It's time somebody in this house told you things with the door open to you."

---

The plan, laid out in pencil and carbon, was simple in the way that avalanches are simple.

Tomas was alive. He was being held near the harbor — the telegram had come Wednesday night, while Nell was at choir practice, which she now understood was why everyone had been so terribly cheerful about her singing. Brecht held him as leverage against the ledger. Tomorrow — Saturday — Ada would go to Pier Nine herself, carrying the original ledger, and trade it and herself for Tomas. The three berths on the *Corvina* were already arranged: Tomas, Vann, and Nell.

"And you?" Nell asked.

"I'll manage my own arrangements," Ada said, in the voice she used for balancing columns that wouldn't balance.

"That's not an arrangement, that's a shrug."

"Nell—"

"No. No, I'm not done." Nell stood up, and was surprised to find her legs shaking, and decided to stand anyway, because if she sat down she'd cry, and crying was ammunition in this house and she refused to hand it over. "You've spent twenty years deciding things *for* us. That's what Marta says. She says you decide things quietly so nobody has to argue with them. Well, I'm arguing. You don't get to give yourself to that man. You especially don't get to do it while handing me a boat ticket like a — like a consolation prize at a church raffle."

"It isn't—" Ada pressed her fingers to the bridge of her nose. "Nell, listen to me. Brecht wants the ledger and me. Not you. If you're aboard that ship Sunday morning, you're beyond his reach, and everything I'm doing tomorrow means something. If you're standing on that pier—"

"Then I'm family," Nell said. "That's what happens on piers. Families see each other off. Or families get seen off. What we don't do is split up by *arithmetic*."

The word landed harder than she expected. Ada flinched — actually flinched, a small motion, like a woman dodging a thrown cup — and Nell realized she had hit something real.

"You think I haven't done the sums?" Ada asked quietly. "I have been doing these sums since I was younger than you. I gave up a berth once already, Nell. I know exactly how heavy a ticket is that someone else carries."

"Then you should know better than anyone!" Nell's voice cracked and she hated it. "You should know what it does to a person! Twenty years, Aunt Ada. Twenty years of you carrying whatever you carried off that pier, alone, in the dark, because somebody once made your choices for you with love and it nearly killed you. And now you're going to make mine."

The room went very still. Somewhere below, the gulls were still arguing with the fish carts. The kettle in the kitchen began its low complaint.

Ada looked at her for a long time. Then she did something Nell had never seen her do in nine years under her roof.

She laughed — a short, cracked, exhausted laugh, half sob, and put her head down on the soft old list, and said, "Marta is going to kill me. Or possibly adopt you outright. I can't tell which."

---

By seven, the argument had moved to the kitchen, because in their house all serious business eventually migrated toward the kettle, the way iron filings move toward a magnet.

Vann was there, trying to make himself small behind a newspaper he wasn't reading. Marta stood at the stove with her arms crossed, wooden spoon at rest like a saber she'd decided not to draw yet. Nell sat at the table with her chin up. Ada stood by the window with her coffee untouched, which was itself alarming; Ada never let coffee go cold.

"She won't sail," Ada reported, to the room generally.

"Noted," said Marta.

"She has to sail. There are three berths. Tomas needs—"

"Tomas needs his sister alive more than he needs her on a dock," Vann said from behind the newspaper. It was the first thing he'd offered all week without a joke attached, and it cost him visibly; Nell watched him pay it.

"And *she*," Nell said, pointing at herself with entire injustice, "needs to stop being treated like luggage. I'm fourteen. I read everything in this house. I found the tickets, I found the telegram copy in the flour bin — yes, Marta, the flour bin, worst hiding place in Christendom — and I found the letter Vann hid in his boot, which, honestly, boot? Amateur hour."

"The boot was a decoy," Vann said weakly. "There's nothing in the boot."

"There's a receipt from a man named Ostrik and it's dated before you ever told us you arrived, which means you were here days early doing something you haven't explained, and I have decided to be gracious and table that item until after the crisis, but it is *tabled*, not *closed*."

For a moment — one bright, absurd moment — the kitchen almost laughed. Even Marta's mouth twitched. Then Ada set down her cold coffee and the gravity of her settled over the room again.

"Nell," she said. "Tell me why. Truly tell me. Not because you're angry. Because if you can't give me a reason stronger than anger, I'll carry you to that ship myself."

And Nell, who had rehearsed this on the stairs, who had rehearsed it in the bath and during long division and under the covers with a torch, found that the rehearsal fell away and only the true thing was left.

"Because belonging means staying," she said. "You taught me that. You didn't teach it with words, you taught it by keeping me. When my parents died and every relative in the district did sums of their own about whether I was worth feeding, you didn't. You just kept me. Nobody handed you a ticket out of that either — you could have sent me to the orphanage school upstate, everyone said you should, and you stayed. So no. I'm not getting on a boat so that this family can do to me what the world keeps trying to do, which is decide I'm the portable one, the keepable one, the one who gets saved while the ones who love me stay behind in danger. I've been the rescued one my whole life. I'd rather be the *family* one."

The kettle screamed. Nobody moved to it.

It was Marta, finally, who took it off the heat, because Marta believed the universe could end or hold but tea must proceed either way. She poured four cups with her back to them all, and when she turned around her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"Well," she said. "Twenty years I've waited for somebody in this house to say a true thing out loud at full volume. I'd hoped it would be me. Never mind. Girl's earned her cup."

---

They argued until noon. It was, Nell thought later, the first honest argument the house had ever had, and it was magnificent, and terrible, and she wouldn't have traded a minute of it.

Marta sided with Nell, mostly, on the principle — she announced — that Yarrow women had done enough solitary heroics to stock a museum, and she was sick of visiting it. "You gave up a berth in 'eighteen," she said to Ada, jabbing the spoon. "Look how well that arranged our lives. Twenty years of burnt corners and locked drawers and your brother a ghost. No. We are done making martyrs by committee of one."

"One berth is Tomas's, that's settled," Vann said, counting on his fingers. "That leaves two. Four people."

"Then we need a fifth solution," Nell said.

"There is no fifth solution. That's the whole problem with solutions."

"Then we make a sixth plan. You're all so busy dividing the pie you haven't noticed we can change the size of the pie. Marta knows resistance people. Vann knows forgers — tabled, but known. I know the harbor clerks' schedules better than the harbor clerks do, because I deliver Mrs. Petchy's rent to one of them every month and he likes to talk. Between the four of us we have one honest ledger, one dishonest translator, one widow with dangerous friends, and a girl who reads everything. Brecht has soldiers. Soldiers are terrible readers."

Ada stared at her. Then at Marta. Then at Vann, who raised both hands in surrender and said, "Don't look at me. I've been outvoted since Tuesday. I just work here."

"We will talk about the pie," Ada said at last, slowly, as though the words were new coins she was testing between her teeth. "All of it. Tonight. Everything on the table — the evacuation, Father, the letters, all of it. If we're staying together, we do it with nothing hidden. That's my condition."

"That's not a condition," Nell said. "That's a beginning."

But the afternoon had other plans, because the afternoon came knocking — literally, three sharp raps at the street door, and through the frosted glass a silhouette that stood the way men stand when they carry orders in their pockets.

Vann crossed the hall ahead of everyone and opened the door a hand's width, and Nell, who followed because following was apparently now her permanent occupation, saw the clerk from the harbor office on the step — the talkative one, Mr. Renz, whose hat brim trembled slightly.

"Beg pardon for the hour," Renz said, too loudly, in the voice of a man reciting lines he'd been made to memorize. "Message for Miss Yarrow. From the consular office. The Saturday exchange at Pier Nine is — is cancelled." He swallowed. "Moved forward. Tomorrow morning. Six o'clock. The Colonel regrets the inconvenience."

He put the envelope in Vann's hand and walked away very fast, without looking back.

Behind him, down at the end of the street, a gray motorcar idled with a man in it who was not pretending to read anything at all.

Ada took the envelope. Her thumb broke the seal. She read it twice, and Nell watched the color leave her face in the orderly way that Ada did everything — column by column, bottom line last.

"He's closing the net," Vann said quietly. "He was never going to wait for Sunday."

"No," Ada said. She folded the note along its crease, precisely, twice. When she looked up at the three of them crowded in the doorway — the widow, the liar, and the girl who refused to be luggage — something in her expression had changed weight, the way ballast shifts in a hull.

"Then we stop planning like we have a week," she said, "and start planning like we have one night. Nell — fetch the flour-bin telegram. Marta — your friend with the boat. Everyone else sit down. We're doing the sums out loud, all of us, right now, and nobody in this house makes anybody's choice for them ever again."

Outside, the gray motorcar pulled away from the curb, slow as a held breath finally let out.
