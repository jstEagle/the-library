## 31. Brecht Closes the Net

Elias Vann had spent enough years around translators and smugglers to know that bad news rarely announced itself. It arrived wearing a good coat. It bought you coffee. It asked after your health.

So when a boy of perhaps fourteen pressed a folded scrap of paper into his hand outside the fish market on Wednesday morning—saying nothing, only looking at him with the grave solemnity of a child who has been paid in advance and takes his employment seriously—Vann knew before he opened it that the arithmetic of their ten days had just been done over by someone else.

*The clerk talked,* the note read, in Marta's cramped, furious hand. *They moved it. Tonight. Pier Nine at eleven.*

He stood very still among the barrels and the shouting gulls while the market went about its business around him. A woman argued over the price of herring with the passion most people reserved for theology. Somewhere a bell buoy clanged its idiot rhythm. The world, in other words, had not stopped to acknowledge that everything had changed.

Vann folded the note, considered eating it—he'd heard of couriers doing that, though the practice seemed to assume a sturdier stomach than his—and instead tucked it into his boot, where all important documents in this city seemed to live eventually.

Tonight. Not Saturday. Tonight.

Which meant Ada had perhaps fourteen hours to finish retyping a ledger she'd planned five days to copy, Nell had perhaps fourteen hours to be told anything at all, and he himself had fourteen hours to decide what kind of man he intended to be when the soldiers came.

He had, he reflected, walked into this city eight months ago with forged papers, a courier's fee burning a hole in his conscience, and no plan beyond *deliver the letters and disappear.* And now look at him. He had a household. He had opinions about how Marta arranged her kitchen. He had a ward who could pick apart his alibis like a crow unpicking a scarecrow. He had—

Well. He had Ada, which was a category of possession he didn't have a word for, in any of his six languages.

He started for home at a pace just short of running.

* * *

Ada took the news better than he did, which was somehow worse.

"Tonight," she repeated, not looking up from the typewriter. The keys clattered on under her fingers, steady as rain. "Of course tonight. Brecht was never going to let us keep our schedule. Schedules are for people who trust each other."

"Ada."

"I'm nearly through the third section. If I do nothing but type until nine, I can finish." She pulled the page free, squared it against the others in the biscuit tin that now held the future history of a nation, and fed in a fresh sheet. "You'll want tea. You've got your worried face on. It looks like a dog waiting to be scolded."

"My worried face has been complimented in three countries."

"Not by anyone whose opinion survived contact with you." But she glanced up then, and whatever she saw in him made her hands go still on the carriage return. The room got quieter. Even the typewriter seemed to hold its breath.

"They'll search the house first," she said. "Before they ever come near me. That's how Brecht works—I remember how he worked. He'll want the ledger found somewhere incriminating, so my arrest looks like tidiness rather than theft."

"So we move it."

"We move it tonight, before eleven, along with ourselves. Which means—" she counted silently, lips moving, and Vann loved her a little desperately for the fact that even facing ruin she counted like a bookkeeper, "—Marta can't walk far, the original goes with me, the copy goes somewhere Brecht will never think to look because it's too obvious, and Nell—"

"Nell doesn't know yet."

"No," Ada said, and the word came out flat as a slammed door. "Nell doesn't know. Because the moment Nell knows, Nell will do something brave, and I cannot afford brave tonight. I need her dull. I need her asleep."

"You've met Nell, haven't you?"

"Once or twice." Something almost like a smile crossed her face, brief as a fish rising. "Go find her. She's meant to be buying bread. She's been gone forty minutes, which means either she's talking to someone she shouldn't or reading something she shouldn't, and either way we're out of time."

* * *

He found Nell exactly where he'd feared: on the harbor wall, cross-legged, in animated conference with a boy selling chestnuts, both of them watching the pier district with the studied casualness of two conspirators who believed they were invisible.

"—so if the crates come off the *Kestrel* tonight instead of Friday, that means they moved everything up, which means somebody tipped them, which means—" Nell caught sight of Vann and had the decency to look approximately twelve percent guilty. "Oh. Hello. I'm buying bread."

"You're conducting naval intelligence."

"The bread was closed." She stood, brushing off her skirt with dignity. "Papa Ferenc said the ovens broke. Everyone knows the ovens didn't break; he's keeping them hot for the sailors. Anyway." Her eyes flicked to the harbor, and the brightness in them banked down to something older and more careful. "There are soldiers at the customs house. New ones. They weren't there yesterday. And the *Kestrel*'s berth has a chain across it, and men are unloading chain, Elias, which is not a thing you do to a ship you intend to let sail."

Vann looked at the harbor and saw what she saw: the slow closing of a fist. Crates stacked where they could be searched easily. Officials at every gate who hadn't been there Monday. The whole waterfront rearranging itself, gently, politely, into a trap.

"When did you last eat?" he asked, because one of them had to ask normal questions.

"Is that a real question or an adult question?"

"It's a question that buys us four minutes of walking before I answer yours."

She fell into step beside him. Four minutes lasted almost two.

"They're going to take her, aren't they," Nell said quietly. "At the exchange. They're going to take the ledger and take her and put Tomas on a boat alone, if they produce him at all. I did the sums weeks ago. Three berths, four of us, and Aunt Ada always leaves herself out of sums. She thinks nobody notices." She kicked a stone into the gutter with unnecessary force. "I notice."

Vann said nothing, because everything he could say was either a lie or a promise he couldn't keep, and Nell had a documented talent for detecting both.

"She gave you her papers once already," Nell said. "In 'thirty-eight. On the pier. Marta told me the shape of it, not the details—the details are locked up like the rest. But she stood on a pier and put a child on a boat and stayed behind with soldiers coming, and everyone called it heroism afterward, and she's never once called it that herself. She calls it bookkeeping." Nell stopped walking and faced him, chin up, thirteen years old and armored in fury. "I am not doing that sum again. Whatever she's planning, I want to hear it from her, tonight, or I will make so much noise that Brecht hears about it himself. Tell her that. Use those words. She'll understand them."

"What words?"

"*Bookkeeping,*" said Nell, "goes both ways."

* * *

By seven that evening the house on Tanner Lane had the atmosphere of a theater between acts—everyone moving with purpose, everyone speaking in code, everyone pretending the set wasn't being struck around them.

Marta presided over the packing like a general who had lost wars before and intended to lose this one with better luggage. She wrapped plates. She burned letters. She made Vann drink broth on the grounds that "you cannot outrun soldiers on an empty stomach, and you especially cannot outrun them on your personality."

"You knew," Vann said to her, quietly, by the stove. "About the clerk. How?"

"Marta knows things," said Marta.

"Marta."

She sighed and kept wrapping. "The customs clerk drinks at the Anchor. His wife drinks better gin than they can afford. I bought her a bottle on Tuesday and listened. That is the whole of espionage, Elias—it's shopping with follow-up questions." She looked up, and beneath the dryness her eyes were tired and clear. "Brecht sealed the district because he's frightened. Remember that. Men seal doors when they've begun to suspect the letter has left the house."

"And if the letter hasn't? If Ada's copy isn't safe—if he finds it—"

"Then we are all of us finished, and I would rather be finished having packed properly." She pushed the broth at him again. "Drink. And then go stand next to Ada, because that girl has been typing for six hours and has begun glaring at the semicolons, and in my experience that is the hour before either tears or decisions."

* * *

It was, in the end, both.

Vann found her at the typewriter with the lamp turned low and Nell's ultimatum hanging over her like weather. She'd heard it. Of course she'd heard it—Nell had delivered it standing in the doorway at full volume, then withdrawn with the satisfaction of a diplomat who has filed her protest and gone to bed.

"She's right," Ada said, before he could speak.

"I know."

"I gave you my seat on that pier because you were fifteen and I was twenty-two and someone had to do the arithmetic, and I have been doing that arithmetic for twenty years as though it were a virtue." She fed the last page into the machine. Her hands, he noticed, were shaking—not much, just enough to show. "And now the sums have changed and I don't know how to change them back. There are still three berths. There are still four of us, plus Tomas, plus whatever Brecht intends to subtract. And I promised myself I would not choose wrong twice."

"You didn't choose wrong in thirty-eight."

"Didn't I?" She laughed, short and unamused. "I chose silence. For twenty years I chose silence, and silence is how Brecht got fat and Father got buried under a clean name and Tomas got to be dead for two decades while writing letters. Every quiet thing I did felt safe, Elias, and every single one of them was a wall, and walls don't protect houses. They just make sure no one hears what happens inside."

The carriage bell rang. She pulled the page free—the last one, the final leaf of twenty years—and laid it on the stack with a gentleness that undid him more than tears would have.

Then she did something he hadn't seen her do. She reached across the table and took his hand.

"Elias Vann," she said formally, "you came to my door with letters from the dead, and you stayed to become a living nuisance, and somewhere in between I stopped being able to imagine this house without you in it, which is inconvenient, given the schedule." Her grip tightened. "Whatever happens at Pier Nine tonight—whatever I have to trade, whoever I have to leave—you should know the ledger was never the truest thing I carried out of that country. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you," Vann said, and found his voice had gone rough. "Though I'd argue about the nuisance part. I prefer *fixture.*"

"*Fixture* implies permanence." She smiled, small and real. "Let's survive until morning and then negotiate terms."

* * *

They moved at half past nine.

The plan, such as it was, had the elegance of all plans made under deadline: Marta would go by tram to her cousin's over the laundry, carrying nothing that mattered and therefore carrying nothing that could hang her. The original ledger would go with Ada in the false bottom of a market basket, beneath cabbages, because—as Marta observed—no soldier alive searches under cabbages; they fear worms. The copy, sewn into its oilcloth, would go where no one sensible would look: mailed, tomorrow morning, by ordinary post, to the offices of three newspapers, with postage already affixed and instructions written in Nell's best schoolgirl hand so that any intercepted parcel would look like a child's pen-pal project.

"And the berths?" Vann asked, as they gathered in the kitchen for the last time. "Three berths, and Brecht holding them like cards he never intends to play honestly."

"Berths are promises," Ada said, pulling on her coat. "Brecht breaks promises. But piers are places, and places can be crowded." She looked at each of them in turn—Marta, straight-backed with her hatpin deployed like artillery; Nell, pale but upright, sworn to dullness and visibly suffering from it; himself, with his forged papers and his honest heart, the strangest inventory he'd ever owned. "We go to Pier Nine early. We watch. We learn whether he's brought Tomas or a photograph of Tomas. And when the net closes—" her jaw set, "—we decide, in the moment, who slips through it. No speeches. No sacrifices announced in advance. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Marta.

"Agreed," said Nell, in the tone of someone agreeing to a ceasefire she fully intended to violate.

Outside, the bells of the harbor church began to strike ten, each note falling into the next like stones dropped down a well, counting out the hour in that patient, terrible way clocks have—reminding them, and the whole sealed district with them, that time was no longer something they had.

Time, tonight, was something that was happening *to* them.

Vann held the door, and they stepped together into the dark.
