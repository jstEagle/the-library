## 32. The Night Before the Ship

### Ada

The lamp in the kitchen had a habit of buzzing when it was asked to do too much, and tonight it was being asked to illuminate four people, a pot of Marta's stew, and the entire accumulated weight of twenty years of things unsaid. It buzzed accordingly.

Ada sat with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking. Across the table, Nell was watching her the way Nell watched everything—openly, hungrily, without the decency of pretending not to. Vann sat at the corner, his long frame folded onto a chair built for smaller men and more honest purposes. Marta stood at the stove even though the stew was done, because Marta believed that difficult conversations were best conducted while holding a wooden spoon.

"So," Ada said.

"So," said Marta.

"That's a fine beginning," Vann offered. "Two so's. The historians will weep."

"Let them," Marta said. "They weren't here."

Ada looked at the table—the scarred wood, the ring where her father's coffee cup had left its mark before Ada was old enough to know what a mark like that would come to mean—and understood that if they did not speak tonight, they never would. Tomorrow there would be soldiers, and a pier, and arithmetic. Tonight there was only this kitchen, and the lamp, and the four of them.

"I gave up my berth," she said. "In thirty-eight. On the *Corvella*."

Nell's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth.

"There were two berths," Ada went on, in the flat voice she used for figures that wouldn't balance any other way, "and three of us. Me, Tomas, and our mother. I was nineteen. I told the harbormaster I'd changed my mind about sailing. I told him I'd stay with an aunt." She glanced at Marta. "Which was true, though I hadn't met you yet."

"And your mother?" Nell asked quietly.

"Took my berth. Tomas took his. The ship made it as far as the straits." Ada turned the cup in her hands. "You all know what happened after. The route was sold. Half the passengers were taken off at the port of call. My mother among them. Tomas among them—though it seems the taking didn't take, in his case."

"The berth you gave up," Vann said slowly, "was the one that—"

"Yes."

The word landed on the table and nobody picked it up. The lamp buzzed. Somewhere down the street a dog announced its opinions on the night, and was overruled by a second dog.

"If I'd sailed," Ada said, "it would have been me taken off at the straits. Or not. There's no way to know. That's the cruelty of it—you can carry a what-if for twenty years and it weighs exactly the same whether it's justified or not." She set the cup down. "I've never told anyone that story. Not even Marta."

Marta's wooden spoon paused midair. "Not even me," Marta confirmed, to the room at large, in the tone of a woman adding a line to a ledger she intended to present later.

"You kept enough silences for both of us," Ada said. "It seemed rude to add mine."

* * *

### Vann

Vann had been in many kitchens in many countries, and he had developed a theory: you could tell everything about a household by how it handled silence. Some houses filled it with noise, terrified of the gaps. Some houses weaponized it. This house did something he'd rarely seen—it *inhabited* silence, the way a family inhabits an old coat, knowing where every hole is.

He decided, since Ada had gone first, that honesty was apparently the evening's currency, and he was embarrassingly overdrawn.

"When I took the courier job," he said, "I told you I found the letters among a dead man's effects. That was true. What I didn't say is that I was paid to find them. Paid by Brecht's office, through two intermediaries, because Brecht doesn't like fingerprints." He watched their faces. "I told myself it was just delivery. Paper moves; I move paper. It's a respectable trade for a man whose country stopped existing between one Tuesday and the next."

"And now?" Nell asked.

"Now I know the paper was a person. Your brother. A trap with handwriting." He smiled, because smiling was the tool nearest to hand. "So tomorrow I intend to be a very unreliable employee."

Marta snorted. It was, he suspected, the highest compliment her vocabulary contained for a man in his position.

"My turn for a confession," he added, before courage could reconsider. "I nearly ran. Three days ago. Train tickets bought, bag half-packed. I got as far as the station square." He looked at Ada. "And then I thought about what happens to people who spend their lives delivering other people's mail without ever reading it. So I came back and read everything."

"What stopped you?" Nell asked. "Really?"

"Honestly? The stew." He gestured at the pot. "I've eaten in eleven countries, and this is the first kitchen where leaving felt like it would cost more than staying."

"It's cabbage," Marta said. "It costs almost nothing."

"That," said Vann, "is the miracle of it."

Nell laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the kind of sound that reminded him what all this was actually for—and for a moment the kitchen held something that was almost peace, wearing borrowed clothes.

* * *

### Marta

Marta had planned, for sixty years of life, to die with her mouth shut. It was a reasonable plan. It had served her through her husband's trial, through the war, through the long gray decades of saying *he was a good man* at parties where everyone knew he was not.

But the girl had given away a berth. And the translator had come back from a train station. And Nell was seventeen and deserved, at minimum, one honest adult before the world finished forming her.

So Marta set down the spoon—which required actual physical effort; her hand resisted—and sat.

"I knew," she said.

She let it sit there. Let them work out which *knew*, because there were several, and she intended to address each in order, like courses.

"I knew about your father," she said to Ada. "Not the details. Enough. He signed things. Small things at first—permits, lists, names of people who could be passed over or not. Everyone told themselves it was small. That's how it works. No one wakes up a collaborator; they go to sleep slightly compromised, every night, for years." Her voice stayed level. She'd rehearsed level for decades. "Your mother knew too. Less than I knew, but some. We agreed—she and I—that the children would be told nothing until they were old enough to carry it."

"I was old enough at nineteen," Ada said quietly.

"No one is ever old enough. That's the lie we tell ourselves so we don't have to say the truth, which is that we were protecting ourselves." Marta folded her hands. "When the evacuation failed, I told myself the silence was still protection. By then it was just habit wearing protection's coat. I am sorry. I'm telling you this now because tomorrow someone may pay a price, and I refuse to let any of you pay it carrying my secrets instead of your own."

Nell was staring at her. "You're not my blood aunt at all, are you. You're not anyone's aunt."

"I'm everyone's aunt," Marta said. "It's a job title, not a genealogy. I earned it honestly—by interfering where I wasn't wanted for longer than most women hold any profession."

That got a wet, hiccuping laugh out of Nell, and Marta counted it a fair return on sixty years of investment.

"One more thing," she said. "Tomorrow, whatever happens at that pier—I want it recorded somewhere, by someone, that Yarrow family dinners were loud. That there was music. Your mother sang badly, Ada, and I say that with love, and I never once told her, and that is the last small silence I intend to keep." She stood, retrieved the spoon, and pointed it at the pot. "Now eat. Cabbage waits for no confession."

* * *

### Nell

Nell had spent her whole life believing she was the footnote in this family—the ward, the afterthought, the girl who'd arrived aged six with a cardboard suitcase and no history worth mentioning. Tonight, sitting in the lamplight with three people who had each just handed over a piece of themselves like bread broken at a table, she understood the footnote had been reading the book all along.

But fairness cut both ways. If they were emptying pockets, she should empty hers.

"I read the ledger," she said.

Three faces turned. Vann's eyebrows performed a small acrobatic routine.

"The hidden part. Weeks ago. I was looking for wrapping paper." She lifted her chin. "I know about the code. I know about Brecht. I know—" her voice wobbled and she steadied it, furious at it, "—I know about your father, and about the berth, and I know why Marta checks the street twice before lowering the blind. I've known most of it for weeks and I said nothing, because I thought if I pretended not to see, it couldn't touch me."

"And now?" Ada asked gently.

"Now I think pretending is how good people end up signing small things." She looked around the table. "I'm not giving anything back. Whatever happens tomorrow, I've decided. I'm not getting on that ship unless everyone I love is either on it or safe behind it. I've done the arithmetic. I'm allowed to do the arithmetic too."

"Nell—"

"You always say my name like a full stop," Nell said. "I'm asking you, just for tonight, to use it like a comma."

The kitchen went quiet again—but it was a different quiet now, swept clean, the kind that comes after a storm has taken the roof and everyone discovers the stars were right there the whole time.

It was Vann who broke it, softly. "For what it's worth, the arithmetic says four berths, three available, and one colonel with terrible taste in employees. Someone should tell the shipping company their math is insulting."

"Somebody should tell the shipping company lots of things," Marta said darkly.

* * *

### Ada

Later—after the dishes, after Marta had gone up muttering about resistance contacts and the moral fiber of clerks, after Nell had fallen asleep at the table mid-sentence defending her right to do arithmetic—Ada sat by the window with the lamp turned low and the typewriter case open at her feet.

Vann appeared beside her with two cups of tea, handing her one as though they'd arranged it, which in some country of the heart perhaps they had.

"They'll sleep," he said, nodding at the stairs.

"Marta doesn't sleep before operations. She says sleeping before operations is how you get surprised."

"She said that tonight?"

"She says it most nights. It covers a multitude of naps."

He laughed, then sobered, looking out the window at the dark harbor district, where the cranes stood against the sky like patient gallows. "Ada. If it goes wrong tomorrow—"

"It won't."

"It might."

"Then we'll improvise," she said. "We're a household of improvisers. You forge, Marta schemes, Nell disobeys beautifully, and I retype things." She looked down at the typewriter case, and felt the strange calm that had been settling over her all evening—the calm of a woman who has finally put down a weight and discovered her arms still work. "Do you know what I figured out tonight? Every silence in this house was kept by someone who believed they were protecting someone else. Twenty years of people guarding people from the truth, and it nearly destroyed all of us anyway. The truth was going to arrive eventually. It always does. The only question was whether it arrived as a knife or as a letter."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow it arrives as both," Ada said. "But I'll decide which hand holds which."

Vann studied her for a moment. "You know, when I knocked on your door with those letters, I thought I was bringing you grief."

"You were."

"But it's not grief, is it. It's—" he searched, "—an accounting."

Ada smiled, and touched the typewriter case. "Everything," she said, "is an accounting. The trick is making sure the books balance before the auditor arrives." She stood, and carried the case toward the back room, where the ribbon was fresh and the night was long. "Go to sleep, Elias."

"Aren't you—"

"I have a great deal of typing to do," she said, "and only until dawn to make the truth portable."

Behind her, in the kitchen, the lamp finally stopped buzzing—as if even it understood that some pages were meant to be copied in the dark.
