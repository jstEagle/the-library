## 33. Ledger and Typewriter

The typewriter had come with them out of the old country in a crate marked SEWING MACHINES — PARTS, because Marta had insisted that no customs officer on earth would open a box labeled sewing machine parts twice. He had opened it once, in '38, at the border crossing outside Hradec, and Ada had watched his fingers hover over the ribbon spool while her heart performed arithmetic she still remembered: one girl, sixteen years old, worth exactly nothing if he looked closer, worth everything if he didn't.

He hadn't looked closer. He'd wanted a cigarette instead, and Marta had given him two, and that was how their entire surviving estate had crossed a border on the strength of tobacco and a mislabeled crate.

Twenty years later the typewriter sat on the kitchen table under the lamp, its keys worn to the shape of other people's words, and Ada sat down before it at eleven o'clock at night the way she had sat down before ledgers every working day of her exile: spine straight, sleeves pinned, tea cooling at her right hand because she took it at her left like a left-handed person who had been corrected as a child and never quite forgave the world for it.

She squared the original ledger half in front of her — Tomas's half, the remainder, recovered from its hiding place inside the lining of her mother's hatbox, where it had slept through two moves, one flood scare, and Nell's entire childhood — and she did not touch it yet.

First she tested the machine. She typed the alphabet. She typed the alphabet again. On the third pass, the *q* stuck, as it always stuck, and she pressed it four times until it loosened with a small metallic grudge.

"Still arguing with the *q*," said Marta from the doorway.

"The *q* has always been a coward. It only works under pressure."

"Like most men."

Marta came in carrying her shawl and the particular expression she wore when she intended to stay up all night supervising something while pretending merely to be present in the room. She pulled her chair to the corner where the lamplight wouldn't reach her eyes but would reach her knitting, which was not knitting at all tonight but a ball of black wool she was winding and unwinding like a woman strangling time itself.

"You should sleep," Ada said.

"I should have slept in 1938," Marta said. "Here I am anyway."

Ada almost smiled. Almost. Her mouth was busy holding its shape around the thing she was about to do.

* * *

The plan was Vann's originally, back when he'd still been a liar with good posture, and it had survived his confession the way a good idea survives most things: by being correct. Two copies. One to surrender to Brecht at Pier Nine, one to go into the world by other hands entirely. Brecht would take possession of a ledger and believe himself safe, and safety, for a man like Brecht, was the longest rope anyone could hand him.

But there was a problem with two copies, and the problem was that Ada Yarrow had spent twenty years being a bookkeeper, and a bookkeeper knows what a copy is.

"A copy is a translation," she told the table quietly. "And every translation loses something."

Because here was the truth of the ledger, the truth that lived in her hands and nowhere else: the pages were only half the code. The cipher's third layer — the confession layer, the one that had cost her a full night's sleep and several years off her life — existed partly in the numbers and partly in her memory of how Tomas had arranged them, the little crooked habits of a boy who'd dotted his i's too high and carried sevens wrong on purpose so teachers would leave him alone. Anyone could retype the figures. Only Ada could retype them *truthfully*, and even then only if she was very careful, and very awake, and very much herself.

So the copy would be hers, made by her hand, checked against her memory, line by line. And the original —

The original she would give to Brecht.

Her stomach objected to this. She let it. A bookkeeper learns early that the stomach is not a department with voting rights.

* * *

She began at midnight.

The first page went slowly, because the first page always does. She typed each figure from the original, then closed her eyes and recalled it from memory — the number as it lived in her head, where she had kept it since she was sixteen and memorized everything in one terrible burning hour — and compared the two. When they matched, she typed it clean. When they didn't match, she stopped, drank her cold tea, and asked herself which version was lying, and it was astonishing, it was always astonishing, how often the answer was neither: both were true, and the discrepancy itself was part of the code, a deliberate wobble Tomas had built in so that any single transcription would fail without the second reading.

Her brother had designed a ledger that required love to decode. You had to know how he thought. You had to have grown up watching him cheat at cards.

"Oh, you insufferable genius," she murmured to the page, and heard Marta's wool pause, and resume.

By two o'clock she had eleven pages. By three, nineteen. The house breathed around her — the tick of the stove, the sea doing its patient work against the harbor wall, and upstairs, once, the creak of a floorboard that meant Nell was awake, which meant Nell was listening, which meant Ada would find her teacup moved tomorrow and say nothing, because some surveillance you simply allow.

At half past three, footsteps on the stairs, and Vann came in with his coat over his pajamas like a man who dressed for emergencies even in bed. His hair stood up on one side. Ada found this unfairly endearing and filed the finding away for destruction.

"You're supposed to be sleeping," she said.

"I am sleeping. This is a dream. In the dream you've been typing for three and a half hours without stopping and your handwriting hand is cramping." He came around behind her, gently lifted her right hand off the desk, and worked the fingers straight with his thumbs. "There. The dream continues."

"You're insufferable."

"I'm told it runs in my family. All none of them." He pulled a chair up beside her — not across, beside; he had learned that about her, that she hated being loomed over at work — and looked at the stack of finished pages. "How many?"

"Thirty-one done. Forty-two total."

"And the originals?"

"In the hatbox. Where your paymaster can't reach them and neither, incidentally, can you."

"I respect that enormously."

"You should. It's the only reason I'm letting you stay in the room." She fed a fresh sheet into the platen and rolled it true. "Actually, no. It isn't. Hand me the original, please. Page thirty-two."

He fetched it from the hatbox with the reverence of a man handling a relic, which, Ada supposed, was exactly what it was: a reliquary of everyone who hadn't made it out, bound in water-stained board. He set it down, open, weighted with the salt cellar at either side.

For a while the only sounds were keys and sea. Then:

"May I ask you something improper?"

"You may ask. Improperness is not guaranteed an answer."

"When you finish the last page — when the copy exists and the original goes to Brecht — what happens to the truth? The real one. If the newspapers don't print it. If the mails are watched. If—" He stopped himself, which was growth; six months ago he'd have run the whole catastrophic sentence to ground. "What if it fails?"

Ada typed the final digit of page thirty-two, checked it against memory, and let the silence sit between them long enough to develop a personality.

"My mother used to say," she said finally, "that paper is the most patient weapon ever invented. It doesn't need you to win. It just needs to exist somewhere, waiting, until someone picks it up." She tapped the finished page. "Brecht can burn one copy. He cannot burn all of them, because he won't know how many there are. That's the whole trick, Elias. Certainty is expensive. Doubt is free, and I'm mailing it everywhere."

"That," said Vann, "is the coldest sentence I've ever heard spoken next to a teapot."

"I keep the teapot for warmth. The sentences do the rest."

* * *

At five in the morning she hit the middle section — the names — and had to stop.

Not because the typing was hard. Because the names were. Row after row of them, the network people, coded as freight categories in the original and decoded now in plain type: route captains, harbor contacts, the doctor in Brno, the schoolteacher who'd forged transit papers, and — her pen hovering, her keys silent — her father.

Aron Yarrow, entered in Tomas's careful cipher as *supplier, grain category, compromised 1936*, and beneath it, in the confession layer only Ada could read, the fuller record: the concessions, the small betrayals that fed the large ones, the way fear had eaten a decent man from the edges inward until the man who sold the escape route's schedule wasn't a stranger at all but someone who had taught her to ride a bicycle.

Marta's wool had gone still.

"He didn't choose it," Marta said, into the quiet. Not for the first time. But this time Ada didn't wave it off, because tonight was for the whole truth, unburned and unedited, and the whole truth included this: that she had retyped her father's name forty minutes ago and her hand had not shaken, and she was still deciding what that meant about her.

"No," Ada said. "He didn't choose it. He chose it a little bit at a time, which is worse, or easier — I've gone back and forth on that for twenty years." She rolled a fresh page. "Tomas decided. Look." She turned the confession page toward Marta. "He wrote it down. Knowing what writing it down would cost our name forever. He decided we could survive shame better than silence. And he was sixteen when he decided it."

"Sixteen-year-olds are frequently the only honest people in the room," Marta said. "It's why we lock up the liquor around them."

Ada laughed — actually laughed, at five in the morning, over her father's treason — and the laugh cracked something loose in her chest that had been rusted shut, and she wiped her eyes with her wrist and got back to work, because the typewriter didn't care about her feelings and that was, she reflected, the great comfort of machines.

* * *

Nell appeared at seven, in her nightgown, with the specific gravity of a teenager who has been awake all night pretending otherwise.

"You look terrible," Nell announced, pouring herself tea from the pot Ada had refilled twice.

"You look awake."

"I'm always awake. You've all just agreed to pretend otherwise, which I appreciate, because it lets me hear things." She settled onto the table bench, chin on her fists, and surveyed the stacks: finished pages squared and clipped, originals returned to the hatbox, the typewriter gleaming faintly with use. "That's it, isn't it. That's the ledger."

"It's the ledger," Ada said.

"All of it? Everything you've never told anybody?"

"Everything I've never told anybody," Ada confirmed, "except everybody in this room, apparently, by Thursday."

Vann snorted into his tea. Nell grinned, then sobered, looking at the pages with an expression older than her face had any business holding.

"Will it work?" she asked. "The newspapers. Will it actually — will people read it and *know*?" 

Ada considered lying to her, briefly, the way you consider taking a shortcut you know ends at a cliff. Then she remembered the plan for tomorrow, and the berth arithmetic, and the fact that Nell had already refused once to be protected by ignorance and would refuse again.

"Some will read it," Ada said. "Some will want to burn it. Some will read it and do nothing, because doing nothing is a popular sport. But enough. Enough will know. That's all truth ever gets, Nell. Enough."

Nell nodded slowly, absorbing this, and then — because she was fifteen and mercy was not yet among her skills — said: "You misspelled *harbor* on page twelve."

Ada froze. Checked. Page twelve. Third paragraph.

She had.

*HARBOUR.*

Twenty years of exile in an English port city, leaking into the one document that mattered, like damp through plaster.

"Nobody," Ada said, with tremendous dignity, "will notice."

"Brecht will," said Nell. "If he knows the original, he'll know your copy's fake the moment he reads the spelling."

The kitchen went very quiet. Vann set down his cup. Even Marta's wool stopped.

And Ada Yarrow, bookkeeper, keeper of silences, sat back in her chair and felt something extraordinary happen in her chest, which was not dread at all but delight — fierce, unholy, completely inappropriate delight.

"No," she said softly. "No, he'll think it's a slip. A tired woman's slip, the night before an exchange. People make slips." Her mind was already moving, quick and bright. "But if the copy is *supposed* to look imperfect... if the forgery wears its flaws openly..." She looked at Vann. "Get me the original. Every flaw in my copy has to be a flaw I chose. If I'm going to lie to Colonel Brecht, I'm going to lie like a professional."

"You are a professional," Vann said, already moving to the hatbox.

"I'm a bookkeeper. Tomorrow I become a counterfeiter. There's a difference, and the difference is nerve." She rolled the offending page out of the machine, squared it, and reached for a fresh sheet. "Nell. More errors. Find me more."

Nell's grin spread slow and wicked, and for one moment the whole kitchen looked like what it had never once managed to look like in twenty years: a family firm, all hands employed, everyone in on the secret.

They worked until the light came up gray over the harbor. Ada retyped page twelve and salted six more pages with chosen imperfections — a transposed date here, a smudged initial there, nothing a hurried man would catch, everything a suspicious one would seize on wrongly. At half past eight she typed the final line of the final page, checked it against the memory she had carried across borders and oceans and twenty years of silence, and found it true.

Then she did the last thing. She took the copy — the true copy, flawless, every cipher intact — folded it into oilcloth, sealed it, and handed it across the table to Vann.

"Three envelopes," she said. "Three papers. You post them yourself, separately, from different boxes, before you sleep tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight."

"I understand."

"And Elias." She held his eyes. "Whatever happens at Pier Nine — whatever goes wrong — those envelopes go out first. Promise me."

Vann looked down at the packet in his hands, and then at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop performing charm altogether, which on his face was like watching a building take off a mask.

"They go out first," he said. "I promise."

Outside, the gulls were making their morning argument about the fishing boats, and somewhere down toward Pier Nine, soldiers were counting crates for a ship that sailed in two days, and Colonel Brecht was drinking his coffee believing that tomorrow he would own the last dangerous secret in Europe.

Ada washed the teacups, pinned her sleeves, and put the original ledger in her handbag, where it lay against the leather light as paper and heavy as everyone she had ever loved.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow at the pier.
