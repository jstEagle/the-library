## 34. The Exchange at Pier Nine

The fog came off the harbor before dawn, the way it always did in October, and Ada stood at her window watching it swallow the cranes one by one until the port looked like a drawing half-erased.

She had not slept. Nobody had pretended otherwise. Marta had spent the night in the kitchen chair with a blanket over her knees and her eyes open, and when Ada came down at four o'clock to make tea she found Nell curled on the settle, fully dressed, pretending with great dignity to be asleep.

"Nell," Ada said softly.

"I'm asleep," said Nell.

"You're snoring with your eyes open."

"It's a talent." She opened them anyway and sat up, hair wild, thirteen years old and suddenly looking much older in the gray light. "Is today the day?"

"Today's the day."

Nell nodded as though Ada had announced a piano recital. Then her chin wobbled, just once, and Ada crossed the room and held her, and felt her daughter—she had stopped correcting herself years ago; *ward* was a word for documents—breathe against her shoulder.

"He'll come back," Nell said into the wool of Ada's cardigan. "Tomas. He has to."

"People do," Ada said, "when you stop expecting them to."

It was the sort of thing Marta said, and it sounded better in Marta's mouth, but it would do.

* * *

By seven they were all in the kitchen, which had become over the past weeks something like a war council conducted around a bread board. Vann stood by the stove turning an egg with more attention than any egg deserved. Marta laid out slices of the dark loaf she'd bought yesterday from the baker who owed her a favor, and did not explain why a baker owing her a favor mattered, and nobody asked.

Ada set the ledger on the table between the salt cellar and the marmalade.

They all looked at it. It was a plain thing, that ledger—half-bound in cracked leather, its burned corner long since trimmed square, its pages dense with columns of figures that meant nothing to a customs inspector and everything to a certain kind of man. Twenty years she had carried it, first across a border sewn into a coat lining, then through two cities and three rented rooms, and now here it sat next to the marmalade like a guest who'd overstayed.

"Once it leaves this table," Marta said, "we don't get it back."

"That's rather the point," said Vann.

"I wasn't talking to you, translator."

"Marta," Ada said, mildly, and Marta subsided with the air of a woman permitting herself to be governed.

The plan was simple, which Ada distrusted on principle; the plans that had saved her life had all been complicated, and the ones that nearly ended it had been simple. But Brecht had set the terms himself, in a note delivered by a clerk with damp hands: *Pier Nine, eleven o'clock. Bring the remainder. You will have your brother and three berths on the *Corvane*. No soldiers will approach you. This is the last time I will be generous.*

Three berths. She had counted them a hundred times since. Tomas. Vann. Nell. And herself, standing on the pier with empty hands, which was—she kept telling herself—a fair price. She had done the arithmetic so many times the numbers had gone soft and warm, like coins rubbed smooth. A life for a life. A book for a family. Any reasonable bookkeeper would call the accounts balanced.

The trouble with being a reasonable bookkeeper, Ada thought, buttering toast she wouldn't taste, is that you forget some debts aren't yours to pay.

* * *

They walked down through the fish market, which was already roaring awake—gulls screaming over the gutting tables, a boy hauling ice, a woman arguing about the price of herring with the passion other people reserved for religion. Nell walked between Ada and Vann, holding a parcel of sandwiches Marta had insisted upon ("Hostages who faint are inconvenient hostages"), and Vann wore his second-best coat because, he'd explained, the best coat made him look like a man with something worth taking.

Ada carried the ledger in a canvas satchel against her hip. Her hand had been resting on it so long that the strap had worn a groove into her palm she suspected would outlast the morning.

At the mouth of Fisherman's Row, Marta stopped.

This had also been planned. Marta was not coming to the pier; Marta had errands, Marta said, and a face the old harbor clerks might remember, and there was no sense spending all their liabilities in one place. But the way she stopped—the way her hand came up and gripped Ada's wrist, brief and hard—was not in the plan.

"Whatever happens down there," Marta said, "remember what I told you about your father. Both halves of it. The bad half and the reason."

"The reason being?"

"That silence isn't always cowardice. Sometimes it's just love wearing its work clothes." She released Ada's wrist and straightened her hat. "Go on, then. You'll be late, and Colonel Brecht strikes me as a man who counts other people's lateness and never his own."

"Marta—" Ada began, and didn't know how to finish it. *Thank you* seemed too small. *Forgive me* too large.

Marta solved it by kissing her once on each cheek, briskly, as though stamping a document, and walking off toward the tram stop with her spine very straight.

"She's going to do something," Nell said, watching her go.

"Marta is always doing something."

"Yes, but today she's doing something *dressed like that.*"

Ada looked. Marta had put on the good brooch—the amber one she claimed was paste and everyone knew was not. Ada filed this away in the growing drawer of things she could not afford to think about yet.

* * *

Pier Nine smelled of tar and cold iron. The *Corvane* sat at anchor beyond the mole, a gray shape in the fog with her funnels breathing smoke, and along the quay stood the usual morning population of a departure: porters with trunks, families clustered around luggage like sheep around a fold, a clergyman saying something consoling to a weeping woman. Ordinary enough. Except that there were too few gulls over Pier Nine itself, and Ada had learned long ago that birds know what people only suspect.

Brecht was waiting beneath the crane housing, and he had dressed for the occasion. Gray overcoat, gloves, a folded newspaper under one arm as though he'd simply stepped out of a café. Two men flanked him at a respectful distance, wearing the loose jackets and patient faces of soldiers told to look civilian. Further off, near the harbor office, a third leaned against a bollard smoking, and Ada marked the position of every one of them the way she'd once marked figures in a column—automatically, without letting her eyes linger anywhere long enough to alarm anyone.

"Frau Yarrow." Brecht's voice was pleasant. That was the worst of him; twenty years of hearing his name spoken in whispers had prepared her for a monster, and instead there was this tidy man with a kind uncle's face, as though cruelty had been sent away to be pressed. "You're punctual. I find punctuality reassuring in a businesswoman."

"Where is my brother?"

"Direct. Good." He glanced at Vann. "Herr Vann. You've been busy."

"I've been unemployed," Vann said lightly. "It looks similar."

Brecht almost smiled. Then he extended one gloved hand. "The remainder, please."

Ada opened the satchel. Her fingers were steady—twenty years of practice—and she drew out the ledger and held it a moment, feeling its weight, feeling the weight underneath the weight. Somewhere in those columns was her father's name. Somewhere was the confession Tomas had coded into the freight totals, line after line, believing she would live to read it. Somewhere was the whole rotten architecture of the escape route's betrayal, spelled out in numbers only she and a dead-seeming man could parse.

She put it in Brecht's gloved hand.

He weighed it too, in his way—thumbed the pages, checked the binding, ran a finger down a column of figures with the satisfaction of a collector confirming provenance. "You've kept it well."

"It was never mine to keep badly."

"No," Brecht agreed. "It was your family's. And now it's mine, and your family is concluded." He raised two fingers, casually, the gesture of a man hailing a cab.

From behind the crane housing, soldiers emerged—six of them, rifles slung, boots ringing on the wet planking—and from the harbor office door came two more, and the smoking man by the bollard dropped his cigarette and ground it out with terrible thoroughness.

Beside her, Vann said, very quietly, "Ah."

"Ah," Ada agreed.

"You were told no soldiers would approach," Vann said to Brecht, louder, with the tone of a man raising a point of order at a meeting that has already been dissolved.

"I lied," Brecht said. He said it without heat, almost apologetically, the way a man admits to rounding up in an invoice. "You'll find, Herr Vann, that generosity is a currency. I extended credit. Credit is called in. That is the whole of commerce." He tucked the ledger under his arm beside the newspaper, and for a moment the two objects sat together—news and history, both of them, in different bindings—and Ada thought, absurdly, of her mother saying *never let a man hold your paper, Ada, he'll hold you.*

"Tomas," she said. Her voice came out flat and even, and she was grateful to it. "You said my brother."

"And I produce him." Brecht turned and nodded toward the harbor office.

A door opened. Two soldiers came out, and between them, blinking in the gray light, a man in a prison-gray coat walked down the ramp onto the pier.

Twenty years. Twenty years of a name spoken carefully, of an empty chair at memory's table, of a grave with nothing under it. Ada had imagined this moment perhaps a thousand times and in none of the versions had she been calm, and she was not calm now—but her body, traitorously practical, simply went still, the way it had gone still at nineteen on a station platform while the world burned behind her.

He was thinner than the boy she remembered. His hair had gone gray at the temples. He walked with a stiffness that spoke of years on stone floors, and he was squinting against the fog-light like a man who hadn't seen open sky in a long time.

But when his eyes found hers, they were Tomas's eyes. They were the eyes of a boy who had taught her a cipher using the names of their grandmother's cats, and they filled, and he said, in the accent she'd kept alive in her own mouth for two decades like a coal banked in ash:

"Adka. You got *old.*"

"You got *dead,*" Ada said, and her voice broke on the second word, and she didn't care.

They were permitted three steps toward each other before the soldier between them raised a hand. Three steps was enough to see his hands—scarred, steady—and the small scar above his eyebrow from the bicycle accident of 1921, and the way he stood with his left foot slightly turned out exactly as he always had, as if the whole world might change except the angle of Tomas Yarrow's feet.

"They told me you'd come," he said. "I didn't believe them. I told them my sister doesn't trade with colonels."

"I don't," Ada said. "I trade with liars. There's a difference. Colonels at least announce themselves."

Something flickered in Brecht's pleasant face—not anger, worse, amusement. "Touching. Truly. I have a weakness for family reunions; they're so rarely repeated." He gestured, and the soldiers' formation shifted, closing—not around Brecht, Ada saw, but around *them*, around Ada and Vann and Nell and Tomas, folding inward like a hand making a fist.

"The berths," Vann said. His charm was gone now, stripped clean off, and underneath it was something harder and younger. "The *Corvane* sails at noon. You gave your word—three berths, verified passage."

"I gave my word," Brecht agreed, "to a woman bringing me a ledger, in exchange for which she and her dependents would leave my jurisdiction forever, carrying nothing, testifying to nothing, existing nowhere. Instead, Frau Yarrow brings me the ledger in the company of a courier who has been double-dealing with my own clerk for a month"—Vann's jaw tightened—"—and a child, and a brother whose continued existence is, frankly, an embarrassment I have paid to conceal twice already." He smiled at Ada with genuine regret. "You must see the difficulty. Every person on this pier is a loose thread. The ledger was meant to cut them all off at once. Instead you've brought them to me *tied in a bow.*"

"So there were never any berths," Nell said.

Everyone looked at her. She stood with her chin up and her fists at her sides, and her voice only shook a little.

"There was a ship," Brecht conceded. "There are berths. Whether they are *yours*—" He spread his gloved hands, a small elegant shrug that contained six rifles.

Ada's mind, that tireless clerk, was already working the problem. Six soldiers visible, probably more. One gangway, controlled. The *Corvane* too far out to matter. Vann at her right, coiled; Nell at her left, her hand finding Ada's sleeve; Tomas three paces off with a soldier's grip on his arm, and his eyes moving over the pier with an alertness that said twenty years in cages had taught him things about doors and moments.

And then, over the water, thin and clear through the fog, came the sound of a ship's whistle—one blast, two—and beneath it, nearer, the chuff and clang of the tram line coming down Fisherman's Row, earlier than the schedule, running fast, bell clanging in a way that suggested the driver had been persuaded rather than instructed.

Ada did not turn her head. But beside her, Vann exhaled very slowly through his nose, and she heard him murmur, barely a breath, pitched for her alone:

"Now, Frau Yarrow, would be an excellent time to remember whatever your aunt keeps in that handbag."

On the tram, silhouetted against the lifting fog, a small straight-backed figure stood at the front rail wearing a good hat and an amber brooch that was not paste, and Colonel Brecht—who had spent twenty years buying silence—had not yet seen her arrive.
