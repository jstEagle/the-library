## 35. Marta's Gambit

Marta Yarrow had spent seventy-one years learning that the difference between courage and foolishness was mostly a matter of whether anyone had time to talk you out of it first.

She dressed in the dark so she wouldn't wake Nell. This was not easy. Her good coat—the gray one with the fox collar she'd bought the year before the world ended the first time—had been hanging behind the winter blankets for two decades, and it smelled of camphor and secrets. She put it on and looked at herself in the cracked mirror above the washstand. A thin old woman with white hair pinned like a helmet and eyes that had learned to look bored in three languages.

"Well," she told her reflection, "you always said you'd die of something interesting."

Her reflection declined to argue.

* * *

The letter had come at noon, delivered by a boy who smelled of fish and didn't charge for the delivery, which in this city was either charity or a warning. The envelope was heavy paper, cream-colored, and the handwriting inside was careful, upright, and familiar in the way an old scar is familiar.

*Marta,* it began. *You will remember me as Fenik. I remember you as the woman who could forge a customs stamp better than the men whose job it was to make them. I am told you have need of me. I am also told you have never needed anything from anyone, which is why I am curious enough to answer.*

*If you wish to meet: the tram shelter on Kessel Street, tomorrow, six o'clock in the morning. Come alone. Come unarmed. Come sober, though I know you'll want to be otherwise.*

*—F.*

Marta had read it three times, then burned it over the kitchen sink and stood there watching the ash float down into the drain like small gray moths giving up.

Fenik. Anton Fenik, who had run documents across three borders when Marta was still young enough to lie about her age and mean it. Who had once hidden a radio transmitter in a coffin and attended his own funeral to retrieve it. Who had, by every account she trusted—and there were very few accounts left that deserved trust—survived the war, the purges after the war, and at least two marriages.

She had written back one line: *Kessel Street. I'll bring the stamps if you bring the nerve.* Then she'd burned that too, because some habits are load-bearing.

* * *

The tram shelter at six in the morning was empty except for a pigeon with opinions and a man reading a newspaper he was holding upside down.

"Your paper's inverted," Marta said, sitting down on the bench beside him.

"I know," Fenik said. He was older than she remembered, which was unfair of him, and thinner, which was expected. His coat was shabby but his boots were excellent, and Marta had always judged men by their boots first. "I'm checking whether anyone's watching me closely enough to notice. You'd be amazed how few people read anymore."

"And did anyone notice?"

"One policeman glanced over about four minutes ago. He saw two pensioners complaining about the weather." Fenik folded the paper. "Which is what we were doing, so everyone's story agrees."

"It's raining sideways."

"Then we're doing excellent work."

He turned to face her, and up close she could see the years had done their accounting honestly: the same sharp jaw, now softened; the same eyes, pale as dishwater and twice as unsentimental.

"Marta Yarrow," he said. "Twenty years, and you write to me out of nowhere. Either you're dying, or someone you love is."

"My sister-in-law is about to hand herself to Colonel Brecht on a pier," Marta said. "My grandniece is going to try to stop her by standing in front of soldiers. And there's a man in my house—a translator, a stranger, a decent fool—who gave away his own papers last night so a dead man could live again." She smoothed her skirt. "So yes. Someone I love is dying. Several someones, on a schedule."

Fenik was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked against the shelter's tin roof like a patient typist.

"You always did skip the pleasantries."

"Pleasantries are what people use when they haven't decided yet. I decided in 1919."

"What do you need?"

"Brecht," Marta said. "Everything on him. The wartime dealings, the route he sold in '38, the names he traded, anything with ink on it. Enough that when he stands on Pier Nine tomorrow looking respectable, someone official can look at him and see a traitor instead."

Fenik whistled low. "That's not a favor, Marta. That's a funeral request. Yours or his."

"That depends entirely on how fast you work."

* * *

They went to a room above a shuttered print shop, because apparently men like Fenik never stopped renting rooms above shuttered print shops—it must be something they taught in whatever school produced them. The room held a table, two chairs, a lamp with a frayed cord, and a filing system that consisted of a biscuit tin, a cigar box, and a hollowed-out Bible.

"The Bible was my wife's idea," Fenik said, catching Marta's look. "She said no one would ever search it. She was right. She was also, eventually, right about everything else, which is why she left me."

He opened the Bible. Inside, where Leviticus should have been, were folders.

"Brecht," Marta repeated.

"Brecht." Fenik pulled a folder thick as a sandwich and dropped it on the table with a slap. "Where would you like to begin? There's the escape network he sold in the autumn of '38—I've got the payment records, routed through a shipping company that technically never existed. There's the prison manifests, which list certain prisoners as transferred when they were in fact ransomed, quietly, to men who wanted their silence more than their deaths." He tapped the folder. "There's a ledger of his own, if you can believe it. The great irony of our age: the man who hunted everyone else's codes kept his own accounts in plain figures, because he thought no one would ever dare audit him."

Marta opened the folder. The pages were carbon copies, photographs, receipts—twenty years of a man's sins, filed neatly by a stranger who had never met her family but had, apparently, never stopped keeping score.

"Why?" she asked. "Why keep all this?"

Fenik lit a cigarette with hands that trembled only slightly, which for him was restraint. "Because in '38, when the route fell, forty-one people went into the ground or into cells. I knew eleven of them personally. I carried letters for three of them that were never answered." He exhaled smoke through his nose. "Everyone told me to let it go. Time passes, borders move, the dead stay dead, the living get tired. But I'm a stubborn man, Marta. It's my only virtue and I've cultivated it like an orchid."

"And now?"

"Now a woman who forged customs stamps better than God writes to me after twenty years and asks for ammunition." He smiled, and it was the smile of a much younger man, one who had once hidden radios in coffins for the sheer pleasure of it. "I've been waiting two decades for someone to ask. Do you know what it's like to hold a loaded file for twenty years and have nobody want it?"

"I know what it's like to hold silence that long," Marta said. "It gets heavy. Like carrying water in your hands all day and pretending you're not thirsty."

Fenik studied her. "You've been carrying something too."

"Since 1938." She closed the folder and held it against her chest like a sleeping child. "Tomorrow I intend to put it down."

* * *

By evening she was home, and the house was in its final-night hush—the particular quiet of people packing years into suitcases, deciding what a life weighs. Ada sat at the kitchen table with the typewriter, feeding it fresh pages, her fingers moving with the steadiness of a woman who had made her peace with something terrible. Vann stood at the window pretending to watch the street. Nell was asleep, or pretending to be, which amounted to the same thing in a house like theirs.

Marta set the folder on the table between Ada and Vann.

Ada looked up. "What is that?"

"Colonel Brecht's entire career," Marta said, "with footnotes."

Vann crossed the room and opened it, and she watched his face go through the stages: disbelief, recognition, and finally a kind of grim delight that she found entirely appropriate.

"This is—" he began.

"Evidence. Payment routes. Prison manifests. A shipping company that existed only on paper and in bribes." Marta poured herself the last of the tea, cold, and drank it anyway. "Enough to hang him, if hanging were legal here, which it hasn't been since before the war, but enough certainly to make every newspaper in three countries forget about ship schedules and remember about traitors."

Ada's hands had gone still on the typewriter keys. "Where did you get this?"

"A friend," Marta said. "From the old days. The days before I became your boring aunt with strong opinions about tea."

"You were never boring," Nell said from the doorway, in her nightgown, hair loose, having heard every word—which of course she had; the girl could hear a secret through brick the way other children heard dinner bells. "Aunt Marta, what did you do today?"

Marta looked at her—this sharp-eyed girl who had arrived as a bundle and a name and become the only future Marta believed in—and felt the old instinct rise, the one that said *protect them from the truth, wrap it in wool, tell them it's nothing.*

She had spent twenty years obeying that instinct. It had kept everyone safe and nobody happy, and tomorrow it would get them all killed anyway.

"Nell," Marta said, "sit down. All of you. Because tomorrow I'm going to trade this folder for your lives, and the price is likely to be me, and you're going to want to argue, and I'd rather get the arguing done tonight."

Silence, except for the rain starting up against the window.

"Marta," Ada said slowly, "what did you do?"

"I made a telephone call yesterday," Marta said. "To a man I haven't spoken to since before you were widowed, Nell, and before you were born, and before your brother died—or didn't die, we'll get to that. And this morning he gave me the only weapon in this city that frightens Colonel Brecht." She set down her cup. "And tonight I sent word that I'll deliver it to the harbor authority at Pier Nine myself, in person, during the exchange, because Fenik's contacts won't act on a folder handed over by a translator with forged papers. They need a name they recognize. They need a witness they trust."

"They need you," Vann said quietly.

"They need a collaborator's widow with nothing left to lose," Marta corrected. "Which is a category I've occupied for twenty years without once finding a use for it. I'm rather pleased to finally have a purpose for the position."

"You are not trading yourself," Ada said. The typewriter keys rang under her fist as her hand came down. "Not after everything—not after Tomas—"

"Tomas is exactly why," Marta said. "Listen to me, girl. In 1938 I watched your father sign papers that destroyed us, and I said nothing, because I thought silence would keep you children whole. It didn't. It just made the breaking quieter." She reached across the table and took Ada's hand, and her grip was still strong; whatever else the years had taken, they had left her the hands. "I am done being quiet. Tomorrow, when Brecht reneges—and he will renege, Ada, men like him always do, it's practically a medical condition—I will walk up to that pier with this folder and Fenik's people at my back, and I will spend the last coin I have."

"And what does Brecht do to you?" Nell asked. Her voice was small and steady, which was worse than crying.

"He arrests me," Marta said. "Probably. Detains me, questions me, loses my file for a decade—that's the optimistic scenario. The pessimistic scenario involves a train and a closed door, and I've lived long enough that I refuse to schedule around it." She looked around the table, at each of them in turn. "Here's what I need from you. When I step forward tomorrow, nobody follows me. Nobody argues with soldiers. Nobody plays the hero, especially you, Elias Vann, you have hero written on you like a stain. Ada gets Tomas on that ship. Nell gets on that ship. Vann gets wherever the ledger goes. That is the entire plan. Everything else is noise."

"And you?" Ada whispered.

"I get to be honest," Marta said, "for approximately four minutes. Which, when you add it all up, is more than I managed in the previous twenty years." She allowed herself a smile, dry as August. "Besides, I'm seventy-one. If Brecht wants to arrest me, he'd better hurry. My doctor gives me five years, and I intend to waste at least three of them embarrassing nurses."

Nobody laughed. She hadn't really expected them to. But Nell came around the table and put her arms around Marta's neck, fierce and unembarrassed, the way only the young can grieve in advance, and Ada pressed her forehead to Marta's shoulder, and even Vann—awkward, foreign, decent—rested his hand briefly on her arm like a man touching something he wasn't sure was permitted.

"There," Marta said briskly, before any of it could become a scene. "Now that's settled. Nell, back to bed. Ada, finish your typing. Vann—stop looking at me like that, you'll wrinkle your face and it's your best feature."

But later, alone in the kitchen with the lamp turned low, Marta took the folder from under her coat and laid it on the table, and beneath it she placed a second thing: a single sheet of paper, yellowed at the edges, which she had carried in the lining of the fox-collar coat for twenty years.

It was a customs stamp impression, dated October 1938, authorizing passage for three—her brother's family, the night of the evacuation. The stamp that had been used to wave them through. The stamp whose route Brecht had sold.

She had kept it the way one keeps a bullet removed from a wound: proof that the damage was real, and survivable, and over.

Almost over.

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a whistle sounded—long, low, unmistakable. Not the ten o'clock freight. Something earlier. Something moving ahead of schedule.

Marta stood at the window and watched lights come on along the pier road, one after another, like a fuse burning toward the water.

"Well," she murmured. "Either that's my imagination, or Colonel Brecht has decided not to wait for morning."

She reached for her coat.
