## 36. Which Family Member Sails

The berth ticket was the color of weak tea and weighed, in Ada's hand, more than the ledger ever had.

She stood at the window of the harbor office waiting room and turned it over twice, as though a second reading might reveal a second berth printed on the reverse. It did not. One ticket. One name to be written on the line in ink. The clerk behind the grille had already begun stacking his papers for the evening, and beyond the glass the pier lights were coming on one by one, each a small yellow accusation.

Behind her, the soldiers Brecht had stationed at the door were playing cards. They were not pretending to do anything else. That was the thing about men who had already won, Ada thought; they stopped wasting effort on the appearance of doubt.

She put the ticket in her coat pocket, next to the folded pages she had retyped until three in the morning, and walked out past the soldiers as if she were a woman who had merely come to ask about sailing times. One of them looked up. She gave him the flat, bored expression of a bookkeeper confronted with arithmetic errors, and he went back to his cards.

* * *

Marta was waiting in the kitchen with tea she had made and not drunk, which was how Marta prayed.

"Well?" her aunt said.

"One berth."

Marta closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, they were dry and very clear, the way they had been on the night of the evacuation twenty years ago, when she had stood on a quay and counted children onto a boat. "Then we do the arithmetic," she said. "We are good at arithmetic in this family. It is practically our religion."

"It's not arithmetic," Ada said. "It's division. There's a difference."

"There is," Marta agreed. "Arithmetic tells you how many. Division tells you who gets hurt." She poured a second cup anyway, out of habit, and pushed it toward Ada. "Sit down and say the names."

Ada sat. "Tomas. If he's alive, and the telegram says he is, and if Brecht actually produces him at the pier instead of producing a photograph of him, Tomas sails. That's not a choice. That's the whole point of everything I've done since 1938."

"Agreed."

"Vann. He has no country, no papers that would survive ten minutes of inspection, and a warrant with his name on it that Brecht wrote himself. If he stays, he dies badly and uselessly."

"Agreed."

"Nell—" Ada stopped.

"Nell is fourteen," Marta said, "and has the papers of a verified former citizen, because I spent four months and most of my savings arranging them, and she qualifies for the ship in her own right. She does not need the berth. She needs a berth. There are other ships. Slower ones, worse ones, but other ships, once this one is gone and Brecht's attention goes with it."

"You've been planning this."

"I've been planning it since the day she learned to walk toward danger instead of away from it," Marta said. "Which was at approximately fourteen months."

"And you?"

"I," said Marta, with the mild satisfaction of a woman laying down a winning card, "am not going anywhere. My papers name me as a collaborator's widow. I told you that. I have made my peace with it, and my peace is quite comfortable; it has a good chair and a view of the garden." She turned her cup a quarter-turn, a gesture Ada had watched all her life and only now understood as a way of not looking at someone while saying the hardest thing. "Which leaves you, Ada. And Vann. And one ticket."

* * *

Vann came in at half past nine, smelling of rain and of the particular fear that Ada had learned to read on him the way she read a column of figures that didn't sum. He had a bruise coming up along his jaw where Brecht's men had reminded him, that afternoon, that debts do not expire.

"They've moved the exchange to seven o'clock tomorrow," he said. "Brecht's clerk told me himself, and then apologized, which was the worst part. He apologized like a man apologizing for the weather."

"Seven o'clock," Marta repeated. "They want the dawn crowd. Witnesses who look like fog."

"They want it done before the newspapers open their morning post," Ada said, and saw Vann look at her sharply. She had not told him about the three envelopes she had mailed, the true ledger retyped and addressed to three editors who owed their careers to other people's courage. Some things you kept even from the people you were about to lie to with your whole face. "Vann. Sit down. We're dividing a berth."

He sat. He was good at sitting when the news was bad; she had noticed that about him early, that he had the refugee's talent for making stillness look like patience rather than terror.

"Tomas sails," she said. "You sail."

"I don't—"

"You sail, and you sail on my papers." She took her document wallet from her handbag and laid it on the table between the teacups. Her name, her photograph, her verified status, twenty years of careful, suffocating, blameless bookkeeping, all of it folded into a folder that could carry a man across a pier and out of a colonel's reach. "The photograph is ten years old. You'll say the camera was unkind. The clerk at the gangway is half-blind; Marta checked."

Vann looked at the wallet the way men look at things they want and know they shouldn't. "Ada. If they look closely—"

"They won't look closely. And if they do, you'll be past the gangway, and Brecht will have a ledger in his hand that he believes is real, and by the time he discovers it isn't, three newspaper offices will have discovered it is." She kept her voice level. It was the voice she used for closing accounts.

"And you?" Vann said. "What happens to the woman whose papers are in my pocket?"

"I stay," Ada said. "With Nell. In the crowd."

"That isn't a plan, that's a wish with a coat on."

"It's a plan," Marta said, "because I'm in it. There is a fish cellar on the north side of the fish market with a door that opens onto the coal yard. I have had the key since 1941 and no one has ever asked me why. Ada and Nell will be inside it before the exchange begins, and they will come out when the ship has gone and the shouting has stopped, and by then—" she glanced at Ada, and Ada gave her the smallest nod, permission granted at last— "by then the newspapers will have done their work, and Brecht will have larger problems than two women who were never at the pier at all."

Vann looked from one of them to the other. "You've had this planned since the telegram."

"Since the second letter," Ada said. "I've been planning exits for twenty years, Elias. It's the one subject on which I've never been silent."

* * *

She found Nell on the back step, sitting with her knees up and the cat—there was always a cat; the household attracted them the way the harbor attracted fog—doing his evening inspection of her shoelaces.

"I know," Nell said, without turning around. "Before you say it. I know about the cellar, and I know about the papers, and I know you're giving Vann your documents, which means you're giving him your name, which I think is either the bravest or the stupidest thing anyone in this house has ever done, and this house has set a very high bar."

Ada sat down beside her on the step. The cat, offended by the crowding, relocated to Nell's lap with dignity.

"You're not angry," Ada said.

"I was. For about an hour. Then I did the sums." Nell scratched the cat's ear. "If I make a scene at the pier, soldiers notice me. If soldiers notice me, they notice you. If they notice you, the whole thing comes apart and Tomas hangs from whatever Brecht hangs people from these days. So I go in the cellar, and I hate it, and I go in the cellar." She said it fast, the way you pull a splinter. "But I want it recorded, for the historical record, that I am going in that cellar under protest, and that when I'm old I intend to bring it up constantly."

"Recorded," Ada said. "Witnessed. I'll even notarize it."

Nell laughed, and then, without any transition at all, she was crying, quietly, the way she did everything—thoroughly and without wanting an audience. Ada put her arm around her and let her, because there were things a household was for, and this was the chief one.

"He's really alive," Nell said into Ada's shoulder. "Your brother. He's really alive, and I'm going to see him walk up a gangway, and then he's going to be gone, and you're going to be here, and it isn't fair, any of it."

"No," Ada agreed. "It isn't. It hasn't been for twenty years. But fairness is a luxury good, Nell, and we've always shopped in the discount section." She tilted the girl's chin up. "Listen to me. Tomorrow, when it's done—when the ship is out and the shouting is over—you and I are going to walk out of that cellar and we are going to be the two most unremarkable women in this city. And in a week, maybe two, an envelope is going to arrive, or a telegram, or a knock at the door, and it will be from Vann, or it will be from Tomas, or it will be from a newspaperman who has read his morning post. And when that happens, this ends. All of it. The hiding, the names, the silences. We come out from under."

"You promise?"

"I've spent twenty years not promising things," Ada said. "I've got excellent credit. I'm spending it on this."

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat at the typewriter in the front room until two in the morning, not typing—everything that needed typing was already typed, copied, mailed, hidden—but running her fingers over the keys the way you'd touch the face of someone sleeping, memorizing.

The typewriter had come with her out of the republic in 1938, wrapped in a shawl, the only object she had saved besides the ledger and her own life. It had typed invoices since. Rent receipts. Nell's school essays, corrected. Twenty years of small, blameless paper. Tomorrow it would have typed the thing that mattered, and she would leave it behind, and someone would find it, and they would never know what it had done.

She thought about her father, then. She had been doing that a great deal since the third layer of the cipher had opened under her hands—his name in Tomas's careful confession, his collaboration, his silence bought and paid for. She had hated him for a week, grieved him for another, and arrived somewhere in the middle that she suspected was simply the truth: that he had been a frightened man who made a frightened choice, and that she was his daughter, and that the difference between them was not courage but consequence. He had sold a route to save his family. She had spent twenty years paying the bill.

Tomorrow the account would close. Not balanced—accounts like this never balanced—but closed, the ledger carried forward, the loss acknowledged, the business, such as it was, handed to the next generation. Nell would carry it. Nell, who asked questions. Nell, who went into cellars under protest and intended to bring it up constantly. That was the inheritance, Ada thought. Not the code, not the name. The asking.

She typed one line, just to hear it: *Tomas Yarrow, born 1911, alive.* Then she pulled the sheet out, folded it small, and put it in her pocket with the tea-colored ticket she would not be using.

* * *

The fish cellar smelled of brine and old coal and, faintly, of Marta's stubbornness, because Marta had been down the night before with a lamp and a broom and had evidently declared war on the cobwebs. There was a crate to sit on, a blanket that smelled of camphor, and a crack in the door through which, if you knelt in exactly the right position, you could see a grey slice of the pier.

At ten to seven, Ada knelt in that position, and Nell knelt behind her, and Marta stood at the door with the key in her fist like a woman holding a grenade.

The crowd was thin and grey, exactly as Brecht had ordered it. Dockhands who had no choice about their mornings. Two gulls conducting their usual negotiations. And at the foot of the gangway, flanked by four soldiers, a man in a good coat holding a leather folder—Brecht himself, come to collect in person, because a man who trades in secrets likes to watch them change hands.

Then the second group came down the pier, and Ada's whole body forgot its training.

Tomas. Older, thinner, grey at the temples, walking with the careful gait of a man whose body had learned not to trust its own ground. But it was his walk. It was the walk that had come down their stairs a thousand times, late for everything, whistling. Twenty years she had carried a dead brother, and here he was, alive, breathing, squinting at the morning light like it owed him an apology.

She felt Nell's hand close on the back of her coat—Nell had found the crack in the door too—and neither of them said a word, because the word would have undone them both.

The exchange happened the way exchanges do: too fast, too quiet, too ordinary. Vann walked Tomas forward—Vann, wearing Ada's name, Ada's face in a ten-year-old photograph, Ada's whole careful life folded in his coat pocket. Brecht opened the folder. A soldier checked the pages. Brecht's eyes went over the ledger with the expression of a man counting money he had already spent, and Ada, kneeling in a fish cellar with her heart going like a dock crane, understood that he was not reading it. He was reading the *fact* of it. The weight in the hand. Men like Brecht never read; they appraised.

Tomas reached the gangway. A clerk with bad eyes squinted at Vann's papers—*my* papers—and Ada held her breath with the whole of her twenty years behind it.

The clerk waved them through.

Tomas went up the gangway. Vann went up the gangway, half a step behind, and at the top of it he turned—she saw him turn, the grey slice of pier tilting with her—and looked back at the crowd, at the fish market, at the coal yard, at the exact spot where he could not possibly have known she was kneeling. Looking for her. She pressed her hand to the crack in the door, as if a hand could be seen through a wall, as if that were a thing that could be done, and watched him turn away and go up into the ship with her name in his pocket and her brother at his side.

The gulls went on negotiating. The soldiers relaxed into their cigarettes. Brecht tucked the folder under his arm like a man carrying his lunch.

"Out," Marta whispered at the door. "Now, while the crowd's still thick. Ada. *Out.*"

Ada rose. Her knees had gone to wood. Nell's face was wet and shining and trying very hard to be a soldier's face, and Ada took her hand, and Marta opened the door onto the coal yard and the grey morning and the rest of a life.

They had made it perhaps thirty yards into the crowd—Nell's hand in hers, Marta ahead, the ship's horn sounding the first long note of departure—when Ada heard, behind her, at the pier, a voice she did not know saying, in a tone of dawning and terrible clarity:

"Colonel. The newspaper man at the gate says he's had a letter. He says everybody's had a letter."

Ada did not turn around. She held her daughter's hand, and she walked north through the crowd, unremarkable, unnamed, free—and behind her, faint under the ship's horn, she heard the sound of a colonel's morning beginning to come apart.
