## 39. Two Years Later

The town was called Sankt Ilma, though Elias Vann learned this only after he had already walked through it twice, because the sign at the crossroads had been shot through in the war and nobody had gotten around to replacing the letters that mattered. What remained read *SANKT I MA*, which Tomas insisted looked like a blessing delivered by someone who had lost interest halfway through.

"Saint I-Ma," Tomas said, adjusting the strap of his bag for the fourth time in a hundred meters. "Patron saint of almost."

"Of unfinished things," Vann said.

"Of us, then."

They had come up from the valley on foot because the bus ran twice a week and neither of them trusted it to arrive, and because after two years of trains that were held, searched, delayed, and once — memorably — commandeered by a colonel of absolutely no one's army, both men had developed a superstition about arriving anywhere important under their own power. So they walked the last nine kilometers along a river the color of gunmetal and old coins, past orchards that had been neglected into wildness and were now, ironically, more generous than they had ever been under management. Apples lay in the grass like something spilled.

Vann carried the ledger.

Not the original — the original belonged to history now, or to the archives, or to whichever ministry had claimed it in the great postwar scramble for documents that could hang people. He carried the copy. Ada's copy. The one she had typed page by page on a typewriter with a temperamental *e*, in the last quiet nights before Pier Nine, while the household slept and Marta sat up with her not because Marta expected trouble but because, as Marta put it, "someone should witness you doing something brave, so you can't later pretend you didn't."

He had offered to leave it behind a dozen times. It was dangerous. It named names. Half its names were dead and the other half had lawyers. But Ada had pressed it into his hands at the rail with an expression he had spent two years failing to describe accurately, and had said only: "You're the translator. Translate it for people who don't read silence."

So he translated it. To tribunals, mostly. To tired judges and tireder clerks, in three languages, until Brecht's name stopped being a rumor and became an indictment and finally a sentence handed down in a courtroom where Brecht himself had sat very straight and very pale, listening to his own career read aloud in the flat voice of a man reading a shipping manifest.

Tomas had attended every session. He never spoke about them afterward. He just bought two coffees instead of one and drank both.

Now here they were, in Saint Almost, following a lane that climbed between garden walls toward an address written on a scrap of paper that Vann had checked against his breast pocket perhaps four hundred times since the border.

*Frau Lenz. The house with the blue door. Ask for eggs.*

*Ask for eggs.* Nell's addition, when she had finally written to tell them where they'd gone — a letter that took eleven months to reach them and arrived smelling faintly of apples and containing, in her round determined hand, the entire story compressed to a paragraph: *We are fine. Aunt Ada keeps bees now. Marta would have hated the bees but we keep them anyway. Come whenever you've finished being heroes. Bring nothing. We have everything.*

The blue door, when they found it, was less blue than it wished it were — sun-faded at the top, repainted badly at the bottom, with a brass knocker shaped like an acorn that some previous owner had installed with more optimism than skill. Behind the wall, bees kept their low industrious music. Somewhere a pump handle squeaked in a rhythm that suggested a child playing at work rather than working.

Vann stood at the gate with his hand raised to knock and discovered, to his considerable embarrassment, that he could not make himself do it.

"You're hesitating," Tomas observed.

"I'm composing."

"You've been composing for forty seconds. In my experience your compositions peak at ten."

"It's been," Vann said, "two years. There is a version of me that rehearsed this arrival on every train platform in central Europe, and in all of those versions I say something devastatingly charming, and in none of those versions does my heart attempt to exit through my throat." He straightened his collar, which did not need straightening. "What if the door opens and it isn't—"

"The letter gave the address."

"Letters lie. People move. Wars happen. Whole cities change their names halfway through." He looked at the acorn knocker. "What if we knock and it's just a woman named Frau Lenz with eggs?"

"Then we buy the eggs," Tomas said, "and ask if she knows anyone who used to be someone else, and follow the trail. We are, between us, reasonably good at finding people who don't wish to be found. It's practically the family trade now."

That word — *family* — landed between them the way it always did, softly and heavily at once. They were not brothers by any paper that existed. But somewhere between the prison camp where Vann had first read aloud the smuggled letters of a man officially dead, and the tribunal benches where they had sat shoulder to shoulder for eighteen months, the papers had become irrelevant. You could survive a lot with a person. Surviving was a kind of kinship no registry required.

Vann knocked.

The squeaking pump stopped. The bee-music continued, indifferent. Footsteps crossed gravel — unhurried, deliberate, the footsteps of someone who answered doors on her own schedule — and then the door opened, and it was Nell.

She was sixteen now. She had gone up and out and sideways in two years, all at once, the way children do when no one is watching closely enough to stop it. Her hair was shorter. There was a smear of what appeared to be honey across one forearm and a beekeeping veil pushed up on her head like a crown worn ironically. She looked at Vann. She looked at Tomas. Her face went through approximately six expressions in two seconds, and then she did the thing she had always done, even as a small girl with too-perceptive eyes: she skipped every available sentiment and went directly to the practical.

"You're late," she said.

"We wrote—"

"Eleven months for a letter, Elias. I did the arithmetic. If you'd left when you got mine, you'd have been here by spring. Instead you finished the trial." She folded her arms, honey smear and all. "You finished the whole trial. Every session. Tomas went to *all of them*, Marta's contact told us, even the boring ones, even the ones about railway schedules."

"Somebody had to listen to the railway schedules," Tomas said mildly. "Justice is mostly schedules."

Nell's composure held for another three seconds. Then it didn't. She came through the gate like weather, hugged Tomas hard enough to elicit a grunt, then turned on Vann and hugged him differently — fiercely, briefly, the way one embraces a person one has decided to forgive in advance for everything — and stepped back wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand and saying, with tremendous dignity, "There's honey on you now. Both of you. That's permanent. That's how it works."

"I wouldn't dream of contesting it," Vann said.

And then the door opened wider, and there was Ada.

Two years. He had prepared himself for two years — for greyer hair, a deeper stoop, the particular weathering that exile and tribunals and waiting stamp on people. He had not prepared himself for this: that she would look *lighter*. Not younger, exactly. Unburdened in the way of a woman who has set down a thing she carried for twenty years and found her hands still worked afterward. She wore a man's cardigan over a dress that had clearly lost several arguments with the garden. There was flour on her sleeve. In her hand, absurdly, was a wooden spoon.

She looked at them the way one looks at a document one has read a thousand times and never quite believed.

"Well," she said at last. "You're both thinner than your photographs."

"There weren't photographs," Tomas said.

"No," Ada agreed. "There weren't. That was the problem, wasn't it. Twenty years of no photographs." She came down the path, spoon still in hand, and stopped in front of her brother, and for a long moment neither of them moved, as if each feared the other might be a forgery — and these were two people who knew forgery intimately, who had built lives out of forged papers and retyped pages, who had earned the right to check.

Then Tomas said, "Your handwriting's improved."

"You're alive," Ada said, "which beats neat penmanship," and the two of them collided — the spoon clattering to the gravel, forgotten, the embrace of siblings who had buried each other in absentia and now had to unbury themselves standing up, in a garden, in front of witnesses, with bees looking on. Vann watched Nell watch them, and saw her mouth shape the words *finally* with an exasperation that was entirely Marta's inheritance.

Ada pulled back, held Tomas at arm's length, studied him. "You look like Father."

"Don't," Tomas said.

"You do. Around the eyes."

"Around the eyes I look like a man who spent five years in a cell. Let's leave Father out of the eyes."

"He's in chapter twenty-one," Ada said quietly. "He'll be in the book whether we like it or not. That was rather the point of the book."

*The book.* Vann felt his chest do something complicated. Because that was the other news, the news that had traveled ahead of them through three countries like a rumor with excellent references: Ada Yarrow — writing now as A. Lenz, because old habits of anonymity died hard — had spent two years turning the ledger into prose. Not the raw cipher; the story. The evacuation, the betrayal, the father's collaboration told plainly, the silences and their costs, all of it set down in the unadorned account-keeper's style that turned out, to everyone's surprise including hers, to be exactly the right instrument for the truth. The manuscript had gone to a publisher who read it in one night. The book was coming out in the autumn. The advance notice had called it, in the phrase Vann suspected Ada had cut out of the newspaper and burned, *"the accounting no one dared keep."*

But all of that could wait. The garden could not. Ada retrieved her spoon, declared lunch to be imminent regardless of anyone's travel schedule, and marched them inside past a hallway lined with jars of honey labeled in Nell's handwriting (*SUMMER, GOOD / SUMMER, SMOKY / MISTA'S LAST BATCH — DO NOT JUDGE HER*) and into a kitchen that smelled of bread and thyme and the particular peace of a room where no one has lied for a long time.

* * *

Lunch was bread, soup, cheese from a neighbor whose name Ada pronounced with the fond exasperation reserved for neighbors everywhere, and a running negotiation over whether Vann was permitted to help and had thereby ruined two plates' worth of table arrangement.

"You fold napkins like a man escaping somewhere," Ada observed.

"I have folded many napkins under duress."

"In our household," Nell said, "we fold them like we mean to stay."

It was said lightly. It was not meant lightly. Vann set down the napkin he had mangled and refolded it, slowly, corners matched, like a man meaning to stay, and Nell nodded once, satisfied, the way a customs officer nods.

Over the meal the stories came out in the way stories do among people who trust each other — not performed, but surfaced, one at a time, each one making room for the next. Tomas described the camp obliquely, in the manner of a man who had learned to carry it rather than set it down: the library cart he'd been assigned to push, the books he'd read four times, the guard who had looked away at the right moment and whom Tomas refused to call a good man but admitted, grudgingly, had been a convenient one. Ada described the bees — "Marta would have called them sentimental, and then she'd have stood out there for an hour watching them, and pretended she was counting" — and Nell described school ("I'm the best in my class at mathematics, which surprises no one in this family, arithmetic being our love language") and Vann described the tribunals only as much as was polite at a table, saving the rest, sensing correctly, for the evening.

It was during the cheese that the question came, from Nell, in her usual fashion — direct, aimed, unavoidable:

"When does Marta get her chapter?"

The kitchen went quiet the way a room goes quiet when everyone in it has been thinking the same thing and one person finally says it.

Ada reached across the table and took Nell's hand. "Chapter thirty-five," she said gently. "I told you. She has the best scene in the book. She walks onto that pier with Herr Brandt and the evidence, and she trades herself for three berths, and she doesn't even let the soldiers help her with her bag. I checked with Brandt. Twice. He confirmed the bag detail independently." Her voice caught on something and she let it. "She gets the whole pier. She gets the sentence everyone remembers."

"She should get more than a chapter," Nell said. "She should get the bees named after her."

"They are named after her," Ada said. "All of them. Every hive. It's not efficient, calling four thousand bees Marta, but it's accurate. She never could abide being just one person."

Vann looked down at his plate. He had met Marta Yarrow exactly once before the pier — the evening she had warned him, with terrifying precision, that men like him arrived with debts attached — and he had thought, at the time, that he had her measure. He had been wrong by a country mile. He had spent two years telling her story in courtrooms, and he still wasn't sure he had her measure. Some people you could only measure by the size of the hole they left.

"She'd have hated the funeral," Tomas said.

"She'd have hated everything about it except the turnout," Ada agreed. "Which was large. Mostly people she'd insulted, coming to confirm she meant it." A pause, and then, softer, to the table generally: "I write her honestly. That was the bargain. Remembered honestly. No halo. She'd rise from whatever they bury you in to correct the record."

* * *

Evening came down over Sankt Ilma the way evenings do in places that have decided to be gentle — slow, gold, without urgency. After supper Vann found Ada in the garden, standing at the fence line watching the hives settle, and came to stand beside her in a silence that had long ago stopped needing to be broken.

"They've offered me a position," he said eventually. "The translation bureau. Permanent. Here, or in the capital."

"And?" Ada asked, because Ada did not waste questions.

"And I told them I'd answer in the autumn."

"After the book comes out."

"After the book comes out." He turned the copy of the ledger over in his hands — he still carried it; he suspected he would carry it forever, the way other men carried watches. "I wanted to return this to you. Formally. On the property. It seemed—" he searched, "—it seemed like paperwork that wants a signature."

Ada took it. Held it. For a moment he thought she might burn it, right there, ceremonially, and he realized he would have helped her strike the match. But she only weighed it, the way she weighed everything, and then tucked it under her arm like groceries.

"It goes in the trunk with the others," she said. "Under the floorboard. Where the typewriter lives." A glance at him, dry as good paper. "Yes, Vann. I know you know about the floorboard. Nell told you everything within four minutes of your arrival. She's been sitting on that information since spring, apparently, saving it."

"She said you'd want to be the one to show me."

"Mm." Ada looked out at the darkening rows of hives. "Twenty years ago I burned half a code and memorized the other half, and I thought that was the safest way to live. Carry the dangerous part where no one can find it." She tapped her own temple, once. "Took me a lifetime and a war to learn that the dangerous part rots in there unless you take it out and type it up."

"Is that the moral of the book?"

"That's the *first* draft's moral. The final draft hasn't decided yet. Final drafts are like that. They hold out on you until the last possible minute." She started toward the house, then paused, and said — casually, far too casually, in the tone of a woman who had been composing this for eleven months and hoped it sounded improvised:

"There's a desk in the back room. Second one. It was Marta's. The typewriter sits on it. And the publisher's letter said — well, she said the second book would be better with a second voice. Someone who was there for the parts I couldn't see. Someone fluent in silence *and* paperwork." Ada examined the horizon with great attention. "She used the word 'collaborator.' In the good sense. If such a sense exists anymore."

Vann stood very still in the cooling garden, holding absolutely nothing, feeling the weight of everything.

"Frau Lenz," he said carefully, "are you asking me to stay and help write the sequel?"

"I'm asking you to fold napkins like you mean it and see where that leads," Ada said, already walking, spoon somehow back in her hand. "Dinner's at seven. We type after. And Elias—" she stopped at the door, silhouetted against the warm kitchen light, and her voice gentled all the way through, "—the spare room's made up. It's been made up since spring. Nell changes the sheets weekly. Neither of us ever discussed it out loud. That's how we do things now — we prepare the truth and wait for people to arrive and claim it."

Behind him, in the hives, four thousand Martas hummed their low evening song. Inside, Nell was arguing with Tomas about railway schedules, cheerfully, endlessly, like justice itself. And on the desk in the back room, under the window where the light came in honest and early, the typewriter waited with a fresh page in the platen — blank, patient, and ready for the whole story, unburned and unedited, to begin again from the top.
