## 1. The Weight of Small Parts

The summons came on a Tuesday, which I remember because Tuesdays were the days Marta Kessler boiled the wash-water and the whole of Voss & Sons smelled of lye and wet wool. There was no Sons anymore — there had not been for eleven years — but the sign was painted by my father and I have never had the heart, or the paint, to change it.

The letter was delivered by a boy with a bicycle too large for him, who leaned it against my doorframe at an angle that made me flinch. The paper inside bore the seal of the Observatory on the Hill, which surprised me twice: once because I did not know the Observatory employed anyone who could still write a legible sentence, and again because the handwriting, cramped and precise, belonged to Halvard Brann.

*Master clock is failing,* it said. *Come Tuesday. Bring your own oil. Bring nothing you cannot carry up four hundred steps.*

I read it three times. It was very like Halvard Brann to make his summons sound like an insult. He had been my father's rival, then my father's colleague, then, briefly, my father's only mourner at a funeral attended by six people and one dog that would not stop barking. He had never once visited the shop since.

Still. A failing master clock in the last winter of a failing war. Somewhere above the town, the thing that told the harbor, the trains, the church bells, and half the wristwatches of the garrison what hour it was had begun to lie. That was not a repair job. That was a small civic emergency wearing the clothes of one.

I did what I always do when the world asks something of me. I made a list.

---

My father taught me arithmetic before he taught me letters, and before either he taught me this: *a man who carries everything carries nothing well.* He said it while packing a tool chest, and he said it as though it were scripture, and in our household it functioned as such. Voss men traveled light or not at all. When the army took my brother Emil in '14, he carried a rifle, a photograph, and forty rounds, and none of it came home. When the fever took my mother the following spring, she carried nothing out that she hadn't carried in. And when my father sat down at his bench one October evening and did not get up — quietly, between one gear and the next, the way clockmakers die if they're permitted any dignity at all — he left behind a shop full of things, every one of them heavier than the man who'd owned them.

So: the list.

One leather roll, containing:

— Three screwdrivers, brass-handled. Weights, respectively, 40 grams, 38, and 41; the third has a hairline crack in the handle I have been meaning to fix since before the war and now regard as an old acquaintance. Necessary. A screwdriver is a promise you make to a machine: *I will turn you gently.*

— One pair of pliers, flat-nosed, 95 grams. Possibly necessary. Possibly vanity. I brought them anyway because there are moments in every mechanism when diplomacy fails.

— Tweezers, two pairs, fine and finer. Together, less than the weight of the letter that summoned me. Absolutely necessary. Half of clockwork is holding on; the other half is letting go precisely when instructed, and tweezers are how a human hand learns to do both without trembling.

— One loupe. Nine grams, counting the case. Without it I am a man squinting at the world. With it I am a man squinting at the world *correctly.*

— Oil in a glass vial, 30 grams full, perhaps 24 as packed. Halvard had said *bring your own oil*, which meant his had thickened, or gone sour, or he wanted to see whether I'd obey. With Halvard those are usually the same thing.

— Files, three. Burnisher, one. Pegwood, a bundle. Bluing solution, a bottle the size of a thimble. Mainspring let-down keys, a matched pair, 60 grams the pair, and worth their weight in the continued existence of my fingers.

Total: just under two kilograms, plus the roll itself. Plus, because I am not a monk, one loaf of black bread, one hard sausage, and the last jar of my father's plum preserves, which I had been saving for an occasion and which the war had so far declined to provide. Plus a coat with holes in the elbows that I had darned myself, badly, in a pattern Marta called "geometric grief."

Everything else stayed behind the counter, under lock, catalogued. That is the other half of my inheritance: not only *carry little*, but *know where everything is.* The shop's ledger records each tool, each part, each customer debt down to the pfennig. Eleven years of entries in my own hand. If a bomb falls on Voss & Sons tonight, some future clerk will be able to reconstruct my entire life from the inventory and conclude, correctly, that I owned almost nothing and accounted for all of it.

Marta says this is no way to live. Marta says many things. Marta also weighs her soup bones before she buys them, so we have an understanding.

---

The walk up to the Observatory took an hour and cost me most of my opinions about hills.

The town fell away below in its winter grays — rooftops smoking, the harbor a dull pewter plate, the cranes standing over the quay like patient herons waiting for ships that no longer came. Beyond them, the sea. Beyond the sea, whatever the war was doing this week. From up here it looked almost orderly, the smoke rising straight from the chimneys, the town keeping its shape the way a mechanism keeps time: thousands of small parts, each doing its small duty, none of them aware of the others.

Four hundred steps, Halvard had promised. There were four hundred and twelve. I counted. Of course I counted. My father used to say I'd count the teeth in my own grave if someone handed me a shovel.

The tower itself was older than the war, older than the last three wars, a stone finger pointed at the sky with the serene confidence of a thing that intends to go on pointing long after everyone currently annoyed by it is dead. At the top, under the copper dome, lived the great telescope, and below the telescope, in a room of oak and iron, hung the master clock.

Halvard met me at the door. He had grown no taller and considerably more horizontal, a man built like a barrel that had been stored badly, with white eyebrows like frost on a window and hands that were the finest I have ever seen on a living person. He looked at my tool roll. He looked at my coat. He looked at the jar of plum preserves sticking out of my pocket like an accusation.

"You brought food," he said.

"I brought provisions."

"You brought sentiment in a jar." He turned and led me inside. "Clocks don't eat."

"People do."

"Then you've come to the wrong establishment." But he took the jar from me as he said it, and I saw him weigh it in his hand — Halvard weighs things too; it's the one habit we share, though he'd deny it under torture — and set it on a shelf with the care of a man laying down something breakable.

The master clock filled the back wall of the room like a cathedral door lying down. Two meters of case, walnut and iron, a face the size of a wagon wheel, and behind the dial the movement itself: going train, striking train, escapement, pendulum — nine hundred parts give or take, each one machined a century ago by hands that expected to be forgotten and got their wish. It drove the dial outside, the bells, and by wire and lever half the public clocks in the town below.

"It stopped?" I asked.

"Worse." Halvard folded his arms. "It *lies*. Loses ninety seconds a day, then catches up, then loses again. Irregular. Like a soldier reporting drunk." He spat the word *soldier* the way other men spit tobacco. "A clock may run fast or slow, apprentice. It may not run *whimsically.*"

"Something's dragging," I said. "Escapement, likely. Or the suspension."

"Obviously. I could have diagnosed that by post." His eyes, small and bright as jeweled bearings, went over my tool roll again. "You travel light. Your father did too. Used to say a man who carries everything—"

"—carries nothing well. Yes."

"Hm." He unfolded his arms and unfolded them again, a man whose body had forgotten what idle hands were for. "Well. It won't be a day's work. The case alone will fight you. You'll want to stay the nights. There's a cot. Marta Kessler sends up bread sometimes, when the ration office isn't looking. The astronomer's widow keeps the east room and will pretend you don't exist, which you'll find restful."

I noted the inventory of the household — cook, widow, old man, cot — the way I note everything: automatically, in columns, with weights. Four people in a tower above a town at war. It sounded less like a home than a storage problem.

"I'll want lamplight after dark," I said. "And quiet."

"You'll want a miracle. The garrison requisitioned our lamp oil in September." He said it without heat, the way he said everything, and turned to the clock. "Start with the escape wheel. And apprentice—" he paused at the door, hand on the frame, not looking at me, "—your father would have started with the strike train. He always did it wrong first. Sentimental about bells."

He left me alone with the machine.

---

I worked until my fingers ached and then past that, because aching fingers still know their business. The room was cold enough to numb judgment, which I find helps. I opened the case panel by panel, cataloguing as I went, writing weights and conditions into my notebook in the shorthand only I can read: *escape wheel, good; pallets, worn left, worn left again; suspension spring, cracked at the shoulder — there you are, liar.*

A cracked suspension spring explains whimsy. The pendulum swings free and true until the crack closes, and then it doesn't, and the whole vast honest machine above the town begins telling time the way governments do: mostly correctly, and always with a private agenda.

I could have fixed it in an afternoon with a new spring. I did not have a new spring. I had files, burnisher, pegwood, and the particular stubbornness of a man who has spent a war learning to make one thing out of another thing, which is the national craft these days.

So I knelt under the pendulum with a candle flame and a file, drawing the crack's temper out slow, annealing the metal back toward softness so it could be re-hardened true. Heat, hold, cool. Heat, hold, cool. It is work that cannot be hurried, which is why I love it. For those hours there was no war, no ledger of debts in the shop below, no empty rooms in a house built for four names that held one. There was a spring, and a flame, and my hands, and the narrow, total country of a single task.

That is the secret I don't put in ledgers: the accounting isn't greed. It's armor. If everything is weighed, nothing can ambush you. If everything is catalogued, nothing can be lost that you haven't already mourned in advance. My brother died carrying too much. I intend to die carrying exactly what I brought, itemized, and let the record show I was neither robbed nor sentimental.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above me, through the dome, the sky was doing whatever the sky does at night in wartime — stars, clouds, searchlights pretending to be both. Below, the town slept badly, as towns do, and kept its appointments anyway.

I set the repaired spring, hung the pendulum, and watched it swing. True. Steady. Ninety seconds a day recovered from the forces of whimsy.

It was nearly dawn when I climbed the last stair to splash water on my face, and I stepped out onto the gallery landing to see the sun coming up gray-gold over the harbor — and stopped.

Because on the stone steps below me, in the cold, in the thin light, there was a bundle that was not a bundle.

It was small. It was breathing. And it was clutching a piece of paper to its chest with both fists, the way a child holds the one thing in the world it has been told matters.
