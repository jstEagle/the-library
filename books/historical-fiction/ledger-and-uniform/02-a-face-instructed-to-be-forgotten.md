## 2. A Face Instructed to Be Forgotten

I had catalogued the dawn before I saw the child.

That is not a boast; it is a confession about the way my mind works. November dawns in this town arrive like debt collectors — grey, insistent, itemized. I had been awake since four, packing and repacking my tool case, because the master clock at the observatory had begun losing eleven seconds a day and eleven seconds is not a loss to me, it is an entry. Somewhere above the town, in a tower built to argue with the sky itself, time was going soft, and I had been sent — apprentice Voss, third year, wages of one mark sixty per week plus coal allowance — to make it hard again.

The walk up the hill took twenty minutes. I know because I counted. Counting is what I do instead of praying.

Here is the inventory of that walk, as recorded by the ledger I keep behind my eyes: frost on the cobbles, thin enough to be dishonest. Smoke from Marta's chimney at the observatory cottage, which meant she had risen, which meant there would be bread, which meant the day was already better than its arithmetic suggested. One cart horse, lame on the near foreleg, pulling turnips toward a market that would overcharge for them because war has made greed respectable again. And overhead, the last stars going out one by one with the modest dignity of candles being pinched rather than blown.

I carried very little. I always carry very little. A clockmaker's kit weighs less than a loaf of bread if you are honest about what you need: three screwdrivers, oil in a vial no larger than a thimble, tweezers, a loupe, pliers fine enough to thread a needle's argument, and the brass weight I use to test pendulum swings. Everything else — everything people say you ought to bring, spare parts, contingency tools, the whole anxious luggage of preparedness — is just fear with a handle attached. My father taught me that before he went to the front and stopped being a man who could be written to. *Carry little, boy,* he said. *A man loaded down with things cannot run, and a man who cannot run cannot stay either.*

I have thought about that sentence for nine years and I am still not sure it means anything. But I keep it the way I keep all entries: faithfully, without understanding.

The observatory steps came into view as the light did, both of them arriving together the way the tower's great dial and the town's bells were supposed to arrive together and lately did not. And there, folded into the second step from the bottom — tucked into the shadow beneath the stone lip as neatly as a mislaid receipt — was a child.

I want to be precise here, because precision is the only honesty I trust. My first thought was not *a child*. My first thought was: *that is an irregularity.* A child on the steps at 6:14 in the morning in the fourth winter of a war is an irregularity the way a wrong tooth count in a gear train is an irregularity — something that will stop the whole mechanism if it is not addressed. I stood at the bottom of the steps with my tool case in my hand and I performed, God help me, a risk assessment. Cold exposure: significant, given the frost. Proximity to the tower: convenient, since I had business inside anyway. Likelihood of the situation resolving itself: low. Children do not resolve themselves. Children are the one category of problem that grows while you stand still.

So I climbed the steps. Eleven of them. I counted those too, though by then my counting had gone from habit to something closer to a handhold.

Up close, the child resolved into specifics, and specifics are where my system has always failed me. A girl or a boy — I genuinely could not tell, the head shaved close either by lice or by economy, the coat three sizes wrong, a man's coat belted twice around a small body so that it hung off the shoulders like a tent after a storm. Maybe seven years old. Maybe eight. The face was smudged with soot in the deliberate way of someone who has tried to become unnoticeable, and under the soot the skin had the bluish paper look of cold working its way toward something worse. The child was asleep, or pretending to be asleep, or had simply decided that consciousness was another thing too heavy to carry, and in one fist — knuckles white, grip absolute, the grip of a drowning person on driftwood — was clutched a piece of paper.

Paper, in this town, in this winter, was worth more than the child's entire outfit. Paper was ration cards and death notices and shipping manifests and the only kind of wealth that could not be eaten but could be traded for food. A child holding paper that tightly was holding something worth killing for, or dying for, or both, and the distinction between those two categories had grown thin everywhere in Europe that winter.

I crouched down. I said, in the voice I use for nervous customers — low, level, promising nothing — "You're on private steps."

No response. The breath came out in small measured clouds. Even unconscious, even freezing, the child breathed like a metronome: in for four, out for four. I noticed that. Of course I noticed that. I notice rhythm the way other men notice faces.

"Private steps," I said again, because apparently when confronted with human suffering my instinct is to cite property law.

And then the eyes opened.

They were dark and enormous and they looked at me with an expression I had never received from another living creature in my twenty-three years: they looked at me *without asking for anything.* Not help. Not mercy. Not even acknowledgment. The child simply confirmed I existed, filed me away somewhere behind those eyes, and closed them again. It was the most unnerving thing I had ever witnessed, and I once watched Herr Brandt's widow return a cuckoo clock by throwing it through his shop window.

"I'm going to pick you up," I announced, because I narrate my actions when I am frightened; it makes them feel authorized. "You'll be warmer inside."

At the word *inside* the child's eyes opened again and stayed open, and I understood I had said the correct word. There is a whole language of correct words in this world and I had learned perhaps six of them, and one of them, evidently, was *inside.*

The child weighed almost nothing. That is the fact I could not put down afterward, the entry that refused to balance: the child weighed almost nothing. Less than my toolbox. Less than the pendulum bob I'd come to replace. I lifted Lior — I did not yet know the name; I learned it an hour later, from a note, of all things, a note that instructed me to forget the face that belonged to it — I lifted this small armful of frost and soot and carried it up the remaining steps, and the whole time some clerk in the back office of my mind was stamping forms: *irregularity, unauthorized, not yours, not yours, NOT YOURS.*

Marta found us in the entrance hall. She took one look — me, the child, the frost melting off the child's coat onto the observatory's black-and-white marble floor — and said, "Put it by the stove," in the tone of a woman who has fed strays, soldiers, and at least one fleeing Belgian family without ever once appearing to deviate from her schedule. Marta does not ask questions the way other people ask questions, as requests. She asks them the way she adds salt: as a formality preceding the inevitable.

"What do we do," I began, "about—"

"Bread first," said Marta. "Questions after. Questions keep."

Ines arrived next, drawn down from the widow's gallery by some astronomer's sixth sense, and stood over the child with her hands clasped and her mouth doing something complicated. Ines reads skies and faces with equal fluency. She looked at that smudged, shuttered little face and said softly, "Oh, you poor *thing*," and the child flinched — not at the words but at the tenderness in them, flinched the way you'd flinch from a match struck in a dark room.

Then Halvard came down the stairs.

I should describe Halvard properly, since this chapter of events concerns him nearly as much as it concerns me. Master Clockmaker Halvard is seventy-one years old, built like the tower he keeps — narrow, vertical, apparently indestructible, hollow in places nobody has inspected. He has hands like root systems and a face that has spent seventy years practicing neutrality. He is my employer, my teacher, and the closest thing to a father I have left, a fact neither of us has ever said aloud and both of us maintain through the medium of criticism. He descended the stairs, saw the child by the stove, saw the frost-water pooling, and his face performed its neutrality so perfectly that I knew, instantly, it was costing him something enormous.

"No," said Halvard.

"Herr Halvard—" I began.

"We cannot keep it." He said *it* the way you say a number you don't want to enter into the books. "August. We are four adults living in a tower during wartime on forged ration allocations — don't look at me like that, Marta, the whole town knows your arithmetic — and now you propose to add a mouth with no papers, no name, and no explanation? A child on the steps is not an accident. Someone put it there. Whoever puts a child on steps wants the child found by someone, or lost by everyone, and either way we want no part of the transaction."

"It weighs less than my toolbox," I said. Which was, I admit, a strange defense. But it was the only vocabulary I had.

Halvard looked at me for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes — I would learn later what it was, though I would not learn it from him; he guards his history the way the tower guards its clocks — and whatever it was, he buried it fast, shoveling neutrality back over the grave.

"Weight is not the question," he said quietly. "Weight is never the question. The question is what carrying costs."

And then the child spoke. Or rather — the child did not speak. The child held out the paper.

It had not let go of that paper once. Not when I carried it, not when Marta wrapped it in a blanket, not through the bread. Now the small fist unclenched finger by finger, stiff with cold, and offered the page up to Halvard like a tithe, or a surrender, or a key. Halvard took it. He read it. His face did its neutral trick again, but slower this time, and I watched him fold the note along its crease with the exact deliberation he used when closing a clock case.

"Well?" said Marta.

Halvard handed it to me. "Read it yourself," he said, and his voice had gone somewhere flat and far away. "You'll want to read it twice anyway. Everyone does."

The note was written in a hurried, educated hand, the ink blotted in places as if the writer had been shaking. It said:

*This is Lior. Age uncertain, probably seven. Feed the child. Forget the face. Do not ask where the child came from and do not let anyone else ask either. If anyone comes looking, the child was never here and you never saw this note. Forget the face. Forget the face. Forget the face.*

The last line written three times, each repetition pressed harder into the paper until the nib had nearly torn through, as though the writer had been trying to drive the instruction into their own heart as much as ours.

"Forget the face," Ines repeated softly, looking down at the child, whose face — soot-smudged, sharp-boned, watchful — was at that moment entirely unforgettable. "Whoever wrote this has never met a face."

"They've met worse than faces," said Halvard. "They've met soldiers."

The child — Lior — watched us discuss it with the patience of furniture. When I looked over, those dark eyes met mine again, and again asked for nothing, and I felt something happen inside my chest that I can only describe as a clerical error. Some figure I had carried for years — the total at the bottom of the page, the sum of what August Voss owes and is owed, carries and is carried by — some figure in that long column suddenly refused to add up. I checked it twice. It still refused.

Because here is what I had always believed, and believed cleanly: you carry what you can carry, and you leave the rest, and the leaving is not cruelty, it is physics. My father's rule. The apprentice's creed. Every tool weighed, every burden justified, every extra gram accounted for. And here was a seven-year-old weighing less than my toolbox, requiring nothing — asking for nothing, *asking for nothing* — and every rational line in my ledger said *return to sender, refuse delivery, this is not your consignment,* and my hand, my stupid sentimental hand, had already reached out and straightened the blanket Marta had put around those shoulders.

Straightened it. As if it mattered. As if a straightened blanket were an entry in some account I had agreed to open.

"The tower has room," said Marta, who had already decided and was merely informing the rest of us, in the manner of weather. "There's the old chart room. It's warm off the clock shaft."

"Marta." Halvard's voice had an edge on it now, honed rather than angry. "Every person in this building who shelters that child becomes complicit in whatever that note is hiding from. You understand that. All of you understand that. This is not a kitten."

"This is not a kitten," Marta agreed placidly, ladling porridge. "Kittens get a saucer. This one gets breakfast."

"Ines?" Halvard turned, seeking one ally, any ally, in the fortress of his own common sense.

Ines looked at the child for a long moment, and then at Halvard, and said something I did not understand until much later: "Some things are placed where they're placed, Halvard. Stars included. Arguing with the placement doesn't move them."

"That is not astronomy, that is fatalism."

"That is astronomy," said Ines, "as actually practiced."

Halvard stood in his own entrance hall, in his own tower, defeated by a cook, a widow, and a child who had not yet managed a single word, and I watched him lose. He lost slowly, the way he did everything — with terrible dignity, with the air of a man consenting to a delay rather than a defeat. His eyes went from the child to the note to me, and lingered on me longest, and what passed between us in that look was not permission. It was more like a warning issued by one drowning man to another: *careful, boy. The water rises faster than you think.*

"One night," he said finally. "One night, until we find where this child belongs. Then it goes to the authorities, or the church, or wherever children go in wartime, and we go back to being people who fix clocks." He jabbed a root-like finger at me. "Voss. Your clock is losing eleven seconds a day. Go lose yourself in it. And take the—" he stopped, corrected himself with visible effort, "—take Lior with you. If the child must exist, it may exist where I can see it."

Which is how, at half past seven on a frozen morning in the last November of the Great War, I climbed the two hundred and six steps of the observatory tower with a strange silent child on my heels and my tool case in my hand, bound for the greatest clock on the coast, carrying — against every rule I kept, against the whole tidy weight of my life — exactly one thing more than I had planned.

Behind us, in the entrance hall, the note lay on the table where Halvard had left it, three times telling the world to forget a face that four people were already finding impossible to forget.

And below the tower, far off toward the harbor, a ship I did not yet know existed waited in the grey water with one empty berth — and a man I did not yet know existed was already asking questions about a child on some steps.

But that morning I knew none of it. That morning I knew only eleven seconds of drift, two hundred and six steps, and the sound of small feet climbing behind me, keeping time.
