## 3. Halvard's Hands

I have been a clockmaker for fifty-one years, and in that time I have learned that everything honest can be said with the hands. Words are for people who haven't yet learned to trust their fingers. My father said it differently — he said talk is what a man does while his hands are resting — but my father was also wrong about pendulums, so we needn't consult him on every matter.

The boy Voss came back at seven o'clock sharp, which I noted with approval and did not mention, because mentioning a person's virtues is the fastest way to make them self-conscious of them, like pointing out that someone is breathing.

"You're early," I said anyway, because I am not always wise.

"The tram was running," he said.

"Trams are unreliable."

"Yes, sir."

He stood there holding his little canvas roll of tools like a soldier presenting arms, and behind him, on the landing, sat the child. The forgotten child. The face we were all instructed to erase. It had spent the night — this was Marta's doing; Marta does things the way weather does things, without asking permission — wrapped in two blankets and half of Ines's spare coat, sleeping against the doorframe of my observatory as though the building were something one could lean on for comfort rather than merely stone and mathematics.

I looked at the child exactly once, the way you look at a crack in a mainspring: assessing damage, deciding whether the part is worth saving or must be replaced.

I decided nothing. That was the trick of it. You decide nothing, and then the thing decides itself, and by the time anyone asks when you made up your mind, the decision has been sitting there so long it has dust on it.

"The clock," Voss said.

"The clock," I agreed. Grateful, almost, for the clock. A clock is a fine thing to hide behind. It has hands, but they never point at you.

* * *

The master clock lives in the tower's belly, a room I have known longer than I have known my own name's weight. Six meters of case, oak and iron, a pendulum eleven meters if it's a centimeter — it isn't, I checked in 1889 and again in 1904, because a man should re-examine his certainties at least twice a century. The great wheel turns the works that turn the hands that tell the town the hour, and the town believes it, because the town has never once thought to check.

That morning the escapement was dying. I could hear it from the stairs. A healthy escapement ticks with a sound like a small confident heartbeat; a failing one hesitates, catches, stutters — the mechanical equivalent of a man trying to explain himself.

"Listen," I told Voss.

He listened. He had his head cocked at an angle I recognized, because I had worn that same angle at seventeen, under another old man's instruction, in another tower that has since been repurposed into a warehouse for army boots.

"It's missing beats," he said.

"It's *dropping* beats. Missing implies it went looking for them." I opened the access panel. "An escapement has one job. It releases. Everything else in this clock stores energy — weights, springs, the mainspring coiled like a grudge. But the escapement? Its whole purpose is letting go. Controlled letting go. One tooth at a time, measured to the ten-thousandth. If it holds on too long, the clock stops. If it lets go too fast, the clock runs away and tells lies about the time."

Voss wrote this down. Of course he wrote it down. The boy kept ledgers the way other men kept diaries or mistresses — constantly, secretly, with more feeling than he'd admit to.

"Sir," he said, "why would a clock run fast?"

"Why indeed."

I put his hands where mine had been. This is the whole of teaching, if you want to know: you put their hands where yours have been, and you stand behind them, and you say *no, there*, forty times, until one day you say it and they've already done it, and then you are obsolete, and that is the entire point.

* * *

My son had hands like mine.

I will say that plainly now, here, in the privacy of my own skull, because I say it nowhere else. Emil had broad palms and short fingers, terrible for watchwork, hopeless at filing — he'd hold a pivot between thumb and forefinger and it would go flying across the bench like a startled insect. And he laughed when it happened. Every time. As though losing tiny steel parts across a workshop were the most natural comedy in the world.

He was conscripted in the second year of the war, at nineteen, because the empire had run out of men who wanted to go and had begun taking men who could still walk. His last letter said the mud in Flanders came in three colors and none of them were colors. His last letter also asked me to send him a small screwdriver, because apparently even in a trench a man wants to fix things.

I sent the screwdriver. It came back to me with his effects, along with his boots, his pipe, and a photograph of our shop that I did not know he carried. The screwdriver had been used. That detail undid me more than the rest combined. Something in him had still been mending, right up to whatever moment stopped him, and whatever he'd fixed with it stayed fixed somewhere, in some trench, holding together some object I will never identify.

People say time heals. Time does no such thing. Time is simply an escapement. It releases you one tooth at a time, and if you're lucky the mechanism holds and you keep ticking, and nobody looking at you from the outside can hear how many beats you drop.

* * *

By midmorning Voss had the anchor plate off and was examining the pallet stones with the concentration of a monk copying scripture. I took the opportunity to do what I had resolved not to do, which was look at the child again.

It — she? he? I genuinely could not tell, and the note had not enlightened us, being occupied entirely with its single mad instruction — sat at the top of the stairs, knees drawn up, watching us work with eyes too old for the rest of the face. Children evacuated from the border regions get those eyes. So do children whose parents simply stop coming home. The war manufactures that look industrially, efficiently, the way Krupp manufactures shells.

"Hungry?" I asked, before I could stop myself. I phrased it as an accusation, which helped.

The child shook its head.

"Liar," I said. "Everyone's hungry. The question is whether they admit it." I pointed at the stairwell. "Marta's kitchen is below. She'll feed you whether you ask or not, so asking saves her the trouble of pretending she wasn't going to."

The child didn't move.

"I'm not going to eat you," I said. "I'm seventy-one years old and I smell of machine oil. You're perfectly safe."

Still nothing. Fine. I had raised one stubborn child already; I knew the posture. Stubbornness in the young is just courage that hasn't found its direction yet. You wait. You let the mechanism settle. You do not force the tooth.

I turned back to the clock and pretended, loudly, to be absorbed in the pallet stones.

Ten minutes later I heard small feet on the stairs, going down. Toward Marta. Toward bread.

I felt absurdly victorious and showed it to no one, which is the correct way to feel anything.

* * *

"Now," I said to Voss, "the recoil. Watch what happens when the escape wheel pushes back against the pallet."

He watched. He had good hands, the boy — narrow, precise, the opposite of Emil's — and a mind like a well-kept accounts book. Everything entered, everything balanced, nothing left loose. I had noticed this about him over the years of his apprenticeship: August Voss trusted measurement the way other men trusted God, and with somewhat better evidence.

There was a cost to it, of course. There is always a cost. A man who measures everything is a man who carries nothing, because carrying things means admitting they matter, and admitting they matter means they can be taken, and Voss had the look of a young man who had calculated early and thoroughly what could be taken from him and had decided, sensibly, to own nothing that could be.

I recognized the arithmetic. I had done it myself after the telegram came. I had sold the house in town, moved permanently into the tower, reduced my possessions to tools, books, and clocks. A man cannot be robbed of what he refuses to carry. It is a magnificent strategy and it does not work, because grief finds you anyway, and grief weighs nothing and takes up all the space.

"Sir?" Voss said. "You were saying."

"Was I saying something?"

"You stopped mid-sentence."

"I was demonstrating the value of pauses. An escapement that never pauses is called a runaway." I tapped the plate. "Lock. Draw. Drop. Lock again. Say it back."

"Lock, draw, drop."

"And the drop must never be wasted. Wasted drop wears the teeth. Everything in a clock that seems like idleness is actually precision."

He nodded and wrote it in the little notebook he kept in his breast pocket, and I watched him write, and I thought: *there is a boy who believes the ledger is protection.* I had believed that myself once. I had believed that if I recorded everything — every repair, every customer, every date — then the record would outlast the losses. Entries instead of sons. Columns instead of arms around shoulders at a funeral.

Then the war came and taught the entire continent the same lesson simultaneously: paper burns, records lie, governments erase names with the stroke of a pen, and the only ledger that cannot be falsified is the one scratched inside your ribs.

But he was twenty-three and entitled to his arithmetic. I'd had mine at his age too.

* * *

Marta appeared at noon with a tray — bread, cheese, coffee that could have stripped varnish — and set it down among the tools with the disregard for order that defines her entire existence. Marta treats organization as a personal insult.

"There's a child eating in my kitchen," she announced.

"I know," I said.

"It ate three rolls and is eyeing the pot."

"I know."

"Somebody should sit with it. It flinches when the door opens."

"Ines is reading in the parlor."

"Ines is reading with one ear," Marta said, which was true and which we both knew. Ines missed nothing; she had simply learned, as widows do, that noticing aloud is different from noticing silently. "And you're up here hiding behind your clock."

"I am repairing a clock. That is my profession. Hiding is incidental."

She gave me the look — the one that has made stronger men than me confess to crimes — and left. The child remained downstairs. The coffee remained terrible. The work remained good.

That is the mercy of craft, if you want the truth of my life: when your hands are busy, your heart can grieve at its own pace, unsupervised. Fifty-one years of repairs and not one of them was only about the mechanism.

* * *

We worked until the light through the tower slit went amber. By four o'clock the escapement ran clean — lock, draw, drop, no hesitation — and the great pendulum swung with the patience of something that has outlived several empires and expects to outlive ours.

Voss closed the panel and stepped back and did the thing I have seen apprentices do for half a century: listened with his whole body, checking his work against the silence afterward, listening for the flaw he might have left.

"She'll keep time now," I said.

"Yes, sir."

"Say it properly. She'll keep time for another thirty years, barring artillery."

"Barring artillery," he repeated, and something crossed his face — the flicker of a young man remembering that the world outside the tower had opinions about whether any of our clocks would be permitted to finish their careers.

I dismissed him. He gathered his tools, each one into its designated pocket — I told you, an accounts-book mind — and paused at the stairs.

"The child," he said. "What do we do about—"

"Nothing," I said, with the crisp authority of a man who has decided nothing very firmly. "It's Marta's problem. Marta collects problems. Ask her sometime how many."

He looked at me a moment too long. Young men do that; they think looking hard enough will reveal the contents of an older man the way x-rays reveal fractures. Then he went down the stairs, and I heard him pause on the landing where the blankets still lay folded, and I heard him say nothing, and continue down.

I stayed with the clock.

* * *

After he'd gone I opened the panel again. Not for maintenance. For the other reason, the reason I open it perhaps once a month, at dusk, when the tower is empty and the town below is lighting lamps it may or may not be permitted to keep lighting.

Inside the case, low on the frame where no inspector would ever look, there are scratches. Small ones, made with a jeweler's graver by a boy with clumsy hands who should have been filing pivots and instead was carving letters. *EMIL WAS HERE,* says the largest, aged twelve, defiant. Below it, added over the following years, dates and jokes and the names of cats. And lower still, in my hand, added six years ago with a steadiness I did not feel: *Emil Halvardsen, 1898–1916. He fixed things.*

A clockmaker's son, memorialized inside a clock. Some would call it morbid. I call it accurate placement. He is inside the machine that keeps time now, which is more than the mud of Flanders can claim.

I touched the letters once, the way you touch a banister in the dark, and closed the case.

The child was still in Marta's kitchen. The note was still in Marta's apron pocket — *forget the face*, written in a hand that shook, instructing an entire household to perform an erasure, as though faces could be un-happened. I had read it twice and disliked it twice. I am an old man and I know something about instructions: the urgent ones are nearly always lies, and the people who write them are usually protecting themselves, not you.

Forget the face. As if I hadn't spent six years failing to forget one.

Below me, faintly, I heard the child laugh. Once. A small, rusty sound, like a mechanism turning over after years in storage — hesitant, catching, then catching properly.

I stood in the clock room with my hand still on the warm oak of the case, and I thought about lock, draw, drop, and how everything that keeps time must learn to release, and how I had released nothing in six years and was therefore the worst-running mechanism in this entire tower.

Then I heard August Voss's footsteps stop on the stairs above — stop, and stay stopped — and heard him, very quietly, begin to go through the child's belongings.
