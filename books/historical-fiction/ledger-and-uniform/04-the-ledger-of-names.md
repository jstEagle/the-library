## 4. The Ledger of Names

The child slept in the east alcove that first night, on a pallet Marta made from a horse blanket and what she called "the good straw," which I did not know the observatory possessed. Lior curled into it like a comma, all punctuation and no sentence, one fist closed around something beneath the blanket.

I noticed this because I notice everything, not because I cared. There is a difference, and I have built my whole life on the difference.

I had come to the observatory to repair a clock. That was the arrangement. Halvard had signed my work order with a hand that trembled only at the descenders, and I had cataloged my tools accordingly — forty-one items, eleven pounds four ounces, distributed across two satchels so that neither strap cut the shoulder at an angle. A man who carries little survives long. This was arithmetic I trusted.

Now there was a child in the tower who carried nothing visible except a note instructing the world to forget them, and the note itself, which is the kind of paradox that would make a lesser clockmaker lie awake.

I lay awake anyway. Not because of the paradox. Because of the fist.

* * *

In the morning I found out what the fist was holding, and it was worse than a paradox.

It happened because of the clock, or rather because of the child's habit of standing near it. The master clock occupied the tower's second chamber, a case of black oak tall as two men, its pendulum swinging behind glass like a metronome for the war itself. It had been losing eleven seconds a day when I arrived — an insult I took personally — and by the third morning I had it down to four, which meant I could leave the escapement alone for six hours while I attended to the strike train.

I left the chamber. When I came back, Lior was inside the case.

Not inside it exactly — kneeling before it, the lower cabinet door open, both hands buried in the weight chasm where the driving weights descend through their run. Children put things in clocks. Marbles, buttons, the occasional dead bird. I had extracted all three varieties from cases over the years and had developed a professional serenity about it.

"Lior." I kept my voice at workshop pitch. "Out. Now."

The child turned, and I saw the face properly for the first time in daylight: sharp little chin, eyes too large for it, the particular gray pallor of someone who has been hungry longer than hunger is interesting. And the fist was open now, and in it was a book.

A ledger. An actual ledger, pocket-sized, quarter-bound in green cloth gone gray at the corners, its spine cracked in the way spines crack when a book has been opened hundreds of times in wet weather and dried badly each time. The kind of damage you see on account books carried in coat pockets through winters.

"That's not yours," I said, which was stupid, because nothing in the world was currently the child's, including the child.

Lior looked at me the way people look at a locked door they have already decided to pick. Then the child held out the ledger.

I should have refused it. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever internal register I keep — I understood even then that taking it was a decision, that ledgers are never neutral objects, that a man whose heart is allegedly a ledger ought to know better than to accept one from a half-starved child found on steps with instructions to be forgotten. But my hands were already reaching, because a ledger in poor condition offered to a clockmaker is not a temptation; it is a summons.

I opened it.

The first page was dated fourteen months back. The hand was small and hurried and got more legible as the pages went on, which told me the writer had been learning urgency's opposite — that whoever kept this book had begun in panic and grown methodical, and methodical record-keeping in wartime is either commerce or resistance, and neither is safe.

Names. Columns of them. Name, age, origin, and then a notation column that used symbols I didn't immediately parse: a small circle, a triangle, a dash with two strokes.

"Deserters," I said quietly. It wasn't a question. The circles sat next to names with regimental numbers — I knew the format; every garrison town knows the format. Men who had walked away from uniforms, recorded here with the towns where they'd been hidden. *Vasch, P., 24, cobbler's cellar, Rellin.* *Ostrava, M., 19, farm loft, two miles south of Tern.*

Lior watched me read. The child's breathing had gone very careful, the way mine goes when I am pretending a mechanism is simpler than it is.

I turned pages. Hidden families next — whole households compressed into single lines, mothers with numbers of children appended like subentries. *Brandt, widow, 3 children, granary, Kesselweg, since October.* A triangle marked these. The dashes with two strokes marked something else entirely, something that appeared only seven times in the entire book, always written larger than the rest, always with a place and a date.

On page thirty-one I found out what the dashes meant, and the floor of my professional serenity went out from under me.

*Meridian. One berth. Sails with the tide before the armistice. Berth held under the name H. Iversen.*

Iversen. My surname. Not my name — I had never booked passage anywhere, I had never in my life been near enough to a coast to smell salt — but my surname, entered in a stolen ledger in a child's fist, attached to the last berth on a relief ship that intended to leave before anyone signed anything.

I sat down on the weight-chasm step without meaning to. The great case loomed over us both, ticking its four-seconds-lost tick, patient as everything old and large.

"Where did you get this?" I asked.

Silence. Lior had not spoken since arriving — I had noted it in my mental inventory under *conditions, unexplained,* between *malnutrition, moderate* and *fear, chronic.* The muteness had seemed like another weight the child declined to carry. Now it seemed like strategy.

"Who wrote this?"

Nothing. But the child's eyes went to the last entry, and I followed them, and the last entry was fresh ink, less than a week old, and it was not a name.

It was a description. Of the observatory. Of the tower, the hours the door went unlocked, the days Halvard climbed to the lamp room, the fact that the cook bought more bread than two old men and one apprentice could eat. Written in the same small hand as everything else. Dated five days ago.

Five days ago was the night before Lior appeared on the steps.

Someone had scouted this tower. Someone had written down its habits the way I write down the weights of tools, and then had handed their observations to a child along with a note saying *forget the face*, and the child had climbed our stairs carrying a book full of hidden men and smuggled families and one berth under a false name that happened to share mine, and everyone responsible for all of it had evidently agreed that the correct response was forgetting.

I am good at many things. Forgetting is not among them. It requires a looseness of mind I have never possessed; my memory is a rack gear, it holds what is cut into it.

I closed the ledger. My hands were steady, which annoyed me. Some moments deserve trembling and refuse to grant it.

"This stays in the clock case," I said. "The bottom compartment, behind the strike-weight guide. Nobody looks behind a strike-weight guide; nobody even knows it exists. You will tell no one I've seen this, and if anyone asks, you've shown me nothing, because I haven't seen it, because I don't exist in relation to this object."

Lior blinked. Then, slowly, the child nodded — and there was something in the nod that I recognized with genuine horror. It was relief. Professional relief. The relief of one meticulous person handing custody of records to another meticulous person and watching the other person immediately devise a hiding protocol. I had just been conscripted, in silence, into whatever this was, and the enlistment papers had been signed by my own competence.

The war had spent four years staying politely outside the tower, reduced to rumors, ration counts, the occasional list of the fallen read out at market. I had liked it that way. I had assumed the tower's altitude exempted it, the way a clock is exempt from the weather it reports.

But the weather had climbed the stairs. It was sleeping on Marta's good straw. It was holding a stolen ledger full of crimes the state would hang people for, and it had written my surname on a berth I had never booked, sailing on a ship toward a coast I had never seen, before an armistice that might be weeks away or might be a rumor soldiers died believing in.

I put the ledger behind the strike-weight guide, wedged it flat, closed the compartment, and dusted the seam with brass filings until the hinge looked untouched. Forty-one tools, eleven pounds four ounces, and now one book I could not weigh, because it contained, by my rough count, sixty-one lives, and mass is not the same as weight.

Behind me, the master clock lost another second.

I stood there in the ticking dark doing the thing I do instead of praying, which is counting. Sixty-one names. Seven berths' worth of dashes. One berth remaining. One child. One apprentice who had accepted custody of all of it in under four minutes because his hands were incapable of refusing an object in poor condition.

And then, from the stairwell, footsteps — Halvard's uneven tread, and Marta's heavier one behind it, and voices, low and urgent, and a word that stopped my blood mid-gear:

They were saying a soldier had been seen at the foot of the hill. Asking questions. Asking, specifically, about children.
