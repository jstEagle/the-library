## 5. What Can Be Carried

There is a discipline to packing a toolbox, and I have practiced it the way other men practice prayer. Every tool has a weight, every weight has a purpose, and anything without both stays home. My father taught me this before he taught me to read, or perhaps instead of teaching me to read — the ledgers came later, and they were easier than people.

A clockmaker's kit for travel: one loupe, four grams. Three screwdrivers, brass-handled, eleven grams apiece, because steel scratches and brass forgives. One pair of tweezers, two grams, though I have seen grown men weep over what those two grams can accomplish. Oil in a glass vial no bigger than a thimble. Peg wood. A roll of blue paper. The whole kit fits in a satchel that hangs from my shoulder like a second heart, and I know its weight to within an ounce, because I have weighed it, more than once, on the observatory's balance scale, when the nights were long and my hands needed something honest to do.

This is how I survive: I carry little, and I know exactly what it weighs.

The war has made a philosophy of me. Rations are weighed. Coffins are counted. The trains run on schedules written by men who have never missed a departure and never once arrived anywhere themselves. Everything in this country has been reduced to what can be carried — onto trains, across borders, into the ground. And so when the question of Lior's ledger finally cracked open in front of me like a dropped watch, all its gears spilling across Marta's kitchen table, I did what I always do.

I took inventory.

* * *

It began, as most of my worst ideas do, with arithmetic.

The ledger lay between us at breakfast — Lior's ledger, the stolen one, the one with names in three different hands and ink of three different ages. Marta had put out bread that was mostly sawdust and called it bread anyway, which is her way of lying for the common good. Halvard sat at the head of the table not eating, which is his way of eating. Ines had already gone up to the roof with her charts, because the sky does not keep human hours and neither, apparently, did she.

And Lior sat across from me, small and silent, hands folded around a cup of chicory they were too polite to drink, watching me look at them the way I would look at a movement that wouldn't run.

I want to say I was thinking about mercy. I want to say some better self was whispering about compassion, about the child's hollow wrists, about the note that said forget the face. But the truth is uglier and more useful, so I will write it down the way I write everything down:

I was calculating escape weight.

One berth remained aboard the *Meridian*, sailing from the coast before the armistice — if the armistice came at all, and if the ship waited for it, and if the berth still existed by the time anyone reached it. One berth. The ledger said so in a clerk's careful hand, the last entry before the page went blank. One berth, and five people under this roof who might need it, plus a child the berth had arguably been stolen *for* in the first place.

Five candidates. One seat. Even Halvard could do that division, and Halvard refused to do any mathematics after 1914 as a matter of principle.

So I did it for him. Somebody had to, and I had the training.

Marta first, because Marta was easiest. Marta Brenner, fifty-one years old, cook, widow of a fisherman lost so far back that even she referred to him in the past-perfect tense of legend rather than grief. Weight carried: herself, roughly eleven stone of opinionated stubbornness; a ration book she kept hidden inside a hollowed-out hymnal; knees that had been bad since before the war and would be bad after it, if there was an after. Could she flee? Physically, yes, slowly, complaining in three dialects. Practically — Marta had not left this town during two invasions, a cholera scare, and the year the bakery burned. She was the kind of woman who becomes furniture in the best sense: load-bearing. You cannot evacuate the load-bearing walls. The house comes down.

Verdict: Marta stays. Not because she couldn't go. Because asking her to would be like asking the tower to take a walk.

Ines next, and here the arithmetic got harder, the way it always does when the numbers involve someone you've begun to — I was going to write *respect*. Let it stand. Respect.

Ines Halloran, thirty-four, astronomer's widow, nine months widowed and counting, though she'd tell you her husband died years earlier, incrementally, each night he spent on the roof instead of in their bed. She was born in another country — I will not write which; the ledger taught me that some facts are ammunition — and spoke our language with an accent she had spent a decade sanding down to nearly nothing. Nearly. There was a town two hundred kilometers east where a customs officer would hear three words from her mouth and start writing.

Weight carried: her husband's charts, his telescope — forty kilograms if it was an ounce, and she would sooner leave an arm — a folio of star tables in her own hand, and one wedding photograph she kept behind the lining of her coat like a smuggled document, which, in a sense, it was.

Could Ines flee? Her mind said yes. Her legs said yes. Her accent said *maybe, if nobody talks to you at the border.* And her face — I had watched her face for weeks now, up on the roof, tracking troop lanterns moving along the coast road the way other widows track clouds — her face said she had already decided the sky was the only country issuing visas anymore.

Verdict: Ines could go, but going would cost her the work she'd built out of her grief, and grief is the one possession the war hasn't figured out how to tax yet. Uncertain. Flag it. Revisit.

Halvard.

Master clockmaker Halvard Brandt, sixty-eight years old, hands like tree roots, eyes like a man perpetually reading something just over your shoulder. Weight carried: sixty years of tools, a workshop that could not be packed, a tower full of clocks that would stop within a month of his leaving — and everyone knows what stopped clocks mean in a town like this. Abandonment. Bad luck. A story told in the tavern for a generation: *the old man walked away and time walked off with him.*

Could Halvard flee? His body said barely. His pride said never. His craft said the master clock needed him through winter regardless of armistices, because a mechanism half-repaired fails faster than one left alone, and I should know, since I was the half-repair.

But there was another entry in Halvard's column, and my pencil hovered over it a long time. He had lost a son. Everyone in the household knew it the way you know weather coming — not discussed, but felt in the joints. And I had begun to notice, in the fortnight since Lior arrived, that Halvard's indifference to the child was the most elaborate performance I had ever seen a craftsman mount. He ignored Lior with tremendous precision. He found reasons to be in rooms the child occupied. He complained about the noise Lior didn't make.

Verdict: Halvard would not flee if the harbor itself came to the doorstep and offered to carry him. He would stay with his clocks until the clocks or the war finished him. This was not courage. It was the same thing as mine, probably — a man who carries little because everything heavy was taken from him years ago and he refuses to be robbed twice.

Which left Lior. And me.

Lior could be carried — that was almost the entire problem. A child of perhaps eight, light as a stolen loaf, designed by fate to fit into exactly one berth on exactly one ship. The ledger had been built around this child the way the master clock was built around its escapement: everything else existed to make this one motion possible.

And me? August Voss, apprentice, twenty-three, weight carried: one satchel, one ledger habit, one set of principles that had never once been tested against open water. I could go anywhere. That was the joke of it. Of the five of us, I was the most portable and the least entitled. The berth wasn't mine. The child wasn't mine. The family — and I use the word with tongs, the way you handle a hot part — the family had assembled itself around me while I wasn't looking, and now the ledger wanted me to choose which pieces of it to pack.

I did what I always do with problems too large for the workbench.

I got out paper.

* * *

Marta caught me at it that evening, because of course she did. You cannot hide anything from a cook. She knows your appetite better than you do; she can read a mood in how much bread you leave.

She came down into the workshop where I'd spread my notes across Halvard's bench — sacrilege, but Halvard was upstairs pretending not to listen to Lior breathe — and she stood over the pages with flour on her forearms and read my survival arithmetic upside down, which she did effortlessly, being fluent in at least six things she pretended not to know.

"What's this, then?" she said. "Weights?"

"Inventories."

"Of us." It wasn't a question. Marta reads upside down and sideways and straight through a man. "You've got me down at eleven stone."

"Estimate."

"Flattering estimate. Keep going." She picked up the sheet with Ines's name on it, and her eyebrows climbed in a way that made me want to snatch it back and also confess everything, and I settled for standing very still, which is my best move. "Accent. Photograph. Telescope." She set it down gently, gentler than I expected. "August Voss. What exactly do you think you're doing?"

"Working out who fits in the berth."

"The last berth. On the ship." She wiped her hands on her apron, a gesture that in Marta meant she was preparing to touch something dirty, possibly me. "And who decides? You? With your little weights?"

"Somebody has to count."

"No," Marta said. "Somebody has to *cook*. Counting is how it started, you understand? Somebody counted, and then somebody else counted, and then there were orders, and the orders had numbers, and now there's a boy at my table who doesn't speak because somewhere a man with a list decided his mother was one number over." She leaned in. She smelled of onions and woodsmoke and, beneath it, something iron and old. "You want to help that child? Stop weighing people. Weighs are for flour."

"It's just arithmetic," I said, and heard, even as it left me, how much it sounded like the men who ran the trains.

"Everything is just arithmetic right up until it isn't," Marta said. "That's the whole trick of this century." She took the bread knife off the bench — I flinched; she noticed; she almost smiled — and used it to point at my papers. "Burn these, or finish them, I don't care which. But know what you're holding, boy. That's not a packing list. That's a triage chart. And triage is just a polite word for choosing who bleeds."

She swept out. The door banged. Somewhere above us, faint through three floors of stone, the master clock ticked on, half-repaired, keeping imperfect time, patient as a confessor.

I looked at my neat columns for a long while. Eleven stone. Forty kilograms. Two grams of tweezers. It had all seemed so clean an hour ago, so honest, the way a ledger is honest — which is to say, honest about nothing except what you chose to enter and what you chose to leave out.

Because here is the thing I hadn't entered. Here is the line item missing from every column, the omission that makes the whole account fraudulent:

Lior had watched me write it. From the doorway, silent as ever, the child had stood in the workshop shadows long enough to see their own name at the top of a column marked, in my tidy hand, *can be carried.*

And had turned around, without a sound, and gone back up the stairs.

I sat in Halvard's workshop feeling the specific shame of a man who has balanced his books perfectly and discovered, too late, that he was robbing the wrong till. Outside, the wind came off the coast road carrying the smell of snow and something else — engines, maybe, or only my imagination, which had lately developed a talent for engines.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Ines was coming down off the roof early, ahead of schedule, and Ines never came down early unless the sky had told her something.

She was already calling my name before I reached the landing.
