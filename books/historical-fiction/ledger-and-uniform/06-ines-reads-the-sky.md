## 6. Ines Reads the Sky

My husband spent thirty years teaching me that the sky is a ledger. Every star a fixed entry, every planet a correction in the margin, every comet — God help us — an error someone else will have to reconcile. Henrik died believing the universe was honest bookkeeping. I have kept his charts, his brass instruments, and his habit of looking up when everyone else looks down, and I can tell you now, with the authority of a woman who has been widowed by arithmetic: the universe keeps its books, but it does not care whether you like the totals.

I say this so you understand why I was on the observatory roof at eleven o'clock at night in November, wrapped in Henrik's old coat with a thermos of Marta's chicory substitute, waiting for Mars to clear the chimney of the east wing. There are worse ways to spend a war. There are also warmer ones.

"Ines." August Voss came up the ladder with the particular caution of a man who catalogs everything he carries, including himself. "Marta said you wanted me."

"Marta says a great many things, most of them true, all of them loud. Sit down before you fall down. And give me your hands — you're freezing."

"I'm fine."

"You're the color of unpolished pewter, boy. Hands."

He surrendered them. They were cold as a dead man's promise, and they had the faint smell of clock oil that never quite leaves him, that I suspect has entered his blood by now. I held them between mine and thought, not for the first time, what a strange thing it is to warm a young person's hands and know that no amount of warming reaches whatever is cold underneath.

"Look there," I said, nodding at the southeast horizon. "Above the tree line. The bright one."

"That's a star."

"That is Mars, you heretic, and if you'd spent one evening in thirty years listening to my husband instead of counting screws, you'd know it. Stars twinkle. Planets hold steady. It's the single most useful thing astronomy ever taught anyone, and it took mankind four thousand years to learn it, which should comfort us about our own slow progress."

August sat beside me on the bench Henrik built the year we married, when the observatory was new and the century was young and neither of us knew what the twentieth century intended to do with itself. He looked where I pointed, obedient as an apprentice.

"Why did you want me?" he asked. Not rudely. August doesn't do rude; he does efficient, which from a distance resembles rudeness the way a ledger resembles literature.

"Because you're awake at odd hours, because you're discreet, and because I need a second pair of eyes that isn't Halvard's. Halvard sees clocks. If I showed him a supernova he'd tell me it was running fast."

"He would."

"And Lior—" I stopped myself, watched him stop himself too. We had both learned, these past two weeks, to approach the child's name the way you approach a bird on a windowsill: slowly, without sudden movements, without expectations. "Lior sleeps," I finished.

"For now. Marta fed them soup at six and they slept until eight, which Marta calls a miracle and I call the body settling its debts."

"The body is the only honest creditor left," I said. "Now look. Tell me what you see."

"A red star. Planet. Above the trees."

"Steady?"

"Steady."

"Good. Now watch it for ten minutes and don't talk. Can you manage ten minutes of silence? I ask sincerely, because I've known you since you were nine and I'm not certain."

He almost smiled. It was a small victory and I took it.

* * *

Let me explain what I was doing up there, because August asked eventually, in his sideways fashion, and because this part matters.

Henrik, before the war made liars of the newspapers, had corresponded with naval observatories in three countries. He charted weather the way other men charted investments — obsessively, superstitiously, convinced that patterns hid in clouds the way profits hide in losses. When he died, I kept the work going. Partly grief, partly stubbornness, partly because a widow with a telescope is ignored by everyone, including censors, and I discovered early in this war that being ignored is a kind of power.

You can see a great deal from a rooftop. Not troop movements themselves — I am not a magician, whatever Halvard implies when he thinks I can't hear — but their consequences. Trains run at night now, windows blacked, but blacked windows glow differently than dark countryside, and a string of glows moving west along the valley line three nights running tells you something no newspaper will. Smoke rises over towns that shouldn't be burning anything at that hour or that season. Birds move ahead of artillery the way fish move ahead of storms — refugees on wings, and I have learned to read them the way Henrik read barometric pressure. And the weather itself, the cold coming down out of the north earlier and harder than any winter in my charts, the fog sitting in the lowlands like a guest who won't leave — weather decides wars more often than generals admit, though no general has ever thanked a cloud.

So I keep Henrik's logbooks alive with new entries in my own hand. Temperature, visibility, wind, the trains, the birds, the smoke. And lately, overlaid on all of it, the rumor that moves through the town like warmth through a wall: armistice. Any week now. The papers say it. The barber says it. Marta's cousin's neighbor's brother-in-law, who knows a man at the rail depot, swears it will be signed before Advent.

Everyone wants me to confirm it. As if the sky kept a calendar of men's promises.

* * *

"Ten minutes," August said finally. "It hasn't moved."

"It moved. About a finger's width. You just didn't see it, which is the whole lesson." I poured him some chicory. He drank it without complaint, which proves either courage or despair. "Now. You've been hearing the same rumors I have. Armistice before Advent. Perhaps before the ship sails."

His jaw tightened. He tried to hide it behind the cup. I let him try.

"The Meridian," I said. "That's the name on the berth, isn't it? In the ledger."

"We don't speak of the ledger."

"We speak of nothing else in this house; we simply do it in code. The ledger, the child, the berth. Marta hums louder. Halvard polishes things that were already polished. You've developed a twitch in your left eye whenever the harbor is mentioned. We're all very subtle, August. It's one of our finest qualities."

He set the cup down. "Ines. What did you bring me up here to say?"

Straight to it. That's the thing about August people misread — they think the ledger-heart means coldness, when really it means he can't bear the space between question and answer, so he abolishes it. I find it exhausting and rather touching.

I turned back to the sky and gathered my evidence the way I'd gathered it all week, checking each item against the last.

"I'll say it plainly, then, because you're the only one in this house who can hear it plainly without either praying or throwing something. The armistice may not come when promised. Or it may come late. Or it may be announced and then unravel, the way the last round of talks unraveled in spring — you remember, everyone lit candles and then the candles were wasted. I have been reading the sky and the rails and the birds, and here is what they tell me."

I held up fingers, one per point, an old lecturer's habit Henrik used to mock until students started doing it back to him.

"One. The trains. Three nights this week, military transport moving west, heavy, unscheduled. Armies do not redeploy westward when they expect peace within the month. They do it when they expect one more offensive, or when they expect to have to hold a line through winter. Either way — not the posture of surrender."

"Could be withdrawal. Could be the beginning of the end."

"Could be. But withdrawal looks different — lighter, faster, and they burn what they leave. This is buildup. I've seen buildup twice before in my logs, and both times it preceded offensives."

"Two?"

"Two. The weather." I gestured at the north, where the stars burned hard and close, no haze, no mercy. "The high pressure sitting over the Baltic is the deepest cold I have on record for this date, and my records go back twenty-two years. Cold like this freezes mud. Frozen ground makes armies mobile. Spring thaws stop armies; deep frosts start them. If anyone planned a last push, this is the sky to push under."

August said nothing, but his breath came out slower. I pressed on, gently now, because the third point was the one that mattered.

"Three. The ships." I looked at him directly. "The relief sailings out of the harbor — the civilian ones, the ones carrying families south — have been moving up their dates. The coastal packet went four days early. The freighter that was supposed to wait for the grain convoy left half-loaded. Captains don't sail early and light unless they're afraid of something. Mines, submarines, a change in orders, a change in government — take your pick. But fear travels downhill from admiralties to captains, and it's arrived."

"The Meridian might sail early."

"The Meridian might already have moved its date once. I heard the harbor bells change pattern Tuesday — a different sequence, the one they use for schedule amendments. Nobody else notices. I notice. I've had thirty years of practice noticing things nobody else notices; it's what happens when you marry an astronomer and outlive him."

The wind came across the roof and I pulled Henrik's coat tighter. Below us the town lay dark, properly dark, wartime dark, except for the observatory's own lamps and the thin gold line under Marta's kitchen door, because Marta believes — against all regulation, all sense, and all precedent — that darkness is for people with something to hide and she has nothing to hide except an entire second life of forged ration books, but that's her confession to make, not mine.

"So," August said at last. His voice had gone very quiet and very level, the way it does when the numbers have come out wrong. "If the ship sails early—"

"Then the berth goes with it. Whoever holds it boards when the gangplank drops, or the berth sails empty to whoever's next on the manifest. Ships don't wait. That's the first rule of the sea and the cruelest."

"And the armistice—"

"May save everyone in this house, or may arrive two weeks after it mattered, or may be signed and broken and signed again. I don't know. The sky is honest but it doesn't editorialize." I set down the thermos and turned to face him fully, because the next part needed my eyes, not my charts. "Here's what I actually brought you up here to say, August. Everyone in this house — Marta, Halvard, even little Lior, who understands more German than they let on — everyone is living inside the rumor. Before Advent. Any day now. Hold on, help is coming. And hope is a fine fuel, I burn it myself every morning. But hope with a date attached is a debt, and if the date passes, the debt comes due all at once, and I have watched this town owe that payment before. In spring. You remember the candles."

"I remember the candles."

"So I need you to do what you do. Keep the books honestly. Don't let this household plan around a promise. Because if the Meridian leaves early and we've spent December believing the armistice would make the question moot—"

"Then we'll have traded a real choice for a rumor." He was staring at Mars now, steady and red above the trees, and his face had the expression I imagine he wears over a ledger when the columns refuse to balance. "And we'll have spent the time we had deciding nothing."

"There's my boy."

"I'm not—"

"You are, a bit. Don't fight it, it's undignified at your age." I took the cup from his hands before he could freeze to it. "One more thing. Look at the northeast. Low. That smudge."

"That's cloud."

"That is the glow of a town called Wesselmark burning coal it doesn't have, forty kilometers past where the front was supposed to be when the talks began. Lines move. Maps lie. Skies don't." I stood, joints announcing themselves as they do now, and gathered the thermos. "Go to bed, August. And when Halvard starts talking about how much time we have — and he will, he's already begun, I've seen him doing sums with his lips while he winds the regulator — you remember Mars. Steady, patient, and utterly indifferent to what any of us want the calendar to say."

* * *

He didn't go to bed. I knew he wouldn't; I'd counted on it, frankly. August Voss processes information by walking, and the observatory roof at midnight is as good a place as any.

For a while we stood at the parapet together in silence, two insomniacs keeping vigil over a sleeping house, and I confess I studied him sidelong the way I study the planets — fixedly, and pretending not to. He had changed since the child arrived. Not softened — I suspect softening will come to August the way spring comes to the Baltic, late and grudging and all at once — but complicated. A man who measures everything had been handed something that refused measurement: a mute child with a stolen book and a note instructing the world to forget. I've watched him circle it for two weeks like a cat circling a thing that is either a gift or a trap, unable to determine which, unwilling to walk away from either possibility.

"Do you ever regret it?" he asked suddenly. "The charts. Knowing things."

I laughed before I could stop myself — a short, unlovely sound, steam in cold air.

"Boy, knowing things is the only inheritance my husband left me that the tax office couldn't touch. Regret is for people who had choices." Then, because the night was dark enough to afford honesty: "Yes. Sometimes. Tonight, a little. It's easier to be hopeful when you haven't read the rails."

"Then why tell me? Why not let the house keep its hope?"

"Because hope that survives contact with the truth is the only kind worth having, and someone in this house has to build that kind before the rest of them need it." I touched his shoulder through the coat. His bones were sharp under the wool; the war taxes everyone, even those it hasn't formally billed. "And because you're the one holding the ledger, August. Whatever gets decided about that berth, about that child, about all of us — it will get decided by the person who can count fastest under pressure. I intend for that person to count accurately."

"And if accurate counts to something none of us want?"

"Then we count again in the morning. That's the whole secret of astronomy, if you must know. The sky is always telling you bad news — the universe expanding, the sun dying someday, Mars indifferent above the chimney. Astronomers survive it because tomorrow there's another sky, and you check your work." I smiled at him, and the cold made it feel like a real smile, the kind that costs something. "Check your work, August. Whatever you decide."

He nodded slowly, and I saw him begin the sum behind his eyes — the child, the old man, the cook, the widow, one berth, an unreliable calendar, a ship that might already have changed its sailing time. I left him to it. Some arithmetic you have to do alone, standing up, in the cold, with your own breath for company.

I was halfway down the ladder when I heard it — not from August, not from the sky, but from below, from the street beyond the observatory wall.

Boots. More than one pair, unhurried, deliberate, stopping at the base of the steps where a child had been left in the dawn frost two weeks ago.

I froze on the ladder rung and listened, an old woman in a dead man's coat, doing the one thing I am truly expert at.

Noticing.
