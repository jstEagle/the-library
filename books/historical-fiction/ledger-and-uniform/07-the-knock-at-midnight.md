## 7. The Knock at Midnight

There is a particular sound a clock makes when it is about to be wrong. I had learned it in three weeks at the observatory: a hesitation in the tick, a small swallowed breath between the escapement's release and catch, as if the mechanism were considering lying to you and then deciding against it. The master clock did this every night at 11:47. Halvard claimed he could not hear it. Marta claimed she heard it from the kitchen and found it "smug." Ines said nothing, because Ines only spoke of the clock when it was convenient to her astronomy, which was never.

So when the knock came — not at 11:47 but at some hour after midnight, deep enough into the dark that even the town below had stopped pretending to be awake — my first thought was not *who* but *what*. What part of my life had developed a hesitation. What was about to be wrong.

The knock itself was wrong too. Three blows, evenly spaced, hard enough to rattle the iron hinges but not frantic. A knock that had been rehearsed. People who knock in fear knock badly — too soft, or all at once, or they stop halfway and stand there with their knuckles hovering like moths. This was a knock by someone who understood that doors open for confidence and close for anything else.

I was in the workroom, because I was always in the workroom now. The lamp was down to its last inch of oil. Around me lay the anatomy of the great clock, spread across felt in ordered rows: pallets, escape wheel, the pendulum's suspension spring coiled like something sleeping. I had learned to sleep beside machinery. It is more honest than people. When it breaks, it does so in front of you.

Lior was asleep in the corner on the cot Marta had smuggled up from somewhere — everything in that household arrived by smuggling; we were less an observatory than a port without water. The child slept the way children sleep in wartime, which is to say lightly, one fist closed around nothing, ready to become a smaller, faster version of themselves at any moment. Six weeks since the steps. Six weeks, and still no more than a dozen words from that mouth, most of them numbers. Lior counted things. I recognized the habit the way you recognize your own handwriting.

The knock came again. Same rhythm. Rehearsed twice now.

Above me, through two floors of stone and timber, I heard Halvard's door open. No light, no call down the stairwell. Just the old man's tread, slow and deliberate, crossing his room to stand above the stairwell like a gargoyle deciding whether the cathedral was worth defending tonight.

I put out the lamp. This was instinct, and I will admit it was cowardly instinct — the logic being that whatever stood outside our door should find a dark house, a dead house, a house with no one worth knocking for. Then Lior sat up in the dark, eyes open and absolutely awake, and I understood that the child had been listening longer than I had. Had perhaps been waiting for this knock for six weeks, counting the nights toward it the way I counted the clock's hesitations.

Lior looked at me. In the dark I couldn't see the face properly, only the shape of attention, the absolute stillness of someone doing arithmetic with terror.

"Stay here," I whispered, which was absurd, because where would Lior go? But the words came out anyway, and what I mean by that — what I have decided it means, looking back — is that they were not instructions. They were vows. My first. I had gone twenty-two years without making one, on the principle that anything you promise can be repossessed.

I went up the stairs.

* * *

Halvard was already at the top, dressed fully — waistcoat, collar buttoned — as though midnight were simply an appointment he'd kept for forty years. He held no lamp. His hand rested on the great bolt of the observatory door the way mine rested on a balance wheel: with ownership.

"You heard it," he said. Not a question. Old men don't ask questions they can read off your face.

"Three knocks," I said. "Evenly spaced."

"Mm." He tilted his head, and I swear to God he listened to that knock the way he listened to the escapement — diagnosing it. "Soldier's knock. Regulation interval. They teach it to them before they teach them to shoot." He turned his head slightly. "You woke the boy?"

"He was awake."

"They're always awake," Halvard said, and there was something under the words, something worn smooth by handling, like a coin rubbed past its inscription. "That's what nobody tells you. You think they sleep through it. They don't sleep through anything. They just learn to lie still while it happens."

The knock came a third time. Harder. And then — this was new — a voice, pitched to carry through oak and iron:

"Herr Clockmaker! Open in the name of the garrison!"

Halvard and I looked at each other in the dark. The name of the garrison. Nobody says that phrase unless they are alone on a step at midnight with no witness and no authority, borrowing a building they do not own.

"That's not how the garrison knocks," I said quietly. "Marta says the requisition officers come at noon, in pairs, with paper."

"Marta," Halvard said, "says a great many useful things." He unbolted the door.

I want to record exactly what happened next, because for years afterward I misremembered it as drama, and it was not drama. It was craftsmanship.

The man on the step was maybe thirty, uniform correct but worn at the elbows, boots mended with wire — a soldier from late in the war, when the army had run out of everything except orders. He had a lantern and a face built for being believed: open, honest, faintly worried, the face of a man who has lost a niece and wants her back. If faces were clocks, his would have kept perfect time. That was precisely what made me distrust it. Nothing keeps perfect time. Not even us.

"Good evening," the man said. "Forgive the hour. I'm looking for my sister's boy." He produced this information the way a merchant produces credentials — smoothly, with a little bow of the head. "He was left — well. There was trouble. I've been searching since autumn. Someone said a child was seen near the observatory."

"Someone said," Halvard repeated pleasantly.

"A woman at the market. She wasn't certain."

"Women at the market are rarely certain. It's why their fish is cheap." Halvard leaned against the doorframe with the ease of a man discussing weather. "What is the child's name?"

The pause was very small. I measured it — I measure everything, it is both my trade and my affliction — and it was perhaps half a second, the length of time a man needs to remember which lie he chose. "Lior," the soldier said.

"Lior," Halvard agreed, tasting it. "And the family name?"

Another half-second. "You wouldn't know it. Foreign."

"Foreign names are the only ones I know lately." Halvard sighed, and the sigh was a masterpiece; I have tried to reproduce it since and cannot. It carried exhaustion, sympathy, and mild bureaucratic irritation in equal parts. "Herr Soldier, I will tell you what I tell everyone who comes up this hill at night. There was a child on my steps in October, yes. The whole town knows it. The priest knows it, the police know it, the garrison commander's adjutant drank my schnapps and asked me questions about it for an hour." He let that sit. "The child was taken into church custody within the day. Orphanage of Saint Aldric, south quarter. Papers and all. I keep clocks, not children. Children are inaccurate."

Every word of this was false, and every word of it was delivered with the flat certainty of a man reading a train schedule. I stood behind him in the dark of the doorway, out of the lantern's reach, holding my breath so completely that I began to see spots.

The soldier's eyes went past Halvard's shoulder. Just once. A flick, like a tongue checking a missing tooth. I stepped back into the deeper dark, heart performing some irregular percussion of its own.

"The orphanage," the soldier said slowly. "Saint Aldric's."

"I can write you the address. I believe they've moved some of the children east — the bombing, you understand — but the records—"

"No need." Something had shifted in the voice. The worry was still on the surface, but underneath it the water had stopped moving. "If the church has the boy, the church has the boy. I'll apply through channels."

"Through channels," Halvard agreed warmly. "That's the way. Everything through channels. It's slower, but the trains arrive eventually. Most of them."

They shook hands. I watched the soldier's lantern descend the hill, bobbing, unhurried, pausing once — I saw it clearly from the doorway, and I have seen it every night since behind my eyelids — pausing once to look back up at the tower. Not at the door. At the windows. Counting lights, I thought. Counting which rooms were lived in.

Halvard closed the door and shot the bolt, and then stood with his forehead nearly touching the wood, and for a long moment neither of us said anything. The lantern shrank down the hill and turned toward the harbor road, and went out of sight among the warehouses.

"Well," Halvard said finally. "Now we know."

"Now we know what?"

"Why the note said to forget the face." He turned and looked at me, and in the dimness his eyes were wet, though his voice was dry as old varnish. "Somebody wanted that boy erased, Voss. And somebody else has come to check whether the erasure took."

* * *

We told them in the kitchen, because Marta refused to be told things anywhere except over food — she held that bad news digested poorly on an empty stomach, and good news was wasted on one. So at one in the morning the four of us sat around the scrubbed table with bread and dripping and chicory coffee, and Halvard repeated his performance almost verbatim, and I watched the others the way I watch a movement going into beat.

Ines listened with her chin propped on her laced fingers, eyes half-shut, the posture she used for stars and for lies — she said you aimed the same scrutiny at both. When Halvard finished, she didn't comment on him at all. She said, "He paused before the family name."

Halvard blinked at her. "You heard that? You were upstairs."

"I hear everything. It's the widow's curse. You spend years listening for a second set of footsteps and afterward you can't stop." She took a sip of the chicory and grimaced at it with affection. "Twice he paused. Once before the name, once before the orphanage. A man remembering a rehearsed story pauses where the story was written down. A man telling the truth stumbles anywhere."

"So he's lying," Marta said, "and he's careful, and he's alone, and he came at night instead of with papers, which means he hasn't got papers." She said this while cutting more bread, the knife moving in long even strokes. Marta performed all her intelligence through domestic labor. You learned to read the kitchen like a telegraph office: fast chopping meant anger, slow stirring meant grief, and tonight the knife was going slow and deliberate, which I had never seen before, and it frightened me more than the knock had. "A man without papers doesn't go through channels. Channels require documents. Which means he'll come back the other way."

"The other way being?" I asked.

"Over the wall, through a window, or with friends," Marta said. She set the knife down. "We had a boarder in '15 who owed money to men like that. They always knock first. It's politeness, and it's reconnaissance. They want to see your face when you lie, so they know how much force to bring next time."

Nobody spoke. Lior sat at the table's end, too big for the chair, feet not reaching the floor, hands wrapped around a cup of warm milk Marta had pressed on them against all protest. The child hadn't touched it. Lior was looking at the middle of the table, at nothing, at some interior ledger of their own, and I watched those still shoulders and understood — the way you understand a mechanism suddenly, all at once, the whole train of wheels engaging — that Lior knew this man. Knew the knock. Had perhaps lain awake in some other house, some other year, listening to that same regulation triple blow, learning to lie still while it happened.

"Lior," I said softly.

Nothing. The eyes stayed fixed on the wood grain.

"Don't," Ines murmured, barely audible, and when I glanced at her she gave a tiny shake of the head. *Don't press.* She of all people knew about silence; she had lived inside one for two years since the astronomer died, and she guarded everyone else's like property.

It was Halvard who broke the quiet, and he broke it strangely. He pushed back his chair, went to the dresser where Marta kept the household accounts — a fat notebook bound in oilcloth, defended like a fortress — and set it on the table with a thump.

"Then we settle it now," he said. "Who lives in this house. Who eats from this kitchen. Who sleeps under this roof. Because tomorrow that list becomes ammunition, and I'll not have anyone on it who doesn't choose to be." He looked around the table, at each of us, last and longest at Lior. "I told that man downstairs there was no child here. I have been keeping time for fifty years, and that was the first lie I've told that mattered. I find—" his voice caught, just barely, a hesitation in the tick, "—I find I don't regret it. So. Are we agreed? Whatever comes up that hill, it comes to all of us, and none of us opens that door alone?"

Marta said, "Agreed," the way she said grace.

Ines said, "Agreed," the way she charted stars — quietly, as fact.

And I said, "Agreed," and discovered that the word cost more than any of my tools weighed. All my life I had kept myself light. Portable. Ready to leave any room, any town, any arrangement, carrying nothing that couldn't be packed in ten minutes. And here was an old man asking me to sign my name to a household in a war's final winter, to become weight, to be carried or to carry — and the terrible thing, the thing I set down here honestly, is that I didn't hesitate at all. Half a second, maybe. The length of a man remembering which lie he chose. Except I wasn't choosing a lie. For the first time in my life I was choosing true.

Lior lifted the cup at last and drank the cold milk to the bottom. It was, I understood, the closest thing to a signature the child could give.

* * *

We banked the fire and went up separately, Marta last, pausing at the foot of the stairs to check the bolts twice — front door, kitchen door, the little door to the coal chute that she informed me was "an invitation with hinges" and promised to deal with. I carried Lior's cot blanket up myself; the child walked beside me, silent, and at the workroom door stopped and looked at the scattered clock parts on the felt, the escape wheel, the pendulum spring, all the small honest pieces of a thing that only works by letting go and catching, letting go and catching.

Then Lior looked at me and pointed — not at the clock, not at the cot, but at the floor beneath the loose third floorboard where I had hidden the stolen ledger weeks ago, the ledger whose existence we had never once spoken of aloud.

The child pointed, and raised one finger to their lips.

I nodded. We understood each other perfectly, which frightened me more than the lantern on the harbor road had. Because I knew what Lior was telling me: *the man who knocked tonight is in these pages. I have seen his name. And if he comes back over the wall, through a window, or with friends — he isn't coming for me.*

He's coming for the book.

I lay down beside the cot and listened to the house settle, and below us, faint through the floors, I heard Marta moving in the kitchen — not cleaning, not banking the stove. Doing something deliberate at the dresser drawers, drawer by drawer, quiet as forgery.
