## 9. Escapement

I ran up the stairs empty-handed, and I want it noted — by whom, I no longer know, perhaps by the version of myself who kept accounts — that this was the first time in my recorded life I had gone anywhere without first weighing what to bring.

The clock room was cold. That was the wrongness of it, more than the silence. The master clock had a warmth, a body-heat of brass and oil and motion, the way any living thing at work has heat. You could stand near the case on a January morning and feel it breathing against your cheek. Now the case stood open-mouthed and still, and the air around it was just air.

I put my hand flat against the pendulum rod. Stone. I checked the weight train: wound full, all three barrels taut, every gram of driving force still sitting there in its drums like money in a locked drawer. The clock was not out of power. It was out of *permission*.

"Escapement," I said aloud, to nobody, because saying the word made it a diagnosis instead of a dread.

Here is what an escapement is, since you will ask, and since explaining things has always been easier for me than feeling them. A clock's weights want to fall. Gravity is patient and total; given its way, the whole train would spin down in one glorious uncontrolled instant and be done. What makes time out of that hunger is the escapement — a small toothed wheel and a pair of pallets that catch, release, catch, release, letting the train fall exactly one tooth at a time, sixty times a minute, forever. Every tick you have ever heard in your life is a small controlled surrender. A clock keeps time by letting go. Constantly. On purpose. It cannot hold on and run simultaneously. No mechanism can.

I knew this. I had known it since I was nine years old, since my father put a dead anchor escapement in my hands and said, *tell me why it stopped,* and I had said, *the pallets are worn,* and he had said, *wrong — it stopped because someone oiled it with fear. Too much oil. It drowned.* I had repeated the lesson to customers for eleven years without once applying it to anything with a pulse.

I lit the lamp and opened the case fully.

* * *

The failure, when I found it, was almost insultingly small. The escape wheel's fourth tooth had sheared — not worn, not bent, but snapped clean off, a flaw in the metal or a flaw in the century, hard to say. One missing tooth out of thirty, and the whole apparatus had simply refused. The pallets had dropped into the gap where the tooth should have been, found nothing to catch, and the entire train had locked itself against its own momentum like a man grabbing a doorframe rather than step into a dark room.

One tooth. Sixty years of quarters, and one tooth.

I sat down on the floor with my back against the case and did what I do, which is count.

Tools required: pin vise, broach, files three grades, the good loupe. Time required: eight hours minimum to fit a new tooth, and I did not have a blank of the right brass, which meant machining one from stock, which meant the lathe in Halvard's cellar, which meant asking Halvard for the cellar key, which meant conversation. Materials: brass, oil, silver steel for the pivot if the pivot had suffered, which it likely had, because when a wheel fails it never fails alone — failure travels through a mechanism the way bad news travels through a town, knocking on every door.

Cost, total: one day. Possibly two.

And then I stopped counting, because the counting had brought me somewhere I didn't want to arrive, which was here:

If I fixed the tooth today, the clock would run again today. And if the clock ran again, the chimes would resume, and the town would set its watches by us again, and everything would return to the schedule we had all been pretending still existed — Marta's bread rising to our bells, Ines's star-charts keyed to our hour-hand, Lior's meals arriving on time, the whole household ticking along inside the great regulated machine of before-the-armistice, waiting-for-the-armistice, counting-down-to-the-ship.

The ship. The *Meridian*. One berth. Sailing early, Ines thought, maybe much earlier than anyone promised.

I looked up into the open case, at the weight train sitting full and useless above me, all that stored force with nowhere lawful to go, and I recognized the arrangement. I had seen it in a mirror my whole life. Weights wound tight, holding everything, releasing nothing. A household locked against its own momentum because one part had failed and none of us would admit which part.

It was the ledger, of course. It was always going to be the ledger. Lior's stolen book of names lay hidden in the flour bin — Marta's idea, and a better one than mine, mine had been under a floorboard, everyone looks under floorboards — and every day that book sat there, the arithmetic got worse. One berth. Four people who might take it: Lior, who had earned it by suffering. Halvard, who had earned it by age. Marta, who had forged papers for half the coastline and deserved sainthood or prison, possibly both. Ines, who could read the war coming through a telescope better than the admiralty could.

Four claimants, one berth, and me holding the only tool that could decide: the numbers.

That was the job I had quietly assigned myself, I realized, sitting on the cold floor. Not clockmaker. Arbiter. I had been keeping a second ledger in my head — weight of grief versus weight of years versus weight of service — as if love were a commodity that could be weighed against other love, as if the whole question were a matter of getting the tare right. And the terrible thing, the thing I could feel in my chest like a hairline crack in a pivot, was that my arithmetic kept producing different answers depending on the hour. At dawn, Halvard should go; the man is seventy-one and his lungs whistle. By noon, Marta; she'd be hanged if the occupation ever audited her flour. By dusk, Lior, always Lior, because a child outranks everyone, doesn't he, isn't that the one law left standing —

But the numbers wouldn't close. There was no entry that balanced. I had been trying for two weeks to make four names fit one berth the way you fit a new tooth into an old wheel, filing away everything that doesn't match until something holds, and every night the sum came out wrong, and every morning I tried again with slightly different figures, and the trying was eating me alive from the inside the way rust eats a mainspring — slowly, invisibly, structurally.

* * *

Marta found me there at midday. She came up with bread and salt fish on a tray, took one look at the open case, and said, "Dead?"

"Sheared tooth. Fourth position."

"Can you mend it?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"A day. Two." I hesitated. "I haven't started."

She set the tray down on Halvard's workbench with the particular care she used when she had decided not to shout. "You've been up here since dawn."

"I've been thinking."

"You think with your hands, boy. I've watched you. When your hands stop, it means the thinking has gone somewhere you don't want it to go." She wiped flour on her apron and looked into the case with the frank appraisal of a woman who had fixed worse things than clocks with fewer tools. "So. Where did it go?"

I should have lied. I am, when necessary, a competent liar — it is one of the uses of a precise mind. But Marta had spent a year forging ration documents, which is to say she was a professional reader of lies, and lying to her would have been like bringing a candle to argue with the sun.

"There's one berth," I said. "On the ship. And five of us. And I keep trying to calculate—"

"Stop."

"—who weighs least. Who costs least. Who can be carried most cheaply, and—"

"*Stop.*" She said it quietly, which was worse. She crouched down so we were eye to eye, and her face had the expression of a woman looking at a mechanism she understands completely and does not like at all. "You know what my mother called people like you? *Godensboekhouders.* God's bookkeepers. People who believe somebody upstairs is keeping double-entry on suffering. That one lost more, so that one gets saved. It's a comfort, I'll grant you. It makes the world look like a shop with honest scales." She stood. "But nobody's keeping those books, August. There's just the ship and the water and us."

"And if someone must choose—"

"Then choosing wrong is survivable," she said, "and choosing *alone* is not," and she went downstairs, and left me alone with the clock and the bread I could not eat.

* * *

I worked through the afternoon. Of course I worked; grief and confusion are no excuse for leaving brass uncut, and besides, my hands needed to be doing something while the rest of me conducted its slow collapse.

I turned the blank on Halvard's lathe. I cut the tooth profile with a slitting file, checking it against the neighboring teeth every forty seconds, adjusting, rechecking. This is the craft entire, if you want it honestly: not brilliance, but the willingness to check the same thing nine hundred times without going mad. By lamplight the new tooth emerged — a little proud, a little soft at the root, needing another pass, then another.

And all the while the allegory worked on me like water working on limestone, patient, finding every crack.

Because here is the thing about fitting a tooth into an escape wheel. You cannot make it strong enough to hold. Strength is not the requirement. If you make the tooth too robust, too eager, too *certain*, the pallets cannot release it cleanly, and the clock either stops or runs wild — races, actually, runs fast and mad, all that held-back time pouring out at once. The tooth must be made deliberately, precisely *weak*. Weak enough to let go. Its whole virtue is its willingness to be caught and released, caught and released, ten thousand times, surrendering every few seconds and thereby keeping perfect time.

A clock does not keep time by holding on. It keeps time by the quality of its letting-go.

I set the file down.

I thought about my father, dead these six years, whom I had loved in the only grammar I possessed — inventory. I had catalogued his funeral the way I catalogue everything: fourteen mourners, one wreath, the coat I inherited, the tools I inherited, the debts. I had carried him out of the world by listing him. And I had told myself for six years that this was discipline, this was competence, this was how a careful man survives — carry little, weigh everything, release nothing that isn't finished with.

Release nothing.

I had released nothing. Not him. Not the war. Not the possibility of loss. I walked through these months holding every outcome at once, gripping the future so tightly it couldn't move, and calling the grip prudence.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, Marta pulled loaves from the oven, and the smell climbed the stairwell, and Lior laughed at something — actually laughed, a small rusty sound like a hinge remembering its purpose — and the sound went through me sideways.

Some things must be let go to function.

Not forgotten. That was the distinction, and I gripped it like the edge of the workbench. The escapement releases the wheel; it does not abandon it. Release is not erasure. The note on the observatory steps had commanded the world to forget a face, and I understood now, with the new tooth cooling in my palm, that forgetting was never the opposite of carrying. Letting go — real letting go — meant trusting the thing you released to come back to you on the next beat. Tick. Caught. Released. Tock. Caught again. A rhythm, not a possession. You don't hold the person. You hold the *return*.

I could not decide who deserved the berth. Fine. Perhaps deciding was the failure, not the answer. Perhaps the whole enterprise — one arbiter, one scale, one name chosen — was the sheared tooth, the single broken part locking the entire train. Halvard was already arranging something behind closed doors, I'd seen the letters. Marta had refused my arithmetic to my face. Even Ines, even the sky itself, refused to promise the armistice would come on schedule. Everyone in this house had already begun to let go of the old logic except me.

I picked up the file and finished the tooth properly — weak at the root in exactly the right measure, strong everywhere it mattered — and I fitted it to the wheel, and staked the pivot true, and oiled the pallets with three drops, no more; my father's voice in my ear, *not fear, boy, fear drowns them.*

Then I sat back on my heels and did not wind it. Not yet.

Because winding it meant restarting the town's time, and the town's time was the ship's schedule, and the ship's schedule was the choice, and I was not done being afraid yet. I knew that. I could admit it now, at least in the privacy of the clock room: I wanted to leave the mechanism stopped, because a stopped clock makes no demands. As long as the tower stood silent, the decision stayed theoretical, suspended, wound-tight, safe.

Safe, and drowning in its own oil.

I heard footsteps on the stairs then — Halvard's uneven tread, slower than it had been even a month ago — and beneath it, lighter, quicker, the child's. They came up together. Halvard carried a lantern and a folded paper, and his face had the look of a man who has decided something enormous and intends to disguise it as housekeeping.

"The clock?" he asked.

"Mended," I said. "Unwound."

He raised an eyebrow. Behind him, Lior peered past his coat into the open case, eyes wide at the hanging weights, the brass moon of the wheel.

"Unwound," Halvard repeated. "In wartime. With the town setting its bread and its curfew by us." He set the lantern down. "There's a reason I sent for you in November, Voss, and it wasn't the tooth."

He unfolded the paper. And I saw, before he even turned it toward me, the admiralty watermark — and beyond his shoulder, Lior had gone very still, staring not at the clock but at the folded page in the old man's hand, as though recognizing it from a nightmare.
