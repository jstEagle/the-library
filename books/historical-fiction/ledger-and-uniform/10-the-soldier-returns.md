## 10. The Soldier Returns

I had not spoken in forty-one days.

I know the number because I kept it the way other people keep coins — pressed flat, counted over, hidden somewhere no one would think to look. Forty-one days of opening my mouth and finding it full of stones. Not real stones. Worse ones. The kind that look like words from the outside but weigh like a collapsed house when you try to lift them out.

The people at the observatory did not seem to mind. That was the first strange thing about them. In the places I had been before — and there had been four, if you counted the barn as a place, which my mother had, and the cellar, which she hadn't lived long enough to count — silence was treated like a debt. People stared at you until you paid it. Here, they simply worked around me, the way water works around a rock in a stream. Marta set bread in front of me without demanding thanks. August explained clock parts to me in a low voice whether or not I answered, as though talking were a thing he did for his own benefit and I happened to be furniture with good hearing. Halvard pretended I wasn't there at all, which I understood perfectly, because pretending is its own language and I am fluent.

And Ines looked at the sky and told me things about it, quietly, as though the sky and I were colleagues.

So the days passed. I swept steps. I learned that the fourth stair from the bottom creaks on the left side only. I learned that Marta hums when she thinks no one is listening and stops the instant anyone is. I learned that August counts everything — spoons, screws, minutes, the exact weight of his tool roll — and that this counting is not madness but armor, and I recognized it immediately because I do the same thing with days.

Forty-two. Forty-three.

Then the soldier came back to town, and the counting stopped mattering, because some numbers are too small to hold what happens next.

* * *

It began, as most terrible things do, with an ordinary sound. Boots. Not one pair — several — and the particular rhythm of men who have been given permission to be loud. I was in the kitchen grinding coffee, which is a job Marta gave me partly to be kind and partly because I do it better than anyone, having arms built like wire and nothing else to do with them.

"Stay here," Marta said, and went to the window, and I watched her back change shape. I have spent my whole life reading backs. A back will tell you what a face has been trained to hide. Marta's back said: *not again.*

The knocking at doors started two streets over. You could hear it move toward us like weather. Knock, voices, knock, voices. Somewhere a dog lost its argument with a boot. Somewhere a woman was crying in the specific way people cry when they are trying to cry quietly enough to protect someone.

Marta came back from the window and put her hands on my shoulders, and her hands were floury and strong and they said what her mouth didn't: *you are a sack of potatoes now. Potatoes have no faces.*

I nodded. I am excellent at being a sack of potatoes. It is one of perhaps three skills the war has given me, and the other two are knowing how many stairs are in any building, and hating precisely.

But then August came down the tower stairs two at a time — he does that when he's frightened, which he'd deny under oath — and said, "They're asking about a child," and the stones in my mouth all shifted at once.

Not about a ledger. About a child.

You would think those are the same question. They are not. A ledger can be burned. A child, apparently, must be found.

* * *

I should explain what I knew, so you understand why my hands went cold before my head caught up.

The man who came to the observatory at midnight — the one Halvard turned away with that magnificent lie about a fevered apprentice — I had seen him before. Before the observatory. Before the note. Before forty-one days of silence.

He had not always worn a uniform well. That was my first noticing, even at seven years old, even in the dark of the harbor office where my mother held my hand too tightly: the uniform fit him the way a lie fits a coward, all shoulders and no substance. He stood at the manifest desk with a paper in his hand and a story in his mouth, and the story was that he was family. Uncle. Guardian. The words changed depending on who he was talking to, but the paper never changed, and the paper had our name on it — or a name close enough to ours that the clerk squinted and shrugged, because it was the last week, and the last berth, and everyone was tired of checking.

My mother had the berth. She had traded three winters of mending and one entire summer of silence for it. One berth, one child, and she had spent every night of October telling me about the ship — the *Meridian*, she said it like a prayer with a hull — how I would go first and she would follow on the second sailing, and how the sea would smell different from anything I knew, and how I was to hold the ledger flat against my chest and let no one see it, and how forgetting was a kindness we would owe each other.

She said: *if anyone asks, forget my face.*

She meant it the way mothers mean things — as armor, as inheritance, as the last thing she could build with her hands. And then the uncle — the word tastes like a nail — produced his paper, and the clerk was tired, and the harbor was cold, and my mother argued with a voice like iron bending, and it did not matter. Iron bends. Paper wins. The berth went into his pocket, and my mother's hand went slack in mine, and the last thing she did was crouch down and press the ledger into my coat and say the words again, softer, the way you'd say them to a child you were about to leave in a forest:

*Forget my face. Forget it, little star. Forgetting is how you survive me.*

I did not forget her face. I want that written down somewhere permanent. I forgot nothing. I carried everything, which is what ledgers are for, and which is why I ran, and which is why I ended up on the steps of a tower full of clocks at dawn with a note pinned to my coat instructing the world to discard me.

Notes can be ignored. I was betting my life on it.

* * *

Through the kitchen window, past the gap in the shutters that Marta thought nobody knew about, I watched him work the street.

That is the word. Work. He moved down the row of houses the way August moves down a disassembled clock — methodical, unhurried, certain that every piece will yield if you apply the right pressure. Two soldiers behind him doing the knocking; him standing slightly back, hands clasped, wearing the patient face of a man doing tedious but necessary paperwork. His cap was new. His boots were not. Soldiers' boots tell the truth about how long they've been walking, and his had walked through mud I recognized.

He stopped at the baker's. The baker's wife came out, shook her head, wrung her apron. He smiled at her. I remembered that smile. It was the smile he'd used on the clerk — warm as a lamp, empty as one too, light without heat, designed to make you feel attended to while he took something.

He came one door closer.

Marta had gone to stand with Halvard at the observatory door, because that is what you do — you meet them on your own ground, in your own doorway, with your own people visible behind you. I heard fragments through the shutter gap. Halvard's voice, dry as old varnish: "A child? We repair clocks here, not families." Marta's voice, thick with the accent she usually sands smooth for strangers: "No children. Only mouths we feed, same as every winter." And underneath both, the soldier's voice, reasonable, regretful, a voice that apologized the way a flood apologizes.

"I understand. Difficult times. But the boy may be in danger. Family obligation — you understand, I'm sure. If you see him." A pause. "There's a reward for information. And consequences for withholding it."

Consequences. The war's favorite word. It does so much work while saying so little.

Halvard said something I couldn't hear, low and flat, and the soldier laughed — a small laugh, a bookkeeping laugh — and then the boots moved on, one door, two doors, toward the corner, and I breathed for the first time in a hundred years.

Except he didn't turn the corner.

He stopped. At the foot of the observatory steps. Where I had been found. He stood there looking up at the tower the way a man looks at a safe he intends to open eventually, and he took off his cap, and he scratched his head, and he said something to Halvard and Marta that made Marta's shoulders go rigid as a mast.

Then he turned, deliberately, slowly, and looked directly at the kitchen window.

At the gap in the shutters.

At me.

* * *

Here is what I know about terror, having studied it longer than most children get to study arithmetic: it is quiet. Everyone expects screaming. Nobody warns you that the loudest part of fear is the silence it installs in you, floor by floor, like a landlord renovating your body for a tenant who never arrives. My heart was making more noise than a bombardment and my throat was a locked door in an empty house.

He looked at the window for three seconds. Maybe four. Long enough for me to count them, because counting is what I do instead of dying.

Then he replaced his cap, inclined his head to Halvard — courteous, almost tender — and walked away around the corner, and the street exhaled, and Marta came inside fast and bolted the door and stood with her back against it like a drawn bolt herself.

"He saw me," I tried to say.

What came out was air. A shape of air. The stones had all slid down against the lock at once.

August appeared in the doorway, pale, tool roll still in his hand as though he'd come prepared to fix the situation with a screwdriver. His eyes went from Marta to the window to me, and I watched him do his arithmetic — I could nearly hear the abacus clicking behind his face — and arrive at the answer I was trying to give.

"He saw you," August said quietly. "Through the shutter."

I nodded. Nodding costs nothing. Nodding is the currency of the speechless.

"He'll be back," August said, and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't panic either. It was a measurement. He crossed the kitchen and knelt down in front of me, which nobody had done yet, nobody in all forty-three days, and he said, in the careful voice he uses on clockwork he respects: "Lior. I need to know what he wants. Not what he says he wants — what he wants. Can you tell me?"

And the thing is — the awful, unlocking thing — is that I could. I had been able to for weeks. The story sat in me fully formed, complete with dates and names and the exact gray of the harbor office wall, waiting for a door. Every day the silence got heavier not because I had less to say but because I had more, and more, and more, a ledger of my own with no page large enough.

But saying it meant giving away my mother's face. Saying it meant the forgetting failed. She had spent her last coin buying my silence and here was a man kneeling on a kitchen floor offering to spend it for me.

Then, through the shutter gap, faint but unmistakable, I heard the boots start again. Coming back. Not strolling this time. Coming back at the pace of a man who has finished his paperwork and decided on his method.

August heard it too. He turned his head toward the door, and I watched the fear move through him and get filed somewhere, categorized, weighed, put in its place — and I understood suddenly that this was what courage looked like in person: not the absence of the stones, but a man picking them up anyway.

"They're coming back," Marta said from the door. "Halvard's sending them off again, but they're coming back."

"Lior." August's hand was on my shoulder now, steady as a pendulum. "Whatever you decide to say or not say — it changes nothing here. Do you understand? You are already entered. You are already kept."

*Entered. Already kept.* Words from a boy who talks like a ledger, and they went into me deeper than any I'd ever been given, deeper than *forget my face*, because they pointed the opposite direction entirely.

The boots reached the bottom step.

And the stones in my throat — all forty-three days of them, the whole collapsed house of them — shifted, and cracked, and one of them broke clean through the lock, and what came out of me was not a scream and not a whisper but something between, ragged and rusted and absolutely unmistakable, my first sentence since the harbor:

"That's him. That's the man who took her berth."

August's grip tightened. Marta turned from the door. And outside, the knocking began — not at a neighbor's house this time, but at ours.
