## 11. Testimony in Small Hours

The knocking went on for a long time. That was the strange part — not its violence but its patience. Three raps, a pause, three raps again, as though whoever stood below believed that houses were honest things and doors opened eventually if you only asked politely enough.

We did not open it. We stood in the kitchen like furniture that had learned to breathe: Marta with a carving knife she had taken up without appearing to move, Halvard with his hand flat against the doorframe as if he could feel the house's pulse through the wood, Ines at the window with her face turned just enough to watch without being seen. And Lior beside me, small and rigid, having said those seven words and then closed again like a shop shutter.

The knocking stopped at last. Boots scraped on stone, receded down the observatory steps, and were swallowed by the street.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. Then Marta set the knife down with a click and said, "Right," in the voice she used for announcing soup, and put the kettle on, because that is what Marta did instead of collapsing.

But I was looking at Lior.

*That's him. That's the man who took her berth.*

A sentence. A whole sentence, from a child who had not spoken since arriving on our steps forty-three days ago — I knew the count precisely, because I keep counts, because counting is how I keep the world from sliding. Forty-three days of silence, broken by one sentence about a stolen berth, spoken in the voice of someone much older than eight or nine or whatever age Lior was. We didn't know that either. The note had said *forget the face*, and faces don't come with birthdates attached.

"Tea first," Marta said, which meant *everything else after tea*, which meant *I am frightened too and refuse to say so*.

Halvard lowered himself onto the bench by the stove with the sound of an old clock case settling. He looked at Lior for a long moment — the old man had been looking at Lior that way for weeks, pretending he wasn't, the way he pretended indifference to everything he loved — and then he looked at me.

"He'll be back," Halvard said. "Men like that knock twice. First polite, then otherwise. Before the *otherwise* comes, boy—" he nodded at Lior, then at me— "we'd best know what we're standing between."

Lior looked at the table. At the tea Marta set down, steam climbing like something trying to escape. At my hands, folded, because I did not know where else to put them.

Then Lior began, very quietly, to talk.

* * *

It came out the way water comes through a cracked wall — slowly at first, in threads, then all at once when the pressure grew too great.

"There was a ship," Lior said. "The *Meridian*. It goes from the coast. My mother had a paper. A real one. She traded her mother's ring for it, and half the winter's coal money, and she said it was the last berth, the actual last one, and we would go together." Lior's hands were around the teacup, not drinking, just holding, the way you hold something when letting go is unthinkable. "She said the sea is loud but it isn't angry."

Marta made a small sound and turned back to the stove, giving us her broad back.

"A man came to the boarding house. He wore a uniform but he wasn't — he wasn't anyone's soldier really, he was just wearing it, there are so many uniforms now that don't mean anything except that they can take things. He said he was checking papers. My mother gave him hers and he held it a long time and read it and read it, and then he said the name on it wasn't right. But it *was* right. It was her name. He just said it wasn't." Lior's voice stayed level, which was somehow worse than crying. "He kept the paper. He said she could collect it at the harbor office. She went every day for six days. On the seventh day the ship sailed and the harbor office said there was no record of any berth under that name."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. In the tower above us, the master clock I had spent weeks coaxing back to life measured out its patient seconds, indifferent as God.

"She cried once," Lior said. "Only once, at night, where she thought I was asleep. Then she got practical. Practical people survive, she always said. She found another way — overland, with a smuggler who took payment in bread. But the roads were watched and there was a checkpoint, and the man at the checkpoint—" Lior finally looked up, and the eyes in that thin face were the oldest thing in the room, older than Halvard's grief, older than the tower itself— "was the same man. The uniform man. He recognized her. He smiled. He said, *you again.*"

I felt my own face do something I had never felt it do before. Later I would try to catalog it — I catalog everything, it is how I am built — and fail. Fury does not fit in ledgers. It has no column. It will not balance.

"They took everyone off the road," Lior went on. "Into a yard. There was a fence and wire. My mother told me to crawl through the gap at the bottom, the gap behind the drainage grate, and to keep crawling until I reached the town, and to find the tall building with the clock, because a woman at the market had said — she'd heard — that the clock people helped people sometimes. And she pressed this into my hand." Lior touched the front of their shirt, where the ledger lived beneath, sewn into the lining — we had all known it was there and none of us had asked. "And she wrote the note that morning. On the back of a ration card. She wrote it fast."

"What did it say?" I asked, though I had read it a hundred times. *Forget the face. Forget where he came from. If you love him, forget him completely.* I had thought it was a stranger's cruelty, some bureaucratic instruction for erasure. I had hated its author for six weeks.

"It said forget," Lior said. "Because forgetting keeps you safe. If nobody remembers me, nobody can follow me to her. If the family that takes me doesn't know anything, they can't tell anything." A pause. "She wasn't telling them to throw me away. She was teaching them how to hide me. She used to play that game with me since I was small — *what's your name today?* — so I'd know how. So I'd be good at it." The cup trembled now, a fine rattle against the saucer. "I was supposed to be good at it. I'm sorry. I was supposed to forget you too."

The silence after that was the kind that has weight, that presses on the eardrums like deep water. Marta had stopped even pretending to work. Halvard's hand lay open on the table, palm up, an old man's unconscious offering.

"And the ledger?" I asked gently. "How does a child come to carry a book of names?"

"The yard," Lior said. "After I crawled out. I went back — at night, two nights later, I crawled back along the fence. Because I thought she might still be in the yard, and also because—" the child's jaw worked— "because I wanted to bring something of hers. Anything. And there was a shed by the yard where the uniform men kept their papers, boxes of them, names of everyone they'd turned away or taken or sold passage to and then un-sold it. They sell berths twice sometimes. Three times. To different families who each think they're sailing." Lior pulled the ledger out from the shirt lining then, and set it on the table between us, that grubby, swollen, ordinary notebook that had sat in our house for six weeks like an unexploded shell. "This was on top of the box nearest the fence. I grabbed it because it was closest. I couldn't read most of it then. I can now."

I opened it. I had looked through it before, of course — columned names, ports, dates, sums of money, and in the margin of certain entries a small mark like a tick or a cross-out. I had assumed smuggling records, black-market freight, the usual wartime accounting of human cargo. I had read it the way I read everything: as arithmetic.

Now I read it as testimony.

Because I saw it — the entry, third page from the end. A woman's name. A port. A sum paid. And in the margin, in a different ink, a second sum, and a second name, and a notation in a clerk's cramped hand: *resold.* The same berth, sold twice. Sold to Lior's mother, and then sold again to someone whose name I did not know and who had perhaps sailed away believing honestly in their own paper while a woman crawled through wire.

And beside that entry, in the same cramped hand, initials I recognized. Because the man who had knocked at our door — polite, patient, *three raps and a pause* — had left a calling card on his first visit, had written his name and regiment in Halvard's visitor book in handwriting that slanted the exact same way.

"He's in here," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, distant, as if someone else were speaking through my mouth. "His initials. His annotations. He kept the records himself — he was the clerk, or he worked for the clerk. This book proves what he did. Not just to your mother. To dozens of them. Resold berths, confiscated papers, names struck through and passed to—" I stopped, because the next column heading, when I understood it, was worse than theft. Names handed over. Delivered. The word *delivered* appears eleven times in that ledger, and I have counted, and I will count it every night for the rest of my life.

"That's why he wants it," Ines said quietly, from the window. She had been listening with her whole body; astronomers listen that way. "Not for the child. The child is nothing to him. The book is a confession he lost track of."

"He thinks I died in the yard," Lior said simply. "Or the town. Everyone in it. When the note didn't come back and I didn't come back, he started looking anyway — not for me. For the book. Because a book walks. Books get found. Books get *read*." The child looked at me then with terrible directness. "You read it. You've had it for weeks. Why hasn't he found it?"

"Because we sewed it into your shirt and told no one," Marta said, "and because this house has been hiding people longer than you've been alive, little one, and we're practiced."

Lior blinked at that. So, honestly, did I.

* * *

Later — the candles burned low, Halvard had gone up to the clock, Ines to her charts, and Marta had finally, forcibly, put Lior to bed in the alcove off the kitchen, wrapped in blankets that smelled of bread — later I sat alone at the table with the ledger open in front of me and tried to do the thing I always do, which is to put everything in its column.

It would not go in.

I want to explain what that is like, for someone like me. All my life I have survived by carrying little. Tools weighed and chosen. Griefs inventoried and shelved. When my father died I made a list of everything he had owned and it came to ninety-one items and I balanced the list and I slept. When the war took the boys from my street I entered their names in my own private book with dates, and the dates made it bearable, because a thing with a date is a thing that ended, and a thing that ended can be filed. This is not coldness. Or — it is, perhaps, but it is the coldness that keeps a body warm enough to go on. A ledger for a heart. I have been called that. I have answered to it.

But sitting there with that child's testimony settling over the table like ash, I felt something rise in me that refused every column I offered it. It had no date. It would not end. It was not grief, exactly, and not fear, though both were in it. It was larger and hotter and it oriented entirely outward, away from myself, toward a man in a borrowed uniform with patient knuckles — fury, real fury, the protective kind, the kind that has an object and intends to reach it.

I thought: *he knocked politely because he believes politeness works on people like us. Clockmakers, cooks, widows. Careful people. People who weigh things.*

He had miscounted. He had done his sums on the wrong household.

Because here is what I understood in that small hour, with the candle guttering and the master clock overhead keeping its repaired, faithful time: he does not merely want a book. He wants the past to have gone differently. He wants the yard and the resold berth and the woman who crawled through wire to become things that never quite happened — rumors, rounding errors. And there is only one place where the truth of it still lives outside that child's memory, and the child had carried it straight to us, sewn against a heartbeat, trusting strangers because a dying market rumor said the clock people help.

We help. Apparently we help. Marta had known it for a year before I did.

I took out my pen — my good pen, the one Halvard had given me when I finished the escapement work — and I did not write in the ledger. Instead I wrote on a scrap of paper, for myself, a single line:

*Some things cannot be carried. They must be defended.*

Then I heard footsteps above me — quick ones, Ines's, crossing the tower floor — and her voice, low and urgent, calling down through the stairwell for Halvard, saying words that dropped through the house like stones into still water:

"The harbor master's list is posted. The *Meridian* moves up its sailing. It leaves in three days — armistice or no armistice."
