## 12. The Meridian's Manifest

My husband used to say that the sea kept better records than any government, because the sea never had an interest in lying to itself. Tomas Iversen, lieutenant of the coastal survey, dead four years now and still the most useful man in my household, on account of a tin box buried under the floorboards of his old study and the names written inside it.

I should explain about the tin box before I go further, because everything that follows depends on it.

When the navy decommissioned the survey office and burned most of its correspondence — as governments do when the truth becomes inconvenient rather than merely embarrassing — Tomas brought home a single file. He called it his pension. "Not for me," he said, tapping his temple with one finger, "for whoever's clever enough to need it." Inside were manifests, harbor schedules, the private telephone numbers of three quartermasters who owed him money or favors or both, and a letter of introduction addressed to no one in particular, which was the genius of it. *To the bearer: the hand that holds this is trusted.* Signed by a captain who had since been promoted high enough that admitting he'd ever written such a thing would cost him more than honoring it would.

I had kept the box for four years without opening it. Grief makes an excellent lockbox; you cannot bring yourself to touch what he touched, so nothing inside ever wears out.

But grief, I have learned this winter, is also a coward. It hides while other people's children starve on your doorstep.

* * *

It began, as most of my worst decisions do, with Marta banging around the kitchen at an hour no decent woman should be awake.

"You're up," she said, not looking up from a pot she was stirring at half past five in the morning, which told me the pot contained something she didn't want identified. "Good. Sit down. I need someone to lie to."

"I beg your pardon?"

"The boy ate. That's all you need to know. If Halvard asks, there were exactly four potatoes in this pot last night and there are exactly four potatoes' worth of stew now, and arithmetic is arithmetic." She stirred harder. "Ines. The ship. The Meridian. Has anyone actually checked whether it exists?"

The question landed in my chest like a dropped wrench. I sat down because my knees suggested it.

"We have a ledger," I said. "We have a berth. We have—"

"We have a child's handwriting in a stolen book," Marta said, "and a date, and a port, and a great deal of hope, which in my experience spoils faster than fish. Somebody has to find out if that ship is real, if that berth is real, and whether anyone official will honor either." She finally looked at me, and her face did something complicated. "You're the only one of us who knew how to talk to officers. And you're the only one who isn't afraid of them anymore, because what are they going to take from you?"

I thought about answering that honestly — they could take Lior, they could take the household, they could take the fragile new thing we had become without ever naming it — but Marta had already turned back to her pot, and Marta's pots end conversations the way judges end trials.

So I went upstairs to Tomas's study, knelt on the cold boards like a widow in a painting, and pried up the loose plank by the window.

* * *

The letter of introduction smelled of cedar and gun oil and, faintly, of him. I sat at his desk — his chair still fit me badly, he'd bought it for a taller man's spine — and read the captain's name four times before I could make my hand copy it onto fresh paper. Rear-Commander Aldous Venn. Port authority. A man I had met twice at naval dinners, where he'd complimented my husband's charts and my collarbones in that order, and I had smiled at him the way women of that era were trained to smile: as though the compliment were weather, unavoidable and beneath comment.

I wrote to him as a widow seeking passage. It was even mostly true. *A member of my household requires berthing aboard the relief vessel Meridian, departing on or about the twentieth. I ask only confirmation that the berth exists and will be honored.* I signed my married name, because a dead officer's widow could ask questions that a living woman couldn't, and I sealed it with Tomas's wax and his signet, which had spent four years in the same drawer as his dress gloves, both waiting.

Then I did the hardest part. I waited.

Waiting, in that house, meant watching August pretend not to watch everyone else. The boy — and I maintain he is a boy, whatever his careful hands claim, twenty-two years old and built entirely out of ledgers — had taken to following Lior through the observatory with the expression of a man auditing his own heart. He'd fixed the master clock twice more that week. Each time, the clock ran beautifully for eleven hours and then lost four minutes, as though it too were counting down to something and resented being asked to be cheerful about it.

"Your clock is dying," I told him on the third afternoon, while Lior napped in the chart room and Halvard pretended to catalog lenses in the next room, listening to every word.

"It's not dying. It's hesitating." August wound the escapement with a fingertip, delicate as a surgeon. "There's a tooth on the escape wheel that's worn past tolerance. Every rotation, it catches, releases late, loses time. You can compensate for years. But eventually—" He stopped.

"Eventually you can't compensate anymore," I finished. "You have to replace the tooth, or replace the wheel, or admit the clock needs parts it doesn't have."

He looked at me then, really looked, the way people look when they realize a conversation has been about something else the entire time. "Ines," he said carefully, "what do you know?"

"I know," I said, "that I've sent a letter to a rear-commander asking whether our salvation has a schedule, and I'll know more within the week. Until then, keep compensating."

* * *

Venn's reply came in six days, which in wartime postal terms was practically telepathy. I took it up to Tomas's study to open alone, because some doors you don't want witnesses at, in case what's behind them is worse than what's in front.

His handwriting hadn't improved. *Dear Madame Iversen,* it began, and then two paragraphs of condolence so formulaic they could have been printed, followed by the paragraph I'd actually paid for:

*Regarding your inquiry: the Meridian is real, contracted under coastal relief auspices, scheduled to depart Harbor Quay Three on the night of the twenty-second, armistice or no armistice. I will be frank, madame, as I owe your husband that much. There is pressure from above to move the sailing forward. The naval escort allocated to her may be reassigned within days, and no master worth his salt sails a relief ship unescorted into those waters. If the escort goes, she goes early — possibly the eighteenth, possibly sooner, and without public notice. Manifests are being amended daily. Berths confirmed by the relief committee are being honored, but confirmation must be presented at the quay, in person, before the amended manifest closes. After that, madame, even friendship cannot conjure deck space.*

*I am sorry to add haste to grief. Your servant, A. Venn.*

I read it three times. Then I did what any sensible widow does with catastrophic news. I made tea, drank none of it, and let it go cold while I stared at the wall where Tomas's charts used to hang.

The eighteenth. Possibly sooner. We were living inside a clock whose hands someone else was moving.

* * *

That evening I gathered them in the kitchen, because kitchens are where households tell the truth, and because Marta refused to hold serious conversations anywhere without access to bread.

Halvard sat with his hands flat on the table, the way he did when he was listening with his whole body. August stood by the stove even though the stove wasn't lit, holding himself the way he held tools — precisely, usefully, ready to be picked up and put to work. Lior sat between Marta and the wall, close enough to the wall to feel its loyalty. The child still barely spoke, but watched everything, cataloging us in that small fierce way children have, deciding nightly whether we were safe.

I laid Venn's letter on the table.

"The Meridian is real," I said. "The berth is real. And the ship may sail four days early, without notice, and our berth dies the moment the manifest closes at the quay."

Silence. Then Halvard said, quietly, "How many days?"

"If the escort stays, ten. If it goes — six. Perhaps fewer."

August took the letter and read it the way he read everything, twice, hunting for errors, as if the war might have made an arithmetic mistake he could correct. "Confirmation has to be presented in person," he said. "At the quay. Before the manifest closes."

"Yes."

"So someone has to be standing at Quay Three, holding proof of a berth, on a night we can't predict, in a town under curfew, with a soldier asking questions about that child." He set the letter down. His voice stayed level, which is how I knew how much it cost him. "That's not a plan. That's a wager with moving odds."

"That," said Marta, cutting bread with unnecessary force, "is what passes for good news in this house."

And Lior — small, silent Lior, who had given us perhaps forty words in six weeks — looked at the letter, and then at each of us in turn, and said, in a voice like something long rusted beginning to turn:

"They changed the date. They always change the date. That's how they took Mama's."

The kitchen went very still.

Halvard reached across the table and covered the child's hand with his own, those enormous cracked clockmaker's hands, gentle as falling snow. "Not this time," he said. "This time we know first."

But I watched August's face while Halvard said it, and I saw the ledger behind his eyes doing terrible arithmetic — six days, one berth, four of us, curfew, a soldier, a child who had already lost this game once — and I understood, sitting in my dead husband's kitchen with his enemies' letter cooling on the table, that the clock running down was not the one in the tower.

It was the one in all of us. And somebody in this house had already decided, without telling the rest of us, which hand they intended to sacrifice.

I just didn't yet know it was Halvard.
