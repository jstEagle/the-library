## 13. Halvard's Offer

I have buried a wife, a son, and three apprentices to this war — the last one only in the sense that he wrote from Zurich and did not write again — so I will tell you plainly: I know how to survive a loss. You wind the clocks. You eat your bread. You do not say the names out loud after the first year, because the mouth is a door and grief is a thing that comes through doors left open.

What I do not know how to survive is hope. That is a different animal entirely, and it has gotten into my tower like a mouse into a grain sack.

* * *

The boy sleeps in the room off the clock room now. We call it the storeroom because calling it what it is would require conversations I am not equipped to have. There is a cot there that belonged to my son, Emil, who would be thirty-one this winter if the sea had been kinder in '14, and there are blankets that Marta boiled clean, and there is a boy in them who does not speak except when the nightmares pull the words out of him like teeth.

I check on him at night. I tell myself this is security — a tower full of contraband ledgers and forged ration papers wants a watchman — but I am an honest man with my hands and a liar everywhere else, so let us not pretend. I check on him the way I used to check on Emil. I stand in the doorway and count his breaths. Seven to the minute when he sleeps deeply, which is a rate no healthy child should sleep at, as though even unconscious he is trying to take up less of the world than he occupies.

Last night he had the ledger under his pillow. The stolen one, the one with all those names in it like a congregation. He clutches it the way other children clutch a mother's hem. August found it weeks ago and we have all been pretending we didn't read it, which in my case was a lie, because I read it twice.

That is where the trouble started. Reading it twice.

* * *

Here is what I know about berths on relief ships: there are never enough, and the people who allocate them have learned to think of human beings the way I think of gear trains — interchangeable, weight-bearing, replaceable. I spent forty years refusing to think that way about clock parts; I will not start thinking that way about children.

The *Meridian* sails before the armistice. Ines confirmed it through her late husband's naval contacts, which she produces from her widowhood the way a conjuror produces coins — always one more than you expected. Sails before the armistice, sails possibly early, carries perhaps two hundred souls out of a town holding four thousand, and one of those berths has Lior's name attached to it in a hand I now recognize as the boy's mother's, written in a ledger that a soldier wants back badly enough to come to our door at midnight wearing his uncle-hood like a borrowed coat.

August thinks the uncle wants the ledger to erase himself. August is probably right. August is twenty-three and reads people the way I once did, quickly and with terrible accuracy. But August also still believes that being right is a strategy. I remember believing that. It was a comfortable decade or so before life corrected me.

So I made my decision the way I make most decisions: alone, at night, with my hands busy.

* * *

There is a man named Oskar Brandt who runs the harbor office, and forty years ago he owed me money for a regulator I built him and never paid for, and I have held that debt the way banks hold grudges — patiently, at interest. Brandt is not a bad man. He is a tired man with a rubber stamp and a queue of desperate faces outside his window, and tired men with stamps can be moved by old debts and older friendships.

I went down to the harbor on a Tuesday, in the rain, carrying a pocket watch I told myself I was delivering for repair. Brandt saw through that inside of ten seconds, because Brandt has known me since we were boys stealing apples off the consul's tree, and you cannot lie to a man who watched you fall out of that same tree.

"Halvard," he said. "You want something."

"I want to ask about the *Meridian*."

"Everyone wants to ask about the *Meridian*. There's a line out my door—"

"There is no line," I said. "There's you, a stove, and a clerk who's gone to lunch. And I'm asking about one berth. A child's berth. Already listed."

He took off his spectacles and polished them, which is what Brandt does instead of sighing. "The Voss boy came asking too. Through Ines. Quietly." He looked at me over the lenses. "You're all very quiet about this child. Which tells me quiet is necessary."

"The uncle is real," I said. "And he is not an uncle."

Brandt put his spectacles back on and became an official again, which took him about four seconds, and I understood why the war had promoted men like him while burying men like my son. "A berth can be transferred," he said, "if the original holder is deceased or unreachable, and if a guardian signs, and if nobody asks questions I don't have answers to. Can you get the boy to me with papers?"

"I can get the boy to you with papers," I said.

"And yourself? There's room in the numbers if—"

"No."

It came out faster than I intended, and Brandt looked at me with the particular pity of an old friend watching an old fool. "Halvard. They're shelling the coast road. The armistice may not—"

"I have forty clocks in that tower," I said, "and every one of them needs winding, and someone taught an apprentice to oil the escapement this autumn, and I am seventy-one years old, Oskar, and I have already crossed one ocean of grief and I am not seaworthy for another. The boy goes. I stay. Write it down."

He wrote it down. Old friends know when the argument is finished.

* * *

The walk home took longer than usual because I kept stopping. At the fishmonger's, shuttered. At the corner where the conscription notices go up, where a new notice was going up even now, paper pasted over paper like sediment layers of the same disaster. Outside the church, whose bells my master clock governs, because everything in this town keeps time by my tower whether they know it or not, and there is a vanity in that I have never fully confessed.

I stood outside the church and I thought about Emil.

Not the way I usually think about him, which is in flashes — a boot by the door, a laugh down a stairwell, the particular way he held a screwdriver between two fingers like a cigarette. I mean I stood there and thought about the whole shape of him, beginning to end, which I almost never allow, because the whole shape includes the ending.

Emil wanted to go to sea. Did I ever write that down anywhere? No. Nobody writes down the small ambitions; they die unrecorded. He wanted to go to sea, and I told him the sea was unreliable, that a man should keep to mechanisms, to things that could be fixed with a steady hand. He joined the navy in the second month of the war to prove some point at me, and the sea took him in the fourth, and I have spent five years quietly concluding that I was right about the sea and wrong about everything else.

A berth on a ship is just a ship's way of saying *you matter enough to save*. And I am going to give the boy mine — well, the boy's own, but arranged, secured, made certain — because I cannot give him the thing he actually lost, and because a man my age should spend what he has. Time is the one currency I hold in surplus relative to everyone else in that tower. Marta has sisters. Ines has her stars and her husband's memory planted here like a tree. August — August has finally stopped measuring everything, and I will not send him back to the scales. But the boy. The boy has decades. The boy has a whole unwritten ledger.

You give the surplus to the deficit. Any accountant knows it. Any clockmaker knows it better: you do not let the spring run down holding tension it can no longer use. You release it into the train. You let it do its work.

* * *

I told no one, which was easy, because telling no one is my native language.

But secrets in that tower have a short half-life, so I began the preparations anyway, quietly, the way you pre-set a mechanism days before it fires. I forged a guardianship letter — my hand is good; sixty years of engraving serial numbers onto brass plates will do that — signing as the boy's maternal grandfather, a fiction I tested against my conscience and found surprisingly comfortable. I copied the relevant page references from the stolen ledger onto a separate slip, so that if the ledger itself were seized, the berth's documentation could stand alone. I wrapped the papers in oilcloth and put them in the false bottom of the regulator case in my workshop, beneath the pendulum, where I have hidden things from two wars and one marriage.

Marta caught me at it. Of course Marta caught me at it. The woman runs a kitchen under rationing for five mouths and a stream of ghosts; she notices everything that moves.

"You're planning something," she said, kneading dough that had done nothing to deserve her violence.

"I'm servicing a movement."

"You're servicing a movement with your reading spectacles on and your jaw set like a vice. Halvard Iversen, if you are arranging something foolish and noble, I will—"

"You will what?" I asked, genuinely curious. "Evict me from my own tower?"

She glared. Then, horribly, her eyes went wet, which is cheating, which is the oldest trick in the domestic arsenal, and she said, "Whatever you've decided, decide it with us. We're not your apprentices. We're—" and she stopped, because neither of us has a word for what we are yet, and then she said, "We're the household," and went back to murdering the dough.

The household. Well. If she insists.

* * *

Tonight I climbed to the clock room and worked on the master clock without needing to. August has it running better than it has run in years — the boy has a gift, and more than a gift, he has attention, which is rarer — but I like to be near it. My son's names are scratched inside the case, small letters behind the strike train, and I touched them the way you touch a banister in the dark. Emil, and the dates, and the little anchor he carved beside his name because even dying he was stubborn about the sea.

Then I went and stood in the storeroom doorway and counted breaths. Six to the minute tonight. The ledger under the pillow, its corners soft as cloth from handling.

Lior woke. The boy wakes often; I suspect the boy has not slept all the way through a night since before the note, since before whoever wrote *forget this face* and meant it as mercy. The eyes opened and found me in the doorway and did not flinch, which after everything is either trust or exhaustion, and I choose to believe it is trust, because an old man is entitled to a few chosen beliefs.

"Can't sleep?" I whispered.

A shake of the head.

I did the thing I have been practicing in secret for weeks, the way you practice a confession. I sat down on the edge of the cot, which creaked, and I said, "When I was younger than you, I had a dog. A yellow dog. Ugly as sin. And when the flood came — this was another war, don't ask — we had room on the cart for the dog or the tools. My father chose the tools." The boy watched me, unblinking. "I hated him for eleven years. Then I became a clockmaker, and I understood he'd chosen wrong in the right direction, because the tools fed us and I'd have starved, and understanding it didn't help one bit. You can be right and ruined. Remember that."

The boy's hand crept out and touched my sleeve. Just that. Just fingers on wool.

And I nearly said it. The whole arrangement, the harbor, the papers, the *Meridian*, the date — it rose up my throat like water finding a crack. And then I looked at that face, that instructed-to-be-forgotten face, and I understood that the moment I spoke it aloud, the boy would begin to argue, and the boy would win, because children argue from the future and I argue from the past, and the future always wins.

So I said, "Sleep. The clock needs me at six," and I tucked the blanket the way you tuck a thing you are losing, and I went out, and I closed the door softly, and I stood in the dark hallway with my heart going like a cheap alarm clock.

Three days. The tide turns Thursday. I will put the papers in August's hands tomorrow — no, not tomorrow. Tonight. Because Marta is right, curse her, and because a man who has chosen wrong in the right direction once should not gamble on doing it twice.

I went downstairs to find him. His lamp was still burning in the workshop, and through the door I could hear him working — the small patient sounds of a file on brass, a young man teaching his hands something his heart hasn't agreed to yet — and I stood outside that door with my secret in my pocket, and knocked.
