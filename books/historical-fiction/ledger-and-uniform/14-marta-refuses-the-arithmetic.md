## 14. Marta Refuses the Arithmetic

I have been cooking for forty years, and in that time I have learned one iron law of the universe: everything can be stretched except a lie and a loaf. Flour you can stretch with water, water with patience, patience with prayer. But sit four people at a table set for three, and somebody's stomach knows it before their manners do.

So when August came into my kitchen that grey morning with Halvard's plan hanging off him like a wet coat — the boy takes the berth, the old man stays, everyone else keeps quiet and calls it mercy — I knew exactly how many places I was short at that table.

He stood by the door the way he always does, as if kitchens were observatories he hadn't been cleared to enter. Hands folded. Eyes on the ledger under his arm like it might bite him. Which, to be fair, it had.

"Sit," I said. "Eat."

"I've eaten."

"You've eaten bread standing up over the sink like a man afraid of being caught enjoying something. Sit down, August Voss, before I make it a matter of discipline."

He sat. That's the thing about August nobody gives him credit for: underneath all that clockwork, he obeys like a good apprentice. It's only when you ask him to feel something that the gears seize.

I put soup in front of him. Barley, mostly, and a carrot I'd been saving for morale purposes. He looked at it the way he looks at everything — counting it, weighing it, filing it under *cost*.

"Marta." He said it carefully, the way you'd test ice. "Halvard has arranged passage for Lior. On the Meridian. It's done, mostly. Ines confirmed the berth is real. The ship may sail early."

"I know. Ines told me. Ines tells me everything; she thinks because I'm stirring a pot I'm not listening, which is the most useful mistake anyone ever made about me."

"Then you understand why it has to be this way." He picked up the spoon and put it down again. "Lior is the youngest. Halvard is—" His jaw did the thing it does when arithmetic hurts. "Halvard is seventy-one, Marta. He says so himself. He says the crossing would kill him faster than staying would, which is not true, but it is what he needs to believe, and we need him believing things or none of these clocks will run."

"And you?"

"What about me?"

"You're twenty-three and healthy and you've been doing sums since you walked in. I can hear them. They sound like a mouse in the wall. So where do you put yourself in the column?"

He had the decency to look at the soup. "There isn't room for me anywhere. There's barely room for Lior. One berth. One child. That's the whole of it. I'm just — I'm trying to make it clean."

And there it was. *Clean.* The word that men use when they mean *finished*, and *finished* is the word they use when they mean *buried*.

I sat down across from him. Not opposite like an argument — across, like a witness.

"August. I want to tell you about ration books."

He blinked. "Is now—"

"It's now. You'll eat while I talk, that's the arrangement." I pushed the bowl closer until the spoon touched his hand, which is as close to force-feeding as I get before breakfast. "Three winters ago, when the queues started and the town began to smell like fear boiled in cabbage, a woman came to my back door. Frau Ostergaard. You won't remember her name because she made sure nobody would. Her husband had been taken for labor detail and her papers said she fed one mouth when she was feeding three. She asked me for nothing. She stood there with her hat pulled down and her hands shaking and she said, 'You don't know me, and after tonight you never will.' And then she showed me how the ink could be lifted from a date and reprinted, if you had a steady hand and no conscience worth mentioning."

August had gone very still. August goes still the way clocks go still — you can feel all the stopped motion inside him.

"I told her I had a conscience," I said. "She said, 'Then lend it to someone who hasn't, once a week, and keep the rest for Sundays.' And I laughed. First laugh I'd had since the mobilization notices went up. And then I did it. For a year, August. Maybe more. Ration cards for strangers. Dates moved. Names spelled wrong on purpose so the clerks would wave them through rather than admit they couldn't read. I've fed deserters off that stove. A family from the coast who spoke like birds — quick, quiet, gone by morning. A boy your age who cried into his soup and apologized for the salt he used. I kept a list. Not written down. Up here." I tapped my temple. "A ledger of my own. Except mine doesn't have columns for who deserves it. Mine just has names and what I fed them."

"Why are you telling me this?" His voice had gone thin. "If anyone found out—"

"If anyone finds out, I'll be shot, or worse, and I'll die having eaten well, which is more than most of this war's dead can say. That's not why I'm telling you. I'm telling you because you're sitting in front of me doing division, and I want you to see what division costs."

"The berth is real, Marta. It's not division, it's fact. One seat. One child. Halvard decided—"

"Halvard decided because deciding is the only way left for that man to grieve without falling over. Don't you dare dress it up as mathematics. He's not calculating. He's bleeding quietly where he thinks we can't see, same as he's done since the day his son's name stopped being a person and became a thing he scratches inside clocks." I leaned forward. "And you're letting him, because letting him is easier than arguing with grief, and because your little ledger loves a tidy answer. Youngest goes. Oldest stays. Cook keeps cooking. Apprentice keeps winding. Everyone in their row. Isn't that how the army does it? Isn't that exactly, precisely, down to the last button, how the war thinks?"

The word landed on him. I watched it land — right between the shoulder blades, where you carry weight you won't look at.

"That's not fair," he said quietly.

"No. Nothing about any of this is fair. Fairness sailed already; we missed that boat too. But listen to me." I took his hand off the table — his fingers were cold, always cold, a boy built out of brass and winter — and I held it flat, palm up, like I was reading it. "Choosing one person is how the war thinks. The war is very good at choosing. It chose my brother. It chose Ines's husband. It chose Halvard's boy. It stands at every gangplank in Europe with its finger out saying *you, not you, not you, you*, and every single time it makes that choice, it tells itself it's arithmetic. Necessary. Clean. And every single time, somewhere behind it, there's a kitchen like this one with a pot going cold because somebody got chosen away."

His hand turned under mine — not pulling free, turning, until we were holding on instead of holding still.

"So what do we do?" he asked, and I'll give him this: he didn't say *what can we do*. He said *what do we do*. Present tense. Committed tense. "There is one berth. I've seen the manifest. Numbers don't negotiate."

"Numbers negotiate constantly. You'd know that if you'd ever forged one." I let go of his hand and stood, because some truths you have to say standing up, with your back straight, or they come out apologetic. "Here's what we don't do. We don't put a child on a boat alone and call it salvation. Have you thought past the gangplank, August? Have you thought about who meets that boy on the other side? What name he travels under, what happens to a half-starved child with a stolen book in his coat when the relief workers start asking questions? We'd be spending our one sure thing to buy an uncertainty, and calling the change profit."

"He'd be alive."

"Maybe. Probably. Alive alone is a coin with one face, boy. It doesn't spend."

"Then what?" And here his voice cracked, just a hairline fracture, the kind you find in a movement before it stops entirely. "Tell me the answer, Marta, because I've run it every way. If Lior stays, the uncle comes back and takes him, and we've learned what that man does with children. If Halvard goes instead, he dies on open water with strangers, alone, which is the one thing he's spent thirty years making sure will never happen again. If I go, I abandon all of you and I'd deserve it. There is no line through this problem that doesn't end in loss. I've checked. I check everything. I have checked it forty times."

"And how many times have you checked it with us?" I asked. "Not around us. With us. When did Ines get a vote? When did I?"

That stopped him cold. Because the answer was never, and he knew it was never, and August Voss cannot bear *never*. Never is a number that won't balance.

"We're four people," I said, gentler now, because the fight was out of setting and the truth needed a soft pan. "Four people who, six weeks ago, were three strangers and a clock. And in six weeks you've made a family out of us without asking permission — you feed us, you worry about us, you count us. Well. Count us properly, then. All four. Plus the boy, five. Five people against one berth. Your ledger says impossible. My ledger says: then the ledger is too small."

"The ledger isn't mine to resize. The names in it aren't mine. Those are people who trusted a stranger enough to write themselves down—"

"And the first entry in that book, August — the first one, before all the deserters and smuggled families — whose name is that?"

He hesitated. "It's smudged. Water damage. It's illegible."

"It's Lior's mother," I said. "Ines worked it out weeks ago; she's been sitting on it like a hen. The first name in the book of saved people is the woman who started the book. Who wrote her own son onto the list of things worth carrying. And then she gave up her own berth so another soul could take it, and wrote a note telling the world to forget her child's face so that forgetting would keep him safe." I let that sit in the kitchen air with the steam. "She refused the arithmetic too, August. She's the whole reason there's a ledger at all. You're holding a book whose first lesson is that the math is a liar."

He was crying. He didn't seem to know it — August wouldn't, he'd file tears under condensation — but his eyes were shining and his jaw was locked and his hands were flat on the table like a man bracing against weather.

"I don't know how to think any other way," he said finally, and it came out small and furious and true. "Weighing things is all I am. It's how I survived. Before the tower, before any of you — I counted what I could carry and I carried that and I left the rest, and I lived, and everybody who tried to carry more—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Everybody who carried more didn't make it."

"Oh, love." I came around the table and put my hands on his shoulders, and he didn't shrug me off, which for August is an embrace. "You were a child doing triage. Nobody blames a boy for dropping stones so he can swim. But you're not swimming anymore. You're standing in a warm kitchen with flour on the shelf and a family that keeps showing up, and the stones are people now, and I am telling you — I, who have broken more laws with a pen than that soldier has with a rifle — I am telling you that some weights you don't put down. Some weights you build your life around, and the building holds, and that's the whole secret nobody puts in books. The house doesn't stand because it's light. It stands because the walls agree to hold each other up."

He breathed. Once, twice, the way Halvard taught him — timing it against the master clock's tick, that boy, even his lungs keep time.

"So we refuse," he said slowly, testing each word like a gear that might strip. "All of us. No one boards alone. No one is spent to save the others."

"Now you're cooking."

"And if it fails? If the uncle comes, if the ship sails, if—" He shook his head. "If we all stay and the war ends badly, we lose everything at once. At least the ledger spreads the loss out. Spreads it thin."

"Slicing a grief into five pieces doesn't shrink it, August. It just means five people bleed." I went back to the stove because the barley wanted me, and because sometimes the kindest thing you can do for a man mid-revelation is give him something to watch that isn't his own soul. "Besides. You're forgetting what I know that your ledger doesn't."

"What's that?"

"This household has been refusing the arithmetic for a year. The forged cards. The strangers at the back door. Ines's star-charts going to men who move families by night. Halvard hiding deserters in the clock room — oh yes, don't look shocked, I've dusted around sleeping soldiers in that room since spring. We were a refuge before your boy ever climbed those steps. The war never actually got into this tower, August. It knocked. We never answered." I stirred. "The question isn't whether we can afford to refuse the ledger's logic. The question is whether you'll finally notice we already do."

Behind me, the silence changed texture. I know my silences like I know my doughs — by feel. This one was rising.

When he spoke again his voice had shifted registers, dropped down into something steadier and more dangerous. "The uncle offered me a bargain last night. Safe passage. Mine, and whoever I named. In exchange for the ledger and Lior. I stalled him. Told him I'd answer tomorrow night."

I set down the spoon.

"You *what*?"

"I bought us a night. I wasn't going to take it. I want you to know that — I wasn't. But I wanted the choice to exist, and that frightened me worse than the man did." He stood, and picked up the ledger, and held it against his chest the way you'd hold a wound closed. "Marta — if we refuse the arithmetic, then I need to go and tell a soldier no. And I've never told anyone no in my life. I've only ever told things *later*."

"Then later is over," I said. "Go tell him now. And August—" He stopped at the door, half-turned, lit grey by the window like a figure in one of Ines's charts. "Take the whole family with you. That's rather the point."

He nodded once, and the door swung shut behind him, and upstairs, faint through the floorboards, the master clock struck the hour — steady, repaired, keeping perfect time over a house that had just decided, all together, to stop counting.

I picked the spoon back up. The barley wanted watching.

Wars end. Kitchens don't. Somebody has to keep the pot on for whatever walks in next.
