## 15. The Uncle's Bargain

He came back on a Tuesday, because of course he did. Catastrophe in my experience keeps banker's hours and prefers the middle of the week, when everyone's guard is down and the bread is stale.

I had been in the clock room since dawn, third day of the escapement repair, my hands black with the fine grease Halvard calls *Schwarzes Blut* — black blood — though I suspect he made that up years ago to frighten apprentices and it worked on me for a decade. The master clock had been running again for two days on a jury-rigged pallet I distrusted the way you distrust a ladder borrowed from a stranger. It kept time. Mostly. Within a minute a week, which for a town clock is respectable and for my conscience was an itch.

Lior was below with Marta, learning — against all sense — how to roll dumplings. The child still spoke perhaps forty words in my hearing, rationed out like sugar, but they had begun asking questions, which is its own kind of recovery. That morning's question had been: "Why does the dough have to rest?" And Marta had said, "Because everything that works hard needs resting," and Lior had looked at me over the rolling pin as if I were included in that sentence, and I had pretended to check my loupe.

So when the knock came — three blows, spaced like a metronome, deliberate — I knew before Ines opened the door who it would be.

* * *

The soldier stood in the courtyard below with his cap under his arm, which I will grant him was a touch of theater. Up close, in daylight, without the midnight dark to inflate him, he was smaller than I remembered. Not small the way Lior was small — diminished by hunger — but small the way a man is small when you finally see the seams of him. His uniform fit badly at the shoulders. His boots were resoled twice. He had the face of someone's brother, which is the most unforgivable face a man can wear.

"Clockmaker," he said. He remembered my trade. Men like him always remember what you can do for them.

"Voss," I said. "August Voss."

"Voss." He tried it out like a coin bitten for gold. "You have a good tower here. High ground. I imagine the view is worth climbing for."

"The view is clocks," I said. "And a widow who charts weather, and a cook who guards her kitchen the way other men guard munitions depots. If you've come about the child—"

"I've come to make things simple." He smiled. It was a well-practiced smile, the kind that gets folded and unfolded so many times the creases have gone soft. He glanced up at the tower, at Ines's silhouette in the window where she sat with her charts, and lowered his voice as if the sky might report him. "May we speak privately? Man to man."

"There are no private rooms in an observatory," I said. "Everything here is built to be looked through."

That earned a flicker of something — respect, or its counterfeit. He set his cap on the low wall beside him, and I noticed his hands: clean nails, a soldier's hands, but with a tremor in the left one he kept closing into a fist when he thought I wasn't watching. A man with a tremor wants something badly. A man who wants something badly can be bargained with, or so the arithmetic said.

Here is what my arithmetic said next, and I record it now in full, because a ledger that hides its entries is no ledger at all:

*One berth remains aboard the Meridian. The Meridian sails before the armistice, possibly days before. The soldier standing in front of me commands, or claims to command, the means of safe passage for two — myself and one companion of my choosing — out of this town, past the checkpoints, onto a train, away from the war. The cost: one stolen notebook containing the names of every person between here and the coast who has ever trusted another human being, and one child.*

Two columns. Gains on the right. Losses on the left. And the terrible thing — I want to be honest here, since honesty is the only inheritance I have to offer anyone — the terrible thing is that the columns balanced. For about four seconds, they balanced. My mind did what my mind always does. It weighed. Safe passage for August Voss and, say, Halvard — old Halvard, whose lungs sound like a bellows with a split seam, who would not survive another winter of coal rations — weighed against a book of names belonging to strangers, and a child who had been instructed by a dying woman's note to be forgotten. Strangers versus family. Paper versus blood, if you'll permit the word *blood* for people who share none.

Four seconds. Then the scales tipped, not toward mercy but toward disgust, and disgust, I find, is heavier than either.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I heard them at midnight, when you knocked the first time. I'm hearing them now in daylight, and the answer is the same in both lights."

* * *

But he was patient. That was the worst of it. He leaned against the wall beneath the tower like a man admiring architecture, and he laid out his case the way a peddler lays out cloth, smoothing each fold flat before moving to the next.

"The war ends soon," he said. "Everyone says so. Maybe weeks. Maybe days." He said *days* with a little too much care, and I thought of Ines at her chart table, tapping the glass with her fingernail: *the sky says sooner than the politicians admit.* "When it ends, there will be a reckoning. Descriptions circulated. Lists. Men who took what wasn't theirs will be found holding it. Do you understand me, clockmaker?"

"I understand that you stole a ledger from a dead woman's kitchen," I said, "and lost it to a child, and have been chasing paper ever since."

His fist closed over the tremor. "I chased *survival*. There's a difference."

"Is there? I keep accounts. Show me the entry."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind his eyes that was almost human. Almost the face of a man who had once been a boy somewhere, doing sums at a kitchen table while his mother sewed. Then it shut, the way a case shuts, and the peddler came back out with his cloth.

"Here are my terms," he said. "The ledger. The child. In exchange: papers for you and any adult you name. Real ones, not Marta's kitchen-table forgery work—" he smiled at my flinch; he'd done his homework on us, God help us all "—military transit passes, stamped, signed. You could walk out of here tomorrow with your old clockmaker and be in the mountains by Friday. Clean air for his chest. You've thought about it. I can see you thinking about it."

And here is the second honest entry: I *had* thought about it. I had thought about it at 3 a.m. over Halvard's medicine, counting his breaths the way I count pendulum beats, sixty to the minute, fifty-eight, fifty-five — a slowing clock, gentlemen, a clock running down. I had thought about it while inventorying the household in my head, that cold column I keep behind my ribs: Marta, who cannot leave her sister's grave; Ines, who will not leave her husband's stars; Halvard, who cannot leave anything at all. Four people who could not flee and one child who could, and one berth, and one soldier offering to multiply the arithmetic in my favor if only I'd hand him the paper that proves what he did.

"You want the ledger so no one reads it," I said.

"I want the ledger so the war ends *cleanly*," he said, "for everyone. Including the child. Do you know what happens to children carrying lists when the armies change sides? Nothing good. I am offering to save that child from its own inheritance."

"You're offering to erase yourself from it."

"And wouldn't you?" he said, and his voice cracked, just slightly, on the word *wouldn't*, just enough that I saw down inside him for half a second — saw the fear, enormous, animal, the fear of a man standing on ice and hearing it talk. "Wouldn't you erase yourself, if you could? If some clerk's book held your whole life in thirty lines and every line was a rope?"

I thought of my own ledgers. Every tool cataloged by weight. Every hour accounted. The life I had built precisely so that no one's survival depended on my handwriting.

"No," I said. "I'd want it read aloud."

* * *

He studied me then, really studied me, the way a man studies a lock before deciding whether to pick it or break the door. And I understood that I had made an error — not in refusing him, but in refusing him *well*. A bad refusal invites force. A good refusal invites negotiation. I should have been dull. I should have been a boring man, a clockmaker with no opinions, a man whose face gives back nothing. Instead I had answered like someone with convictions, and convictions, to men like him, are price tags waiting for the right buyer.

So I did the thing I am apparently becoming: I lied with craftsmanship.

"But," I said, and let the word hang.

His head tilted. The peddler scenting a purse.

"But," I repeated, "I am not the sole owner of this decision. There is the woman upstairs who charts troop movements for fun. There is the cook who has forged more documents than your entire quartermaster corps. There is the old man who owns this tower and trusts me with his clocks. If I simply handed you the ledger tonight, they would ask questions by morning. Questions attract attention. Attention is the one thing neither of us can afford." I paused, and let him watch me weigh it — let him see me *being* the man he needed me to be, a careful man, an arithmetical man, a man who could be bought slowly. "Give me until tomorrow night. One day. I'll put it to the household. If they agree — and if the terms hold — you'll have your ledger and the boy at the harbor road, dusk tomorrow. Alone. No witnesses."

"And if they don't agree?"

"Then you'll storm a tower full of witnesses, and we'll both find out how much of what you told me at midnight was bluff." I shrugged, the way Halvard shrugs when a customer balks at a fair price. "I don't recommend finding out. But it's yours to choose."

He didn't like it. I watched him not like it — watched the tremor climb from his left hand to his jaw. But I had offered him exactly what he'd come for: the picture of a man who could be turned, given time. Men like him trust their own reflection.

"Dusk tomorrow," he said. "Harbor road. The ledger and the boy, or the bargain dies and I stop being polite." He picked up his cap, settled it with a soldier's crispness that didn't match his eyes. "You're smarter than the last people who held that book, clockmaker. Smarter people are harder. But everyone sells. Everyone has a price. I've never met the thing that doesn't."

"I mend clocks," I said. "People bring me broken things and expect them back fixed. You'd be surprised what gets repaired, given time, and what gets scrapped."

He laughed — a short, ugly bark — and walked out through the gate into the gray street, and I stood in the courtyard listening to his boots fade until the master clock struck the quarter-hour above me, and even that familiar voice sounded, today, like a countdown.

* * *

They were all in the kitchen when I came down. Of course they were. Marta at the stove with her wooden spoon planted like a halberd. Ines at the table with a chart of coastal winds spread over the flour. Halvard in the corner chair with his hands around a cup of tea he was warming rather than drinking. And Lior on the stool by the fire, dumpling flour to the elbows, looking at me with those eyes that take inventory better than I ever did.

"Well?" Marta said.

"He offered me papers," I said. "Real ones. Military transit. Me and one adult of my choosing. Out by Friday. Mountains, clean air, Halvard's chest, all of it."

Silence. Halvard's cup stopped halfway to his mouth. Ines's pencil stilled over the wind chart. Somewhere above us the master clock ticked on, indifferent, keeping perfect time over a room full of people deciding whether to survive honestly or otherwise.

"And the price," Ines said quietly. It wasn't a question. Widows don't waste words on questions they already own the answers to.

"The ledger," I said. "And Lior."

Marta set the spoon down. Deliberately. The way she does it when someone is about to get fed something they didn't order.

"And you told him—"

"I stalled him." I pulled the stolen ledger from inside my coat and laid it on the flour-dusted table between us, and every head bent toward it like iron filings. "Dusk tomorrow. Harbor road. I told him I'd deliver it."

Ines looked up. "August."

"I know."

"Do you?" she said. "Because there's something you need to hear first, and you need to hear it sitting down." She turned her chart toward me and put her finger on a date circled twice, ink pressed deep enough to tear the paper. "The Meridian isn't sailing when we thought. My husband's old contacts confirmed it this morning. She moves up her departure — *two days early*. She sails the night after tomorrow."

The clock ticked. Halvard breathed. Lior watched me across the table, flour-white, silent, and in that silence I felt the whole weight of the tower settle onto my shoulders — every gear, every name, every hour we had left, which was now fewer than the number I had promised to stall him for.

"One day," I said. "He gave me one day. And the ship leaves before I've spent it."

Marta reached over and closed my fingers around the ledger's cover, her hand warm and floured and immovable.

"Then," she said, "we'd best decide tonight what we're actually delivering to that harbor road — and I promise you, August Voss, it will not be the boy."

Above us, the master clock began to strike the hour, and I counted every bell like a man counting what's left.
