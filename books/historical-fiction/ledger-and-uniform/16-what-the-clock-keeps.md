## 16. What the Clock Keeps

There is a particular silence inside a clock case that no other silence matches. It is not emptiness. It is the held breath of a thousand small decisions — the tooth of every wheel waiting its turn, the pendulum at the top of its arc deciding, always deciding, to come back down. I have spent sixty years listening to that decision and I have never once heard it hesitate.

I hesitate constantly.

The boy was asleep in the kitchen chair, Marta's shawl over him like a tent pitched by someone who had given up on tents. August had gone up to bed an hour past, or had pretended to; the boy could hear August's footsteps in the loft above the workshop, pacing out a thought he couldn't put down. Ines's lamp still burned in the chart room. The whole tower was lit like a ship making ready, which is precisely what it was, though none of us had said so aloud yet.

I went up to the clock room alone. That part of the plan required no courage, only the habit of solitude, which I have in abundance — more than abundance. A surplus. There are men who hoard money and men who hoard grief and I have always been the second sort, keeping mine in a place where no one would think to look for it.

I unlocked the master clock's case.

* * *

You should understand something about this clock before you understand what I did, because the two things are the same thing.

The clock was built in 1741 by a man named Oskar Brandt, who signed his work inside the case where no one but a repairman would ever see it — *O.B., fecit, ut tempus teneat et oblivioni tradatur nihil*, "made so that time be kept and nothing be given over to forgetting." Clockmakers were a superstitious guild. We believed a clock remembered everything that happened near it. Every wedding it tolled for, every funeral, every lie told beneath its face because people assume clocks cannot hear. A clock hears everything. It simply has the good manners never to repeat it.

My son Eirik learned that from me when he was six years old, standing on the stool I keep for exactly that purpose, his nose level with the great brass wheel. "Does it hear us now?" he asked. "It does," I said. "What do we do about that?" And I said — God forgive me, I was young and clever and thought cleverness was love — I said, "We give it something worth hearing."

He took that literally. Children take everything literally; it is adults who teach them not to, and we call that education.

* * *

The key turned and the case opened on hinges that needed oiling, and there it was, the interior I knew better than my own chest cavity: the going train, the striking train, the great wheel with its worn teeth, the escapement ticking its patient arithmetic. And along the inside of the back panel, low down, at the height of a boy of six, then eight, then eleven, then fourteen —

Names.

Dozens of them. Scratched into the wood with whatever came to hand — a compass point, a nail, once, I am nearly certain, one of my own gravers, taken without permission, which taught me to lock my tools away and taught him to pick locks, and which of us was educated thereby I leave to the reader.

*Anna R., who shares her bread.*
*Tomas the ferryman, who asks nothing.*
*Marta K., kitchen, feeds everyone twice.*
*Frau Lindqvist and the twins, cellar on Kirkegaten.*
*Papa, who pretends he doesn't see.*

On and on. Sixty-one names. I have counted them more times than I have counted my own teeth, and I know my teeth better than most men know their teeth, having made several replacements myself.

Eirik kept a ledger too. Not paper — paper burns, paper is confiscated, paper gets a family shot. He kept it here, where the war could not reach it without dismantling the thing the whole town set its days by. Every person who passed through this tower during the occupation — the deserter we hid in the bell loft for nine weeks, the Jewish watchmaker whose shop was burned and who slept three nights in the clock room among the ticking, the smuggler who moved families along the coast and stopped here between runs — Eirik scratched them in. *Given over to forgetting: nothing.*

He was sixteen when they conscripted him. Seventeen when the letter came. I received the letter in this room, standing where I stand now, and after I read it I sat on the floor of the clock case with the door open around me like a coffin standing upright, and I listened to the escapement tick, tick, tick, the sound of a decision being remade ten thousand times a day, and I understood for the first time why my father's generation built these things so large.

So they could hold what we could not.

* * *

Here is what I have not told August. Here is what I intend, tonight, finally, to tell him, or half of it, the half that can be spoken without the other half falling out after it like coins from a torn pocket.

I have arranged for the child to sail.

The Meridian leaves Thursday — earlier than announced; Ines's naval contacts confirmed it today, and Ines confirmed it to me with the particular flatness she reserves for news that must be delivered as fact rather than feeling. One berth remains. I bought it three weeks ago, quietly, through a shipping agent in the harbor district who owes me for twenty years of clock repairs and does not ask questions, because a man who repairs the town's clocks learns where every debt is buried.

Lior goes. The child with the face everyone was instructed to forget. Lior goes on the Meridian with the stolen ledger tucked against that thin chest, carrying the names of the deserters and the hidden families out ahead of the armistice, out beyond the reach of any uncle with a soldier's rank and a criminal's need for clean paper.

And I stay.

I stay with my clocks. This is not sacrifice. Do not let anyone — August, Marta, that insufferable saint of a cook — do not let them make it sacrifice, because sacrifice implies loss, and I will lose nothing. I will lose the company of a child I have known eleven weeks, whom I have fed and ignored and secretly adored in the manner of all men of my generation, which is to say badly and completely. I will lose August's steady footsteps overhead. These are not losses. They are — expenditures. A clockmaker understands expenditure. You spend the mainspring's tension to move the hands. The spring is not diminished by spending. It is fulfilled.

That is what I will tell August, anyway. I have been rehearsing it for three days and it sounds worse each rehearsal. Tomorrow night I will show him the case instead. Words fail; brass does not.

* * *

But tonight, tonight I climbed the stairs and unlocked the case for another reason entirely, and I will confess it because a wry old man confessing is the only honest entertainment left in this country.

I climbed up to ask Eirik's advice.

Not aloud. I don't talk to the dead; the dead have enough opinions already and none of the responsibility. But I stood before the open case with my lamp raised, reading the sixty-one names for perhaps the four-hundredth time, and I asked him silently, the way you ask a father or a teacher or a well-made tool: *Was I right? Is sending the child away the keeping of time, or the abandoning of it?*

And the answer came, as answers do from the dead and from clocks, in my own voice:

Look at the names, old fool. Look at what your son thought was worth remembering. Not the victories. Not the dates. *Who shared bread. Who asked nothing. Who fed everyone twice.* He didn't scratch the war into the wood. He scratched the refuge. The tower was never a fortress against forgetting — it was a guesthouse. It always was. Your son understood it at six years old, standing on a stool, asking what the clock heard, and you have taken until now, at seventy-one, to catch up.

A clock keeps time. But what a clock *keeps* — that is decided by the people who wind it.

* * *

I closed the case gently. Gently, as one closes a door on a sleeping child.

Then I heard the stair creak — third step from the top, the one I have meant to fix since 1911 — and August came into the clock room with his coat over his nightshirt like a man who had dressed for an emergency and found only an old man and a lamp.

"You're up," he said.

"Observational as ever. You'll make a fine clockmaker."

He looked at the closed case, then at me, and I watched him do the thing he does — the weighing, the cataloguing, the little internal ledger of mine opening behind his eyes. He has a ledger for a heart, that boy. I recognized it immediately because I invented the design.

"You've been in the case," he said.

"I'm often in the case. I repair it."

"No." He stepped closer. "You've been *in* it. Sitting-in-it. The door latch is warm."

I could have lied. I am, as established, excellent at lying; I turned away a soldier with a clockmaker's calm last week and slept well after. But the latch was warm and the boy's eyes were sharp and somewhere below us a child slept under a borrowed shawl with sixty-one names sewn into his coat lining, and I found — to my considerable irritation — that I did not want to lie to August Voss. He is the only person left to whom I have any interest in telling the truth.

"There are names inside," I said. "Sixty-one of them. My son cut them there over ten years, starting when he was six. Everyone this tower sheltered. Everyone the town forgot on purpose." I put my hand flat on the case door, feeling through the oak the faint vibration of the escapement, that tireless yes-no-yes-no. "Eirik believed the clock should remember what the world preferred to misplace. I have kept his ledger polished for four years and told no one. Tonight I sat in front of it and asked a dead boy whether I was doing the right thing, and the dead boy, characteristically, answered with a riddle."

"What was the riddle?"

"'What the clock keeps.'" I looked at August and felt the strange lightness that comes when a decision stops being a weight and becomes a motion, a wheel engaging a wheel, the whole train of it beginning to turn. "Tomorrow night, after supper, I will show you. Both of you. And then I will tell you what I have arranged, and you will argue with me, because you argue the way your ledger balances — thoroughly — and I will win, because I am older and deafer and have less to lose."

August's jaw did the thing it does. "Halvard. What have you arranged?"

"The berth," I said. "The last one. On the Meridian."

"For Lior."

"For Lior."

"And you—"

"Stay with my clocks. Yes. Spend the spring, keep the time." I picked up the lamp. "Go to bed, apprentice. Tomorrow we oil the strike train, and tomorrow night I show you what your inheritance looks like."

He went. Slowly, at first, then all at once, the way he does everything — a man who measures every step and then takes three at once when the measuring frightens him.

I stayed a while longer. Third step from the top creaked under his weight and went quiet, and the loft overhead stayed dark, which meant either he was asleep or he was standing very still, thinking, and with August the two are indistinguishable.

I put my palm against the case one last time.

"Thursday," I told the sixty-one names. "One more name goes out into the world, and it carries all of you with it. Given over to forgetting—" 

Tick, said the escapement. Yes, said the wheel. No hesitation anywhere in the mechanism except in me, and even mine, I found, was smaller than it had been at supper.

Below, faintly, a floorboard in the kitchen. Someone was awake down there too — moving toward the stairs, unhurried, deliberate. Marta, or Ines, or the child himself, sleepless under the shawl.

By morning, I suspected, everyone in this tower would know what I had arranged. And by morning after that, if I knew my household at all — and I did, better than I knew the clock, though I would sooner admit the reverse — someone was going to refuse my arithmetic outright.

I just hadn't yet guessed who, or how loudly, or how right they would be.
