## 17. The Night of the Sailing

There is a particular weight to a night when you know it will be remembered. I had spent my whole life weighing objects — brass against steel, a mainspring's coil against the palm — but I had never before weighed an evening. It came in at roughly four lanterns, one stolen ledger, two coats we could not all wear, and a child who had finally learned to run.

Ines came down from the roof at half past nine with her charts rolled under her arm and her face doing something I had never seen it do. Ines's face was a well-regulated instrument; it kept mean time through funerals and ration queues alike. That night it was running fast.

"The Meridian moves up her departure," she said. "Tide turns at eleven-forty. Her captain won't wait past midnight — he told my husband's old signalman that if the armistice comes, the harbor master will invent reasons no ship leaves, and if it doesn't come, the artillery will find reasons instead. Either way: tonight."

Marta set down the pot she was scrubbing as though setting down a weapon she had decided against using.

"Then we go now," she said.

"We can't all go," Halvard said from his corner, and the room went quiet in the way rooms do when someone has said the true thing too early and everyone must pretend not to have heard it. He was oiling the small carriage clock on his knee, his hands steady, his eyes anywhere else. "The berth is one berth. We discussed this."

"We discussed nothing," Marta said. "We *avoided* discussing it, which is different, and I have cooked for this household long enough to know the difference between a discussion and a stew everyone pushes around the plate."

I stood at the window with the ledger under my coat and watched the curfew streets below. Empty, but empty the way a clock case is empty — everything in motion behind the faces you couldn't see. Patrol lamps swung at the far end of the quay road. Somewhere out there was the soldier who wanted Lior and the ledger, and somewhere out there was a gangplank that would rise whether we reached it or not.

"Lior," I said.

The child looked up from the floor, where they had been teaching themselves, silently and with terrible patience, to fold paper boats from the backs of Ines's obsolete star charts. Lior did not ask what was happening. Children who have been abandoned once learn not to ask; they watch hands and doorways instead.

"Put your shoes on," I said. "We're going to the sea."

* * *

You cannot sneak through a curfewed town with four people and a child unless you divide the problem the way a clock divides an hour — into small movements, each one accountable.

Marta went first, because Marta had walked these streets at illegal hours for a year delivering forged ration papers and had the municipal geography of a smuggler. She wore her good shawl and carried a basket of bread as cover, walking like a woman with a perfectly dull errand, which is the most invisible thing a person can be during a war.

Halvard went second, which surprised me until I understood it. The old man moved slowly, deliberately, exactly like a clockmaker called out for an emergency repair, and he had dressed for the part — apron folded into his coat, tool roll under one arm. If stopped, he was answering a summons from the naval yard. It was even true; there was a chronometer at the yard that had been running slow since September, and Halvard had promised twice to see to it.

Lior went between us, wrapped in Ines's late husband's pea coat with the collar turned up so high only the eyes showed. Ines herself took the rear with her charts, because a widow walking home from an evening of astronomy is beneath suspicion, and because — she informed me in a whisper — she intended to keep her eyes on the sky, and if anyone was going to notice a patrol before it noticed us, it would be the person who spent her nights reading the sky for troop movements.

And I carried the ledger.

I want to be honest about what that felt like. For six years I had organized my life around the principle that a man should carry little — tools by necessity, possessions by weight, attachments by their cost. My kit weighed four pounds and three ounces and I knew it to the dram. The ledger in its oilcloth wrap weighed less than a pound and dragged at me like an anchor chained to the spine. Every name in it was a person the war had tried to erase, and every step toward the harbor was a step toward deciding, again, whose name would survive the erasure.

"You're counting," Lior whispered as we slipped through the gap in the garden wall that Marta had loosened with a crowbar sometime around the second year of the war, apparently on principle.

"I'm always counting," I whispered back.

"Count me too," said the child. "So you don't forget."

I had no answer for that. Some weights cannot be entered into a ledger without changing the ledger's nature entirely.

* * *

The town at curfew was a study in negative space. Lamplight leaked through shutters in thin blades across the cobbles. Our breath hung behind us like a confession. At the corner of the chandler's row, Marta raised one hand — flat, fingers together — and we pressed into the doorway of a boatwright's shed while a patrol passed, four men and a dog, their boots striking the stones in a rhythm I hated myself for measuring. Four-four time, slightly dragging on the left foot of the second man. The dog paused at our doorway, snuffled, and was hauled onward by a soldier who wanted his bed more than his duty. I held Lior's shoulder and felt the small bones underneath the coat vibrating like a struck bell.

When the boots faded, Ines touched my elbow and pointed up. Between the chimney stacks, a slice of sky. Clouds moving fast from the southwest, torn open in places.

"Wind's backing," she breathed. "It'll hold until the tide. After that — rain, heavy. Their patrol lamps will be useless in it, and so will ours." She almost smiled. "The sky is on nobody's side, August, but tonight it's disorganized enough to favor the desperate."

We crossed chandler's row in three dashes and came down toward the fish market square, and that was where the plan, such as it was, began to come apart — because plans, like escapements, are mechanisms designed by people who have never actually been dropped down a staircase by fate.

A lantern flared at the mouth of the quay road.

Not a patrol lamp. A hand lantern, held low, and behind it a figure in a greatcoat that had been fitted for a bigger man, standing with the particular stillness of someone who has been waiting and is pleased about it.

The soldier.

He had found us. Later I would understand how — a forged ration stamp traced to Marta's kitchen, an informant outside the observatory, or simply the patient animal luck of men who hunt children. It didn't matter then. What mattered was the geometry: him at the mouth of the quay road, us at the top of the fish market steps, the harbor lights beyond him, the tide turning at eleven-forty, and the church clock — *my* clock, the tower clock I had rebuilt with my own hands over those winter weeks — beginning to strike the quarter hour.

Ten forty-five. Fifty-five minutes.

"Go left," Ines said, calm as a weather report. "Through the net lofts. There's a stair to the north quay. He knows the main road, not the lofts."

"He'll follow anyway," Marta said.

"Then we'd better be interesting to follow," said Halvard.

And before any of us could stop him — before I, who had inventoried that man's remaining years down to the ounce, could so much as reach for his sleeve — Halvard stepped out of the shadow of the shed, lifted his lantern, and said in a carrying, aggrieved, thoroughly believable voice:

"If this is about the yard chronometer, young man, I have told your lieutenant *twice* that a balance spring cannot be hurried, and neither can I—"

The soldier turned toward the voice. Of course he did. A loud old man in a clockmaker's apron is either an irrelevance or a target, and hunting men are greedy.

"Ines," I whispered. "Take Lior. Go."

"And you?"

"Marta and I will collect him." I did not believe it when I said it. I said it anyway. There is a kind of arithmetic you perform with your mouth while your heart does the real sums.

* * *

The net lofts were black and smelled of tar and herring and thirty years of other people's labor. Ines took Lior's hand and went up the interior stair with the certainty of a woman who had grown up in a port and never fully left it. Marta and I followed the gallery walkway above the drying racks, and below us, through gaps in the floorboards, we saw the lantern swing into the loft entrance, saw the soldier's shadow climb the wall enormous, and heard Halvard's voice below, still complaining beautifully about balance springs, leading the man away along the ground floor like a Pied Piper of horology.

"He'll get tired of him eventually," Marta murmured.

"Halvard doesn't tire," I said. "He has a sixty-hour movement and he winds himself daily."

But the soldier tired of being led sooner than we hoped. The lantern stopped. Halvard's monologue stopped. And then, in the dark under our feet, the soldier's voice, quiet and horrible:

"Where is the child, old man?"

"Young man, I am seventy-one years old and half-deaf, and I am trying to tell you about a balance spring—"

"*Where is the child?*"

A scuffle. Not large — I heard a tool roll hit the floorboards, heard a grunt, heard Marta's breath catch beside me — and then Halvard's voice again, winded but unhurried, and this was the moment I understood that the old man had planned this, had wound this hour like he wound every clock in his keeping:

"Boy," he called upward, clearly, into the dark of the lofts, in the voice he used for lessons. "*Escapement.* What does the escapement do?"

I nearly laughed. Nearly wept. He was testing me. He was buying seconds with a grammar lesson.

"It releases!" I called back down, already moving. "It lets the wheel go — one tooth at a time!"

"That is correct," said Halvard, with immense satisfaction, and then made a sound like a man being shoved against a wall.

We came down the stair into the lantern light. Marta had a fish-gaff off the wall and held it the way she held a bread knife, which is to say with the confidence of a woman who has dismembered larger things than soldiers. I put myself between Halvard and the man.

Up close, the soldier was younger than his coat and more exhausted than his posture. His eyes went from me to Marta to Halvard slumped against the rack, and there was a flicker in them — not doubt exactly, but the cousin of doubt, the thing doubt sends ahead to scout.

"The ledger," he said. "And the child. Give me both and I walk away from all of you. That is the last generous sentence I own."

"Generous," Marta repeated, tasting it. "My mother had a word for men who call robbery generosity. It isn't printable in front of the boy."

"There is no boy here," I said, and heard my own voice come out flat and strange, ledger-flat. "There is a child asleep three streets away who has never spoken your name, and a book of names you'll have to read before you burn it, and I promise you—" and here the arithmetic failed me, broke open, spilled — "I promise you that if you take one step toward either, these women and this old man and I will make enough noise to wake the garrison, and you will explain a stolen ledger and a hunted child to military police who are *looking* for a deserter-escort who sold a berth."

I hadn't known I was going to say it. It wasn't strategy. It was fury wearing strategy's coat — my first emotion too large for the ledger, spilling over the margins, and I let it spill.

The soldier looked at me for a long moment. Somewhere above us the loft timbers ticked and settled. Then he stepped back, one deliberate pace, and said, softly: "Midnight. North quay. Bring the child or I start naming names to the authorities — yours, cook. Yours especially, widow." And he turned and went out into the dark with his wrong-fitted coat, leaving the lantern burning on the floor between us like a debt.

* * *

We ran.

There is no dignified word for what we did through the last streets to the north quay. We ran like people who have finally admitted that time is not a thing you keep but a thing that keeps you. Halvard refused my shoulder for the first hundred yards and accepted it for the next fifty, breathing in short iron strokes, and every breath was a price I tallied and hated tallying. Marta threw the bread basket's contents to a family hiding in a cellar grating as we passed — "for the journey," she said, though they weren't going anywhere — and didn't break stride. Ines met us at the head of the quay stairs with Lior's hand crushed in hers and the sky behind her finally opening into rain, fat cold drops that killed the lamplight and blurred the harbor into a smudge of masts.

And there was the *Meridian*, black on black water, her rails lined with shapes, her gangplank down and — as we came onto the stones — beginning, horribly, to rise.

"No!" Marta's voice cracked across the water like a whip. "Wait! You have a passenger!"

A figure at the rail shouted something lost to wind and rain. The plank kept rising. Eleven-thirty-something. The tide muttering against the pilings, the church clock somewhere behind the rain getting ready to spend another quarter hour we did not have.

Halvard pulled free of my arm.

I felt it happen — felt the old man's weight leave my shoulder the way a weight leaves a scale, and some part of me, the part that weighs everything, registered it with terrible clarity before my mind caught up. He straightened. He buttoned his coat. He took the tool roll from under his arm and pressed it into my hands, closing my fingers over it the way he had closed my fingers over my first file, six years ago, saying *feel the cut, don't look at it.*

"August," he said, under the rain, under the shouting, "the berth is mine by arrangement and you know it, and I am going to walk up that plank before it rises, and you are going to let me, and none of it is negotiable, because I am seventy-one years old and I have finished my work—"

"Your work isn't finished," I said, and my voice broke on it. "The yard chronometer runs slow. You promised twice."

Something moved through the old man's face — grief and pride and love passing through like weather, like Ines's clouds. Behind him, the gangplank hovered at the angle of no return. Ahead of us, at the far end of the quay, a lantern came bobbing through the rain. The soldier. Midnight coming like a tide of its own.

"August," Halvard said gently, "what does the escapement do?"

And I stood in the rain with everything I loved arriving at once — the plank rising, the lantern approaching, Lior's hand finding mine, Marta shouting at the ship, the ledger hard against my ribs — and the tower clock behind us began to strike, and I did not yet know what I was going to do.
