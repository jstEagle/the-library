## 19. Armistice Bells

I have spent forty years reading the sky, and I will tell you the first thing any astronomer learns: the heavens are punctual and indifferent, and everything we love lives in the space between those two facts.

On the morning of the eleventh of November, the sky over our town was the color of pewter gone soft at the edges — a thinning of cloud that I had charted two days before and did not trust until I saw it with my own eyes. Clearing by midmorning. Winds light from the southwest. Visibility excellent for bad news or good.

I had been up since four, as I had been every morning since Henrik died, not from grief exactly but from habit, which is grief's quieter cousin who moves in after the funeral and never leaves. I sat at my husband's old desk by the east window of the observatory with my charts spread out before me and my cold tea going colder, and below me in the tower shaft the master clock ticked with the sound of a heart that had been mended by an apprentice and an old man working side by side through October like two surgeons over an open chest.

August Voss had fixed it. That was the marvel nobody said aloud. The clock that had wandered — lost four minutes a week, then six, then eleven, so that the town set its watches by rumor instead of by us — now kept time to within a second a day. I know because I checked it against the transit of stars, which is the only honest referee there is. August would have blushed if I told him. August blushed at everything, that boy, as though praise were an accusation he hadn't prepared a defense for.

And Halvard was gone.

There it is. There is where the sentence always snagged, that whole week, whenever my thoughts ran their usual circuits: the clock is well, the household is fed, the soldier is dealt with, and Halvard is on the sea somewhere between here and a coast I could not name, alone with his tools in a berth given up to him by a boy who measured everything and finally measured wrong, or right — I still argue with myself about which.

I had stood on the harbor road and watched the *Meridian* go. I had seen the fog take her. And I had climbed back up the hill with Marta on one side and Lior on the other, all three of us silent in the way people are silent when they have made a decision they cannot yet afford to feel.

But this chapter is not about that morning. This chapter is about eleven o'clock.

* * *

Marta found me at half past nine, carrying a tray she had no business carrying because her hands were shaking and the teapot lid chattered against the cup like teeth.

"You haven't eaten," she said.

"I've eaten."

"You've eaten yesterday." She set the tray down on top of my charts, directly across the constellation Cygnus, which would have scandalized Henrik and which I therefore allowed. "Ines. Sit with me a moment."

Marta does not ask to sit with people. Marta sits with people; asking is a formality she performs only when something in her has come loose. So I sat, and she lowered herself into Henrik's reading chair — she is the only one of us who uses it without apology — and looked out the east window at the clearing sky for a long time before she spoke.

"They're saying eleven o'clock," she said. "The Paris time. The wireless man told the baker, the baker told the fishwife, the fishwife told everyone. Eleven o'clock, and the war stops."

"Paris keeps its own reckoning," I said. "Our bells will ring at noon by our clock, if the armistice holds to the hour they promise."

"Noon," Marta repeated, tasting the word. "So we have three hours of being at war and then three hours more of waiting to find out if we were ever at peace." She laughed, a short hard sound. "That's the war entire, isn't it. Always one clock behind what you're living."

"Marta—"

"I keep thinking about Halvard." She said it flatly, the way you'd say *it's raining*. "He'll be at sea at eleven o'clock. He won't hear any bells. He'll hear a ship's bell, maybe, if the crew marks it. One small bell, way out in all that water, ringing for the end of the world, and him standing there alone because we—" Her hands closed around each other. "Because August gave him the berth."

"We gave it to him," I said. "Don't hand August the whole weight. He carried it, yes. But we stood at that gangplank and we let it happen. I let it happen. I read the sky, Marta. I'm supposed to see things coming, and I saw that choice coming for days, and I said nothing, because some part of me believed the arithmetic too. The old man gets the seat. The man with the least time left."

"And do you believe it?"

I looked at the sky, which was doing what I had predicted, being blue at the top with rags of cloud burning off toward the harbor. "I believe Halvard would have thrown any of us overboard before he took a seat meant for a child," I said. "I believe August knew that. And I believe knowing it hasn't made the knowing hurt less for anyone in this house."

"No," Marta said. "It hasn't." She stood, and gathered the tray, and paused at the door with her back to me. "The fishwife also says there'll be a crowd at the tower at noon. Whole town, likely. If that clock fails in front of everyone—"

"It won't fail."

"If it does—"

"Marta." I waited until she turned. "August wound the striking train himself last night. I watched him do it. He checked the fly, he oiled the pins, he counted the locking plate teeth twice — once forward, once backward, which I am told is how Halvard taught him, which means Halvard's hands were on that work even though the rest of him is a hundred miles at sea. That clock will ring today if I have to climb inside it and be the bell myself."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she went down to her kitchen, where she spent the rest of the morning doing what she always does with unbearable feeling, which is bake it into bread.

* * *

At ten I went up into the tower.

You should understand something about observatories: people think we live among stars, but really we live among machinery. The dome, the drives, the clocks that drive them — everything turns, everything is geared to something else, everything must release precisely in order to advance. Henrik taught me that. He used to say a clock is just a machine that agrees, over and over, to let go. I thought of him saying it while I climbed the worn stairs past the great case, and I stopped, as I had stopped every day that week, at the open panel.

Inside, scratched into the paint and wood in a boy's uneven hand, decades faded: names. Dozens of them. Henrik had known, I think — or suspected — and never said, because some sanctuaries only work if you don't inspect their walls. Halvard's son had filled the belly of his father's master clock with the people he refused to forget, and now, near the bottom, in a newer hand, careful as engraving: *Halvard Iversen*, and beneath it, fresher still, ink-black scratches cut deep enough to outlast the paint: *August Voss. Marta Brenner. Ines Almeida. Lior.*

Lior's name was smallest. Cut by a child's hand, or guided by August's large one over the child's small one — I couldn't tell, and I didn't ask, because there are things you leave unexamined the way you leave bread to rise.

I put my fingers on Halvard's name for a moment. The wood was cold.

"Wherever you are," I told him, "your clock is keeping better time than it has in thirty years, and your apprentice has learned to lie beautifully, and the child eats like a stevedore, and we are all of us ruined and staying. I hope the sea is kind. You never learned to be kind to yourself, so I hope the sea does it for you."

From below, faintly, the tick of the escapement, steady as breathing. Letting go, letting go, letting go, advancing.

* * *

The town began gathering before eleven.

They came up the hill in twos and threes at first, then in streams — I watched from the parapet, wrapped in Henrik's coat, while the streets below filled with people who had been summoned by nothing except the collective animal certainty that history was about to require witnesses. The baker came flour-white and hatless. The fishwife came with her whole contingent of sisters. Old men who had sat outside the café since August of '14 in the same chairs rose from those chairs and walked uphill leaning on canes and on each other, faces tilted toward the tower like plants toward light.

And among them, moving through the crowd with the loose-jointed walk of men who have recently been relieved of a purpose and not yet assigned a new one, soldiers. Our soldiers, home or passing through, and here and there a uniform that made the crowd part slightly, uneasily, around it.

I saw the military police before most people did, because I had spent the week watching for that particular uniform. Four of them, walking in pairs, unhurried, and converging — I tracked it with an astronomer's patience — converging on a man standing at the edge of the square below the tower steps. A tall man, gray at the temples, wearing the posture of someone who had been searching a town for several days and had run out of places to search.

The uncle. Lior's uncle, if the word could stretch that far. The man who, per the testimony Lior had finally given us in the small hours — haltingly, in a voice rusted from disuse, while August held the child's hand so tightly I thought the bones might complain of it — had taken Lior's mother's berth on an earlier ship and then written a note instructing the world to forget her face, and had come hunting the ledger that recorded the crime.

I watched the police reach him. I watched him turn, and I saw — even at that distance, even without glass — the exact moment he understood. It is a look I have seen before, in other years, on other faces: the look of a man whose arithmetic finally comes due. There was no struggle. There was a brief exchange, one of the officers holding up a paper, the man's shoulders dropping a centimeter, two, like a clock weight running down. They walked him away along the harbor road, four abreast and then five, and the crowd parted for them too, but differently — no unease now, just the solemnity of people witnessing a debt collected.

Below me on the steps, August stood with Lior's hand in his, and I saw the boy watch the soldier go, and I saw August watch the boy watching, and whatever passed between them stayed between them. But Lior did not hide behind August's coat, which that child had done for a month. Lior stood in full view and watched until the harbor road bent and took them all out of sight.

Then Lior looked up at August and said something I couldn't hear, and August said something back, and they turned together toward the tower doors.

I went down to meet them.

* * *

"Did you see?" Lior asked me. The voice still startled people, ours included — small and clear and used now, exercised daily at increasing length, mostly at Marta, who encouraged it shamelessly with pastry. "They took him."

"I saw."

"He didn't fight."

"People rarely do, at the end," I said. "Fighting is for the middle parts. The ends are quiet. That's how you know."

Lior considered this with the gravity the child brought to everything, that terrible thoroughness of children who have been made to grow in the dark. "August says the ledger goes to the magistrate. With testimony. So her name is in the record. Officially. Not forgotten."

"Not forgotten," I agreed. "In ink, in a government building, forever. Bureaucracy is good for very little, Lior, but it is very good at never throwing anything away."

Something flickered in the child's face — the ghost of a smile, quickly mastered. August hovered at the edge of the conversation the way August hovers at the edge of everything, wanting to speak, cataloguing the impulse, filing it under *later*.

"The clock," August said finally. "We should check the striking train. Noon's in — " he consulted the pocket watch Halvard had left coiled in its chain at the bottom of his tool chest, bequeathed by silence and omission rather than by word or will — "an hour and twelve minutes."

"Go," I said. "Both of you. I'll mind the door."

They went up together, the tall stooped boy and the small straight child, and I stood in the tower doorway watching the square fill, and I confess I let myself do something I almost never permit: I imagined Henrik beside me. What he'd say. Something dry about crowds and weather, something about how the sky had behaved exactly as charted, and wouldn't I gloat about it insufferably later. Yes, I told him silently. I will. Save me a seat.

* * *

Noon came the way noon always comes — all at once, having taken all day.

I was inside by then, at the base of the tower, in the room with the great case, and the whole household had assembled as if drawn by the mechanism itself: Marta still floured, August standing too close to the clock the way new fathers stand over cradles, Lior cross-legged on the floor with a heel of Marta's bread, and I by the window with my hand flat against the stone, feeling for the vibration I knew was coming.

Outside, the crowd had gone silent. I heard it happen — the murmur of hundreds thinning, fraying, stopping, until there was nothing but wind and gulls and, underneath, the tick of the escapement counting down the last seconds of the war.

Sixty. Fifty. August had his eyes closed. Marta had flour on her cheek and tears drying on the other one, and did not appear to know about either. Lior had stopped chewing.

Ten. Nine.

I felt it through the stone before I heard it: a shiver in the masonry, the hammer lifting, the great wheel releasing —

Letting go, Henrik murmured in my memory. That's all a clock is. A machine that agrees to let go.

The master clock struck noon.

Twelve strokes, each one round and complete, rolling down out of the tower and across the roofs and down into the square where the crowd made a sound like the sea coming in — not words at first, just noise, the raw material of joy before anyone remembers what shape to give it. On the third stroke the church bells joined, St. Aubin's and the chapel and the convent, all of them late and eager and slightly off the beat, because none of them had ever been regulated by anyone with August's conscience. On the fifth stroke the ships in the harbor began blowing their horns, and on the seventh somebody below started singing, some anthem, some hymn, it hardly mattered, and by the ninth stroke the singing had multiplied and forked and harmonized into that magnificent municipal cacophony that no composer would dare write down.

The twelfth stroke rang out over all of it and faded, and the war was over.

Eleven o'clock in Paris, noon by our tower, and the guns — I learned later, from the wireless man repeating dispatches — had fallen silent on schedule, to the minute, as if they too had been regulated by an obsessive apprentice with a pocketful of weights.

Marta was crying openly and laughing at the same time and hitting August's arm with a dish towel she had apparently been carrying all morning for exactly this purpose. Lior had both arms raised toward the ceiling like a conductor. And August — August stood with his eyes still closed and his palm pressed flat against the clock case, over the hidden names, over his own name, feeling the mechanism settle back into its long patient ordinary work, and his mouth was moving. Praying, or counting, or saying thank you. With August it was always all three.

I turned to the window because I needed a moment where no one could study my face.

Relief, they tell you, feels like lightness. Nobody tells you it arrives braided with its opposite — that the exhale and the grief come out on the same breath, inseparable, like smoke and air. Because there in the square, in the noise and the singing, people were already turning to each other with the question underneath the celebration: *who isn't here?* Every embrace contained an absence. Every cheer had a hole in it shaped like someone specific. I saw the fishwife suddenly stop mid-laugh and stand very still with her hand over her mouth. I saw old men in the café chairs down the hill gripping each other's wrists. The war doesn't end. It just stops making new widows. The existing inventory stands.

Halvard is on the sea, I thought. Right now, this minute, while they sing — he is on the sea, and he cannot hear a note of it.

Or perhaps he can. Perhaps the horns carry. Perhaps a ship's crew marked the hour and rang their small bell, and an old man stood at the rail with the wind taking his hair and listened to the world come apart into peace, and thought of a tower full of stubborn people who loved him badly and well.

"Come outside," Marta called to me, wet-eyed, tow-headed, radiant. "Ines. Everyone's out there. Come be a person for once."

"In a moment," I said.

But I did go. I stood on the tower steps in the November sun with the whole roaring town below and the sky performing flawlessly above — visibility excellent, winds light, the future arriving on schedule — and Lior found my hand without looking, the way children do, and August stood at my other shoulder, and the bells went on and on.

It was Lior who tugged my sleeve, later, when the crowd had begun to drift homeward and the first bonfires were being argued into existence down by the quay.

"Ines," the child said. "August says tonight we read the ledger. All of it. Every name. Marta's getting candles."

I looked at August. August looked at the ground.

"All of them?" I asked. "There are nearly two hundred names in that book. It will take all night."

"I know," said August Voss, apprentice clockmaker, keeper of the tower, the boy who had once survived by carrying nothing — and who now proposed to spend the first night of the peace weighing every single thing the war had made him carry, aloud, by candlelight, refusing to set one of them down. "That's why we start at sundown."
