## 20. Reading the Ledger Aloud

I have fed people through two funerals, one wedding, and a war, and I will tell you what nobody believes until they've done it: grief is a matter of timing. You can't serve it too early or it curdles. You can't let it sit too long or it goes hard as old bread and nobody can get a knife into it.

Halvard had been gone nine days when I decided the mourning had gone hard enough.

The house had been doing that thing houses do after a death — moving around a person instead of with him. Ines kept charting stars she no longer needed to chart for anyone's survival. August wound every clock in the tower twice a day with the devotion of a man watering a grave. Lior sat at the kitchen table for hours with the ledger open in front of them, not reading it, just resting a hand on it the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping dog.

And me, I cooked. That's my liturgy. Grief walks into my kitchen and I hand it a potato and a knife and tell it to get to work.

But on the ninth day I looked at the three of them — the clockmaker's apprentice, the astronomer's widow, the child who hadn't spoken for the first month of their life under this roof and now wouldn't stop asking questions — and I thought: we are going to do this properly or not at all.

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about Marta Brenner, since apparently I'm the one telling this: I am not a sentimental woman. I have been called hard, thrifty, and once — by a quartermaster who is now dead and whose funeral I catered anyway, because that's the kind of woman I am — "a fortress with a soup pot." I take all of these as compliments.

But I had spent a year forging ration papers in this kitchen, and here is the thing about forging papers for strangers: you learn their names. You have to. A forged ration book with the wrong name is worse than useless; it's a death sentence with neat handwriting. So I wrote out names — Anselm Kovač, the Duret family of four, a girl called only Sparrow because her real name was worth more than her life — and I said each one aloud as I inked it, like a blessing, like a spell, like a cook checking her list before market.

Forty-one names over fourteen months. Every one of them walked out of my kitchen alive. Not all of them stayed alive — war doesn't honor paperwork — but they left fed, and documented, and called by their right names.

So when I found the household circling Halvard's absence like water around a stone, I knew the cure, because I'd invented it already without knowing why.

We would read the ledger. All of it. Every name. Out loud, at my table, with food in front of us, because mourning on an empty stomach is just despair wearing a nicer coat.

* * *

"You want us to read *names*," Ines said, when I announced it that morning. She was mending a sock that belonged to a man eleven years dead, which told me everything about how her week was going. "All of them?"

"There are a hundred and six," August said. He'd counted. Of course he'd counted. The boy probably knew how many names per page, how many pages, how many smudges where someone's tears had gotten to the ink before he did.

"A hundred and six names," Ines repeated. "At what, a minute apiece? Two hours."

"Three," said Lior. "Some of them have families listed. And there are notes." The child's finger found a page. "*'The Wehrmann brothers, ages nine and eleven, last seen on the road to the coast.'*" Lior looked up at me. "If we read fast, it's like they died fast. They didn't die fast."

Out of the mouths of babes, as my mother used to say, usually right before telling me to shut mine.

"Three hours then," I said. "With a break in the middle. There will be plum cake, because Halvard liked plum cake, and if he can't eat it, that's precisely the point of eating it."

Ines put down the sock. "You're turning mourning into a meal."

"I'm turning a meal into mourning," I said. "It's the same work. Ask any widow. Ask any cook. The difference between a funeral feast and Sunday dinner is only whether the empty chair has a name."

That silenced her, which is rare enough to note in a ledger of its own.

* * *

We held it at evening, because evenings are when kitchens tell the truth. I made the plum cake and a soup that took most of the afternoon — barley and root vegetables and the last of the good smoked ham, the meal of a household that intended to survive the winter with its soul intact. August set the table. He set it for five.

Nobody commented on the fifth place. That was the first miracle of the night, and I want it recorded: five places, four living people, and not one word of argument about it. August laid Halvard's napkin — the good linen one, the one the old man saved for solstices and never used for anything as common as dinner — and folded his hands and waited.

Lior brought the ledger to the table like carrying a sleeping infant, both arms, careful of the head.

"We'll go in order," August said. "Page by page. Whoever's name it is, we say it whole — given name, family name, whatever else is written. Then the next person reads the next line."

"Why?" Lior asked. Not defiantly. Genuinely. The child wanted the mechanism explained, same as August with a clock. I was beginning to suspect those two were related in some way genealogy couldn't touch.

"Because a name spoken is a name kept," Ines said quietly. "My husband believed the sky forgets nothing. Stars burn out and the light keeps traveling. I think names are the same. Say them and they're still moving somewhere."

"And because forgetting is what the war does best," I added. "It's very efficient at it. We're going to be inefficient."

August opened the ledger to the first page. His hands were steady — the boy's hands are always steady, it's his curse and his gift — but I saw him take one breath before he began, the way you breathe before lifting something heavier than it looks.

"Anselm Kovač," he read. "Deserted from the 3rd Regiment, October. Hiding in the tannery district. Has a sister, Petra."

"Kept," I said.

I don't know where the word came from. It arrived the way words do when they're needed — uninvited and correct. But Lior picked it up instantly, the way children pick up anything that sounds like a rule of a game, and after that it was liturgy:

"Petra Kovač. Kept."

"The Duret family. Henri, Odile, Céleste, and the baby, who is listed only as 'the baby.' Kept, kept, kept, kept."

"Sparrow. Real name unknown. Kept."

* * *

I will not pretend it was solemn throughout. Households are households. Around page twelve, Lior got hungry — a child can mourn and be starving simultaneously; I've watched it happen — and we paused for soup, and Ines knocked the salt over and declared it an omen and August, dry as tinder, observed that if spilled salt were an omen of anything, the entire war would have ended in 1914. Ines threw her napkin at him. He caught it without looking up from the ledger. I laughed so hard I had to sit down, and then I cried a little while laughing, which is the particular alchemy of a kitchen table and cannot be manufactured anywhere else.

But between the soup and the laughter, the names went on. And here is what happened, here is the thing I could not have predicted even though I had spent a year doing a smaller version of it alone:

Saying them together changed them.

Alone, at my stove, inking a stranger's name onto forged paper, each name had been a secret weight I carried privately, forty-one stones in a pocket nobody inspected. Spoken aloud, passed around the table like a dish — August taking the regiment pages, Ines taking the families, Lior insisting on reading the children themselves, voice small and absolutely level — the names stopped being weights. They became guests. The table filled up. By page thirty I looked around at our four chairs and saw a room crowded with the absent, and it was not a haunted feeling. It was a *fed* feeling.

There was a moment — page fifty-one — when August came to a name and stopped.

"Read it," Ines said gently.

"It's—" He cleared his throat. "It's a boy. Tomas Brandt. Age sixteen. Note says: 'Took my place in the queue for the coastal ship. I hope the sea is kinder than the queue.'" August's thumb pressed flat against the page. "He gave his berth away. Someone wrote that down. Someone thought that was worth writing down."

"He was right," said Lior.

"Kept," I said, before the silence could set like concrete. "Tomas Brandt, kept. Turn the page, August."

He turned the page. That's the whole of heroism some nights: turn the page.

* * *

Near the end of the second hour, Lior reached the entries near the back — the recent ones, the ones in a shakier hand — and went quiet.

"This is where my mother's name should be," the child said.

The fire ticked. Ines reached across and covered Lior's hand on the table, and I did the thing I do, which was stand up and put more soup in everyone's bowl whether they wanted it or not, because my hands needed employment and so did the silence.

"She isn't written anywhere," Lior continued. "She didn't desert and she wasn't smuggled. She just — worked, and starved, and got sick, and died in a room I wasn't in. There's no ledger for people like her. Nobody stole anything for her. Nobody had to. The war just... collected her. Like rent."

"Then we'll write her," I said.

Everyone looked at me.

"In the book. Now. Tonight. Pass me a pen." I held my hand out to August, who produced one from his vest with the reflexive speed of a man who carries pens the way other men carry knives. "A ledger that only records crimes is half a ledger. The other half records what was owed. Sit up straight, child. What was your mother's name?"

"Liora," Lior whispered. "Liora Venn."

"Liora Venn," I repeated, writing it slow, making the letters beautiful, because if I was going to commit forgery in my own kitchen one more time it might as well be my finest work. "Mother of Lior. Fed strangers when she had less than they did — don't look surprised, children inherit appetites, I can see yours. Died in the winter of the war. Owed: everything. Paid: everything."

I turned the book toward Lior. "Read it back. Names aren't kept until they're said."

Lior read it. Voice cracking on "mother," steady again by "everything." And then the child did something I will carry to my own grave, which is coming along sooner than I'd like: Lior picked up the pen and, beneath the entry, in careful letters, wrote *kept*.

Then, without being asked, Lior turned back through the whole book and wrote *kept* beside every single name. A hundred and seven entries. It took another hour. Nobody stopped the child. August fetched more ink twice.

* * *

We finished near midnight. The plum cake was gone, the candles were stubs, and the fifth place setting still stood untouched and perfect, and I confess I looked at it and spoke to it, because a woman who talks to her dough is allowed to talk to a napkin.

"Well, old man," I said. "You missed a fine reading. There was cake."

"He heard it," August said. Not mystically. Matter-of-factly, the way he'd say the escapement is worn. "The clock case. His son's names are inside it. He told us. He — this tower has been keeping names longer than any of us. He was listening. He was probably taking notes."

"That's the most comforting thing you've ever said," Ines told him, "and also slightly horrifying."

"He's a clockmaker," August said with the ghost of a smile. "Horrifying and comforting is the whole trade."

We washed the dishes together, all four of us, shoulder to shoulder at my basin, which is a thing families do and strangers cannot, and I thought about how a year ago this sink had held only my dishes and my secrets, and how now it held four sets of hands and a hundred and seven names and one empty chair that felt, tonight, less like a wound and more like a reserved seat.

Grief doesn't leave. I've buried a husband and a son and most of my illusions, and I can report that grief is a lodger who never pays rent and never moves out. But it can be put to work. Set it a task — knead this, stir that, say these names — and it stops breaking the furniture.

We had put it to work. The house felt it.

* * *

It was August who noticed, near the very end, drying the last bowl, that the ledger had a final blank page.

"One left," he said. He held the book under the candlelight, frowning at the emptiness the way he frowned at a clock running fast — as though blankness were a malfunction. "Every page is full except this one."

"Room for more names," Lior said. "People we save next."

"Or," Ines said slowly, looking around the table with an expression I recognized from the nights she came down from the observatory having seen something in the sky she didn't like, "room for the names of the people in this room. So that if the war comes back for any of us — and wars do come back, they're like bad weather — somebody knows to write *kept*."

The kitchen went quiet in a new way. Not grieving quiet. Considering quiet.

I looked at the blank page. I looked at my strange, stubborn, assembled family — the apprentice with the ledger heart, the widow who read the sky, the child who taught us all the word *kept*. And I thought about what a terrible waste it would be, after everything, to leave a page empty in a house this full.

"Tomorrow," I said. "Tonight we've mourned enough. Tomorrow we start a new page."

August was already reaching for the pen.
