## 21. The Apprentice's Mark

A year is a long time to be dead.

Halvard had been gone eleven months and six days when I finally climbed the tower stairs with the case key in my pocket and a pen I did not need, because you do not sign your name in ink inside a clock. You scratch it. That was the lesson of the place, if it had one: everything permanent in that tower was scratched, not written — names in brass, notches on the doorframe where Lior had measured himself against Marta's flour sacks, the tally Ines kept in chalk on the south wall of her observatory room, one mark for every clear night she'd charted since the armistice. Four hundred and something. She'd stopped counting aloud because the number embarrassed her, or maybe because it didn't anymore.

I climbed slowly. Not from age — I was twenty-three and my knees were a scandal waiting to happen, but they worked — from ceremony. Some stairs you take two at a time for efficiency, and some you climb one at a time so that arriving means something. The master clock's room smelled the way it always had: oil, cold brass, dust burning off the lamp glass, and underneath it all the faint iron-and-snow smell of winter coming through stone that had been letting winter in since before anyone alive was born.

The clock was running. Of course it was running. It had been running since the night I rebuilt the escapement with Halvard's tools in my hands and his voice in my head saying *release, boy, it only keeps time by letting go* — the most annoying true thing anyone ever taught me. Eleven months of perfect time. The town had stopped calling it the observatory clock and started calling it Voss's clock, which I corrected every single time, and would continue correcting until I died, and possibly after.

I set down the lamp. I took out the key.

The case door swung open on the little world inside — the pendulum swinging its patient scythe, the weights descending their slow inches, the gear train turning in its oiled silence. And there, on the inside of the back plate, low where no inspector would ever look and no casual eye would ever fall, the scratches.

I'd found them the night the clock failed completely, the night before everything — the harbor, the gangplank, the soldier, the bells. Halvard hadn't shown them to me. He'd let me find them, which was more instructive and more cruel, and very much him. *A.V.* in a child's unsteady hand. *A.V.* again, older, steadier, cut deep with what must have been a jeweler's graver. And below them, smaller, almost shy: *H.I. teaches A.V. the escapement. He lets go. Finally.*

He'd cut that last one while I slept, or while I was at the harbor, or sometime in those hours I can't account for and have stopped trying to. It was his goodbye and he'd hidden it inside a machine, because Halvard Iversen could not say a tender thing out loud if you held a pistol to his ration book. I loved him for it and resented him for it in roughly equal measure, which is, I'm told, what family is.

Below his marks there was space. There had been space for eleven months.

I took out my graver — his graver, technically, but tools stop belonging to the dead faster than anything else does, mostly because the dead don't oil them — and turned it in the lamplight. Then I put it away again, because I am who I am, and who I am writes things down first.

I had brought a notebook. Of course I had brought a notebook. Marta says I would draft a eulogy in pencil before weeping at a funeral, and she's right, and I've stopped apologizing for it. The ledger heart doesn't break; it revises.

Here is what I had drafted, over several nights, in my small even hand:

*Candidate inscriptions, interior of master clock case:*

1. *August Voss, apprentice.* — Rejected. Inaccurate. Apprentice to whom? The man is dead. You cannot apprentice to a memory, though God knows I tried for the first three months.
2. *August Voss, clockmaker.* — Rejected. Presumptuous. Also inaccurate. A clockmaker makes clocks. This clock made me.
3. *A.V., who stayed.* — Closer. But "stayed" implies I had somewhere else to be, and the whole point of the past year is that I never did. The Meridian sailed without us. The war ended without our permission. Staying wasn't a choice I made so much as the ground I discovered I was standing on.
4. Blank. — Considered seriously. Let the space speak. Very artistic. Marta would approve. Marta approves of blank pages the way other people approve of open windows.

But here is the trouble with leaving a page empty, as I told the kitchen table the night after we read Lior's ledger aloud: a house this full cannot afford vacancies. And a name left unwritten is a name someone else will write for you, eventually, in worse handwriting, with worse intentions. I had spent the entire war being described by other people's entries — the apprentice, the boy with the ledger heart, the one who counts. Halvard had spent his whole life being "the master." Even Lior, who could not speak for those first weeks, had been entered into the world by a stranger's hand: *forget the face.*

Well. I remembered the face. All of them. And I was done being an entry in someone else's book.

* * *

You should understand what the tower looked like that autumn, a year on, because it matters to what I wrote.

Marta had won her argument with the arithmetic — she wins all her arguments; she simply outlasts them like weather — and the observatory had stopped pretending to be a workplace with incidental residents. There were beds where charts used to be. Ines still read the sky every clear night, but now she read it for farmers and fishermen instead of fugitives, publishing her forecasts in the town paper under a byline she'd chosen herself: *The Widow's Almanac.* When the editor suggested something less morbid she'd looked at him the way she looks at clouds that promise rain and won't commit, and he printed *The Widow's Almanac* exactly as dictated.

Lior went to school. Spoke full sentences now, mostly, though the first word of any morning was still liable to arrive late, like a train making up time. The child kept things — that word again, *kept*, the first one spoken, the one that named the whole household before we knew we were one. Kept a tin of buttons. Kept the forged ledger page I'd shown the soldier at the gangplank, the lie that saved us, folded flat as a pressed flower. Kept, in a matchbox, the pencil stub Halvard had used to correct Lior's letters, and would not hear one word about throwing it out.

And me. I kept the books — the real ones now, legitimate ones, a proper shop ledger for the clock repair business the town had decided I was running whether I'd applied for the position or not. Half the town's watches came through my bench. The other half came through my bench after failing at someone else's. I cataloged every tool by weight and necessity, same as always, same as the day I packed for the observatory expecting a fortnight's work and found a child on the steps instead. Some minds survive by carrying little. Mine survived by carrying precisely, which is different, and heavier, and mine.

But there was one tool I had not touched in eleven months. It sat in Halvard's roll, wrapped in the flannel he'd wrapped it in, at the bottom of the drawer where he'd kept it, and I had opened that drawer perhaps forty times to take other things and closed it each time without lifting the flannel.

His graver. The one that cut *H.I.* and *A.V.* and the sentence about letting go.

An heirloom, you'd call it. And I want to say plainly, because this chapter of my life deserves plain language: I did not want an heirloom. An heirloom is identity inherited, identity handed down like a coat too big in the shoulders, worn because someone else wore it well. I had spent a year wearing Halvard's coat — his title, his customers, his chair at the head of the kitchen table, which nobody sat in, which was its own kind of monument and a stupid one, because Halvard would have hated it. He'd have sat in it himself just to spite us, then complained about the view.

The town called the clock Voss's clock. The town assumed. Towns always assume; it saves them thinking. And every time someone said it, some small dishonest part of me accepted the credit while the honest part filed a correction that never got published. *Voss's clock.* As if I'd built it. As if the escapement weren't his design refined, his tolerances, his thirty years of knowing exactly how much this old tower swells in summer and settles in frost.

So: the graver. The name. The space below his marks.

I climbed down, fetched the notebook, crossed out candidates one through four, and stood a long while in the clock room with the lamp turned up and my breath fogging, deciding.

* * *

What I decided, in the end, came from Lior.

Not from anything the child said — though Lior says plenty now, a full year's worth of accumulated sentences pouring out like a spring that froze mid-run and thawed all at once. No. It came from something Lior *did*, three nights earlier, at the kitchen table, during the reading.

We still read the ledger. Not every night — grief that schedules itself becomes a chore, and Marta put her foot down about that within the month: "We are not a parish, we are a family, and families mourn when mourning arrives, not by appointment." So the reading happens when it happens. Someone has a bad dream, or a name surfaces in conversation, or the anniversary of nothing in particular lands wrong, and one of us fetches the book and we read until the weight redistributes.

That night it was Lior who fetched it. Read five names aloud in a clear schoolroom voice — deserters, smuggled families, the ones who made the Meridian and the ones who didn't — and then closed the book and said, in the tone of a child reporting a fact of nature:

"Halvard's name isn't in it."

"It isn't," I agreed.

"He should be. He kept me." A pause, the particular pause of a child assembling grammar around something too large for it. "Kept people go in ledgers. That's the rule."

"That isn't—" I began, and Marta's hand landed on my shoulder with the weight of divine authority, and I shut up.

"We'll add him," Marta said. "In ink. Tonight."

And we did. Right there, in stolen property, in a book whose every other entry had been written in fear by strangers in cellars — we added *Halvard Iversen, who kept,* in Marta's fat confident hand, and beneath it, because Lior insisted on protocol, *entered by his family.*

*Entered by his family.* Five words, and they rearranged the entire architecture of my chest. Because a ledger records what happened, but an inscription *declares* — it doesn't wait for events to authorize it. Halvard's death was an event. Our keeping of him was a decision. And decisions, unlike events, get signatures.

So that is what I carried up the tower stairs, one at a time, on the eleventh of November, one year to the week since the bells: not a draft of the perfect epitaph, but the understanding that I was not writing an epitaph at all. I was making an entry. And entries are made by the living, in their own hand, on purpose.

* * *

The pendulum swung. I counted ten beats without meaning to — habit, affliction, comfort, take your pick — and then opened the notebook one final time and wrote the candidate I'd been circling all along without admitting it:

5. *A.V., who was kept, and keeps.*

It wasn't elegant. Elegance was never the house style here. Halvard's own inscription — *he lets go. finally.* — wouldn't have passed muster with any engraver's guild, and it was the truest sentence in the building. Mine rhymed with it, sort of, the way family members rhyme: similar structure, different damage.

*Who was kept:* the child on the steps, the note that failed, the soldier turned away at midnight, the berth given to the man with the least time left. Kept by Marta's forged papers and Ines's starlight and Halvard's calm lies and a child who chose us by walking up our stairs and refusing to leave. I had spent twenty-two years measuring the world in what could be carried. They had spent one year teaching me that carrying and keeping are different verbs, and that the second one holds more.

*And keeps:* the clocks on my bench, the books in my office, the names read aloud at the table, the face remembered against explicit instructions to forget it. Keeping as a trade. Keeping as a vocation. Keeping as the thing I would do with the one life I had, starting with the next sixty years of winding this clock.

I took the graver from the flannel.

It was lighter than I expected. Most feared objects are. The steel had gone slightly dull with disuse — Halvard would have had words — so I honed it first, right there on the case's edge, ten strokes, twenty, testing the edge against my thumbnail the way he'd taught me when I was sixteen and thought sharpening was beneath theory. His hands had been big for the work, knuckly, precise. My hands are narrow and exact. Neither of us chose our hands. What we chose was what to do with them.

I braced the graver against the back plate, below his marks, below the space that had waited a year, and I cut.

Brass takes a line beautifully if you don't rush it. *A.V.* — the letters coming out steadier than I felt, because that's what hands are for, holding steady what the rest of you can't. Then the longer line, slower, the graver singing its small note against the metal, the curls of brass falling into the case bottom like bright shavings of time:

*A.V., who was kept, and keeps. Entered by his own hand.*

I blew the filings clear. Set the graver down — in the drawer, not the flannel; the flannel era was over. Closed the case. The pendulum went on swinging, indifferent and faithful, and the escapement ticked its ancient argument with gravity, releasing, catching, releasing, keeping time the only way time can be kept: by letting go of every second in order to hold the next.

Then I did the other thing. The harder thing, though it sounds small.

I went down to the kitchen, where Marta was threatening bread into shape and Ines was annotating tomorrow's forecast and Lior was doing sums badly on purpose to provoke me, and I moved Halvard's chair.

"Finally," Ines said, not looking up.

"You've been standing behind it for a month like a man afraid of furniture," Marta said.

"I was—" I started, and stopped, because the honest answer (*I was waiting for permission from a dead man*) was too pathetic even for this family, which forgives nearly everything. I sat down in it. It fit better than I expected. Head-of-table chairs usually do; that's the trap of them.

Lior slid the sum book across to me. "Check my work."

"You got the third one wrong on purpose."

"Did I?" said Lior, with the serene dishonesty of a nine-year-old who has learned it from watching adults forge manifests, ration papers, and naval contacts, and I signed off on the sum with a correction, and the evening proceeded, ordinary as bread.

But upstairs, the clock struck seven — my clock, Halvard's clock, ours — and inside its case, in brass, in my own hand, the new entry held. Identity chosen, not inherited. A name that fit because I'd cut it to measure.

* * *

There was one thing I hadn't accounted for, because there always is.

Lior appeared at my workbench door two days later, holding the matchbox — the one with Halvard's pencil stub — and wearing the expression the child wears when about to make a proposal of terrifying scope.

"The ledger needs a second volume," Lior said. "For the kept ones. Ours."

I looked at the child. I looked at the blank notebook I had, in fact, already bought for exactly this purpose and hidden under invoices out of what I can only describe as professional embarrassment.

"Lior," I said, "you are going to be a clockmaker someday, and I mean that as a threat."

The child grinned, set the matchbox on my bench like a deposit, and asked me to teach the escapement next.
