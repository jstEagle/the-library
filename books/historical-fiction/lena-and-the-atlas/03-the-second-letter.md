## 3. The Second Letter

The envelope came on the morning of November 17th, and Vesper knew it was trouble before her fingers touched it.

She knew it by the weight—too heavy for a single page—and by the postmark, which was not a postmark at all but a smudged cancellation that might have been done anywhere in London, or nowhere. She knew it most of all by the address, written in a hand that was careful without being elegant, each letter formed with the deliberate patience of someone who had practiced not being recognized.

*Mrs. Edith Halloway, Wexford House, Pimlico.*

The same hand as the first letter. The one with nothing inside it but a date.

Vesper stood in the doorway of the servants' entrance with the post tray balanced against her hip and let the cold off the river work its way through the gap under the door. Behind her, the kitchen smelled of toast and coal smoke; Mrs. Pell had the kettle on and was complaining to no one about the price of milk. Above stairs, she could hear Tobias's boots on the landing, Edmund's door still shut as it always was until nine, Constance at the piano picking out something slow and minor.

Ordinary. All of it ordinary. That was the trick of a house like this one—it could hold catastrophe in one hand and breakfast in the other, and neither would know about the other until it was too late.

"Anything for me?" Edith asked from the morning room, where she sat with the *Clarion* folded to the suffrage columns and a pen already uncapped. At fifty-eight, Edith Halloway had the posture of a woman who expected arguments and the eyes of one who enjoyed them.

"Nothing pressing, ma'am." The lie came out smooth as poured cream. Twelve years will do that. "Circulars. A bill from the coach builders."

Edith nodded and returned to her paper, and Vesper carried the tray down to the kitchen and set it on the scrubbed table beside the iron that was always warm these days, always waiting.

Here is what no one in Wexford House understood, though they had all benefited from it: letters were not sealed so much as *paused*. A kettle's steam held an inch above an envelope flap for ninety seconds—the length of time it took Mrs. Pell to butter toast—and the gum surrendered like a shy thing asked twice. Then the blade, flat and patient, and the flap lifted whole, resealable, innocent. Vesper had learned it at nineteen from a woman named Ada Fenn who ran letters for the movement and who had told her, once, with cigarette breath and terrible kindness: *You're not reading secrets, girl. You're reading weather. You want to know when the storm's coming before it comes.*

Vesper read the weather every morning at the kitchen table while the household slept or bathed or dressed above her head. In twelve years she had steamed open perhaps four hundred letters and resealed them so cleanly that even Inspector Reeve himself—if he ever came, if he ever looked—would find nothing but wax and trust.

The first letter, the one with only the date inside, had come eleven days ago. She had burned it in the grate at midnight and told herself it was a prank, a crank, a mistake of delivery. December 30th. Nothing else. No signature, no threat, no name. Just the date sitting on the page like a stone dropped down a well, waiting for the splash.

This letter was not a prank.

She waited until Mrs. Pell went out to the greengrocer, until the house settled into that mid-morning hush broken only by Constance's piano drifting faintly through the floorboards. Then she filled the kettle, held the envelope over the steam, counted, and slid the blade.

Two pages. The same deliberate hand. And this time, words.

*Mrs. Halloway,—*

*You were warned once. Consider this the second and final courtesy.*

*The Admiralty docks will speak on December the 30th, and when they do, the names of those whose money and meetings made it possible will be read aloud alongside the smoke: HALLOWAY among them. Your parlors. your pamphlets, your "evening conversations"—the authorities have long suspected that sedition wears skirts in Pimlico. Now suspicion will have proof.*

*There is one way this may yet be prevented. You will know how.*

*—A friend of the cause, and no friend of yours*

Vesper read it three times. The kitchen clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs the piano stopped, and the silence that followed felt louder than the music had.

The phrase *you will know how* meant nothing and everything. It was the kind of sentence designed to make the recipient invent its meaning—to make her confess to whatever bargain the writer wished she'd made. Edith Halloway had made no bargain. Edith raised money for the movement through lectures and subscriptions, kept radical company, said outrageous things at dinner parties precisely because she believed them—but bombs? Edith couldn't so much as light the drawing room fire without calling for Finch.

Which meant the letter was not true. Which meant it was intended to be *believed*, and by someone other than Edith—or to frighten Edith into some act that would itself look like guilt.

Or—and here the thought arrived unbidden, cold as the doorstep draft—it was true in a way Edith didn't know. Someone using her name, her money, her meetings, the way ivy uses a wall.

Vesper sat very still. Above her, footsteps crossed the landing—Tobias, late again, taking the stairs two at a time—and the ordinariness of it nearly undid her.

The iron was warm. She pressed the flap down, held the wax stick to the match flame, and dripped a seal indistinguishable from the original. Then she did not put the letter on the tray.

She wrapped it in a linen cloth and put it beneath the loose floorboard behind the flour bin, where Ada Fenn had taught her to keep things twenty years ago in another kitchen, another life. And she walked up the back stairs to the morning room with empty hands and a face arranged into its usual mild competence, and she said, "Shall I bring the motor round at two, ma'am?" as though the world were exactly the size it had been at breakfast.

"You may," Edith said, not looking up. Then she did look up, and for one unguarded second her eyes moved across Vesper's face the way a hand moves across a shelf, checking. "Vesper. Is everything quite—"

"Perfectly, ma'am."

The lie sat between them, small and polished as a bead. Edith returned to her newspaper. Vesper went to ready the car, and only when she was alone in the garage with the smell of petrol and leather did she let her hands shake.

She was protecting them. She told herself this while she checked the oil she had checked yesterday. She was protecting them from a poison that might prove harmless, from panic that might do more damage than the threat itself. If she gave Edith the letter, Edith would go straight to the police—or worse, straight to a public platform, because Edith Halloway had never once in her life met intimidation with anything but a louder voice—and then the thing would be real, would be in the newspapers, would be *findable*. Whereas if Vesper simply watched, quietly, as she had watched for twelve years—

If she simply watched, she could find whoever wrote this before anyone else knew there was anything to find. She could snuff it out like a match. Nobody need ever know how close the house had come to burning.

That was the story she told herself, and it was even mostly true. But underneath it, in the place where stories are made, she could hear the older truth speaking plainly: *if they learn of the letter, they will ask how you know its contents.* Every protection she offered was built from the same material as her crime. To keep them safe, she had to keep deceiving them. The lock and the key were forged from one piece.

She drove Edith to the meeting at Caxton Hall and sat outside in the grey drizzle watching women in dark coats file in, and she thought: *one of you. One of you wrote it, or one of you knows who did.* Because the letter had known things. Not just the date—it had known the *evening conversations*, the private salons, the shape of the household's life. That was not surveillance from Whitehall. Whitehall surveilled clumsily and officially. This had the intimacy of someone who had sat in those parlors.

Someone close. Someone trusted.

Vesper gripped the wheel and watched the rain crawl down the windscreen and understood that she had crossed a line she could not see behind her anymore. Eleven days ago she had found a date and burned it, and that had been discretion. Today she had found a threat against the family that employed her, loved her, fed her—and hidden it under the floorboards. There was no longer any version of events in which she emerged clean.

* * *

Constance noticed it at dinner, three days later, and it bothered her the way a splinter bothers—not enough to stop working, too much to forget.

Her mother was holding forth on the futility of petitioning Parliament—"we might as well petition the tide, and at least the tide is honest"—and Edmund was performing his customary role of amused opposition, and Tobias was eating as though food might be abolished tomorrow, and none of it was remarkable. What was remarkable was Vesper, standing at the sideboard in her quiet grey dress, and the fact that she had refilled Edmund's glass without being asked, which she never did, because Edmund took advantage, and the further fact that when Mama had laughed—a real laugh, head tipped back—at her own joke about the tide, Vesper had not smiled.

Twelve years, and Constance could count on one hand the times Vesper hadn't smiled at Mama's laughter.

It was a small thing. It should have been a small thing. But Constance had built a whole education out of small things: the tremor in Papa's left hand that preceded his bad weeks by two days; the way Edmund's generosity always arrived attached to an invoice; the particular quality of silence in a house where someone was keeping something. She had been the still, observing child in a family of loud convictions, and observation had become her native language. Her mother spoke in speeches. Constance spoke in attention.

So she watched Vesper clear the plates, and saw that her hands were perfectly steady and her eyes were somewhere else entirely—somewithin, some cellar of herself—and Constance felt the old, wordless recognition stir between them, the one that had begun when Constance was sixteen and had cried in the conservatory after Edmund broke her telescope, and Vesper had appeared with a cup of tea and said nothing at all, and the nothing had said everything.

Later, Constance found her in the hall, winding the clock.

"The Hendersons are coming Saturday," Constance said. "Mama wants the good china."

"I'll see to it." The clock's weights rose. Click, click, click.

"Vesper."

Click.

"What's happened?"

The winding stopped. For a moment they stood together in the lamplit hall, and Constance watched something move behind Vesper's face like a fish under ice—there, gone, there.

"Nothing's happened, miss."

"You wound that clock every evening and it takes forty seconds and you didn't miss a turn. You're not distracted. You're *deciding* something." Constance kept her voice low. From the drawing room came the sound of Edmund arguing with Tobias about the war in Transvaal, the two of them orbiting their usual grievance. "I'm not Mama. I don't need to know. I'm only telling you that whatever it is, carrying it alone makes it heavier, and you've been carrying things alone since before I knew you."

Something flickered across Vesper's face then—something that looked almost like the beginning of speech, almost like surrender—and Constance leaned in, ready to receive it.

And Vesper closed. Gently, completely, the way a door closes in a house where people sleep. "Forty seconds," she said, resuming the winding. Click. "You count well, Miss Constance."

"It's a useful habit. Counting." Constance stepped back. "One of us ought to say what we notice, sometime."

She left it there, because pressing Vesper was like pressing water. But climbing the stairs afterward, she felt the splinter go deeper, and for the first time it occurred to her that the thing Vesper carried might not be about the family at all—that it might be *from* outside, aimed inward—and that thought frightened her more than any of Mama's stories of police spies and prison cells, because Vesper, of everyone in the world, was the person Constance had never once thought to worry about.

* * *

At half past eleven that night, the house asleep, Vesper knelt at the loose floorboard behind the flour bin and took the letter out, and read it a fourth time by candlelight, and this time she read it not as a threat but as a text—as evidence—and forced herself to ask the questions fear had been drowning.

The paper was good stock. Cream wove, medium weight, the kind sold at any stationer's in Westminster. The ink was iron gall, common. The hand was disguised—she could see the places where the writer had slowed to alter their natural slant, and one place, on the second page, where the disguise had slipped: the terminal stroke of the *y* in *yourself* curled back on itself in a small impatient hook. A natural formation. Whoever wrote this wrote that hook ten thousand times a day when no one was pretending.

She had seen that hook before. Recently. Within these walls, within these weeks, on something filed away in her memory because filing things was what she did instead of having a life.

She sat back on her heels in the dark kitchen, the letter trembling very slightly in her hand, and refused—absolutely refused—to finish the thought. Because finishing it meant choosing whom to suspect, and every candidate was someone whose boots she heard on the stairs, whose tea she poured, whose childhood she had witnessed from the edge of rooms.

December 30th. Six weeks away.

Outside, the river fog pressed against the windows, and the city beyond it went on sleeping around its own buried things, and Vesper folded the letter along its original creases, put it back beneath the floorboard, and lay awake in her narrow bed listening to the house breathe, counting its heartbeats, wondering which one was lying.
