## 4. Tobias at the Window

The rain came down on Cadogan Square like a verdict, and Tobias Halloway watched it from the window of his room as though it might, if he stared long enough, be persuaded to mean something.

He was twenty-three years old and he had never done anything. That was the arithmetic of his life, and he performed it nightly now, the way other men said their prayers. Educated at Harrow, educated at Oxford—halfheartedly, both times. Given a desk at his uncle's shipping concern, which he attended with the diligence of a man watering a plant he intended to abandon. Given a room in a fine house with a fine view, given meals, given money, given *time*—and what had he made of any of it? Nothing that would survive him. Nothing that had ever been asked of him except to exist decorously, and he had not even managed that without complaint.

Below, the square glistened. A lamplighter moved along the railings with his long brass pole, touching flame to wick, and Tobias thought: there is a man who does something. There is a man whose hands alter the world by dusk every day of his life. What did my hands do today? They turned pages. They held a teacup. They were steady when they ought to have trembled, because steadiness is what is expected of a Halloway even when the blood inside is shouting.

His mother would have understood—or so he told himself. Mother, who stood on platforms before crowds of hostile men and spoke about property and persons as though the distinction were obscene. But Mother's rebellion had been licensed, in a way. It had a name, a movement, a newspaper, a drawing room full of allies who drank tea from the good china while they planned the dismantling of an empire's habits. His rebellion—if that was the word for the thing coiled in his chest—had only a basement room off Chancery Lane and eleven strangers and a smell of damp wool and pipe smoke.

And a name no one used aloud. The circle called itself nothing. That was the first rule: *we are nobody*. Tobias had laughed the first time he heard it, a short incredulous laugh he'd smothered in his sleeve, because he alone among them had been somebody for twenty-three years and knew exactly how little it was worth.

He turned from the window. On his desk lay a pamphlet, cheaply printed, its type slightly crooked, its paper the colour of weak tea. He had read it four times. He knew passages of it by heart the way he had once known Horace—the difference being that this, he believed. *Property is the fiction; hunger is the fact.* He ran his finger along the line again though he didn't need to. Somewhere in Southwark a child went to sleep with a stone for a pillow while he counted raindrops in Chelsea. The arithmetic of that was not complicated. Only the answer was unbearable.

There was a tap at the door—not a knock, a tap, two knuckles, brief—and then it opened without waiting, which meant it could only be one person.

"You'll catch your death standing at that glass," Vesper said. "Miss Edith asks whether you're coming down. She has a letter she wants read to her and she says your voice puts Constance's to shame, which between us is flattery of the most transparent kind."

He smiled despite himself. Twelve years she had been in this house, and he could not remember a time before her; she was as fixed in the furniture of his childhood as the scarred walnut of the staircase banister. She had taught him his letters, in a manner of speaking—she'd read to him through the measles, doing all the voices, and he had been seven and feverish and certain she was the cleverest person alive. He was twenty-three now and the certainty had survived every revision.

"I'm coming down." He hesitated, hand still on the curtain. "Vesper. Could you—do you ever think about how it's all decided? Who gets what. Who does what. Before any of us is even born, it's all—arranged."

She paused in the doorway. Rain-light made her face hard to read; it always had. "I arrange breakfast," she said. "Beyond that I take things as I find them."

"But you must think about it. You read everything. Every letter that comes into this house goes through you before it goes to anyone else." He hadn't meant to say it quite like that—it sounded like accusation—but she didn't bristle. She never bristled. She had a way of receiving things, even clumsy things, as though setting them down gently somewhere behind her eyes.

"Reading letters isn't the same as answering them," she said.

"No," he said. "No, I suppose not."

But something in him wanted to tell her. That was the strange thing, the thing he kept turning over afterward: of everyone in the house, it was Vesper he wanted to confess to, and Vesper he couldn't. Because she belonged to his mother's world, or served it, or embodied it—twelve years of quiet competence in the cause of Edith Halloway—and the circle had opinions about women like Edith Halloway too. Reformers, they called them. Half-measures with good posture.

"Supper," Vesper said, and left the door open behind her, and Tobias looked back at the window once—at the rain, at the lamplight, at the whole arranged and glittering surface of the world—before following her down.

* * *

He told her nine days later, on the night the frost came.

It was nearly midnight. Tobias had slipped out through the area steps with his collar up, walked the long cold way to Chancery Lane, sat through two hours of argument about rent strikes and a man named Ferrand who talked with his fists, and walked home again past the shuttered shops with his heart going like a boy's. He let himself in expecting a dark house. Instead the kitchen lamp burned low, and Vesper sat at the table with a cup of tea gone cold beside her and a stack of the morning's correspondence squared in front of her, and she looked up at him with an expression he had no name for.

"You're wet through," she said.

"It's a fine night."

"It's December."

He hung his coat on the peg anyway, dripping, and stood there in the yellow light feeling twenty-three years old and entirely transparent. She waited. That was another thing about Vesper—she could wait the way stone waits, without impatience, without judgment, until whatever you were carrying got too heavy to hold alone.

"There are people," he said. "I've been—meeting people. Reading. Discussing." He heard how it sounded, the cowardly vagueness of it, and hated himself, and pushed on because stopping would be worse. "We read everything. Proudhon, Kropotkin, some American—Thoreau. We talk about what England costs the people who actually live in it. Dockers. Match-girls. Men who work twelve hours and sleep six." His voice was rising and he pressed it flat. "You'll think I'm playing at revolution in a warm coat."

"Will I?"

"Mother would. Edmund would laugh. Constance would worry beautifully." He dragged a hand through his hair. "You're the only one who might just—listen."

Vesper turned her chair a few degrees toward him. Outside, the wind found the chimney and moaned down it, and the lamp flame leaned and righted itself.

"So listen," she said.

And he told her. Not everything—he kept back the address, the names, some animal instinct keeping those back even now—but the shape of it. The room below a print shop, the eleven faces, the rule of anonymity. The pamphlets passed hand to hand like contraband although half of them were legal. The way it felt, that first night, to say aloud that the world was arranged wrongly and hear no one correct him. He talked for a long time, and the tea went colder, and the frost feathered the kitchen window, and Vesper listened the way she did everything: completely, and without giving anything away.

When he finished, the silence had weight.

"You think less of me," he said.

"I think," Vesper said slowly, "that you should be careful."

"That's it? Careful?" He laughed, hurt. "Not *don't*. Not *you foolish boy*. Not—"

"Do you want me to forbid you? At twenty-three?" Something moved behind her face, quick as a fish under ice. "I'm not your mother, Tobias. And your mother chose her battles long ago, and you are not one of them."

"And what am I, then?"

"A man deciding who he is," she said. "It's not mine to decide for you. But—" she stopped herself. He watched her stop herself; he saw the words gather and be swallowed, and it frightened him more than anything he had said that night, because Vesper did not swallow words. Vesper said precisely what was needed and not one syllable more. Twelve years he had known her, and he had never once seen her afraid.

"But what?" he pressed.

"Nothing." She rose, took his coat off the peg, held it out. "Go up. Dry off. Sleep."

He took the coat but didn't move. "You've gone strange," he said. "You've been strange for weeks, since—" he cast about, "—since the letters started coming. Since that first one. Has something happened? Is someone writing to us? Is it about Mother?"

For a moment—less than a moment—the kitchen seemed to hold its breath. Then Vesper said, "Letters come. Letters go. Miss Edith sleeps better for my reading them, and so do I. Go up, Tobias."

He went. But at the door he turned, and by the low lamplight he saw her sitting again, very straight, her hands flat on the table on either side of the dead cup of tea, staring at the frosted window as though the dark garden beyond it were a page she was trying to read.

Vesper's POV

He climbed the stairs whistling, softly, thinking himself unobserved, thinking the house slept around him like a faithful dog. Vesper sat in the cold kitchen and listened until the sound faded above her, and then she put her face in her hands and allowed herself, for exactly sixty seconds by the clock, to feel what she felt.

Fear. Old fear, in a new shape.

Because she knew what Tobias did not know. She had steamed the seal on a letter addressed to Edith Halloway and found a date inside it—December 30th—and nothing else, and dates do not travel alone. Dates are the visible tip of intentions. And now here was the youngest son of the house slipping out at midnight smelling of pipe smoke and cheap print, talking of circles and anonymity and men who argue with their fists, glowing with the particular radiance of a boy who believes he has finally found the door out of his own life.

*They will use him.* The thought arrived fully formed, the way the worst thoughts do, dressed and ready. A family already marked—a name already whispered in connection with sedition, however absurdly. A son restless, idealistic, seen slipping into radical company. If anything happened—anything at all—on the 30th of December, whom would the police come for? Not Edith, too public, too protected by newspapers and sympathies. Not Edmund, too careful. Not Constance, too frightened.

They would come for the boy with the pamphlet in his pocket and the conscience on his sleeve. They would come for the one who wanted so badly to matter that he would mistake being wanted for being valued.

Vesper lifted her head. The frost on the window had thickened; the garden beyond was white and featureless, a world erased. In the trunk under her bed—she had not opened it in twelve years—lay her father's handwriting, and the memory of a man hanged for believing the world was arranged wrongly, and the lesson she had drawn from the rope: that belief is a luxury of the protected, and the unprotected pay retail.

Tobias was protected. That was supposed to be the point of him, the point of all of them. She had spent twelve years making sure of it—reading their letters, burning the dangerous ones, standing invisible between the Halloways and the century's appetite. And now the danger wasn't coming through the front door where she could intercept it. It was walking in through the area steps wearing Tobias's own coat.

*Careful,* she had told him. As though caution were a coat one could lend.

She banked the fire, trimmed the lamp, squared the correspondence she had already memorized. Tomorrow she would find out where the print shop was. Tomorrow she would learn the names he had kept back, the address, the faces—quietly, without him knowing, the way she learned everything. If someone intended to make a patsy of the youngest Halloway, then someone was going to discover what twelve years of invisibility could do when it turned its attention outward.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked—Edmund's room, the middle of the house, three o'clock in its habit of waking. Vesper noted it, filed it, let it pass.

December 30th. Three weeks away. She blew out the lamp and sat a moment longer in the dark, listening to the house breathe around her, counting its heartbeats like a woman counting her own.
