## 5. A Man from Whitehall

The card arrived on a Tuesday, carried up from the hall by the new parlourmaid with both hands, as though it might go off.

*M. C. Reeve, Special Branch, Metropolitan Police.* Beneath that, in smaller type: *Whitehall Place.*

Vesper read it twice before Edith did. That was her privilege and her habit—twelve years of standing between the Halloways and the post—but this time she wished she had not looked at all. The card was heavy stock, cream-coloured, unornamented except for that one word that sat on it like a stone dropped into still water: *Special.*

"Routine inquiries," Edith said later, in the morning room, turning the card over as if the back might explain the front. "What routine requires Special Branch?"

"The kind that isn't," Vesper said, and then wished she hadn't.

Edith looked at her over the rims of her spectacles—a look Vesper knew well, the look that weighed and measured and filed things away—and said nothing. That was worse than a question.

* * *

She dressed for it without meaning to. She caught herself doing it, standing before the narrow glass in her attic room, tugging her collar straight, pinning her hair with more care than a driver's hair required, and she stopped with her hands half-raised and laughed at herself, a short ugly sound. Twelve years of being furniture, and one cream-coloured card had her preening like a girl at a presentation.

It was not vanity. It was the oldest instinct she owned: *when the wolf comes to the door, be something the wolf has no appetite for.*

She chose the grey dress. The plain one. The one that made her look exactly like what she claimed to be—competent, unremarkable, forgettable.

At half past eleven a hansom drew up under the plane trees, and Inspector Callum Reeve stepped out of it the way men step out of cabs when they have already decided what they will find inside the house.

He was younger than she expected. Forty, perhaps less, with the kind of face that would pass unnoticed in any crowd in London until you looked at it directly and then could not stop looking—long, pale, unlined, the eyes a grey so light they seemed almost colourless, set deep under a shelf of brow. He wore his coat well but not expensively. His hat was brushed. His boots were quiet.

That last detail she noted and disliked him for. Men whose boots were quiet had chosen them that way.

He did not give his card to the parlourmaid. He gave it to Vesper, who met him in the hall, and he held it a moment longer than necessary while he looked at her—not at her clothes or her face but at her hands, which were bare, and steady, and betrayed nothing.

"You'd be Miss Yarrow," he said. Not a question either. Everyone in this affair kept mistaking statements for questions.

"I'm the Halloways' driver and secretary."

"Both?"

"Mrs. Halloway finds economy efficient."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile; more like the memory of one, kept in a drawer somewhere. "Efficient," he repeated, tasting the word. Then he handed over the card and removed his hat, and became, with a smoothness that must have taken practice, merely a civil servant making a call.

But Vesper had grown up in rooms where men practiced being harmless, and she knew the difference between a man who took off his hat out of manners and a man who took it off so he could see better.

* * *

They put him in the drawing room because it was the largest room and therefore the least intimate, and because Edith understood—as Vesper understood—that the choice of room was itself a message. We are not afraid of you. We have nothing small enough to hide in corners.

Vesper brought the tea herself. She always did for strangers; the maids' hands shook around officials, and spilled tea told stories. She arranged the tray on the low table between Reeve and Edith—Edith upright in her chair by the window, silver-haired, spine like a ship's mast, wearing her grey silk with the high lace collar she saved for battles—and poured three cups.

Three. Because Reeve's glance had flicked to the door once when she entered, and she preferred to be inside the conversation where she could see its teeth.

"Mrs. Halloway." He accepted the cup. "Thank you for receiving me. I'll be brief. There's been a good deal of correspondence lately between certain societies and—certain parties abroad. Nothing improper. But Whitehall likes to keep its maps current." He smiled at his own metaphor, faintly, the way a man smiles at a thing he has used many times. "You preside over the Women's Franchise League's Westminster committee."

"I have that misfortune, yes."

"And you correspond with societies in Paris, Berlin, Geneva."

"With editors, mostly. Women who print newspapers the police confiscate." Edith's voice was pleasant. Her hands, folded in her lap, were pleasant. Only Vesper, pouring, saw the small adjustment of the fingers—the tightening, then the deliberate loosening, like a sail reefed in rising wind. "If the Home Office objects to women writing to one another, it should say so plainly and spare us all the theatre."

"The Home Office doesn't object, Mrs. Halloway. It catalogues." He opened a leather folder on his knee—thin, unhurried—and consulted it as though reading a shopping list. "A letter of yours to Madame Delacroix in Paris, March. One to Frau Eisner, May. A circular regarding the Manchester demonstration, August. Your handwriting is distinctive. The clerks enjoy your letters."

"They're welcome to them."

"They are welcome to everything," Reeve agreed mildly, "which is why I'm here rather than a constable. Routine." He closed the folder. "Though I confess I've developed an interest beyond the routine. Your son Tobias. Mr. Tobias Halloway."

The clock on the mantel ticked through two full seconds before anyone moved. Vesper counted them. She was good at counting seconds; she had spent twelve years learning how much could happen inside one.

"Tobias is twenty-three," Edith said. "He reads too much and works too little. If the Empire is threatened by that, we're all finished."

"He attends meetings. Thursdays, generally. A bookshop off Clerkenwell Road." Reeve said it the way other men discussed weather. "The International Reading Circle. Very respectable, I'm sure. Kropotkin, some Marx, a great deal of poetry." He paused. "And occasionally, of late, some gentlemen who don't read at all."

"I wasn't aware my son's library required police supervision."

"No one's library does, Mrs. Halloway. It's the company a book keeps that interests us." He turned the cup in his saucer, a quarter turn, precise. "There was an incident at the Admiralty docks in October. You'll have read about it."

"A fire," Edith said. "In a warehouse. The papers blamed a carelessly dropped lamp."

"The papers blame lamps." For the first time, something came into his voice that was not performance—something flat and cold and very tired. "Two watchmen were hurt. The men responsible left nothing behind but a great deal of confidence. Confidence requires money and organisation, Mrs. Halloway, and both leave trails. Some of those trails run through drawing rooms considerably finer than this one." He looked up, and the colourless eyes went first to Edith, and then—deliberately, unmistakably—to Vesper, standing by the tea tray with the pot in her hand. "I'm sure you understand. I'm given to understand you know a great deal about trails."

The teapot made a small sound against the rim of the cup as Vesper set it down. She let the sound happen. A trembling servant was natural. A still one, at that sentence, would have been a signature.

"I couldn't say, sir," she said. "I only take down what Mrs. Halloway dictates."

"Do you." He studied her another moment—two, three—with the patient attention of a man reading a difficult page, and then he inclined his head and let it go, and the letting-go was the most frightening part. Men pressed you when they suspected. Men like Reeve released you, and wrote your name down, and waited to see what you would do with your freedom.

* * *

He stayed forty minutes. She timed it against the case clock in the hall.

He asked about the League's funds and received figures. He asked about the Geneva congress and received dates. He asked whether Mrs. Halloway had ever employed foreign servants, and Edith said her household had employed the same four people for a decade, and Reeve said, "So I understand," in a tone that made the words sound like the beginning of a longer file.

When he rose to go, he thanked Edith for her time, and then he turned to Vesper and thanked her for the tea, and added—lightly, almost kindly—"Your father was in trade, was he? The accent. Northern."

"Yorkshire," Vesper said.

"Ah." He put on his hat. "My mother was Yorkshire. I never mistake it."

The door closed behind him. Through the window Vesper watched him pause on the pavement below, look up at the house—the whole facade, floor by floor, unhurried, the way a surveyor looks—and then walk away toward Whitehall without looking back.

Her hands, which had been steady throughout, began to shake now that there was no one to see them. She clasped them behind her back and stood at the window until the shaking passed, watching the plane trees drop their last leaves onto the empty street.

*Yorkshire,* she thought. *He gave me Yorkshire. And I took it.*

Her father had been born in Kent. She had forgotten—no, not forgotten, buried, deliberately, twelve years ago, along with everything else—until the lie came out of her mouth smooth as water and she heard it land in Reeve's notebook.

He would check. Of course he would check. Men like Reeve checked everything; checking was the whole of their profession. Somewhere in a registry office in Kent lay a record of a marriage and two baptisms, and one of those baptisms was hers, under a name she had not spoken aloud since she was nineteen years old.

* * *

Edith did not speak for a long time after the door shut. She stood at the window with her back straight, watching the street, and when she finally turned, her face had settled into the expression Vesper privately called the campaign face—the one she wore before platforms and magistrates and hostile editors. Composure worn like armour, polished until it shone.

"Well," Edith said. "That was a courtesy call the way a bayonet is a handshake."

"Will you cancel Thursday?" Vesper asked. "Tobias's meetings?"

"No." Edith came away from the window, and something in her jaw hardened. "Canceling tells them we're frightened, and frightened women lose elections and sons. Tobias will stay home from the bookshop because I shall tell him to stay home, and he will obey because he is twenty-three and still believes his mother knows best." A ghost of a smile. "Give him another year and I'll have to negotiate."

"And the letters? To Paris? He has copies of everything, Edith. Dates, names—"

"Of course he has copies. He's meant to have copies. That's the point of the exercise." Edith sat down heavily, and for just a moment—only a moment—the mast bent, and Vesper saw beneath it the tired woman of sixty-eight who had been fighting the same war for thirty years. "They want us to stop writing, Vesper. Stop meeting, stop printing, stop existing loudly. Every letter they catalogue is a letter they fear. Let them fear it."

"Yes, ma'am."

Edith looked at her then—really looked, the weighing look, the filing look. "You served him tea for forty minutes with a gun in the room, child, and your hand never shook. I noticed. I notice everything in my own house; it's the one territory I've never ceded." She reached for the bell-pull. "Send Tobias to me. And then come back—we'll need to draft a reply to Frau Eisner tonight, and make it duller than usual. Dull enough to disappoint a clerk."

Vesper went. In the corridor she stopped, one hand against the wall, and breathed.

*Duller than usual.* As though the danger were a matter of style.

* * *

She found Tobias in the conservatory among the dying geraniums, reading—he was always reading—some pamphlet with a French title, his long legs hooked over the iron chair, his coat already smelling of pipe smoke he was too canny to smoke indoors. Twenty-three, with his father's dark hair going early grey at the temples and his mother's stubborn chin. Of all the Halloways he was the one who reminded her most of a lit match.

"Mama wants you. Now."

"Now?" He marked his place with one finger. "Is it about the man from Whitehall? Cook says he had police eyes." He grinned. "Did he ask about me? Constance says everyone asks about me eventually; I'm the family liability."

Vesper looked at him—at the grin, the pamphlet, the careless young certainty that the world was a debating society and the worst consequence of losing an argument was embarrassment—and felt the dread settle into her chest like cold lead poured slow.

"Go to your mother," she said. "And Tobias. Don't go Thursday."

The grin faded. "You've heard something?"

"I've heard a policeman recite the address of your bookshop from memory."

He stared at her, and she watched the understanding arrive in stages—the way dawn comes up, grey first, then colour. "They're watching the circle."

"They're watching everything. Go."

He went. At the door he paused and looked back at her, and for a moment he was not a boy but a man asking a question he didn't yet know how to frame. Then he was gone down the corridor, footsteps quick on the parquet.

* * *

That evening, after the duller-than-usual letter to Frau Eisner had been sealed and set aside for the morning post, Vesper climbed to her attic room and locked the door behind her—an old habit, meaningless against anything but habituation—and sat on the edge of her narrow bed in the dark.

She made herself think it through, the way she made herself do everything unpleasant: thoroughly, once, without flinching.

Reeve knew the bookshop. He knew the Thursdays. He knew the dock fire was no lamp. He had a folder with her mistress's correspondence itemised like an inventory, and he had looked at her hands, and he had tested her with a lie about accents and written down whatever answer she gave.

Somewhere in Whitehall, or soon enough, there was a paper with her name on it. Not *Yarrow*—he would check, and *Yarrow* would dissolve like sugar in rain—but her real one. And attached to it, in some clerk's copperplate, the fact that had ended her old life: a father tried at the Old Bailey in the spring of '87, convicted of sedition and conspiracy, hanged at Wandsworth while his nineteen-year-old daughter stood in the crowd outside the walls with her hat pulled low, listening to the bell.

They had changed her name in the newspapers after the trial. *Daughter of the traitor Marchetti, said to have fled abroad.* She had not fled abroad. She had walked north across London with her mother's wedding ring sewn into her hem and taken a position answering advertisements under a name invented on a park bench, and she had been lucky—lucky beyond deserving—until Edith Halloway hired her without references and never asked for any, and Vesper had spent twelve years repaying that mercy with silence, with service, with the slow daily labour of being nobody.

She had believed the debt paid. She had believed the past buried deep enough.

Now a man with quiet boots and colourless eyes had sat in the drawing room drinking tea and shown her, courteously, the exact size of the shovel he intended to dig with.

*Trails,* he had said. *Some of those trails run through drawing rooms considerably finer than this one.*

And he had looked at her when he said it.

Vesper lay back on the bed and stared at the ceiling beams, and the fear came at last—not the sharp fear of the moment, which she knew how to manage, but the slow deep fear underneath, the fear that had slept for twelve years and now turned over and opened one eye.

Because there was a second sealed letter in her trunk upstairs, the one with nothing in it but a date—December 30th—and she had told herself it was a prank, a crank, a coincidence.

But Reeve had mentioned the docks. Reeve had mentioned the circle. Reeve had sat in the Halloways' drawing room and named, one by one, every thread that led to this house.

Someone was pulling those threads. Someone wanted the Halloways tangled in them. And whoever it was had chosen December 30th, and had sent a card from Whitehall, and knew—or guessed—that the quiet woman who read the mail would be the first to know.

She got up, lit her candle, and unlocked the trunk.

Tomorrow she would begin checking. Tomorrow she would find out what Callum Reeve already knew about a dead man named Marchetti, and how much of it was true.

Tonight she simply sat with the trunk open, her father's name rising in her throat like floodwater, and did not let herself cry.
