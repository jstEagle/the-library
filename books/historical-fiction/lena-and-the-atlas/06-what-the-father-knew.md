## 6. What the Father Knew

The trunk had not been opened in twelve years. It sat in the corner of the cellar beneath the Halloway house on Cheyne Row, under a shelf of Edith's late mother-in-law's preserved quinces and a broken garden roller, wrapped in sacking that had once been grey and was now the colour of old teeth. I had put it there myself, in my first week of service, when I still flinched at the sound of doors closing behind me.

I had sworn an oath over it—not aloud, not formally, but the kind of oath a nineteen-year-old makes with her whole body, the kind that sets into bone like weather into timber. *Never again.* Never again would I read what he wrote; never again would I carry his words in my mouth for other men to judge; never again would I let Nathaniel Yarrow's hand reach up out of the ground and take hold of his daughter.

For twelve years the oath had held. For twelve years I had gone down into the cellar perhaps twice a year, to fetch preserves or lamp oil or the spare coal scuttle, and each time my eyes had passed over the sacking-wrapped shape without stopping, the way one passes a grave one has decided not to mourn at.

Tonight I knelt in front of it with a candle in one hand and both letters in my breast pocket, and my hands were shaking so badly that the first knot defeated me entirely.

* * *

It had begun, as these things do, with something small.

I had spent the afternoon in the morning room with the household accounts, because Edith liked them kept in a fair hand and because fair hands were the only inheritance my father had left me that could not be confiscated. The fire had burned low. Rain had come against the windows in the desultory way of November rain, more mist than water, and the light in the room had gone the colour of pewter. And I had taken out the second letter—the one naming the Halloways in connection with the Admiralty docks—and laid it beside the first, the one containing nothing but *December 30th*, and I had done what I had been doing since I was nine years old and my father first set me to sorting his correspondence: I had read the hand itself, apart from the words.

A hand is a face. It cannot lie about everything. My father's writing had been narrow and upright, slanted forward like a man leaning into wind, with a peculiar habit of doubling his t-crosses—one stroke laid over another, quick, as if impatient even with his own letters. He used to say it saved time. He used to say a great many things about saving time, most of which were excuses for carelessness.

The anonymous letters were narrow and upright. They leaned forward like men leaning into wind. And every t was crossed twice—once with a hesitation, once with a flourish—as if the writer had begun the stroke, stopped, and then committed himself to it anyway.

I told myself it meant nothing. Thousands of hands in London were narrow and upright; the schools taught slant the way they taught catechism. A doubled cross-stroke was a mannerism, a fashion, a trick of the pen nib. I told myself this while I carried the letters upstairs and locked them in my room, and then I went down to the kitchen and made bread that did not need making, kneading dough until my shoulders burned, because my hands required occupation or they would go looking for the trunk on their own.

Mrs. Pell found flour on my cheekbone at supper and said I looked peaky. Tobias asked whether I was well. Constance watched me across the table with those grey eyes of hers that missed nothing and remarked on less, and I smiled the smile I keep for occasions of exactly this kind—the smile of a woman who is perfectly well and has always been perfectly well—and Constance looked away, and I knew she had filed my face away somewhere, the way she filed everything, to be examined later by lamplight.

At eleven o'clock, when the house had settled into its night noises—Edmund's door clicking shut, the last creak of Tobias pacing his attic, the tick of the hall clock counting out hours nobody wanted—I took the cellar candlestick down from its hook and went below.

* * *

The knot surrendered on the third attempt. The sacking came away in a sheet of dust and dead spiders, and there it was: a sea chest, iron-banded, my father's initials burned into the lid by his own hand on some long-vanished evening in some long-vanished kitchen. N.Y. The letters looked small now. Everything about him looked smaller than it had.

I set the candle on the floor and lifted the lid, and the smell rose up out of the chest and took me by the throat.

Not decay—nothing so simple. Camphor and old paper and pipe tobacco, Ghost of Kensington Mixture, the smell of the parlour in Bermondsey where I had grown up among pamphlets and printing ink and men who lowered their voices when they spoke of the Republic. It is a strange cruelty of memory that it keeps its strongest hold in the nose. Twelve years fell away from me all at once, and I was nine years old again, sitting under the table while the men argued above me about the Coercion Acts, and my father's boot rested an inch from my knee, and I was safe, and I was loved, and I did not yet know that love of that kind ends with a rope and a crowd and a newspaper headline your daughter will read standing in a shop doorway with her collar up so no one sees her cry.

I made myself breathe. I made myself lift out the first layer.

Clothing first—a corduroy coat gone stiff with age, a muffler, a cap with a badge from some trade congress whose name had dissolved into history. Then papers, tied in bundles with string gone brittle: subscription lists for a workers' reading society, minutes of meetings, a half-finished essay on the land question with a coffee ring stamped across the third page like a seal. His voice was everywhere. Not the words—I couldn't bear to read the words—but the *hand* of it, the narrow upright lean, the doubled t-crosses marching down every page like fence posts.

I sat back on my heels in the cold and let the candle flame stand very still, and I understood that I had come down here not to find something but to find it out. There is a difference. One is a search. The other is a verdict you already fear.

The anonymous letters were in my breast pocket. I took them out and unfolded them on top of the essay—the Admiralty letter, then the date-letter beneath it—and I set them side by side with his handwriting, and I held the candle close, and I looked.

* * *

There are moments when the mind, offered two terrible doors, walks calmly through one while insisting to itself that the other remains open. I stood before both doors for a long time.

Door the first: my father was alive. That Nathaniel Yarrow, hanged on the eighth of March, 1887, before a crowd that included his daughter in a borrowed black coat, had somehow not died at all—that the drop had failed, or the body had been another man's, or the whole apparatus of trial and sentence and burial had been theatre staged for reasons beyond my knowing—and that he had spent twelve years in whatever shadow such men inhabit, and had now chosen, of all the houses in England, to write threatening letters to mine.

I tested it the way one tests a rotten rail, putting weight on it gently. He would be sixty-one. The hand in the letters was steady, unhurried, strong. Grief makes us capable of believing anything, and for nearly a minute I believed it—felt it bloom in my chest like heat, horrible and warm, *he is alive, Papa is alive*—and then I remembered the last time I had seen him, through the prison glass, how thin his wrists had gone, how the warders had had to steady him on the stairs. Men do not come back from the rope. The state is inefficient in many things but not in that one.

So: door the second. Someone was forging him.

Someone who had access to his handwriting. Someone who had studied it closely enough to reproduce not merely its general character but its particular vices—the doubled crosses, yes, but also the way his capital D closed into a loop, the odd flat-topped 3, the slight upward drift at the end of every line as though the sentences themselves were trying to escape the page. These were not things a forger invents from imagination. These were things a forger learns from specimens. From letters. From documents.

And here the cold arrived, the true cold, the cold that starts in the understanding rather than the skin.

Because who in the world had access to Nathaniel Yarrow's correspondence?

His daughter had. His daughter, who after his death had gathered up what the police had not confiscated—who had carried it out of Bermondsey in a carpet bag, who had buried it in a stranger's cellar, who had spent twelve years filing and sorting and steaming open other people's letters in the house above it. If any collection of Nathaniel Yarrow's hand existed outside a government archive, it existed in this trunk, in this cellar, under this roof.

Which meant one of two things. Either someone had come down into this cellar—past Mrs. Pell's domain, past Edmund's locked study overhead, past every lock and habit of mine—and taken specimens from the chest without disturbing the sacking I had folded back with my own hands not ten minutes ago.

Or the forger had seen the hand somewhere else. Somewhere public. Somewhere official. Somewhere like a trial, where evidence is read aloud and passed along the bench and copied by clerks, and where a man's letters to his comrades—letters his own daughter had sorted and addressed, letters written in the hand now staring up at me from the Admiralty letter—might have entered the record of the Crown against Nathaniel Yarrow, and thence the possession of anyone curious enough to ask.

Anyone, for instance, from Special Branch.

Inspector Reeve's face rose in front of me unbidden: the pale eyes, the too-long pause before he accepted his tea, the moment his gaze had crossed mine and stayed a half-second past politeness, the way a man's fingers stay on a book spine a half-second past browsing. *You have been with this family a long time, Miss—*

He had left the title hanging. He had left it hanging deliberately, and I had supplied *Yarrow* without thinking, because that is the name I wear, the name I gave the Halloways' late housekeeper as reference and the parish register as fact, and only afterward, lying awake, had I understood the shape of the trap: that he had not needed my name at all. He had needed to know whether I would give it smoothly. And I had given it smoothly, and he had smiled, and I had thought at the time: *a courteous man.*

Now, kneeling in the cellar with my father's ghost spread out on the flagstones, I thought instead: *a patient one.*

* * *

I do not know how long I knelt there. Long enough for the candle to gutter twice and need trimming. Long enough for the cold to climb through my skirts and settle into my knees like a tenant.

I should have repacked the chest. I should have restored the sacking, snuffed the candle, climbed the stairs, and locked the knowledge away in the same iron room where I kept everything else—the opened letters, the withheld ones, the date that sat in my pocket like a splinter working toward the heart. Twelve years of practice said: *bury it, carry it, say nothing.* Twelve years of practice had kept me alive and kept me employed and kept the people I loved from ever having to know what their driver did with her evenings.

But grief does not consult practice. Grief came up out of that chest with the camphor-smell and put its arms around me, and for the first time since March of 1887 I let it.

I wept the way children weep—ugly, graceless, with my fist pressed against my mouth so the sound would not travel up through the floorboards into the house where the family slept, the family whose name was now written in my father's hand on paper I had carried in secret for weeks. I wept for the man under the table in Bermondsey with his boot near my knee. I wept for the girl in the shop doorway. I wept most of all, I think, for the sheer insolence of it—that someone had reached down twelve years and dug him up and made him write threats, had dressed their malice in my father's skin, and had done it *here*, in the one place I had built to be safe from him.

And somewhere in the middle of the weeping, between one breath and the next, the grief turned over and showed me its other face, which is fury.

Because whoever had forged those letters had counted on exactly this. Had counted on the hand being recognized—not consciously, perhaps, but in the deep place where recognition lives. The letters were not merely accusations. They were bait. *December 30th.* The Halloways' name. A dockyard and dynamite. Every line of them engineered to draw a particular kind of attention to a particular house—and to flush out anyone in that house with reason to recognize the hand and tremble.

They had known about the trunk. Or they had known about the trial records. Either way, they had known about *me*.

I wiped my face with the heel of my hand until it stung. I folded the letters away into my breast pocket, over my heart, where they had lived too long already. I bundled the clothing back, retied the string on the papers—the essay on the land question I allowed myself one last look at, the coffee ring, the drifting lines, *the earth belongs to those who work it,* and beneath it his signature, N. Yarrow, upright and leaning into wind—and I closed the lid on him a second time.

"I'm sorry," I whispered, to the chest, to the cellar, to the dark. "Someone is wearing you. And I am going to find out who."

The oath lay broken around me like the sacking. Let it lie. Oaths are for keeping the past out; the past had come in through the post.

I climbed the stairs and blew out the candle at the top, and stood a moment in the black of the stairwell listening to the house breathe above me. Edith sleeping with her spectacles on the bedside table. Constance sleeping badly, as she had slept badly for a fortnight. Tobias dreaming whatever restless boys dream. Edmund—

Edmund's light had been burning when I went down. I checked the crack beneath the study door now, on my way past, silent as smoke.

Dark. He had gone to bed.

But the smell of pipe smoke hung faintly in the corridor, fresh, and Ghost of Kensington Mixture was a blend I had not smelled in twelve years except in the bottom of a sea chest—and I stood in that dark corridor with my heart going like a trapped bird, understanding that the house had just asked me a question I did not yet know how to answer.
