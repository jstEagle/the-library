## 7. Constance's Bargain

The letter had come on a Tuesday, folded inside the *Clarion*'s weekly edition, and for three days Constance Halloway had carried it against her body like a coal that would not cool.

She had burned the envelope in her bedroom grate—carefully, feeding it to the flame a corner at a time so the ash fell whole—and kept only the inner page, which she now read again by lamplight while the house settled around her into its evening noises: her father's cough through two walls, the clock on the landing, Vesper's footsteps passing below with the particular softness of a woman who had spent twelve years learning not to be heard.

*Miss Halloway,—Your mother's committee is admired. Your discretion is known. The arrangement can continue, but the rate must change. £3 per item, and I will want names as well as dates. Mr. Fenn.*

Three pounds. It was extortion dressed as journalism, and she had signed herself into it with her own hand, and there was no one in the world she could tell.

Except, perhaps, the woman below stairs who already knew everything and said nothing.

Constance pressed the letter flat between the pages of Shelley and sat a long moment listening to her own pulse. Then she pinched out the lamp.

* * *

She found Vesper where she always found her after ten o'clock: the small kitchen, the banked range, the table wiped down to bare wood. Vesper sat mending one of Tobias's shirts, needle catching the light, and did not look up when Constance came in, though of course she knew—the whole house knew—whose step it was.

"You'll ruin your eyes," Constance said.

"I'll ruin them either way. Candles cost." Vesper set the shirt down. Whatever was in Constance's face made her go still, the way animals go still. "What is it?"

"I need to tell you something, and then I need you to help me bury it."

Vesper's hands folded together on the table. "That's rather a lot to ask before midnight."

"It's rather a lot to carry alone any hour of the day." Constance pulled out the chair opposite. The kitchen smelled of tallow and yesterday's bread and the faint iron tang of the range, and it struck her, not for the first time, that this room had been more honest with her than any drawing room she'd ever kept. "Do you remember Mrs. Pankhurst's rally in March? When we couldn't pay the printers?"

"I remember your mother selling her garnets."

"My mother sold her garnets because I told her the funds were sounder than they were. Because they weren't sound at all. Because I—" Constance stopped, started again, hating how rehearsed the confession sounded even though she had never once said it aloud. "I've been selling things to the papers. Not stories about us. Dates. Attendance figures. Which MPs wavered after which meeting. Little facts, harmless ones, I told myself. To a man named Fenn at the *Clarion*. He paid well, and the payments went to the printing fund, and Mother's movement didn't starve, and no one was hurt."

Vesper's face did not move. That was the thing about Vesper that had unsettled Constance for years and comforted her more lately: nothing crossed it uninvited.

"How long?" Vesper asked.

"Two years."

"And he's raised his price."

It wasn't a question, and Constance felt the floor tilt under it. "You know?"

"I read the post." Vesper said it plainly, without apology, as if reporting weather. "I have for twelve years. I don't read yours often—you're careful—but I read enough to know you burn some envelopes before anyone else sees them."

The kitchen was very quiet. Constance discovered she wasn't shocked. She should have been—twelve years of opened letters, their family's correspondence handled nightly by a servant's ink-stained fingers—and instead what she felt was something closer to recognition, the way one recognizes a face across a crowded station. Of course. Of course it was Vesper. Who else in this house actually worked?

"Then you know why I can't tell my mother," Constance said. "If Edith learns the campaign's been funded by leaked information—if she learns *I*—" Her voice caught on the name, which happened sometimes; her mother had become an institution so vast that using the Christian name felt like blasphemy even indoors. "She'd never forgive me. Worse, she'd forgive me, and spend the rest of her life trying to make it mean something, and I couldn't bear to be her lesson."

"And Fenn? What does he want now?"

Constance slid the letter from the Shelley and laid it on the table between them, face up, like a card she'd already lost with.

Vesper read it twice. Her finger hovered over *the rate must change* and then over *names as well as dates*, and Constance watched her arrive at the same arithmetic: information was a currency, and currencies attracted thieves.

"He's not squeezing you for money," Vesper said slowly. "Money's just the lever. He wants access. Names." She looked up, and for the first time since Constance had known her, Vesper looked—not frightened, exactly. Alerted. A deer hearing a sound too far off to name. "Who else knows about this channel?"

"No one. You're the first."

"That's not quite what I asked." Vesper pushed back from the table and stood, restless, one hand flat on the counter as if steadying the house itself. "A channel works both ways. If you've been passing him household facts for two years—dates, movements, who attends what—then someone else could pass things *through* you to him. Or use the trail you've left. Paper leaves trails, Miss Constance. Ink leaves trails."

"Vesper." Constance laughed, short and disbelieving. "You talk as though the house were under siege."

"Don't you feel it?" Vesper turned. The lamp threw her shadow enormous up the pantry wall. "Your father's doctors writing more urgently. Your mother's meetings drawing men who take notes and don't applaud. Tobias coming home smelling of smoke and secrets. And last week—" she paused, weighing something, visibly deciding how much of her own hand to show— "last week a letter came for your mother with nothing in it but a date. December the thirtieth. Nothing else. Someone is counting days in this house, and counting them *toward* something, and I do not like the sum."

Cold moved down Constance's spine, unhurried, thorough. "Why didn't you say anything?"

"Because saying things makes them real, and because I hoped I was wrong. I am rarely wrong about letters." She came back to the chair but did not sit. "Tell me exactly what you gave Fenn. Everything. Dates of meetings, yes—but what else? Times the house was empty? Travel plans?"

Constance searched backward through two years of small betrayals and found, to her horror, that the inventory was longer than she'd allowed herself to keep. "Meeting attendance. Subscription shortfalls. Once—a list of which committee members had withdrawn their donations, with amounts. And travel. When Mama spoke in Manchester. When Edmund was in Edinburgh. When Papa was admitted to the sanatorium in Brighton." She heard her own voice flatten with dread. "Vesper, I gave him our comings and goings for two years. I handed a stranger the shape of this family's days."

"Yes," Vesper said quietly. "You did."

There was no cruelty in it. That was somehow worse.

* * *

They talked until the coals went grey.

It was Vesper who built the plan, piece by piece, in her flat unornamented way, and Constance sat and marveled—as she had marveled a hundred times in twelve years—at how much intelligence the world wasted on people it refused to see. Vesper proposed that the payments continue, once, at the new rate, with a single harmless item attached: the date of a meeting already public. A thread left dangling to reassure Fenn that the channel lived, while Constance began, item by item, to feed it dry. Meanwhile Vesper would open every incoming letter addressed in Fenn's hand—or claiming to be from the *Clarion*—before it reached the hall tray, and anything that smelled of leverage, of threat, of *names*, would be diverted or destroyed before it could be used.

"You'd lie to my mother," Constance said. "Daily. Again."

"I've lied to her daily for twelve years," Vesper said. "One more habit won't break me." But something crossed her face when she said it—quick, gone, like a fish under ice—and Constance, watching, understood that she had seen something true and would never learn what.

"Why?" Constance asked instead. It was the question beneath all the others. "You owe this family nothing. Less than nothing—you sweep our floors and mend our shirts and steer us through London like a cabhorse, and we pay you thirty shillings a month. Why risk your place for my stupidity?"

Vesper considered the question with the seriousness she gave everything, turning the mended shirt over in her lap, smoothing a seam.

"Because I know what it is to fund a cause with things you shouldn't have to sell," she said at last. "And because if this goes wrong, it won't be you they come for first. It'll be whoever they can reach easiest. Servants are reachable, Miss Constance. We're doors. Everyone walks through us and no one remembers the door." She threaded the needle again, and the light caught her hands—capable, scarred at the knuckle, older than her years. "Go up. Sleep. Tomorrow you write to Fenn accepting his terms, and you write it in a hand slightly worse than your own, and you give me the draft first."

Constance stood. At the door she paused, hand on the cold latch, and looked back at the woman at the table in the dying light.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me yet," Vesper said. "Thank me in January, if we're both still here."

* * *

But sleep did not come, and Constance lay in the dark cataloguing the shape of her own trap, and somewhere past two in the morning she understood the thing she had been refusing to understand all evening.

Fenn had not raised his price arbitrarily. Men like Fenn did nothing arbitrarily. He had raised it because someone had told him the channel was worth more now—because someone had told him the Halloways were worth more now, worth *names*, worth pressure, worth whatever was being counted toward December the thirtieth. Her little pipe of gossip had been mapped by another hand entirely, and that hand was not finished drawing.

She rose, lit a candle, and stood a long time looking at the street below, black and empty, the gaslamp at the corner hissing its small eternal complaint.

Then she saw it: a figure crossing toward the house—not hurrying, not skulking, walking with the deliberate pace of a man delivering something—and stop at the front steps. An envelope went through the brass slot. The figure waited, precisely, until she heard the soft slap of paper on marble inside the hall. Then it turned and walked away without once looking back, and Constance, pressing her forehead to the cold glass, realized two things at once.

First: it was nearly three in the morning, and deliveries did not come at three.

Second: she had recognized the walk. Broad shoulders, slight favoring of the left leg, hands clasped behind the back like a man inspecting a property he already owned.

It was Edmund.

Downstairs, faint through the floors, she heard Vesper's door open, and bare feet on the kitchen stairs, going toward the hall tray, going toward the letter, and Constance stood in the dark with her candle guttering, knowing that whatever was written in that envelope would reach Vesper's hands first—and knowing, with a certainty that felt like falling, that she had just helped build the channel through which her own family would be undone.
