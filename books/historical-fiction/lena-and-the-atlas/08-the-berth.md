## 8. The Berth

The shipping office on Wapping High Street smelled of tar, wet wool, and other people's departures. Vesper had been coming there for twelve years—first with Edith's letters of introduction for friends emigrating to the Cape, later alone, on small errands Edith never asked about and never needed to. The clerk behind the counter was named Silas Fenwick, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the patient eyes of someone who spent his life watching others leave while he stayed.

She had told herself the visit was routine. That was the first lie of the morning, and she made it carefully, the way she did all her lies now: dressed in plain serviceable grey, hands folded, face arranged into the mild blankness of a woman carrying nothing more interesting than laundry lists.

"Miss—" Silas began, then caught himself, as he always did. He had never learned what to call her. *Vesper* was too familiar; *miss* presumed a gentility neither of them believed in. They had settled, over the years, into a comfortable absence of address altogether.

"Silas." She set her gloved hand on the counter. "I need to know about sailings. End of December. Transatlantic."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "Going somewhere warm?"

"I'm asking for a friend."

"The friend being you."

"The friend being hypothetical."

He almost smiled. In another life—one where she had not spent twelve years learning to be furniture—they might have been friends properly, might have shared suppers and grievances and the small honest currency of people who had nothing to hide from each other. Instead they had this: his ledger books, her careful questions, and a mutual agreement never to look too closely at what passed between them.

"Cunard's out," he said, turning the great ledger toward himself. "White Star's full till February—it's the season, everyone with money wants to see the century in on the other side of it. What's your budget?"

"Modest."

"Steerage, then." He ran a cracked thumbnail down the columns. "There's the *Bavaria*, Hamburg line, sails the twenty-eighth—but she's a cattle boat with pretensions, you'd smell the hold before you saw the berth. There's the *City of Chester*, American line, January second—"

"What about the thirtieth?"

His thumbnail stopped.

It was a small thing, that stop. A hitch no longer than a heartbeat. But Vesper had built a career out of heartbeats—the pause before Edith opened a letter, the breath Tobias took before lying about where he'd been, the silence Constance left at the end of sentences when there was something folded inside them. She knew what a stopped thumbnail meant the way other women knew weather in their knees.

"Why do you ask about the thirtieth?" she said quietly.

"I didn't say there was anything."

"You didn't say there wasn't."

Silas closed the ledger. That was worse. Men like Silas did not close ledgers; ledgers were their spines.

"Vesper." He used her name deliberately, and hearing it aloud in this room felt suddenly obscene, like seeing a locked door standing open. "You should go home."

"I'm standing in a shipping office asking about sailings. I am home in every sense that matters today." She kept her voice level, but her heart had begun its slow climb toward her throat. "Tell me."

He looked past her shoulder at the office door, at the fog pressing against the glass, at the boy sweeping the step outside who was very interested in his broom and very clearly listening.

"Not here," Silas said. "Ten minutes. The Anchor. And Vesper—" he hesitated, and the hesitation frightened her more than anything the ledger had done. "Don't let anyone know you came here. Not even the family."

*The family.* As if they were already suspects. As if the word itself had grown teeth.

---

The Anchor at half past ten in the morning was nearly empty: two dockers arguing over wages, an old man asleep over his porter, the publican polishing glasses that were already clean. Silas took the corner table by the window where the light was bad and conversations died of natural causes. He ordered nothing. Neither did she. The publican looked at them both and wisely went back to his polishing.

"Well?" Vesper sat across from him, back straight, hands still. Twelve years of practice. Her pulse, underneath all that stillness, was a runaway horse.

"The *Aurelia*," Silas said. "Cunard-adjacent charter, Liverpool registry. Sails December thirtieth, high tide, 8:40 in the morning. New York direct."

"All right."

"One berth remains unsold. Second class. It has been unsold for three weeks."

"December is a hard month to fill a ship."

"This berth isn't for sale." He leaned forward, lowering his voice until she had to lean in too, until the space between them filled with the smell of beer and his nervousness. "It's held. Reserved under a name, paid in full, six weeks ago. Cash. The booking's marked private—no forwarding address, no agent listed. I only know because old Marsh who does the manifest asked me to check the spelling against the passenger list, and I remembered the name."

Vesper waited. The dockers' argument had ended; the sleeping man snored; the publican polished. Somewhere beyond the fog a ship's bell rang, flat and muffled, counting an hour that meant nothing.

"The name is Yarrow," Silas said.

The room tilted—not literally, the floorboards were sound enough, but something in her equilibrium went over like a chair kicked backward. She heard herself say, quite calmly, "That's my name," as though reporting a mistake at a dressmaker's. Wrong hem, wrong name, easily altered.

"I know whose name it is," Silas said. "That's why we're drinking tea in a pub instead of talking at my counter."

"Someone booked passage in my name. Six weeks ago. Paid cash."

"Yes."

"Six weeks ago," she said slowly, working it through, laying the facts end to end like bones on a table, "was the middle of November. Before the second letter. Before Reeve. Before any of—" She stopped herself. Silas didn't know about the letters. Silas didn't know about Special Branch. To him her life was linens and liveries and letters read by lamplight.

But six weeks ago. Six weeks ago she had steamed open nothing but Christmas cards and a bill from the fishmonger. Six weeks ago the plot—if plot it was—had already been moving beneath her feet, laid down like rails in the dark, and she had been walking along the top of it thinking the ground was solid.

"Could it be coincidence?" she asked. "Another Yarrow?"

"How many Yarrows do you know?"

"One," she said. "And she's sitting here."

"Passenger list says Miss Y. Yarrow." He watched her face as he said it, and whatever he saw there made him reach across and briefly cover her hand with his—a gesture so unlike him that it frightened her more than everything else combined. "Vesper. Someone expects you on that ship."

---

She walked afterward. Not toward Wapping, not toward Kensington, just—walked, through streets going grey with the shortening afternoon, past costermongers calling the last of their winter pears, past children dragging sledges over cobbled frost that wouldn't last the night. The fog thickened around her and she let it, grateful for the way it erased distances, made every street corner a possibility of concealment.

*Someone expects you on that ship.*

Six weeks. She turned the arithmetic over and over, the way one worries a loose tooth. Six weeks ago, who had known her name? Everyone in the household, certainly, but as *Vesper*—driver, letter-reader, fixture. *Yarrow* was the name on her references, the name she had given the Halloways' solicitor when Edith hired her, a name chosen at nineteen with a liar's instinct for plausibility. It was on no documents that mattered. It was, she had thought, a name she owned entirely because no one else had ever wanted it.

Except someone had wanted it. Someone had gone to a shipping office with cash and bought her escape before there was anything to escape *from*. Which meant—

Which meant the plot had never been aimed at the Halloways at all. Or rather: not *only* at them. The letters naming the family were the visible crime, the fire in the front parlor. But the berth on the *Aurelia* was the back stairs, the servant's passage, the route prepared for the guilty party's flight. Someone had built her a door out of the burning house and expected her to use it.

*Scapegoat.* The word arrived whole, cold, complete. Not witness. Not accomplice. Sacrifice. When the arrests came—and arrests were coming, Reeve's visits had seen to that certainty—there would be a tidy narrative waiting: the servant girl with the seditious father, the woman who handled the family's correspondence, the outsider with access to papers and post and the private rhythms of the household. Let the daughter of a hanged man take the blame. Let *Yarrow* vanish across the Atlantic on the thirtieth of December, and let the century begin with the case closed.

She stopped at the edge of the Embankment, where the Thames moved below her, brown and patient, carrying its cargo of barges and secrets toward the sea. The river would carry the *Aurelia* too, eventually, after Liverpool. Eight-forty in the morning on December thirtieth. Eleven days from now.

Eleven days to decide whether to run.

That was the vertigo of it—that was the thing that made her grip the stone balustrade until her fingers ached. Because some traitorous part of her, some animal part that wanted only to live, looked at the held berth and felt not horror but *relief*. A door, open. A ship, provisioned. A name, reserved. All she had to do was walk through and keep walking, and the fog would close behind her, and Vesper—whoever Vesper was, whoever she had pretended to be for twelve years—would be someone else's problem, someone else's history, dissolving into steerage smoke and salt air.

And the Halloways would burn without her.

Edith, standing in the dock with her chin up, ruined. Constance, arrested for a journalist's promises. Tobias, radical and gentle and utterly unequipped for the machinery closing around him, already the perfect second scapegoat if the first one fled. She could see it all with terrible clarity, the way one sees the whole of a fire from the street: the flight of the servant confirming the guilt of the house. Her escape would be the final piece of evidence. Someone had designed it that way. Her disappearance was not a mercy being offered her; it was a sentence waiting to be served.

Unless.

Unless she stood in the dock instead. Unless the berth sailed empty. Unless *Yarrow* stayed in London and became, for once, exactly what the name claimed to be—an arrow, pointed inward, at whoever had forged her father's hand and bought her coffin of a cabin.

Her father. She thought of him suddenly, wholly: the smell of his coat, ink and tobacco; the way he had read aloud to her from banned pamphlets as though they were fairy tales; the scaffold she had never seen but had imagined ten thousand times, always at dawn, always in winter. They had called him a traitor and killed him for words. And now someone was using his handwriting—his ghost, his stolen voice—to hang a new treason on a new generation, and had offered her the same choice he'd had, dressed up as a lifeboat: flee and be guilty, or stay and be caught.

He had stayed. Look what staying had bought him.

She turned from the river. The fog was closing in earnest now, swallowing the far bank, swallowing the lamps one by one, and somewhere inside that blindness London went on with its evening—cattle driven to slaughter, letters carried to houses, families sitting down to suppers they believed were safe.

Eleven days. One berth. One name that wasn't hers and had never belonged to anyone else.

She began to walk home through the darkening, rehearsing the lie she would tell Edith about where she had been—*errands, ma'am, the stationer's ran short of the good ink*—and it struck her, with a fresh and quieter horror, that she had not yet asked the question that mattered most.

Whoever booked that berth had paid cash, in person or by agent, six weeks ago. Six weeks ago, inside that house, someone had already chosen her. Had already studied her long enough to know her false name, her true desperation, her usefulness as a sacrifice. Had sat across the breakfast table from her, perhaps; had thanked her for the post, perhaps; had watched her steam open their letters at the kitchen table, perhaps, and smiled.

One of them had bought her a ticket out of the world.

And tomorrow she would have to pour their tea.
