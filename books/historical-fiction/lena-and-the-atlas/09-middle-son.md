## 9. Middle Son

Edmund Halloway had always believed that a house revealed its true character after midnight. By day, number fourteen Belgrave Row performed respectability the way an actor performs a role—lamplight in every window, the rattle of the tea trolley, his mother's voice carrying through the halls like a bell that would not stop ringing for a cause. But at two in the morning, when the gas had been turned down to blue pilot flames and the whole structure settled on its foundations with small wooden sighs, the house told the truth. And the truth, Edmund had come to understand, was that it was his.

Not legally, not yet. Legally it belonged to his father, whose lungs were failing by increments so slow and dignified that the doctors had stopped pretending to set a date. But Edmund did his arithmetic the way other men did their prayers: daily, and with conviction. The Halloway fortune—his grandfather's shipping money, salted away in consols and dock leases while Edith spent her allowances on pamphlets and lecture halls—would pass through his father's hands and into the hands of whoever could hold it. Constance wanted nothing but her mother's movement; she'd have signed the whole estate over to a suffrage trust within a year of inheriting. Tobias wanted nothing but to be forgiven for wanting something. That left Edmund.

He had done everything correctly. He had taken the position at Whitcombe & Marsh because his father's old friend held the partnership he would one day inherit. He had courted no one unsuitable, drunk no more than was expected of him, kept his opinions where opinions belonged—in the ledger books, where they compounded quietly. He was thirty-four years old, and he had never once made a spectacle of himself, which in that household was a form of sainthood.

And then someone had begun mailing anonymous letters, and the whole careful architecture of his inheritance had started to tremble.

It should have been simple. A few letters, some hysteria in the newspapers about the Admiralty docks, a police inspector sniffing around the hall—and his mother's movement, already ridiculed in the press, would be finished. He had not planned for Inspector Reeve to be thorough. He had not planned for the way suspicion behaved like water, finding every crack, pooling in rooms he had meant to keep dry.

So now there were documents to destroy.

He worked in the study by the light of a single candle, the shutters closed, his coat still on because taking it off would mean admitting he intended to stay, and staying admitted intention, and intention was the one thing he had never allowed this house to see. On the desk before him lay a folder of correspondence—not the forged letters themselves; those were long since dispatched through channels that left no trail back to him—but the raw material. Old letters from his father's files. Letters Vesper Yarrow had sorted and filed twelve years deep, letters in his father's hand from the years when his father had been young and angry and briefly, disastrously associated with men who threw bricks through Home Office windows.

Edmund had found them in October, doing an inventory of the study he'd already begun thinking of as his own. His father's youthful correspondence—furious, eloquent, treasonous-adjacent. And beside it, in the same drawer, the household stationery: cream-laid paper, watermarked with the Halloway monogram, bought in reams from the same stationer his mother had used for twenty years.

A man with a steady hand and patience could learn anything from such letters. Edmund had learned his own father.

The fire in the grate had burned down to a red eye. He fed the first letter to it, watching the paper curl—the ink going brown, then black, his father's young voice ascending the chimney as smoke. *They will call it sedition,* one line had read, dated 1868. *Let them. Sedition is only loyalty with its coat off.* His father had grown out of it, married, buttoned his coat forever. Edmund sometimes wondered if his father had ever regretted the buttoning, and decided it didn't matter. What mattered was that the letters existed, and existence was evidence.

Page by page he fed them in. He was methodical about it—one sheet at a time, never enough at once to flare or float ash up the flue. This was the discipline no one credited him with. Tobias got credit for passion. Constance got credit for principle. Edmund got credit for nothing, because nothing was what he showed, and showing nothing was the hardest work of all.

He was three sheets from finished when the floorboard in the hall spoke.

It was a small sound—a single soft complaint of wood under weight, the kind of sound a house makes even when empty. But Edmund had lived in this house for thirty-four years, and he knew its vocabulary, and he knew that board did not speak on its own.

He did not turn around. Turning around was admission. He simply closed the folder with his left hand, let the last unburned pages slide into the drawer beneath the blotter, and sat very still, watching the fire eat what he had already given it. Ash settled on the fender like grey snow.

*Whoever is out there,* he thought, *has been out there long enough to hear nothing incriminating. Long enough to have heard nothing but a man burning correspondence at two in the morning, which half of London does.*

Except that half of London did not do it by shuttered candlelight, wearing its coat, sweating.

He waited. Ten seconds. Twenty. The house breathed. Somewhere upstairs his father coughed, once, the wet rattle that had become the metronome of their lives. And then the sound came again—footsteps moving off down the hall, unhurried, almost silent, toward the servants' stair.

Edmund exhaled slowly and looked at the fire. Whoever it was—Vesper, most likely; the woman heard everything and appeared to be nowhere—had seen him burning papers at two in the morning and had said nothing. Had walked away.

That was worse than being caught. Being caught would have required a conversation, and conversations could be managed, redirected, lied to. But silence was data. Silence meant she had seen something worth keeping. And Edmund Halloway had spent a lifetime studying people who collected things quietly—his mother collected grievances, his sister collected causes—and he knew exactly what a silent woman with a secret could cost him.

He put out the candle. He would deal with Miss Yarrow tomorrow. There were ways of testing what a servant knew; there always had been, since servants had existed.

In the dark study, the fire died to embers, and Edmund sat with his hands folded, thinking about arithmetic.

* * *

I heard the floorboard give me away before I ever heard him.

I had not intended to spy—I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger God keeps for women who open other people's letters. I had woken at half past one with Reeve's visit sitting on my chest like a cat that wouldn't move, and I had come downstairs for the brandy I don't drink, and then I had heard, through the service door, the small domestic sounds of a fire being fed. Paper. At two in the morning, in a house that Special Branch had already visited once.

So yes. I stood in the dark hall with my back against the cold wallpaper and I watched Edmund Halloway burn his father's letters, and I said nothing, and I walked away, and both of those choices are going to cost somebody dearly.

Here is what I know about Edmund, gathered across twelve years of being furniture: he is the only one of the three who has never once forgotten I'm in the room. Constance talks past me. Tobias talks *to* me, which is its own kind of blindness—he mistakes kindness for equality. But Edmund sees me the way a card player sees the deck. He notices when I've moved. He has never once tipped me, never once spoken carelessly in my hearing, and twice—twice in twelve years—I have caught him watching me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere.

So when he froze at that floorboard, I understood two things at once. First: he knew I was there. Second: he had arranged himself so fast, so smoothly, that the arrangement itself was the confession. Innocent men startle. Guilty men *compose*.

But here is what I couldn't make fit, standing in that cold hall with my heart going like a clock running fast: what he burned wasn't evidence. I've seen the anonymous letters—I've steamed enough envelopes in my time to know them intimately, the slant of the hand, the pressure of the nib, that peculiar way the writer crossed his *t*s like a man slashing a debt. Edmund's handwriting is cramped and vertical, a clerk's hand, a ledger's hand. Whatever he was destroying tonight, he wasn't destroying the letters that are going to hang his family.

He was destroying his *father's* letters. I recognized the hand on the page before it curled—even upside down, even by firelight, I know that handwriting better than anyone alive, because I spent eleven years filing it and four months comparing it against a forgery. Those were the letters I had catalogued myself, in my first year here, and shelved in the study drawer, and thought no more about, because grief was a luxury the staff didn't carry.

Which means Edmund knows about the letters. Which means Edmund has *read* the letters. Which means Edmund has spent the autumn learning his dead-and-dying father's treasonous youth by heart—for what purpose, if not the one I'm afraid of?

I climbed the servants' stairs slowly, letting each step announce me, because a guilty household runs quiet and I needed mine to run loud. Outside my door, the corridor smelled of coal smoke and cold plaster. Inside, I lit no lamp. I stood in the dark and did the thing I do instead of praying, which is to lay the facts out on a table and look at them until they confess.

Fact: someone in this house wrote—or had written—the letters naming the Halloways.
Fact: the forged hand is my father's, or a copy of it, which is impossible unless—
No. Not that. Not yet. One horror per night; I ration them like sugar.

Fact: Edmund burns documents at two in the morning and composes himself like a man closing a ledger.
Fact: Edmund benefits. If the family falls, if Mother's movement collapses in disgrace, the estate consolidates. Constance would scatter it to the cause. Tobias hasn't the spine to fight for a shilling. Edmund inherits everything intact, and respectable, and *avenged*—poor Edmund, whose family was slandered.

It fits. God help me, it fits like a key.

And yet.

Tobias attends a radical reading circle and tells everyone, cheerfully, dangerously, including me. Edmund attends nothing, says nothing, belongs to nothing—which is either innocence or craftsmanship. The plotters chose a patsy, and every instinct I own says the patsy is meant to be Tobias, the idealist with the membership card and no sense. You don't build a frame around the brother who keeps no records, joins nothing, and burns his tracks by candlelight.

You build it around the boy who signs his name to things.

Unless—unless the frame is double. Unless the plan is for Tobias to hang for the plot and Edmund to walk away clean, having tidied the family papers first. Unless the man feeding documents to the fire isn't cleaning up his crime but curating his alibi.

Or unless I am a woman who has spent twelve years reading other people's secrets and has finally begun finding her own in the blanks.

I sat on the edge of my narrow bed in the dark and listened to the house. Above me, Mr. Halloway coughed his slow metronome cough. Below, the study stayed dark. Somewhere between those floors, the truth was moving around like heat, rising through the walls, and I couldn't tell yet whether it would warm us or burn us down.

Tomorrow I would go back to the study drawer. Tomorrow I would count what remained and compare it against my own catalogue, written in 1887 in a hand I barely recognize as mine.

And tomorrow, I reminded myself, pulling the blanket to my chin like a child, Inspector Reeve might return, and Tobias had a meeting on Thursday, and Constance had gone pale at breakfast in a way I hadn't yet explained—

Tomorrow. Everything was tomorrow now. December was eating the days like a man eating through a wall.

Below me, very faintly, I heard the study door open and close. Edmund going up to bed, unhurried, a man with nothing on his conscience and everything on his mind.

And I lay awake until the house went truly silent, telling myself the wrong brother was the safe conclusion, and not quite believing it.
