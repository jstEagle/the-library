## 10. Reeve Returns

He came back on a Thursday, when the fog was the color of dishwater and the gas lamps along Cheyne Row burned like drowned candles all through the afternoon.

I saw him from the kitchen window before Mrs. Pell announced him—saw him pause at the iron gate, look up at the house with the unhurried attention of a man taking inventory, and then remove his hat as though he had all the time in the world. Inspector Callum Reeve did not hurry. Hurrying, I understood even then, was for men who feared being seen. Reeve wanted to be seen. He wanted to be remembered. He dressed like a solicitor in mourning and moved like a man who had learned early that a quiet step opened more doors than a loud one.

"Tell Miss Halloway," I said to Mrs. Pell, "that Mr. Reeve is here again."

"And you'll be wanting to bring the tea up yourself, miss, same as last time?"

Same as last time. She made it sound like habit. Perhaps it was becoming one.

"Yes," I said. "The good china."

* * *

Edith received him in the morning room because it was the smallest of the formal rooms and she was too shrewd to offer him comfort. He sat with his knees together and his hat on his knee and declined the tea I poured him, which told me more than anything he said that day. A man who refuses tea in an English house is a man who does not intend to stay long enough for courtesy to matter—or a man who does not trust what might be put in the cup. With Reeve, I suspected both.

"You've had no further correspondence of an unusual nature, Mrs. Halloway?"

"Nothing beyond bills and my aunt's opinions on the government, which are unusual only in their length."

He smiled at that—a thin, appreciative smile—and I hated how easily he could be liked when he chose to be. That was the danger of him. Not menace; charm held in reserve, like a weapon wrapped in oilcloth.

"And your son Tobias continues his studies?"

"Tobias reads everything and finishes nothing," Edith said pleasantly. "It is the privilege of the young."

"It is indeed." He turned his cup—I had given him one after all, and he had accepted it once Edith pressed it upon him, because refusing Edith Halloway twice required a boldness even Whitehall hadn't yet funded—"though I wonder if he's found time for his reading circle. There was some talk of it."

The room went very still, the way a forest goes still when something moves at its edge. Edith's teacup did not so much as tremble. Twelve years of watching her face had taught me that stillness was her armor, not her calm.

"A reading circle," she repeated. "Young men reading books. How alarming. Shall we expect the cavalry next?"

"We live in alarming times, madam. The century is ending badly. It tends to make people reach for ideas they don't fully understand." He set down the cup. "Thank you for your hospitality. I won't trouble you further today."

Today. The word sat in the air like smoke after he'd gone.

When the front door closed, Edith looked at me across the cold tea things, and for a moment neither of us performed anything at all.

"He knows about the circle," she said quietly.

"He suspects. There's a difference, and he's hoping we'll close it for him."

Her eyes came to mine and stayed there a moment longer than usual—searching, almost. Then she rose, straightened her skirts, and said, "Constance will want to know," and left me alone with the dishes and the sound of my own heart.

* * *

He caught me three days later. Or perhaps he had been waiting three days for the chance; with Reeve there was never any telling the difference between luck and design.

I had gone out on an errand for Edith—to the stationer's in Sloane Square, a fool's errand, since I could have sent the boy, but I wanted the walk, wanted ten minutes of being nobody in particular among the crowds. I was coming back along the Embankment with the parcel under my arm when I felt rather than heard him fall into step beside me, matching my pace without effort, as though we were old friends who happened to be walking the same way home.

"Miss Yarrow."

The name went through me like a needle finding a nerve. Not Vesper. Yarrow. He said it lightly, testingly, the way a man taps a wall to hear where the hollow places are.

"I don't believe we've been introduced," I said, not stopping.

"No. We haven't. That's rather the point." He glanced sideways at me. "You have a remarkable talent for not being introduced, Miss Yarrow. Twelve years in one household and not a single society page has ever mentioned you. Not a photograph. Not a christening, a wedding, a funeral. You drive the carriage, you read the letters, you pour the tea, and then you evaporate. It's quite extraordinary. I've met trained agents who weren't half so careful."

"There's nothing remarkable about being a servant, Inspector."

"Nothing at all," he agreed pleasantly. "Except that servants leave records. References. Wage books. Parish registers. And you—" he let the sentence hang, admiring it, "—you leave nothing. Do you know what that tells a man in my profession? It tells him that someone worked very hard to build that nothing. And people only work that hard to hide something worth hiding."

We walked on. The river slid past beneath the parapet, brown and patient, carrying its cargo of coal barges and secrets. My hands were steady on the parcel. I remember being proud of that, later. At the time I felt nothing except a terrible clarity, the way one feels in the instant before a fall—the world gone sharp and slow and lit from within.

"My father was a clerk," I said. "He died when I was young. My mother followed soon after. I came to London because there was nowhere else, and I took work where I could find it. If that requires effort to hide, Inspector, then the effort is England's, not mine."

"Your father was a clerk," Reeve repeated. He stopped walking, and so I had to stop, or run, and running would have confirmed everything. We stood by the parapet with the fog coming off the water and the gulls crying somewhere above us, invisible. "Silas Yarrow. Clerk. Convicted of sedition in the spring of '87, at the Old Bailey. Hanged at Pentonville that August. You'd have been seventeen."

The river kept moving. The gulls kept crying. Somewhere behind us a cab horse stamped and a driver swore at it, and all of ordinary London went on with its noise while my whole life stood still on the Embankment in the fog.

"You're mistaken," I heard myself say.

"I'm never mistaken about names. It's the one thing I'm good at." He took off his hat—not a flourish, just a man being honest for a moment—and held it against his chest, and I understood that this gesture was the most dangerous thing he had done yet, because it was kind. "I pulled the file myself. I read the trial transcript. Your father named no accomplices, refused counsel, and asked only that his daughter be told he died quickly. Which, for what it's worth, was true. The drop was clean. I checked."

"Why?" My voice came out low and strange, a voice I didn't recognize. "Why would you check?"

"Because I like to know what I'm looking at." He put his hat back on. "And now, Miss Yarrow, let me tell you what I see. I see a woman who has spent twelve years inside the most prominent suffragist household in Chelsea, reading its letters, hearing its conversations, knowing its debts and its quarrels and its secrets better than any of the Halloways know them themselves. I see a woman whose father died for exactly the kind of cause Edith Halloway preaches at her drawing-room meetings. And I see that woman saying nothing, doing nothing, belonging to nothing—except to them. It doesn't add up. Unless," and here he leaned slightly toward me, and the fog seemed to lean with him, "unless it adds up to loyalty. Real loyalty. The kind that can't be bought."

"Then why try to buy it?"

"Because everything can be bought. It's only a question of price." He smiled again, and this time there was no warmth in it at all. "Here is my price, and it's generous, so listen carefully. The Admiralty letter. The dock plot. Someone in that house is involved—someone is feeding information out of it, or letting it be planted there, and I don't much care which. Give me a name. Any name. Convince me. And in exchange: full immunity. For the Halloways, for the household—for you. Whatever your part in it, whatever your father's name costs you, it stops costing. New papers, if you want them. A new name, even. You seem talented at those."

The temptation arrived the way he must have known it would—not as a shout but as a whisper, reasonable and soft. Say Edmund's name. Say the word *suspicion*, hedge it, qualify it, let Special Branch do the rest. Save yourself. Save them all, maybe, if you chose rightly. Wasn't that what I had been doing for twelve years—choosing, silently, which letters to burn and which to deliver, playing Providence with other people's post? This was only the same work with higher stakes.

And underneath the whisper, another voice, colder: *he knows*. He knows what you are. He has carried your father's name around in his pocket like a coin, waiting to spend it. Every safe year, every quiet morning, every unremarkable Tuesday of your life has been balanced on his ignorance, and the balance is broken now, and it will never hold again.

If I did not give him a name, he would come back. He would keep coming back. And each time, the price would rise.

"I don't take bribes," I said.

"It's not a bribe. It's an arrangement. Bribes are for the guilty."

"Then it's worse than a bribe. It's a confession I haven't made." I turned to face him fully, and I watched him register the change in me—the servant's deference draining away, leaving whatever it was underneath, whatever my father's blood had put there. "You want me to inform on the family that fed me, clothed me, and never once asked me where I came from. You want me to sell twelve years of their trust for a piece of paper with a seal on it. So let me tell you what I see, Inspector, since you like to know what you're looking at. I see a man from Whitehall who can't find the anarchists, so he's decided to manufacture some out of a widow and her children. I see a government that hanged my father and called it justice and would hang me tomorrow if it saved them an afternoon's embarrassment. And I see that you knew who I was the moment you first sat in our morning room drinking our tea—and you came back anyway, again and again, not because you needed to, but because you were deciding whether I was worth the price of immunity. You've already priced me. Don't pretend otherwise."

For a long moment he said nothing. The fog thickened around us; a barge hooted somewhere out on the water, mournful and far away.

"You have your father's way of talking," he said finally. "I read his statement to the court. He was eloquent too. Right up until the end." He settled his hat more firmly. "The offer stands until midnight on the twenty-ninth. After that, Miss Yarrow, I stop offering and start taking. And I should warn you—I take broadly. Households. Reputations. Whole families, if that's what the case requires." He paused at the edge of the fog, a gray shape becoming grayer. "Think about whose name you'd rather I learn first. Yours, or theirs."

Then he was gone, and I stood alone on the Embankment with the parcel from the stationer's clutched against my chest like a shield, and the river ran on below me, dark and indifferent, carrying everything toward the sea.

* * *

I do not remember driving home. I remember the horses' backs, the wet shine of their coats, my own hands on the reins performing their office perfectly while the rest of me stood on that embankment still, arguing with a dead man's ghost. *Say a name. Save yourself.* My father had refused to name his accomplices. They had hanged him for it. And here was his daughter, offered the opposite bargain—name someone, anyone, and live—and discovering that the refusal was not courage but inheritance, something in the blood that would not let her hand sign the paper.

But my father had died alone. I would not. That was the difference. That was the trap of it. Every hour I withheld a name, Reeve tightened the circle around people who had done nothing but trust me—around Edith with her stillness, around Constance with her secrets, around Tobias, God help him, with his borrowed pamphlets and his open face.

By the time I turned the carriage into the mews behind Cheyne Row, the lamps were being lit and the fog had begun to lift, and through the study window I could see a figure bent over the desk, working late by lamplight.

Edmund.

Alone in the house, everyone else at dinner, and Edmund Halloway burning something in the grate—or so it looked, at this distance, at this angle, in this light. A small flame flickering against the window glass, and his shadow leaning over it, feeding it.

I sat in the darkening carriage with the reins slack in my hands and understood that Reeve's question had answered itself, or thought it had. *Whose name would you rather I learn first?*

Perhaps, after all, I would have one to give him.

I just had to be certain it was the right one.
