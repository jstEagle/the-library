## 13. The Forgery Confirmed

The house slept the way houses do when they have been pretending all day—noisily at first, then in guilty starts. A pipe ticked somewhere above my head. The fire in my room had burned down to a red eye that watched me work and judged nothing, which was more than I could say for myself.

I had laid everything out on the floorboards because the table was too small for what I intended to do, and tables are honest surfaces; floors are not. On the floor, things can be arranged like evidence or like a spread of tarot cards, depending on what you're asking them to tell you. I was asking for evidence. I was terrified I would get it.

Three letters. The first, from October, with its single word of prophecy: *December 30th*. The second, from November, naming the Halloways in connection with the Admiralty docks—the one I had hidden from Edith behind the loose brick in the scullery wall, where the house's older secrets lived. And the third, which had come only two days ago, addressed this time not to Edith but to *the mistress of the house*, as if whoever wrote it had wanted to be formal about his treachery.

Beside them, weighted down with the brass candlestick so they wouldn't shift, lay my father's papers.

I had gone down to the trunk at midnight, after the last lamp on the upper floor went dark—Edmund's lamp, always Edmund's, he kept scholar's hours though there was little scholarship in him—and I had knelt on the cold flagstones of my room with the trunk between my knees like a child with a drum. The lock was rusted but obedient. It had waited twelve years for me and bore no grudge.

Inside: the life of Thomas Yarrow, hanged man. A prayer book with the flyleaf torn out. A pawn ticket for a watch I never saw him wear. A bundle of letters tied in butcher's twine, most of them to a woman named Margaret who was not my mother—or who was, and whom he'd addressed so formally out of some habit of distance I couldn't now untangle. And beneath those, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, the thing I had actually come for: drafts. Speeches. Half-finished pamphlets. Pages covered in his hand.

I hadn't looked at them since I was nineteen. That was the whole point of the trunk. You don't open a grave twice unless you suspect the body has moved.

Now I knelt on my bedroom floor in my nightdress with my hair unbound and compared the dead man's handwriting to a living threat, and the candle guttered, and I made myself be methodical, because methodical was the only way I knew to keep my hands steady.

* * *

My father wrote the way water runs downhill—with economy, with a certain impatience, taking the shortest line available and cutting corners where the ground allowed. His *a* was a small closed loop with no flourish at all. His *t* crossed low and leftward, a hurried stroke, as though even crossing his t's he was already moving to the next word. He broke his *d*—never finished the bowl properly, left a gap where the letter ought to close, as if every *d* were a door left ajar. When I was a girl learning my letters at the kitchen table while he worked beside me, he told me: *Never close your letters completely, Vesper. Leave a way out.* He said it laughing. He said a great many things laughing that ended without laughter.

The anonymous letters were written in that same hand. Not similar—*the same*, down to the broken *d*, down to the low-crossing *t*, down to the particular way the descender of the *y* curled back toward the word above it like a hook wanting its fish. I laid the third letter over one of the old pamphlet pages and held them to the light, and the strokes sat on top of each other like a reflection in still water.

Twelve years ago I had stood in a crowd outside Newgate and heard the sentence read, and I had watched enough of the proceedings through a friend-of-a-friend's borrowed position in the public gallery to know that the hand that wrote sedition belonged to Thomas Yarrow beyond any magistrate's doubting. So either my father had not died—that was impossible; I had seen the burial notice, had paid, with money I still resented spending, for the cheap stone in the paupers' ground at Ilford—or someone had studied his writing long enough and closely enough to become him.

And there was only one place on earth where a person could study Thomas Yarrow's hand at leisure, page after page, year after year.

There was only one person who filed his correspondence, read his letters aloud when asked, copied his speeches into fair hand when his eyes tired, and handled his papers daily for twelve years without ever being seen to look at them.

Me.

The thought arrived the way cold water arrives—not all at once but in a slow, climbing line up the body, until it reaches the places you thought were warm. If the forger had learned my father's hand, he had learned it from materials that passed through my keeping. Every document in this house that carried my father's name had been catalogued, shelved, dusted, and occasionally—on Edith's instruction, for her memoirs—read aloud by me. I was not merely near the forgery. I was its probable library.

Unless. Unless someone else had access. Unless—

No. First things first. I made myself finish the comparison before I let the larger animal out of its cage.

Ink next. My father had used iron-gall, the cheap kind that browns as it ages, that eats through paper given decades. The anonymous letters were fresh—weeks old at most—but the ink had been doctored somehow, aged with something, tea perhaps, or smoke, because it had the right brown cast and none of the right bite. Under my magnifying glass—a jeweller's loupe I'd bought years ago for exactly this species of paranoia—the forged ink sat *on* the paper. Real ink sinks. This floated. Someone had written these letters recently and dressed them in an old man's colours, the way you'd put a dead soldier's coat on a scarecrow.

So: forgery. Confirmed. I let myself feel one full second of relief—that my father was not alive, that no ghost was writing across the Atlantic or from some Whitechapel garret, that the dead stayed dead and the trunk had lied to no one.

Then I turned the third letter over, because I am thorough, because thoroughness is the religion I converted to the day I learned that carelessness gets people hanged.

And the relief died.

* * *

Paper tells stories too. Most people never listen, but a woman who has spent twelve years steaming envelopes open learns the language of stock and watermark the way a midwife learns heartbeats—by touch, by weight, by the faint rattle of the fibres under the thumb.

The Halloway stationery came from Perkins & Vane on Cheapside. Edith had ordered it by the ream for twenty years; I knew it the way I knew the sound of her footsteps on the stairs. It had a particular quality—rag content, heavy, with a deckle edge that Perkins & Vane alone produced in that weight, and a watermark so discreet you needed to hold it to a flame to find it: a small anchor, tilted, half-swallowed by the chain pattern of the border. Tobias used to joke that the family posted its letters on little ships and didn't know it.

I held the third letter to the candle.

Through the paper, glowing amber, the anchor swam up out of the border chains. Tilted. Discreet. Perkins & Vane rag stock, heavy weight, deckle edge.

Household stationery.

Not bought. Not stolen from a shop. This paper had lived in the drawer of the study desk, or the writing stand in the morning room, or the tray in Edith's own boudoir. It had been within arm's reach of five people and one servant, and of those six, four were named Halloway, one was me, and one—

One was Edmund.

I set the letter down very carefully, as though it might go off. My hands had stopped pretending to be steady. I pressed them flat against the floorboards and felt the house through my palms—the great dumb sleeping mass of it, the pipes ticking, the banked coals settling, the wind finding the gap in the sash I kept meaning to have mended. Twelve years I had listened to this house breathe. I had memorised its coughs. And all that time, apparently, it had been harboring a forger the way a body harbors a fever—quietly, internally, in the warm dark.

*Home is where they have to take you in,* my father used to say, quoting something, laughing. But home is also where they can ruin you best, because home is where everyone knows which lies you'll believe.

* * *

Let me count the household, then, the way Reeve might count it, the way a magistrate would want it counted, and see who remains standing when the counting is done.

Edith. Sixty-eight years old, and I would stake my life—which is precisely what this amounted to—on her incapacity for this. She had spent fifty years fighting causes in the open, signing her name to things that got her jeered at railway platforms. A woman like that does not learn forgery; she doesn't need it. Her weapon has always been daylight.

The master. Ill past illness, into something gentler and more final. He could not climb the stairs without resting twice. His hands shook too much to write his own name legibly; I had watched him struggle over a birthday card for Constance in March and finally dictate it to me.

Constance. God, Constance. She had secrets—she'd shown me one of them, the journalist, the money funnelling to her mother's campaign through channels that made my teeth ache—and secrets make suspects. But Constance's sins were sins of loyalty, committed clumsily and at cost to herself. Forgers are careful. Constance had never once in thirty-one years managed to keep a surprise party a secret.

Tobias. Twenty-three, restless, attending reading circles whose names he wouldn't say aloud, coming home late with his collar smelling of tobacco and other men's arguments. Tobias was being *built into* this plot—I had known it since the window conversation, had felt it in my stomach like swallowed ice. He was the patsy, not the pen. The letters were meant to hang around his neck eventually, I was sure of it; a boy with radical friends and a famous suffragist mother was exactly the shape of scapegoat the Special Branch mind dreams of. But the hand that wrote the letters was not a young man's hand. Whoever wrote them had the patience to age ink, and patience is not among Tobias's gifts.

And me. Well. I knew where I was at midnight because I was kneeling on the floor comparing documents. Alibis don't come cleaner than that, though no one would ever hear it from me, since my alibi required confessing twelve years of crimes.

Which left Edmund.

Middle son. Thirty-four. Clever in the cold way, ambitious in the colder one, courteous to his mother with a courtesy that had always reminded me of a man checking the weather before deciding whether to carry an umbrella. He kept the family accounts. He had argued—politely, reasonably, in that voice he used for making other people feel unreasonable—that Edith's campaign was draining the estate, that the name Halloway was becoming synonymous with scandal, that discretion was worth more than principle. He had said all this at dinner, smiling, refilling the wine.

And three nights ago, I had seen him burning papers in the study after midnight.

I had told myself then that it was accounts. Debts. Something shabby but human—a man destroying evidence of losses he couldn't bear to show his mother. I had watched from the dark hall and said nothing, because saying something would have meant explaining what I was doing awake, and my silences are load-bearing; pull one out and the whole structure of my life comes down.

But accounts don't require forgery. Debts don't need a dead man's handwriting. What requires a forged letter naming your own family in an anarchist plot is a *motive*, and Edmund's motive had been sitting at that dinner table for years, smiling into the claret: discredit the campaign, frighten the matriarch, seize the purse-strings of a family too scandalised to resist. And if the plot should happen to catch up his foolish younger brother on the way—if Tobias's reading circle should supply the police with a convenient radical—well. Accidents of war. Edmund would attend the trial in a black armband and speak beautifully about the burden of being the responsible son.

The fire had gone out. I noticed this the way you notice a sound stopping. The candle was down to a thumbnail of itself. Somewhere below me, a door opened and closed—softly, carefully, the click of a handle turned rather than released. Footsteps crossed the hall below, unhurried, and paused, and crossed again.

I did not move. I did not breathe. In this house I had learned to be furniture, and furniture does not flinch.

The steps retreated. A stair complained on the second landing—always the fourth step, that one, I'd meant to tell Cook for a decade—and then silence, total, restored, false.

* * *

I sat on the floor until the candle drowned in its own wax, and I thought about what happens next, and what happens next is this: I cannot unknow it, and I cannot yet prove it, and between those two facts lies a country I have to cross alone.

Proof. I would need proof that would survive a magistrate's skepticism, because the moment I accuse Edmund Halloway of forging anarchist letters in his dead driver's hand, every question in the world lands on *me*. How do you know the dead man's hand, Miss Yarrow? Why were you comparing documents? Why have you been steaming open this family's post for twelve years? Inspector Reeve already knows my father's name—he told me so with that terrible gentleness of his, laying it on the tea table like a card he'd been saving. One accusation from me and he plays the rest of the hand: *sedition's daughter reads the mail.* I would hang in the newspapers if nowhere else, and worse, Edith would learn that the woman she trusted with her correspondence had been its thief all along.

Unless I confess first. Unless I walk into Edith's morning room tomorrow and put twelve years of opened letters on the table between us before Edmund can put anything anywhere. Unless I trade my invisibility—the thing I have guarded longer than I've guarded anything, longer than my name—for the chance to save them all from their own blood.

Or unless I go to Reeve, take his immunity, sell the family piece by piece, and keep breathing. There is always that door. It is always the best-lit one.

The house creaked above me—Edmund's room, or Constance's, or only the wind lying. Home. Crime scene. The words fit together now and could not be taken apart, the way you can't unring a bell or unread a letter, and I have built my whole life on unread letters.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow I would open the trunk in daylight. Tomorrow I would kneel before Edith Halloway and confess the smaller crime to stop the larger one, and whatever she did to me afterward—dismissal, fury, that worst of all punishments, pity—I would deserve it and pay it gladly, if paying it bought Tobias a future and Constance a clean name.

I blew out the candle. In the dark, the third letter lay on the floorboards with its little anchor glowing faintly in my memory, patient as a hook, and somewhere below me in the warm dark of the house he had tried to burn down, Edmund Halloway was sleeping the sleep of a man who believes no one is reading his mail.

He was almost right.
