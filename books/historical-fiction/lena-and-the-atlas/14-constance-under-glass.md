## 14. Constance Under Glass

The knock came at nine in the morning, and Vesper knew before she opened the door that it was not a knock meant for courtesy.

Three raps, evenly spaced, with the patience of a man who had all day and the authority of a man who needed none. She had heard that knock once before, two weeks ago, when Inspector Reeve had come under the pretense of routine inquiries and drunk tea in the front parlour while his eyes catalogued the room like an inventory clerk. She had served him then. She would not serve him now.

"Miss Marchetti," Reeve said, when she opened the door. He did not smile. He was a narrow man, grey as a filing cabinet, with the kind of face that would look identical in twenty years. "Is Miss Constance Halloway at home?"

"She is at breakfast."

"Then I'll wait."

"You'll wait in the hall, then," Vesper said, and stepped back to let him in, because refusing him would only make things worse, and because some animal part of her brain—the part that had kept her alive since she was nineteen—was already calculating exits, alibis, the weight of every word she might have to carry.

Edith was not yet downstairs. Edmund had left early, as he had left early every morning that week, his collar high and his eyes elsewhere. Tobias was still abovestairs. Which left Constance alone in the breakfast room with the paper and the toast going cold, entirely unaware that the morning had come for her.

Vesper moved ahead of Reeve down the corridor, and the strangeness of it struck her like cold water: twelve years of walking these halls ahead of visitors, ahead of tradesmen, ahead of the police, always the invisible one, the one who opened doors and then dissolved. Now she was walking ahead of the law itself, and the law was watching her back.

Constance looked up when they entered, and Vesper watched the understanding move across her face in stages—confusion, then recognition of Reeve, then the terrible slow arithmetic of why.

"Miss Halloway." Reeve set his hat on the table, uninvited, next to the marmalade. "I wonder if you might accompany me to Scotland Yard. There are some questions regarding your correspondence with a Mr. Alfred Pell of the *Clarion*."

The paper lay open on the table between them. Vesper could not read it upside down, but she could read Constance, and Constance had gone the colour of the tablecloth.

"Mr. Pell is a journalist," Constance said. Her voice was steady. Vesper had heard that steadiness before, at their father's funeral, at the suffrage meetings when the hecklers turned ugly. It was a voice built like a seawall. "I correspond with half the press in London. My mother's movement depends upon it."

"No doubt." Reeve produced a paper from inside his coat and unfolded it with the care of a man handling something dead. "Then you won't mind explaining certain payments. Forty pounds over six months, drawn from an account under the name C. Hartley. Mr. Pell has been most forthcoming. He says he was buying information. He says the information concerned a certain bombing at the Admiralty docks."

The clock in the hall ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked under Tobias's restless weight, and Vesper thought: *not him, not today, please not him too.*

"I gave Mr. Pell background," Constance said. "Nothing more."

"Background," Reeve repeated, tasting the word. He turned to Vesper as though she were furniture that might have opinions. "You'll be the letter-reader. The one who opens the post."

"I read aloud what the family asks me to read aloud," Vesper said. It was the truest lie she had told in twelve years.

"Of course you do." Something passed behind his eyes—not belief, but a note being made, a drawer being opened in that grey cabinet of a mind. "Miss Halloway. I'll trouble you to come along now. Voluntarily, if you prefer. The alternative is a warrant, and I'd hate for your mother to read about that in the evening edition."

"I'll dress," Constance said.

She rose, and as she passed Vesper on her way to the door their eyes met, and in that half-second Constance did something she had never done in all the years Vesper had served her: she looked afraid *of Vesper*. Or perhaps not of her—of what Vesper knew, of what Vesper might say, of the whole sealed architecture of secrets that had grown up between them like ivy on a wall, beautiful and strangling.

"Tell my mother," Constance said quietly, "I've gone to call on a friend."

And she went.

* * *

Edith took the news standing up.

Vesper had expected—she did not know what. Weeping, perhaps, or rage, or that terrible aristocratic fainting that women of Edith's class performed when the world misbehaved. But Edith Halloway had been arrested twice before Vesper was born, had been carried out of a meeting hall in Manchester by four constables and had given a speech from the police van window. She did not faint. She put down her cup, listened to Vesper's account—edited, every dangerous detail planed smooth—and said:

"Did he say what evidence?"

"Payments. An account under a false name. Mr. Pell's testimony."

"Pell." Edith said the name as though spitting a pip from her mouth. "A creature who sells what he's told for free. And they took her *voluntarily*?"

"There was a warrant threatened."

"Then we go and get her back." Edith was already reaching for her coat, and Vesper saw with a lurch that the old woman was trembling—not with fear but with a fury so old and so practiced it had become a kind of grace. "Vesper. My hat. And send for Mr. Grieves, the solicitor. And Vesper—" she paused at the door, and for one moment the matriarch fell away and there was only a mother, seventy years old and frightened, "—she didn't do it. Whatever they say. Whatever they've found. My Constance is reckless and proud and she lies badly, and she did not do this."

"I know, ma'am," Vesper said.

And she did know. That was the unbearable part. Vesper knew precisely what Constance had done and precisely what she had not, because Vesper had read the letters—had steamed them open at the kitchen table at midnight, had watched the payments to C. Hartley flow through the correspondence like a hidden stream, had told herself for six months that she was protecting the family by knowing and saying nothing. She had held the evidence in her hands and filed it away in the dark of her own silence, and now that silence had a shape, and the shape was a cell.

* * *

The waiting room at Scotland Yard smelled of coal smoke and wet wool and something antiseptic that only made the other smells worse. Edith sat with her spine like a ramrod and her hands folded, and Vesper stood behind her chair in the position she had occupied for twelve years—half a step back, half a step to the left, present and unremarked. Mr. Grieves, the solicitor, a damp man with a briefcase like a coffin lid, had been swallowed into an office twenty minutes ago and had not emerged.

An hour passed. Then another. A constable brought tea that no one drank.

Vesper stood and let her mind do what it always did in the bad hours: it went to work, cold and quick, sorting. If Constance named the account, she would have to explain C. Hartley, and C. Hartley led to Pell, and Pell led to the letters, and the letters led to the question of who had read them and what they had contained and whether the reader had known. Vesper had known. Vesper had steamed every seal herself. If Constance, cornered and honest, said *my letter-reader will confirm the correspondence was innocent*—then Vesper would have to stand in a witness box and explain how she could confirm anything about letters that arrived sealed.

Twelve years of invisibility, and it would end not by choice but by necessity, in a courtroom, as an accessory.

Or Constance would lie, badly, as her mother had said she always did, and they would take her anyway, and the Halloway name—the name Edith had spent forty years building into something a woman could stand behind in public—would come apart in the evening editions.

There was a third door. Vesper could feel it standing in the wall of her mind like a draft. She could go to Reeve herself. She could tell him what she knew—not to save herself but to save Constance: that the correspondence was real but the plot was not, that the payments bought background and nothing more, that the bombing letters had been *planted* through the channel Constance had opened. She could tell him about the paper stock. She could tell him about the handwriting that looked like a dead man's hand. She could tell him everything, and the price of everything was that she would have to begin with the words *I have opened every letter in this house for twelve years*, and once those words were spoken, Edith would hear them, and the twelve years would be over—not with a door closing but with a door that had never really been closed at all, only painted on.

"Vesper." Edith's voice, low, without turning her head. "You're thinking very loudly. Sit down before you wear a groove in their floor."

Vesper sat.

* * *

The interrogation room was small and painted the green of old hospitals, and Constance Halloway sat with her back not touching the chair, the way she sat through bad speeches and worse sermons, upright out of pure defiance of the furniture.

Inspector Reeve sat across from her. A second man took notes. On the table between them lay a folder, and Constance had not looked at it, because looking at it would be an admission that it existed.

"Your mother's movement," Reeve was saying. "It requires money. Meetings, printing, travel. Forty pounds is a great deal of printing."

"Forty pounds over six months is a modest subscription to the truth," Constance said. "You should try purchasing some."

The note-taking man's pen paused, appreciative despite itself.

"Mr. Pell says you gave him a schedule. Dates of meetings. Names of attendees. And in November, a description of certain correspondence your mother received regarding the Admiralty docks."

"I gave him nothing about any correspondence."

"Then how," Reeve said, and here he leaned forward, and his voice did something almost gentle, which was worse than cruelty, "did the men who planned the dock bombing know that the Halloway household had received a letter about the Admiralty? A letter, Miss Halloway, that was never opened by anyone but your family's servant. A letter that was burned."

The room went very quiet. Constance's seawall voice, for the first time, showed a crack.

"Someone in this house burned a letter?"

"Someone in this house," Reeve said, "has been very busy. And either that someone is you—or that someone used you. Used your little arrangement with Pell as a pipe to run poisoned water through. Think, Miss Halloway. Who knew about Mr. Pell? Who could have watched the letters come in, read what they pleased, and fed your journalist exactly the morsels needed to make you look like the conduit for a bombing plot?"

And Constance, who was not stupid—Constance, who had been the cleverest of the three Halloway children since the year she could talk—went still, and Vesper, who was not in the room, who was standing in a coal-smelling corridor two floors above, felt the answer arrive in her own chest like a dropped stone.

Reeve did not suspect Constance. Reeve had never suspected Constance. This was not an interrogation. It was an invitation. He was laying the pieces on the table one by one and waiting to see who in the Halloway house would reach for them.

He knew about the burned letter. Which meant he knew about the letter-reader. Which meant he had known for some time, and had let Vesper walk through his morning with her tea tray and her steady hands, and had said *you'll be the letter-reader* the way a man says *checkmate* in a game the other player doesn't know has started.

* * *

They released Constance at four o'clock, without charge, "pending further inquiries," which everyone present understood to mean *pending your servant's decision*.

She came out into the grey afternoon with her chin up and her hands cold, and Edith wrapped her in her coat and her arms at once, and for a long moment the two of them stood on the steps of Scotland Yard like a statue of some older, harder century. Then Constance looked past her mother's shoulder, directly at Vesper, and her eyes said: *come with me.*

They walked half a block before Constance spoke, Edith a few paces ahead hailing a cab.

"He knows about the letters," Constance said. It was not a question.

"He knows about one letter."

"He knows enough to build a gallows out of it." Constance stopped walking. In the flat December light her face looked older, the resemblance to Edith suddenly total. "Vesper. I have to ask you something, and I need you not to hate me for asking."

"I don't think I could manage that."

"Tell him." Her voice dropped. "Tell Reeve what you know. All of it. That the correspondence was innocent, that the plot letters were planted, that whoever did this used my arrangement—*my* arrangement, which I will swear to, publicly, if I must, I will stand in a courtroom and say I paid a journalist and I was a fool and I was used. But you're the one who can prove the letters themselves were forged. You're the only one who's seen them. You and—" she stopped.

"Say it," Vesper said quietly.

"You and whoever burned them." Constance's eyes were dry and fierce. "I don't believe it's you. I want you to know that. Whatever else happens, whatever you've read and whatever you've hidden, I don't believe it's you. But Reeve does, or he's close, and if you don't speak, he'll close his hand around this family one person at a time until he's holding whoever he decides to hold. So I am asking you—God forgive me, I am asking you—to end your own silence to save mine."

A cab drew up. Edith held the door.

Vesper stood on the pavement with the traffic roaring past and felt the two halves of her life grind against each other like millstones. On one side: twelve years of the only safety she had ever known, the safety of being no one, of steaming seals in the dark and burning what needed burning and being, in return, allowed to stay. On the other: Constance's cold hands, Edith's trembling fury, Tobias asleep upstairs believing the world was a place where idealism was survivable. The whole household, balanced on the silence of a woman who had never once been asked to speak.

"Get in the cab, Constance," Vesper said. "I'll follow. There's something I have to do first."

"What?"

Vesper looked at her—at the girl she had met at nineteen, who had once, in the first winter, brought her a blanket to the coach house without a word, the only unprompted kindness of Vesper's adult life.

"I have to go and confess to your mother," she said. "Before I confess to anyone else."

Constance's face did something complicated—relief and fear and something that might have been grief, arriving all at once, like weather.

And behind them, across the street, beneath the soot-black columns of a shuttered tobacco shop, Inspector Callum Reeve stood watching, hat in hand, patient as a filing cabinet, waiting to see which door Vesper would open—and knowing, with the terrible patience of his profession, that whichever one it was, she would have to walk through it before midnight.
