## 17. December 29th

The last day of the year arrived grey as unwashed linen, and I woke before the bells of St. Saviour's with the certain knowledge that something had already gone wrong. Twelve years in a house teaches you its weather; you learn to read the creak of a stair the way a sailor reads cloud. This morning the house held its breath.

I dressed in the dark and went down through the service passage, past the kitchen where Mrs. Pell had not yet lit the range, and stood a moment at the study door. It was shut. Edmund's door upstairs was open, his bed unslept-in, or slept in so briefly it hardly counted. The study smelled faintly of smoke that had been fanned away rather than extinguished—tobacco over char, char over paper.

I did not go in. I had learned, long ago, which doors to stand outside of.

By seven I was at my post in the hall, brushing frost from Edith's coat when she came down wrapped in wool and resolve, on her way to address the Women's Franchise Committee about funds that no longer existed because half their donors had withdrawn since the *Gazette* printed the word *anarchist* within four lines of her name.

"You look tired, Vesper," she said, letting me settle the collar at her throat.

"I slept badly, ma'am."

"Then sleep tonight." She met my eyes in the hall glass, hers sharp and kind at once. "Whatever you are carrying, put it down for one night."

I almost told her then. God knows I have replayed that moment a hundred times since—the fog on the window, the smell of her lavender water, how easy it would have been to say *there is a man who wants me to destroy your son*. But Constance was still under remand at Bow Street, Tobias was still marked, and Reeve's men were still parked across the square like crows on a fence. A confession spoken too early is just another weapon someone else can pick up.

So I said only, "Yes, ma'am," and drove her into the city through streets hung with bunting for a century nobody felt much like celebrating.

* * *

Reeve was waiting when I returned. Not at the door—he never used the door—but leaning against the garden wall by the mews, hat brim low, breath steaming, looking for all the world like a man admiring the architecture.

"Miss Yarrow," he said, and the name landed on me like a hand on the shoulder. He knew it wasn't mine. He had known for weeks.

"Inspector."

"Walk with me. The horses need walking whether we do or not."

I took the mare out because refusing him in the open street would cost more than compliance, and we went along the canal path where the ice had thickened overnight and the barges sat locked in like teeth in a jaw. He walked with his hands behind his back, unhurried, a man strolling off a good lunch.

"The Aurelia," he said. "New York packet. Sails tomorrow, noon tide, Pier Four."

My hands did not tighten on the reins. I had practised that, at least. "Is that so."

"There's a berth held on her. Cabin class. Booked under the name Yarrow, paid in cash, six weeks ago." He glanced sideways at me. "Six weeks ago, Miss Yarrow. Before the second letter. Before the docks. Whoever booked it knew what was coming before the rest of us did."

The ice groaned somewhere out in the channel. I kept my eyes on the mare's ears.

"So either you are running," Reeve continued, "or someone means to make it look as though you ran. And here is what troubles my sleep, if you'll permit it: both possibilities suit my purposes. If you flee, I close the file on the Halloways with a servant girl's guilt assumed and everyone goes home to their dinners. If you stay—" he stopped walking, and turned to face me fully, and I saw that beneath the amiable slouch there was a mind like a trap set with the plate off, "—then you will stand up in a courtroom the day after tomorrow and tell the magistrate whose hand forged those letters. Because I know it isn't yours. Your father wrote a finer rounder script than whoever cut those letters out and pasted them together, and I've spent three months reading everything that man ever touched."

"You've read my father's file," I said. My voice came out flat and far away, as though someone else were using it.

"I've memorised it. Josiah Yarrow, hanged at Pentonville, '68, sedition. Daughter aged seven, remanded to—" he waved a hand, "—wherever such children go. You built yourself a new name out of spare parts and you've worn it so long it fits better than the old one ever did. I don't blame you. It's well made." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice, though there was no one on the path but gulls. "Here is my offer, and it expires at midnight tonight. Testify against Edmund Halloway tomorrow morning. Tell the magistrate about the stationery, the ledger, the letters you filed and he copied. Do that, and your father's name stays buried, your twelve years of opened letters stay between you and your conscience, and you walk out of that court a witness instead of an accomplice. Refuse, and when this comes apart—and it will come apart, Miss Yarrow, tomorrow or next week—I take the whole household down, and I start with you, because you are the one who read every letter that came through that door and said nothing. You are the most guilty innocent woman in London."

"And if I testify," I said, "I lose the family anyway. No one keeps a servant who spied on them for twelve years."

"No," he agreed, without cruelty, which was worse. "No one does. That's the arithmetic of it. Either way you lose them. The only question left is whether you lose them *and* your name, or just the first."

He tipped his hat then, actually tipped it, the courteous devil, and walked back toward Whitehall with his coat collar up, leaving me standing on the frozen towpath holding a horse that wanted its stable and getting nothing of the sort.

Midnight. He'd given me until midnight, and it was already past two o'clock, and the light was going out of the day like a coin down a drain.

* * *

Tobias found me at dusk in the coach house. I heard him on the gravel before I saw him—that quick uneven stride of his, twenty-two years old and always arriving slightly ahead of himself. He came in rubbing his hands, breath smoking, and stood in the doorway a moment watching me rub down the mare, and I knew before he spoke that Edith had told him about the berth. Or Reeve had. Or he had gone to the shipping office himself, because Tobias was the sort who, once given a thread, pulled until the whole coat unravelled.

"It's true, then," he said. "There's a berth. Under Yarrow."

"It's true."

"Then you're taking it." He said it as fact, bright and fierce, the way he said everything. "Vesper—you have to. Listen to me. Reeve gave you until midnight, Constance told me, she got a note out—don't look like that, she's resourceful, she's *fine*—but listen. If you testify, they'll dig into you. Everything. Father's name, the letters, all of it. You'll be torn to pieces in the papers and the family name with you, and for what? Edmund deserves prison but he doesn't deserve *you*." He crossed the straw and caught my wrist, and his hand was hot from walking and shaking slightly. "Take the ship. Be in New York by the time the ice breaks. Start again. You've done it before—you told me, you can do it again—"

"I lied to you about that," I said quietly. "About starting again. It doesn't get easier. You just carry more."

"Then carry it somewhere warm." He laughed, or tried to. His eyes were wet in the lantern light. "Vesper, please. You saved me from that circle before the police ever kicked the door in. You've saved all of us a dozen times over and none of us ever asked what it cost you. Let someone save *you* for once. Let it be me. Let me at least be the reason you're alive in January."

And there it was—the whole terrible weight of it, compressed into a boy's warm grip on my wrist. Every debt I owed laid end to end: Edith, who had kept a liar and called it charity and meant it as love. Constance, sitting in a cell for a bargain she'd struck to fund her mother's cause. The old man dead eleven years whose handwriting was being used to hang a family. And this one, this youngest one, who had come to me in October with radical pamphlets in his coat and trust all over his face, and whom I had sworn silently to keep whole.

If I fled, Edmund walked free and Constance rotted in remand and the Halloway name went into the ground with my father's. If I stayed, I burned—my alias, my safety, my twelve careful years—all of it fed to the magistrate's clerk and the evening papers, and the family would learn what I was and could never unlearn it.

Either way I lost them. Reeve had the arithmetic right. He'd simply left out the only column that mattered, which was that some debts aren't paid to be released from. Some you pay because the paying is who you've become.

"Tobias," I said. "Go inside. Have supper. Don't say anything to your mother yet."

"You haven't answered me."

"I'm answering you all day tomorrow." I freed my wrist gently and pressed his hand once, brief as a seal on wax. "Trust me one more day. It's the last thing I'll ever ask of you."

He searched my face for a long moment, and whatever he found there frightened him more than comforted him, because he understood, finally, that I had already decided something. He just hadn't guessed what.

* * *

The house clock struck eleven while I sat at the kitchen table with everything spread before me like a surgeon's tray: the forged letter, the sheet of Halloway stationery with its telltale watermark, the page of my father's hand I had carried against my breast for three days until the paper grew soft as cloth. Outside, the last deliveries of the decade rattled through the mews. Somewhere across the river, the Aurelia lay at her berth with steam up and one cabin empty, waiting on a passenger named Yarrow who was never coming—or who was, depending on what I decided in the next hour, and under which name.

I thought of my father on the scaffold, or rather I thought of the thing I had imagined at seven years old, because no child is allowed to see it: the drop, the crowd, the bell. I had spent my whole life since trying to prove that blood was not destiny, that a seditionist's daughter could hold a respectable position and keep her hands clean and her name borrowed and her heart locked in a trunk. And now the choice had come around at last, the way choices do, wearing the faces of the people I loved, and it offered me exactly two doors, and behind both of them stood the same figure: myself, visible at last, undone.

Unless.

I looked at the berth ticket's description Reeve had recited—one berth, cabin class, name of Yarrow—and a thought moved through me slow and cold and certain, the way ice moves across a pond. Reeve assumed the berth was mine. Tobias assumed it was mine. Even Edmund, wherever he was tonight, sweating over his ledger, had built his escape route around the assumption that the scapegoat and the runaway were the same woman.

They had all forgotten the simplest fact about names, the fact I had lived for twelve years: a name is not a person. A name is a coat. And I had taken in a boy once, years ago, and learned to dress him in respectability, and I could do it once more.

The clock began to strike midnight.

On the first stroke, I folded my father's page back into its oilcloth. On the third, I blew out the lamp. On the twelfth, I stood in the dark kitchen of a house that had been my whole world, and I said aloud, to no one, in the voice I had not used since I was nineteen years old:

"My name is Vesper Yarrow. And tomorrow it belongs to someone else."

Then I went upstairs to wake Tobias early—for there was packing to do, and a ship that sailed at noon, and only eleven hours left in which to give away everything I had and call it saving a life.
