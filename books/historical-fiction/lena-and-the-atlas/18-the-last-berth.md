## 18. The Last Berth

The morning of the thirtieth came in grey and salt-smelling, as though the river had climbed the streets overnight and left its breath on every windowpane. Vesper woke before the bells of St. Saviour's, before the scullery fire needed laying, before any sound in the house but the tick of the hall clock counting down hours she had already spent.

She dressed in the dark. She did not light the lamp. There was a particular economy to moving through a room you were leaving—she had learned it at nineteen, leaving a lodging in Southwark with everything she owned in one canvas bag—and her hands remembered it now without being asked. The trunk stayed shut. The letters from her father's papers went back beneath the false bottom where they had lived for twelve years. She took only what could be carried without explanation: a comb, a spare collar, the small tin of needles she had never once used because mending was the one household task Edith Halloway refused to let her perform.

*You are not staff,* Edith had said, the first winter, when Vesper had knelt to darn Tobias's sock. *You are family that we pay, which is a different thing entirely, and don't let anyone tell you otherwise.*

Vesper had believed her. That was the cruelty of it. She had believed her for twelve years.

Downstairs, the kitchen was cold enough to see her breath. She built the fire with the efficiency of long habit, set the kettle on, and laid out the breakfast things in their usual order—the good china for Edith, who would take it standing by the window regardless of the hour; the chipped blue cup for Tobias, who was still asleep and would remain so until she went up and knocked twice, which was their code, two knocks meaning *up, now, there is a day to be got through.*

Two knocks. She stood outside his door longer than the knocking required, her knuckles resting against the painted wood, listening to him stir. Nineteen years old. The same age she had been when she arrived with a stolen name and nothing else. He opened the door with his hair flat on one side and his eyes already too awake for the hour, because he had not truly slept since the raid, since the two boys from the reading circle were taken, since he understood that the noose being woven in this city had been measured against his neck.

"You're up early," he said.

"There's an errand."

"At this hour?"

"Shipping errands keep dockside hours." She made her voice light, made it the voice of the woman who read letters aloud at the kitchen table and drove Mrs. Halloway to meetings and knew nothing of anything worth knowing. "Dress warmly. I want you to come with me."

He frowned. Twelve years had taught him to read her face the way she read handwriting—for pressure, for hesitation, for the truth between the lines—but she had also taught him, without meaning to, that some things she would tell him only when she was ready. He dressed without arguing.

In the hall, Edith was waiting. Of course she was. She stood by the coat stand in her grey wool, silver-haired and upright as a mast, and her eyes went from Vesper to Tobias and back again with the terrible patience of a woman who had survived 1848 and knew exactly what a morning like this one meant.

"The Aurelia," Edith said. Not a question.

Vesper's hands went still on Tobias's scarf. "You know."

"I have known since Tuesday, when you asked Mr. Pell about sailings and thought I hadn't noticed." Edith came forward and took Vesper's chin in her hand, gently, the way one steadies a bird. "I raised three children and employed you for twelve years, my dear. You do not move through this house unseen. You only believe you do."

"Mrs. Halloway—"

"One berth." Edith's voice did not waver, but something behind it did, some deep structural thing, like ice shifting on a pond. "One berth, and two of you who need it. I am old enough to know how arithmetic like that ends." She released Vesper's chin and turned to her son. "Tobias. Come here."

He came. She took his face in both hands and looked at him for a long moment, and whatever passed between mother and son in that silence, Vesper stood apart from it, holding the scarf, holding the door key, holding everything she could not say.

"He goes under your name," Edith said then, to Vesper, without turning. "I assume that was the plan."

"It was."

"And you'll stay and face Reeve."

"I'll testify tomorrow. Today, if he'll take me early."

Edith nodded once, briskly, as if approving a menu. Then her composure cracked—not visibly, never visibly, but Vesper saw the seam of it, saw the woman inside the suffragist, the exile inside the matriarch, the girl of 1848 who had once stood on a dock herself and watched someone sail away rather than go with them.

"Tell him at the last minute," Edith said quietly. "Not before. If he knows on the walk there, he will argue. If he knows at the gangway, it will be too late to argue, and he will hate you for a year, and then he will understand for the rest of his life." Her eyes glistened and did not spill. "That is the correct exchange rate. I have checked it myself."

* * *

The walk to the docks took forty minutes through a London wrapped in fog and coal smoke, the gaslamps still burning pale at nine o'clock, the streets full of the last ordinary traffic of the decade. Tobias talked the whole way—nervous talk, bright talk—about whether New York would have reading circles worth joining, about whether Constance would write to him (she would; Vesper had already secured the promise), about whether the century would feel different on the other side of the water.

"Do you think it's true," he said, "what they say—that the new century actually starts next year? That this year is still the old one?"

"I think people argue about calendars when they're frightened of bigger things."

He laughed. "You always do that. Answer the question underneath the question."

Someone had to. Someone in that house had been answering questions underneath questions for months—had been writing them, forging them, feeding them to Special Branch—and Vesper walked beside the youngest Halloway and felt the knowledge of Edmund sit in her chest like swallowed stone. Last night she had watched him burn the final documents in the study grate and had finally understood the whole architecture of it: the forged letters in her father's hand, learned from the correspondence she herself had filed away in perfect alphabetical order; the planted evidence threaded through Constance's journalist channel; the berth held in the name Yarrow, so that when the arrests came, the trail would end at a servant girl with a hanged man for a father and a one-way ticket in her pocket. Edmund had built his frame around her invisibility. He had used the very quality that kept her safe to make her the perfect culprit.

Well. Invisibility worked both ways. A woman nobody saw could stand in dark halls. Could compare paper stocks by candlelight. Could carry a forged letter to a magistrate's clerk and wait.

But first: the docks.

The Aurelia rode at her moorings like a sleeping animal, black hull, white superstructure, steam already breathing from her funnels in slow white columns that the fog absorbed. Stevedores moved along the quay with crates and trunks. Somewhere a bosun's whistle cut the air. Vesper found the purser's table beneath a canvas awning, showed the letter from Mr. Pell, and watched the clerk run his finger down the manifest.

"Yarrow," the clerk said. "Single berth, second cabin. Paid in full." He looked up. "Passenger present?"

Vesper stepped aside. "This is Mr. Yarrow."

Tobias blinked at her. "I—Miss Yarrow, I'm not—" He caught himself, confused by the name, by the formality, by the clerk already extending the pen. "There's been a mistake. I haven't paid for—"

"Paid in full," the clerk repeated, patient as tide. "Sign here, sir. We sail on the noon tide and we do not wait."

Tobias took the pen. He signed *Yarrow* in his quick slanted hand—his own name, after all, or half of it—and it was only when the clerk stamped the paper and handed over the boarding card that he turned to Vesper with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he does not want to finish.

"One berth," he said slowly. "You asked Pell for one berth."

"Yes."

"For you. Under your name. Your alias." His voice began to climb. "Vesper. Where is *your* ticket?"

"I'm not sailing."

The words landed between them and neither moved. Around them the quay went on—crates swinging, gulls screaming, the ship breathing her steam—and Tobias stood absolutely still in the middle of all that motion, staring at her, and Vesper watched the understanding arrive in him the way dawn arrives: not all at once, but irreversibly.

"No," he said. "No, you're coming. You're—you bought this for yourself, and I'm escorting you, that's the—that's the arrangement, we'll explain to the purser—"

"Tobias."

"—there has to be another sailing, there are ships every week, we'll find you a place on the next one, I'll wait—"

"*Tobias.*"

He stopped. His eyes were wet and furious and nineteen years old, and he looked so much like Edith in that moment that Vesper's throat closed.

"They'll arrest you," he whispered. "Reeve gave you until midnight. You told me. You told me yourself."

"And I mean to use the time. I'm going to testify, Tobias. This afternoon. Before Edmund can burn anything else, before Reeve decides I'm more useful silenced than sworn. I'm going to stand in front of a magistrate and open every letter I've ever steamed and name the man who forged them." She took his hands. They were cold. "Your mother's name gets cleared. Constance walks free. And none of it works if you're standing on that quay when the police come for the reading circle's surviving members. You are the loose thread. Edmund left you loose on purpose. I'm cutting you free instead."

"You can't send me away alone."

"You won't be alone. You'll be the first Halloway of the new century, and you'll be carrying the name I've worn for twelve years, and you'll take care of it better than I ever did, because it was never really mine either." She pressed the boarding card into his palm and folded his fingers over it. "It's yours now. Both names. Make something honest of them."

The whistle blew—a long, low note that seemed to come up through the stones of the quay itself. Stevedores began calling the final positions. Somewhere behind them, a hatch clanged shut like a verdict.

"That's the first call," the clerk said. "All aboard, sir."

Tobias didn't move. "Come with me. Right now. We'll both go, we'll let the magistrate sort it without us, Constance will—"

"Constance is in custody because of what Edmund did, and she stays there unless I speak." Vesper heard her own voice go steady, go flat, go into the register she used for reading bad news aloud to families who needed it delivered gently and could not afford tears. "There isn't a version of this where we both get on the ship and the family survives. I've done the arithmetic, Tobias. Your mother checked it too."

"She'll lose you."

"She'll lose a driver and a letter-reader." Vesper smiled, and the smile cost her more than the testimony would. "She keeps the rest."

He grabbed her then, suddenly, hard, his arms around her shoulders the way he had not embraced her since he was a boy, and she held him and felt the whole twelve years compress into the space of one breath—the kitchen table, the steamed envelopes, the two knocks on his door every morning, the sock she had tried to darn and been forbidden to mend. *Family that we pay.* Family, full stop. She closed her eyes and put her mouth close to his ear.

"Second cabin, starboard side. When you clear the Narrows, go up on deck and look east. That's the last of England. Look at it properly, for both of us."

"Vesper—"

"Go. Now, before I do something selfish."

He pulled back. His jaw was set in the Halloway way, the way Edith's set before a platform speech, and for one wild moment Vesper thought he would simply refuse—would plant himself on the quay and dare the ship to leave without him. But the second whistle sounded, shorter, sharper, and the crowd of passengers began to flow toward the gangway like water finding its level, and the current took him. He walked backward a few steps, watching her, clutching the boarding card, mouthing something she could not hear over the noise—or perhaps he was not mouthing anything at all, perhaps it was only his face trying to hold every word at once.

Then he turned, and went up the gangway, and was folded into the ship.

* * *

She stayed until the lines were cast off. She stayed while the tugs nudged the great black bow away from the quay, while the propeller churned the grey water white, while the Aurelia gathered herself and slid downstream toward the river's mouth with the fog closing behind her like a curtain drawn by careful hands. She stayed until the funnel smoke was indistinguishable from the sky.

Then she stood alone on the quay with the gulls and the empty mooring posts and the whole of the last day of the decade pressing down on her, and she allowed herself sixty seconds—one minute, no more—to feel the size of what she had done. She had given away her escape, her alias, her carefully constructed self, to a boy who would hate her for a year and understand for the rest of his life. Edith's exchange rate. Edith had known. Edith had priced it in advance, from her own dock, her own century ago.

Vesper took out her watch. Half past eleven. Reeve expected her at the magistrate's chambers by two. Edmund believed he had until midnight. Constance sat in a cell in Leman Street believing no one was coming.

Vesper buttoned her coat to the throat, turned her back on the river, and walked into the city to spend the last hours of her invisibility.

Behind her, the tide ran seaward, carrying a ship and a borrowed name toward a century that had not yet begun—and ahead of her, somewhere past the fog, a courtroom waited to hear what a letter-reader had seen in twelve years of sealed things.
