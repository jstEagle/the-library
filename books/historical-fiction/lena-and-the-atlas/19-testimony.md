## 19. Testimony

The magistrate's chambers on Bow Street had the smell of every institution Vesper had ever feared: cold stone, lamp oil, and the particular staleness of paper that has been handled by too many anxious hands. She arrived at ten minutes to two and gave her name—*Yarrow*—to a clerk who wrote it down without looking up, and she understood that this was the last hour in which that name would mean nothing.

Reeve was waiting for her in the corridor outside the courtroom, hatless, his collar dark with fog-damp. He looked older than he had in October, when he'd sat in the Halloway drawing room performing the part of a bored official. Or perhaps it was only that she could see him clearly now.

"You came," he said.

"You gave me until midnight."

"I gave you a choice." He studied her face the way he had studied it over the teacups, that forensic patience, cataloguing. "You look as though you've already spent it."

"I put my brother on a ship an hour ago." She said *brother* deliberately, and watched him decide not to correct her. "Whatever happens in that room, Tobias Halloway is beyond your reach. I'd like that noted."

"It's noted," Reeve said quietly. "For what it's worth—and I know what it's worth is very little—I'm glad you chose this. The other road would have eaten you alive within a year. Informants don't retire. They just stop being people."

"Then we have something in common," Vesper said, "because I stopped being a person twelve years ago. Today I intend to start again, whatever it costs."

He opened the door for her. "Then let's find out the price."

* * *

The courtroom was smaller than she had imagined, and colder, lit by high windows that turned everyone below them slightly grey. The magistrate was a dry, precise man named Harrow who listened the way a scale weighs—nothing wasted, nothing given back. Edmund sat in the dock between two officers, and he had dressed for the occasion: dark wool, white collar, the composure of a man attending a funeral he believed was someone else's. When Vesper entered, his eyes found her at once, and something moved behind them—not fear. Recalculation. She saw him arrive at the arithmetic she wanted him to arrive at: *she knows everything; therefore she can be bought; therefore there is still a transaction available.*

She took the stand and swore the oath on a Bible so worn its cover had gone soft as cloth, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her. Twelve years of reading aloud other people's words had trained her voice for exactly this—to carry, to hold steady, to say plainly what was written down.

"State your name and occupation for the record," Harrow said.

"Vesper Yarrow. For twelve years I have been driver and letter-reader to the household of Mrs. Edith Halloway of Cadogan Gardens."

"And you are here today voluntarily?"

"I am here because no one else can say what I have seen."

Harrow inclined his head a fraction. Reeve, seated at the counsel table, unfolded a fresh page. And Vesper began.

She began with the first letter—the sealed envelope she had steamed open at the kitchen table on an ordinary grey morning, containing nothing but a date. *December 30th.* She described the paper, the ink, the hand: a sloped, old-fashioned script with a peculiar doubled crossbar on the *t*, the kind of hand learned from copybooks forty years out of date. She described the second letter, naming the Halloways in connection with the Admiralty docks, and the third, and the fourth, each one arriving like a stitch pulled through the family's name. She told the court how the letters had been used—how fragments of them had surfaced in the hands of Special Branch, how the journalist Constance Halloway had corresponded with had received documents he had never asked for, planted through the very channel Constance had built to fund her mother's cause. How Tobias Halloway's reading circle had been watched, its discussions reported, its innocence arranged like scenery around a crime.

And then she said the thing she had crossed the city to say.

"The letters were written on stationery belonging to the Halloway household. I compared the stock against papers in my own keeping—correspondence filed over twelve years. The watermark matches. The forgery was done inside that house, by someone with access to the writing desk, the post, and the family's private letters."

The room shifted. Somewhere a bench creaked. Edmund's face did not change, but his hands, folded in his lap, went very still.

"Who had such access?" Harrow asked.

"We all did," Vesper said. "That is the design of it. That is why it worked. A plot against a family must be stitched from inside its own coat."

"Name him."

She looked at Edmund then, fully, for perhaps the first time in twelve years without pretending to be furniture. What she felt was not triumph. It was closer to grief—the grief of confirming what she had known since the night she stood in the dark hall watching him feed documents into the study fire, since the afternoon she laid his forged pages beside her dead father's true ones and watched the same doubled crossbar rise from both. He was thirty-one years old and he had spent a year building a machine to destroy his own mother's life's work, and he had done it competently, patiently, the way one does any piece of housekeeping.

"Edmund Halloway," she said.

The name fell into the room and the room absorbed it, and Edmund turned his head slowly and looked at her with an expression she would keep for the rest of her life—not rage, but a kind of offended wonder, as though a chair had spoken.

* * *

They broke for an hour while the evidence was examined—the letters themselves, brought in under seal; the samples of household stationery; the ledger from Edmund's rooms at Oxford, twenty years old, in which a young man had practiced his father's hand for sport, or for rehearsal. Vesper waited in the corridor with her back to the wall and her palms flat against the cool plaster, and she made herself breathe.

It was there that Edith Halloway found her.

Vesper heard the cane before she saw her—three measured strikes on stone, unhurried, a rhythm she had walked beside for twelve years. Edith came down the corridor in black, upright as a mast, and stopped in front of her, and for a long moment neither woman spoke.

"They told me a servant had come forward," Edith said at last. "I told them they were mistaken. I said, *that is not a servant you have in there.*"

"Mrs. Halloway—" Vesper began, and found she had no sentence ready. In twelve years she had composed a thousand deflections, and now, when it mattered, none of them would come.

"I know what you are about to do in there," Edith said. "You are about to tell a courtroom that you opened our post. Every letter. For twelve years." Her voice did not rise. It never rose. "You are about to hand them your whole self, wrapped, because it is the only way to save my daughter. I know this because I raised three children and I have learned to recognize a sacrifice before it finishes introducing itself."

"I should have told you years ago."

"Yes," Edith said. "You should have. And I should have told you that I knew."

Vesper looked up. "You—"

"A woman does not employ a girl for twelve years without learning what she is," Edith said. "You think I never noticed the way you held an envelope? The way you knew about the Millbank committee's quarrel before I did? I am not a foolish woman, Vesper. I am a woman who chose, long ago, not to ask questions whose answers would cost me someone I needed." She set her hand briefly, lightly, on Vesper's arm—a touch that weighed more than any embrace. "When you go back in there, you will tell the truth, all of it, including the parts that shame you. And you will look at me when you do. Do you understand me? You will not be alone in that room. Whatever they take from you today, they will take it in front of a witness."

Vesper's throat closed. She nodded.

Edith straightened. "Good. Now stand up straight. They're about to learn who works for me."

* * *

The second session began at four, and Veper kept her promise. She told them everything.

She told them she had steamed open the Halloway post from her first month in the house—that a nineteen-year-old girl with a false name and a hanged father's blood in her veins had taught herself the trick with a kettle and a knife, and had read twelve years of other people's secrets because reading them was the only power she had ever possessed. She told them about the letters she had burned: two, early, careless cruelties from a suitor of Constance's, destroyed to spare a girl humiliation. She told them about the trunk in her attic room, her father's papers, the handwriting she had recognized with a horror she could not describe—the horror of seeing a dead man's hand move across paper that had been written last spring. She told them how she had confirmed the forgery, stroke by stroke, and traced the stationery, and followed the trail to the study fire and the ledger and the man in the dock.

"I opened letters that were not mine," she said, "and I lied about my name, and I withheld the first of these letters from Mrs. Halloway because I told myself I was protecting her, and I will carry all of it. But I will not carry it alone, and I will not carry it silently, and above all I will not let an innocent woman sit in a cell while the guilty one sits in a dock wearing a good coat."

The questions came like weather. Where were you born—she answered truthfully, at last: Manchester, and her father was Daniel Yarrow, executed at Kirkdale in '87, and yes, she had concealed it, and no, concealment was not complicity, though she understood if they wished to make it so. Why had she not gone to the police in October—because she had been raised by the gallows to believe the police were weather that happened to other people, and she had been wrong, and Inspector Reeve, whatever else he was, had given her a door instead of a trap, and she had walked through it. Had anyone paid her—no one had ever paid her anything but wages, and she had earned every shilling.

Through all of it, Edith Halloway sat in the front row with her hands folded in her lap, upright, unblinking, meeting Vesper's eyes whenever Vesper dared to look—as promised, a witness, a fixed point, a shore.

And once—only once—Vesper faltered. It was when Harrow asked her, gently, almost kindly, whether she had loved the family she spied upon, and she heard herself say, before any caution could catch it:

"I didn't spy on them. I kept them. There is a difference, sir, and I have twelve years of evidence to prove it."

The silence afterward lasted four seconds. She counted.

* * *

They took Edmund at half past five.

He did not struggle; struggle was beneath him. As the officers took his arms he paused at the dock rail and looked back—at his mother, who did not lower her gaze; at Vesper, who did not either. And he smiled, thin and terrible, the smile of a man closing an account he intended to reopen elsewhere, and said, loud enough for the front benches:

"You've saved the name and lost the fortune, Mother. Ask her what that's worth in ten years."

"Take him out," Harrow said, and the doors swallowed him, and the sound of his steps going down the corridor was the sound of a chapter ending.

Then Harrow turned to the matter of Constance Halloway, and Vesper stood in the well of the courtroom and listened to her own testimony read back against the charge sheet—the planted documents, the exploited channel, the journalist's sworn statement, delivered that afternoon by special messenger from a man who had realized, rather late and rather publicly, that his anonymous benefactor had made a fool of him. The charge dissolved the way frost leaves glass: not dramatically, just all at once, gone.

They brought Constance in from Leman Street an hour later. She came through the doors pale and rumpled and furious, and the first thing she did was find Vesper in the crowd, and the second thing she did was cross the room and take both of Vesper's hands in hers, in front of the magistrate, in front of the reporters already gathering at the doors, in front of everyone.

"You absolute idiot," Constance said, her voice cracking on the word. "You magnificent, impossible—you let them arrest *me* rather than tell me?"

"If I'd told you, you'd have confronted him."

"Of course I'd have confronted him!"

"And he'd have burned the ledger and buried the stationery and you'd be in that cell until spring." Vesper's hands were shaking now, finally, now that it was over. "I couldn't risk you. It's the only lie I ever told you that I'd tell again."

Constance stared at her. Then she laughed—a wet, broken laugh—and pressed Vesper's hands to her forehead like a woman taking an oath. "Twelve years," she said. "Twelve years I thought you were simply quiet."

"I was never quiet," Vesper said. "I was listening."

* * *

They came out of Bow Street into a blue-black evening with snow beginning, small dry flakes turning in the lamplight. Reporters had gathered on the steps—she could hear their pencils already scratching, could see tomorrow's headlines assembling themselves out of the fog: *FORGERY PLOT AT BOW STREET; SUFFRAGIST MATRIARCH'S SON HELD; SERVANT-GIRL'S STARTLING TESTIMONY.* Servant-girl. She supposed she should mind. She found, examining herself, that she did not—not much, not tonight.

Reeve appeared at her elbow, buttoning his coat. "There will be inquiries," he said. "About you. Your father, your name, your twelve years. Whitehall doesn't forgive being played, even by one of its own instruments."

"I know."

"And the boy on the ship—"

"Is my brother," Vesper said, "and if that name appears in any report, Inspector, I will spend the rest of my life making yours extremely tedious."

Something that was almost a smile crossed his face. "Miss Yarrow," he said, tipping his hat, and walked away into the snow.

Edith was waiting by the carriage, and Constance beside her, and when Vesper reached them Edith did not offer her a seat inside. Instead she stood there a moment in the falling snow, studying her—the way one studies a thing returned after long loss, checking it for damage.

"There will be a place for you at Cadogan Gardens," Edith said. "Not the old place. A new one. Think about what you want to be called, because God knows you've earned the choosing of it."

Vesper looked past her, down the hill toward the river, invisible now under the snow and the dark—but not invisible to her. Out there, somewhere past Gravesend, past the lightships, a ship was carrying a boy with her name toward a new century. He would land in New York calling himself Yarrow, and strangers would believe him, and someday he would write to that name, and she would have to decide whether to answer to it.

"I'll think about it," she said. "That's more than I've ever been allowed before."

Edith nodded slowly. "Yes. Well." She climbed into the carriage, and Constance followed, and Constance caught Vesper's eye once through the window—a look that said *come when you're ready, and you will be ready*—and then the wheels turned and the lamps swung away down Bow Street, leaving Vesper alone on the steps with the snow settling on her shoulders like a hand.

Tomorrow would bring the inquiries Reeve had promised, and the headlines, and Edith's offer sitting open on the table like a sealed letter she hadn't yet decided to steam. Tonight there was only the walk home, and the strange lightness of a woman carrying nothing hidden for the first time in twelve years.

She turned up her collar and stepped down into the snow, and began—slowly, testing it, like a new pair of boots—to walk as a person with a name.
