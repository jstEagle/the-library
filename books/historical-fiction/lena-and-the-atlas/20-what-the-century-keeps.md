## 20. What the Century Keeps

The first morning of the new century arrived grey and unhurried, as though the sky itself had stayed up too late and could not be troubled to brighten. Snow had fallen in the night—not the sooty slush of December but a clean, reluctant dusting that softened the rooftops of Belgravia and made the Halloway house look, from the street, like a photograph of itself taken years ago, before anything had gone wrong inside it.

Vesper stood at the kitchen window with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she was not drinking, watching the snow come down in fat, indifferent flakes. Behind her, the kettle ticked as it cooled. The kitchen table—her table, her workbench of twelve years, where so many letters had been steamed open over the spout of that same kettle—was bare except for the morning post: three envelopes, all ordinary, all sealed, all untouched.

She had not opened them. That was the strange thing. She had carried them in from the front step at seven o'clock, set them down, and then simply stood there, looking at them, the way a reformed drunkard looks at a bottle on a shelf. The seals sat there in their wax, smug and intact, and Vesper Yarrow—who had read more of other people's secrets than any priest in London—found she had no appetite for them.

*Miss Yarrow.* The name still startled her when it surfaced. Tobias's name now, officially, on the Aurelia's manifest, sailing somewhere in the middle of the Atlantic with her alias stitched to his coat like a borrowed scarf. She had given it away on a dock in the freezing rain four days ago, pressed the ticket into his hand, watched his face move from confusion to understanding to something close to grief—and then she had turned away before he could refuse, because if he had refused she would have weakened, and if she had weakened, Edmund would have won.

He had not won. That was the arithmetic of the past seventy-two hours, and she turned it over each morning like a coin she kept expecting to come up different. Edmund arrested in the study among his ledgers and his forged letters. Constance released from Holloway with her name cleared and her eyes red-rimmed and her arms around her mother in the magistrate's corridor while photographers shouted questions through the doors. The Halloway name dragged through every paper in London and then, slowly, grudgingly, hauled back out again—the *suffragist matriarch vindicated*, the *traitor son unmasked*, the *loyal servant whose testimony saved a family*. The *Times* had called her testimony "remarkable for its precision." The *Evening Standard* had called her "the quiet woman at the centre of the storm."

No one had called her what she was: the daughter of a hanged man, who had spent twelve years lying about her name and reading other people's post. But perhaps that would come. The century was young.

Footsteps on the stairs—the particular uneven rhythm of them, one hand trailing the banister—and Vesper set down her cup and composed her face into the expression she had worn for twelve years: attentive, neutral, ready.

Edith Halloway came into the kitchen dressed in black wool, her silver hair pinned with the severity of a woman who had decided that mourning her reputation was finished and mourning her son would be conducted privately or not at all. She looked older than she had a week ago. She also looked lighter, the way a tree looks after a storm has taken a limb—damaged, but standing straighter somehow, relieved of weight.

"You're up," Edith said, as though this were remarkable.

"It's a new century, ma'am. I wanted to see it arrive properly."

"It arrived at midnight. You were asleep."

"I heard the bells from my room. All of St. Margaret's, and half the river answering."

Edith crossed to the window beside her and looked out at the falling snow. For a long moment neither of them spoke. This was the thing about Edith that Vesper had understood only recently, only since the confession—that the silences between them had never been empty. They had been full the whole time, both women standing in them knowing more than they said, and the silence had held because both of them had chosen, day after day, year after year, to let it hold.

"I always knew," Edith had said, five days ago, when Vesper had stood in this kitchen with her hands shaking and confessed twelve years of opened letters, expecting dismissal, expecting the police, expecting at minimum the door. And Edith had poured her a sherry instead, and said, *I always knew, and I kept you anyway,* and Vesper had learned in that moment that being seen after a lifetime of invisibility is not gentle. It is like sunlight after twelve years underground. It burns even as it saves.

"Constance is sleeping," Edith said now. "Tobias—" She stopped. Started again. "The ship will have made open water by now. Past Ireland, I should think, if the weather held."

"The weather didn't hold," Vesper said. "But Captain Merrick is a careful man. He'll have hugged the coast until it cleared."

Edith glanced at her. "You know the shipping schedules better than my late husband ever did."

"I know one berth on one ship, ma'am. That's a different education."

Something moved across Edith's face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go, the way a chair holds the shape of the person who has just left it. She pulled out a kitchen chair, which she never did, and sat down at the bare table among the unopened letters, and folded her hands, and looked at Vesper with the directness that had unnerved magistrates and cabinet ministers alike.

"Sit down, Vesper."

Vesper sat. The chair scraped. Outside, the snow went on falling, erasing the street, erasing the world, leaving only the kitchen and the two of them in it.

"Three days ago you stood up in a courtroom full of men who wanted very much to destroy this family," Edith said, "and you told the truth. All of it. The forgery, Edmund, the stationery, the reading circle, the berth on the Aurelia—and your own name, which you did not have to give. Your father's name. Twelve years of opened letters. You gave them everything, knowing it might cost you everything, and you did it to save us." She paused. "I have spent forty years fighting for the principle that women's words carry weight. I have never once heard words carry the weight yours carried in that room."

"Ma'am—"

"No. Let me finish, because I have rehearsed this and if you interrupt me I shall lose my nerve, and I have never once lost my nerve in front of a servant and I don't intend to begin." Her chin lifted. "I am offering you a position. Not the position you had. A new one. Secretary to my correspondence and my campaign—paid, salaried, named in the household accounts under your own name, whatever name you choose. Because that is the second part." She drew a breath. "You may choose your name. Whatever you wish to be called. Yarrow, if you want it back—I know what Tobias said at the dock, and I know what you gave him, and I also know that a name is not a coat, Vesper. It cannot truly be handed off. It can only be worn or refused. So. Choose. And choose the work. And stay."

The kitchen was very quiet. The kettle had gone entirely cold. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked—Constance turning over in sleep, or the house itself settling into the new year.

Vesper looked at her hands. They were steady. That surprised her; she had expected them to shake.

*Stay.*

The word sat in the air between them, enormous. And underneath it, Vesper could feel the whole architecture of what staying would mean: the mornings at this table, the letters—opened honestly now, openly, with permission—or perhaps not opened at all; the drives through the park with Edith beside her talking strategy; Constance's campaigns and Tobias's empty bedroom and the study where Edmund had sat with his ledgers, the room no one would enter for months. She could feel how easy it would be. That was the terrible thing. It would be so easy. She loved these people—she had never once said it aloud, but the word was there now, exposed by the trial like a bone through skin, and everyone in the house had seen it and no one had flinched.

And precisely because it would be easy, she found she could not say yes. Not yet. Not today.

"If I stay," Vesper said slowly, "I stay as what I've always been. The woman in the kitchen. The one who knows things. And I would spend the rest of my life wondering whether you kept me out of love or out of usefulness, and whether I stayed out of love or out of habit, and whether every kindness in this house was real or only the machinery of dependence running smoothly." She looked up. "I don't believe that's true. I believe you love me, ma'am, God help us both. But I can't prove it to myself from inside these walls. I've been invisible here for twelve years. If I become visible, I'd like it to happen somewhere I chose, doing something I chose, so that when I do come back—if I come back—it will mean something."

Edith was silent for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way she nodded at the end of a speech that had landed exactly where she intended.

"That," she said, "is the most suffragist thing anyone has said in this kitchen, and I include my own speeches."

"Ma'am—"

"Hush. I'm not finished, but I'm nearly done, and then you may refuse me properly." She reached across the table and took Vesper's hand—hers dry and strong and cold from the window. "The offer stands. Not for a fortnight, not for a season. It stands as long as I do. When you are ready, write to me. Under whatever name you've chosen. I will know your handwriting anywhere—I've had twelve years of practice pretending I didn't." Her grip tightened. "And Vesper. One more thing. You will not disappear. Do you understand me? You will not do what you did for twelve years—make yourself small and useful and gone. If I hear nothing from you by Lady Day, I will find you. I found the vote against the whole of Parliament. I can certainly find one stubborn woman in London."

A laugh escaped Vesper—short, cracked, entirely involuntary—and she found that her eyes were burning and did nothing to stop it. "Yes, ma'am."

"Good." Edith released her hand, rose, smoothed her skirt, and became once more the matriarch, the monument, the woman the papers had spent a week trying to bury and failed. At the door she paused. "There's kippers in the icebox. Constance will want some when she wakes, and I am constitutionally incapable of cooking them without setting off the alarm. Consider it your last official duty."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And Vesper—" Edith did not turn around. Her voice, when it came, was quieter. "Whatever name you choose. Make sure it's one you can live inside. I wasted thirty years wearing one that fit someone else's ambitions. Don't repeat my mistake. Learn from it instead. That's what the young are for."

Then she was gone, up the stairs, her hand trailing the banister, and Vesper sat alone in the kitchen of the family she had saved and was now, gently, terribly, leaving.

* * *

She packed that afternoon.

It did not take long. Twelve years in one house, and her whole life fit into a carpet bag and a hatbox: two dresses, her driving gloves, a hairbrush, the copy of *Jane Eyre* Constance had given her when she was nineteen and pretending to be someone else. At the bottom of the trunk—the trunk, her father's trunk, which she had sworn never to open and had opened in December and could not now close again—she folded the oilcloth packet of his papers. The real ones. The ones that had proved the forgery, matched stroke for stroke, and hanged Edmund Halloway's scheme in open court. She had testified with them. She would keep them. A man's handwriting, she was learning, is a kind of inheritance, even when the man was a traitor. Even when the man was a liar. Perhaps especially then: it taught you what to watch for.

She left the room she had occupied above the stables made up, bed turned down, as though she might return that night. Habit, or hope. She couldn't tell the difference yet.

At four o'clock, Constance found her in the hall.

Her eyes were still shadowed from the week in custody, but she stood straight, and when she saw the carpet bag her face did something complicated—grief and approval fighting for the same ground.

"So it's true. Mother told me you refused her."

"I didn't refuse her. I postponed her. There's a difference."

"There is indeed. She's furious, by the way. She's hiding it behind the newspaper, but she's furious, and she's also—" Constance stopped, and laughed, wetly. "She's proud of you. Which is worse. You've no idea what it costs to be the object of Edith Halloway's pride. Ask any of her enemies. Most of them are dead."

"Constance—"

"Don't apologize. If you apologize I shall cry, and I have cried enough this week to fill the Thames." She gripped Vesper's shoulders. "You put yourself in the dock for me. You read my letters—yes, I know about that too, I've known since the journalist business, don't look so stricken, I decided I didn't mind—and then you stood in front of a magistrate and burned your own life down to light mine out of prison. Whatever you need to do next, wherever you need to go to do it—you go. And you write to me. Not Mother. Me first. Swear it."

"I swear."

"On the atlas?"

Vesper blinked. "What?"

"Tobias's atlas. He left it for you. On your bed, under the Jane Eyre—he must have slipped it in before he sailed, the little wretch, he never said a word to me." Constance's mouth trembled into a smile. "Go on. Look. I'll mind the kippers."

Vesper climbed the stairs two at a time. There it was on the bed: the old schoolroom atlas, its spine cracked, its cover stamped with a *T.H.* that someone had tried to scratch out and then, evidently, thought better of. She opened it. Inside the front cover, in Tobias's quick slanted hand:

*For Miss Yarrow — who knows where all the ships go. Come find me. N.Y.C. General Post. I'll wait till the ink dries and longer. — T.*

Below it, a single line of coordinates, latitude and longitude, pointing at the middle of the Atlantic like an arrow shot at the future.

Vesper sat down on the narrow bed and held the atlas against her chest and let herself, for exactly one minute, cry.

* * *

She left at dusk, before the lamps were lit, because goodbyes at dusk require less light.

Edith shook her hand at the door like a colleague, which was the highest honor the woman possessed. Constance kissed her cheek and whispered, "Lady Day," a threat and a promise. The house stood behind them in the snow, every window gold, and Vesper walked away from it down the white street with the carpet bag in one hand and the atlas in the other, and did not look back, because looking back was a luxury for people whose futures were settled, and hers—for the first time in her life—was not.

She had one address in her pocket, written on the back of a shipping manifest: a small freight office off Limehouse Reach, run by a man named Pollock, who owed her a favor dating back to the Aurelia and had said, gruffly, the night after the trial, *if you ever want honest work reading manifests and bills of lading, girl, I pay sixpence above market and I don't ask names.* Sixpence above market. No history required. No name required—or rather, a name required, finally, but one of her own manufacture, hers alone, the first document she would ever sign as herself.

Snow squeaked under her boots. Ahead, the river showed through the gaps between warehouses, dark and moving, busy with the century's first traffic—barges and ferries and, far out, the lights of some steamer heading for open water. Somewhere out there, on such a ship, a young man was carrying her name toward America like a lantern.

Vesper stopped at the corner, took out the atlas, and opened it against her knee to the page marked NEW YORK. She studied it the way she had once studied seals before breaking them: thoroughly, hungrily, committing every street to memory.

Then she closed it, tucked it under her arm, and turned toward Limehouse, toward the office with the lamp already lit in its window, toward the desk where tomorrow morning a blank ledger would be waiting for her signature—

—and behind her, faint under the sound of the river, the bells of the city began to ring the hour, and she realized she had not yet decided what name to sign.
