## 22. The Atlas Opens

The cemetery at Kensal Green had never been a place I intended to visit. For twenty-two years it was simply a fact of the city, like the fog or the price of coal—a place where men who died badly went to lie under stone while their children learned to say nothing about them. My father had been in the ground there since 1887, and in all that time I had walked past its gates perhaps four hundred times, on omnibuses, on foot, once in the Halloways' carriage with Edith beside me speaking of something else entirely. I had never once looked at the gates with intention.

Spring made it possible. That is the only way I can explain it to myself now. Winter had held me shut like a fist; when April came and the plane trees along the Harrow Road broke into leaf all at once, as if someone had turned a page, I found that the fist had opened without my noticing.

I went on a Tuesday, my half-day from the shipping office, wearing the grey coat Constance had pressed upon me in March with the words, *You cannot testify before a magistrate in mourning clothes for the rest of your life, Ves.* The morning was cool and rinsed-clean, the sky the pale blue of old porcelain, and the gravel paths of the cemetery were loud under my boots after months of office carpet and dockside cobbles.

I had written ahead—not to the dead, but to the living—to ask where he lay. The registrar's clerk, a young man with ink-stained fingers and the incurious kindness of his profession, had copied out the section and row number without once looking up. Section 94, row 12. No flowers had ever been recorded. No visitors. The entry had sat in his ledger for thirteen years like a coin at the bottom of a well.

I found the stone at half past ten.

It was smaller than I had expected, and plainer: a low granite marker, weathered to the colour of wet slate, the lettering softened by frost until the name seemed less carved than grown into the stone itself.

*JULIAN YARROW*
*1839–1887*

No epitaph. Of course not. There had been no one to commission one who dared to write one. The state had hanged him for sedition in February of '87, and the state's idea of an epitaph was the column of small print in the *Times* that had recorded the execution between an advertisement for cough lozenges and the shipping news. I had read it standing in the Halloways' kitchen with a letter-knife still warm in my hand, twelve days into my employment, and I had felt the strangest sensation: not grief, which came later and stayed, but a kind of terrible tidiness. As though a line had been ruled beneath my life and everything above it filed away.

Now I stood above the ruled line and looked down at it.

"Hello," I said. My voice sounded absurdly loud among the crows. "I'm late."

* * *

I had brought two things, and I set them on the grass beside the stone before I knelt, because my knees were not what they had been at nineteen, and because kneeling is easier when you have given yourself something to do.

The first was the letter.

Not the original—the original had gone into evidence in March and would sit in some Home Office drawer until paper rotted—but the fair copy Reeve himself had allowed me to make, in his office at Scotland Yard, watching me across the desk with an expression I had come to understand by then was not suspicion but a kind of exhausted respect. It was the letter that had begun everything: the second one, the one naming the Halloways in connection with the Admiralty docks, written in my father's hand—or rather in Edmund Halloway's imitation of it, learned from the old correspondence I myself had sorted and filed through twelve years of opened envelopes. I could have burned it. There were nights in January when I had stood at the grate in my lodgings with it in my hand and very nearly done so. But burning it would have been another kind of silence, and I had spent enough of my life keeping silences for other people. This copy was mine—won, in a sense—and I meant to spend it here.

"You wrote these," I told the stone quietly. "Not this one. But you taught me the hand, and the hand is why any of it happened." That was true in ways I was only beginning to understand. My father had been a printer by trade before he became a martyr, and he had taught me letters the way other fathers taught children to skip stones: patiently, obsessively, making me copy out passages until my hand could disappear into any style he set before me. It was how I had earned my bread at the Halloways'—not merely reading letters aloud, but *knowing* them, knowing the weight of the paper and the pressure of the pen and the difference between a hand hurried by fear and a hand steadied by resolve. Edmund had stolen a dead man's script from my filing. But the filing had been mine, and the skill of seeing it, and so in the end it had been mine to give back.

"I don't forgive you," I said. "I want you to know that, wherever you are. You chose your cause over me, and Edith Halloway chose hers over her lover too, and she told me about it in December in a voice like she was handing me a mirror. But—" and here I stopped, because the next part was harder than forgiveness, and I made myself say it anyway. "But you are the reason I can read a room full of liars. You're the reason Tobias Halloway is alive in New York tonight. Whatever else you were, that came from you, and I'm tired of pretending it didn't."

A crow dropped onto the next headstone and regarded me with one flat eye. I took it as acknowledgment. In my experience, acknowledgment rarely comes dressed better than that.

I laid the copied letter flat on the stone, weighted its corners with four small pebbles I had chosen on the walk from the gate—the kind of foolish, deliberate ceremony grief demands of us—and left it there. Let the rain read it. Let the rain deliver it at last.

* * *

The second thing I had brought was the atlas.

It had cost me eleven shillings and sixpence from a shop off Charing Cross Road—a new Bartholomew's Royal Atlas, bound in blue cloth, its maps crisp as frost. The proprietor had asked if it was a gift, and I had said yes, and it had not occurred to me until I was back out on the pavement that I had spoken truer than I knew.

I carried it to the bench near the cemetery wall, an iron-and-wood thing older than both of us, and sat down in a stripe of sunlight with the book across my knees. Around me the cemetery did what cemeteries do in spring, which is refuse, absolutely, to behave like a graveyard. Bees worked the ivy. Somewhere beyond the wall a man was selling watercress, crying his wares in a voice that rose and fell like weather. A woman in widow's weeds walked the path with a terrier trotting before her, and the terrier, at least, was enjoying himself thoroughly.

I opened the atlas to the North Atlantic.

There it was: the great blue emptiness between the Old World and the New, crossed by the faint printed arcs of the great steamer routes, each one a promise rendered as geometry. I found Liverpool at the top right corner of the map, the Mersey a thin silver thread, and I followed with my fingertip the route of the Cunard line west-southwest, out past the last smudge of Ireland, into the open chart where the soundings gave way to blankness and the blankness gave way, three thousand miles on, to the coastline of America.

The *Aurelia* had sailed on the thirtieth of December, nine minutes past noon, in a snow squall that turned the Mersey white. I knew this because I had stood on the landing stage and watched her go, and because Tobias had leaned over the rail until the last possible moment, and because I had put him there.

I do not think I will ever manage to tell that story plainly, even now. Perhaps it is the nature of such decisions that they cannot be narrated, only survived. But I remember the shape of it. I remember the shipping clerk's ledger with the single berth marked *Yarrow*—a berth someone in Edmund's conspiracy had reserved for me, expecting me to run, expecting my flight to be the final proof of the family's guilt. And I remember understanding, in one cold clean instant on the dock, that the berth was a trap shaped exactly like a door, and that the only way to defeat a trap shaped like a door was to send someone else through it. Tobias had begged me to come. He had wept, actually wept, nineteen years old and furious with love, and I had lied to him with the best tool I possessed—I had used the truth. *You go,* I said. *You carry the name. If they take me, they take no one.* And then the whistle had sounded, and the gangway had gone, and he had stood at the rail becoming smaller and smaller until the snow took him, and I had turned around and walked back into London to testify.

He writes every month now. His letters come to the shipping office addressed to *Miss Yarrow*, which is to say, to the name I gave him and am learning to take back. In March he wrote that he had found work with a bookseller on Fourth Avenue and a room in Brooklyn with a view of the East River, and that the light in New York in the mornings was "the colour of beginning again," which is exactly the sort of thing a nineteen-year-old says, and exactly the sort of thing I would have crossed an ocean to hear.

On the atlas, I took out my pencil and drew a small, careful line along the route of the *Aurelia*, from Liverpool to New York. Then, at the point where the line ended, I wrote, in letters no bigger than a grain of rice:

*T. — Dec. 30th.*

Anyone reading it would see only a traveller's marginal note, the sort of thing atlases accumulate over decades. Only I would know it was a monument. I decided that was enough. I had spent twelve years believing that the truest things must be hidden to survive. I was revising my opinions, slowly, the way the sea revises a coastline.

* * *

While I sat there, a thought arrived that I had been circling since January without ever letting it land, and it landed now, sitting on that bench in the sun, with the patience of a bird that has decided you are harmless.

Edith Halloway had offered me a position the first day of the new century. Not my old position—that was finished, and she knew it better than anyone. A new one. She had stood in the parlour of the house on Cheyne Walk with the winter light behind her, straight-backed as ever, her hair whiter than it had been in December, and she had said: *You will not be our driver or our reader anymore, Vesper. Those jobs required you to be invisible, and you have made that impossible, thank God. But I find I cannot run a household, much less a movement, without the one person in it who reads everything truly. Name your terms. Or better—name yourself. Take any name you like. You've earned a real one.*

And I had declined. Not forever—I had been careful to say not forever, and I had seen in her face that she accepted this, because Edith Halloway understood better than most people alive that freedom given too quickly is just another form of captivity. I had declined because I wanted, for the first time in my adult life, to stand on ground I had chosen rather than ground I had been assigned. The shipping office was small and smelled of tar and tea, and Mr. Pellow paid me thirty shillings a week to read correspondence aloud to illiterate sailors and write replies in a fair hand, openly, honestly, with nothing steamed and nothing burned. It was the least important work I had ever done and the most honest, and on certain afternoons, dictating a love letter for a stoker from Cardiff to a girl in Cork, I felt more useful than I ever had in the grand house on Cheyne Walk.

But here was the thought that landed: Edith had said *name yourself*. And I had realized, sitting in the sun among the dead, that I had already done it, years ago, without knowing.

Vesper. Evening star. The star that is not a star at all but a planet, wandering close to the sun, bright enough to be mistaken for something it isn't. My father had chosen it—or claimed to; with him, one could never be sure what was invention and what was inheritance—and I had kept it through everything, the lie wrapped inside the alias, the true thing hidden in the false one. Yarrow was the name I had invented at nineteen to hide behind. But Vesper—

Vesper was the name I had been carrying toward this bench all along.

* * *

I returned to the grave once more before I left. The pebbles still held the letter against the stone; the wind had not touched it. I crouched down and cleared a little drift of last year's leaves from the base of the granite, and I noticed then, for the first time, a detail I must have missed in my first pass: a faint second line of carving below the dates, so weathered it was nearly illegible, cut by some mason long ago at the instruction of—who? The parish, perhaps. Some anonymous decency. I bent close and made it out letter by patient letter:

*HE TAUGHT MANY HANDS TO WRITE.*

I sat back on my heels and laughed—one short, unsteady sound that startled the crow off its headstone. Either the universe had a sense of humour or the mason had known my father's trade, and either way I chose to believe it was a message thirteen years in transit, finally delivered.

"All right," I told him. "Message received."

* * *

I walked back through the gates at midday, and the world outside was doing its ordinary magnificent business: carts and omnibuses, a flower-seller arranging daffodils in a barrow, boys running past with a hoop, the whole roaring unfinished machinery of the new century. I stopped at the barrow and bought sixpence of daffodils, not for the grave—I had already left what belonged there—but for the windowsill above my desk at the office, where they would sit in a jam jar and be seen by everyone, which was the entire point of them now.

Then I opened the atlas one last time, standing there on the pavement with the traffic going by, and turned past the Atlantic, past the Americas, all the way to the frontispiece—the blank page at the very beginning of the book, where an owner was meant to declare themselves.

I had been thinking about what to write since Charing Cross Road. I had considered leaving it blank. I had considered *Ex libris*, with nothing after, a little joke for whoever found the book in forty years. But blank pages were my father's medium, not mine, and jokes were Edmund's, and I was done with both.

I uncapped my pen, braced the atlas against my knee, and wrote in a clear, upright hand—the hand I had learned at my mother's knee before my father ever got his lessons into it, the one hand in all the world that had never imitated anything:

*Vesper Yarrow.*
*London. Spring, 1900.*
*Her own.*

I closed the book. Somewhere down the river a ship was sounding its horn, slow and enormous, and I found myself wondering—not with dread, for the first time, but with the plain animal curiosity that had been growing in me all spring—where it was bound, and whether, someday soon, I might go and see.

The daffodils nodded in the wind off the water. I tucked the atlas under my arm and started walking, and did not look back at the gates behind me, because everything I needed from that direction was already written down, and everything else was ahead.
