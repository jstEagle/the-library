## 1. The Weight of Lead

The first thing Rosalind Hartwell did each morning, before she lit the lamps or stoked the stove or said a word to anyone, was to run her thumb along the edge of the type case nearest the window and read the dust.

Dust told the truth in a print shop. Dust settled on everything—on the cases, on the imposing stone, on the great iron press that squatted in the center of the room like a patient animal—but it settled unevenly, and the pattern of it was a record of the day before. A clean stripe across the upper tray meant her father had opened it late, hunting for a particular size of bodkin. A smudge along the composing stone's near edge meant she herself had leaned there too long while setting yesterday's last column, her hip pressed to the wood as though the work could hold her up. The dust was an honest ledger. It kept accounts the way she wished all ledgers were kept: without flattery, without omission, in a hand no one could forge.

This morning the dust told her it had been three years and four months since her mother died, because this morning was the fourth of March, and on the fourth of March the shop always smelled faintly of cloves, because her father ground a little clove into his coffee and would not say why, and Rosalind would not ask, because some griefs are load-bearing walls and you do not knock on them to see if they will hold.

She lit the lamps. March light came late to Binder's Lane; the buildings opposite stood so close that the sun had to be coaxed down into the street like a shy horse, and by the time it arrived the morning's work was half done anyway. She preferred the lamplight, if she was honest about it—which she permitted herself to be only in the privacy of her own skull, where honesty cost nothing. Lamplight made the lead shine. It turned the shallow trays of type into small dark cities, every letter a house with its roof toward heaven, waiting for someone to come and read it.

"Rosa." Her father's voice from the cutting room at the back, muffled by the partition wall, worn smooth as river stone. "Is the kettle on?"

"It is never off," she called back. This was true. The kettle lived on the stove the way the crucifix lived on the wall—not out of constant use but out of principle, a small domestic argument against a cold universe.

Alois Hartwell appeared in the doorway with a matrix blank cupped in his left hand like a bird's egg. He was sixty-one years old and built like the furniture he'd inherited from his own father: heavy, plain, made to last, showing its grain but never its weakness. His apron was spotted with old ink in shapes he had long ago stopped apologizing for. He looked at the lamp over the composing stone, then at her, then at the matrix, and seemed satisfied by whatever arithmetic he performed.

"Punches need filing," he said. "The six-point is drifting again."

"The six-point has been drifting since the coronation," Rosalind said. "Perhaps it is loyal to the old measurements."

Her father did not laugh. Alois Hartwell laughed perhaps four times a year, at Christmas, at the midsummer fair, and twice more at moments he chose himself, the way other men chose their hats. But something moved at the corner of his mouth—a tectonic event, barely registered—and he took his coffee and went back to his bench, and that was their morning conversation entire.

It was enough. It had been enough for three years, and before that, when her mother was alive, the shop had needed no words at all, because Marta Hartwell had supplied them the way the river supplies water, constantly and without being asked. Now the silence belonged to the two of them equally, and they kept it between them like a piece of good furniture neither wanted to admit was too large for the house.

Rosalind sat down at the type case and began to set the morning's job: two hundred funeral invitations for the Kessler family, black-bordered, the names to be centered, the dates in roman, the verse in italic. She knew the verse by heart. Everyone in the city knew it by heart; it appeared in the official prayer book, which meant it appeared nowhere else, because the Censor's Office had opinions about unauthorized verses for the dead. Grief, like everything else in the empire, required a license.

*Rest, soul, in the Emperor's peace,* the licensed verse ran. *Your labor is given up to God, your loyalty to the Crown.*

Her mother had hated that line. *Loyalty to the Crown,* Marta used to say, sorting type with those quick clever hands, *as if the Crown were standing at the graveside holding its hat.* And then she would laugh, and glance at the door, and lower her voice, and go on sorting, because the work went on regardless and laughter was free even when speech was taxed.

Rosalind set the letters now the way her mother had taught her: by feel, mostly, the fingers reading the nicks on the shanks of the type the way a blind man reads a face. An honest lowercase e had two nicks. The italic f had a groove worn deeper on the left side because it was older than the others, cast before Rosalind was born, and she liked to think it remembered things. This was nonsense, of course. Lead remembers nothing. That was the whole point of lead. You poured your thought into it and it held the shape exactly and gave back nothing, asked nothing, betrayed nothing. Lead was the most trustworthy material in the empire precisely because it had no interior life whatsoever.

Sometimes Rosalind envied the type.

* * *

By ten o'clock the Kessler invitations were locked in the chase, packed tight with quoins and furniture until the whole form sat solid as a paving stone, and she carried it to the press with the care of a woman carrying soup through a sickroom. The press itself—the old Albion, secondhand when her father bought it, older than the censorship laws that governed what passed beneath its platen—stood ready, its bed wiped down the night before, its tympan fresh.

The rhythm took her the way rhythm always did. Pull. Release. Feed. Pull. The paper went down white and came up printed, and somewhere in the middle of the hundredth impression she stopped being a person thinking thoughts and became instead a pair of hands attached to a machine, and this was the closest thing to rest she knew. Other girls her age found their sabbath in church. Rosalind found hers in repetition.

The bell over the loading door rang once—wind, probably, or the coal man's cart jolting past—and she did not look up. In the print quarter you learned not to look up. Looking up was how people got noticed, and getting noticed was how people got asked questions, and questions in the year 1848 were not the harmless things they pretended to be. Questions had teeth behind them. The Censor's Office paid informers by the report, and a girl who looked up too often might find her curiosity recorded in a neat bureaucratic hand, filed under *susceptible.*

So Rosalind Hartwell had made herself a science of being unremarkable, and she was, by any measure, very good at it.

She dressed in gray. Not mourning gray—that would invite sympathy, and sympathy invited conversation—but the gray of weather, of wool, of things not worth remarking upon. She walked to market at the same hour each day and bought from the same vendors, who greeted her with the same nods, and she returned their nods with an expression she had practiced in the back of a polished copper tray until it achieved perfect neutrality: pleasant enough to pass, dull enough to dismiss. When soldiers came into the shop to collect the weekly proof sheets of the *City Gazette*—which her father printed under contract, every word pre-approved, every comma accountable—she served them coffee and answered their questions about her health and the weather and whether her father kept regular hours, and she did it so competently, so forgettably, that she could see the boredom settle over them like snow. Boredom was her armor. Nobody arrested a boring person. Nobody followed home a woman whose most distinguishing feature was her lack of one.

And it worked. That was the thing no one understood about invisibility—it worked. It had kept the shop open through two rounds of new press ordinances. It had kept her father off the lists when other printers' names went up on the notice boards, their licenses suspended, their type confiscated and melted down for bullets to police the very streets that read their papers. Being unremarkable had kept bread on the table and the kettle on the stove and her father's name out of Voss's little black books.

What it cost, she tried not to calculate.

But sometimes, pulling the lever, feeding the sheet, watching her own competent hands do their excellent work, she felt the cost arrive anyway, the way cold arrives through a wall—no door open anywhere, and yet the temperature dropping. There were sentences in her that had never been set in type. There was a version of Rosalind Hartwell who argued, who sang badly on purpose, who had opinions about the price of flour and the conduct of the garrison and the way Magistrate Voss smiled with his mouth while his eyes did sums—and that version lived entirely indoors, behind her face, with the shutters closed, growing stranger and louder in the dark like anything kept from the air.

Emil used to say she was the loudest quiet person he'd ever met. Emil had said a great many things. Emil had been dead for two years, officially drowned at the frontier with his regiment, officially mourned with a licensed verse and a black-bordered card her own hands had set and pulled—two hundred copies, roman names, italic verse—and she had printed her brother's death with the same steady rhythm she printed everything else, pull and release, feed and pull, and she had not wept until the two hundredth copy was dry and wrapped and tied with string, and then she had wept exactly as long as it took to unwrap the string again and begin the next job, because the next job waited, and the work goes on regardless.

That was the family creed, though no one had ever written it down. *The work goes on regardless.* Her mother had stitched it somewhere, maybe, in a sampler or a hem. Her father practiced it daily with the devotion other men gave to saints. And Rosalind—Rosalind had made it a fortress and moved inside it and bolted the door, and some mornings she could not quite remember whether she lived there by choice or by siege.

* * *

At noon she took the proofs to her father's bench. He was working on a new matrix, and she stood a moment watching him cut, because watching Alois Hartwell cut steel was the closest thing to church she allowed herself.

He bent over the punch with the loupe screwed into his eye socket, graver in hand, and the steel whispered under the tool in curls so fine they drifted away like smoke. He was cutting a lowercase g—a double-story g, old-style, with the little ear and the looping tail—and he had been at it for two days, and he would be at it for two days more, and when it was finished it would be perfect, and then he would strike it into copper to make the matrix, and from the matrix would come however many thousand identical little g's, and not one reader in ten thousand would ever notice it, and that, her father said, was the entire dignity of the trade.

"Anyone can make a letter people notice," he'd told her once, when she was twelve and impatient. "Only a craftsman makes a letter nobody notices, that does its work perfectly. Remember that, Rosa. The best of anything is invisible."

She had thought he was talking about type.

She wasn't sure anymore that he had been.

"The Kessler proofs," she said, laying them beside his elbow. "Centering holds. I'd tighten the leading under the date, maybe half a point."

He raised the loupe and looked—not at the proofs but at her, briefly, with that flat assessing gaze he applied to matrices and daughters alike. "Half a point," he repeated. "Yes." He set down the graver, flexed his cut fingers one at a time, and took up the proof with a printer's lens rather than the loupe, holding it to the window. "Good," he said. "You have your mother's eye."

It was the closest thing to tenderness the Hartwell shop produced before sundown, and Rosalind took it in silently and stored it away with all the others, in the place where she kept things too valuable and too fragile for daily use.

Outside, the city went about its morning. Cart wheels on the cobbles. The fish bell. Somewhere beyond the rooftops, the river lay still under its lid of ice, gray-white, patient, holding its breath the way the whole city seemed to hold its breath these days—since the harvest failures, since the bread riots in the western towns, since the newspapers (the licensed ones, the safe ones) began printing words like *unrest* and *agitators* in bold type, words that were supposed to frighten people into stillness and instead, if you watched the faces at market, seemed to be doing something else entirely. Something quieter. Something gathering.

Rosalind noticed none of this, or told herself she didn't. She noticed leading and kerning and the drift of the six-point. She noticed dust patterns and the coal man's schedule and the exact degree of gray that made a woman invisible. She was twenty-three years old, competent and careful, the daughter of the most respectable printer in the quarter, and she intended to remain exactly what she was: nothing remarkable, nothing dangerous, nothing anyone would ever write down.

The bell over the loading door did not ring.

But that evening, closing up, when she went around the side of the building to bar the loading door as she always did—last check, every night, her father's rule—she found that the latch would not seat. Something was holding it. Something on the other side, something heavy, breathing.

She stood in the blue dusk of Binder's Lane with her hand on the door, listening to the weight of it lean against the wood, and understood that her careful, invisible, unremarkable life had just come to the end of its shelf.
