## 2. A Stranger at the Loading Door

The bell above the shop door had not rung all day, and Rosalind had come to be grateful for that.

Gratitude was a small discipline, like keeping her burin steady on the curve of an *e*, or sweeping the type cases in the same order every evening—roman first, then italic, then the drawer of ornaments nobody ordered from anymore. Her father said a printer's hands learned patience the way a riverbed learned water: slowly, and by being worn upon. She had thought about that saying all afternoon while she trimmed matrices by lamplight, the lead shavings curling away like pale petals, and she had decided it was one of the few true things Alois Hartwell had ever told her.

Outside, Binder's Lane was going blue with dusk. The gas lamps came late to their street—the city spent its light where the magistrates lived—and so the lane filled up with shadow the way a cellar fills with cold air, from the bottom, patiently. Somewhere two streets over a dog was barking at nothing, or at something, and the sound carried along the frozen gutters with the smell of coal smoke and horse dung and, beneath it, the river itself: that vast mineral silence under the ice, waiting.

Closing time was a liturgy she performed alone now. Three winters since her mother died, and the rituals had not loosened; they had only narrowed until they fit one pair of shoulders instead of three. She banked the forge where the type-metal was cast. She wiped down the stone press with oiled cloth until the iron gleamed dull as a closed eye. She counted the day's orders—eleven broadsheets for the fishmongers' guild, a wedding announcement for the Kessler girl, four hundred handbills for Herr Brandt's auction of furniture that everyone knew had been seized from a debtor who owed the wrong man money. Honest work. Censorable work. Work that asked no questions and answered none.

That was the whole art of it, she had learned. To be unremarkable was a craft, and she had studied it longer than she had ever studied letterforms. When the watchmen made their rounds, she kept her lamp burning at exactly the same hour each night—not too early, which suggested haste, not too late, which suggested secrets. When the tax assessor came, she had his receipts ready in a folder tied with plain string. When neighbors spoke of politics over the fence, she smiled the way one smiles at weather, agreeing that spring would be late again this year, that prices were criminal, that the Emperor looked tired in the new portraits. Nothing to remember her by. Nothing worth remembering.

Her mother had called it *keeping your head below the type line*—a joke, once, before the jokes left the house. Emil used to say the opposite. Emil, at sixteen, standing on this very loading dock with snow in his hair, declaring that ink was the loudest thing in the world and he intended to make some noise of his own. Two years ago the army's letter had come, the one with the black border and the careful, impersonal sympathy of clerks, and after that even her father stopped speaking his name above a whisper, as though grief were also subject to censorship.

She was reaching for the shutter hooks when she heard it.

Not a knock. The loading door of the print shop opened onto the alley behind the building, a heavy oak affair banded with iron, built for carts hauling reams of paper. What she heard was weight—a body's weight settling against wood that was not meant to hold it—and then a sound like breath dragged through wet cloth.

Rosalind stood very still, one hand still raised toward the shutter, and listened the way she had taught herself to listen in the years since the war took her brother: completely, and expecting the worst.

It came again. A scrape. Then, unmistakably, a voice—low, cracked, barely more than the idea of a voice.

"Water. Please."

She should have blown out the lamp. She should have gone through the shop, bolted the front door, climbed the stairs to the apartment, and let the alley keep whatever it had collected. That was the discipline. That was the craft. In a city where the secret police paid informers by the name, where a printer's cellar could be searched on a magistrate's whim, the wise woman did not answer voices in alleys at closing time.

She found herself walking toward the loading door anyway, her heart beating somewhere high in her throat, and the strangest part—the part she would turn over later, again and again, unable to account for it—was that her fear felt less like a wall and more like a door left unlatched.

She lifted the bar quietly and opened the door the width of a hand.

Cold poured in, smelling of snow and iron. He was slumped against the doorframe with his shoulder taking his weight, a young man, younger than she'd expected, maybe twenty-three, twenty-four, with dark hair matted to his forehead and a face gone the color of candle tallow. His coat had been fine once—good wool, city-cut—but it was torn along the ribs and stiff there with something darker than mud. One arm cradled a leather satchel against his chest the way a farmer carries a lamb out of a flood. Not loosely. Possessively. As if the satchel and not the wound was the reason he'd crossed whatever distance had brought him here.

His eyes found hers. They were gray, fever-bright, and they did what eyes do when they find the last fixed point in a tilting world: they held on.

"You're the printer," he said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite a statement either—more like a man confirming land beneath his feet. "Hartwell's. I saw the sign."

"You need a surgeon," Rosalind said. "Two streets east, past the—"

"No." The word came out flat and final, and then he swayed, and his knees went, and she caught his arm without deciding to. He was heavier than he looked and hot as a stove rail. Through the torn wool her fingers came away slick.

The alley lay empty in both directions, blue-shadowed, silent except for that distant dog. The watch would pass within the hour; they always did, their lanterns swinging their slow pendulum arc down Binder's Lane, their boots ringing off the cobbles in a rhythm she could set type by. And here was a bleeding stranger at her door, holding a satchel like scripture, asking her—with his eyes, if not yet with words—for the one thing she could not give anyone without becoming someone else entirely.

He slid down the doorframe. She let him, guiding him into the shallow space between the door and the paper store beyond, where the stacked reams made a kind of room.

"What's your name?" she whispered.

He tried to smile. It was a terrible effort and it failed halfway, but it told her something: that he had been charming once, before whatever had happened to him along the river road.

"Tomas," he said. "I need ink and paper. That's all. Ink and paper, and an hour, and I'll be gone, I swear it on—" His breath caught. He pressed his hand harder against his side. "Nothing else. I'm not asking you for anything dangerous."

"You're bleeding on my loading floor," she said. "Everything after that is already dangerous."

He laughed—or made the shape of a laugh—and then his head tipped back against the paper stack, and his eyes lost their focus, and she understood with a cold drop of certainty that he was going to faint, and that if he fainted here, in the open doorway, with his blood writing its confession across her threshold, everything would end tonight before it had even begun.

She did the thing she could not afterward explain. She put her shoulder under his arm, took the weight of him—ink-stained hands, printer's hands, strong from lifting formes of type since she was nine years old—and hauled him inward, and drew the loading door shut behind them, and dropped the bar into its brackets with a sound like a verdict.

* * *

The lamp in the paper store threw their shadows enormous against the bales. She got him sitting on the floor with his back against the wall, legs straight, the way her mother had positioned fainting apprentices long ago—head low, blood where it belonged. Up close he smelled of sweat and river water and, under it, something medicinal and sharp, some herb dressing hastily applied and soaked through.

"I'm not a criminal," he said. His voice had a slur at its edges now, the fever working at the mortar between his words. "Or—I am, depending on whose law you keep. But not the kind you're thinking."

"I'm not thinking anything," Rosalind said. "Thinking is how people get arrested."

That startled a real flicker of life into his face. "You sound like—"

He stopped himself. She watched him stop himself, watched the sentence die behind his teeth, and filed the interruption away the way she filed everything: precisely, in its place, to be examined later.

"Hold still." She had fetched the shop's medical tin—her mother's, actually, camphor and linen strips and a bottle of spirit that Alois allowed himself two fingers of on the anniversary of things—and she knelt beside Tomas and peeled back the torn coat far enough to see the wound. A slash, not a stab, running along the lowest rib, ugly but shallow, the kind of cut that bleeds theatrically and kills only through neglect or infection. Someone had wanted him slowed, not dead. Or had missed.

He hissed when the spirit touched it, gripping the satchel tighter.

"You can set that down," she said. "No one is taking it."

"No one is," he agreed, and did not set it down.

She bound the wound in silence, her hands moving with the economy of long practice, and the strange intimacy of it struck her only afterward—that she had knelt in her own paper store and pressed her palms against a stranger's warm skin before she had even learned his surname, and that the act had felt less like scandal than like setting type: something done carefully, correctly, because it needed doing and she was the one whose hands were nearest.

"The watch comes at half past eight," she said, tying off the strip. "Sometimes quarter to nine, if Voss's men ride with them. You can't be seen through the windows, and you can't make a sound. There's a stair behind the paper stacks down to the old cellar—we don't use it since the damp got into the foundation. There's a pallet and a blanket. If you value my life as much as your own, you'll be on it and silent before the lanterns reach the corner."

He was looking at her with an expression she couldn't parse—something between gratitude and grief, as though her competence were a wound of another kind.

"Why?" he asked simply.

"Because you're dying in my alley and I don't want the mess." It was a lie, and they both knew it was a lie, and the knowing sat between them like a third person.

She helped him down the narrow stair, one careful step at a time, his arm across her shoulders, his breath ragged in her ear. The cellar received them with its smell of cold stone, old size, and paper gone soft with damp—decades of misprints and spoiled stock, the graveyard of everything the shop had ever gotten wrong. She lit no lamp. The gray glow from the stairwell was enough. She got him onto the pallet, pulled the blanket over him, and stood with her arms wrapped around herself, feeling the cold of the stones rise through her skirts, feeling the enormity of what she had just done arrive all at once, the way cold water arrives through a boot.

"Ink and paper," he murmured, already sliding under. "Tomorrow. Please."

"Sleep," she said. "Ink keeps."

She climbed the stair, barred the door above it, and stood a long moment in the dark of the paper store with her heart going hard and uneven, listening. The dog had stopped barking. The river groaned somewhere under its ice—a long, tectonic complaint, the sound the frozen Danube made at night when the cold deepened, as if the whole winter were a held breath that occasionally trembled.

Then the lanterns came, right on schedule, two yellow smears moving down Binder's Lane, and the boots rang out their meter, and a voice called the hour to its partner, bored and human, and passed. Rosalind did not move until the sound had faded entirely and the frost had begun to breathe silver on the window glass.

Only then did she notice the smear on the loading door's lower panel where his shoulder had rested—a single dark streak, drying brown at the edges, like a stroke of the wrong ink applied by a careless hand.

She took a rag and turpentine and cleaned it before it could set. She worked by feel mostly, in the near-dark, scrubbing until the wood showed bare, and she understood, as she worked, that she was not erasing evidence so much as signing something. Every wipe of the cloth was a choice. Every choice narrowed the road behind her. By morning there would be no version of this night in which she had simply heard a noise and stayed abed.

Her father would be home soon from the magistrate's dinner, smelling of wine and cigar smoke, and he would kiss her forehead and ask if the day had been quiet, and she would say yes. Yes, Father. Quiet. Eleven broadsheets, a wedding, four hundred auction bills. Nothing worth remembering.

She banked the lamps and climbed to the apartment, and lay awake above the cellar where a stranger breathed in the dark with a satchel full of whatever was worth bleeding for, and she waited for fear to close over her like the river's ice.

Instead, what rose in her, unwelcome and undeniable, warming her from the inside like contraband, was curiosity.

Somewhere below her floorboards, a man had said *you sound like—* and stopped.

She meant to find out what he'd been about to say.
