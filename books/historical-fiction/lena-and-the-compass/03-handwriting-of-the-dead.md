## 3. Handwriting of the Dead

Fever is a country with its own weather.

Tomas drifted through it for what felt like days, though the cellar told him nothing—no windows, only the smell of ink and lamp oil and the sweet rot of apples stored too long. Sometimes a bell tolled somewhere above and he counted the strikes the way his mother had taught him to count waves, waiting for the sea to give up its anger. Sometimes he heard footsteps on the stairs, measured and careful, and he understood without knowing how that they belonged to the woman who had dragged him inside, and that she was deciding, with each descent, whether he was worth the risk.

He talked in the fever. He knew this because fragments came back to him like fish rising: names he should not have said, half a prayer in his grandmother's tongue, once—the word *river* repeated until it lost its meaning. He dreamed of the crossing he had failed to make, of ice groaning under a sledge, of a boy's mitten left behind on the bank because there had been no time, no time, never any time—

When he finally surfaced for good, it was night. The lamp above him burned low and steady. His shoulder had been bound with linen torn from something—a shirt hem, by the stitching—and the wound beneath throbbed with the dull, honest ache of a body that had decided, provisionally, to live.

She was sitting at the foot of the cot. The printer's daughter. Rosalind—he had heard her father call down the stairs once, a single syllable clipped short as if even her name cost money. She was sewing a button onto a man's coat with small, precise motions, and she did not look up when he stirred, though he was certain she had been listening to his breathing for hours.

"You talked," she said.

The words landed in him like stones into still water. He tried to swallow and found his throat full of sand. "What did I say?"

"Names." She bit the thread. "Some of them twice."

He closed his eyes. Above them, through the floorboards, came the ordinary sounds of a house settling: a chair pushed back, water poured, the shop's great press ticking as the building cooled around its iron bones. An honest house, or a house very good at pretending. He no longer knew the difference.

"I need paper," he said. "And ink. That was all I asked for."

"And you'll get it." She set down the coat. Her hands were stained to the second knuckle with lead-black, the mark of her trade; he had noticed it even in delirium, the way a drowning man notices light. "After you explain one thing to me, Tomas—" she had pried the name out of him on the first night, along with the rest of his feverish inventory "—which is why a man bleeding from a gendarme's sabre carries a letter written by my dead brother."

* * *

He should have refused. Every rule of the route said refuse: the courier knows the letter, not the contents; the letter travels sealed; the letter dies before you do. But the fever had already broken every seal in him, and besides, he was tired—tired in a way that went deeper than wounds, down into the marrow where a man keeps his reasons.

"Show me your hand first," he said.

"What?"

"The letter. It's sewn into my coat lining. Left side, over the ribs." He watched her face while he said it, watching for the flicker that would tell him she was crown, or watch, or simply desperate enough to sell a stranger for bread money. But her face did what faces in the print quarter did—it went still, went careful, went blank as an uninked page. Even so, something moved behind the stillness. Something vast.

She fetched a knife from the shelf—not a weapon's knife but a compositor's blade, thin as a fingernail—and slit the seam in four strokes without cutting the cloth beyond repair. A courier's respect, even now. She drew out the oilskin packet, weighed it in her palm, and looked at him.

"It's yours to open," he said. "It was always going to be yours."

She broke the seal with her thumb.

He had imagined this moment on the road south, in the forests, in the ditch where he'd lain while cavalry passed close enough to hear the horses breathe. In his imaginings, the letter always opened onto some clean resolution: gratitude, gold, a door closing safely behind him. Instead what happened was this: Rosalind Hartwell read three lines and made a sound he had never heard a living person make—a sound like a rope parting under load, like something long taut suddenly snapping free—and pressed the back of her hand against her mouth as though to push the sound back into her body where it could do no harm.

"Tomas." Her voice came out flattened, strange. "Read this. Read the date. Read it to me, because if I read it myself I will think I've gone mad."

He took the page. The handwriting slanted rightward, quick and confident, the letters of a man who wrote fast and trusted his hand. *To R., care of the loading door, by the hand that brings this.* And below, instructions in a cipher of trade-marks and printers' signs that Tomas himself could only half follow—quads and ems standing in for streets, a reference to the *long f* meaning the ferryman, something about the *widow's measure* that he knew meant a safehouse on Binder's Lane.

The date sat at the bottom, written in the style of the imperial calendar, unambiguous, four months old.

Four months. Two years after the frontier dispatch had announced that Lieutenant Emil Hartwell, third son of Alois, printer, had died of camp fever and been buried where he fell, name and rank confirmed, effects returned.

"He taught me that cipher," Rosalind said. She had risen and was walking the length of the cellar and back, three steps, turn, three steps, turn, like something caged that had only just discovered the dimensions of its cage. "We invented half of it ourselves. When we were children we used letters to pass notes across the dinner table because Father disapproved of talking during meals. The widow's measure—that was our game with the type cases. You set a line too wide and the compositor has to solve it. We called it setting the widow loose." She stopped. Turned. "I cut that ligature. The one in the salutation. Emil couldn't manage the join, kept breaking the counters, so I cut him a proper one on Father's spare graver and he practiced for a week until he could write it faster than I could."

Her voice held steady through all of it, which was somehow worse than weeping. Tomas had seen men receive news like this before—in the border villages, women reading the frontier lists. There was a kind of person who broke loudly and a kind who broke inward, and the inward kind frightened him more, because you could not tell when they would finish falling.

"Rosalind," he said carefully. "What does the letter say? All of it. I only ever carried it."

She laughed—one note, dry as a snapped type. "It says my brother is alive. It says he deserted the imperial army two years ago, that the fever story was arranged by someone inside the regiment, someone whose name he won't write even here. It says he guides crossings from the far bank now. It says—" her finger traced the lines, and he saw her lips move slightly, translating the cipher back into the language of children "—it says *tell her the compass still points true, and ask her to forgive me for letting her grieve.*"

The lamp flame leaned and steadied. Somewhere overhead a cart passed, iron-rimmed wheels ringing on cobbles, going somewhere legal, somewhere ordinary.

"He let me grieve," Rosalind said. She said it quietly, testing the weight of each word like a compositor testing the fit of a letter in the stick. "Two years, Tomas. I stood at the graveside—we put up a stone, Father paid for granite, granite, because Emil deserved granite—and the whole time he was on the other side of the river, alive, choosing that I should believe him dead. Do you understand what that is? That isn't protection. That is a man deciding my grief was cheaper than my knowledge."

"It might have been," Tomas said. "Knowledge gets people killed here. You know that better than I do—you live under the censorship laws. If he'd written, if anyone had known—"

"I know what things cost." She turned on him. "That doesn't stop it from being a wound. Both things are true. Both things can be true." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes and stood that way a moment, breathing. When she lowered her hand her face had reorganized itself into something new—not the blank competence of the first nights, not the fear. Vertigo. The look of a woman standing on ground that had just informed her it was not ground.

"Why were the gendarmes chasing you?" she asked. "The truth. Not the version you gave me at the door."

He hesitated. Then he told it plainly, because she had earned plainness: the safehouse upriver burned, two couriers gone ahead of him and one taken, the letter too important to burn and too dangerous to keep anywhere but against his own ribs. He had run the last six miles with a sabre cut in his shoulder and the certainty that he had been followed, though he could not prove it. He had come to the Hartwell door because the letter's outer wrapper named it, and because in all the network there was no safer address than a loyal crown printer's shop, and everyone knew it, and that was precisely the joke of it.

"Your brother sent me to you," he finished. "Or to your father. The wrapper says *Hartwell,* not which Hartwell."

"My father dines with the magistrate tomorrow night." She said it flatly, and he heard in it everything she was not yet ready to say about what that fact might mean. She folded the letter along its original creases, exactly, reverently, the way one folds a thing that must survive being hidden many more times. "You'll stay in the cellar. You'll not go up, not for air, not for anything. And Tomas—" she paused at the foot of the stairs, the lamp throwing her shadow huge against the brick "—the man who wrote this letter is the only family I have left. Whatever happens, whatever this costs, he comes home. Do you understand me?"

He understood her completely, and he understood also—though he said nothing then—that the letter contained one more line neither of them had spoken aloud, a line written small in the margin, in the same quick confident hand:

*Trust the daughter. Distrust the network.*

And below that, smaller still, almost erased: *Someone inside talks.*

Tomas lay back on the cot and listened to her climb the stairs, and to the bar sliding home above him, and thought: I have delivered the letter. God help us both—I have delivered the letter.
