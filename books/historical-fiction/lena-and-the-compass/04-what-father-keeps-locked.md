## 4. What Father Keeps Locked

The magistrate's carriage arrived at dusk, its lamps lit early against the gathering fog, and I watched from the composing room window as my father descended the shop steps with his hat at the correct angle and his coat brushed free of lead dust. He had spent an hour preparing for this dinner. I knew because I had heard him above me, pacing the floorboards of his room, and because he had asked me twice whether his second-best collar looked "respectable enough," and because respectability was, in our house, a garment worn so long it had grown to fit the shape of the body beneath it.

"You'll lock up?" he said, pausing at the loading door. The fog had come up off the river and stood in the street like something poured.

"Always," I said.

He hesitated—one heartbeat, no more—then touched his hat brim and went down into the grey. Through the glass I watched him hand my mother's old shawl—no, it only resembled it; my mother had been dead three winters and her things were sold or folded away—watched him settle into the carriage beside Magistrate Voss, whose face in the lamplight wore the expression of a man being pleasant as a formality. The wheels ground away toward the Ringstrasse, where the respectable people dined on venison and discussed the price of flour and the loyalty of their neighbors.

I turned back to the shop.

It should have felt like freedom, that empty hour. Usually it did: the press stilled, the type cases gleaming under the lamp like a field of black ice, the whole building mine alone from cellar to garret. Usually I used it to work past closing on the private orders—the wedding invitations, the memorial cards—that paid for coal in February. Usually I did not think at all, which was the skill I had cultivated most carefully since Emil died: the ability to stand in one place and be nothing more than what the moment required. A compositor. A daughter. A woman of no particular interest to anyone.

But Tomas lay in my cellar with a ball of linen packed against his shoulder, sleeping the shallow sleep of the fevered, and I could not stop thinking.

* * *

I told myself I was going down to change his dressing. That was true, in the way a printed label is true—it named the thing without describing it. I took clean linen and the jar of comfrey salve Mrs. Brandt kept for her rheumatism and carried them down the stairs that smelled of paper dust and lamp oil, past the storeroom where the bales of waste paper waited for the rag merchant, into the low brick vault where we kept the winter stores and, since two nights ago, a stranger who bled on my father's floor.

He was asleep on the pallet we'd made of sacking and an old sailcloth apron. In sleep his face lost the desperate cunning it wore awake and became simply young—not much older than Emil would be now, if Emil were alive, which he was not, which the army had assured us in a letter written by a clerk who could not even spell our surname correctly. Hartwell. H-a-r-t-w-e-l-l. The clerk had written Hartlein, and my father had corrected it in ink, in his own precise hand, before filing the letter in the drawer where we kept the deeds and my mother's burial papers. My father filed grief the way he set type: exactly, once, without revision.

Tomas stirred when my boot scraped the flagstone. His eyes opened—grey, too clear for a fever—and found me in the dark.

"Water," he rasped.

I gave him water. I changed the dressing while he held the lamp for me, his hand not quite steady, and neither of us spoke except when necessary. There was a language developing between us made entirely of necessity: lift your arm; hold this; don't ask. When I tied off the bandage he said, quietly, watching me fold the bloody linen away, "You should burn that."

"I know how to dispose of evidence. I work for a censor."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment.

"Your father," he said, as I gathered the salve and the lamp, "dines with Voss tonight?"

"The magistrate is an old friend of the family." I said it evenly, but I felt the sentence go strange in my mouth, the way a line of type goes strange when a single letter is wrong—everything around it subtly tilted, waiting to be noticed. "Why?"

"No reason." He lay back. "Good night, Fräulein Hartwell."

"It's Rosalind."

"Rosalind." He said it as if testing the weight of the type. Then, when I was three steps up the stairs: "Lock the cellar door behind you. Not against me."

I did not ask against whom. I climbed the stairs and did not lock the door, and then I stood in the dark of the storeroom with the lamp guttering, and I asked myself the question I had been refusing since the loading door: why had my father gone to dine with Voss tonight, of all nights, with a bleeding smuggler in his cellar and a letter in his dead son's hand somewhere in this building?

Unless the letter was not somewhere in this building. Unless my father already knew precisely where it was.

* * *

Here is what I knew about my father, Alois Hartwell, master printer of Binder's Lane:

That he rose at five and worked until nine and never once, in all my life, had missed the anniversary of any order due date. That he had taken the imperial printing license in 1821 and hung it behind glass and dusted the glass every Sunday. That he voted as instructed, tithed as expected, submitted every proof to the censor's office within the hour it was set, and had raised two children on the principle that the safest thing a printer could print was other people's certainty. That after Emil's death he had not raged, though my mother's death had at least taught me what his rage looked like; he had simply become quieter, more exact, his grief compressed like lead into a matrix, taking the impression of nothing visible.

That was the father I had. And yet.

I went first to his office, because it was the obvious place and because I wanted, desperately, to find nothing—to confirm the world as printed. His desk was locked, but the key lived in the drawer of the standing cabinet where he kept his pipe tobacco, because my father believed in hiding things where habit made them invisible. I opened the drawer. The key was there.

And beside it, on a square of cloth, lay a second key. Smaller. Newer. Brass still bright at the teeth, oiled but barely used. I had never seen it before in my life, and I knew every key in this building the way I knew every ligature in every font we owned. This one belonged to none of our locks—or rather, it matched no lock I had ever seen opened. But its size, its cut, its weight in my palm said cellar. Said the cellar door at the far end, the old iron one behind the paper stacks, the one my father had ordered nailed shut the year after Emil left for the army, citing damp.

We had never pried it open. It had never occurred to me to wonder why.

I put both keys in my pocket. My hands were steady, which surprised me; inside, something was moving the furniture of everything I knew.

In the desk I found what I expected—ledgers, legitimate, dull, columns of orders and payments stretching back years like train tracks running straight through the empire's history. Wedding invitations for the Kessler family. Funeral notices for the garrison chapel. Censor's receipts, stamped and countersigned, a neat little archive of obedience.

Then, bound in plain grey board with no title, tucked flat beneath the drawer of the desk itself—a place I only found because the drawer stuck and I pulled too hard and the bottom panel shifted—I found another ledger.

* * *

It was smaller than the others. Thinner paper, the kind bought cheaply in lots from the stationer on Weavers' Row, the kind that leaves no record in a shop's accounts. The handwriting was my father's, but altered—slightly smaller, slightly faster, the hand of a man writing quickly and often, the way a hand changes when it carries a secret long enough that the secret reshapes the muscles.

Columns of figures. Dates going back—my breath caught—going back twenty-six months. Going back to within six weeks of the letter announcing Emil's death.

Payments. Recurring. To names.

*M. the ferryman — 14 fl.*
*A. the carter (south road) — 9 fl.*
*B. the midwife — 6 fl.*
*For the boy at the seminary — 12 fl.*
*Lamplighter — 4 fl.*
*The widow on Salt Street — 8 fl.*

Names I did not know. Amounts too regular to be charity and too small to be bribes—or rather, amounts exactly the size of bribes paid not to buy a thing but to keep a thing running, month over month, the way one pays rent on a hidden room. Some entries were marked with a small symbol in the margin, a triangle, and beside one of these, dated last November, my father had written in the margin, in that same compressed hand:

*E. confirms route holds. Winter crossings riskier since the new patrol schedule.*

*E.*

Emil.

The ledger slipped in my hands. I caught it against the desk edge and stood very still in the lamplight, listening to the shop settle around me—the tick of cooling metal, the river muttering somewhere below the street, and from the cellar, faintly, nothing at all, which meant Tomas was either sleeping or listening.

My brother was dead. My brother was dead; the clerk had misspelled his name; my father had corrected it in ink. And here was my father's hand writing *E.* in the margins of a book of secret payments, writing it casually, the way one writes the name of a business associate, a supplier, a man one corresponds with about *patrol schedules* and *winter crossings*.

Crossings. Of what? The river froze solid by December—you could walk a cart across it. Crossings of what, then? Of whom?

I thought of the waste-paper bales stacked against the south wall, always more of them than the rag merchant seemed to collect, always replaced before they thinned. I thought of the cellar door nailed shut against "damp." I thought of my father's face at dinner with the magistrate, the collar, the hat angle, the whole careful architecture of a respectable man—and I understood, with the slow cold slide of a compositor realizing a page has been imposed backwards, that I was looking at the reverse of everything. That the print was on the other side. That the man who had taught me to be unremarkable, to keep my head down, to let the censors see only what they expected to see, had been teaching me his own craft. His masterpiece was not the shop. His masterpiece was the performance of the shop. Twenty-seven years of flawless obedience, and underneath it, a ledger in a false hand paying strangers to move—something—south.

And I had been cast in it without consent. The dutiful daughter, the invisible compositor, the one who never asked questions—had that been protection, or casting? Had he needed me unremarkable the way a forgery needs clean paper?

I sat down in his chair. It was the first time I had ever sat in my father's chair, and it smelled of tobacco and lamp oil and, faintly, of the river, though the river was four streets away.

* * *

I read the ledger twice more, front to back, because reading twice is how you catch a misprint, and because I could not make myself stop. The final entry was four days ago—the night before Tomas came through our loading door.

*Payment deferred. Visitor expected. If he arrives, all depends on the letter.*

All depends on the letter. I closed the book. The brass key pressed against my hip through my pocket like a small hot coal.

I should have put everything back exactly as I found it. That was the rule of my life: restore the forme, leave no fingerprints, let the page read as it always read. Instead I sat in the stolen chair with the stolen ledger in my lap and let myself feel the thing I had been holding off since the loading door—not fear, exactly, though fear was in it, but a terrible, vertiginous hope, the sensation of ice shifting underfoot just before it decides whether to hold or break.

If Emil wrote letters, Emil was alive. If Emil was alive, then the frontier, the battle, the clerk's misspelled notice—all of it was a fiction someone had constructed, and the question that mattered, the only question, was who had built it and why. Had Emil deserted, as deserters' families sometimes claimed and never proved? Had my father arranged the death notice himself—bought it, forged it, filed it in his tidy drawer—to explain a son who had vanished across the river? The thought should have horrified me. What horrified me was how little it did. How easily it fit. How well my father lied with documents; he had taught me typography, after all, and typography is only the art of making lies beautiful and truths unreadable.

From the street below came the sound of wheels. I froze. The clock in the office said half past eleven—too late for the magistrate, too late for customers, too late for anything innocent. The wheels slowed. Stopped. A door. Boots on cobblestone, unhurried, the tread of someone who belongs wherever he stands.

I blew out the lamp. In the dark I slid the ledger beneath my apron, feeling like a thief in my own inheritance, and moved to the window in time to see a figure below in the fog—not uniformed, not walking on, but standing at the corner opposite the loading door, still as a signpost, faceless in the mist. Watching the shop.

Watching the shop the way a man watches a thing he has been told to watch.

Behind me, from the depths of the cellar, came three soft knocks against the old iron door—the nailed-shut door, the door with the bright brass key waiting in my pocket. Three knocks, deliberate, spaced, patient.

Not from Tomas. From the other side.
