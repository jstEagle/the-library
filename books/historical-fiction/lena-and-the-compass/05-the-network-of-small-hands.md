## 5. The Network of Small Hands

The cellar smelled of ink and cold stone, and Tomas had learned, in the four days since he'd been carried down its stairs half-conscious, to read the hours by its sounds. Above him the shop woke in stages: first the groan of floorboards as Alois Hartwell crossed to the stove, then the ring of the poker through coals, then—always last, always after a pause Tomas imagined was a man standing alone with his thoughts—the soft percussion of type being sorted. A thousand small iron feet marching into their cases. It was a sound like rain on a roof, if rain were orderly. If rain had been trained.

He was propped on a cot between stacks of paper bales, his shoulder wound bound in linen that smelled faintly of lavender water, which meant someone's mother had once kept a supply for someone's daughter, and that someone was dead now and her medicines were being spent on him. The guilt of that sat in him like a swallowed stone. Every kindness in this house cost someone something.

She came down at midmorning, as she did every day now, carrying broth and a candle she didn't need in daylight but lit anyway, because the single high window gave only a gray smear of river light and because, he suspected, she liked having a reason not to look directly at him until she'd composed herself.

Rosalind Hartwell. He knew her name now. She'd given it reluctantly, the way people give coins to beggars they suspect are organized.

"You're sitting up," she said.

"I've been sitting up since yesterday."

"You've been *slumping* since yesterday. There's a difference." She set the bowl on an upturned crate beside the cot and stood over him with her arms crossed, and he saw what he'd been seeing more clearly each day: that she was not beautiful in the way of the women painted on the walls of the cafés he'd passed through, but she was *precise*, beautiful the way a well-cut letter is beautiful, every line of her arrived at deliberately. Her hands were stained at the knuckles with ink that never fully washed out. He had begun, against all sense, to look forward to those hands.

"I need to tell you things," he said. "Before I'm strong enough to be a liar again."

"That's a strange sentence."

"It's an honest one. Sit down, Fräulein Hartwell. What I have to say will take longer than your patience, and you'll want your hands free for when they start shaking."

* * *

She did not sit. She leaned against the paper bales instead, arms still crossed, chin up—a posture he recognized from a hundred farmwives and dockworkers' daughters who'd fed fugitives in their kitchens while pretending it was nothing. The posture of a person daring the world to make this mean what it meant.

"Your brother," Tomas began, "did not die at the frontier."

Her face did something complicated and quick, like weather crossing a field. "I know," she said. "I read the letter."

"You read the instructions. You haven't heard the route. There's a difference there too." He shifted, and the wound pulled, and he let the pain sharpen him. "Emil wrote that letter four months ago from a village called Ostrova, on the far bank. You know where that is?"

"The ferry landing. Before the ice breaks, you can cross anywhere. After—" She stopped herself, and he watched her decide not to finish the sentence, because finishing it meant admitting how much she already understood.

"After the ice breaks," Tomas said gently, "you can't cross at all until autumn. Which is why everything I'm about to tell you has a clock inside it." He took a breath. "There is a road south. Not a road—no one would call it that. A chain. Printers, mostly. Small shops like this one, in towns along the river: Brennau, Kesselstadt, the mill town at Hohenfels. Men and women who take in pamphlets by day and people by night. Fugitives. Deserters like Emil. Jews fleeing the tax collectors in Galicia. Students whose essays got too honest. A whole country of people who said one word too loudly and now must be elsewhere."

"And your shop moves them under waste paper."

"Bales of spoiled printings. Misprints, overruns, the sheets no one wants. Every printer has them; every printer ships them south to be pulped. Nobody counts bales of waste. Nobody opens them—it's paper, it's heavy, it's worthless." He shrugged, and paid for it. "A fugitive lies flat in the bottom of the cart. Bales go around and on top. At each stop, a new printer takes the load, the papers stay, the person moves to the next cart. Like a bucket brigade. Except the buckets breathe."

"How many shops?"

"Nine that I know of. Probably eleven. Maybe more—the ones at the ends don't ask questions, that's the design. Each link knows only the next link. A courier carries sealed instructions, reads them aloud once, burns them. No one holds a map of the whole thing except—" He hesitated. Here was the part he'd turned over for two days, testing it for cracks. "Except the one who funds it."

Something changed in the cellar's air. Even the drip of meltwater from the foundation stones seemed to pause.

"Funds it," Rosalind repeated. "Paper costs money. Carters cost money. Bribes at three toll stations. Yes. Someone pays."

"A benefactor. None of us know the name or the face. The money arrives by different hands—a carter here, a widow there—always with the same token enclosed. Always the same."

"What token?"

"A scrap of lead type. Cut fresh, never used. Different letter each time, but cut by the same hand. We call him the Cutter."

Tomas reached into his shirt, wincing, and drew out the little scrap hanging on a cord around his neck. He held it up so the window-light caught it: a lowercase *a*, in a face he'd never seen anywhere else, the bowl of it slightly too generous, the terminal ending in a small hooked serif like a bird's foot.

"He sent this with my last payment. Emil told me—if you're ever lost, if you're ever hunted, show the Cutter's letter to the printers on the chain. They'll move you even if the money's stopped, because none of them will risk insulting the man who keeps them all alive."

Rosalind took it from him without asking. That was the moment, he thought later—that unhesitating reach, the way her fingers closed around the lead as though it were warm—when he understood she was already halfway into this life whether she chose it or not.

She turned the type over. Ran her thumb across the counter. Held it to the light again, tilting it, reading the metal the way other people read faces.

"This is a punch-cutting hand," she said quietly. "Not cast from existing matrices. This was carved direct into the blank, see—the serifs are cut with a graver, you can feel the tool marks under the—"

She stopped.

Tomas watched her stop. Watched the color leave her face in the specific order it leaves: lips first, then cheeks, then the steady brown of her eyes going glassy and far away, as though she'd stepped backward into some room inside herself.

"Fräulein?"

"My father," she said, very evenly, "has a graver. He cuts matrices. It's what he does, it's what we do, it's—" She laughed, one note, entirely without humor. "Every printer cuts type. Half the shops on the river cut their own display faces. This proves nothing. It's a coincidence. Lead is lead."

"Lead is lead," Tomas agreed. "And yet."

"And yet." She pressed the little *a* flat against her palm, hard, as though testing whether it would bite. Then, abruptly, she thrust it back at him. "You should keep this hidden. If the watch searches—"

"They won't search a cellar they don't know exists. And I'll die before I lose it."

"Don't say things like that. People say things like that and then the saying becomes a debt and then they pay it." She turned away, busied herself with the broth, held the bowl out to him with a stiffness that wasn't coldness—he was learning her language now—was fear wearing work clothes. "Eat. Then you'll tell me about the courier who carried my brother's letter. How he came to be bleeding at our loading door."

* * *

So he told her, and the telling was itself a kind of confession, because he had not spoken the whole of it aloud to anyone.

His name, he told her, was Tomas Brandt, and he had been a compositor in Vienna until the spring of '46, when he set an article about the harvest taxes and the magistrate's men came for the shop's owner, and Tomas had done the cowardly sensible thing and run before they came back for the apprentices. Two years of moving upriver, working under borrowed names, until he'd fallen in with the chain—not as a passenger but as a worker, running letters between links, because a man who cannot go home might as well be useful.

"In March I carried word to Ostrova. That's where I met your brother."

"Describe him." The words came out fast, hungry. "No—don't. Don't describe him, everyone describes him wrong. Tell me what he *does*. His habits."

Tomas smiled despite himself. "He stands with his weight on his heels, like a man perpetually about to step back from something. He whittles when he thinks—little animals, mostly, terrible ones. He made a horse once with five legs and refused to admit the error, called it modern. He talks in his sleep, in full sentences, arguments with people who aren't there. And he—" Tomas paused. "He keeps a list. Of every person he's brought across. Names in a little book, dates, where he sent them. He says someday the empire falls and every name gets read aloud in the square."

Rosalind had turned her face toward the high window. Her jaw worked once. When she spoke, her voice was roughened. "That sounds like him. Five-legged horse and all." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, quickly, efficiently, the gesture of a woman who had practiced grief into a narrow lane and did not intend to widen it now. "Go on. The letter. How did it come to be in your keeping, and why does the man carrying it arrive looking like a butcher's mistake?"

"Two weeks ago, Emil asked me to carry instructions into the city. To the network here. There are six links in this city alone—six shops—and one of them is compromised. He wouldn't say which. The letter names a meeting place and a sign. I was supposed to deliver it to a bookbinder on Binder's Lane."

"And instead?"

"And instead I was followed from the toll station. Or I think I was—I never saw him twice, which is how you know it's a professional. I went to Binder's Lane and the shop was dark and there was chalk on the doorpost. Three strokes. That's a warning sign on the chain—we use it when a link goes quiet. I couldn't deliver the letter to a dead drop, and I couldn't carry it back across the river before the ice broke, and I had soldiers behind me and one place left ahead." He looked at her steadily. "Your shop. Your father is respected, licensed, above suspicion. A wounded journeyman at the loading door of Alois Hartwell is either the safest place in the quarter or the most dangerous, and I gambled."

"You gambled my family."

"I gambled myself," Tomas said. "You were the stake I found lying on the table. Forgive me. It's the arithmetic of my whole trade—every choice spends somebody who isn't choosing."

She was silent a long moment. Downstairs, faintly, the front bell rang—a customer, the ordinary commerce of the world above, absurd and distant as birdsong. Rosalind glanced up at the sound, and he saw her weigh it, the visible effort of remaining a daughter in a print shop rather than becoming whatever she was becoming down here among the bales.

"The Cutter," she said finally. "If he exists. If he's real and not a story the chain tells itself. Six shops in this city, nine downriver, carts, bribes, linen, food for the people in the bales. That's a fortune spent over years. Quietly. Invisibly." Her thumb moved absently across her own ink-stained knuckles, and Tomas watched her arrive somewhere she didn't want to be. "Who hides a fortune inside respectability? Who has access to lead type, to carts, to shipping schedules, to the trust of magistrates?"

"Someone patient," Tomas said.

"Someone who was paying for all of this," she said slowly, "before I ever saw you bleed on my step. Someone who has been spending money and cutting tokens and staying invisible for years. While I sorted type ten feet above his ledger and noticed nothing." She looked at him then, and her eyes were dry and fierce and frightened all at once. "Do you understand what you've told me? You've told me I live with either a traitor or an angel, and I don't know which, and either way I've been blind in my own house."

"Or both," Tomas said softly. "In my experience they share a wardrobe."

That startled a laugh out of her—brief, surprised, quickly folded away, but real. It was the first time he'd seen the invisibility crack all the way through, and what showed underneath was not the careful print-shop daughter but someone quicker and braver and more furious, and he understood with a lurch that he was in danger from her in a way the soldiers could never manage.

* * *

He ate the broth. She stayed, ostensibly to change the dressing, actually because neither of them wanted the conversation to end and neither would say so. Outside, the river made its long grinding noises, ice shifting against ice, the sound of a season deciding.

"There's one more thing," Tomas said. "About the letter. The part you may have missed, because Emil writes like a lawyer when he's frightened."

"He was always like that. Full sentences hiding sharp edges."

"The letter warns that someone inside the city network talks. To Voss's people. Arrests follow certain couriers like shadows follow a cart. Emil doesn't know who. But he wrote—" Tomas recited it from memory; he had read it forty times. "*'Trust the small hands, not the familiar faces. The small hands have everything to lose and nothing to gain.'*"

"Small hands," Rosalind repeated.

"Errand girls. Apprentices. The children printers hire to run deliveries because no one suspects a child with a parcel." He watched her carefully. "Does that describe anyone in your father's shop?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, floorboards creaked—light footsteps, quick, light as a bird hopping—footsteps Tomas had heard every day this week delivering bread and taking away parcels, footsteps belonging to a girl whose name he didn't know, and he saw by Rosalind's face that she knew exactly whose they were.

"Petra," Rosalind said at last, barely above a whisper. "She's fourteen. She adores me. She brings me chestnuts in winter." Her voice hardened against itself, like paper creased to make it fold clean. "It isn't her. It can't be her. It's never the ones who bring you chestnuts."

"No," Tomas agreed quietly. "But it's sometimes exactly the ones who bring you chestnuts, because whoever threatened her knew that too."

She stood then, suddenly, needing to move, needing to not be in this cellar with this knowledge and this stranger and the sound of small quick feet overhead. At the foot of the stairs she paused, one hand on the rail, and did not turn around.

"Tomas Brandt." She said his whole name as though filing it somewhere permanent. "My father dines with Magistrate Voss tonight. He'll be home late. And tomorrow morning, if you can stand, I want you at the composing stone. If you're going to pass as a journeyman in this shop, you'll need to look like one, and right now you hold a composing stick like a soldier holds a knife."

"Yes, Fräulein Hartwell."

"And Tomas—" Now she turned, and the candle threw her shadow huge against the stone wall, taller than any soldier, taller than any magistrate. "The Cutter's letter. The little *a*. If it turns out to be what you think it might be—what I think it might be—then everything I am is built on a lie my own house told me. So give me tonight to hate the possibility properly. Tomorrow we work."

She climbed the stairs. The door closed. The candle guttered and steadied.

Tomas lay back against the bales and listened to the river grind, and above him the small quick footsteps crossed the floorboards overhead, pausing—just briefly—directly above his head, before hurrying on.
