## 6. Emil, Alive and Elsewhere

The letter had been in the cellar all along, folded inside Tomas's satchel like a seed waiting for soil, and Rosalind could not bring herself to open it until the third morning.

She told herself it was prudence. The shop above was open, her father's chisel singing against steel in its patient rhythm, and a girl who read contraband letters at midday deserved whatever came to her. But the truth was simpler and less flattering: she was afraid. As long as the letter stayed sealed, Emil's handwriting remained a possibility. Opened, it would become an answer, and answers—she had learned this three winters ago, when the official envelope arrived with its black border and its printed condolences—answers were often smaller than hope.

So she waited for the hour between closing the shutters and lighting the lamps, that grey seam of the day when the city pretended not to look. She carried the satchel down the narrow stairs, past the racks of drying paper, past the locked cabinet she still did not have the key for, into the corner where Tomas lay propped on a pallet of old broadsheet misprints. His fever had broken in the night; his eyes followed her across the cellar with the wary attention of a man relearning where he was.

"You've decided," he said. Not a question.

"My hands decided." She held them up. They were not quite steady. "The rest of me is catching up."

He tried to sit and failed, then managed it on the second attempt, dragging himself upright against the stone. Up close, in the lamplight, he looked younger than he had at the loading door—twenty-five perhaps, with a farmer's broad hands and the kind of face that would go unnoticed in any market crowd, which she suspected was exactly what he had been built by circumstance to be. He worked the letter from its oilcloth wrapping with fingers still clumsy from fever, and when he offered it to her, he did so the way one offers something breakable.

"Read it yourself," he said. "I've read it so many times I no longer see the words. Only the road they describe."

* * *

She knew the hand before she knew the words.

That was the terrible thing—the thing that made her breath catch and her eyes sting before sense arrived. She knew the slight forward lean of his capitals, the way Emil crossed his *t*s with a single impatient stroke that always overshot the stem. She had taught him half of those letters herself, kneeling beside him on this very floor twenty years ago, guiding his fist around a slate pencil while their father's press clattered overhead. Their mother had made them practice in the margins of spoiled proofs, and Emil had filled every margin with drawings of ships—always ships, though we lived nowhere near the sea—and she had scolded him for wasting paper, and he had laughed, and the laughter had a shape too, and here it was again, resurrected in ink, dated four months ago.

Four months ago. Two years after the frontier, two years after the dispatch that said *died of fever in service to the Crown* in a clerk's indifferent copperplate.

*Rosalind.*

Not Rosa. Not Miss Hartwell. Just her name, written the way he used to shout it up the stairs when dinner was ready.

*I will write this quickly because quick letters travel better than careful ones. If you are reading it, then Tomas reached you, which means the river gods are kinder than the imperial ones. Forgive me for letting you mourn me. It was the only disguise I could afford. A dead man can walk through checkpoints; a deserter cannot. When the regiment broke at [the word was scratched out, rewritten] — when I understood what we were being ordered to do at the frontier, I walked away from my uniform and kept walking south, and then east, and then to the water.*

*You will want to know if it is true, what the letter says about my death. It is true in every way that matters to the Empire. There is a grave. I have visited it. Someone else lies in it, nameless, and I have paid a peasant woman to keep flowers on it, because even a lie deserves tending.*

Rosalind stopped. She pressed the paper flat against her knee and breathed the way her father had taught her to breathe over a fresh matrix—slowly, or the metal remembers your haste. Below her, Tomas watched without speaking. Above her, faintly, the house settled and ticked. Somewhere out in the lane a cart went by, ordinary as bread.

*Here is what I do now,* the letter continued. *I guide people across the ice. Deserters mostly, at first. Then Jews from the eastern districts. Then printers whose presses were confiscated, students whose names are on lists, a whole congregation once, thirty-one souls walking single file at night behind my lantern. The far bank is a country of small rooms and borrowed names, but it is a country, and I am one of its ferrymen.*

*And here is what I need you to know, little sister, and forgive me for asking it: someone in the city network talks. Three crossings have gone wrong since autumn. Men taken at the river's edge, safehouses burned, and always just hours after the word goes out. Voss's people are good, but they are not this good. This is a leak, and leaks in a network of small hands drown everyone.*

*I don't know who it is yet. Neither does the Cutter—I know you'll have heard that name, everyone downstream has, though no one has ever seen his face. He funds the route. He is careful, and careful men are hard to betray, which may be why whoever talks hasn't found him either. But they will. Careful men get careless in exactly one way: they love something, and then they protect it, and protection leaves footprints.*

*I am telling you this because Tomas will vouch for you—he'd better, or I'll come back and haunt him properly—but also because I may need you. Not for sentiment. For type. For paper. For a press that still runs clean under a loyalist's roof. The Empire watches the loud rebels and ignores the quiet shops. Be quiet, Rosa. Be quiet and be ready. That is all I ask, and it is already more than I should ask of anyone.*

*Your brother, alive and elsewhere,*
*Emil*

P.S. *Tell Father nothing. Whatever else is true, tell him nothing. Some men keep their hearts in ledgers, and ledgers can be seized.*

She read the last line twice, and something cold moved through her, down at the level where understanding happens before language. *Tell Father nothing.* Why? Her father grieved Emil openly—kept the boy's first set of type samples in a drawer by his bed, took the anniversary of the death-letter with a silence the whole street learned to respect. What did Emil know, or fear, that she didn't?

She looked up at Tomas. "You knew him out there. On the bank."

"Two years." Tomas's voice was rougher now, the fever having left gravel in it. "He pulled me across myself, last winter. I'd been shot at the checkpoint outside Vaszary—" he touched his side, where the wound sat under her brother's old field bandage, "—and he carried me half a mile on the ice with the patrol lamps sweeping behind us. Then he wrote this letter and gave me the satchel and said, *Find the print shop on Binder's Lane. Ask for ink and paper, nothing else. My sister will understand.*" A pause. "Did you?"

"I hid you from the night watch within four minutes of meeting you," Rosalind said. "I think some part of me understood before I did."

Something moved through his face—not quite a smile, but the ground where one might grow. Then it faded, and what replaced it was graver.

"There's more in the satchel," he said. "Lists. Dates of the crossings that failed. He asked me to memorize them and burn the paper, but—" his jaw tightened, "—I couldn't burn his writing. Not while I thought you might be able to read it better than I could."

* * *

They spread the papers across a flour sack between them: three months of the route's anatomy, rendered in Emil's cramped, urgent figures. Dates. Berths. Names reduced to initials. And beside each failed crossing, in a different ink—a cheaper ink, the kind sold in the chandler's on the market square, the kind any household might buy—small annotations in Tomas's own hand, added during his recovery days while she slept upstairs:

*Nov 12 — courier taken at the tannery steps. Word sent out Nov 9.*
*Dec 3 — safehouse on Binder's Lane emptied by soldiers six hours after the signal.*
*Jan 18 — two men lost at the ice hole. Patrol waiting at the exact bend. The exact bend, Rosa. Out of nine miles of riverbank.*

She stared at that last annotation until the numbers blurred. Nine miles of river, and the soldiers had chosen the one bend. That was not intelligence gathered. That was information delivered.

"It's someone close to the scheduling," Tomas said quietly. He had gone pale again, though not with fever. "Someone who sees the signals before they move. In the city network there are maybe eight people at that level. Eight, Rosalind. A courier, two safehouse keepers, the woman who runs the rag-and-bone front, the ferryman's cousin who watches the bank, the man who distributes the pamphlets—" He stopped.

"What?"

"The man who distributes the pamphlets," Tomas repeated slowly. "The route uses waste-paper bales. Spoiled stock. Misprints. Paper nobody audits, going to buyers nobody suspects." He looked at her, and she saw the thought arrive in him the same moment it arrived in her, and neither of them wanted to say it aloud.

Because she knew where waste paper came from in this quarter. Every printer within a mile bought theirs from the same handful of suppliers, and the largest of those suppliers—

No. She stood up too fast and the lamp guttered. Her father's shop ran honest work: almanacs, customs forms, wedding invitations, the magistrate's own annual reports, bound and stamped with the crown's approval hanging framed above the ledger desk. Her father fell asleep over scripture most Sundays. Her father had wept at Emil's memorial service, actually wept, in front of the magistrate Voss himself, who had put a hand on his shoulder before the whole congregation.

But Emil had written: *tell Father nothing.* And there was a locked cabinet in the cellar, and a second key missing from her father's ring, and she had heard—just once, half-asleep, weeks ago—her father's footsteps crossing this floor at an hour when honest men were asleep, and the sound of a drawer, and then nothing.

"You're thinking what I'm thinking," Tomas said.

"No," Rosalind said. "I'm thinking eight things, and I refuse to line them up."

But they lined themselves up anyway, in the dark behind her eyes, and she hated each one for standing so obediently in its place.

* * *

She went up the stairs two at a time and stopped herself at the door to the shop, hand on the latch, listening. The lamp in the cutting room still burned. Through the frosted pane of the door she could see her father's shadow, bent over the bench, the small precise motions of a man finishing a matrix—stroke, check, stroke—as he had finished them every evening of her life. Steady as tide. Honest as arithmetic.

*Some men keep their hearts in ledgers.*

"Father?" She pushed the door open.

Alois Hartwell looked up, and for one unguarded instant—less than a heartbeat—something crossed his face that she had never seen there before. Not guilt. Closer to readiness. The look of a man who has been waiting years for a knock and finally hears one.

Then it was gone, smoothed away beneath the mild tiredness of a craftsman working late, and he smiled at her, and the smile fit his face so perfectly that it frightened her more than the other expression had.

"You're down late," he said. "The boy sleeping?"

"Sleeping." She kept her voice level, kept her hands loose at her sides, and understood suddenly, completely, why Emil had written what he'd written—because lying to this man felt like lying to a mirror. "Father, a question. Purely professional." She nodded at the matrix under his chisel. "If a man needed to print something the crown hadn't licensed—quickly, quietly, a thousand copies—could this shop do it?"

The chisel stopped.

For a long moment Alois Hartwell looked at his daughter across the litter of steel shavings and lead dust, and the lamp hissed between them, and somewhere out on the river the ice groaned—a long, deep, shifting sound, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"A shop can do anything," her father said at last, carefully, "that its owner permits." He set down the chisel and aligned it with the bench edge, precisely, the way he aligned everything. "Why do you ask, Rosalind?"

And she heard herself say, smoothly, fluently, in a voice she barely recognized as her own: "A customer. Hypothetical. I wanted to know our capacity."

Her father studied her a moment longer—long enough for her heart to slam twice—and then returned to his work, bending over the steel, and said, mildly, "We have more capacity than you'd imagine."

She climbed back down to the cellar with her pulse roaring in her ears, and Tomas looked up at her face and knew before she spoke.

"He knows something," she whispered. "Or he is something. And Emil told me to trust no one, and I'm starting to think my brother included our father on that list for a reason." She sank onto the crate beside the pallet, the letter heavy in her pocket like a stone, joy and dread fused now into a single weight she would carry from this hour forward. "Tomas—whoever talks, whoever betrayed those crossings—they started after you entered the city."

He went very still.

"And tomorrow," she said, "we find out whether that's coincidence."
