## 7. The Magistrate's Invitation

Alois Hartwell had dressed carefully for dinner, which was itself a kind of confession.

He had not dressed carefully for a meal since his wife died. Grief had made him indifferent to the mirror; the shop had made him indifferent to everything else. But tonight he stood before the narrow glass in his bedroom with a brush in one hand and a dark coat over his arm, and he caught himself doing what Rosalind did when she wanted to disappear—arranging himself into something unremarkable. A respectable widower. A printer with ink under his nails and no opinions worth arresting.

The trouble was that Magistrate Voss had known him for thirty years, and thirty years of friendship reads a man like a broadsheet read by candlelight: every line, every smudge, every letter cut slightly too deep.

He went down through the shop. The press stood silent under its canvas sheet, a sleeping animal. On the stone sill of the window above the composing table, someone—Rosalind, surely—had left a sprig of dried lavender from last summer's bundle, and its faint sweetness fought the smell of lead and lampblack and won only in the smallest radius around it. Alois paused there. He always paused there. Three winters, and the pause had never shortened.

"Father?" Rosalind's voice came up the stairwell, along with the click of her shutting the type cabinet. She appeared at the foot of the stairs with her sleeves rolled and her hands still dusty from the day's matrices. "You're going out?"

"Dinner at Voss's."

She said nothing for a moment. He watched her decide not to ask why, and loved her for it, and hated himself for being grateful.

"There's stew on the hob if you want it before you go," she said instead.

"I ate." He hadn't. His stomach had been closed all afternoon, the way it closed before the tax assessors came, before the censor's annual inspection, before any visitation where a man's future might be weighed in front of him. "Lock the loading door. And Rosalind—" He stopped. What could he say? *Be careful*? Careful had been her whole character since she was nine years old. *Trust no one*? That lesson would cost more than he could pay to teach.

"Mind the damp," he finished. "It warps the paper stock."

Her eyes held his a beat too long—the way hers did lately, ever since the night he'd come home late and found the cellar lamp still warm—but she only nodded, and he let himself out into the blue cold of the evening.

* * *

The Voss house sat on Cathedral Square, where the gas lamps burned brightest and the cobbles were swept by municipal brooms, and Alois felt the difference in his boots long before he reached the door: here the streets were smooth, maintained, obedient. The print quarter's cobbles were broken teeth. The empire spent its money where the money already agreed with it.

Voss met him in the hall himself, without servants, which was either an honor or a warning.

"Alois." The magistrate's handshake was dry and warm and lasted exactly as long as propriety demanded. He was a heavy man gone soft at the edges the way old fruit does, but his eyes had stayed hard as buttons. They had been at school together; they had both courted the same girl once, and Alois had lost her to neither of them, because she'd married a wine merchant and moved to the coast. Thirty years of shared history, and how much of it was load-bearing now?

"You look well," Alois said, because one said such things.

"And you look tired." Voss led him into the study—a room of dark wood and darker leather, books arranged by the spine-gilt rather than the contents, a fire built high enough to be expensive. "Sit. Wine? It's Hungarian. Don't tell the customs men."

They drank. The fire ticked. Somewhere above them a clock counted with the patience of an institution.

"Your daughter keeps well?" Voss asked.

"She keeps the shop better than I do."

"A capable girl. Unmarried." Something in the mildness of it. "Still cutting matrices at twenty-three. There's a waste in that, Alois. A waste of good material."

Alois set down his glass with care. "I don't follow."

"No," Voss said pleasantly. "You rarely do. It's one of your best qualities."

And then, because Voss had never in his life wasted a move, he rose and crossed to his desk and brought back—not papers, Alois noticed, not evidence, nothing so crude—a single printed sheet, laid flat on the low table between them like a card turned face-up.

It was a playbill. A theater program, months old, for a production of Schiller that had run in the autumn. Innocent. Except that the typesetting was wrong—or rather, it was right in a way that made Alois's chest tighten. The ligature in *SCHILLER*, the way the double-L joined at the crossbar. He knew that join. He had invented that join, twenty years ago, cutting a matrix by lamplight while his infant son screamed in the next room, because the foundry's standard L was ugly and Alois Hartwell had never been able to leave ugliness alone.

"Do you see it?" Voss asked softly.

"The theater used a local printer," Alois said. "There are perhaps six shops in this city that could cut that face."

"Yes. Six." Voss settled back into his chair. "And five of them are on my list of suspects already. Which is why we're having dinner, old friend, instead of a summons."

The fire popped. Alois kept his face doing nothing at all. This, too, was craft—the same craft as cutting a counter, the negative space inside a letter, the shape of what isn't there. His whole life he had made letters that spoke; tonight he needed to make his own face into blank metal.

"Suspects of what?" he asked.

Voss sighed, and for a moment the magistrate fell away and there was only Karl Voss, sixty-one years old, gouty, fond of fishing and bad jokes, a man who had wept at Lena Hartwell's funeral and pressed forty gulden into Alois's hand and told him the city owed her more than flowers. That man looked at Alois across the fire with genuine sorrow.

"The quarter is talking, Alois. Pamphlets moving south. People moving south. Do you understand me? Not pamphlets—people. Deserters, debtors, a rabbi's family, God knows who else, going across the river ice all winter like it was a market road. Someone in this city is funding it. Feeding it. And the crown—" he spread his soft hands, "—the crown wants names."

"And you thought I'd have names."

"I thought you'd have ears. You hear everything on Binder's Lane. Every apprentice, every errand girl, every drunk journeyman who talks too much over the type cases." Voss leaned forward. "Give me three names, Alois. Three, and I'll close the file on your street. Your shop stays clean. Your daughter stays clean. I'll even—" and here his voice dropped into something almost tender, "—I'll see to it that the girl makes a good match. A merchant's son. Respectability, protection, a life where no one ever knocks on her door after dark. All of it, for three names."

The clock above the mantel measured out ten seconds. Alois counted them the way he counted em quads when setting a difficult line—one, two, three—because counting was the oldest trick he knew against shaking hands.

Three names. Three people, alive tonight because of money that had flowed out of his own ledger under entries marked *paper stock, foreign*. Three people whose crossing he had paid for while dining at this very table, laughing at Voss's jokes about the bishop, accepting his brandy, letting the friendship stand like a wall behind which the treason worked.

*What am I,* he thought, *that I can sit here.*

But the fear—that was the thing he could not explain to anyone, the fear that lived in him sideways, aimed at the wrong target. Voss expected him to be afraid for himself, and so Voss would read self-preservation in whatever Alois did next. Let him. Self-preservation was the perfect disguise; everyone believed in it; no one looked twice at a coward.

His fear was for a boy on the far side of a frozen river. His fear was for a stranger bleeding against his loading door whom he had never seen and could picture anyway, because Emil's letter had described him. His fear was for Rosalind, walking home through the wet streets with her collar up, carrying knowledge in her head that would hang her if it spilled.

His fear was that he had taught his daughter invisibility so well that she would vanish entirely, the way her mother had vanished—slowly, politely, into illness and silence—and he would be left alone in a shop full of voices that had all learned to speak from him.

"Karl," Alois said, and heard his own voice come out steady, which surprised him, "if I gave you three names tonight, what would happen to those three men?"

Voss waved a hand. "Process. Interrogation. Perhaps release, if they're innocent."

"Perhaps."

"You know how it works."

"I know exactly how it works." Alois rose, slowly, and walked to the window. Below, the square lay empty except for a sentry stamping his feet outside the cathedral, his breath ghosting white under the gaslight. Beyond the rooftops, invisible, the river lay under its ice, holding its cargo of secrets the way the ground holds the dead.

Thirty years ago, he and Karl Voss had stood together on a bridge over that same river, young men, drunk on cheap wine and the 1844 fair, and Voss had put his arm around his shoulders and said: *Whatever happens, whatever they make us become—you and I stay honest with each other.* Alois had laughed. Neither of them had known yet what the empire would ask them to become, or how honestly each would answer.

"I'll tell you what I can give you," Alois said to the glass.

"Name them."

"I'll give you this: the print quarter is frightened. Frightened men talk loosely, but frightened men also lie, and half of what my street whispers is smoke. If I start pulling threads for you—if my shop starts asking questions—the quarter closes like a fist, and you lose the real trail forever. Is that what the crown wants? A closed fist?"

Voss was quiet. Alois could feel him calculating, weighing the offer of a live informant against the risk of a spooked network, and Alois let him weigh it, because the arithmetic favored them both. That was the terrible secret of their friendship, the one neither had ever spoken aloud: they understood each other completely, and had built everything they had on that understanding, and only one of them had ever used it as a weapon.

"You're protecting someone," Voss said at last. Not angry. Almost wondering. "After all these years, Alois. Who is it?"

*My son,* Alois did not say. *My son who has been dead for two years and crossed the river four nights ago on legs I paid for.*

"My trade," he said. "You're asking me to burn my own trade, Karl. Ask a man to name his brother and he'll hesitate. Ask him to name his livelihood and he'll fight you."

"Mm." Voss rose with the effortful grace of a large man and refilled both glasses himself, waving off the servant who had appeared at the door. When the man was gone, he said, lightly, "There's talk of a stranger in the quarter. New face, no papers worth the ink. Seen near the river streets." He handed Alois the glass. "If he comes to your door, you'll send for me. Won't you?"

The wine was excellent. Alois tasted none of it.

"If he comes to my door," Alois said, "he'll be buying type or selling rags, and I'll sell him the first and buy the second, and that will be the whole of it. I'm a printer, Karl. I'm sixty years old. My adventures are all behind me, in the form of unpaid invoices."

Voss laughed—a real laugh, sudden and booming, the laugh of the bridge in 1844—and clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to stagger a smaller man. "There's the Alois I know. Come. Stay for the soup. Marta made it with the saffron you can't get anymore, and I'll tell you what the bishop said at the consistory, and you'll pretend to be scandalized."

They ate. Voss talked; Alois listened; between the soup and the cheese the magistrate told two stories about the censor's office that were funnier than they should have been and more damning than he realized, and Alois laughed in the correct places and stored every word in the locked drawer where he kept things that would matter later. Once, mid-anecdote, Voss's eyes flicked to him—quick, professional, a jeweler's loude glance—and Alois met the look with the bland, tired amiability of a man thinking about his paper order, and saw Voss dismiss him, satisfied.

That dismissal hurt worse than suspicion would have. To be underestimated by a friend was a kind of death; to depend on it was a kind of prostitution. He finished the wine anyway.

* * *

The walk home took twenty minutes. He took the long way, past the river streets, though he told himself it was merely habit. The ice groaned somewhere out in the dark—a long, deep note, like a church organ played underwater—and he stood a moment at the rail of the little footbridge and listened to the river turning over in its sleep.

Soon. Days, maybe a week. Then the water would open, and the crossings would end until autumn, and whatever was going to be decided would have to be decided fast. He thought of the ledger hidden behind the false back of the drying cabinet, and the second key on its ribbon under his shirt, warm against his sternum all through dinner like a second heart. He thought of Emil, aged nineteen in his memory and twenty-six in fact, guiding strangers across that black ice toward a country Alois would likely never see again.

*I could go,* he thought, and the thought arrived whole, fully formed, wearing its own coat, as if it had been waiting in the vestibule of him for years. *When the berth comes. One place, they say. I could take it, and the shop could pass to Rosalind, and no one would ever connect the Cutter of Binder's Lane with the respectable widower who dined with the magistrate and gave up nothing—*

No. He pressed his palms flat against the frozen rail. That was vanity dressed as sacrifice. The shop was the network's lungs; the money moved through the invoices; take the printer out and the whole body suffocated within a season. And Rosalind—Rosalind, who had started looking at him lately with those new, measuring eyes, as if she were proofing a page and finding errors—

She knew something. Or suspected. She had that quality from her mother: she never accused, she simply waited, and the waiting was worse than any tribunal.

The ice groaned again, closer now, a sound like the world clearing its throat.

He let himself in through the shop's front door with his own key, quietly, out of habit—and stopped.

Lamplight below. In the cellar.

He descended the stairs without hurrying, each step deliberate, and pushed open the cellar door onto warmth and light and a scene he would carry to his grave: his daughter, sleeves rolled, standing over a workbench where a gaunt stranger with a healing wound sat hunched over a case of type, setting letters with the careful clumsiness of a novice—while beside the bench, propped against a sack of rags, sat a letter in a hand Alois had last seen addressed to him two years ago, in an envelope stamped by the War Ministry, containing a sentence he had never finished reading.

Emil's handwriting. Emil's ligatures. The double-L joined at the crossbar.

The stranger looked up. So did Rosalind, and her face did the thing it never did—it went completely open, unguarded, a page with nothing set upon it.

"Papa," she said.

Alois stood in the doorway with the cold still on his coat and Voss's wine in his blood and thirty years of flawless performance hanging off him like a wet garment, and heard himself say, in a voice he did not recognize as his own:

"How long," he asked, "has he been here?"

Behind the stranger, on the bench, the letter trembled slightly in the draft from the stairwell—four months dated, written in a dead man's hand—and Alois Hartwell, who had lied to the magistrate over saffron soup not three hours before, felt the whole architecture of his careful life shift and groan beneath him like the ice on the river, and begin, very slowly, to break.
