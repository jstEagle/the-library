## 8. Lessons in Disguise

My father rose before dawn in those days, and so did I—not from piety but from necessity, because a shop that opens at six must have its type distributed by half past five, and lead does not sort itself. On the third morning after Tomas came down from the loft to eat bread at our table like a man who belonged there, I found him standing in front of the type cases with his arms crossed, studying them the way a man studies a locked door.

"They're not alphabetized," he said.

"No."

"Then how does anyone find anything?"

I set my coffee down and pulled open the upper case. "California job case. Lowercase here, small caps above. The letters live where your fingers learn they live, not where your eyes think they should." I let my hand drift over the compartments without looking. "A compositor reads with his hands. The eye is too slow and too honest. The hands will lie for you all day if you train them."

He looked at me then with an expression I would come to know well—the look of a man recalibrating something he had assumed was simple. "Show me," he said.

And that was how it began: three hours each morning, before my father descended and the shutters came down and the respectable business of Hartwell & Son resumed, while Tomas learned to be a journeyman printer named Ferenc Kovács, late of Pest, hired on for the spring rush.

* * *

He was a poor student in every way that mattered, which is to say he was quick, and quickness made him careless. His first attempt at setting a line came out backwards—of course it did; everyone's first line comes out backwards—and his second came out right-reading, which was worse, because type must be set mirror-wise or it prints wrong, and he had corrected the error in the wrong direction.

"You've made it worse," I told him, holding the stick up to the lamp. "Now it's a sentence no one has ever written, in a language no one speaks."

"It says something, though." He squinted at it upside down. "It looks like it means something."

"That's the whole danger of this trade. Everything looks like it means something. Lead is very confident. It will set any lie you ask of it and never once flinch." I tipped the stick and let the letters clatter back into their compartments. "Again. Slower. Your thumb goes here, your forefinger here. You don't grab the sorts, you receive them. Like—" I paused, hunting for the word.

"Like what?"

"Like catching rain. You can't chase a drop. You put your hand where it's going to be."

He tried again. His hand brushed mine over the composing stick—he reached for the same em quad I was reaching for, because neither of us had thought to say whose hand it was—and we both froze, briefly, foolishly, two people caught touching a piece of blank metal worth less than a groschen. He withdrew. I withdrew. The em quad sat between us in its little brass-walled cell, taking up exactly the space it was entitled to.

"You first," he said.

* * *

By the fifth morning he could set a line of eight-point Baskerville without dropping a single letter into the wrong box, and by the seventh he could do it while talking, which was the real test, because a journeyman who cannot talk while he works is a journeyman nobody believes. We gave him a history. He had been born in Komárom, apprenticed at sixteen, walked out of a bad shop in Győr when the master beat an apprentice boy for sneezing during a press run. The story had the great advantage of being mostly true—I had only changed the town and softened the beating—and he told it with such flat, unlovely detail that I believed it myself by the third telling.

"The trick isn't the story," I said, sorting leads into their drawer while he composed. "Anyone can invent a history. The trick is being bored by it. A liar tells his story like a gift he wants unwrapped. A man telling the truth tells it like a chore. Be bored, Ferenc. Be bored about Komárom."

"I am extremely bored about Komárom."

"Good. Tell me about your master's press again."

"A Columbian. Iron, cast like some cathedral monstrosity, with an eagle on top that spreads its wings when you pull the lever." He said it with his eyes half-closed, weary as Sunday. Then, opening them: "Is it true your father cut the matrices himself? For his display faces?"

"Some of them. The rest he bought from Stamm in Vienna before the customs got vicious." I slid the drawer shut. "Why?"

"He cuts like no one else I've seen. I've handled a hundred shops' worth of type. There's a way his serifs finish—a little flare, like a signature at the end of a stroke. I saw it in the broadsheet he printed for the guild last autumn and I knew the shop before I read a word." He turned a lowercase *a* over in his fingertips, examining the foot of it against the window light. "You can tell a cutter's work the way you can tell a walker by their stride. Even across a street. Even in the dark."

Something moved through me then, cold and quick, a fish turning under ice. Because I had been raised inside that stride and had stopped seeing it, the way you stop hearing a clock, and here was this stranger holding my father's handwriting in a single letter of type and reading it like a face.

"You notice things," I said carefully.

"It kept me alive on the road." He set the *a* back precisely in its compartment, first try, no fumbling, and I understood that his hands had already decided something his mouth hadn't announced.

* * *

The mornings were ours. That was the arrangement, though no one spoke it aloud. My father worked the front office until nine, dealing with the magistrate's office, the paper merchants, the guild; the composing room was mine until then, and Ferenc Kovács was my hire, and whatever a journeyman and the cutter's daughter discussed over the stone was nobody's affair. But I knew—knew with the particular clarity of someone who has spent her whole life watching what other people pretend not to see—that my father knew. He began descending later. He began taking his coffee upstairs. Once, passing the composing-room door, he paused just long enough to look at Tomas bent over the case and at me standing nearer to him than instruction required, and said nothing at all, which from Alois Hartwell was a paragraph.

Guilt is a strange companion. It didn't come for the lying—we were all lying now, the shop had become a theater and we were all in costume—but it came for the hours. Every hour I spent teaching Tomas the difference between an en dash and a hyphen was an hour in which the informer, whoever he was, was working. Emil's letter lay folded in its oilcloth under a floorboard in my room, and its warning ran through me on a loop: *someone inside talks.* Inside. The word had teeth. Inside could mean Petra, who had carried our parcels since she was twelve and called me *madame* as a joke. Inside could mean old Binder, whose safehouse had held forty souls across six winters. Inside could mean anyone, anyone at all, and meanwhile I was standing in a warm room smelling of ink and lamp oil, laughing—actually laughing—at a wounded smuggler's impression of a Viennese customs officer, while somewhere across the city a man or woman was selling names by the pound.

"You've dropped a letter," I said, sharper than I meant to.

Tomas looked up. Whatever was in my face made him set down the composing stick entirely. "Rosalind."

"It's the *h*. It's gone. Where did you put the *h*?"

"Rosalind." He said it again, quieter. "Where did you go just now?"

"Nowhere. I'm here. I'm teaching you where the *h* lives."

But he waited, the way he waited for everything, patient as water finding its level, and eventually I heard myself say: "Every hour we spend doing this is an hour they're working. Whoever they are. You said it yourself—you may have been followed. And I stand here showing you how to kern a ligature while—"

"While what? While you sharpen a knife? While you write to Voss?" He shook his head. "This is the work. Do you understand that? A courier who can't pass is a corpse with a satchel. Every hour you spend making Ferenc Kovács boring about Komárom is an hour closer to me walking out that loading door alive, and every hour I walk out alive is an hour the route holds. You are not flirting while the house burns. You are carrying water."

"I wasn't flirting," I said, with the dignity of a woman who absolutely had been.

"No," he agreed gravely. "Of course not. Purely instructional." And he picked up the stick and found the *h*, first compartment, without looking, because his hands had been learning even while his attention was elsewhere, and something in my chest went loose and warm and entirely inconvenient.

* * *

On the ninth morning I taught him the press.

Not to run it—no one runs a double-pulled Albion without a season's apprenticeship—but to stand at it correctly, to ink the plate with the confidence of familiarity, to pull the proof lever and curse at the register the way printers have cursed since Gutenberg. We printed nothing. We pulled proofs of nonsense: my name, his false name, the alphabet marching across the sheet in ranks. His false name looked very small coming off the press. FERENC KOVÁCS, four words of lead and a lifetime of invention, drying grey on cheap paper.

"Hold it up to the light," I said. "Always check your forme against the light. The light catches what the eye forgives."

He did, and the lamplight shone through the thin paper, and for a moment his face behind the page was lit like a saint in a church window, all edges and shadow, and I had to look away and fuss with the ink ball that needed no fussing.

"My brother taught me that," I heard myself say. "Check against the light. He was twelve and I was ten and he'd gotten hold of Papa's galley and printed a whole page of nonsense—*EMIL HARTWELL IS A GENIUS*, thirty times—and Papa made him pull every letter and distribute it back, all night, by candlelight. But Papa also kept one copy. Folded it up and put it in his ledger. I used to think it was punishment. Now I think—" I stopped.

"Now you think it was pride," Tomas said.

"Now I think my father keeps things." The words were out before I could weigh them, and they rang in the composing room like a dropped sort, and Tomas looked at me with that careful stillness of his, and I realized I had said both too much and not nearly enough, and that he had noticed the *too much* and filed it away in whatever drawer his hands kept sorted better than his tongue did.

"Your brother," he said gently, "sounds like a man worth crossing a frozen river for."

"He's worth more than that," I said. "He's worth the truth. Which is rarer."

* * *

That afternoon the errands went out as always—Petra with her basket and her quick feet and her smile like a struck match, Binder's boy with the waste-paper bales bound for the wharf—and the shop settled into its honest evening rhythm, my father totaling accounts in the front office, the gas lamps hissing, the presses cold and waiting. I stayed late in the composing room, distributing the day's practice type back into the cases, and found myself moving slowly, unwilling to end the ritual, because putting away the letters meant putting away the morning, and the mornings had become the only part of the day that felt fully lit.

Tomas appeared in the doorway with two cups of coffee, which was against every rule of master and journeyman, and handed me one, which broke several more, and leaned against the stone beside me, which broke all of them.

"Ferenc Kovács is ready," he said. "Bored about Komárom. Afraid of Columbian presses. Competent enough to be hired, not so good as to be remembered." He blew on his coffee. "Tomorrow I should start walking the quarter. The network needs to see a new face before it trusts an old problem."

"Tomorrow," I repeated, and heard how the word came out of me: heavy, reluctant, a stone rolled against a door.

He glanced at me sideways. "Three streets east of here there's a chandler's on Binder's Lane that takes deliveries after dark. If a journeyman named Kovács happened to be walking home that way tomorrow evening—"

"Then he'd want to know which streets the watch walks," I said, "and which corners have no lanterns, and which doorways are deep enough for a man who suddenly remembers an appointment." I set down my cup. "I grew up on these streets, Tomas. I know them the way you know your own hands."

"Then tomorrow," he said, "you'll walk me home."

We stood there a moment longer than the sentence required, shoulder almost touching shoulder, the cooling coffee forgotten, and outside the window the February dusk was coming down over the rooftops like a lid closing, and somewhere in the city—in a tavern, in a clerk's office, in a voice we trusted—someone was writing down names.

I did not yet know that one of the streets we would walk together the next evening ran past Binder's Lane.

I did not yet know what we would hear coming from it.
