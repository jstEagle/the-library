## 9. The Raid on Binder's Lane

Tomas heard the boots before he heard the shouting.

He was in the cellar of the Hartwell shop, flat on his back beneath a workbench, stitching shut the wound in his side with a sailmaker's needle Rosalind had boiled in ink—*ink*, of all things, she'd said, because it was what they had and because her father would notice spirits missing. The needle's bite was nothing now. He had learned to file pain under other names. But the sound that came down through the floorboards, muffled by joists and coal dust and two feet of respectable brickwork, went through him like the needle had gone through him on the first night: slow, then all at once.

Boots. Many boots. Not the shuffling patrol-step of the night watch, who walked like men paid by the hour, but the hard synchronized tread of imperial grenadiers, forty pairs of heels striking cobbles in time, moving fast toward somewhere that was not here.

Not here. *Two streets over.*

He knew the rhythm of this city's nights now, nine days into his convalescence. He knew when Binder's Lane emptied and when it filled; he knew the widow Ostrava's dog began its idiot barking at the quarter-hour and stopped only when bribed with sausage; he knew the print quarter breathed in a certain way after midnight, a settling of timbers and cooling presses, like a large animal going to sleep. And so he knew, with the certainty of a man who had spent three years listening for exactly this sound across half an empire, that the boots were not passing through.

They were arriving.

* * *

He climbed the cellar stairs with his side screaming and put his ear to the door.

The shop above was dark, but not silent. Alois Hartwell worked late these days—he had worked late every night since Tomas arrived, though whether from industry or from some instinct Tomas could not name—and the scratch of his pen came faintly through the wood, steady as a clock. Beyond it, out in the street, the world had gone strange. Doors opening. A woman's voice rising and being cut off. The particular silence a street makes when soldiers fill it, the silence of everyone deciding at once to be indoors.

Then a voice, amplified by a brass speaking-trumpet, official and bored:

*"By order of His Imperial Majesty's magistrate: the premises known as number eleven Binder's Lane are seized. All persons will remain within. Resistance will be met with force."*

Number eleven Binder's Lane. The Kolar safehouse. The house where Tomas himself had slept four nights ago, before the fever drove him stumbling through the rain to the only address Emil's letter had given him inside the walls—the print shop, the loading door, the girl with ink-stained fingers who had hidden him without knowing why.

Two streets. He could have thrown a stone from this doorway onto their doorstep.

The first crash came a minute later—a door giving way, splintering, followed by shouts and the toppling of furniture and a woman's voice, high and furious, cursing them in three languages. Tomas recognized the voice. Marta. Marta who carried pamphlets sewn into the linings of fish barrels, who had once talked her way past a checkpoint with a dead goose over her arm and a smile that could have opened a bank vault. Marta whose laugh he could pick out of a crowd.

And another voice, younger, protesting, and then the sound of a body hitting a wall—not struck, just handled, the way soldiers handle things, as if everything were cargo.

Jozef. They had Jozef too.

Tomas stood in the dark stairwell with his hand pressed to his stitched side and felt something open in him that was worse than the wound. It was a familiar feeling. He had carried it since the frontier, since the morning he woke in a farmhouse and learned that the man beside whom he'd lain drunk on cheap wine had been a sergeant in disguise, since the column of deserters he'd been guiding south had been met at the ford by men who knew their names. Shame has a taste, he thought. It tastes like river water in January. Cold, and muddy, and impossible to spit out.

Because he could do the arithmetic. He had been doing it for days, refusing to finish it, and now the boots on Binder's Lane finished it for him.

The arrests had begun after he entered the city.

* * *

He counted backward through the nights, there in the stairwell, while below him the raid ground on and Alois Hartwell's pen scratched on overhead, steady, steady, as if the man could write his way through anything.

Nine days ago he had come through the east gate in a carter's coat with forged papers naming him Josef Brandl, journeyman bookbinder. The papers were good. Václav made good papers; Václav had made papers for half the deserters between the mountains and the sea. But good papers got a man through a gate. They did not explain why, three nights later, the watch had stopped every cart leaving the tannery district, or why a courier named Anka—who had run the river route for two years without a whisper of trouble—had been taken at the flour market in broad daylight, in front of God and the fishwives, as if someone had drawn the watch a picture.

Four arrests in nine days. Anka. The two boys who ran messages between the chandler's and the river stairs. And now Marta and Jozef, taken from the safest house in the quarter, a house that had swallowed fugitives for six years without losing so much as a spoon.

Six years without a spoon. Nine days with him, and the whole eastern line was coming apart like wet rope.

He wanted to believe it was coincidence. He lay awake at night constructing the case for coincidence the way a drowning man constructs rafts: the magistrate had been tightening the screw all winter; the censorship laws had grown teeth in February; any month might have been this month. It was a fine raft. It floated beautifully in the dark. And every morning it sank, because every morning he remembered the road behind him—the inn at Třeboň where he'd drunk one glass too many and talked too loudly about the price of paper, the gray-coated man reading a newspaper in the churchyard at Wittingau who had been reading the same page for an hour, the horseman who had kept pace with the mail coach for six miles and then, inexplicably, turned back.

He had told himself he'd lost them. Men in his trade who believed they'd lost a tail lived longer than men who knew better, but only slightly, and only by luck.

They hadn't lost him. They had never needed to catch him. They had simply let him run, the way a hunter lets a wounded boar run, because a wounded boar knows where the other boars sleep.

And now the other boars were being dragged out of their beds on Binder's Lane while he stood warm and hidden and useless in a stairwell, and somewhere across the frozen river Emil Hartwell was waiting for word that would not come, and somewhere upstairs a girl who had sewn him up with a sailmaker's needle was sleeping ten feet from a father who would hang if the truth about his cellar came out, and all of it—all of it—had begun to unravel the day Tomas Vrána walked through the east gate with his good papers and his bad luck and his tail of gray coats stretching back along the road like a shadow the sun refused to take from him.

* * *

The pen upstairs stopped scratching.

Tomas froze. In the street, the raid had found its rhythm—drawers pulled, floorboards pried, Marta's voice gone hoarse with cursing. And under it all, the stair door at his back seemed suddenly very thin, and the man on the other side of it very near.

He should go down. He should sit in the dark among the bales of waste paper and wait for morning. Every instinct honed by three years of this work said: be small, be still, be furniture. That was the discipline. That was how you survived.

But his feet took him up instead, one step, two, until his forehead nearly touched the door, and he heard Alois Hartwell exhale—a long breath, deliberate, the breath of a man setting down something heavy—and then the scrape of the chair, and footsteps crossing the floor above, unhurried, straight toward the stair door.

The latch lifted.

Alois stood in the frame holding a candle, and the light did something complicated to his face, carving the planes of it and leaving the eyes in shadow, so that Tomas could not read him at all—which frightened him more than anger would have. Behind the old man, through the window at the front of the shop, the glow of lanterns on Binder's Lane washed the ceiling of the print quarter orange.

"You hear it," Alois said. Not a question.

"Two streets over." Tomas's voice came out rougher than he intended. "The Kolar house."

"The Kolar house," Alois agreed. He turned the candle slightly, and the flame leaned, and in its lean the shadows on the wall swayed like hanged men. "Marta Kolar carried my waste paper to the river for eleven years. Eleven years, and tonight she is in a wagon with her hands tied, calling a lieutenant's mother a name I will not repeat in my own shop." Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile. "She always did have a poet's vocabulary."

"I'm sorry." The words were ash. He had said them to too many people in too many doorways. "Herr Hartwell, I have to tell you something, and you're not going to want to hear it."

"They began after you came." Alois said it flatly, without cruelty, the way a physician says *the bone is broken*. "I keep accounts, young man. Of everything. It is my profession and my affliction. Four arrests in nine days, all on the eastern routes, all within reach of wherever you had been." He looked at Tomas for a long moment, and the candle flame stood perfectly still between them, as if even fire was waiting. "I am not telling you this to accuse you. I am telling you because you clearly already know, and a man who knows is more useful than a man who suspects."

"What do you need me to know?" Tomas asked, and hated how his voice shook.

"That whoever follows you does not yet follow you *here*." Alois set the candle on the stair rail, and the light steadied. "Or we would already be having a different conversation, with different men in the room. Which means we have time. Not much. Time enough to ask what you brought with you besides that letter and that hole in your side."

Tomas closed his eyes. The gray man in the churchyard. The horseman turning back. The inn at Třeboň, his own voice too loud, saying words he could no longer remember precisely—only the shape of them, the dangerous shape, talk of paper and prices and the river running low.

"A trail," he said. "I brought a trail. Maybe. I don't know how long it is or who holds the other end. But someone watched me at Wittingau, and someone was asking questions at Třeboň, and the arrests started—" He stopped. Started again. "There may be a description of me in a magistrate's office by now. There may be two."

Below them, out in the dark, a wagon rolled away from Binder's Lane, iron-rimmed wheels ringing on the stones, carrying Marta and Jozef toward whatever rooms waited for them in the citadel. The sound faded down toward the river and was gone, and the street settled back into its lie of quiet, and Alois Hartwell picked up his candle and looked at Tomas with those unreadable eyes for one moment longer.

"Then tomorrow," he said quietly, "you and I are going to have a conversation about every mile of road between here and the frontier. Every face. Every glass of wine. My daughter will tell you I forget nothing." He turned to go, then paused, his back to Tomas, the candle throwing his shadow long down the stairwell like a second man descending ahead of him. "And Tomas—when you count your sins tonight, count them accurately. A man who blames himself for everything is no more useful than a man who blames himself for nothing. Both of them stand still."

His footsteps crossed the shop. The pen resumed its scratching, steady as a clock, as if the world had not just lost two more of its small brave hands.

Tomas went down into the cellar and did not sleep. He sat among the bales of waste paper with his knees drawn up, doing the arithmetic again, finishing it this time, writing the sum in the place where shame lived: *four arrested, two streets, nine days.* And beneath it, in letters he could not stop himself from forming: *and the last crossing is in three weeks, and if they find this house before the ice breaks, there will be no crossing at all.*

Somewhere above him, dawn was hours off. Somewhere across the river, Emil was alive and waiting and did not know. And somewhere behind Tomas, back up the long white road, a gray-coated man was folding his newspaper at last, and setting out, unhurried, in the cold.
