## 10. Father's Ledger, Opened

The ledger lived behind the third row of type cases, in a drawer that stuck unless you lifted as you pulled—a trick Rosalind had known since she was nine years old, when her father still let her fetch things and hadn't yet begun to guard them.

She told herself she wasn't searching. She was looking for the spare quills, which were kept there, which had always been kept there, which her father would expect her to find without asking. The lie sat comfortably enough in her mouth. Lies did, now. She'd been learning their grammar all week: how to answer Voss's clerks with a level voice, how to tell Frau Binder that no, nothing was wrong at the Hartwell shop, how to look through Tomas as if he were a journeyman and not a wound walking upright in her cellar.

The drawer stuck. She lifted as she pulled.

The quills were there. So was the ledger.

It was smaller than she expected—smaller than the order book that lay open on the counter every day for any clerk of the crown to read. This one wore plain brown boards gone soft at the corners, unmarked by title or name, the kind of book a man buys three at a time from the stationer and forgets. But it was wrapped in oilcloth against damp, and tied with string, and string is a kind of sentence: *this matters.*

Rosalind stood in the back room with the thing in both hands and listened. Upstairs, the front door's bell. Her father's tread crossing to serve whoever had come in—the widow Kraus, probably, wanting funeral notices set, paying in small coins counted twice. The ordinary sounds of an honest shop. Beneath them, the river groaned under its ice, a sound like a giant turning over in sleep.

She untied the string.

The first pages were what she expected—orders, deliveries, sums. Waste paper bought by the bale from this mill or that. Bales sold south, downriver, to buyers whose names meant nothing: Herr Falk, a factor in some town she couldn't place; a warehouse concern called Brandt & Sons that she was almost certain did not exist; entries marked only with a compass rose drawn small and neat in the margin, the way a schoolboy decorates his copybook.

Then the columns changed.

Payments. Not received—paid. And paid out at a scale that made her breath go shallow. Sums that could keep a family fed through two winters. Sums that could bribe a ferryman. Sums that could buy silence, or passage, or both. Beside many of them, in her father's small upright hand, a single word or two: *crossing.* *Crossing, lost.* *Crossing—three souls.* *Doctor, for the woman on the ice.*

Three souls.

Her thumb found the dates before her mind agreed to read them. The earliest entry was two years old. Two years and one month.

Two years and one month since the letter had come from the frontier regiment, written by a captain who had never met Emil, expressing the crown's regret and enclosing her brother's effects: a prayer book, a pipe, a coat button. Two years and one month since Alois Hartwell had stood in this same back room holding those few objects and said, in the flat voice he used for bad news and prices alike, *He is dead, Rosalind. We will be dignified about it.*

And one month after that, her father had begun paying strangers to move people across a frozen river.

* * *

She didn't hear him come down. That was the thing about her father—he moved through the shop the way dust moves through light, present everywhere, noticed nowhere. One moment the room held only the ledger and the smell of ink and lamp oil; the next, Alois Hartwell stood in the doorway with his coat still on and his hat in his hand, and his eyes went from her face to the open book and back again.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere overhead the widow Kraus's bell rang, and the door closed, and then the shop was theirs alone, and the silence had weight, like wet wool.

"You lifted as you pulled," he said at last. "I taught you that."

"Yes."

"I should have moved it years ago." He came into the room slowly, set his hat on the workbench, aligned it with its edge—a gesture so familiar it hurt, her careful father, her precise, gray, invisible father—and took the ledger from her hands with the gentleness of a man taking back something borrowed. "Sit down, Rosalind."

"I don't want to sit down."

"Sit down anyway."

She sat on the cutting stool, the one she'd occupied since childhood, and looked up at him, and found she was shaking. Not with fear. With something that had been building since the night a bleeding stranger fell against her loading door and asked for ink and paper, nothing else—something that needed a name, and she was afraid of what the name would turn out to be.

"How long," she said.

"You've read the dates."

"Say it. I want to hear you say it."

Alois turned the ledger closed in his hands and held it against his chest like a shield worn backwards. He looked, for a moment, very old—the lamplight finding every line around his mouth, the ink stains at his cuff that never quite washed out. Then he straightened, because her father could always straighten, could always become the respectable printer, the loyal subject, the man the magistrate played cards with on Thursdays.

"Since they sent me my son home in a box of belongings," he said. "That's how long."

* * *

"He isn't dead." It wasn't a question anymore; Tomas's letter had settled that. "You knew. You've known—"

"I suspected. Then I knew." He set the ledger down on the bench beside his hat and stood with both hands flat on the wood, a compositor's stance, braced over his case. "The letter the army sent was a kindness and a cowardice. Men desert at the frontier every winter. Some freeze. Some are shot. Some—" his jaw worked—"some walk away and are written down as dead because it's easier for everyone. For the regiment. For the family. For the empire." He almost smiled. "The empire loves a tidy ledger, Rosalind. I learned that young."

"And you never told me."

"No."

"Not when I cried at the table. Not when I set a memorial line for him in the parish book with my own hands. You watched me mourn him, Father. You *let* me."

"Yes." The word dropped between them like a piece of lead type, cold and final and exactly shaped. "Because a girl who weeps openly at her brother's grave is safe. A girl whose brother is alive and treasonous is not safe, and cannot pretend to be. Grief was your armor, whether you consented to wear it or not. I'm sorry for that. I'll be sorry for it the rest of my life. But I'd do it again tonight."

She was on her feet without deciding to stand. "You talk about consent. You talk about casting me in your play—did you think I wouldn't find out? Did you think I'd just keep setting type forever while you—" she gestured at the ledger, at the neat compass roses, at two years of secret sums—"while you ran a railroad of fugitives out of my father's cellar?"

"There is no railroad out of my cellar. There's money, moving quietly, to people who know how to use it. I have never seen a crossing. I have never touched a passenger. That's the design." His voice stayed level, but she heard the effort in it now, the strain beneath, like a press screw tightened to its limit. "Tomas can tell you the rest better than I. I know only what the letters say. I am the Cutter. Nothing more."

"The Cutter." The word arrived strangely, half recognition. "Tomas mentioned—"

"Tomas knows the name. Everyone in it knows the name. No one has ever known the face, and that is the entire art of it." He picked up the ledger again, turned it over in his hands. "Voss sits across a card table from me every Thursday evening and asks, in his roundabout way, whether I've heard anything troubling in the print quarter. And I pour him wine and tell him about paper costs and the insolence of apprentices, and he goes home satisfied that Alois Hartwell is the dullest man in the city." A pause. "Dullness is expensive, Rosalind. It has cost me more than money. But it keeps people alive."

"Petra's cousins crossed in January." She didn't know why she said it—that name, that fact—but it rose in her like water finding a crack. "The Weiss girls. Everyone said they'd gone to an aunt. They crossed, didn't they. On your money."

"On everyone's money. Mine mostly." He said it without pride, which somehow made it worse—or better; she couldn't decide. "A bale of waste paper weighs four hundred pounds. A person folded small weighs less. The arithmetic is simple. The courage belongs to others—to the ferrymen, to the guides, to Emil most of all. I only pay the tolls."

*Only pays the tolls.* She stared at him. All her life she had catalogued her father the way she catalogued typefaces: by weight, by wear, by what he could be relied upon to do. Reliable. Careful. A man who checked the weather twice before hanging washing, who read the censor's bulletins aloud at breakfast with disapproval and perfect compliance, who had once made her reprint an entire edition because a marginal comma might be construed as commentary. She had built her whole doctrine of survival on his example—invisible, impeccable, unremarkable—and here was the invoice for what that invisibility had purchased: two years of treason, conducted from the exact center of respectability, under the magistrate's own nose, funded by a man who apologized for nothing except the grief he'd let her carry alone.

The fury was still there. She could feel it, banked but burning. But underneath it, rising through it, unstoppable as spring water under ice, was something else.

"You mourned him too," she said quietly. "All this time. Alone."

Alois Hartwell, who had not wept at the graveside, who had said *we will be dignified about it* and had been dignified through two winters of funerals and inspections and Thursday evenings with Voss, looked down at the ledger in his hands. When he spoke, his voice had a crack in it she had never heard, fine as a hairline flaw in a matrix—one tap and the whole face of type might shatter.

"Every payment," he said, "I entered as if it were a letter to him. Every compass rose—I drew it because he drew them, when he was eight, all over the margins of his copybooks. He wanted to see the world. I gave him a print shop instead. So now I buy him the world, one soul at a time, and I sign it with a boy's doodle, and I tell myself that counts as correspondence." He closed the ledger. "It doesn't. But it's what I have."

Rosalind crossed the room and put her arms around her father, and felt him go rigid with surprise—for he was not a man who was held, had not been held since her mother died—and then, gradually, like ice giving way at the edges of a river, soften.

"You should have told me," she said into his shoulder.

"Yes."

"I could have helped."

"That," he said, "was the one thing I swore would never happen." He drew back and held her at arm's length, and studied her face with the appraising eye he brought to proofs—looking, she realized, for errors. For smudges. For the marks of everything she'd carried these past weeks: a wounded man in the cellar, a dead brother breathing across the river, a secret growing so heavy it bent the shelves. "But you've found the ledger, and Tomas is under my roof, and the ice will break within the fortnight. Which means the time for keeping you clean is over."

"Father—"

"Listen to me." His grip tightened, gentle and absolute. "Voss suspects something. He suspects everything and proves nothing—it's how he works. If he comes for us, he comes for the whole quarter. And when he does, you will know where the ledger is not: it will not be here. You will know nothing of the Cutter. You will be my daughter, a compositor, a woman who cuts typefaces and keeps her head down. Do you understand me?"

She understood him perfectly. And standing there in the lamplight, with the smell of ink in her nose and her father's hands on her shoulders, Rosalind understood something else besides: that she had inherited his carefulness and none of his patience, and that the doctrine of invisibility—the one she'd built her life on, the one he'd built his treason inside—was finished. The city outside was waking. The river was groaning. Somewhere a gray-coated man was folding his newspaper.

"Understood," she lied, easily, fluently, in his own native language.

And upstairs, faint through the floorboards, came the knock at the front door—two measured raps, unhurried, unmistakable.

Magistrate Voss's knock.
