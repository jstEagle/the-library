## 11. Voss's Quiet Question

The bell above the shop door had not rung all morning, and Alois Hartwell had learned, over forty years at the trade, to distrust silence the way sailors distrust a windless sea. It was not that he expected trouble precisely—trouble in this city arrived with boots and shouting, never with courtesy—but that the morning had a quality of held breath about it, the way February always did when the river began to think about thawing. The ice on the Vltava groaned in the small hours now, a sound like some vast animal turning over in its sleep, and every man who lived by secrets felt it in his spine.

He was cutting a matrix when the bell finally rang—not the shop bell, but the smaller one at the loading door, three notes, the signal they had agreed upon. He set down his graver with hands that did not shake, because he had trained them not to, and called out in his flattest voice: "Coming."

*They had agreed upon.* The phrase sat strangely in his mind as he crossed the workroom. When had he become a man who *agreed upon* things? He thought of himself, still, as what he had been for most of his life: a printer, a cutter of letters, a widower who kept his accounts clean and his opinions to himself and his grief locked in the drawer where his wife's letters lived. But a man could perform respectability so long, Alois was beginning to understand, that the performance ate the original, the way type wore down against the platen until the impression came out soft and wrong, and no amount of ink could restore the sharpness of the first cut.

Rosalind opened the inner door for him before he reached it. She had been sorting galleys; there was a smudge of ink along her jaw like a bruise, and her eyes went past him to the street, quick and frightened, then back to his face.

"He's here," she said quietly. "Magistrate Voss. He's asking after you. I told him you were at the forge having a chase repaired." Her voice was steady. She had her mother's steadiness, God rest Anna, and her brother's stubbornness, and she did not know—she could not yet know—how much of her father's life was built on exactly the kind of lie Rosalind had just told without blinking.

"Good girl," Alois said, and hated the words even as he said them, because they were the words a loyal subject would say, and because she believed he meant them innocently.

"No," he said, catching her sleeve as she turned. "Let me see him alone. You have orders to sort. If he speaks to you, be dull. Be boring. You are very good at boring, my dear—it is your finest quality."

Something flickered across her face—hurt, quickly mastered—and he wanted to take the words back, to tell her that her invisibility was armor he had spent years forging for her, plate by plate, the same way he cut matrices: slowly, precisely, so that no crack would ever show. But Voss was waiting in the front room among the specimen books, and there was no time for tenderness that could not pass inspection.

* * *

Magistrate Konrad Voss occupied space the way a heavy press occupies a room: not moving much, but making everything near him aware of his weight. He stood by the window with his hat in his hand—a courtesy, always the courtesy, they had known each other thirty years—and watched the street below with the mild attention of a man watching weather.

"Alois." He turned, and his face arranged itself into the smile of old acquaintance, the one they had both worn at Anna's funeral, at Emil's memorial service, at a hundred dinners where the wine was good and the conversation was careful. "Your daughter tells me you're at the forge. Yet here you are."

"The forge sent word the part wouldn't come till tomorrow. I hate an idle morning." Alois hung his own hat on the peg with deliberate slowness. "You'll take coffee? Rosalind makes it better than any house on the Karlova."

"In a moment." Voss drifted toward the workroom doorway, and Alois followed him the way one follows a slow leak—knowing where it will end, unable to hurry it. "I was just admiring this."

Voss's finger was resting on the matrix Alois had been cutting when the bell rang. A lowercase *g*, double-story, the ear still rough, the bowl unfinished. It lay in its tray of soft metal like a small sleeping thing, and the magistrate's finger—the finger of a man who signed death warrants twice a year and dined with their families afterward—traced its edge with something close to reverence.

"You've lost none of your touch," Voss said. "Thirty years I've known you, and I still say no one in Bohemia cuts a finer letter. There are men in Vienna who'd pay you a fortune to come south, you know. I've told them. I've told them Alois Hartwell will never leave his shop; he's a man rooted like a linden tree."

"Vienna's air disagrees with me," Alois said mildly. "And the coffee is worse than Rosalind's."

Voss laughed, a dry sound like paper folding. Then he picked up the matrix—actually lifted it, turning it in the light—and Alois felt something cold slide down between his shoulder blades, because a magistrate did not pick up a craftsman's work unless he wished the craftsman to know that everything in the room was his to handle.

"I'll come to why I've come," Voss said, setting the *g* down with exaggerated care. "There was a stranger seen near Binder's Lane two nights ago. And before that—" he consulted nothing, needing no notes; his memory was famous and terrible— "a man answering the same description loitering by the grain market on Thursday last. Dark coat, foreign cut. A limp favoring the left leg." His eyes came up, mild as milk. "Seen near your loading door, Alois. By a neighbor who keeps late hours and early grudges."

The pause stretched. Outside, a cart rattled over the cobbles; somewhere a church bell counted eleven, unhurried, indifferent. Alois discovered that his heart was perfectly calm. This was the thing he had learned about himself in these two years since the ledger began, the thing that shamed him more than the treason itself: that lying did not frighten him. That it came easily. That somewhere beneath the honest printer he had performed for half a century there had always been this other man, waiting, fluent, and that he could no longer quite remember which one was the original.

"A stranger at my loading door," Alois said, and let a frown gather, the frown of a man searching his memory. "In February? Konrad, my loading door opens for waste paper and the rag man. No stranger comes to buy type from the back of a print shop—he'd come through the front like a Christian."

"So your neighbor lies?"

"My neighbor drinks. There's a difference of perhaps four glasses between the two." He moved past Voss to the stove, busied himself with the coffee things, giving his hands occupation because occupied hands persuaded the face. "But I'll tell you what I'll do. I'll ask at the taverns. Printers hear everything—the trade runs on gossip like a wheel runs on grease. If there's a limping foreigner buying bread in this quarter, I'll know his name by Friday."

"You are a good friend to the crown, Alois."

The words landed like a weight on fresh type—too much pressure, and the impression would show every flaw. Alois poured the coffee with a steady hand and passed the cup across.

"I'm a good friend to quiet," he said. "A man with daughters"—he caught himself; the correction cost him something, and he made it smoothly—"a man with a daughter to marry off wants nothing in this world but quiet. Trouble is bad for business, and business is all I have left." He gestured at the workroom, the cases, the press under its dust sheet, the whole honest architecture of his life. "This is Anna's dowry money and my father's name and forty years of straight dealing, Konrad. I'd sooner burn it than have it mixed in whatever dirty water flows down Binder's Lane."

It was a good speech. It was, he reflected with distant horror, a *beautiful* speech—every note true except the truth, every sentiment genuine except the sentiment. He had built it the way he built a page: margins measured, lines justified, no orphaned word to catch the eye. And Voss drank it the way readers drink a well-set pamphlet, without seeing the hands that had composed it.

* * *

They talked another hour, of which Alois remembered almost nothing afterward—the new tariffs on paper stock, the health of Voss's wife, the scandal of the young officer who had shot himself in the garrison at Kutná Hora rather than face a debt. Voss talked the way powerful men talk, generously, believing that information given freely creates obligation. Twice he circled back to the stranger, and twice Alois turned him aside, once with the taverns, once with a story about a Hungarian type-founder who had indeed passed through in autumn—dead end, dead end, scatter the trail with plausible bones.

And then, at the door, hat in hand again, Voss paused.

"You never speak of your son," he said.

The sentence hung in the cold air of the doorway. Somewhere down the street a dog barked, and the ice on the river gave a long groan that they both pretended not to hear.

"There's little enough to say," Alois answered. "Dead men don't write."

"No," Voss agreed softly. "Dead men don't." He put on his hat, settled it with two fingers. "Though I'll confess something, old friend, since we're old friends. In twenty-two years of service I have buried many deserters on paper. And do you know what I have learned? Paper is patient. Paper waits. A man declared dead at the frontier can walk into a café in Pest five years later wearing another man's coat, and the paper says he's dead, and everyone believes the paper—because the paper was signed, and stamped, and filed." His eyes rested on Alois with terrible gentleness. "Paper is the crown's memory, Alois. But it is not the crown's knowledge. Sometimes the two disagree."

"I'll drink to that," Alois said, "if you explain to me what it means."

"It means nothing. It means an old man philosophizing on a cold doorstep." Voss smiled, and for one moment—for one unguarded moment—the smile fell away entirely, and underneath it Alois saw not the magistrate but the boy who had once shared stolen plums with him on this same step, fifty years ago, before either of them knew what the empire would ask of them. "Take care of your daughter, Alois. These streets are getting unkind to quiet people. And if a limping stranger should come to your door again—" the smile returned, complete, seamless, awful— "remember that I dine alone on Thursdays, and that I pay well for company."

He walked away up the lane without hurrying, a gray man in a gray coat, and Alois stood in the doorway until he turned the corner, holding the doorframe the way a man holds a railing on a pitching deck.

* * *

Inside, Rosalind was waiting in the workroom, pretending to sort galleys, her hands still. She read his face the way she read proofs—at a glance, missing nothing.

"Well?" she asked.

"He suspects nothing," Alois said. "Or he suspects everything and wishes me to know it. With Konrad those are the same thing." He took off his coat and hung it with great precision. "He mentioned a stranger at the loading door. Someone talked. A neighbor, he said, but neighbors are what magistrates call informers when they're being polite."

Rosalind had gone pale. "Then Tomas has to move. Tonight, or—"

"Tomas stays where he is until his leg can carry him past the first checkpoint, which is three days, and we both know it. Panic moves faster than soldiers, and panic is what kills people in this trade." He heard his own voice, level, instructional, and marveled at it. *This trade.* As if he had served an apprenticeship in hiding fugitives, as if there were a guild for it, a masterwork piece, a journeyman's wage. Two years ago he had been a man who printed hymnals and tax notices and kept his politics between himself and his pipe. Now he spoke of checkpoints and safe houses with the fluency of long practice, and the fluency frightened him far more than Voss did.

Because that was the truth he carried upstairs each night and lay beside like a second wife: not fear of discovery, but ease. He lied to Voss and it flowed. He forged a customs stamp and it looked natural under the lamp. He wrote *the Cutter* in a disguised hand at the bottom of payment instructions and felt—God forgive him—nothing. No terror, no thrill. Just the quiet satisfaction of a job well set, a line justified, a page ready for the press.

His wife had died believing him an honest man. His daughter believed it still. And the worst of it, the thing he could not confess in any confessional in Christendom, was that both women had been right, and both were wrong, and the two truths coexisted inside him as easily as ink and water in a well-stirred vat—apparently one substance, forever refusing to truly mix.

"Father." Rosalind's voice brought him back. She was watching him with an expression he couldn't parse—something between worry and a question she hadn't yet found the shape of. "You handled him well. Better than I could have."

"I've had practice lying to important men," he said lightly. "Forty years of telling clients their schedules are reasonable."

She laughed, and the laugh went through him like a blade through wet paper, and he smiled back at her with the whole of his face, and it was not a lie, and it was, and he no longer knew the difference, and that was the price, that was the whole terrible price of the thing he had chosen: not the risk of the gallows, but the slow erosion of the line between the man who lied and the man who loved, until loving became something you did in the same breath as deceiving, seamlessly, professionally, well.

After she went up to bed, he unlocked the cellar and descended with the lamp, and took out the ledger, and added the week's payments in the disguised hand—three names, four names, sums that would feed a family through spring—and as he wrote he found himself thinking of Voss's finger on the unfinished *g*, tracing the edge of a letter that did not yet exist anywhere else in the world.

*Paper is the crown's memory,* Voss had said. *But it is not the crown's knowledge.*

Alois closed the ledger and sat in the dark of his own cellar, listening to the ice groan on the river, and understood suddenly, with the clarity that comes only too late, that Voss had not come to accuse him at all. He had come to measure him. To lay the rule against the type and see how close the fit was.

And the fit, God help him, had been perfect.

Upstairs, faintly, he heard Rosalind's floorboard creak, once, twice—someone pacing, someone sleepless, someone keeping a vigil over a wounded man whose existence he had just denied to the most dangerous man in the city. Alois blew out the lamp and sat on in the dark, an honest traitor in his dishonest kingdom, and began, methodically, to plan the lie he would need for Thursday.
