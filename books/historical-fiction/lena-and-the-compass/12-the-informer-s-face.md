## 12. The Informer's Face

Tomas had learned arithmetic twice in his life: once as a boy in a village school where the master beat sums into him with a willow switch, and once on the far bank of the river, where Emil Hartwell taught him a different kind of counting—the kind that kept people alive. Dates, distances, weights of men against weights of suspicion. How many hours a fugitive could wait in a cellar before the silence itself became dangerous. How many crossings a route could bear before the ice or the soldiers gave way.

It was the second arithmetic he was doing now, at three in the morning, by the light of a shuttered lamp in the print shop's cellar, with delivery schedules spread around him like the pieces of a game no one would choose to play.

The shop above him slept. Or seemed to. He could hear the building settling, the old beams ticking as the night cold worked its way through them, and beneath that, faint as a pulse, the groan of the river ice—a sound like some enormous animal turning over in its sleep, restless, half-awake. In two weeks, perhaps three, it would break altogether, and then the last chance of the season would go downriver with it.

He turned a page. The paper was cheap and grey, the ink faded to brown in places, and Rosalind's neat hand marched across it in columns: dates, clients, quantities of stock delivered to this address and that. She kept her father's books beautifully. Of course she did. Everything about Rosalind Hartwell was precise—her letters cut clean, her grief folded small and put away in drawers, her fear worn like an undergarment beneath a plain dress, invisible to everyone who didn't know how to look.

Tomas was learning how to look.

* * *

It had begun, properly, four nights ago, after the raid on Binder's Lane.

Two couriers taken from the Kolar safehouse in broad daylight—not dragged out screaming, which would have been sloppy, but led away quietly, politely, the way the secret police moved when they were certain of their ground. Old Binder himself had been left untouched, his shutters closed for a day, then opened again as though nothing happened, which was its own kind of message: *we took what we came for; you are not worth the trouble.*

Tomas had sat in the cellar with that fact lodged in his chest like a fishhook. Because the arrests had not begun when the network began. They had begun—precisely, unmistakably—after *he* entered the city. After a bleeding stranger collapsed against the loading door of the most respectable print shop on Compositor's Row and begged a girl for ink and paper.

He had lain awake since then turning it over. The route south ran through six hands between this city and the border: printers, carters, a widow who kept geese along the towpath, a ferryman's son. Six hands, and every one of them known only to the next link in the chain—that was the design, Emil's design, built like a typecase, each compartment sealed from its neighbors. A leak anywhere should have drowned only one compartment.

But the arrests hadn't come one compartment at a time. They had come in pairs, in patterns, always a step ahead of the schedule changes, always just late enough to seem like coincidence. Someone wasn't reading the chain. Someone was reading the whole page.

So Tomas did the only thing he knew how to do with dread, which was to make it into numbers.

* * *

He worked by rule and column. First, every arrest since midwinter—he reconstructed them from what Emil's letter had warned of, from what the network's survivors had whispered up the line, from the gossip that reached even a hiding man: the binder's apprentice taken in January; the two couriers now; a carter named Dusek whose wagon had been stopped on the south road with nothing found but whose family had been questioned for three days afterward, which meant the police had expected to find something and been disappointed.

Then, opposite those dates, the delivery schedules. Waste paper went out from sympathetic shops twice a week—bales of spoiled sheets and trimmings that were sometimes not entirely waste, that sometimes had a fugitive sewn into their middle like a raisin in bad bread. Rosalind's ledger recorded every legitimate shipment. The illegitimate ones lived in a separate accounting, scrawled on scraps Tomas had collected over the past week from the network's surviving messengers, cross-checked against the days Alois Hartwell had dined out, the days the loading door had stood open past dark.

He laid the columns side by side and looked at them until his eyes burned.

There. Not a shout—an echo. Every arrest had fallen within two days of a scheduled movement. Not after the movement, which would mean the soldiers had simply stumbled onto something. Before it, or almost with it—as if someone had passed word ahead, and the soldiers had arranged themselves at the far end of the journey like cats at a mousehole, patient, unhurried, certain.

And the movements that had gone undisturbed—all but two of them—shared one feature. They had been arranged in person, by word of mouth, at meetings Tomas could trace: the goose-widow's kitchen, the ferryman's loft, the back room of the saddler's on Tanners' Street.

The shipments that had failed were the ones arranged by note.

Someone was reading notes. Someone with access to the messages that passed between the city cells—and access narrow enough, regular enough, innocent enough that no one had ever thought to guard against it.

Tomas sat back in the cold and let the answer assemble itself, the way a line of type assembles in a composing stick: one piece of lead at a time, until suddenly there is a word where before there was only metal.

The errand girl.

Petra.

* * *

He had seen her perhaps a dozen times. She came to the shop three mornings a week with bread and eggs from her mother's stall, and once with a bolt of linen for the loft curtains, and she moved through the print shop like a small bright bird through a library—chattering, touching everything she was told not to touch, asking Rosalind questions about the press until Rosalind's sternness dissolved into something warmer than Tomas had seen her wear anywhere else. Petra adored her. It was written all over her, the way weather is written on a landscape: the way she saved her best smile for Rosalind, the way she lingered over deliveries that needed no lingering, the way she said *Fraulein Hartwell* as if the name were a sweet she was reluctant to finish.

She was sixteen, maybe seventeen. She had a gap in her teeth she covered with her hand when she laughed. Her father had died in the imperial levies the same year Emil Hartwell had supposedly died at the frontier, and her mother sold vegetables from a stall in the Fish Market with her hands wrapped in wool against the damp.

And three mornings a week, Petra carried messages between the city's printer-cells, tucked into the false bottoms of her market baskets, because no watchman in the world stops a girl delivering eggs.

Tomas put his head in his hands.

He wanted very much to be wrong. He went back through the columns a third time, a fourth, hunting for another face that fit the pattern—for the carter, for the saddler's boy, for anyone whose guilt would sit less heavily in his stomach. But arithmetic is honest the way rivers are honest. The pattern held. The messages Petra carried were the ones that preceded arrests; the words spoken aloud in kitchens and lofts sailed safely past the soldiers' ears as though they were made of smoke.

Someone inside had been squeezed. And of all the hands on the route, hers was the smallest, the youngest, the least able to close around its own fate and hold on.

He thought of the interrogation rooms in the fortress by the north gate—everyone on the far bank knew about them, the way sailors know about reefs. He thought of what threat would be laid before a vegetable-seller's daughter to make her talk. It would not need to be elaborate. It would need only to be her mother's name, spoken softly, by a man with a list in his lap. Perhaps a demonstration first. Perhaps only the promise of one. Fear does not require craftsmanship.

No villain here, he thought, and the phrase tasted like ash. Only another person paying survival's price. He had paid it himself often enough—in lies told at checkpoints, in names he had given falsely, in the moment on his own crossing, two years ago now, when a fellow fugitive had broken his ankle on the ice and Tomas had kept walking because stopping meant dying, and he had heard the man calling behind him for longer than he could bear to remember. He had paid survival's price and called it necessity. Petra was paying it in smaller coin, and the collectors were the same men either way.

That was the sickening part—not that she had betrayed them, but that he understood her so completely, and understood too that understanding changed nothing. The knowledge in his head would still get people killed whether his heart bled for her or not.

* * *

The lamp was burning low. Upstairs, a floorboard creaked, and Tomas went still, hand drifting to the iron bar he kept within reach these days—but the steps that followed were light and familiar, descending the cellar stair with the particular care of someone who knew which ones complained.

Rosalind appeared in the lamplight in her shawl, her hair loose and unbound for sleep, and the sight of her struck him as it always did now—with a warmth he distrusted precisely because he could not afford it. She looked at the papers strewn across the workbench, at his face, and whatever she saw there made her come the rest of the way down without a word.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"I've been counting."

"Counting what?" She pulled the second stool to the bench and sat, and he watched her eyes move across the columns—his crude lists beside her beautiful ledger—and saw the moment she understood what the juxtaposition meant. The color left her face by degrees, like water draining from a lock.

"The arrests," she said quietly. "You've matched them to the schedules."

"And the schedules match the messages." He turned the scrap of paper toward her, the one with the pattern marked in charcoal. "Every shipment arranged by note has been met. Every arrangement made aloud has passed clean. Someone is reading our letters, Rosalind. Someone who carries them."

She stared at the page. He watched her do the same arithmetic he had done, watched it arrive in her the same way, and watched her arrive a heartbeat behind him at the same name—because her breath caught, and her hand came up to her mouth, and she said, "No," as if the syllable could unsay the conclusion.

"It isn't proof," he said. "It's a shape. I've been trying to find another shape all night."

"Petra." Her voice cracked on the name. "She's a child, Tomas. She brings us bread. She—" Rosalind stopped. He saw her thinking of the gap-toothed laugh, the lingering goodbyes, the adoration worn as openly as a ribbon. "They'd have threatened her mother. That's all it would take. That's all it would ever take."

"Yes."

"They'll have shown her the fortress." Rosalind's hand had dropped to the table and lay there, fingers slightly curled, as though the ledger had become hot. "Or worse—they won't have shown her anything. They'll have just described it. Men like Voss know that imagination is crueler than a tour." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and clear. "Is she still carrying? Now? This week?"

"If the pattern holds, yes. Which means every message she touches is already in Voss's pocket before the ink dries."

"Then we can't send anything true through her again." Rosalind pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. When she lowered them she had mastered herself, mostly, the way she always did—grief and fury folded into neat squares and put away in drawers. But her voice, when it came, was rough. "We can't turn her out either. If she stops coming, if she vanishes, whoever handles her will know she's been discovered, and they'll squeeze her harder—or use her. And if we confront her, she'll be terrified, and terror makes noise, and noise brings soldiers to this door."

"I know."

"Do you?" There was no cruelty in it. She reached across the table and touched the back of his hand, briefly, an anchor rather than an accusation. "You're the one who's been hunted. Tell me what happens to girls who get caught between the crown and the cause, Tomas. You've seen the far end of it."

He had. He said so. Neither of them said the rest of it aloud: that the far end was usually a river, and the river did not ask questions.

For a long moment they sat in the lamplight while the ice groaned beneath the city like a thing dreaming of spring. Then Rosalind drew a slow breath, and he recognized the sound—it was the breath she took before setting type, before committing letters to metal, before making something permanent out of intention.

"She comes Tuesday," she said. "With the eggs. She'll want to stay and chatter, she always stays, she asks me about the matrices—" Rosalind's voice caught and steadied. "I'm going to talk to her. Gently. Not corner her. I'm going to make it safe for her to tell me, if telling me is what she needs. And if it isn't—if I read it wrong—then I've lost nothing but an hour of chatter over eggs."

"And if you read it right?"

Rosalind looked toward the cellar stairs, toward the sleeping shop above, toward the press that stood in the front room like a black faithful animal waiting for orders.

"Then I'll decide what mercy costs," she said, "and whether we can afford it. But Tomas—" she turned back to him, and in the low light her face was very young and very old at once, "—when she tells me, whatever she tells me, I want you to remember something. Whoever squeezed her used my brother's route to do it. Emil built a road for the desperate, and Voss drove a cart through it using a child as his horse. If there's a villain in this story, I refuse to let it be a girl who sells eggs."

She rose, gathered her shawl around her, and paused at the foot of the stairs.

"Tuesday," she said. "Pray I'm wrong."

Then she climbed into the dark, and Tomas sat alone with his columns and his lamp, and understood that she would not be wrong—she was never wrong about people—and that in two days a frightened girl would walk through the loading door carrying eggs, and secrets, and the rope that was slowly drawing tight around every one of them.

Above him, faint through the floorboards, the river groaned again. Closer now. Impatient.

The ice was running out of time, and so were they all.
