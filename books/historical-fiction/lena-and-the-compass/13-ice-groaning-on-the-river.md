## 13. Ice groaning on the River

The river began to speak on a Tuesday.

Rosalind heard it first as a sound she could not name—a long, low groan rolling up from the water and through the foundations of the shop, through the cellar where Tomas slept, through the floorboards and up into her bones as she bent over the stone sink, scrubbing ink from her nails. It was not the creak of a house settling. It was older than houses. It was the sound of a thing that had held still for four months deciding, at last, to move.

She dried her hands and went to the loading door and stood in the grey morning light with her breath smoking in front of her, and she watched the river.

All winter it had been a road. A white road, rutted and patched with grey where the current ran shallow beneath, wide enough for sledges and smugglers and the occasional fool in a fur cap who crossed at noon out of bravado. All winter it had been the network's artery—Emil's road, though she still could not write the thought without her hand hesitating, without the ink pooling slightly where her pen paused. *Emil is alive. Emil is on the far bank. Emil has been guiding crossings since the month they told her he was dead.* Some truths she had carried for weeks now and still they did not sit level inside her, like type set on a tilted galley, everything true and nothing square.

Now the road was dying. She could see it. The ice had gone the color of dirty pewter, and along the southern shore, where the sun struck longest, black water showed in long weeping seams. The groan came again, deeper, and a shudder ran across the whole white face of the river like a shiver through a sleeping animal, and somewhere far out a crack split the air—sharp as a pistol, sharp as the shot that had supposedly killed her brother—and then silence, and then the groan again, patient, enormous, indifferent to what anyone in the city wanted.

"Days," said Tomas behind her. "Maybe three. Maybe four if the cold comes back at night. After that, nothing until autumn."

She had not heard him climb the stairs. He moved quietly now, in the way of a man who had spent two years learning that quiet was a currency. He came to stand beside her at the loading door, and the cold came in with him, and neither of them shut the door, because shutting it would have meant turning away from the river, and neither of them could.

"Autumn," Rosalind said. "That's seven months."

"Seven months of Voss." He said it flatly, without drama, the way one reads a ledger. "Seven months of Petra carrying our names in her pocket like a lit fuse. Seven months of the magistrate finding one thread and then another. No. Whatever happens, it happens before the ice goes."

She looked at him. The wound in his shoulder had healed to a pink crescent, and he had filled out a little in the weeks of cellar bread and cellar sleep, but there was still a hollowness around his eyes that no amount of feeding seemed to touch. He had told her once, in the third week, when the lamp burned low and neither of them could sleep, what the frontier had been like—what the army had been like, the whippings, the frozen dead stacked like cordwood, the letter he had carried sewn into his coat lining through two hundred miles of being hunted. He did not tell such stories often. When he did, he told them the way he set type: precisely, without flourish, each fact in its place.

And now he stood beside her and did the arithmetic aloud, because someone had to.

"One berth," he said. "One passenger. No exceptions. Those were Emil's words, weren't they."

"Those were Emil's words."

"And there are four of us who might fill it."

Rosalind turned back to the river. She did not want to look at him while they did this—this thing they had been circling for two days, the way one circles a wound, prodding at the edge of it, never the center. Four of them. Herself. Her father. Tomas. And Emil, who was not here to be counted, and who had sent the message anyway, folded inside a bale of waste paper that had come up from the cellar two nights ago smelling of lamp oil and river mud.

*One berth. One passenger. No exceptions. The ice will not wait and neither will the soldiers. Send the name by Friday.*

Send the name. As though a name were a thing one could weigh on the shop's little brass scales, as though it were a shipment of matrices or a ream of paper, as though it did not have hands and a voice and a way of standing at a window.

"I've been thinking," Tomas said, "that it should be your father."

She had known he would say it. She had heard him rehearsing it in the cellar at night, murmuring to the lamp, and she had hated him a little for it, and hated herself more for the hating.

"Say why."

"Because he's the one they'll hang." Tomas's voice stayed level, but his jaw tightened. "Voss doesn't know my face. He knows a stranger was seen at the loading door, nothing more. But your father—he's known. Twenty years in the print quarter. Every censor in this city knows Alois Hartwell's name, and every one of them has wondered, privately, how a man so loyal prints so little that's worth reading. The moment this unravels—and it will unravel, Rosalind, Petra saw to that—your father is the one they come for. He's the one who needs a river and a berth more than any of us."

"And you?"

"I can disappear. I've done it before." He almost smiled. "I'm good at being no one. It's the one trade I learned before printing."

"And I'm not," Rosalind said. "Good at being no one."

It came out before she could stop it, and she heard how it sounded—not the modesty she had worn for years like a well-fitted coat, *I'm nobody, I cut typefaces, I keep my head down*—but something else. Something that had been growing in her since the night a bleeding stranger collapsed against her loading door. She had spent her whole life perfecting invisibility, and she had been very good at it, and it had cost her everything she had not known she was paying: her brother's war, her father's secret, the two years of grief she had spent mourning a living man. Invisibility had not protected her from anything. It had only made sure that no one thought to tell her the truth.

Tomas looked at her then, fully, and whatever he saw in her face made him go still.

"No," he agreed quietly. "You're not."

* * *

The shop was cold that morning because Alois had let the stove go down to coals—a habit from the lean years, though the lean years were long past and the money in the hidden ledger would have bought a hundred cords of wood. He sat at his cutting bench with his spectacles low on his nose, working a punch into a blank of steel with the small, patient taps that had been the sound of Rosalind's childhood. Tap, tap, tap. The letter taking shape one grain at a time. Her father had cut typefaces for thirty years, and he did it the way he did everything: slowly, exactly, leaving no mark on the world that he had not intended to leave.

She watched him from the doorway of the composing room and thought: *He is the bravest man I know, and I did not know it until three weeks ago, and I still cannot say it to his face.*

The ledger lay in her memory now where it had lain in the locked drawer—every payment, every date, every alias, ten years of quiet treason in a fair, unremarkable hand. *The Cutter.* She had laughed when Tomas first said the name, a short ugly laugh, because she had pictured some scarred river pirate and the truth was a stooped man in a leather apron who smelled of oil of cloves and said grace before meals. Ten years of funding crossings. Since before Emil. Since before the war. Since—she had done the dates twice, late at night, unable to believe them the first time—since the year her mother died. As if grief had shown him something. As if losing one person he loved had taught him what it meant that other people, strangers, were losing everything.

"Rosalind." He did not look up from the punch. "You're standing in the doorway like a bill collector."

"We have to decide."

The tap of his hammer stopped. The silence that followed had a texture to it, the silence of a house where a thing long dreaded has finally been said aloud.

"Friday," Alois said. "I know."

"Emil said one name. One."

"I read the letter, Rosalind. I cut the cipher for it myself, if you'll recall." He set down the hammer with exaggerated care, the way he did when his hands wanted to shake and he would not permit them to. He took off his spectacles and polished them on his apron, though they were clean. It was the gesture he made when he was buying time, and she had known it since she was six years old, and she hated that she knew it, and loved that she knew it, and could not have separated the two feelings with a knife.

"Then you know what I'm going to say," she said.

"You're going to say Emil."

"I'm going to say Emil."

"Emil," her father said, "is on the far bank. Emil has a network, a boat, forty people who would die for him. Emil does not need a berth in his own crossing. Emil is offering it because he is my son and he cannot help himself." He put the spectacles back on, and behind the glass his eyes were wet and furious and steady. "The berth is for someone on *this* side. That is the whole point of it. That is the only point of it."

"Then it's you."

"No."

"Father—"

"No." He said it the way he said it to the censor's office when they wanted a name from the imprint page. Not loud. Final. "I have thought about nothing else for two days, Rosalind, and I will tell you what I have concluded, and then we will not speak of it again until Friday, because I find I cannot cut steel and argue at the same time." He laid his hand flat on the bench, on the half-formed letter, on the steel that would outlive them all. "The network needs a financier on this side of the river. The network needs a printer with an immaculate reputation. The moment I cross, the whole southern route starves. And the moment I am missed from this shop, Voss will be at that door within the hour, and he will take the shop apart board by board, and he will find the ledger, and he will find the cellar, and everything Emil has built dies with me. I stay. That is arithmetic, not sentiment. I know you distrust my arithmetic lately, but you will find it is sound."

"It isn't arithmetic," Rosalind said. "It's the same thing you've done my whole life. You decide, and you let me believe the decision was mine."

The words landed harder than she intended. She saw them land—saw the small flinch around his mouth, the flinch of a man who has been struck in a place no one else can see the bruise. He looked down at the punch in the steel, at the letter he had been cutting, and she saw which letter it was, and her throat closed.

It was an R. He had been cutting her initial. He cut one for each of them, once a year, on the anniversary of her mother's death—she had found the drawer of them years ago and never asked, because asking would have meant the ritual would have to survive being spoken of, and some things in their house survived only by silence. He was cutting hers now. Perhaps because he thought he might not be here next year to cut it.

"I let you believe the decision was yours," Alois said quietly, "because I believed you'd made the only safe one. Being no one. Keeping your head down." He shook his head, slow, like a man reading bad news a second time hoping it will improve. "I was wrong about that. It's the only thing I've been wrong about in thirty years of being careful, and it's the one that matters."

* * *

Petra came at noon with the bread, as she always did, and Rosalind watched her come up the lane and felt the old ache and the new knowledge sitting side by side in her chest like two typefaces of the same letter. The girl was seventeen and freckled and walked with a bounce that the whole street knew her by, and she had been selling them to Voss since October because a man in a grey coat had told her he knew where her mother bought her morphine and what the apothecary's ledger said about it.

She knew that now. Tomas had worked it out from the arrest dates, and Petra had confirmed it in a flood of tears in the cellar that Rosalind still heard sometimes when she was falling asleep. And they had not turned her out. They had fed her a false manifest instead, and made her a weapon against the man who owned her, and Rosalind still did not know if that was mercy or madness, and suspected it was both, and suspected that in the years coming she would have to live with both.

"You've heard the river," Petra said, setting the loaf on the counter. Her eyes went to the window, to the grey water showing through the seams. "Everyone's heard it. They're saying the crossing season's over. They're saying anyone still on this side is on this side till autumn."

"They're saying wrong. There's one crossing left."

Petra's face did something complicated—hope and dread fighting for the same ground. "One berth?"

"One."

The girl was quiet a moment. Then, in a voice much smaller than her usual one: "Whoever it is. They'll need papers. Good ones. I could—I know which clerk at the shipping office doesn't check the seals. If it helps. I'd like it to help. I'd like—" She stopped, and her chin trembled once, and she mastered it, because Petra had learned this winter to master things no seventeen-year-old should have to master. "I'd like one thing I did this year to be the right thing."

"It will be," Rosalind said, and put her hand over the girl's on the counter, and meant it, and did not know if meaning it was enough.

* * *

That night she went down to the cellar with the lamp and found Tomas already awake, sitting on the edge of the cot with the ciphered letter open on his knee, though he had read it a dozen times and could recite it. He looked up at her, and in the lamplight his face had the openness it only had down here, below the street, below the world, where no one watched.

"It's you," he said.

"Don't."

"It has to be you. I've been through it every way. Your father stays because the network dies without him—that's true, whatever he pretends about his reasons. I stay because I'm the one Voss can't identify, and because someone has to get your father's name to Emil if this goes wrong, and because—" he stopped, and the lamp guttered, and he started again, lower. "And because I can't do the other thing. I can't be the reason you leave and I stay. I've carried enough of other people's deaths across that river. I won't carry yours at a distance for seven months. So it's you. It was always going to be you. Emil didn't send one berth for his father or his father's apprentice. He sent it for his sister, and everyone in this cellar knows it, and we've spent two days pretending it's arithmetic because arithmetic is easier to survive than the truth."

Rosalind stood on the cellar stairs with the lamp in her hand, and above her the house creaked, and beyond the walls the river groaned again, longer this time, a sound like a door opening at the bottom of the world.

"And you," she said. "What happens to you, when I'm on the ice and the soldiers come?"

"Same thing that happens to everyone in this city when the soldiers come." He smiled, and it was the old hollow smile, but there was something new under it now, something she had put there or he had grown there, she could not tell. "We print."

She came down the last stairs. She set the lamp on the crate beside the cot, and she sat beside him, shoulder to shoulder, and for a long time neither of them spoke, and the ice spoke for them both—cracking, settling, counting down.

"Friday," she said at last. "We send the name Friday."

"I know."

"And it isn't decided."

"Rosalind." He turned to look at her, and this close she could see the lamplight doubled in his eyes, and the scar, and the fear he never let anyone else see. "It was decided the night I bled on your door. We're just arguing about who gets to say it out loud."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, they heard her father's footsteps cross the shop—once, twice, then stop—and then the small, patient sound of steel being cut, tap, tap, tap, a letter taking shape in the dark, one grain at a time, as though a letter could save anyone. As though, Rosalind thought, taking Tomas's hand in the lamplight, it could not.
