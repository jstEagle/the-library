## 14. Petra's Confession

The girl arrived at the loading door the way winter arrives—gradually, then all at once.

Rosalind heard her before she saw her: a scuffing on the cobbles, a hesitation that had no business belonging to someone who had run errands for the Hartwell shop since she was eleven years old. Petra had always entered like weather, loud and certain, her voice arriving two streets ahead of her body. Now there was only that scuffing, and then a knock so soft it might have been the wind testing the door.

It was the hour when the shop should have been empty. Alois was at the magistrate's table again—Voss had developed an appetite for his company these past weeks, and Alois fed it with wine and bland conversation and the impeccable loyalty of a man who cut typefaces for the crown's decrees and asked nothing of the world but quiet. Tomas was below, in the cellar, mending the false bottom of a paper bale by lamplight, his hands steady now in a way they had not been in January. The thaw had begun in earnest; the river spoke at night in long groans, like an animal turning over in its sleep, and everyone in the network had stopped pretending they had time.

Rosalind opened the door herself.

Petra stood in the blue light of early evening with snowmelt soaking through her boots, and she looked—Rosalind would remember this always—she looked *smaller*. Not younger. Smaller, as though something inside her had been scooped out and the rest had settled into the space. Her cheeks were chapped raw. Her eyes were swollen shut at the edges from crying that had clearly been going on for days, crying done in installments, alone, in doorways and stairwells where no one could ask its cause.

"Fraulein Hartwell." Her voice cracked on the name. "Please. I have to tell you something, and if you send me away after, I'll go, I swear it, I'll never—I just can't carry it anymore. It's eating me."

The words were rehearsed. Rosalind could hear the rehearsal in them, the way you could hear the grain in a badly cast matrix—the shape repeated until it wore smooth. But underneath the smoothness was the thing itself, trembling.

Behind her, down the cellar stairs, Rosalind knew Tomas had gone silent. She could feel him listening the way one feels a draft under a door.

"Come in," she said. "Not the parlor. The workroom."

Because the workroom had walls that held ink, and ink remembered everything printed near it, and more than that: because if this was what Rosalind feared it was—if the girl standing in her doorway shivering had been the reason couriers went to prison on Binder's Lane—then she wanted walls around them both, and iron within reach, though she would never have said so aloud, not even to herself.

* * *

Petra sat on the low stool by the stone sink where Rosalind cleaned rollers, and she put her hands flat on her knees like a child called before a schoolmistress, and for a long moment she said nothing at all. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, a cart went by loaded with ice being cut from the river upstream, the blades singing against the frozen surface—a sound the whole city had lived with all winter, and which now, in March, sounded like a countdown.

"I didn't want to," Petra said finally. "You have to believe that first. Before anything else. I didn't want to."

"Petra—"

"No." The girl's chin came up, and for one second she was the old Petra, the one who stole sugar plums from Frau Meissner's stall and dared the watchmen to say anything. "No. Let me do it all at once or I'll lose my nerve, and then I'll have to come back tomorrow, and tomorrow I might be too dead to come back."

The word landed in the room like a dropped typecase. Rosalind kept her hands still on the bench. *Ink remembers,* she told herself. *Be the wall.*

"They came to our rooms in November." Petra's hands twisted together on her knees. "My mother's lungs—you know how she is, she hasn't worked since autumn, and Papa owes the guild bank, and there are four of us younger than me. Two soldiers and a man in a gray coat who never gave his name, and he had a list, Fraulein. A list of names, and mine was on it. Mine, and I'd never given anyone anything. They knew already. That's what I understood, sitting there while they smiled at me—they weren't recruiting me. They were *collecting*. Someone had already told them enough to know I ran errands up and down Binder's Lane twice a week."

The stove ticked. Down the stairs, silence.

"And he said—he said, 'We don't need you to do anything dangerous, child. We need you to keep doing exactly what you do. Tell us what you see. Who carries what. Which doors open late.' And he put money on the table, and my mother coughed, and—" Petra's voice failed and restarted, thinner. "He said if I refused, they would simply find another girl, and the other girl wouldn't be warned about what happens to families of girls who refuse. And I believed him. That's the worst part. I believed him instantly, because I've seen what happens to people who cross the magistrate, haven't I? Everyone has. You see it in the square in winter."

She was crying now, without drama, without wiping her face, the tears simply falling as though they had been waiting all this time for permission.

"So I told them about deliveries. Waste paper going south to Binder's Lane. Bales heavier than waste paper ought to be. And in January they took Herr Lindqvist's couriers, the two boys, and I told myself it wasn't because of me, it couldn't be, because the arrests happened days after I'd reported nothing unusual—but then last week the man in the gray coat asked me about *this house*, Frauline Hartwell. He asked who else comes here besides your father. He asked whether I'd ever seen strangers at the loading door." Her eyes came up, wrecked and direct. "And I lied to him. For the first time. I said no."

Rosalind breathed out very slowly.

"You lied to him."

"I lied to him, and he watched me lie, and I could see he knew, and he wrote something in his little book anyway, and he smiled at me and said 'That will be all for tonight,' and Fraulein Hartwell, the way he said it—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "The way he said it, it wasn't *that will be all*. It was *we know where you live, we know who sleeps in your cellar, and we are deciding what to do about it.*"

The room seemed to contract. Rosalind became aware of every object in it as though seeing it through water: the composing sticks on their rack, the galley of half-set type waiting under its paper cover, the iron frame of the press standing in the corner like a patient beast. Every piece of it suddenly legible as evidence. Every surface a thing Voss's men could point to and say *there*.

"How long," Rosalind said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her; somewhere beneath the levelness was a scream pacing a small room. "How long have you been reporting to them?"

"Since November." Petra lowered her hands. "Four months. And in four months I have learned one thing about myself, and it is that I am a coward with a good memory. Because every time I walked past your window, I thought: today is the day they come for the Hartwells, and it will be my doing, and I will stand in the square and watch it and deserve it. And every week I came here anyway, and you paid me, and you trusted me, and once—" the tears came harder— "once you gave me your mother's scarf when I said mine was worn through. You gave me a dead woman's scarf because I was cold. Do you understand? I have been carrying that scarf to the man in the gray coat in my mind every single week. Carrying it like a stone."

* * *

What Rosalind felt, sitting across from the girl, was not what she had expected to feel.

She had imagined this moment—hadn't she, some cold night, staring at the ceiling, doing Tomas's arithmetic for him? *If there is an informer, who is it, and what will I do when I know?* She had imagined fury. She had imagined the clean, hard logic of the network, which was arithmetic and nothing else: a leak is a leak, and a leak must be sealed, and sealing meant cutting the girl loose entirely, sending her away, out of the city if possible, because a known informer who keeps visiting the suspect house is a noose with legs.

Instead what she felt was grief with a strange double exposure laid over it. She saw the man in the gray coat's smile, and behind it she saw Emil at seventeen, conscripted, marching east with the imperial colors on his sleeve because refusal meant the family's ruin—and hadn't their father paid, quietly, everything, to make sure the Hartwells stayed safe and dull and invisible? Hadn't invisibility been the family religion? *Keep your head down. Cut your matrices. Be unremarkable.* And here was a girl of sixteen whose family had never had the luxury of even that, a girl who had been collected like a debt, and who had knelt under it for four months, and who had finally, at real risk to her own neck, chosen to break.

Mercy, Rosalind thought, is easy to admire in the abstract and ruinous in the ledger. Mercy was a gamble. Mercy bet lives on the assumption that a frightened girl's courage, having surfaced once, would surface again.

"You're going to turn me out," Petra said. She said it steadily, which cost her visibly. "I know. I've thought about it every step of the way here. Turn me out, report me gone, tell your people I left the city. It's right. It's what you should do. I only wanted—" Her breath hitched. "I only wanted to say it to your face first. To one person who matters. So that somewhere in the world there's one person who knows I'm not—that I wasn't—"

"Stop." Rosalind stood. Her knees ached from the stool; she hadn't noticed sitting down. She crossed to the stove and fed it a split of birch, mostly because her hands demanded occupation, and watched the flame take the bark with disproportionate attention. "First question, and answer honestly, because honest answers are the only currency you hold right now. Does the man in the gray coat expect you again?"

"Yes. Friday. After the market."

"Has he ever asked you to carry anything to him? Papers, objects?"

"No. Only words. He says words can't be traced." A pause, and then, bitterly: "He's wrong. Words got the boys on Binder's Lane taken."

"Does anyone else know you come to him? His superiors—do they know your face, your street?"

"He meets me alone. Always alone, by the tannery wall. I think—" Petra frowned, working at it. "I think he doesn't want anyone else to know how good I am. Or how much I know. He calls me *his little ear*. He said once, laughing, that if the network ever found out about me, he'd have to decide whether I was worth saving. Like I was a typeface he'd cut and might recut."

Something cold moved down Rosalind's spine. Not fear—recognition. She had grown up around men who talked about people that way. Voss's kind. The empire's kind. People as matrices, cut to serve, melted down when the casting failed.

She turned back to the girl.

"Here is what is going to happen," Rosalind said, and heard her father's cadence enter her own voice, that measured print-shop gravity she had sworn all her life she'd never inherit. "You are not going to be turned out. Not because I forgive you—forgiveness isn't mine to give; there are people in cells on the fortress island whose forgiveness you'd have to earn, and some of them may not live to grant it. And not because I trust you. Trust is also not on the table; don't insult us both by asking for it."

Petra flinched. Rosalind made herself continue.

"You're staying because you're useful, and because turning you out doesn't seal anything—it just moves the hole. If you vanish, they wonder why, and wondering men dig. But if you keep your Friday appointment—" and here the plan assembled itself in her mouth even as she spoke it, the way a page assembles under the composing stick, line by line, each word locking the next— "if you keep it, and you tell him exactly what you've always told him, and one new thing besides—one thing *we* choose—then every Friday becomes ours instead of his. You stop being his little ear and start being ours. Do you understand what I'm offering you? Not mercy. Work. Dangerous, degrading, terrifying work, for people who will never fully trust you, because that's what your November bought. It's a bad bargain, Petra. It's the only one on the table."

The girl sat very still. Outside, the ice-cutters' blades sang again, closer, downstream now. Then Petra slid off the stool and onto the floor—not kneeling exactly, more a collapse that had arranged itself as kneeling—and pressed her forehead against the cold flagstones at Rosalind's feet.

"I'll do it," she whispered to the floor. "God help me, I'll do it. And someday, if any of us are alive when this is over, you can tell me whether I earned the scarf back."

Rosalind looked down at the bowed head, at the thin shoulders shaking under the patched coat, and felt the terrible vertigo of the merciful: that she had just gambled every life in the network on the loyalty of a girl who had sold hers once already, and that the gamble felt less like courage than like exhaustion—like the simple inability to hate one more person in a season already full of hating.

"It's not yours to earn back," Rosalind said quietly. "It was a gift. Gifts don't keep score. That's rather the point of them."

She helped the girl up, and opened the loading door to the blue dark, and watched Petra go—straight-backed, deliberate, a soldier now whether she'd volunteered or not—until the snowmelt sounds of her steps faded toward the tannery district.

Then Rosalind descended the cellar stairs with the lamp held high, to tell Tomas that the hole in the network had just become a door—and that on Friday, for the first time, they would be the ones deciding what came through it.

Tomas listened without moving, lamplight on the scar, and when she finished he was quiet a long moment. Then he said, softly, "There's one thing you haven't asked her," and Rosalind felt the floor tilt. "Ask her what the man in the gray coat wrote in his book. Last Friday. When she lied to him." His eyes lifted to hers. "Because if he wrote down *the Hartwell house*—then Friday isn't our door, Rosalind. It's his deadline."
