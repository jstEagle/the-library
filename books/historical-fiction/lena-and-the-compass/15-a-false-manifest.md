## 15. A False Manifest

The forgery began, as all honest work does, with the hands.

Tomas sat at the composing stone in the small hours of the morning, when the shop smelled of cold tallow and the river's damp crept up through the floorboards, and he tried to think like a shipping clerk. Not a smuggler—a clerk. That was the whole trick of it, Alois had said, tapping ash from his pipe into a saucer shaped like a fish. *Voss will not be looking for genius. He will be looking for sloppiness. Give him competence and he will believe it, because competence is what he expects of commerce.* The old man spoke of deception the way he spoke of type: as a craft with rules, tolerances, a discipline of small measurements.

So Tomas measured.

He set the manifest in twelve-point Caslon, the same face the Hartwell shop used for legitimate freight documents—Rosalind had pulled three genuine manifests from the files to prove it—and he set it slightly crooked. Not obviously. A hair's drift on the third line, the kind of error a tired journeyman makes at the end of a long day. He inked the form lightly on its left edge, so that the letters there printed faint, as though the roller had begun to run dry. He smudged a thumbprint of lampblack near the bottom, where a handler's grip would fall. Every imperfection was chosen; every flaw was manufactured. It was, he thought, the most honest lying he had ever done, because he could see every lie plainly, could hold each one in his fingers as a piece of lead, and knew exactly why it had been made.

*Barque *Marta*, bound downriver, cargo: rag paper, forty bales; printer's waste, twenty-two bales; tallow, four casks.* All of it true. That was the second lesson, the one Rosalind had insisted on when she reviewed his work and found it too clean. *A forgery fails when everything in it is convenient,* she'd said, leaning over his shoulder, her breath warm against the back of his neck in a way that made setting type suddenly difficult. *Voss's men know what leaves this shop. So the manifest must show exactly what they already believe we ship. The only false thing in it is the date—and even the date is only half a lie.*

Half a lie. The date read the twelfth of March. In truth the goods would move on the fifteenth. Three days of fog between what Voss would be told and what would happen—the space in which a boat could slip downriver before soldiers arrived to count bales. Three days bought with a single digit, set by hand, in a face her brother had taught her to cut.

Tomas pulled the proof and held it to the lamp. The paper was right—aged slightly at the corners with a wet cloth and the warmth of the press bed. The fold marks were right, creased twice as manifests always were, once across and once along, so they fit a courier's coat pocket. Even the coffee ring was right: he had taken the trouble to set the bottom of his cup on the corner and turn it, gently, the way a man does without thinking while he reads.

He looked at the little brown circle and felt something turn over in him, slow and cold, like ice shifting on the river.

There had been a time—not long ago, though it felt like another country—when deception for Tomas meant running. Crouched in a drainage culvert with imperial patrols passing close enough to hear their buttons click against their crossbelts. Forging nothing but his own gait, his own accent, his own face arranged into the dull blankness of a man going about lawful business. Deception then was a whole body's work, exhausting and total, and it kept nothing safe but himself.

Now it was typographic. Now it had ligatures and kerns. And now it was not only his own life folded into the crease of a document, but Rosalind's, and Alois's, and the lives of eleven people hidden in the network's houses who did not even know that this sheet of paper existed—that its small crooked line might be the difference between their children growing up on one side of a border or the other.

Deception had become their native language. He was startled to find how fluently he was learning to speak it.

* * *

Petra came at noon, as she always did, with bread from the baker on Binder's Lane and the quick, light step of a girl whose conscience had learned to walk quietly.

Tomas watched her from behind the half-open door of the drying loft, where he had no business being and less business watching, and hated himself for both.

She was seventeen. She wore her brown hair braided around her head in the style of girls who worked, and she had flour on her sleeve where the baker's boy had shoved the loaves at her too fast, and she laughed at something Rosalind said—laughed the way she always did, too loudly, as if laughter were a debt she was eager to pay. She adored Rosalind. Everyone knew it. She had hung around the shop since she was fourteen, fetching, carrying, sweeping type dust, absorbing printing lore the way other girls absorbed songs, and Rosalind had treated her with the patient kindness of an older sister, which is to say: with occasional irritation and unbreakable loyalty.

And Petra had sold them. Had written down what she saw and carried it to a man named Brandt at the customs house, who wore grey gloves and never raised his voice, and who had made it very clear to her what would happen to her father—a dockhand, a widower, a man with a bad lung—if she stopped writing things down.

They knew all of this now. They had pieced it together over two nights of arithmetic: arrest dates against delivery days, the raid on Binder's Lane arriving four days after the first manifest Petra had ever carried out of the shop. The pattern was so plain once you saw it that Tomas had wanted to strike the wall. Four arrests, two couriers lost, one safehouse burned—all of it flowing through the small neat hand of a girl who still brought them bread.

*No villain here,* Rosalind had said, when they finished. Her voice had been flat, controlled, the voice of someone holding a door shut against a flood. *Only another person paying survival's price. We've all been paying it. She just pays in a different coin.*

And now they were going to ask her to pay in yet another coin—to become, under threat of everything, a double agent, carrying lies to the man who owned her fear.

Tomas came down as the noon bell finished ringing. Rosalind had already drawn Petra into the back room, away from the windows, and sat her on the bench beside the paper store, and there was something in the geometry of the scene—Rosalind standing, Petra sitting, the closed door—that made the girl's laughter die in her throat before it fully formed.

"You know," Petra said. Not a question. Her hands found each other in her lap and held on.

"We know," Rosalind said gently.

"I never—" The girl's breath went ragged. "I never wrote anything that hurt anyone. I swear it on my mother's grave. I wrote what wagons left, what hours the shop kept, whether there were strangers. I didn't know—I told myself I didn't know *what for*. And then Binder's Lane happened and I knew exactly what for, and I couldn't stop, because if I stopped, Brandt said—" Her voice broke and reassembled itself somewhere lower. "He said they'd take my father for the interrogations instead. And my father can't even climb stairs anymore."

Tomas stood by the door and let Rosalind do the talking, because this was her city and her shop and her girl, and also because he did not trust his own face yet. Anger was still living in it somewhere, and anger would kill them all faster than Voss's soldiers.

"Petra." Rosalind crouched so that their eyes were level, and Tomas watched her do it—watched her choose the posture of a sister rather than a judge. "Listen to me. No one here blames you. Do you understand? Not me. Not my father. Not him." A tilt of her head toward Tomas. "You were put in a vise. Anyone who says they wouldn't have squeezed is lying."

"But you're going to use me anyway." Petra's eyes were terribly clear now, the tears held off by sheer adolescent pride. "That's why you're being kind. You need me to keep carrying papers."

"Yes," Rosalind said. No hesitation. Tomas admired it and feared it in equal measure. "We're going to use you. But this time you'll know what for. This time you'll be doing it *to* them instead of for them. And when this is over—when the ice goes and whatever comes after it comes—we will remember which side you chose when you finally got to choose."

Petra looked down at her hands. When she spoke again her voice was small and steady. "What do you want me to carry?"

Rosalind took the manifest from the drawer and laid it on the bench between them, and Tomas watched a seventeen-year-old girl study a forgery he had spent a night perfecting, watched her frown at it with a craftsman's instinct she didn't know she had.

"The date's wrong," she said. "Goods don't leave till the fifteenth. Everyone knows the Marta doesn't take on till the fifteenth. Brandt knows the river schedules better than the harbor master."

For one heartbeat the whole plan lay open on the bench, gutted by a schoolgirl's eye.

Then Rosalind smiled—slowly, and with something almost like delight. "Then correct it," she said. "Change it to the fifteenth. In your own hand. Make it look like the clerk caught the error and amended it. Because that, Petra, is exactly what Brandt will expect a careful shop to do—and exactly what he'll report."

* * *

It worked.

Tomas learned of it two days later, in fragments, the way good news always seemed to arrive in this business—never whole, never clean, always secondhand and deniable. A message wrapped in oilcloth inside a bale of rags from the laundry on Tanner Street: *Manifest received. Customs satisfied. Inspection scheduled for the 15th, customs wharf, six o'clock.*

Which meant that on the fifteenth, Voss's men would stand on the wharf at dawn, counting bales of a shipment that had already passed them in the dark three nights before—or rather, that had never been intended to pass at all, because the Marta was real and really did load rag paper, and the inspection would find precisely what the manifest promised and nothing else. The crossing itself would go by the old weir path, the one Emil's letter described, where the ice was still thick enough to bear a sledge.

The trap within the trap within the trap. Tomas turned the message over in his hands and felt the exhilaration come up in him like heat from a stove—felt it and immediately distrusted it, the way a man distrusts a warm day in late winter, knowing it means nothing except that the thaw is coming for everything.

Because the thaw was coming. He could hear it at night now, lying in the cellar among the paper stores. The river had begun to talk in its sleep—a low groaning, a knocking, deep below the frozen surface, as if something enormous were turning over. Every night the sound was a little more insistent. The crossings had perhaps five days left in them. Perhaps fewer. After that, the channel ran wild until autumn, and everyone they were trying to move south would spend a summer and half a winter waiting in cellars and lofts, waiting to be found.

Five days. And still unsolved, still sitting at the center of everything like a stone in a shoe: the question of the berth. One passenger on the final crossing. Emil's word, sent up from the far bank with the last courier: *One berth. One name in the book. No exceptions. Choose.*

Choose. As if it were arithmetic. As if love could be divided without remainder.

He found Rosalind in the shop that evening, washing ink from the rollers, her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her forearms black to the wrist. She looked up when he came in, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the ordinary domestic sounds of the closing shop—the settling of the stove, the tick of cooling metal—filled the silence like water fills a mold.

"It worked," he said. "Petra carried it clean. Brandt believed it. We bought our three days."

"I heard." She wrung out the sponge. "She corrected your date. Your beautiful, deliberate mistake. She fixed it without blinking."

"She's wasted on errands, that girl."

"She's wasted on a great many things." Rosalind set the sponge down and dried her hands, slowly, and he saw that the exhilaration in her was fighting the same dread that fought in him, and losing ground. "Tomas. When the Marta sails, and the inspection finds nothing, and Voss stands on that wharf counting bales of honest rag paper—what happens in his head?"

Tomas had asked himself the same question forty times. There was only one answer. "He stops believing the paperwork," he said. "He starts believing in people. He'll tear the quarter apart house by house."

"And we'll have used up our one clever lie to buy three days that end in a hunt."

"Yes."

Rosalind leaned against the wash-up sink and looked at the darkening window, where the first lamplighter was making his rounds along Binder's Lane, his little flame moving street to street like a slow fuse burning toward something.

"My father thinks we should close the shop after the crossing," she said quietly. "Board it up. Walk away before Voss connects the manifest to the empty berth."

"And what do you think?"

She was silent long enough that the lamplighter's glow crossed the glass and moved on. When she answered, her voice had changed—lower, harder, lit from underneath by something he had seen in her only rarely and never forgotten.

"I think we are printers," she said. "My family has put words on paper for sixty years, and every one of those years we have asked permission—from the censor, from the magistrate, from men like Voss—for the right to say what may be said. I am done asking permission. If Voss comes for us after the crossing, he will come to a shop that has already spoken."

"Spoken how?"

She turned and looked at him, and in the low light her eyes had the particular brightness of type metal just before it pours.

"There's a broadsheet I've been setting in my head for weeks," Rosalind said. "Every abuse of Voss's office. Every name he's ruined. Every bribe, every seized letter, every man who vanished after his 'inspections.' I have the proofs in my memory, Tomas, all of them, set and locked in the forme of my skull. All we lack is a night, a press, and enough paper."

Outside, far off toward the river, the ice groaned—a long, rising complaint, like a giant waking, or a door being forced.

Tomas felt the sound in his chest and understood that the three days they had purchased were already spending themselves, grain by grain, and that whatever came after them—the crossing, the hunt, the broadsheet, the rise—would begin sooner than any of them was ready for.

Above the cellar stairs, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of Alois Hartwell opening his ledger.
