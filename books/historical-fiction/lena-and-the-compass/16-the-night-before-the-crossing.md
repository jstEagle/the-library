## 16. The Night Before the Crossing

The cellar smelled of ink and cold stone, and by lamplight it looked like the inside of a heart: cramped, warm in places, dangerous to touch.

Rosalind had carried the lamp down the stairs at half past eleven, after her father went up to bed. She had heard his door close—heard, too, the small hesitation before it closed, as though he stood a moment on the landing listening for what she would do with the night. She was past pretending now. They were all past pretending. Tomorrow the ice would break or it wouldn't, and one of them would be on the river when it did.

She set the lamp on the workbench beside the press and began to lay out the tools of her other trade: the good paper, cream-colored and heavy, bought three weeks ago from a stationer in the old town who asked no questions because she paid in coin and smiled like a girl buying ribbon. The steel pen. The blotting sand. The two official forms Tomas had brought back rolled tight in oilcloth, creased once through the middle where he'd kept them against his ribs for six days.

"Passports," she said to the empty room, testing the word aloud. It sounded absurd down here among the lead sorts and the rags of waste paper. *Passports.* As if a life could be folded into a rectangle and stamped.

"It sounds absurd," said Tomas from the stairs, "when you say it like that."

She hadn't heard him come down. His boots made no sound; he had learned that in the army, walking sentry on frozen ground, or so he'd told her once at three in the morning over cold coffee, when the telling had felt less like confession than like handing her something fragile to hold. Now he came the rest of the way into the lamplight, carrying the second chair from the corner by the coal bin, and set it down beside hers without asking whether he should stay.

He was staying. They both knew it. The knowing sat between them all evening like a third person who said nothing but took up room.

* * *

The forging took four hours, and they did not speak for most of them.

It was work she knew how to do with her hands while her mind walked elsewhere—the same as cutting matrices, the same as setting a page of scripture under her father's exacting eye. She had studied the magistrate's office hand for years; every printer in the city had, because every printed thing passed across those desks and came back marked or mangled, and you learned the handwriting of power the way a deer learns the smell of the hound. The capital's clerks wrote with a heavy forward slant, the ascenders looped too wide, the ink pooled at the ends of words because they pressed and paused, pressed and paused, men thinking about their dinners while they signed away lives.

Tomas read out the particulars. Name. Trade. Place of origin—a village south of the marshes that had burned its parish records in a chimney fire, Emil's choice, careful man. Purpose of travel: family business, wool. She wrote it all in the clerk's slant, pressed and paused, pressed and paused, and blew sand across each finished line, and the lamplight caught the grains as they fell like something falling slowly through still water.

"You're better at this than I am," he said, somewhere past midnight.

"I've been imitating hands since I was nine. Father used to make me reproduce typefaces by drawing them, over and over, until I could see the letter in the dark." She turned the passport toward him, angled to the flame. "This is only another typeface. One that happens to be able to hang people."

"Rosalind."

"I'm not being grim. I'm being accurate. There's a difference, though Father spent my whole childhood insisting there wasn't." She dipped the pen again. "He wanted me accurate about type. It turns out I applied the lesson more broadly than he intended."

Tomas laughed—not the quick breath-laugh he permitted himself upstairs, but a real one, low and startled out of him, and then he looked sorry for it, because laughter in that cellar felt like spending money you might need tomorrow. She watched him master it. She watched the scar along his jaw whiten where the skin pulled. Two winters ago she would have looked away from such a face, out of courtesy. Now she looked straight at it and thought it was the truest face she knew.

* * *

At two o'clock the stamp dried and the passports were done: four of them, wrapped in oilcloth, ready for the crossing party at dawn. Three for the others. One whose name they had not yet spoken between them, because speaking it would make it real, and unmade things could still be argued with.

Rosalind washed the pen in turpentine and stood a long moment with her fingers stained black to the first knuckle, watching the solvent swirl gray in the jar.

"My mother used to wash my hands like that," she said. "After the shop. Every night, when I was small. She'd say, *ink gets into everything, Rosalind, guard your skin,* and she'd scrub until my fingers were pink." She flexed them. "Three winters dead, and I still hear her voice every time the turpentine stings."

"What else do you hear?"

"The press. Father coughing. Emil whistling the way he whistled when he was setting type wrong and didn't want Father to notice." She turned to face Tomas fully then, and the lamp threw both their shadows enormous up the stone wall, two figures leaning toward each other like figures cut for a woodblock print. "You carry a letter in his handwriting. You've met him. Tell me—does he still whistle?"

"He does. Badly. He whistles when the ice is safe and whistles differently when it isn't, and I have never once been able to tell the difference, which he finds hilarious."

Her throat closed. She had expected many answers to that question, but not that one—not the ordinary, unbearable domesticity of it, her brother alive and laughing on a frozen river two hundred miles north, whistling badly. She put her hand flat on the workbench to steady herself and found Tomas's hand already there, an inch from hers, and neither of them moved away.

"We shouldn't," he said quietly.

"I know."

"Tomorrow one of us may be—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way a man crosses bad ice, testing each word before putting weight on it. "Whatever happens at dawn, I'll be on that bank or I won't. If I go north to Emil, I can't promise—"

"Don't." Her voice came out sharper than she meant. "Don't make the speech. I've heard you compose it in your head for a week; you get the same look you get reading proofs. Just—" She breathed. The lamp flickered, steadied. "Just don't finish it. Let it stay unfinished. Unfinished things can still be argued with."

"That's the second time tonight you've said that."

"I say true things twice. It's a printer's habit. First impression, then correction."

And then—she could not afterward have said which of them moved first, or whether the cellar simply tilted, the way a press tilts when the screw comes down—his hand covered hers, ink-stained and cold, and she turned her palm up beneath it, and that was all, that was everything: a palm opened in lamplight like a page waiting to be set.

"Tomas." She said his name the way she said the hardest words in a proof, slowly, giving each syllable its full weight. "I have spent my whole life being invisible. Quiet hands, quiet face, the daughter behind the glass, the girl who cuts letters no one reads the name of. I did it because it was safe. I did it because grief taught me that anything you love loudly can be taken loudly." Her thumb moved, barely, against his. "I am done with it. Do you understand? Whatever tomorrow costs—I am not willing to be invisible about this."

His breath left him slowly. Upstairs a beam ticked in the warming wall, the old house talking to itself, and far off, so faint it might have been imagination, the river groaned—the ice shifting in its sleep, dreaming of breaking.

"I crossed a border with a bullet in my side to deliver a letter," he said. "That was the bravest thing I ever did, I thought. Then I spent three weeks in your father's cellar learning to set type, and you leaned over the composing stick to correct my spacing, and I understood I had never been brave at all. Bravery is nothing. Bravery is just fear that hasn't found anything worth stopping for." He lifted her hand—her ink-blackened, turpentine-stung, twenty-three-year-old hand—and pressed his mouth to the knuckles like a man sealing a document he had no right to sign. "I love you. There. Said plainly, set solid, no ligatures. I know what it's worth and when it's worth it, and I'm saying it anyway, the night before, when it may buy nothing but itself."

"It buys itself," she whispered. "That's not nothing. That's the whole price of the world, some nights."

They stood like that a long time, hand in hand, while the lamp burned low and the river muttered in its sleep. Neither kissed the other. It surprised her later, how deliberately they didn't—a kiss would have been a conclusion, and conclusions were for finished things, and they had agreed to leave it unfinished. Instead he touched his forehead to hers, briefly, the way printers touch two faces of type together to feel whether they're level, and stepped back.

* * *

Near four o'clock they climbed the stairs together and found the shop door standing open to the spring dark, and Alois Hartwell sitting at his own workbench with a single candle, a ledger book before him, and the crossing book open on top of it.

He did not startle. He had known they were in the cellar; perhaps he had known since midnight; perhaps he had sat up here all night keeping vigil over the names, the way he kept vigil over everything, silently, impeccably, treason hidden inside loyalty like a hairline crack hidden inside sound lead.

"Papers are done?" he asked. Only that. His voice was even, tired, kind.

"Done and dried," Rosalind said. "Wrapped in the oilcloth with the manifests."

"Good." He closed the ledger—but not quickly enough, not before she saw, in the candle's upward light, the fresh line of ink on the open page. A name, written in her father's small, precise, unmistakably steady hand.

She could not read it from the doorway. But she had spent her life reading hands, and something in the way he closed the book—carefully, the way a man closes a door on a room where he has just made a decision—told her that whatever name lay on that page, it had not been written lightly, and it was not the name she would have chosen.

"Sleep," Alois said, rising, pinching out the candle so that the three of them stood a moment together in the dark that smelled of lead and lamp oil and the thaw coming off the river. "Dawn comes early, and the ice won't wait for any of us."

Above the rooftops, faint and far, the river cracked like a distant cannon—and fell silent again, holding its answer until morning.
