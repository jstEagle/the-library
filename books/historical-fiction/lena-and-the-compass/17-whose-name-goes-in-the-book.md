## 17. Whose Name Goes in the Book

The ice spoke first.

All that evening it had been muttering under the floorboards of the shop, a long groaning syllable that rose from the river and traveled up through the cellar stones, through the joists, through the soles of our feet, as though the building itself had grown a voice. Alois Hartwell had lived beside that river for fifty-one years and knew its grammar the way he knew the grammar of type: when to trust a line, when a page would crack along its fold, when the season was turning against you. And the river was telling him, in no uncertain terms, that there were perhaps four nights left before the crossing became impossible until autumn. Perhaps three. The warm wind off the western hills carried the smell of mud and rotting reeds, and the icemen on the quay had stopped their sawing, which meant they knew too.

He stood in the cellar with the lamp turned low and the crossing book open on the flour barrel, and he read his own name for the fourth time.

*Hartwell, Alois. Printer.*

It looked wrong there. It looked like a misprint, like a character dropped into a line where it did not belong, and yet his hand had written it an hour ago in the dark, steady as setting a column of scripture. He had not let himself think while writing it. Thinking was for other men; the hand that had funded eleven crossings, that had signed receipts in cipher, that had moved more than two hundred souls south under bales of waste paper without once trembling—that hand could do anything, provided the mind behind it stayed out of the room.

Now the mind was back in the room, and it was screaming.

Footsteps on the cellar stairs. He closed the book too quickly, and the sound of it—leather slapping wood—was louder than any confession he'd ever heard.

"Father." Rosalind came down with a candle she didn't need; the lamp threw enough light, but she carried candles the way her mother had, out of some old instinct about darkness and what it concealed. "Petra's asleep upstairs. Tomas is watching the street from the loft window. We should—" She stopped. Her eyes went to the book, then to his face, and he watched her arrive at the arithmetic of it: *he was alone down here; the book was open; something had been decided.* "What have you done?"

"We need to settle this," he said, and was disgusted by how reasonable his own voice sounded. "Tonight. Properly. All of us."

She set the candle down slowly, the way one sets down a thing that might go off. "You've written your name."

"I said we should—"

"You've *written your name*, Father. I can see the ink isn't dry."

Above them, faintly, the floor creaked: Tomas descending. He came into the lamplight still dressed as the journeyman they'd made of him, sleeves rolled, forearms smudged with ink he no longer needed to fake. He looked from father to daughter with the wariness of a man who has walked into a room where the argument began before him.

"The berth," Rosalind said, not taking her eyes off Alois. "Tell me what you wrote."

Alois considered lying. It would have been easy; it had become horrifyingly easy all winter, and he hated himself for the fluency of it, for the smoothness with which falsehood left his mouth these days, as though Voss's visits had sanded a channel down which deceit now ran of its own accord. But Rosalind had her mother's eyes, and those eyes had never once been fooled by him, not even at seven years old with jam on her chin.

"Yes," he said. "I wrote my name."

The silence that followed had weight, like wet snow sliding off a roof.

"No," Rosalind said.

"Rosalind—"

"*No.* Emil should come home. That's the whole point of everything—all of it, the ledger, the money, the years of it—the point was always to bring him home, and now the river offers one night and one berth and you propose to spend it on yourself?"

"On *myself*?" The word cracked something open in him. He heard his own voice rise, and did nothing to lower it; the shop above was empty of everyone but a sleeping girl, and the ice outside was groaning loud enough to cover a great many sins. "Child, your brother deserted the imperial army. If he sets foot on this bank—if his name appears on any manifest, if one soldier at the landing sees his face—he is shot against the customs wall before noon. From the far side he is alive. Here he is dead, only slower about it. The network needs him where he is, guiding crossings, moving people. You don't pull the keel out of a boat because you miss the smell of it."

"He's my *brother*."

"And I am your father, and I am telling you how it must be." He drew breath. This was the part he had rehearsed, standing here alone with the lamp. "One of us goes. Not Emil. So: you, or Tomas, or me. You are twenty-three years old and you have spent your whole life making yourself small, invisible, unremarkable—and I will not have that be the shape of your entire existence. Go south. Start again somewhere the censors have never heard of Hartwell & Son. Print whatever God tells you to print."

"Father—"

"And Tomas—" Alois turned to the younger man, who had gone very still in the way of soldiers and animals. "You are known to them. Wounded in their service, hunted across half the province. Every day you breathe in this city, Petra's confessions or someone else's put a rope around all our necks. If anyone on this earth needs that berth, it is you."

Tomas shook his head slowly. When he spoke, his accent had thickened with emotion, the vowels of the southern valleys bleeding back into his mouth. "With respect, Herr Hartwell—you're wrong twice over."

"Am I."

"First: they don't want me alive. A berth spent on me buys them a corpse and buys us nothing. Second—" he glanced at Rosalind, and something passed between them that Alois felt on his skin like a draft from an opened door, "—second, I'm not leaving her."

The cellar went quiet enough that Alois could hear the lamp burning. He looked at his daughter. She was looking at Tomas, and her face—that careful, guarded, invisible face she had worn like a well-cut coat for twenty-three years—had come entirely undone. There was nothing hidden in it at all. And Alois understood, with the sick clarity of a man reading his own obituary, that this had happened without his noticing, in stolen hours over a composing stick, in a shop he had thought he still governed.

"When?" he asked quietly.

Rosalind blinked. "When what?"

"When did you decide this? Both of you. Because it wasn't tonight, and it wasn't yesterday." He gestured at the space between them. "This. Whatever vow you've made to each other in whispers. When?"

"Father, please—"

"No. Answer me." His voice was gentle now, which frightened him more than shouting. "Because I have spent two years lying to you, and you to me, and I find I cannot bear one more silence in this house."

Rosalind pressed her lips together. Then, in a voice smaller than he had ever heard from her: "Since the raid on Binder's Lane. Since he thought it might be his fault. Since he stood in the loft at three in the morning and told me he'd give himself up to stop the arrests, and I—" She stopped. Started again. "I love him, Father. I know what it costs to say it. I'm saying it anyway."

The ice groaned beneath them, long and low, like a ship changing its mind.

Alois closed his eyes. He saw his wife—not as she died, gray and diminished, but as she had been at Rosalind's age, laughing at him across a case of type, her fingers black to the second knuckle. *You Hartwells,* she used to say. *You love in lead. Heavy, permanent, and impossible to take back once it's set.* He had thought it a compliment. Standing here, he understood it was a warning.

"Then it's simple," he said. "None of you goes. I go."

"No—"

"It is settled, it is written, and it is *right*." He heard his own voice take on the register of the pulpit, though he had never been a churchgoing man. "Think, girl, use the head God gave you. If Rosalind Hartwell disappears the same week her brother is confirmed alive, Voss hangs every thread together inside a day. The network dies, the shop dies, Petra swings for her confession, and your Emil loses everything he's built on the far bank. But an old printer—a widower, respectable, tired, *plausibly* weary of an empire that censors his trade—an old printer retires south for his health. No one follows an old man. No one weeps over him. The story closes itself like a book." He spread his hands. "I am the only name in this cellar whose departure explains nothing and therefore threatens nothing."

"That's not true," Rosalind whispered.

"It is precisely true. It is the truest thing I have said in two years."

"And what does Mother say," Rosalind flared, "from wherever she watches you? That you'll die in some foreign town with no one to cut your matrices, no one to close your eyes?"

"She says," Alois answered, and his voice broke on it, finally, honestly, "what she always said. That loving someone means putting them on the press and letting the screw come down, and standing there while it happens, because the impression is worth the pain." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist, roughly, like a man clearing a jammed roller. "I have been pressing impressions for thirty years, Rosalind. Let this be the last one, and let it be deep."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Tomas stepped forward and laid his hand flat on the crossing book—not over Alois's name, but beside it, an odd, deliberate gesture, as if swearing on it.

"Herr Hartwell. I crossed that river nine times in two winters. I have seen good boats fail and bad ones hold, and I will tell you the one law of the ice: it takes whoever hesitates." His eyes were level. "If you go, I row you myself. That is the only promise I can make you that won't rot. But hear me—I will not write your name in that book while your daughter stands there begging. Decide it with her, or don't decide it at all."

"I decided it an hour ago, in the dark, alone," Alois said. "That is how fathers decide things."

"Then you decided it wrong," Rosalind said.

They argued until the candle burned to a stub and the lamp guttered and Tomas trimmed the wick with a knife. They argued the way printers argue about kerning—endlessly, precisely, over fractions of a life. Rosalind offered every counterweight: that she could vanish as easily as he, that the shop could pass to a cousin, that love was not a debt to be paid in departures. Alois met each one, and met it well, because he had spent two years learning to argue a false position fluently and this one, God help him, was true.

And underneath all of it, under the words, ran the thing none of them said aloud: that the berth was not really being argued about at all. That each of them was trying to buy the others' lives with their own, and that love, in the Hartwell family, had always been transacted exactly so—in lead, in ink, in heavy permanent metal that could not be taken back once set.

Near midnight, exhausted, they climbed to the kitchen and drank tea no one wanted, and Rosalind fell asleep in the chair with her head against Tomas's shoulder, and Alois sat watching them and felt his heart break in the specific manner of a man who has gotten what he most wished for and cannot keep it.

Later—he would never remember deciding to move, only that his legs carried him—he went back down to the cellar.

The lamp. The barrel. The book.

He opened it to the final page. His name was still there in his own firm hand, and beneath the lamplight it no longer looked like a misprint. It looked like the last line of a long composition, set true, justified clean.

He took up the pen. Beside his name he wrote the date, and after the date, in small letters that only a compositor would think to add, a single word:

*Paid.*

He blew out the lamp and stood in the dark listening to the ice talk, and it sounded almost friendly now, almost like an invitation.

Upstairs, in the loft, a board creaked under a weight that should have been sleeping. Alois froze, hand on the stair rail, straining into the black.

Someone was awake up there. Someone had followed him down—or rather, someone had watched him come down, and was now standing at the top of the cellar stairs, silhouetted against the gray square of the kitchen window, holding something in one hand that caught what little light remained.

Paper. Torn paper.

"Father," Rosalind said, in a voice he had never heard from her, cold and level as a freshly cut edge, "show me the last page of that book."
