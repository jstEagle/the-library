## 18. The Last Berth

The cellar had never smelled so much like a beginning.

Rosalind stood at the workbench with the crossing book open before her, its pages soft as cloth from handling, and let herself look at the thing they had built out of fear and ink. The lamp guttered in the damp air. Somewhere above her, the shop floor creaked as the house settled into the cold of the small hours, and beneath that—farther off, down by the embankment—the river spoke. Not cracking yet. Groaning. A long animal sound, like something turning over in its sleep that would not sleep much longer.

Two days. Perhaps three. After that the ice would go all at once, the way it always did, and the crossing route would drown until autumn.

She turned the page to the manifest.

It was a modest document, as such things went: a false shipping order in her own best imitation of a freight clerk's hand, listing bales of rag paper bound for a mill downriver, weights calculated to within a stone of plausibility because Tomas had insisted that lies, like type, must be set true to their measure or the whole line collapses. At the bottom, where a consignor's name belonged, was the berth itself—one line, one passenger, one name written in the careful upright script they used for anything that might be read by unfriendly eyes.

She had come down to check it a dozen times since midnight. She told herself it was diligence. That a forger checks her work; that her father had taught her to proof everything twice, then once more for humility.

But she knew, standing there in the lamplight with her breath ghosting silver, that she had not come to check the manifest.

She had come to look at Emil's name.

Because it was settled now—that was what she kept telling herself, in the voice she used for calming frightened apprentices, which these days meant herself. Emil would come home. Her brother, two years dead in every record and every prayer, alive on the far bank with a scarred river guide's hands and their mother's stubborn jaw, would step onto the ice at dawn on Thursday and walk south out of the empire's reach, and someday—when the world was kinder, or simply different—he would send word, and she would know he had made it. She had torn up every other arrangement to make it so. Tomas had argued for himself first and then for Alois and then, when both arguments failed, had gone very quiet in the way that frightened her more than shouting. But she had won. The name in the book was Emil Hartwell. It had been Emil Hartwell for six hours.

She looked down.

The name in the book was not Emil Hartwell.

For a moment she could not read it at all. The letters swam, the lamp flame doubled, and the cellar tilted the way a deck tilts—not much, only enough to make you understand that the solid thing beneath your feet is water wearing a disguise. She put her palm flat on the page to stop it moving, an old habit from seasickness crossings she'd never actually taken, and made herself look properly.

* Alois Hartwell.*

Written in his own hand. Not forged, not disguised—she knew his script the way she knew his tread on the stair, the slight forward lean of his *A*, the way his *l* descended below the baseline like a man leaning into weather. He had not even tried to hide it. There was no signature of deception here, no clerkly anonymity. He had written his own name into the last berth of the last crossing as plainly as a man signing a confession, and he had done it sometime between the hour she had set the type for the false manifest and the hour the household banked its fires—and he had said nothing.

She heard her own breathing. She heard the river groan again, closer now, or perhaps only louder, as if it too had turned to listen.

"No," she said aloud, and the word fell into the dark of the cellar like a piece of lead into a galley—small, dense, final.

---

She did not cry. That surprised her afterward, remembering it. What she felt was not grief, or not only grief—it was the specific vertigo of a compositor who lifts what she believes to be a full form and finds it half-empty, everything she thought was locked in place suddenly loose and sliding. Her father had lied to her. Not the great useful lie he had maintained for two years, the lie of the loyal printer, the respectable cutter of matrices who dined with magistrates and paid his taxes and asked no questions—that lie she had learned to live inside, had even come to admire once its architecture was revealed to her. This was smaller and worse. This was a lie told in her own house, in her own ledger, against her own decision, in the space of a single evening, by a man who had spent sixty years teaching her that a printed word is a promise.

He meant to take her place.

That was the shape of it, once she stopped fighting the reading and let the sentence stand in all its terrible clarity. He believed—she could see the whole argument assembling itself in his head, patient as setting a page—that the network needed its guide alive, and that Rosalind needed her brother, and that the shop needed someone in it whose face was unknown to Voss's men, and that the person least essential to all three necessities was Alois Hartwell himself. He would walk out onto the ice at dawn carrying nothing but the clothes he stood in and sixty years of quiet treason, and he would send for her from wherever the river delivered him, and he genuinely, sincerely, with the full weight of his careful heart, believed he was doing the arithmetic of love.

And the worst of it—the part that made her grip the edge of the bench until the wood bit her palm—was that the arithmetic was almost right. That was what made it unbearable. If she had found a selfish name in the book she could have despised it and corrected it and slept. But this name was written in selflessness, in the same hand that had signed over fifteen years of secret payments to move strangers across the ice, in the hand that had cut matrices by day and funded fugitives by night without once, not once, letting the mask slip in front of the magistrate he drank wine with every month. He had chosen exile the way he chose everything: deliberately, silently, and last.

She thought of him upstairs, asleep behind the thin wall of his room, having eaten his supper as usual, having commented—as usual—on the price of lamp oil. Having kissed her forehead at the landing, as he had done every night since her mother died, and said, "Mind the damp, Rosalind," as if the only danger in the world were the cellar's chill.

"Mind the damp." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle like spring ice.

---

* * *

She sat with the book for a long time.

The lamp burned low. Twice she rose to trim the wick, and twice she sat back down without deciding anything, because deciding was the thing she could not do. Above her the house ticked and settled. Once, footsteps crossed the floor overhead—her father, going to the privy or pacing, she couldn't tell—and she held her breath like a child until the boards went quiet again. She thought about waking him. She thought about storming up the stairs and throwing the open book onto his blanket and demanding he explain himself, and she rehearsed the words—I tore out your lie, I wrote Emil back, how dare you, how *dare* you decide for me—and each rehearsal collapsed under the same weight: he would only look at her with those grey eyes, steady as a plumb line, and say, "Someone has to stay who can keep the shop. You are the shop now, my dear. I am only the old man who used to run it." And she would have no answer, because it was exactly what she would have said, in his place, and had in fact said, in hers, arguing for Emil.

They were the same, the two of them. That was the wound of it. All her life she had prided herself on being unlike him—invisible where he was impeccable, cautious where he was merely careful, small where he was merely still. And here was the proof, written in his hand on the same page as her own plan: they loved identically. Both of them reached for sacrifice the way other people reached for a hand in the dark. Neither of them could bear to be the one saved.

Somewhere in that long silent hour, the anger burned down to coals, and under the coals she found the thing she had actually come downstairs carrying, though she hadn't known its name until now.

She did not want to choose.

That was the truth beneath all the logistics, beneath the manifests and the berths and the dates of the thaw. For two days she had dressed the choice up in necessity—Emil should come home because he was family, because he was dead on paper and needed killing properly—but the naked fact was simpler and crueler: she wanted her brother back, and she wanted her father safe, and the river had room for one, and the river did not care what she wanted. Every scheme she had built since the letter arrived had been scaffolding around that single unbearable beam. Choose. Choose. Choose.

Her mother used to say, when Rosalind was small and furious at some injustice of portions or privileges: *Life is not fair, little bird. It is only honest.* She had hated the saying. She understood it now, sitting alone in the cellar with a lamp and a book and a name that was not the name she had written. Honesty was exactly what made it cruel. A fair world would have had room in the boat for everyone. An honest one had a manifest with one line left, and ink enough for a single name.

She picked up the pen.

Her hand did not shake. She noticed that with a strange, detached pride—the hand knew its trade even when the heart was in pieces. She turned to the manifest page, to her father's upright script with its forward-leaning *A*, and she did not strike it through. Striking it through would be a correction, and this was not a correction. It was a choice, and choices deserve clean execution, the way her father cut a matrix: one decisive motion, no hesitation marks, no second thoughts left visible in the metal.

She gripped the corner of the page.

The paper resisted—good stock, heavy rag, the kind her father bought precisely because documents that mattered deserved paper that fought back—and for one absurd heartbeat she imagined the whole book crying out, all their forged orders and false weights and smuggled names protesting together. Then she pulled, and the page came free with a sound like a small tear in the world, clean along the binding, leaving the ghost of its previous leaf rough-edged behind it.

She held it a moment. His name in his handwriting, the last thing he had written that night before going up to bed and kissing her forehead and telling her to mind the damp. Then she folded it once, twice, and slid it inside her bodice, against her heart, where she kept the letter in Emil's hand and, lately, the scrap of paper on which Tomas had drawn, one bored evening, a small compass rose that pointed nowhere at all.

Then she took up the pen, dipped it, and wrote.

*Alois Hartwell.*

Her hand, this time. Her forward-leaning letters, learned from his, close enough that only she would ever know the difference. She wrote it slowly, giving each character its full measure, and as she wrote she felt something break and something settle in her at once, the way ice breaks and settles on the river—a rending and a leveling in the same instant. Because she understood, finally, what she was doing, and she did not flinch from it. Emil would stay on the far bank. Emil, who had already survived two years of a war the records said had killed him; Emil, who had made himself necessary to the network, who had a craft and a bank and a crew of small hands who depended on him; Emil, who would curse her name across the frozen water and call it betrayal and be wrong. He would live. He would grieve. He would live.

Her father would go.

She set the pen down and looked at the name and let herself feel the whole cost of it at once, without anesthesia, because she owed him that much honesty: she was choosing the man who raised her over the brother she mourned, and she was doing it knowing he would hate it, knowing he had already voted with his own hand, knowing that somewhere in his careful heart he had already begun saying goodbye to the shop, to the press, to the smell of ink and the weight of lead—to her. She was overriding his sacrifice with her own, and there was no version of this in which one of them did not lose, and the losing would be permanent, and the winner would carry the loser's grief like a stone in the chest for the rest of her life.

The lamp flame steadied. The river groaned.

"I'm sorry, Emil," she whispered. "I'm sorry, Papa."

Both apologies were true. Neither helped.

---

She was still sitting there, her palm resting flat on the fresh ink, when the stairs creaked.

Not the settling of the house. Footsteps—deliberate ones, descending at the pace of a man who has decided to stop pretending he sleeps. She had time to slide the folded page deeper against her heart and close the book, and then the lamp threw his shadow up the stairwell ahead of him, long-armed, familiar, and Alois Hartwell came down into the cellar in his nightshirt and dressing gown with his hair white in the lamplight and his eyes already on the closed book.

He saw it in her face before he saw anything else. She watched him understand, watch the knowledge arrive the way frost arrives on glass—silently, completely, from every edge toward the center at once. His gaze went from her face to the book to the gap in the binding where a page had been, and his hand rose halfway toward the cover and stopped, as if touching it might confirm something neither of them could survive confirming.

"Rosalind," he said quietly. "What have you done?"

"What have *I* done?" Her voice came out low and shaking, and she was glad of it, glad finally to be loud. "You wrote yourself into the book. In your own hand. You didn't even—" She stopped, pressed her knuckles to her mouth, started again. "You kissed me goodnight. You told me to mind the damp. And all the while your name was sitting right there, and you let me sit down here alone believing I'd won."

"You did win." He came to the bench, laid his hand flat on the closed cover, over the place where his name now lived. Up close she could see how tired he was, how the two years of secret payments and secret fear had worn grooves in him that she had mistaken, all this time, for ordinary age. "Emil comes home. That was the only outcome worth winning."

"And you? What are you worth?"

He smiled at that, a small crooked smile she had seen maybe five times in her life, always at moments when the truth got too large for silence. "I'm an old man with a good coat and a talent for boring magistrates. I'll do nicely downriver. Your brother needs his sister more than the network needs its financier's corpse."

"He's not going to need me if you're—" She couldn't finish it. She made herself finish it. "If you're walking onto that ice on Thursday, I will spend every day of my life knowing I let you."

"And I would spend every day of mine knowing I took my son's place home from him." He said it without drama, the way he said everything, and somehow that undid her more than any shout could have. "We are the same, you and I. I've known it since you were nine years old and gave your supper to the binder's boy and went hungry rather than tell me why. We cannot bear to be the ones saved." His thumb moved across the book's cover, slow, tracing the invisible name beneath. "So. We have written our votes. Whose hand holds the pen?"

"The manifest is mine," she said. "The book stays in my keeping. And I am stronger than you, Papa, and meaner, and I sleep less."

Something moved in his face then—grief and pride tangled past any hope of separating them—and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and the lamp hissed softly, and down at the embankment the river gave a long groaning sigh and then, unmistakably now, a single sharp crack, like a distant door closing.

They both turned their heads toward the sound.

"That's early," Alois said. "The ice isn't due to break for two days yet."

"It's not breaking." Rosalind was already on her feet, her heart going hard against the folded page over it. "That crack came from upstream. From the crossing path."

From the direction of the far bank—from the reeds and the blind hut where a dead man had been living for two years—came voices, thin with distance but sharp with urgency, and then a lantern swinging fast along the shore, its light stuttering across the ice like a signal trying to find the strength to become words.

Tomas was coming across the river in the dark. And Tomas only ever crossed in the dark when the news could not wait for morning.
