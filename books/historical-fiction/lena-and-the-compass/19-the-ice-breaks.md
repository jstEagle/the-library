## 19. The Ice Breaks

The river began to speak before dawn.

Tomas heard it first as a sound he mistook for distant artillery—a low concussion rolling up from the water and into the bones of the buildings along Binder's Lane, rattling the shutters of the print shop, loosening a fine dust of plaster from the cellar ceiling that fell like flour onto the bales of waste paper waiting to be loaded. He had been awake all night anyway. Everyone had. But the sound made sleep impossible even for those who had managed it, and soon the whole house was standing: Rosalind with her shawl clutched at the throat, Alois in his good coat with the collar turned up against the cold that no coat could truly keep out, Petra pale as tallow by the stairs, wringing a rag she had been wringing for an hour without noticing.

"The ice is going," Tomas said. It was not information any of them needed. They could feel it through the floor.

He went down to the loading door alone first, because someone had to look, and because he did not want his face to be seen doing it. The sky was the color of iron filings—no, that was wrong; it was the color of the type metal Rosalind melted each week, that dull pewter light that came just before a dawn the sun intended to make ugly. The river lay under a skin of broken white, and beneath that skin something vast was turning over. He watched a crack race across the channel from bank to bank with a sound like a giant tearing linen, and where the crack passed, black water opened its eye and closed it again. The floes were beginning to move. Slowly. Grudgingly. Like a congregation rising from a long sermon.

Behind him, boots on the stair. Rosalind, then Alois, then the others—the two families who would have crossed if there had been berths, come to watch the boat take what they could not. No one spoke. There was nothing left to say that had not been said three times over in the last forty-eight hours, most of it in the language of lists: papers checked, ink dried, bread wrapped, the manifest folded into oilcloth and sewn into the lining of a coat that was not Alois Hartwell's best coat but nearly.

Tomas had argued until midnight. He wanted to say now that he had been wrong to argue, but the truth was more complicated and less useful than that. He had argued because arguing felt like doing something. He had argued because a man who cannot swim should not lecture others about rivers, and yet there he stood on the frozen bank preparing to put an old printer into the current on the strength of a boy's promise from the far shore. Emil had written: *One berth. One passenger. Send him at first light or send no one.* He had signed it with the ligature Rosalind taught him when they were children—the one that looked like a small anchor hooked through the *m*—and seeing that signature had done more to settle the matter than any argument.

"You'll want to check the knots yourself," Alois said behind him. His voice was steady. It had been steady for two days, which frightened Tomas more than weeping would have. A man who has decided to die—or worse, to live elsewhere while everything he loves remains here—can become very calm. "You're the one who'll answer to my son if I arrive damp."

"I'll answer to him regardless," Tomas said. "That's what family means, I'm told."

Rosalind laughed—one short syllable, quickly swallowed—and then pressed her hand flat against the doorframe as another crack boomed upriver, closer this time, the ice groaning in long complaint like a ship changing its mind about sinking.

* * *

The boat was a paper-barge, which was the whole genius of the route and always had been. Nobody questions a barge loaded with baled waste paper going downstream; every print shop in the quarter produces such cargo weekly, and customs men have learned that rags and spoiled sheets are heavy, dull, and unworthy of a bayonet's time. Beneath the topmost bales—visible, official, stamped with the Hartwell press's mark that Voss himself had seen a thousand times—lay a false floor, and beneath the false floor, a space barely long enough for a man to lie flat, lined with sacking against the cold and furnished with a lantern, a flask, and a chamber pot, because Emil understood dignity even at the end of the world.

They carried Alois down in the grey half-light. He insisted on walking to the gangplank unassisted, and then ruined the effect entirely by stopping and looking back—not at the shop, Tomas noticed, but at Rosalind.

She had braided her hair for the occasion, which she never did for work, and Tomas understood that she had dressed for her father's departure the way one dresses for a funeral: to give the dead the courtesy of effort. Her face was composed. Her hands were not.

"Papa," she said.

"My dear." He took both her hands. "The shop opens at seven. Open it at eight instead. Let them wonder."

"Let them wonder," she repeated, and her voice held, though only just.

Alois turned to Tomas last. For a moment neither spoke, and the river filled the silence, booming and cracking, the floes grinding past in the dark like slow animals. Then Alois gripped Tomas's forearm in the grip of a man who has set type for forty years—dry, precise, stronger than it looks.

"You will keep her alive," he said. "Not safe. Alive. There is a difference, and I have spent two years learning it at considerable expense. Safe is a room with the shutters closed. Alive is what she is about to be. Keep up."

"Yes, sir."

"And tell my son—" Alois stopped. Tomas watched him try the sentence three ways and discard all of them. Finally he said, "Tell him the second key is where I told you, and that I was never angry. Not once. Not even at the ledger."

Then he went down into the hold, and the hatch closed over him like water closing, and Tomas stood on the frozen bank listening to the muffled sounds of a man arranging himself into a coffin-shaped space and finding it adequate, and thought: *this is what courage looks like from the outside. It looks like bookkeeping.*

* * *

The barge went out at first light exactly, because Emil's letter had said first light and Emil's letters were contracts.

Tomas watched from the window of the ropewalk loft across the lane—he had refused the bank, refused to stand in a crowd of watchers, because crowds attract eyes—but Rosalind stood on the loading step with Petra beside her, two small figures against the enormous grey river, and he watched them watching the barge shrink. The ice had opened a channel overnight, a winding lead of black water perhaps six barge-lengths wide, and the pilot threaded it with the casual insolence of a man who had done this every spring of his life. The floes knocked against the hull. From the loft, through the glass, Tomas could hear them striking—soft, wooden sounds, patient, like knuckles on a door.

Halfway across the channel, the ice cracked.

Not the groaning of the night—that had been continuous, seismic, the river clearing its throat. This was different. A single report, sharp as a musket, directly beneath the barge, and then a web of white fractures racing outward from the hull in every direction, and Tomas's heart stopped in a way he would remember for years, stopped so completely that he had time to think *I have killed him, I have put a sixty-year-old typesetter into a coffin of his own choosing and the river has declined the arrangement—*

But the channel held. The fracture lines reached the solid ice and died there. The barge rode on, rocking, unhurried, and after a moment that lasted approximately the rest of Tomas's life, it passed beyond the broken water into the far channel, and the far bank received it the way the far bank receives everything: silently, indifferently, behind its curtain of bare poplars.

Down on the loading step, Rosalind raised one hand. She held it up a long time, long after anyone could see whether it was answered, and then she brought it down slowly, deliberately, the way one lowers a flag, and turned back toward the shop.

Tomas met her in the yard. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely dry.

"He's across," she said.

"He's across."

"Say something else. Say anything else. If we stop talking I'm going to fall down."

So Tomas said the only thing he had: "Your father writes a better hand than your brother. Don't tell Emil."

And Rosalind laughed—really laughed, a cracked and gasping thing with grief inside it, the kind of laughter that is only weeping wearing its good clothes—and caught his arm, and for one moment, in the freezing yard with the river booming behind them like bombardment, she leaned her forehead against his shoulder and let herself be held. It lasted four seconds. He counted them. He expected to count them for the rest of his life, whichever length that turned out to be.

* * *

The soldiers came at twenty minutes past seven.

Tomas knew the exact time because the church bells of Saint Aldric were still finishing the hour when the knock came—not a knock but a blow, a fist with the weight of the state behind it, and below it the voice of a sergeant announcing the magistrate's warrant in the bored tone of a man reading a laundry list.

They had rehearsed this. God help them, they had rehearsed it until the rehearsal itself became dangerous knowledge, until Rosalind could recite her part in her sleep: the shop opens late today, my father has gone downriver on the paper run to negotiate spring freight, I am alone except for the journeyman, here are the legitimate orders, here is the customs stamp, here is the tax receipt for the quarter, here is my father's letter of instruction, forged on his own press two nights ago and aged with tea and handled with gloves until it smelled of a man's pocket. Every document true-adjacent. Every lie load-bearing.

Tomas went down the stairs ahead of her, wiping his hands on his apron, composing his face into the mild resentment of a workman interrupted. Behind him he heard Rosalind throw the bolt, and he heard her breath—one breath, drawn and released, the breath of a woman stepping onto a stage—and then the door swung open on grey uniforms and bayonets and the thin morning light, and the day that had begun with salvation began, in the same motion, to curdle.

"Sergeant Brandt," said the sergeant, as if Tomas might have forgotten him from the raid on Binder's Lane. "Magistrate Voss requires entry to the premises."

"Premises are closed. Owner's away on the river."

"Then you won't mind if we inspect what the owner left behind."

There was no minding it; there was no version of events in which minding it mattered. Six soldiers spread through the shop with the thorough, destructive patience of their profession. They pulled type from the cases and let it scatter—they did it almost gently, Tomas noticed, almost lovingly, the way boys pull the wings off flies. They opened the ink drawers and stabbed their bayonets into the paste. One of them discovered the composing stone and stood before it frowning at the half-set galley as though it were an insult delivered in a language he almost spoke.

Rosalind played her part flawlessly. She walked beside Sergeant Brandt reciting orders—the diocese pamphlets, the grain-factor's invoices, the funeral notices for the Kessler family, eleven hundred copies, paid in advance—and her voice never wavered, and Tomas, watching her, felt the strange vertigo of seeing invisibility performed as an act rather than lived as a condition. Two months ago this woman had made herself a ghost to survive. Now she stood in the wreckage of her father's shop narrating inventories to armed men, and her stillness was louder than any scream.

It was working. That was the unbearable thing. It was actually working. Brandt was growing bored; boredom was the ally of every smuggler since the invention of property; and Tomas allowed himself, in the privacy of his own skull, one full second of belief that they would walk out of this—

Then a soldier called down from the loft.

"Herr Sergeant. Up here. There's a loose board."

* * *

Tomas did not go up. He made himself stay at the foot of the ladder with his hands folded and his heart going like a trip-hammer, because a journeyman does not follow soldiers around his master's house, and because if he ran for that loft they would know everything in a single gesture. So he stood and listened to the boards creak overhead, and listened to Rosalind keep talking about the Kessler funeral notices, her voice climbing slightly, valiantly, in pitch, and he did the arithmetic that all of them had done and none of them had finished:

Petra's confession. Written out, signed, hidden under the loft floorboards against the day it might be needed as evidence of coercion—hidden there by Rosalind's own hand, in the belief that the safest place for a dangerous truth was the place only she knew. And Voss's men had found, in two days of searching, the one board that was loose.

Footsteps on the ladder. The soldier descending with a bundle of papers in his fist, bound with the string Petra used for parcels, and Tomas saw Rosalind see it, saw her face do nothing whatsoever, which was the most terrible expression he had ever witnessed on a human being.

Sergeant Brandt untied the string with two thick fingers. He read. His eyebrows rose by a degree, which for Brandt was opera.

"Well," he said. "Well, well. Fräulein Hartwell. Would you care to tell me what this is?"

"It appears," Rosalind said, in a voice like level water, "to be a page from somebody's exercise book."

"It appears," said Brandt, folding it into his coat with exaggerated care, "to be a full confession of smuggling, forgery, and conveyance of fugitives, signed with a name I happen to know, dated four days ago, and hidden under the floor of your loft." He smiled. It was not a cruel smile; it was worse—it was a satisfied one. "The magistrate will want to discuss it with you. Both of you. At his convenience."

"At his convenience," Rosalind repeated.

"This afternoon," Brandt said, and touched his hat brim, almost politely. "Do close up properly. There's thieves in this quarter."

They filed out. The door closed. The bells of Saint Aldric began to ring the half hour, and Tomas stood in the wrecked shop amid scattered type and stabbed ink and the smell of gun oil, listening to the boots fade down Binder's Lane, and beside him Rosalind finally moved—crossed to the composing stone, picked up the stick, and began, with hands that trembled only slightly, to right the fallen letters, one piece of lead at a time, as if order were a thing that could be restored by hand before afternoon.

"They have her name," she said quietly. "Signed. In her own hand."

"Yes."

"Then they have Petra." She set an *e* into its row with a small click. "And by evening, they'll have everyone whose name is in it. Unless—" She looked up at him, and in her eyes he saw the thing being born that would fill the city with paper within the week, the thing she did not yet have words for but already had a plan for. "Unless we move faster than they do."

Outside, the river cracked like cannon fire, and kept cracking, all the way to the horizon.
