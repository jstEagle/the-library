## 20. Soldiers in the Shop

They came at the hour when the shop smelled most of itself—ink and oil and the cold mineral breath of lead—the hour when her father would have been banking the stove and she would have been washing the rollers, and Rosalind thought, absurdly, of the order for funeral broadsheets waiting on the stone, half-set, the widow's name misspelled in the third line. Someone would have to fix that. The thought arrived whole and calm, as if it had been set in type years ago and only now pulled from the case.

The knock was not a knock. It was the loading door giving way against its hasp, wood splitting in a voice she knew better than her own heartbeat, because that door had been speaking to her since childhood—*mind your fingers, mind the wind, mind who stands outside it*—and now it said nothing at all, just hung open on the gray afternoon with soldiers filling it like smoke filling a chimney.

She did not run. Running was for people whose guilt could be outrun.

"Rosalind Hartwell." The officer's voice was young and careful, the voice of a man reciting something he'd memorized and feared to get wrong. Behind him, boots on the cobbles, six or eight of them, bayonets catching the last flat light off the river.

"Yes," she said. She untied her apron, folded it once, laid it across the composing stone. It seemed important that the apron be folded. Whatever they were about to take, they would not take it tangled.

* * *

The magistrate's house had a room without windows, and in that room a table, two chairs, and a lamp that burned with the small offended hiss of a lamp that knows it is being watched. They sat her down at the table and left her alone for an hour, which she understood to be a technique, and so she treated it as one treats any long job: she settled in, she checked her tools, she waited for the work to begin.

Her tools were these: her father's legitimate orders, memorized over eleven years of cutting matrices and setting columns. The almanac for Herr Brandt the apothecary, forty copies, paid quarterly. The wedding announcements for the Fessler family, gold ink, two hundred sheets. The funeral broadsheet for Widow Kranz, due tomorrow, third line corrected. The shipping manifests for the grain barges—she let herself smile at that one, privately, in the dark behind her eyes. The manifest Voss had swallowed through Petra, hook, line, and lie.

When Voss finally came, he came quietly, without ceremony, closing the door behind him with the softness of a man who has done this many hundreds of times and found softness more useful than force. He was smaller than she remembered from the dinner parties of her girlhood, when he had stood by her mother's chair and praised her father's loyalty in a voice like warmed butter. Age had shrunk him toward his eyes, and his eyes were the sharpest thing in the room.

"Fraulein Hartwell." He sat. He folded his hands. "You will forgive the discomfort. I find that discomfort, shared early, saves us both a great deal later."

"I find," Rosalind said, "that my father's customers are owed their deliveries, and you are keeping me from them."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite amusement, not quite its opposite. "Your father."

"My father is away on business. You know this. You've known where he is for three days, or you would have stopped the boat."

He let that pass, which told her he had not stopped the boat, which told her the crossing had succeeded, which was a coin she put away carefully in the vault behind her ribs. Alois Hartwell was on the water, going south, alive. Whatever happened in this room, that ledger balanced.

"You operate a print shop," Voss said.

"I operate a print shop."

"Print shops produce papers."

"When commissioned, yes."

"And what do you print, Fraulein, when no one commissions anything?"

"The same things. No press runs idle; idle presses rust." She kept her hands still on the table, palms down, the way her father taught her to hold them when haggling—a stillness that says *I have all day and you do not.* "Shall I give you the list?"

Voss tilted his head, a small courtesy. "Please."

So she gave him the list.

She began with the almanac—forty copies, Herr Brandt, quarterly, the moon tables always late because Brandt insisted on the older ephemeris. Then the Fessler wedding announcements, gold ink, though the gold had been thin that season and the letters came out the color of weak tea, and Frau Fessler had complained, and Father had remade the whole run at cost. Then the funeral broadsheets—one per month, sometimes two, the city being what it was—and she named each deceased party, each surviving spouse, each correction made gratis out of respect for the grieving. She moved on to the grain manifests, the customs declarations, the price lists for the fish market, the playbill for the traveling theater company that had come through in the autumn and performed, she noted, a comedy the censor had cut twice before permitting.

She spoke evenly, without hurry, the way she set type: one piece at a time, each in its place, the whole line true. She watched Voss's face as she went, and she saw the thing she had hoped to see, the thing Tomas had promised her she would see if she held her nerve: boredom. Not defeat, not suspicion—boredom. A man cannot hang a woman for knowing the moon tables.

"Fraulein." Voss raised one finger, gently, as though stopping traffic. "You are reciting invoices."

"I am answering your question."

"Your question was what we print. This is what we print."

"There were pamphlets," Voss said. "Ten thousand of them. On every door in the quarter by dawn. Paper does not walk, Fraulein. Paper is carried. Paper comes from somewhere."

"Then someone carried it," Rosalind said. "I woke to find it on our door like everyone else. My father was already gone south—I couldn't tell you who printed it. Could you?" And here she leaned forward slightly, and let the first honest thing into her voice, because honesty, deployed precisely, cuts deeper than any lie. "Magistrate, if I could print ten thousand sheets between midnight and dawn, I should have doubled my rates years ago."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them a door closed, a soldier shifting his weight, and Rosalind thought: *he is standing where the loft is. Where Petra's confession hid until they took it.* Her heart turned over slowly, like a barge coming about, but her hands stayed flat on the table.

"They found documents in your loft," Voss said.

"Did they? What sort?"

"A confession."

"Whose? I confess many things. That I burn the bad sheets. That I water the ink when paper is scarce. That I never liked the censor's revisions to the almanac—he makes the saints' days duller than they need be." She heard her own voice go light, and marveled at it, this fluency she had never known she possessed, this second language of the unremarkable daughter. All those years of invisibility, she understood suddenly, had been apprenticeship. Every day she had spent being nobody had trained her hands for exactly this: to set a line of nothing, of invoices, of moon tables, so straight and so true that even a magistrate could find no kerning error in it.

Voss studied her for a long time. In the lamp's small circle his eyes had gone from sharp to something wearier, almost admiring, which frightened her more than anger would have.

"Your brother," he said at last, "was declared dead at the frontier. Yet a letter exists, written in his hand four months ago. We have it. Or we had it—it has proven difficult to locate since." He paused. "Where is Emil, Fraulein?"

And there it was. Not the network, not the pamphlets, not her father—the question underneath all questions, the one Voss had built this whole gray room to ask. Rosalind felt the vertigo rise in her like floodwater, Emil alive on the far bank, Emil guiding crossings, Emil's ligatures she had taught him herself—and she pressed it down the way one presses a sheet to the type, flat, even, patient.

"Dead men write poor letters," she said. "If you have such a letter, perhaps you should ask whoever sold it to you how much you overpaid."

* * *

It went on for hours. She lost count of the lamps, of the changes of guard at the door, of the number of times Voss circled back to the same three questions wearing different clothes: the press, the loft, the brother. Each time she answered with paper. The Brandt almanac. The Kranz broadsheet. The fish-market prices. She began to feel less like a prisoner than like a typesetter working under a maddening client, one who keeps rejecting the proof and asking for the same page again.

Once, deep in the small hours, Voss set down his pencil and looked at her with something close to kindness, and that was the worst moment of the night.

"Child," he said, "your father dined in my house. Your mother sewed with my wife before God took her. I am offering you a door, not a wall. Give me names—names only, no testimony, no court—and you walk home before morning. Printers are tradesmen. Tradesmen survive regimes; it is their gift. Be a tradesman."

Rosalind looked at him across the lamp and felt the strange stillness settle over her completely at last, like snow falling into a full cup without spilling a drop.

"Magistrate," she said, "I'll tell you everything I know. Herr Brandt owes us for March. Frau Fessler never paid the surcharge for the gold ink, and I would thank you to make her. The Kranz broadsheet must go out tomorrow or the widow's family will wait another week to bury their grief properly. Those are my debts and my obligations, and they are the only names I keep." She stood—slowly, because her legs had gone stiff, but she stood. "My father taught me that a printer's word is in her paper. Every sheet that leaves our shop carries our name in the colophon. I have put my name on ten thousand truthful pages, Magistrate. Ask me about any of them."

Voss sat back. For a moment he looked very old, and very tired, and she understood that she was not the first person to sit across this lamp from him and refuse, and would not be the last, and that this, too, was part of his craft: he collected refusals the way she collected matrices, each one shaped to fit the next job.

"Keep her overnight," he said to the door, not to her. "Perhaps the morning improves her memory."

* * *

They put her in a cell that smelled of straw and old iron, and she lay on the plank bed with her coat for a blanket and discovered that she was not afraid, and examined this absence of fear the way she might examine a new font, turning it in the light. It was not courage, she decided. Courage implied a choice made fresh. What she felt was older than choice—the stillness of a thing that had finally been set in its chase, locked tight, ready for the press. Whatever impression was taken from her now, it would be a clean one.

Sometime past midnight—or what she judged to be midnight, counting the watchman's bell through the wall—boots came down the corridor, fast, more than one pair, voices low and urgent, and her first thought was *they've caught Emil*, her second *the boat*, her third—

The cell block door opened, lamplight spilling across the straw, and a sergeant stood there reading from a paper, and behind him, hands bound, head high, mud on his coat from the river road—

Tomas.

He looked worse than the night he'd collapsed against her loading door, thinner, a bruise blooming along his jaw, but his eyes found hers through the bars with a steadiness that undid her more thoroughly than any torture could have. He was smiling. He was actually smiling, the fool, the brave, impossible fool.

"Tomas Weller," the sergeant read. "Print journeyman, presenting himself voluntary, claiming sole responsibility for the printing and distribution of seditious materials recovered in this district. Claims also—" the sergeant's voice faltered slightly, as if the paper itself embarrassed him, "—claims the woman currently held acted under his direction and bears no knowledge of the trade's unlawful applications."

"He claims," Rosalind said aloud, to no one, to the straw, to the whole rotten empire, "a great deal."

They put him in the cell across from hers. When the lamps withdrew and the corridor went dark again, she heard him shift against the far wall, and then his voice, low, carrying across the space between bars the way a whisper carries across a pew.

"Every hour they spend on me is an hour they don't spend finding the others."

"You idiot." Her voice cracked on it, love and fury indistinguishable. "You absolute, self-sacrificing—"

"Rosalind." Quiet. Certain. "You taught me type. Let me teach you this: a press needs two hands. If they take one, the other keeps working. That's not sacrifice. That's mechanics."

She pressed her forehead to the cold bar and laughed, or wept, or some alloy of both, and across the dark he laughed too, softly, and for a moment the cell block sounded like the shop after closing, two people talking over the day's work while the stove ticked down.

"They have nothing on you," he said. "Nothing but stubbornness and invoices."

"Invoices," she said, "are bulletproof. Ask any magistrate."

Above them, through the stone, the city slept uneasily, and somewhere in it ten thousand sheets of paper clung to doors and walls and fence posts like frost that would not melt, naming Voss's abuses in type her own hands had helped to cut, and Rosalind Hartwell lay in the dark with her name in the colophon of a revolution, wide awake, entirely visible, and unafraid.

Toward morning, when the bell counted five and the guards changed with a shuffle of tired feet, she heard the outer door of the block open, and a voice she knew—the young careful voice of the officer from her loading door—say to another, low and troubled:

"They're saying the ice took the boat. All hands. There's wreckage downriver at the weir."

And Rosalind went cold from the crown of her head to the soles of her feet, because the ice had not broken at the weir—she knew that stretch of water, knew the weir's slow eddies better than her own ligatures—and wreckage, convenient wreckage, found exactly where Voss's men would find it, was not the river's work at all.

Someone was lying to the city about her father.
