## 21. The Press Runs at Midnight

*Rosalind*

They let her out at dusk, and the door of the magistrate's cells closed behind her with the softness of an apology.

Rosalind stood on the steps of the district court for a long moment, blinking at the ordinary world—the lamplighter working his way up Binder's Lane with his long brass pole, a woman shaking crumbs from a tablecloth into the gutter, the smell of bread from the corner oven. Three days she had sat in that stone room while Voss circled her like a hawk over winter fields. Three days of reciting legitimate orders: four hundred wedding invitations for the Weiss family, two hundred hymnals for St. Anne's, the customs manifests for Herr Brandt's tannery, the price lists, the playbills, the almanac pages. She had said them until they became a litany, until Voss's face went the color of old porridge, until even he understood there was nothing in her but paper.

No evidence. That was what the clerk had said, stamping her release. No evidence against Rosalind Hartwell—only a father gone downriver in the dark, a stranger who had walked into their custody wearing guilt like a borrowed coat, and a confession found in the loft that named no one living whom Voss could reach. Tomas had given himself up to split the blame, and the splitting had worked: half a crime is harder to hang than a whole one, and Voss had neither half entire.

She did not go home. Home was a word for a shop with its press dismantled and its cellar emptied by soldiers' hands, type scattered across the floorboards like dead insects, her father's matrices pried from their drawers and catalogued as evidence. She walked past it without stopping. She had promised herself she would not weep in front of the windows, and she kept the promise the way her father kept ledgers—precisely, and at great cost.

Instead she went where the note pinned inside her coat told her to go: the cooperage on the river road, third barrel from the wall, knock twice and once.

The man who opened the side door was old Karel the wheelwright, whom she had known all her life as a man who spoke only about axles. He looked at her a long moment, then stepped aside.

"You're thinner," he said.

"You're a smuggler," she said.

"Since Tuesday," said Karel. "Before that I was only a wheelwright who sometimes forgot how many wheels he'd made." He almost smiled. "Your father paid for my daughter's passage in 'forty-four. Never knew I knew."

The cooperage smelled of oak shavings and river damp. Inside, among the staves and hoops, perhaps thirty people waited in the lantern light—and when they saw her, they did not cheer. They simply turned, all of them, the way a field of wheat turns toward sun, and an old woman pressed something into her hand: a heel of bread, still warm.

"We couldn't do it without the press," Karel said quietly. "The city has to wake up knowing. And the city will believe ink from the Hartwell press before it believes any handbill off a streetcorner. Your father's mark is on every honest page in this town." He paused. "It should be on this one too."

"The press is in pieces," Rosalind said. "Soldiers catalogued every piece. It's under seal."

"Yes," said Karel. "That is a problem. We hoped you might have opinions about problems."

She looked down at the warm bread in her hands, and thought of her father cutting matrices by lamplight, patient as water, forty years of small exact work hidden inside an impeccable surface. She thought of Emil on the far bank. Of Tomas in his cell, who had taken the whole weight onto himself and smiled at her through the bars as if to say *you owe them nothing now—so give them everything.*

"I have opinions," she said.

* * *

*Tomas*

They moved him to a different cell on the second night, which he understood to mean the paperwork was catching up with him, and on the third day Magistrate Voss came down the stairs himself instead of sending the interrogator.

Voss was not a large man. He wore his authority the way some men wore perfume—a little too much, applied carefully, meant to be noticed across a room. He sat on the stool across from the bars and folded his hands, and for a while he said nothing at all, which Tomas recognized as a technique and declined to be impressed by.

"You are dying," Voss said at last. "The wound in your shoulder has gone bad. The surgeon tells me three days, perhaps five."

"I've heard similar forecasts before," Tomas said. "From better doctors."

"You came here carrying a letter. You were followed—or you led them; my men disagree—which means everything you touched is poisoned, and yet the poison keeps missing you. A courier arrested with papers on him walks free because the papers were forged so well that arresting him looks foolish. A print shop sealed by my own order produces, yesterday, a manifest proving its owner shipped nothing but Bibles all spring." Voss's voice stayed level, courteous, terrible. "I begin to suspect, Mr.—or shall we say the name on your false passport?—that I am not hunting a network. I am hunting a printer's devil with ten hands."

Tomas leaned back against the cold stone and let himself smile. His shoulder burned; the fever had been climbing since morning, and he knew the surgeon had not lied. But fever clarified things. It burned away everything except what mattered.

"Magistrate," he said, "do you know what happens to a lie when you set it in lead?"

Voss's eyes narrowed. "Enlighten me."

"It gets heavy. It gets permanent. Anyone can pick it up, carry it, put it down again somewhere else. You can burn paper, but lead—you'd have to melt the whole city down to unmake it." He held the man's gaze. "You've spent years making people afraid to speak. But you never once made them stop *reading*. That was your mistake. That was everyone's mistake, in every empire since the first one."

Voss rose, straightened his coat. "There is no empire falling in this city, whoever you are. There is order, and there is disorder, and I am order."

"There's ice on the river," Tomas said. "And ice always breaks."

He heard the magistrate's boots climb the stairs, unhurried, each step deliberate—and beneath the stone, faint as a heartbeat, he began to hear something else. A rhythm from the direction of the river road. A rhythmic knocking, regular as a pulse.

Somewhere in the dark of the city, a press was being reassembled.

* * *

*Rosalind*

The soldiers' seal on the shop doors was wax and ribbon, and wax and ribbon yield to a hot knife wielded by someone who has spent her life around presses. The real problem was the catalogue. Every chase, every galley, every drawer of type had been listed, numbered, and stacked in the front room under guard—except that the guard was one bored corporal, and the stacking had been done by soldiers who believed type was type, and Rosalind had spent three days in a cell composing in her head exactly what she would set if she ever got her hands on lead again.

She got her hands on lead again at eleven o'clock at night, by lantern light, with thirty conspirators breathing softly around her.

The press itself they could not move—it was bolted where Alois had bolted it in the year of her birth—but the seal was broken and remade with Karel's help and a candle stub, and the front-room stacks were carried through the cellar hatch by a chain of hands, and by midnight the Hartwell press stood assembled in the printing hall as though it had never been disturbed, and Rosalind stood before it with a composing stick in her fist and felt her heart hammering like a boy's.

The broadsheet had been written over those three days in her cell—not on paper, nowhere dangerous, only in memory, in the place where she kept her brother's ligatures and her mother's lullabies. Now Petra, pale and steady-handed, read it aloud by lantern while Rosalind set it line by line, and the others checked each forme against the reading.

*To the Citizens of this City.* The words marched under her fingers, cold and obedient. *For six years, Magistrate Voss has imprisoned without trial the following persons...* Names. Forty-one names, gathered from Karel's people and Petra's knowledge of the files, each one verified twice, because a broadsheet with one wrong name would discredit every true one. *...has taken bribes recorded in his own hand... has used coerced children as informers against their own families... has ordered the seizure of goods from families whose only crime was poverty.*

And then the paragraph that had cost her the most, the one she had rewritten a dozen times in the dark:

*We do not ask you to burn anything. We do not ask you to break anything. We ask you to read this to your neighbor. An empire that fears the alphabet has already confessed.*

Petra's voice caught on that last sentence. Rosalind did not look up from the stick. If she looked up she would see the girl's face, sixteen years old, the face of someone coerced by threats to her family into betraying the woman who taught her to read proof sheets, and forgiveness was a debt Rosalind had chosen to pay forward rather than discuss.

"Hold," Petra said. "Last line of the fourth column. You've inverted the 'n.'"

Rosalind checked. She had. She picked it out with her tweezers and reset it, and thought: *my father would be proud, and I will never tell him, and he would understand that too.*

At half past midnight the first forme was locked into the chase. At quarter to one, Karel pulled the first impression, and thirty people crowded around the damp sheet while the ink gleamed, black and wet and alive.

It was not beautiful. The paper was cheap, salvaged from the cellar stores; the margins wandered; the ink was stretched with turpentine because they had only half a tin. It was the ugliest thing the Hartwell press had ever produced, and Rosalind looked at it and felt something in her chest unlock that had been locked since the day the frontier letter came announcing her brother's death—two years of standing invisible, keeping her head down, priding herself on being unremarkable. All of it spent. All of it traded for this crooked, glorious page.

"Run it," she said.

The press ran at midnight, and it did not stop.

* * *

*Tomas*

He heard it properly after the guards changed, in the dead hour when the prison was quietest. Not imagination this time, not fever. A rhythm through the foundations, muffled by streets and walls but unmistakable to anyone who had lived above a press: the pull, the bed swinging, the platen's kiss. Pull-swing-kiss. Pull-swing-kiss.

The other prisoners heard it too. In the cell across, old Ferenc—who had been jailed for shouting a curse at a tax collector—began, very softly, to tap the rhythm against the wall. Another cell answered. Within the hour the whole block was keeping time, a heartbeat passed hand to hand through stone, and the night guard pounded on the doors and shouted threats and could not stop it, because you cannot arrest a rhythm.

Tomas lay on his cot with his shoulder on fire and laughed until the laughing hurt, and then he lay still and listened, and let himself believe.

* * *

*Rosalind*

By four in the morning they had printed four thousand. By dawn, eight. The problem was never the press—it was distribution, and distribution was solved the way everything in the network was solved: by small hands, many of them, each knowing only its own errand.

Bakers carried bundles in their flour baskets and left broadsheets under loaves. Milk carts delivered them rolled inside the cream cans. The lamplighter—Karel's cousin, naturally—slipped them through letter slots on his rounds. Schoolchildren who could not have read half the words carried them folded inside schoolbooks to houses their parents had never entered. By six o'clock, when the city's chimneys began to smoke, there was hardly a kitchen in the working quarters without a copy propped against the salt box, and hardly a servant, a stevedore, a seamstress who had not read it aloud before the masters came down to breakfast.

Rosalind worked the press until her arms shook. Petra fed and stacked. Karel pulled. The formes were washed, reset, inked, run again, ten thousand impressions, and when the last sheet came off the bed the light through the loading door had gone from black to grey to the pale gold of a thawing morning, and outside, the river was groaning—the long, deep, splitting groan of ice giving up its winter grip, a sound like cannon fire slowed to the speed of geology.

They stood in the doorway together, ink-blackened to the elbows, listening.

"It's going," Petra said.

"Yes," Rosalind said.

"Not just the ice."

"No," Rosalind said. "Not just the ice."

Down the lane, a bell began to ring—not the church bell, the guildhall bell, rung out of season, rung wrong, rung the way bells are rung when the people ringing them have decided the day belongs to them. Then another bell answered from across the river. Then a whistle, a shout, the sound of many feet on many streets, converging.

Karel put a hand on her shoulder. "The magistrate's men will come here within the hour. Everyone knows whose press this is."

"Let them come," Rosalind said. "There's nothing here but hymnals and wedding invitations."

Karel grinned. "And ten thousand lies about Voss."

"No." She looked back at the press, still warm, smelling of ink and turpentine, the press her father had kept honest for forty years and dishonest for six, the press that had finally, at midnight, told the truth. "Ten thousand proofs. Every one of them set by hand."

She went back inside, took up her tweezers one more time, and set the final line herself—fourteen picas, centered, the last thing the forme would carry before it went to the archives of everyone's memory:

*Set and printed by R. Hartwell, who signs her work.*

Then she locked the chase, and waited for the knock at the door, and found she was not afraid.
