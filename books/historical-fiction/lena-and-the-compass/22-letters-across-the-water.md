## 22. Letters Across the Water

The first frost came on a Thursday, silvering the loading door's iron hinges the way breath silvers glass, and Rosalind stood in the yard with her hands bare and let it sting her. She had always hated this season—the shortening of the days, the smell of coal smoke settling into everything like a tenant who would not pay—but this year autumn arrived as a kind of mercy. The river was cooling toward its winter hardness. In a few months it would freeze solid again, and frozen things could be crossed.

She had learned that much from the river: that every barrier is also, eventually, a road.

Inside, the shop smelled of ink and cold metal and the particular sweetness of paper stored through a damp summer. She had not changed much. Alois's cutting bench still sat by the north window where the light came cleanest, his gravers laid out in their worn velvet grooves like surgical instruments awaiting a hand. His chair—oak, scarred, one leg shimmed with a folded pamphlet from some forgotten scandal of 1839—stood empty behind it. She could not bring herself to move either. Some mornings she half expected to hear the small bright *tick-tick-tick* of steel against lead, the sound that had been the heartbeat of her childhood, and she would look up from her composing stone with her heart already halfway to hope before remembering that hope, too, had emigrated.

Three months since the ice broke. Three months since the crossing at dawn, since the crack of the river like artillery and the black boat sliding south into the fog with her father sitting straight-backed among strangers, holding his hat in his lap like a schoolboy called before the examiners. Three months since Voss's men kicked open the shop door and found nothing but type and order and a daughter alone—and then, minutes later, found Petra's confession hidden in the loft, and understood they had been played by a girl of seventeen with flour still on her sleeves.

Petra had wept for an hour in the cellar after that, and Rosalind had held her, and neither of them had said anything that needed saying. The girl stayed on. Her hands were quick, her memory for delivery routes flawless, and when customers asked about the new arrangement—a woman running Hartwell & Son, which was now a lie in both halves of its name—Petra answered with such bland confidence that the questions died of embarrassment.

The bell over the front door rang. Morning trade: the apothecary wanting five hundred labels, the widow Brandt collecting the almanac she paid for in eggs. Ordinary commerce, honest work under whatever laws governed them now—and the laws were shifting, Rosalind thought, like sediment in a flooded channel. Voss was gone. Not arrested, not yet; men like Voss did not fall so much as erode, their patronage drying up as the empire's attention turned elsewhere, to larger fires. But the broadsheets had named him, ten thousand copies on ten thousand doors, and a man whose name is known cannot rule by shadow. He had requested a transfer. He had been granted one, to somewhere flat and far, and the city had breathed out all at once, a long exhalation that Rosalind felt in her ribs even now.

She wrapped the almanac in brown paper, tied it with string, made change, smiled the small professional smile. Invisible, she had once prided herself on being. Unremarkable. A woman who cut typefaces and kept her head down.

That woman was dead, and Rosalind had buried her without mourning, the way you bury something that served you well but could not serve you longer.

* * *

The first letter came in October, carried by a grain merchant's boy who did not know what he carried.

It was addressed in a hand she knew better than her own face: Emil's. Every ligature intact, the crossbar of the *t* slanting just so, the descenders looping with that impatience he'd never outgrown. She taught him half of those letters herself, standing behind him at this very bench when he was seven and furious at the alphabet for resisting him. To see that hand on an envelope postmarked from the south—to hold paper that her brother's living fingers had touched—made her knees go strange. She sat down on Petra's stool and read it twice before she trusted her eyes.

*Rosa.*

*You will forgive me for writing instead of coming. There are reasons, most of them bad ones, and I will tell you all of them when I can, which may be spring or may be never—I have stopped making promises to time. What matters is this: Father reached us. He crossed on the last night the ice held, and I was on the far bank to meet him, and I watched him step off that boat into the fog looking exactly like himself, hat in hand, spine straight, a man arriving punctually at his own ruin.*

*I did not recognize him at first. That is a terrible thing to write and a worse thing to confess: I looked at my own father and saw only a respectable stranger, because that is the costume he wore for me my whole life. Then he said—"Your mother would say you've gotten thin," and I understood that the Cutter had come home.*

*He is well. He works. He has taken up cutting matrices again in a shop near here run by people who owe the network more than they can repay, and he says the light is good, though he says it in the tone of a man comparing it unfavorably to a window he will not see again. He sends instructions for the press. Of course he does. He cannot help himself. He wants you to re-cut the eight-point italic because "the old matrix is wearing soft on the terminals"—his words, Rosa, verbatim, as if he had merely stepped out for tobacco and might return any hour to check your work.*

*He is proud of you. He says so in the way he says everything, which is sideways, at night, to the fire. I am telling you directly because someone should. You kept the shop alive. You kept the network alive. You gave up the berth—you gave up Emil, your own brother, whom you mourned two years for—and you chose him, and he knows what that cost, and if he cannot say it plainly then I will say it for him until he learns.*

*Come south when you can. Or don't, if you can't. Either way, know that we are both alive because of you, and that the compass you followed was truer than mine ever was.*

*Your brother,*
*Emil*

P.S. *He also says the kerning on the broadsheet headline was sloppy. I told him you were busy starting a revolution. He said, "There is never time to kern properly except when it matters most." I believe this was a compliment. With Father one learns to translate.*

Rosalind laughed, and then she was crying, and Petra appeared in the doorway with the broom raised like a weapon, ready to defend the shop against whatever had happened, and Rosalind waved her off and read the letter a third time, aloud, to the empty room, to the empty chair, to the ghost of the smell of her father's tobacco that sometimes still haunted the cutting bench on damp days.

*There is never time to kern properly except when it matters most.*

She would have that line cut into brass someday, she decided, and mounted above the door.

* * *

Emil's letter was followed, a week later, by one in a different hand—careful, formal, each word chosen like a typeface selected from a case, weighed for its fitness before use.

*My dear Rosa,*

*Alois writes to you separately, or means to; he has begun four drafts and burned three, which I understand was always his method. I will content myself with reporting that he eats well, sleeps poorly, and argues with the local printers about ink viscosity with a passion I have seen him display for no other living thing. He misses you. He will not say so. I have therefore said so.*

*The work here continues. The route holds. There are fewer crossings now—the authorities in the southern provinces grow cautious—but those who need to move still move, and the money that funded them, as you now know, moved with them. Your father asks me to tell you that the ledger's final page is accounted for and that the account is closed, and I know, from the way he said it, that he means it as a gift and not a boast.*

*One more thing, and then I will spare you my handwriting, which you were always kinder about than it deserved. He keeps the second cellar key on a cord around his neck. He thinks no one notices. I notice. I think perhaps he wears it the way other exiles wear their wedding rings—not because the lock still exists, but because turning that key was the truest act of his life, and a man should carry proof of his truest act close to the skin.*

*Be well, sister. Be loud.*

*E.*

Rosalind folded both letters together and put them in the drawer of the cutting bench, in the shallow compartment where Alois had kept his graving cloths, and she did not cry this time. Something had settled instead, heavy and warm, like lead cooling in a mold. It had taken the shape of her father exactly.

* * *

Tomas's letter came last, and it came not from the south at all but from Havelberg, forty miles downriver, postmark dated eleven days prior.

Forty miles. Two days' walk. Less, if a man had reason.

She read it standing in the doorway where he had collapsed fourteen months ago, bleeding, clutching a satchel, begging for nothing but ink and paper. The loading door swung gently in the autumn wind behind her, creaking on hinges she had oiled herself, and she let it creak. Some sounds deserved preserving.

*Rosalind,*

*Forgive the delay in writing. I have been learning to be a farmer, badly, and a bookkeeper, worse, for people here who print hymnals and agricultural reports and, between the lines, other documents whose nature you can imagine. My hands are stained a new color. I find I miss the old one.*

*Voss is gone. I read it in the papers here and laughed out loud in a public house, which drew looks. They transferred him east. I hope the east suits him. I hope it is very flat and very far and full of paperwork.*

*The others are scattered but safe. The two couriers taken on Binder's Lane were released in the general amnesty—did you know? I checked twice, then three times, because I could not believe it, and then I sat down on a milestone outside town and cried like a child. They are alive, Rosalind. Both of them. Petra's confession bought that, and your stubbornness bought Petra, and I have been trying to draw the map of debts that leads back to you and it has no edges.*

*I should tell you what I have wanted to tell you since the cellar, since the composing stick, since the nights we set type with our hands almost touching and pretended the pretense was necessary. I love you. I loved you when loving you was the most dangerous thing in the city, and I love you now that it is merely the most frightening. I have no right to ask anything. I surrendered myself to Voss's men to split your blame and it bought you three weeks of freedom that mattered, and I would do it again, and I would do more than that, and none of it earns me the right to ask.*

*And so I am not asking. I am telling you that I am forty miles away, that I am staying there through the winter unless given reason otherwise, that the road between Havelberg and your door is a good road, dry in frost, passable in nearly all weather. I am telling you that I will wait as long as waiting serves you, and that if the answer is no, I will keep waiting anyway, quietly, the way one waits for a river to decide its course—not because rivers need our patience, but because patience is all we have that rivers want.*

*Set something in type for me sometime. Anything. I will know your hand in it.*

*Tomas*

She stood in the doorway a long while. The wind moved the poplars along the embankment, and the river ran gray-green and restless below, carrying leaves south toward the sea, carrying, as it always had, everything the city let go of and everything it longed for.

Forty miles.

She went inside and took up her apron.

* * *

Winter came early and hard, which was good for business—everyone wanted reading material—and by Advent the shop was running six days a week. Rosalind re-cut the eight-point italic herself, working evenings by lamplight, and found to her surprise that the graver fit her hand as if it had been waiting for her all along, that her father's craft lived in her wrists the way his treason had lived in his ledger: patiently, invisibly, waiting for its season. When the new matrix was finished she pulled a proof and mailed it south without comment. The reply, weeks later, contained one sentence in Alois's cramped hand:

*The terminals are true. Your mother would have said so.*

It was the longest thing he had written her directly. She framed it and hung it beside the press.

In February, the city council—new faces, nervous ones, men who had read the broadsheets on their own doors and drawn conclusions—granted Hartwell & Daughter its license without a single question. Petra painted the new sign herself, in gold leaf, standing on a ladder in the snow, laughing whenever she slipped. Customers came. Orders came. And once a month, in plain wrappers postmarked from towns along the southern route, manuscripts came: testimony, memoir, the accounts of people who had crossed and wanted their crossing counted. The network had become a publisher. Or the publisher had become the network. Rosalind had stopped distinguishing.

On the first of March, with the ice still holding but groaning at its edges—that deep, seismic complaint she had learned to hear as a language—she closed the shop early, put on her good coat, and wrote a note for Petra:

*Gone downriver. Back when I'm back. Mind the press.*

The road to Havelberg was, as promised, a good road, dry in frost. She walked it in two days, sleeping in barns, eating bread she'd baked herself, and on the afternoon of the second day she crested a low hill and saw the town below, chimney smoke rising straight in the still air, and beyond it the river, and on the river's bank a figure standing very still, facing north, as if he had calculated to the minute when she might appear and had not wanted to risk missing it.

He did not wave. He simply stood there while she came down the hill, and when she reached him she saw that his hands were stained with ink—a dark, familiar stain, the stain of someone who had been setting type in secret, keeping the work alive in exile, keeping faith the only way a printer knows how.

"You've been practicing," she said.

"Every day." His voice was rough. "I wanted my hand to be worth yours."

She looked at him—at the man who had bled on her loading door, who had taught her that survival could be loud, who had waited for a river to decide its course—and she took his ink-stained hand in hers, and the wind came up off the water carrying the first faint groan of the breaking ice, the sound of a barrier becoming a road.

"Come home," she said. "We're shorthanded."

They walked north together along the frozen river as the sun went down, and behind them the ice sang its long cracking song—the same song, she thought, that had carried her father away and her brother back to life and ten thousand truths to ten thousand doors. People had asked her once, in the bad weeks, what guided her through it all: the law, or love, or fear. She had never found the answer until now, walking home in the blue dusk with a warm hand in hers. Not the river. Not the compass points on any chart. Just this: the people worth steering for, and the willingness, when it mattered most, to make landfall.

The shop lights were burning when they reached the city. Petra had left them on.

Rosalind unlocked the door with the second cellar key, which she now wore on a cord around her neck, close to the skin, and stepped inside, and began to set the morning's type.
