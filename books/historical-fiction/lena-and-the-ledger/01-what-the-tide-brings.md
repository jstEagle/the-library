## 1. What the Tide Brings

The storm had spent itself in the night, and by first light the sea lay out flat and bruised-colored, like a thing that had been beaten and was pretending otherwise. Petra Halloran climbed the ninety-three steps of Carrickmoor Light with the oil can balanced on her hip and her boots ringing against the iron treads, counting the steps without meaning to, because she always counted them, because Tomas had counted them, and because some habits are the only inheritance that doesn't weigh anything.

Ninety-two, ninety-three.

The lantern room smelled of brass polish and burned oil, a smell so familiar it had stopped being a smell years ago and become simply the air she breathed. The great lens stood around her like a beehive made of glass, its prisms stacked in rings, each one ground and polished to take a small flame and throw it eleven miles out to sea. She checked the wick first — always the wick first, Tomas's rule, *the flame forgives nothing, Petra* — trimming the charred edge with the little scissors kept on a chain so they could never wander off in a gale. Then the oil reservoir, then the clockwork that turned the whole bright assembly on its bath of mercury, slow as a planet, one revolution every forty seconds.

One flash every forty seconds. Carrickmoor's signature. Every keeper on this coast knew it the way you knew your mother's knock.

She wound the clockwork until the spring resisted her hand, then wrote the night's figures in the logbook: wind southwest, force eight at midnight, moderating by dawn; visibility poor; lamp burned true; no shipping sighted. Her handwriting came out smaller than she intended. It always did in cold rooms, as if even her letters were huddling for warmth.

Outside the gallery rail, the world was wrecked and beautiful. The seawall wore a beard of torn kelp. A fishing punt sat stove-in on the shingle below the point, wedged between two boulders like a bird that had flown into glass. Somewhere down the coast a section of cliff had gone into the sea; she could see the raw scar of it from here, yellow earth against grey water.

Petra looked at all of it the way a doctor looks at a patient — cataloguing damage, estimating recovery, feeling nothing she couldn't put to use.

Then she went down to make breakfast for her daughter, and the loneliness arrived on schedule, the way the tide did, asking no permission.

* * *

The kitchen was warm because Nell had gotten up first and fed the range, which meant the girl was already at the table with her slate and a piece of chalk worn down to a stub, doing sums she wasn't supposed to find interesting. Twelve years old and she did sums the way other children whistled — absent-mindedly, constantly, to fill the silence.

"You missed the worst of it," Nell said, not looking up. "Half the chimney pot came down around four. It sounded like the house was being eaten."

"I heard it." She set the kettle on. "Did you log it?"

"In my book. Force nine, I'd say, at the peak. Mrs. Doran says it was the worst since the big one in '09."

"Mrs. Doran says that about every storm."

"She does," Nell agreed seriously. "I've started keeping a separate column."

Of course she had. Petra hid her smile in the tea caddy. The girl kept her own weather ledger now, columns ruled with a ruler, entries dated and initialed, an exact small copy of her father's habits in a body that was becoming her mother's stubbornness. Children were strange vessels. You poured one life into them and they decanted it however they liked.

Breakfast was barley porridge with a spoonful of treacle split between two bowls, because the sugar had run out three weeks ago and the supply boat from Dún Laoghaire hadn't come in six. Six weeks now. The boat that was supposed to come today — the fortnightly run, flour and paraffin and salt and the mail — would not come either, if the state of the harbor road was any judge. The war had thinned everything: the boats, the men to crew them, the coal, the patience of everyone waiting on both ends of the water.

"We're down to the last tin of the condensed milk," Nell said, watching her mother's face the way she watched the barometer.

"I know."

"And the lamp oil will last maybe five weeks at current burn."

"I know that too."

"You could burn less."

"The light doesn't burn less, Nell. That's rather the entire point of it."

Nell absorbed this, chalk moving on slate. Petra drank her tea and looked out the kitchen window at the garden she'd have to survey for storm damage — the bean poles snapped, probably, the kale flattened — and felt the day arrange itself around her the way days did: tasks first, feelings later, if there was time, which there never was.

Grief, she had learned, was not a wave. Everyone said it was a wave, but waves pass. Grief was more like the lighthouse itself: something you lived inside, climbed daily, kept in order, and whose whole purpose was to warn others away from the rocks you yourself had broken on.

But there was bread to bake and nets to mend and a ledger to open, and none of those things required her to feel anything, which was precisely why she loved them.

* * *

At half past eight she carried the ledger into the front room — the good room, kept for business — and set it on the table beside the inkwell and the reading glasses Tomas had left behind, too strong for her eyes but she used them anyway because looking through his lenses felt like borrowing his attention for a while.

The ledger was a foolscap volume bound in green cloth gone soft as felt at the corners, six years of entries in Tomas Halloran's upright, unhurried hand. Most keepers logged ships and weather. Tomas had kept a second book, and the second book was the reason that when people in Carrickmoor said *the Hallorans*, they lowered their voices slightly, the way you did in church.

Not debts of money, mostly. Debts of everything else.

Petra opened to the marked page and read aloud, as she did every morning, working through the accounts the priest worked through his breviary — not because God required it, but because order required it, and someone had to stand in for order in a village where the war had taken eleven men and the sea took two more last spring.

"'Colm Byrne,'" she read. "'Two nights' labor on the north wall, October last, settled in full by mending of the kitchen roof, which he did badly and knows he did badly, and which I have let him believe is acceptable.'" She turned the page. "'Mrs. O'Shea. One sack of flour, advanced against winter, terms unstated, terms to remain unstated so long as she needs them to.' 'The Doran girl. Care of the light during my illness in March — three nights — owed and acknowledged, payable in kind whenever asked, which it has not yet been, and may it never need to be.'"

The words filled the room the way heat filled the stove. This was what the ledger actually was, though Tomas had never said so and Petra had only understood it slowly, the way you understand a tide — not a record of who owed whom, but a map of how the village held itself together above the waterline. Every entry was two things at once: an accounting and an act of faith that the accounting would someday matter.

And it mattered. She had proof of it. When young Michael Doran had been caught taking paraffin from the harbor store — desperate, foolish, his mother sick and the winter hard — it was the ledger that had saved him, or rather Tomas's quiet word with the harbormaster, backed by the fact that the Dorans' account showed three winters of labor given freely to the lighthouse. *The books balance,* Tomas had told the man. *Mind you don't tip them.* Michael had wept on the doorstep and Petra had pretended not to see, and the entry in the ledger read only: *Paraffin, one gallon, returned with interest.*

Interest being, in that case, a promise extracted and kept ever since.

"Nell," she called. "Bring me the mended netting from the porch. The Byrnes will come for it before the tide turns."

Nell appeared in the doorway, slate under her arm. "Why do we keep score?"

It was an old question, asked often, in different costumes. Petra had answers of varying honesty. Today she gave the short one.

"Because forgetting is easy," she said, "and remembering is a kind of love, and love that isn't written down gets lost when the people who felt it die." She tapped the green cover. "Your father understood that."

Nell considered this with her father's frown. "Or," she said, "because if you write everything down, nobody can say it happened differently."

Petra paused, ink pen hovering. There were days — this was becoming one of them — when her daughter said things that landed in her chest like stones dropped down a well, falling a long time before the sound came back.

"That too," Petra said finally. "Go on with your sums."

* * *

Midmorning brought Mrs. O'Shea up the cliff path, headscarved against the lingering wind, carrying nothing, which from Mrs. O'Shea was itself a message. She was a narrow woman with a widow's stoop and hands that had done fifty years of work and resented none of it openly. She came into the good room, accepted tea she didn't want, and looked at the ledger on the table the way people look at an altar.

"I saw the punt on the shingle," Petra said. "Whose?"

"Fitzgerald's boy. He'll be sick about it. Insurance won't touch it, not with the war on." Mrs. O'Shea wrapped her hands around the cup without drinking. "And the road to the harbor's gone at the bend. Took the wall with it. Colm Byrne's organizing men to shift stone Saturday, if the weather holds."

"It'll hold. High pressure building from the northwest — I read the glass this morning."

"You always know what the sky intends." A thin almost-smile. "Tomas used to say you were the better instrument."

"Tomas flattered instruments."

They sat a moment in the particular silence of women who had both buried husbands and did not discuss it, because discussing it would open a door neither could afford to hold shut.

"The boat didn't come," Mrs. O'Shea said at last.

"No."

"They're saying in Dún Laoghaire the runs are stopping altogether. U-boats, shortages, crews gone to the Navy." She turned the cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, precise as an entry in a book. "Flour's near out at mine. I don't say it to press you, Petra. I say it because you'd want it said plainly, the way you'd write it."

"I have two sacks in the store," Petra said. "One's yours. We'll manage till the next boat, and if there is no next boat, we'll manage anyway. The village managed before the boats ran regular, and it'll manage after."

Mrs. O'Shea nodded, and her eyes went once more to the ledger — quickly, the glance of a woman checking a lock she already knew was fastened — and then away. Something in the look snagged on Petra's attention like a fishhook in a sleeve, but before she could examine it, Mrs. O'Shea was rising, thanking her for tea she hadn't drunk, saying she'd send the Fitzgerald boy up to settle for his punt, or at least to face it, which was sometimes the same thing in this village.

At the door she stopped.

"There's post delayed with the storms," she said, not turning fully around. "A fortnight of it, piled up at the Dún Laoghaire office, they say. Could be anything in it. Christmas letters in November. Bills. News." A pause, one beat too long. "You'd think, after all this waiting, the post would bring nothing but good."

"Post brings what it brings," Petra said. "That's why we have the ledger — to keep track of what's true regardless."

Mrs. O'Shea looked at her then, straight on, and for a moment her face held something that was not guilt exactly but was standing very close to guilt, sharing its coat against the cold.

"Aye," she said. "Well. Mind how you go, Petra."

She went, down the path, small against the enormous grey morning.

* * *

The afternoon went to work, which was a mercy. Petra patched the bean rows, salvaged what the kale could be salvaged from, and spent two hours in the workshop below the tower — her favorite room in the world, though she'd have denied it under oath — where the spare Fresnel panels hung in their padded crates and the grinding wheel waited under its leather cloth. The Admiralty owned the workshop now on paper, had claimed it for the war effort eighteen months ago, though the war had never quite found a use for it, and so it remained hers in practice, smelling of rouge and cerium oxide and clean glass, the one room where her hands knew exactly what they were for.

She polished the scratched prism from the upper ring, slow circles, watching the scratch diminish like a doubt. Outside, the light fell long and gold across the water, and somewhere beyond the horizon men were dying on fields whose names she could pronounce but not picture, and here the glass came bright under her thumb, and both things were true, and only one of them was hers to tend.

By dusk the wind had dropped to nothing. The sea lay like poured metal. From the gallery she watched the fishing boats — what few were left — come home ahead of dark, and counted them out of habit. Five. There should have been seven. Two had put in at Kinsale instead, she'd learn later, riding out a fouled bottom. But for ten minutes, counting five where seven belonged, she stood with her heart going oddly fast, and understood again that this tower was not only her duty. It was her cell. She had married a keeper, inherited the light, and somewhere in six years of faithful burning had forgotten to ask whether the door still opened outward.

Nell called her down to supper. They ate potatoes and the last of the salted fish, and Nell recited her weather figures, and Petra corrected her estimate of the midnight wind by two points, and it was, on the surface, an ordinary evening in the nineteenth year of a century that had promised so much and delivered so much worse.

Afterward, while Nell did her sums by lamplight, Petra heard the dog from the farm two fields over begin to bark — not the ordinary bark, but the sustained, aggrieved complaint of an animal announcing something specific. Then boots on the gravel. Then a knock at the door, firm, official, at an hour when officials did not call.

Petra opened the door on a boy of perhaps fifteen, soaked to the knees, holding out a single envelope with both hands as though it might go off. Behind him, far down the hill, she could see the harbor master's lantern bobbing, and beyond it the black water, and beyond that the dark where the supply boat should have been.

"Came up on the mail cart, ma'am, what there was of the mail," the boy said. "Harbor master said yours was wanting delivering tonight, special-like. It's got a seal on it."

It did. Red wax, stamped with an anchor and crossed keys — the customs seal. The paper was heavy, expensive, absurd against everything else in her life, addressed in a clerk's copperplate hand:

**To The Halloran Family, Carrickmoor Light.**

*Nobody writes to the family*, Petra thought. *People write to the keeper. Or the widow. Nobody writes to the family.*

"Thank you," she said, and her voice came out level, keeper-steady, and closed the door, and stood a moment with the envelope in her hand while behind her Nell looked up from her slate, chalk stopped mid-figure.

"Ma?" Nell said. "Who's it from?"

Petra turned the envelope over. The wax seal gleamed dully in the lamplight, unbroken, patient, certain of itself.

"I don't know yet," she said. "But it's from the government."
