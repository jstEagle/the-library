## 2. The Weight of Small Debts

The ledger lived on the shelf above the stove, between the Bible and a tin of spare wicks, and every morning after the light was doused Petra took it down before she did anything else. Before the kettle. Before waking Nell. She would sit at the kitchen table with her hands still smelling of lamp oil and open the book to the ribbon marking the day, and she would read the last few entries aloud to the empty room — not for any reason she could have defended, only because Tomas had read them aloud too, in his way, muttering over the figures while he ate his bread, and the silence where his voice should have been needed filling.

*Widow O'Shea — flour, 4 lb., carried up the hill in rain. To be repaid in kind or in mending.*

*Byrne boys — two nets patched, twine supplied. Repaid: herring, one barrel, salted.*

*Mrs. Doyle — tallow candles, six. Outstanding since Michaelmas.*

The entries were all in Tomas's hand, that careful clerk's script he'd learned in the customs house at Dún Laoghaire before he'd thrown it over for a lighthouse and her. He had written the ledger the way he'd trimmed wicks: precisely, without flourish, as if neatness were a form of honesty. *To be repaid in kind or in mending.* He'd had phrases like that. Phrases that made a debt sound like a courtesy.

Petra poured the kettle over the leaves and drank her tea standing at the window, watching Carrickmoor come down to its morning. Smoke rising from chimneys in ones and twos. The O'Shea cottage with its slate roof dark from yesterday's rain. The harbor wall where the Byrne boys would already be, because those boys had never in their lives seen a morning they didn't want to argue with.

It was Tuesday. On Tuesdays the village came to settle accounts.

She hadn't invented the custom; it had grown up around the ledger the way moss grows around a stone. Tomas had started it years ago — no, that wasn't honest. She corrected herself, rinsing her cup. She had started it, back when Tomas was alive and the debts were small and the keeping of them had been a game between husband and wife, something to do on long evenings. But it was Tomas who had written everything down, and Tomas who had insisted — gently, always gently — that nothing be forgotten. *A favor unpaid is a favor unvalued, Petra. Write it down and it stays worth having.*

She had thought it a kindness then. Standing at the window now, watching the widow's thin smoke rise, she wasn't sure what she thought it was.

Nell came down with her hair unbrushed and her boots on the wrong feet, twelve years old and perpetually surprised to be awake. "Is there tea?"

"There's tea."

"Is there bread?"

"There's bread if you brush your hair first."

"That's not a fair trade." But she went and brushed it, mostly, and came back, and ate standing up the way children eat, as though food might escape. Petra watched her over the rim of her cup and felt the old ache that was also a kind of fullness — this girl, this table, this room holding its heat against the November sea.

"Tuesday," Nell said, with the tone other children used for funerals.

"Tuesday."

"Can I take the count today? Mrs. Doyle always shortchanges you and you never say anything."

"I hear everything," Petra said. "I just don't always write it."

"Why not?"

Because Mrs. Doyle has six children and a husband lost at Jutland, and because a candle more or less is how I choose whom to forgive. She didn't say it. "Because some things balance themselves, given time."

Nell frowned at this the way she frowned at sums that wouldn't come out even. It was her father's frown, and Petra looked away from it toward the window, where the first caller was already coming up the path.

* * *

Mrs. O'Shea arrived at nine o'clock exactly, which meant she'd been waiting out of sight until nine o'clock exactly, because the widow was a woman who believed punctuality was the poor man's dignity. She was small and upright and wore black so uniformly that the village children said she'd been born in mourning. She carried a covered basket and set it on the table with both hands, formally, as if laying a wreath.

"Flour," she said. "Four pounds. And the eggs are from my own hens, so don't put them down against me as bought goods."

"I wouldn't dare."

"You'd dare fine enough. Your Tomas used to write down weather against me once. Fog, he wrote. As if fog were my doing."

"Tomas wrote down everything."

"Aye." Something crossed the widow's face, quick as a gull's shadow. "He did that."

She unpacked the basket while Petra fetched the ledger: flour wrapped in cloth, six brown eggs nested in ash, and beneath them, folded small, a pair of men's wool socks, darned at the heel. Petra's hands went still over the basket.

"Those are—"

"They're your husband's size or near it. I knitted them in October." Mrs. O'Shea smoothed the wrapping cloth with great attention. "I couldn't give them to him, could I. So they're yours now, and we'll call us square on the flour, and don't you go writing sock against flour like it's arithmetic, because it isn't arithmetic and we both know it."

Petra looked at the socks. They were good wool, dyed the gray of the sea before weather. Tomas had been dead eleven months. The widow had knitted through October anyway, the needles going in her cold front room, making a gift with nowhere to land.

"It's not arithmetic," Petra agreed, and wrote: *O'Shea — flour repaid, plus eggs. Account closed.*

The widow watched her write it and nodded once, satisfied, and stayed for tea she pretended not to want, and told Petra three pieces of village news of varying reliability, and left lighter than she'd come. At the door she paused.

"You'll keep the book going, will you? Now that it's yours?"

"That's the intention."

"Good." The widow said it fiercely, looking at the ledger rather than at Petra. "Good. Some things want remembering."

Then she was gone down the path, upright as a mast, and Petra stood holding the entry she'd just written and wondering why a closed account had sounded so much like an open wound.

* * *

The Byrne boys came at midday, which meant they came loudly, filling the kitchen the way weather fills a doorway. There were three of them — Des, the eldest, broad and serious as a bollard; then Martin; then young Patsy, who was fourteen and all elbows and opinions. They smelled of tar and herring and the particular cold of men who work wet.

"Nets," Des said, by way of greeting, hauling the bundle off his shoulder. "Two. And the twine you gave us in October, we've counted it twice and there's a fathom extra, and Patsy says we owe you for it and Martin says we don't, and I'm here to settle it before they kill each other."

"We counted wrong the first time," Martin said.

"We counted right the second time."

"The second time doesn't matter if the first time was right—"

"It was a fathom," Patsy announced, "of good hemp twine, and hemp is dearer than sin these days, and I'll thank this household to acknowledge—"

"Sit down," Petra said, "all three of you, and there's bread if anyone's eaten, which I doubt."

They sat. They ate. This was also part of Tuesdays; it had been part of Tuesdays since before the war, since their father had been alive and the Byrne table had held five instead of four. Petra spread the nets across the floor by the stove and worked the broken mesh while the boys argued about the fathom, and the argument, she understood, was not about the fathom at all. It was about having a place where counting mattered, where a fathom of twine was worth a court of inquiry. The world outside had stopped being countable. Boys from the next parish over had gone into France and stopped being countable too. But here a fathom was a fathom, and someone would stand behind the number.

"Your father kept accounts like this?" Des asked suddenly, from the table. "Mr. Halloran?"

"He kept everything."

"And now you do."

"Now I do."

Patsy leaned over the back of a chair to watch her fingers work the shuttle through the mesh. "My mam says your book knows more about Carrickmoor than the priest does."

"Your mam flatters the book."

"My mam doesn't flatter." Patsy said it with such flat certainty that the older boys laughed, and Petra laughed too, but something in the sentence lodged under her ribs and stayed there. *Your book knows more about Carrickmoor.* She thought of the pages she'd read aloud that morning. She thought of how many pages there were. Eleven years of them, in Tomas's hand, dense as netting, and how well did she actually know what was knotted into all that mesh?

She finished the nets by two o'clock. The boys loaded them, thanked her with the elaborate indifference of the young, and argued their way out the door and down the path, and she heard the argument continue all the way to the harbor wall, growing fainter, the fathom of hemp still unresolved, still worth fighting over, thank God, in a world where so much else had stopped being worth anything at all.

In the ledger she wrote: *Byrne — two nets repaired, twine settled. Repaid: herring, half-barrel, delivered Friday.* And under it, after a moment, in her own hand for the first time in an entry of substance rather than a mere correction: *Account in good order.*

Her pen hovered. It felt strange, writing under him, in the book that was his. Like speaking in a dead man's church.

* * *

The afternoon brought the smaller callers — Mrs. Doyle with her candles, paid at last with a skein of dyed wool and an apology so worn it was nearly lace; the ferryman Cavan, who settled a month's lamp oil with fish and stayed to complain about the customs men at Dún Laoghaire being "sharp as weasels lately, sniffing at every hull like the war's still on"; old Mr. Ferris, who owed nothing and paid nothing and came anyway, because Tuesdays were when he saw people, and his account in the ledger read simply: *Company. Ongoing.* Tomas had entered that himself, years ago, and Petra had never had the heart to close it.

By dusk the kitchen smelled of everyone who had passed through it — tar and wool and fish and woodsmoke — and the ledger lay open on the table, heavier somehow than it had been at dawn. Petra totaled the day's columns while Nell dried dishes, and Nell dried them slowly, which meant she was thinking, which meant trouble was coming the way weather comes off the Atlantic: visibly, and too late to prepare.

"Mam."

"Mm."

"Why do we keep score at all?"

Petra kept writing. "So nobody's forgotten. Either direction."

"But Mrs. Doyle owes you for candles from a year ago and you let her pay in wool that isn't worth half, and Mr. Ferris pays nothing ever, and you gave the Byrne boys a fathom of twine you know you didn't—" Nell set down the dish she was drying and turned, and her father's frown was on her face again, aimed at Petra this time. "You don't actually keep score. You keep something else and you call it score."

The kettle ticked on the stove. Out the window the light was failing over the water, and in less than an hour she'd have to climb the tower and trim the wick and begin the night's burning, the same as every night, the same as Tomas every night before her.

"What would you call it?" Petra asked.

Nell considered. She was her mother's daughter in this if nothing else — she took questions seriously, turned them over like stones.

"I'd call it a promise list," she said finally. "Things people promised each other. Except—" and here the frown deepened, and she looked at the ledger with an expression far too old for her, "—except Da wrote promises in it that aren't ours. Some of those pages, Mam. Some of them aren't about us at all."

Petra's pen stopped.

"What do you mean, not about us?"

"I mean the back ones. The dates and the little marks." Nell shrugged, suddenly twelve again, suddenly careless, reaching for another dish. "I look sometimes. I'm not stupid. Half of it I can't make out, but it's not flour and it's not nets."

The clock on the mantel ticked into the silence. Down in the harbor, faintly, a bell buoy clanked in the swell. Petra looked at the ledger lying open to Tuesday's clean entries — flour, eggs, nets, herring, company ongoing — and beyond it, on the shelf, at the whole dense weight of the book, eleven years thick, every page in her dead husband's careful hand.

"Finish the dishes," she said, and heard how carefully her own voice came out, level as a logged tide. "And Nell — leave the back pages alone."

"Why?"

"Because some accounts," Petra said, "aren't ours to settle."

Nell looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw in her mother's face made her turn back to the dishes without another word. But it was her father's face she made while she did it, and it was her father's persistence Petra could feel already settling into the girl like a hook into a fish, patient, barbed, waiting to be pulled.

That night, climbing the tower stairs with the oil can in her hand, Petra told herself the child had misread the figures. Told herself it was shorthand, private notation, a keeper's habit of cipher. Told herself all the way up, and lit the lamp, and stood watching the beam swing out over the black water — steady, faithful, illuminating everything but the ground she stood on.

Three days later, the envelope with the customs seal arrived, addressed to *The Halloran Family*, and she remembered Nell at the sink, and wished she had asked more.
