## 3. A Sealed Letter

The letter came on a Tuesday, which was the day the bread boat usually ran, and its arrival in place of the baker's boy told Petra something before she touched it.

She had been on the gallery rail at first light, watching the sea for the supply boat that hadn't come the week before and didn't come now either. The horizon lay flat as poured tin. Somewhere out past it, the war went on eating ships, and Carrickmoor's little coaster with its cargo of flour and lamp oil and sugar stayed home in whatever harbor had swallowed it. The village would feel it by Thursday. The O'Shea widow's flour debt would have to wait longer than anyone wanted.

So when she came down from the tower and saw the postman standing at her gate — not the baker's boy, not the fish girl, but the postman himself, boots muddy to the knee — she knew the day had already turned against her.

"Morning, Mrs. Halloran." He held out a single envelope. "Came up from Cork on yesterday's packet. Nothing else for you."

It was heavier than paper had any right to be. Cream-colored stock, stiff, the kind used for funeral notices and land disputes. Across the top left corner, pressed in red wax gone dull with travel, sat a seal: an anchor within a wreath, and beneath it letters too small to read without holding it close.

Customs. His Majesty's Customs and Excise.

"Thank you, Mr. Feeney."

He lingered a half-second too long, the way men did when they delivered bad news and wanted credit for not being the cause of it. Then he touched his cap and went, and Petra stood in the doorway of her own house with the envelope in both hands like something that might go off.

*To: The Halloran Family.*

Not *Mrs.* Halloran. Not *the Keeper of Carrickmoor Light.* *Family.* As though the name were a household rather than a woman and a child. As though Tomas were still in it.

She carried it inside and set it on the kitchen table and looked at it while she banked the stove and put the kettle on and did every small delaying thing her hands could find. Nell was still asleep upstairs. The clock over the dresser ticked with its familiar uneven tick, the one Tomas had promised for eleven years to fix. The fire settled. The kettle began its low complaint.

Petra sat down and broke the wax.

* * *

There were two sheets. The first was a printed form, dense with officialese, the kind of language designed to make a person feel small and slow. She read it twice before the shape of it came clear through the fog of its own sentences.

An inquiry had been opened into the movement of contraband goods through the harbor at Carrickmoor during the years of the war. Certain persons were named as persons of interest. Among these persons were named — and here the clerk's pen had written it in careful copperplate — *P. Halloran (widow), keeper of the light, and family of the late T. Halloran, deceased.*

*Deceased.* Even the state knew he was dead. Even the state wrote him down as gone. And yet there they all were together, Halloran and Halloran, joined again in ink, summoned by implication.

An officer would attend within the fortnight to examine records and take statements. All books, ledgers, accounts, and correspondence pertaining to trade conducted at or near the said harbor might be called upon and should be preserved intact pending inspection.

*Should be preserved intact.*

Petra set the page down. Her hands were steady — they were always steady; twenty years of trimming wicks and grinding lenses and hauling rope in weather that would have broken other women had made steadiness less a virtue than a fact of her body — but behind the steadiness something was moving, some cold current under ice.

The second sheet was shorter. It repeated the essentials in plain words, as though the drafter suspected the first sheet would fail to frighten properly. Contraband. Inquiry. Fortnight. Officer.

Contraband. In Carrickmoor. Where the biggest crime in living memory was the Byrne boys' habit of lifting mackerel out of each other's pots.

She got up and took down Tomas's ledger from the shelf above the dresser.

* * *

The book was four years old, the fourth of its line, bound in green cloth gone soft at the corners from handling. She had kept his system after he died because the system worked, and because keeping it was the closest thing to hearing his voice the world still offered. Debts in the left column. Favors in the right. Every transaction in Carrickmoor passed through those pages eventually: flour borrowed against the herring season, nets mended against a share of catch, oil advanced against winter, kindnesses logged so that no one could pretend they hadn't happened and no one could pretend they weren't owed.

Tomas had believed in the ledger the way other men believed in the sacraments. *A debt written down is a debt that can't grow teeth,* he used to say. He'd been the lightkeeper then, not she; the ledger had been his second occupation, the one he tended on long nights when the lamp burned itself and the sea gave nothing back. When the fever took him in March of '17, the book had passed to her along with the watch, and she had kept both without missing a night.

She turned to the last entries in his hand.

March 1917. He had been ill six weeks by then, and his writing showed it — letters leaning, ink pooled where his pen had rested too long between strokes. But the entries themselves were what stopped her.

They were short. That was the first wrong thing. Tomas had never written a short entry in his life; he'd once spent half a page recording that young Danny Byrne had returned a borrowed auger *with the handle re-whittled, which was more than asked and noted gladly.* Now the entries ran to a few words apiece. Dates. Names. Amounts. No commentary, no character, none of the running argument with the world that had made his pages such company.

And there were gaps.

Not blank lines — Tomas never wasted paper. Gaps in sequence. She counted them with her finger, the way she'd count flashes from a distant light to get its character. An entry dated the ninth of March, then one dated the twelfth, with nothing between and no note of why. Again in February. Again in January, twice. Three days here, five days there, whole transactions simply absent from a book whose entire purpose was that nothing be absent.

She had read this book a hundred times since his death. She had read it aloud to herself on the worst evenings, tracing his handwriting like a face. How had she never seen it?

Because grief reads differently, she supposed. Because she had been reading for his voice and not for his arithmetic. A wife is the worst possible auditor of a husband's accounts.

Petra laid the customs letter beside the open ledger and looked from one to the other. *Books, ledgers, accounts, and correspondence pertaining to trade.* The officer would come within the fortnight and ask to see this green cloth book, and Petra Halloran would hand it over or she wouldn't, and either way she needed to know what was in it before anyone else did.

She made tea she didn't drink and started at the beginning.

* * *

The morning went. Nell woke and came down and ate her porridge and asked twice what was in the letter on the table, and twice Petra said *nothing that concerns you yet,* which was true in a way she hated, and went back to the book.

By noon she had found the pattern.

The gaps clustered in winter. January through March, every year since 1915 — the years when the blockade squeezed hardest and the bread boat came irregularly and half the village had stretched a sack of flour across two months. In those months, the ledger recorded fewer transactions even as the village needed more credit, not less. It was backwards. It was as if the book itself grew secretive exactly when the village grew hungry.

And in the margins of those winter pages, small marks she'd taken for smudges. Short vertical strokes in groups. Beside dates. Beside initials that belonged to half the households on the coast road — O'S., B., M., C. — initials that also appeared in the honest columns, attached to flour and oil and mended gear, so that a casual reader would see only the ordinary debts and nothing else.

A casual reader. Or a widow.

Petra sat back. The stove had gone low. Outside, the light rain of morning had thickened toward real weather, and the windows had begun their winter rattle, and somewhere below the cliff the sea was arranging itself for the week's first blow. None of it reached her. She was looking at the marks beside the initial *O.S.* — eleven of them in February of 1916 alone — and doing sums she didn't want finished.

Eleven strokes. Eleven what? Nights? Barrels? Pounds?

*Contraband moving through Carrickmoor's harbor.*

The phrase from the letter sat in her mind wearing its official clothes, but underneath it now stood plainer words. Someone had been bringing things ashore that the war said couldn't come ashore. Tea, maybe. Tobacco. Flour off some neutral hull. The kind of trade that fed villages and hanged smugglers, depending on which side of the customs house door you stood when it was weighed.

And the marks were in her husband's ledger. In her husband's hand.

No. She stopped herself. In *a* hand. She was a keeper of records; she knew how records lied. She would not convict a dead man on marginal strokes. There were explanations. There had to be. Tomas had tallied tides for fishermen, perhaps. Kept count of lamp hours. Counted anything, counted everything — a man like that made marks the way other men whistled.

But the gaps. The gaps had no innocent explanation she could reach. A man who counted everything did not stop counting in the exact months when counting mattered most — unless what he was counting had become something he didn't want written down. Unless the ledger she'd been keeping faithfully, faithfully, faithfully, was only half the book it appeared to be, the visible half of something with a hidden face.

The clock ticked its uneven tick. Upstairs, Nell was practicing her letters aloud, sounding out words from the old newspaper she hoarded. The sound came faintly through the floorboards, patient and childish, and Petra found she could not look at it just now. Not at the stairs. Not at the ceiling of the room where her daughter was teaching herself to read in a house that might soon be read by strangers.

*Preserved intact pending inspection.*

She smoothed the green cloth cover with her palm, the way she'd smooth a spooked animal.

* * *

In the afternoon the weather arrived properly, wind backing southwest and hardening, and Petra climbed the tower and lit the lamp early because the sky asked for it. Work steadied her the way it always had. Brass to polish, wick to trim, mercury bath to check, the great lens turning overhead with its slow patient glitter, throwing its white flower out over water going black. For an hour the letter ceased to exist. There was only the light and the keeping of it, which was the oldest contract she knew and the only one she'd never doubted.

Then she came down, and it was waiting on the table where she'd left it, cream-colored and official, the broken wax like a wound.

She reread the second sheet by lamplight. *An officer will attend.* Within the fortnight. Twelve days, then. Thirteen. Some man from Cork or Dublin would sit where she sat now and ask to see the book, and he would not read it the way she had read it for three years, looking for a dead man's voice. He would read it looking for a crime. And the question that had been circling her all day finally landed and stood still:

What would he find?

If the marks were nothing, he would find nothing, and the inquiry would fold, and Carrickmoor would go back to its debts and its storms. If the marks were something — if Tomas had done what the gaps suggested, if the Halloran name had been tied to the harbor's winter trade — then the book in her hands was not a record of the village's life. It was evidence. Evidence with her signature continuing it, entry after entry, three years of her own faithful hand stacked on top of whatever his had hidden.

She thought of the O'Shea widow's flour account, eleven strokes deep in February. She thought of the Byrnes, the Murphys, the Careys. Whole families, whole winters, balanced in those columns. Whatever the hidden ledger had recorded, the honest one recorded the survival that depended on it.

Petra closed the book.

She did not put it back on the shelf. She wrapped it in oilcloth instead — the way one wraps anything meant to survive weather — and carried it to the bedroom and set it in the bottom of the cedar chest beneath Tomas's good coat, and sat on the chest lid a moment in the darkening room feeling the absurdity of hiding a book in her own house from a letter she hadn't answered.

Twelve days. An officer was coming, and the past was coming with him, and it was wearing her husband's handwriting.

Downstairs, Nell called up the stairwell: "Mam? There's someone at the gate — it's Mrs. O'Shea, and she's brought the flour money, only I don't know how much we're owed, do I?"

Petra stood. In the mirror of the dark window her own face looked back at her, calm, competent, the face the village trusted — and behind it, faint in the glass, the lamp turned and turned above the water.

"No," she said. "You don't. Let her in."
