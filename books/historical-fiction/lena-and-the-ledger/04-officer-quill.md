## 4. Officer Quill

He came on a Tuesday, on the mail boat that had finally remembered Carrickmoor existed.

Petra saw the boat first — a gray smudge against a gray sea, riding low with the week's supplies — and then she saw the man standing at the rail, the only passenger, holding his hat against the wind with both hands as though the sea might take it as a toll. He wore a customs officer's coat, but it fit him badly, too broad in the shoulders, and he had the look of a man who had been traveling for days and had stopped pretending he enjoyed it somewhere around the second day.

She met him at the landing with the ledger under her arm. She had not planned to bring it. Her hands had simply gone for it the way they went for the lamp key at dusk, and by the time she'd walked the cliff path down to the harbor she was carrying it against her ribs like a shield, or a hostage, or a child.

"You'd be Mrs. Halloran." He stepped off the gangplank before it was properly secured, which told her something about how long he'd been at sea. "Officer Quill. Customs and Excise." He offered his hand. It was cold and his grip was brief, almost apologetic. "I'll be honest with you, Mrs. Halloran — I'd rather be anywhere on this coast than here."

"Most people would," Petra said.

He almost smiled. He was younger than she'd expected, forty perhaps, with the reddened eyes of a man who slept badly in trains, and a mustache that had given up trying to be authoritative. Under his arm was a leather satchel so fat with papers it would not close. "Is there somewhere we can talk? Somewhere with a roof, ideally. I've been told the wind here has opinions."

"The house, then. It's the climb or the cold, I'm afraid. The harbor office lost its stove to the requisition."

"The requisition." He said the word the way other men said a curse. "Yes. I've heard about your workshop. I've heard about a great many things." He hefted his satchel. "Most of them on paper. Very little of them true."

* * *

She made tea because there was nothing else to make, and because her hands needed the work. The kettle took its time. Quill sat at her kitchen table with his hat on his knee and looked around the room with the weary thoroughness of a man cataloguing: the mended nets drying by the stove, Nell's slate propped against the wall with half a sum chalked on it, the shelf of jars, the clock that ran four minutes fast because Tomas had never fixed it and Petra had never wanted to.

"Your husband," he said. "Tomas Halloran. I'm sorry. I understand it was this summer."

"July. The fever."

"The fever." He wrote it in a small notebook, and she watched the nib move, and understood that everything she said this morning would end up in that notebook, weighed and filed, and that some of it might one day be read aloud in a room with a magistrate in it. She poured the tea.

"Now." He opened the satchel and began, without much enthusiasm, to excavate. Papers came out in strata — carbon copies, mimeographed lists, a map of the coast with pins in it. "I'll tell you how this works, Mrs. Halloran, because I find people cooperate better when they understand the machinery. There's an inquiry. It comes from Dublin, it concerns contraband moving through this harbor during the blockade years, and certain names have come up. Your family's name is among them." He said it flatly, without looking up, the way a doctor says *it will sting*. "I'm required to interview the named parties and examine their records. Records of debts, payments, shipments, that sort of thing."

"And if the records don't exist?"

He looked up at her then, and for a moment the tiredness in his face was replaced by something quicker, sharper — a man doing sums. "Then I write that they don't exist. And Dublin sends someone else, who is younger and more ambitious than I am, and who will dig up your harbor with a spoon if he has to." He set down his pen. "I have a wife in Cork, Mrs. Halloran, and a daughter who writes to me twice a week to tell me what her brother has broken. I would very much like to file a clean report and go home to them. That is my entire ambition in this matter. I want you to understand it, because it is the most honest thing I'll say all day."

Petra wrapped her hands around her cup. The tea was too hot to drink and she held it anyway, letting it scald her palms, because the pain gave her something to stand on.

"The ledger," she said. "Tomas kept a ledger. Debts and favors. Flour, oil, mended nets. It's how the village runs — half of Carrickmoor owes the other half, and the book keeps the peace."

"May I see it?"

She had known the question was coming since the letter came, since before the letter, maybe, since the first night she'd read his final entries and felt the shape of the gaps in them like missing teeth. She had rehearsed answers on the cliff path this morning. She had not known which one she would use until she opened her mouth.

"It's incomplete," she said.

The words came out level. That surprised her. Inside, something tilted — the floor of her, the whole floor of her, tipping a degree off true, the way a ship lists. *It's incomplete.* Three words. A lie so small it fit inside a teacup. She watched it leave her and become a thing in the world, sitting on the table between them next to the milk jug.

"Incomplete," Quill repeated.

"Some pages were lost. Water damage, in the storm last spring. The storeroom flooded." She had not planned that detail either; it had arrived whole, dressed and ready, like a guest she hadn't invited. "I keep the honest accounts. The flour and the oil. But Tomas had his own way of doing things, and some of it went with him."

Quill looked at her for a long moment. She made herself meet his eyes. It was the hardest thing she had done since the fever, since the night she'd sat by Tomas's bed and lied to *him* — *you'll be fine, love, you'll see the spring* — and that lie at least had been a mercy. This one had a smell to it. This one had a taste.

"I see," Quill said, and wrote: *Ledger incomplete. Water damage.* His nib scratched in the quiet kitchen. Nell's slate watched from the corner. Somewhere below them the sea worked at the seawall, patient as a clerk.

"Here is what I can offer you," he said at last. "I'm required to remain in the district fourteen days. I'll say twenty, because I always pad my estimates and Dublin always shaves them, so twenty buys us the truth. In that time I'll interview the named parties — there are nine, and I'll spare you the list, you could recite it yourself, I suspect. I'll examine what records exist. And then I file." He closed the notebook. "If what I find is a poor widow and a village of hungry people who kept books badly, that is what I'll write. If what I find is a smuggling ring, I'll write that too, and neither of us will enjoy the consequences. Do you follow me, Mrs. Halloran?"

"I follow you."

"Because I don't think you're a criminal." He said it simply, wearily, folding his papers back into the satchel that would not close. "I've met criminals. They're usually better dressed and much more frightened. You look like a woman who has kept a light burning every night for four months and hasn't slept properly since July. That's not a criminal's face. That's a keeper's face." He stood, and his knees cracked, and he winced as though even his own body were paperwork gone astray. "Twenty days. Then I file, and what's in the file is what happened, as far as I'm concerned. After that it stops being mine."

At the door he paused, hat in hand, and looked back at her with an expression she couldn't name at first — and then could. It was the look of a man asking, without asking, *are we going to make this easy for each other?* The look Tomas used to give her across the market when the O'Shea widow's pride was at stake and the arithmetic needed to be done quietly, kindly, off the books.

"Mrs. Halloran. The storm last spring. Was it a bad one?"

"The worst in years," Petra said. "It took the roof off the net shed."

"Then I'm sorry for your ledger," said Officer Quill, and went down the cliff path with his satchel banging against his hip, a tired man climbing toward his own report.

* * *

She stood at the window until he was a dot on the harbor road, then until he was nothing at all.

Then she sat down at the table where he had sat and put her face in her hands.

It wasn't the lie itself. She had told lies before — everyone had, the small social mortar of *I'm fine* and *I didn't notice* and *the bread was delicious*. But those lies dissolved on contact, served their purpose and vanished. This one had weight. This one had a filing number. Somewhere in a leather satchel, in a small notebook, in an officer's careful hand, the words *water damage* were now drying into fact, and she had put them there, and they would outlive her the way ink outlives everyone who uses it.

She thought: *this is how it starts. This is how Tomas started.*

The thought arrived unbidden and stayed like a guest with wet boots. She pushed back from the table. She washed the teacups. She went out and split kindling until her shoulders burned, though there was kindling enough for a month, and all the while the ledger sat inside on the shelf where it had sat for twenty years, complete, whole, every page of it — the flour and the oil and the nets, and beneath those, interleaved in that second hand she had not yet dared to study by daylight, whatever else her husband had carried.

*Incomplete,* she had said. The word kept echoing. Not because it was false — she was beginning to suspect it might become true, that before Quill's twenty days were out she would make it true with her own hands — but because of how easily it had come. How natural. How much it had sounded like something she had always known how to say.

Nell came home at four with salt in her hair and a story about a seal in the harbor, and Petra listened and laughed in the right places and did not tell her daughter that their name was in an inquiry, that an officer was sleeping tonight in the harbor office with a stoveless grate, that her mother had lied to the Crown before lunch and found it no harder than mending a net.

That night she lit the lamp. She logged the weather — *wind SW, freshening, visibility fair* — in the official book, in her own careful hand, the hand that did not lie, the hand that had never lied, and she watched it move across the page and wondered how long a person could keep two kinds of writing going at once before one of them started to look like the other.

Twenty days.

Below, in the dark, the sea kept working at the seawall. Somewhere in the house, the ledger waited on its shelf, complete as a confession, and Petra Halloran — who had kept the light, kept the accounts, kept the faith, kept herself upright through fever and grief and a winter that showed no sign of ending — climbed down from the lamp room and stood a long while in the cold kitchen, not lighting the lamp, not going to bed, just standing in the dark with her husband's book above her head and the shape of her own lie still warm in her mouth.

In the morning, she decided, she would read the second hand again. Every page. All of it.

Whatever Tomas had hidden, she would need to know it before Quill did.
