## 5. Nell's Questions

The letter had changed her mother. Nell couldn't say how, exactly, which was the trouble with grown-ups — they broke in places you couldn't see, like a hairline crack in a teacup that only showed when the tea was poured.

But she could see the pouring.

Her mother had read the ledger twice at breakfast instead of once, running her finger down Tomas Halloran's neat columns without saying a word. She'd burned the porridge. Petra Halloran did not burn porridge. Nell had eaten hers anyway, tasting smoke, watching her mother stare through the window at the gray sea as if the answer to something were written on it and she'd lost her spectacles.

"Can I go out?" Nell asked.

"Mm." Her mother didn't look up. "Back before the light. Take your oilskin."

Nell took her oilskin. She also took, from the drawer by the door where her mother thought she kept nothing but string and spare buttons, the pencil stub she wasn't supposed to have, and a folded page torn from the back of her arithmetic book.

She was twelve years old and she had decided to conduct an investigation.

* * *

The tide was going out when she came down to the shingle below the point, and it went out far that morning, farther than usual, dragging the water back from the rocks like a hand pulling a tablecloth. The locals said the winter tides were hungry ones, that they took more than they gave. Nell had always thought the sea was less like a thief and more like a shopkeeper who rearranged his stock every six hours and let you see behind the counter if you came at the right time.

This was the right time.

The caves along the point were known to every child in Carrickmoor and forbidden by every parent, which meant they were visited constantly and discussed never. There were three of them. The first two were shallow, good for shelter from squalls, their walls furred with barnacles and the old soot of smugglers' torches from before anyone living was born. The third went deeper, back under the headland, and flooded twice a day. You had to know your tides or you drowned in the dark, and one of the Byrne boys' uncles had nearly done it in 1911, so there was a story about it, which only made the cave better.

Nell went in at low water with her boots off, slung around her neck by the laces, and her skirt hitched up, and her heart going like a wren in a box.

It wasn't adventure, exactly. Or it was, but underneath the adventure there was a question, and the question had been growing since breakfast, since the burned porridge, since the letter with the customs seal that her mother had read standing up and then put face-down on the dresser as if paper could be made invisible by embarrassment.

*Named in an inquiry.* She hadn't been supposed to see it. She'd seen it.

*Contraband moving through Carrickmoor's harbor.*

And this morning her mother had read the ledger twice and burned the porridge, and Nell had done sums in her head all through the walk down: her father was dead eleven months; contraband moved through Carrickmoor for four years of war; therefore either the smuggling had stopped dead the week he died, which would be a coincidence of the kind that happened in stories, or —

Or her father had been part of it.

That was the sum she couldn't make come out right. That was the sum that sent her into the cold dark of the cave with her boots swinging at her chest.

Because here was the other thing, the thing she'd carried alone for weeks now like a stone in her pocket: she remembered her father's boat coming home late sometimes. Not often. Four times, maybe five, in the last two winters. Coming home on nights when no honest boat went out, because she'd checked against her mother's weather log — she'd checked *last month*, sitting cross-legged on her bed with the old logbooks, because she was her father's daughter and numbers settled things. Late boats on flat calm nights with no moon worth mentioning. And each time, her father had come up from the harbor smelling of salt and lamp oil, and he'd kissed the top of her head and said, "There's my girl," and his voice had been perfectly ordinary, and she had believed him, because you believe your father the way you believe the ground.

She wanted to be wrong. She wanted it so badly that she'd walked past the first two caves and gone straight for the third, because wanting wasn't evidence, and Nell Halloran trusted evidence the way other children trusted God.

The third cave swallowed the daylight fast. She'd brought a candle stub and matches in an oilcloth twist, and she lit it when the dark got ahead of her, and the flame threw her shadow huge and wavering onto wet stone. The floor sloped gently down and the air tasted of salt and old rope. Her bare feet found pools. Once something soft and alive brushed her toe and she stood very still until her heart agreed to continue.

Deeper in, the cave forked. Left went nowhere, ending in a wall of tumbled rock. Right narrowed and then opened again, and it was there, where the passage widened, that she smelled it before she saw it.

Lamp oil. Paraffin. Under the salt-smell, unmistakable — the smell of the workshop, of her father's coat collar, of home.

She raised the candle.

The crate sat wedged back in an alcove above the high-tide line, half-hidden behind a curtain of fishing net gone green with age. It was smaller than she'd imagined — about the size of the family Bible box — and its boards were swollen with damp, but it was whole, and it was new enough that the nails still held bright metal at their heads.

Someone had hidden it well and recently. Someone had meant to come back.

Nell crouched in front of it for a long moment, not touching it yet, just looking, the way her father had taught her to look at anything you meant to take apart. *Eyes first, then hands.* He'd said that over clockwork and gull eggs and once, gravely, over a stranded jellyfish the size of a dinner plate.

Then she touched it.

The lid shifted. It wasn't nailed — it was set loose in its frame, the way crates are set when they're meant to be opened quietly and more than once. Inside, packed in straw gone gray: bottles. A dozen of them, dark glass, unlabeled, sealed with wax. She lifted one. It was heavier than it looked and it sloshed thickly, not like spirits, more like syrup, like medicine.

Brandy, maybe. Or something else. She was twelve, and she knew what brandy looked like from the communion wine at Christmas, and this was darker than that.

She turned the bottle in the candlelight, looking for a mark, because everything has a mark, Da used to say, everything that travels has to say where it's been — and there it was, branded into the crate's end board, faint but legible where the wood had been planed smooth:

**K & S — ROTTERDAM**

Three letters and a city. And Nell sat down right there on the cold wet stone of the forbidden cave, because she knew those letters. She knew them the way she knew her own birthday.

*K & S.* In the ledger. In her father's hand, in the plain columns everyone saw — no, wait. No. Not in the plain columns. In the *other* columns, the odd ones, the entries she'd found three weeks ago when she'd been hunting for a scrap of blotting paper and had instead found pages of figures that didn't add up: dates and initials and sums that matched nothing in the household accounts. Entries in her father's writing that weren't debts at all.

*K&S — 14/11 — 3 doz — T.H.*

Fourteenth of November. Three dozen. Her father's initials.

She'd shown her mother nothing. She'd told herself she'd imagined it, misread it, that ledgers had marks and smudges and that daughters invented fathers' secrets out of boredom. But she hadn't thrown the memory away. She'd folded it small and kept it, and now here it was again, stamped in Rotterdam oak, sitting in a sea cave under Carrickmoor point with a year's dust on it and paraffin-smelling straw.

Her father had known about this crate.

Her father had *written about* this crate.

Nell did what she always did when the world stopped making sense. She took out her pencil stub and her torn page of arithmetic paper, and she copied the mark exactly — K & S, Rotterdam, the nail pattern, the position of the alcove, the count of bottles, the depth of the cave from the entrance measured in her own footsteps, forty-one — and then she wrapped the page in the oilcloth twist with the dead match, and put it inside her boot, and put her boots on, and blew out the candle.

Before she left, she slid the crate's lid back flush and dragged the rotten netting over it, and she made the net look the way she'd found it, which took longer than finding it had. Then she stood at the mouth of the cave while the sea came back for its counter, and she thought.

Here was the shape of it, as best she could hold it: someone was bringing bottles into Carrickmoor against the war rules, hiding them in her cave — *her* cave, the one every child knew, which meant whoever hid it was local, because outsiders used the quarry cut — and her dead father's handwriting sat in the middle of it like a fly in amber.

And her mother, who read the ledger every morning like a psalm, who kept the village's debts the way the priest kept sins — her mother had read it twice today and burned the porridge, because a letter had come naming the family, and her mother's face when she'd read it had not been surprised.

That was the worst part. Nell turned it over and over on the walk home, testing it like a loose tooth. Her mother hadn't looked surprised. She'd looked *prepared*, the way you look when bad news arrives on schedule. Which meant her mother already knew something. Which meant her mother had known something — maybe for a long time — and had said nothing, at breakfast, at supper, at prayers, ever.

You believe your mother the way you believe the sea. But the sea, Nell was learning, rearranges its stock.

* * *

She came up the cliff path with wet hems and salt-stiff hair, and her mother was in the garden turning the mangle, and Nell's whole body prepared a lie before her mind had authorized one.

"Where've you been?" her mother asked, not sharply. Never sharply. That was the trick of Petra Halloran — she asked questions softly, and you answered them fully, and only later did you understand you'd been weighed.

"Wreckers' Cove," Nell said. "Looking for gull eggs."

"There's no gulls laying in November."

"I know." A beat. "I was checking."

Her mother's eyes came up from the mangle and rested on her, blue and level and patient, and Nell felt the folded page inside her boot press against her ankle like a small hot coal. *Don't lie to me twice,* her mother's look said, though her mouth said nothing. Nell had seen her use that look on fishermen shorting their accounts. It had broken men bigger than a twelve-year-old girl.

She held it.

"Gull eggs," her mother repeated.

"For science," said Nell, who had learned at school that science excused almost anything.

Something moved behind her mother's eyes — not suspicion, worse, a kind of tired recognition, as if she were watching her own reflection wear a younger face. "Wash your feet. You'll track sand through the house."

And that was all. That was all her mother said, and Nell went inside and washed her feet in the tin basin, and the folded page stayed dry in her boot because she'd thought to take the boot off first, which was the first lie's first competence, and she hated how easily it had come.

At supper they ate bread and dripping and tinned salmon her mother portioned out like a miser counting coins, and neither of them spoke of the letter, and neither of them spoke of caves, and Nell watched her mother across the table and tried to see her plainly for the first time. Not Ma. Not keeper-of-the-light's-wife, now keeper-of-the-light herself. Just a woman of thirty-eight with chapped hands and a crease between her brows who had lived eleven months with a dead man's ledger open on her table, reading it every morning like scripture, and who had perhaps known all along what was written in the margins of it.

"Do you miss him?" Nell asked suddenly.

Her mother looked up. The lamplight caught the steam off the tea. "Every day."

"But do you miss *him*?" Nell pressed. "Or do you miss knowing him?"

The silence lasted three full turns of the clock pendulum. Nell counted, because counting steadied her.

"He was a good man," her mother said at last, carefully, each word set down like a cup that might crack. "He kept this light through nine winters. He kept our neighbors fed when the boats failed. Whatever else is true of him, that's true too."

*Whatever else is true of him.* Nell turned the phrase over like the bottle in the cave, looking for a label. Her mother might as well have said *whatever else we find in the crate.*

"You talk like the ledger," Nell said. "Both sides of the page."

Her mother set down her knife. For a moment Nell thought she'd gone too far, that the question would break something open right there between the salt dish and the bread, and part of her — a shameful, eager part — wanted it to break. Wanted the truth to come out of her mother's mouth instead of a cave, wanted it warm and witnessed and shared, because a secret carried alone at twelve years old weighs what a grown man's secret weighs.

But her mother only said, "Go and see to the wicks," and Nell went, and the breaking did not happen, and the secret stayed in her boot, and upstairs in her narrow room under the eaves she unfolded the oilcloth twist by night-lamp and read her own handwriting back to herself:

*K & S — ROTTERDAM. 12 bottles, wax-sealed. Alcove R, 41 paces. Net over. Da's writing says 14/11, 3 doz, T.H.*

Three dozen. Twelve bottles in the crate.

Two dozen missing.

Which meant twenty-four bottles had already gone somewhere — sold, drunk, given away, moved — and the arithmetic of it sat in her stomach like swallowed sea-water. Somewhere in Carrickmoor, in houses she knew, on tables she'd sat at, were bottles that had passed through her father's hands. Mrs. O'Shea's kitchen. The Byrnes'. Maybe the rectory. Maybe everywhere.

Maybe that was why nobody in the village had starved in the hard winter of '17, she thought, lying in the dark listening to the wind work at the shutters. Maybe the blockade had a back door and her father was the hinge of it.

She should tell her mother. That was the correct move; even her arithmetic book agreed that errors compound and must be reported. She rehearsed it: *Ma, I found a crate in the third cave, and the mark matches the odd entries, and I think Da —*

But every rehearsal snagged on the same rock: *and I think Da*. Because to say it aloud was to make it true, and to make it true was to choose between them — the father who had said *there's my girl* and smelled of paraffin, and the mother who had read the customs letter without surprise and asked her questions softly. Nell lay in the dark and understood, with the terrible clarity available only to children and mathematicians, that the evidence pointed two directions at once, and whichever parent she aimed it at, she'd lose something she couldn't get back.

So she did neither. She folded the page small, and she did not hide it in her boot where it would wash out or wrinkle or be found, but inside the hollow spine of her arithmetic book, beneath the flyleaf, flat as a pressed flower. Evidence keeps, she told herself. Numbers keep. Whatever her mother decided to do about Officer Quill and the fortnight and the letter, Nell would have the true figures ready, and when the moment came — and it would come, moments like that always came, the tide went out for everyone eventually — she would lay them on the table and watch both their faces.

Below, she heard her mother moving through the lower rooms, the familiar sounds: the lamp trimmed, the bolt shot, the ledger's covers closing with a soft leather clap like a book of hours being shut. Then the stairs, slow, pausing outside Nell's door — a pause long enough to notice, short enough to deny — before continuing to her own room.

Nell stared into the dark and did her sums one final time.

Forty-one paces to the alcove. Twelve bottles present. Twenty-four gone. One father dead. Two parents' stories, and only one of them left to ask.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would go back at low water and measure the tide-tables against the crate — because crates don't float in on their own, someone had rowed it in, and rowboats leave tracks, and tracks have owners.

And tomorrow, too, if the courage held, she would take down the ledger after her mother slept, and read the second hand again. Every page. All of it.

Whatever Tomas Halloran had hidden, she would need to know it before Quill did.
