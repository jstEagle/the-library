## 6. The Workshop Still Stands

The requisition notice came on a Tuesday, folded inside the coal ration papers like a knife wrapped in a dishcloth.

Petra read it twice at the kitchen table, Nell's porridge going cold between them. *By order of the Admiralty, all optical workshops within coastal districts are to be surveyed for conversion to war material production.* The words were dry as biscuit. The implications were not.

"They can't," Nell said. "It's ours."

"Governments don't deal in can't, love." Petra set the paper down and smoothed it flat, the way she'd once smoothed Tomas's collar before his funerals — no, before his funeral, singular, though some mornings the word still came out plural, as if she'd buried him more than once, once for each version of him she'd thought she knew.

The workshop stood attached to the lighthouse tower by a covered walk her grandfather had built, a low stone room with north-facing windows that threw an even, shadowless light across the polishing benches. It had been in Halloran hands for sixty years. Her father had ground lenses there. Tomas had learned to there, his big fisherman's hands surprisingly delicate with rouge and pitch, and Petra had learned beside him, and after he drowned she had gone on learning alone, because the lamp did not care about grief and neither did the sea.

Now a man with a clipboard in Dublin wanted it for grinding gun sights.

She finished her tea. She banked the fire. And then she walked down the covered way, unlocked the workshop door with the key she wore on a string around her neck like other women wore lockets, and began to repair what the requisition might yet take from her.

* * *

There was a crack running through the backup lens housing — hairline, but growing; she'd measured it against a scribed line every month since spring, and this month it had gained a millimeter. The great lens itself, the Fresnel that turned above her head each night, was sound, but its clockwork needed cleaning, and two of the prism panels had chips at their edges that scattered light into halos where the light should have been clean as water.

So she worked.

The work was simple and it was not. Grinding a lens meant patience measured in weeks — coarse grit first, then finer, then finer still, each grade removing the scratches of the last, until the glass held your own face without distortion when you looked close. Polishing meant rouge on felt, hours of circular motion until your shoulder screamed and your thoughts went quiet. There was no hurrying glass. Glass taught you its own time, and if you fought it, you got flaws.

Her mother-in-law, rest her, had called the work idle fiddling. But Petra had come to understand it differently. When Tomas died — when the boat came back without him, which is how she preferred to think of it, the boat returning faithfully and Tomas simply absent, as though the sea had borrowed him — she had not been able to pray. The words wouldn't come. They sat in her chest like stones in a net.

But the workshop took her in. The rhythm of grit and rouge asked nothing of her except attention, and attention, it turned out, was very near to prayer. You could not polish a lens and hate someone at the same time. Your hands were too busy. And when the finished panel went up into the lantern room and the beam swept out over the black water, clean and far-reaching and indifferent to deserving, that was the closest thing to grace she had ever produced with her own body.

So: repairs in earnest. Because if peace came — and the rumors said it might, the wireless at the hotel muttering about armistice terms, delegates, cease-fire lines — then perhaps the requisition would die unsigned, another scrap of wartime paper voided by peacetime. And if peace did not come, well. She would lose the workshop either way. But she would lose it *finished*, every flaw corrected, every mechanism oiled, and that difference mattered to her in a way she could not explain to anyone and did not try.

Hope, she was learning, was not a feeling. It was a quantity. It was measurable in finished work. A millimeter of crack arrested. A panel re-seated. You counted it out like counting stitches, and at the end of the day you had either built up a reserve or spent yourself down to nothing, and today she intended to build.

* * *

The third morning, she found Officer Quill standing at the mouth of the covered walk, rain dripping off his hat brim, looking at the workshop door as though it had addressed him personally and he hadn't decided how to answer.

"You'll get soaked through standing there," Petra said, and stepped aside to let him pass, because whatever else he was, he was a man made mostly of wet wool and paperwork, and it seemed cruel to leave him dripping.

He came in. He took off his hat. He looked around the workshop the way men look in churches they don't attend — politely, taking in the benches and the tools and the half-disassembled clockwork spread on oilcloth, cataloguing everything without appearing to catalogue anything. She knew that look. She'd worn it herself these past weeks, reading the ledger by lamplight, hunting for what wasn't written down.

"I'm told you grind lenses here," he said.

"I'm told you investigate smugglers here," she said. "We're both misinformed. This is a lighthouse."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the ground where one might grow. "The requisition survey," he said. "I've seen the orders. I'm to note whether the facility is operational."

"It's operational."

"And whether it's essential."

She picked up her felt pad. She charged it with rouge from the tin, a slow red spiral, and set it to the panel clamped in her vise. "Officer Quill. Have you ever watched glass being polished?"

"No."

"Then stay or go, but be quiet either way."

And — this was the thing she remembered afterward, the thing that kept snagging on her like a burr — he stayed. He dragged a stool to the wall, out of the light, and he sat, and for over an hour he said nothing while she worked the rouge in slow circles and the window light climbed the wall and fell again. Once, when she stopped to check her progress against the template, she caught him watching her hands, not her face, with an expression she couldn't file under any name she owned. Something like envy. Something like exhaustion. Something like a man watching someone do work that means exactly what it says.

When she finally set the pad down, his knees cracked when he stood. "Thank you," he said, which made no sense, since she hadn't done anything for him. Then he put on his hat and went back out into the rain.

* * *

He came again. And again. He never brought questions. He never opened his notebook. He would appear at the walk's mouth in weather that would keep any sensible man indoors, sit on his stool, and watch her work, and the strangest part was how quickly it stopped being strange. His silence fit into the workshop's silence the way a properly ground panel fits its frame — no forcing, no gaps.

Nell noticed first, of course. Twelve-year-olds notice everything and mistake it for nothing.

"Why does the spy watch you?" she asked, helping carry the grit tins down the walk.

"He's not a spy. He's a policeman."

"Same hat." Nell hefted her tin. "Does he make you nervous? Mam. Does he?"

Petra considered lying. She was getting practiced at it — the lie to Quill about the ledger's completeness still sat in her stomach like a swallowed coin, cold and smooth and always there when she turned over. But Nell's eyes were Tomas's eyes, gray and patient as weather, and lies slid off them.

"A little," she said. "Not because of what he knows. Because of what he might be made to want to know."

Nell frowned at that, turning it over, and let it drop. But that night Petra heard her in her room, long past bedtime, pencil scratching — sums, probably. The girl ran numbers the way other children ran wild, and Petra lay listening and thought: *whatever she's working out, I hope it's only homework.*

* * *

The days shortened. The armistice rumor swelled and shrank like tide — peace signed, peace stalled, the delegates quarreling over some point of honor that would cost, everyone agreed, another winter of boys. Each morning the wireless said maybe, and each morning Petra went down to the workshop and converted maybe into measurable units.

She re-seated the chipped panels, cutting new pitch laps and pressing them warm against the glass. She stripped the clockwork to its bones, degreased seventy years of accumulated filth, and rebuilt it with fresh oil so fine the escapement ticked like a small contented heart. The crack in the backup housing she could not cure — glass doesn't forgive, it only holds — but she bound it with a fitted brass strap, shimmed and pinned, and scribed a new measuring line, and told herself that a bound crack was a kept promise: *not perfect, but holding.*

Some evenings Mrs. O'Shea came down with bread or a bottle of stout and sat where Quill sat, and talked, filling the room with village news the way smoke fills a chimney flue. Petra listened with half an ear and worked with both hands, and watched the widow watch the ledger shelf in the corner — the honest ledger now, the flour-and-nets book, since Petra had taken to keeping the coded pages elsewhere, somewhere even Nell didn't know — and wondered, not for the first time, what the widow's silences weighed.

"You're doing a man's mourning," Mrs. O'Shea said one evening, nodding at the bench. "All this fixing."

"I'm doing a keeper's work."

"Same thing, maybe." The widow turned her cup. "Tomas used to say a lens doesn't make light. It only gathers what's already there and points it true. Maybe that's what you're at."

Petra kept her circles steady. "Since when did you quote my husband?"

"Since always, girl. You just weren't listening." But something in her face, as she said it — something quick and pained, there and gone like a fish under a swell — made Petra's hands slow, and made her think of gaps in a ledger, dates missing, entries in a hand almost like Tomas's and almost not, and she said nothing, because the workshop was no place for that conversation, and because she was not yet sure she wanted to hear the answer.

* * *

On the last Friday of November, Quill arrived late, after dark, rain gone horizontal off the sea. He stood dripping in the doorway longer than usual, and when he finally spoke, his voice was different — flatter, wearier, a report read aloud to himself.

"The armistice talk has stalled again. Properly stalled. Could be weeks. Could be the war goes on into spring." He looked at the bound crack in the backup housing. "Which means the requisition stands. Survey team comes within the fortnight."

Petra set down her pad. The rouge spiraled red on the felt, bright as a wound, bright as anything.

"Unless," Quill said.

"Unless what?"

He was quiet a long moment. Rain hammered the roof. Somewhere below, the sea worked at the seawall with its patient criminal hands.

"Unless the survey finds the facility essential to navigation," he said at last. "A working lens shop, keeping a first-order light in repair on a coast like this — there's a case to be made. A strong one. But it needs evidence. Records of maintenance. Logs of repairs." His eyes came up to hers, and for the first time since he'd arrived in Carrickmoor, Officer Quill looked directly at her, and she saw that beneath the tired courtesy was a mind like a gimlet, and that it had been boring toward something all along. "You'd want those records in order, Mrs. Halloran. Complete. Every entry accounted for."

The word *complete* landed between them like a dropped tool.

She heard it for what it was — a warning dressed as advice, or advice dressed as a warning, a man telling her, in the only language his position allowed, that the ledger existed in two versions and he might know it, and that whichever version she surrendered would decide what happened to more than a workshop.

"I keep careful books, Officer," she said.

"I'm sure you do," he said. "That's rather what worries me."

He collected his hat. At the door he paused, back to her, rain-light silvering his shoulders.

"For what it's worth — the panel you finished Tuesday. It's the finest piece of work I've seen in this county. Whatever happens to this room, that's true, and no survey can unmake it."

Then he was gone into the storm, and Petra stood alone among her benches with her heart going hard, understanding two things at once with terrible clarity: that Quill had just handed her a rope, and that rope, in the wrong weather, was also called a noose — and that the fortnight he'd given her was not for the workshop at all.

Upstairs, the lamp turned. Down in the dark, the sea waited. And on the desk in the parlor, under a stone weight, the ledger lay closed, holding whatever it held, patient as glass.
