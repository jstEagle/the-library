## 7. Entries in Another Hand

The house went quiet the way it always did after Nell climbed to her loft — a creak of the ladder, a thump of boots discarded over the rail, then nothing but the sea working at the rocks below. Petra sat at the kitchen table with the ledger open in front of her and a lamp turned low, because low light was honest light. It showed you what was actually on the page instead of what you hoped was there.

She had told Officer Quill the ledger was complete. She had said the words *every entry accounted for* with a steady voice and had watched him write them down, and she had not thought much about it afterward, because a lie spoken smoothly is easy to forget. But tonight the words came back to her while she turned the pages, and she found herself doing what she'd never once done in three years of keeping Tomas's book: reading it like a stranger.

Tomas Halloran wrote a fair hand. Not beautiful — he'd been a fisherman's son before he was ever a keeper — but even, upright, each letter standing where you expected it to stand. He ruled his lines with a straightedge. He dated everything twice: once in the margin, once above the entry, because he said a man who trusted one date deserved whatever trouble came from forgetting.

Petra knew this hand the way she knew her own reflection.

So when she reached the entries for March of 1916 and felt something wrong under her eyes before she could name it, she stopped and went back three pages and looked again.

There. In the margin, where Tomas's marginal dates ran down the page in his tidy column, the entry for the fourteenth of March sat a half-line lower than its neighbors. Not much. A hair's width. The kind of thing no one would notice unless they were looking for nothing else.

She fetched the magnifying glass from the workshop drawer — the one Tomas used for inspecting lens flaws — and held it over the page.

The ink was different.

Not obviously. Not to a glance. But under the glass, Tomas's iron-gall brown had that dry, hungry quality of ink left too long in a cold room, and the letters of the fourteenth of March were blacker, wetter-looking, laid down by a pen held at a slightly different angle. The date itself matched his hand almost perfectly. Almost. The *7* leaned the wrong way. Tomas's sevens stood straight as pilings. This one tilted like a mast running before weather.

Someone else had written in Tomas's dates.

Petra set the glass down and pressed both palms flat against the table until the wood's chill came up through her skin. The stove ticked. Somewhere out past the point, the swell hit the blowhole and exhaled, long and slow, like the coast breathing in its sleep.

She read the fourteenth of March properly for the first time. *Flour to O'Shea, 2 stone. Oil drawn, 1 gal. Lamp cleaned.* Ordinary. The ordinary things were always where he hid things — or where someone had hidden things inside him.

But below the flour line, in that second hand, there was more. Faint. Written so lightly the nib barely kissed the paper, the way a person writes when they don't want the impression carrying through to the next leaf. She angled the lamp until the flame threw its light sideways across the page, raking it low across the grain, and the faint strokes rose up out of the paper like weed at low tide.

Numbers. A tide table — no, not a table. Tides noted against letters. *H.W. 4:12 — M. H.W. 5:03 — D.* And initials. Three of them, repeated down the page in a rhythm that had nothing to do with flour or oil: *M.D. B.K. M.D. J.O.S.*

J.O.S.

Josephine O'Shea.

Petra sat back so fast the chair legs barked against the flagstones, and upstairs, Nell's floorboard answered with a single sympathetic creak, then silence.

She turned the pages forward with the lamp held low and raking, and the faint writing kept coming. Not every page. Every third or fourth. Always in the margins, always beneath an innocent entry in Tomas's hand, always dated to days she could now see had been bad-weather days — days when no legitimate boat would put out, when the harbor at Carrickmoor lay empty and gray and nobody official would be watching anything but their own fires. Dates and tides and initials, threaded through her husband's honest book like a dark thread through clean cloth.

March 1916. June 1916. November. Through all of 1917, thinning toward the end, and then stopping altogether in —

Petra counted back through her own memory without wanting to. The last coded entry was dated the ninth of October, 1918. Tomas died on the twelfth.

Three days. Whatever had been moving through Carrickmoor's harbor had moved for the last time three days before her husband drowned, and then the hand that wrote in his margins had never written again.

She closed the ledger. She opened it again. She did this three times, as if the pages might rearrange themselves into sense, and each time they gave her the same answer: the smuggling the customs letter spoke of had not merely touched Carrickmoor. It had passed through her kitchen. It had been written down at her own table, by lamplight, in a hand that was almost her husband's and was certainly not, and either someone had used Tomas's book as cover — or Tomas had let them, or helped them, or planned it.

Or written it himself, in a hand he'd disguised, which was a thought so cold she pushed it away and immediately pulled it back, because pushing thoughts away is how widows drown.

*He mended nets for the Byrne boys without charging them,* she told the empty kitchen. *He carried the O'Shea widow's coal up her path every winter. He named our daughter after his mother. Men like that don't—*

Men like what? She didn't finish it. There was no ending to that sentence that didn't require her to know exactly what kind of man Tomas Halloran had been, and she was discovering, hour by hour, that she might not know at all.

The lamp burned low. She trimmed it without thinking, hands doing the work while her mind ran the tides backward — because that was the thing, wasn't it, the thing any keeper's wife could do in her sleep. High water at 4:12 in the morning meant darkness, meant a boat could come over the bar without being seen from the village. Meant the lighthouse itself — *her* light, *her* watch — had been the one fixed point the smugglers steered by. Every night she'd trimmed that wick and logged that weather, she'd been keeping faith with ships she thought were honest, and some fraction of them had been running contraband into the cove under the sweep of her own beam.

Her light had been the smuggler's lighthouse too.

Petra put her head in her hands and stayed that way for a long time, listening to the sea, feeling grief change its shape inside her the way ice changes in a thaw — still the same substance, but suddenly moving, suddenly dangerous, suddenly going somewhere.

When she lifted her head, she made herself do the arithmetic a bookkeeper does instead of the arithmetic a widow does. She took a scrap of paper and copied out every coded entry, date by date, initial by initial, in her own plain hand, and folded the scrap into the bodice of her dress, against her heart, where it sat like a small cold coin. Then she banked the fire, checked the lamp log, wrote *Wind SW fresh, squalls toward dawn* in the weather book in her own unhidden handwriting, and climbed the stairs past her sleeping daughter's door without stopping, though stopping was what every part of her wanted to do.

Tomorrow she would go down to the widow's cottage. Tomorrow she would ask.

Tonight she lay beside the cold place where Tomas used to be and understood, at last, why the customs letter had been addressed to *the Halloran Family* and not to the harbor trust, not to the magistrate, not to any of the men whose names carried weight in Carrickmoor. They hadn't written to the powerful. They'd written to whoever kept the books.

They'd known, somehow, that the books were the whole story.

* * *

Mrs. O'Shea's cottage sat at the bottom of the cliff path, tucked behind a windbreak of gorse that was more brown than green this time of year. Smoke stood straight up from the chimney, which meant the fire was drawing well and the widow was home, and Petra stood outside the gate for a full minute rehearsing sentences and discarding them, because there was no sentence in the language that asked *did my dead husband run contraband with you* gently enough to survive the asking.

In the end she knocked, and Josephine O'Shea opened the door with flour to her elbows, and Petra understood that the moment for gentleness had probably passed somewhere around the time she'd copied initials out of a dead man's ledger by raking lamplight.

"Petra." The widow's face did its usual work — warmth arriving a half-second late, the smile assembled rather than felt. "You'll come in, I've a batch in the oven and it wants eating before it goes hard."

"I'll come in," Petra said.

The kitchen was small and hot and smelled of bread and peat. Mrs. O'Shea wiped her hands and poured tea without asking, and chattered — the price of meal, her knees, the storm coming up from the southwest that everyone could smell now in the offing — and Petra drank her tea and waited, because waiting was a skill she'd learned from three years of keeping a light: you hold the beam steady and eventually everything shows itself.

"Josephine." She set the cup down. "I need to ask you about the ledger."

The change was small. Most people wouldn't have caught it. But Petra had spent years reading faces across counters and tables, faces of people settling debts they were proud of and debts they were ashamed of, and she saw it plainly: the widow's hands went still on the teapot, and her eyes went to the window, and for one full second the chatter stopped as if a switch had been thrown.

"The ledger," Mrs. O'Shea said. "What about it?"

"You owe me two stone of flour since August."

"And I've told you, I'll have it by Martinmas, my nephew's bringing meal up from—"

"It's not the flour." Petra kept her voice level, keeper's voice, the voice that carried over storms without strain. "There are entries in Tomas's book. In the margins. Another hand. Dates and tides and initials, and one of those initials is yours."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Petra counted them, the way she counted everything now.

Then the widow laughed. It was a good laugh, warm and rough, the laugh of a woman who'd buried a husband and raised no children and laughed at funerals because the alternative was worse. "Another hand! Sure, Tomas let me jot the odd note when my memory was failing him — flour, candles, the price of paraffin. You know what my memory's like, Petra. Half the village has scribbled in that book of his at one time or another."

"No," Petra said quietly. "Half the village hasn't. I've kept that book since he died and I know every mark in it. These are older than that. Two years older. And they're not flour."

"Then what are they?" The widow's chin came up, and there was steel in it now, the steel of the generation that had buried sons. "If you know what they are, tell me, and I'll tell you whether I wrote them."

And there it was — the trap, sprung on herself. Because Petra didn't know. She knew the shapes of the entries, the tides, the initials, the terrible three-day gap before Tomas drowned. She did not know what any of it meant, and she had come down that cliff path certain she did, and now she heard her own certainty crumble in front of the one woman who could confirm or destroy it.

"I think," Petra said slowly, "that boats came into Carrickmoor during the war that shouldn't have. I think goods moved that weren't on any manifest. I think my husband's ledger recorded them, in code, in the margins, under his own honest entries — and I think you know whether that's true, because your initials are in the darkest part of it."

Mrs. O'Shea stood up. She began gathering the flour-dusted bowls, stacking them with more force than they needed, her back to Petra, and when she finally spoke her voice had gone flat and careful, a voice scrubbed clean.

"I don't know anything about boats, Petra Halloran. I'm a widow who owes you flour. That's all I am to you, and all you are to me, and if some officer has filled your head with stories about this village—" she set the last bowl down harder than the rest, "—then you should mind what you repeat. Accusations are like sparks in a thatched town. They don't land where you throw them."

"That's not a denial."

"It's none of your business, is what it is." The widow turned around, and her face was composed again, but her eyes were wet, and it was the wetness that undid Petra more than any confession could have. "You want me to say something that will make it simple. That your Tom was a criminal, or that he was a saint, so you can close the book and get on with your grieving. But I'm telling you nothing. Nothing. Do you hear me? Ask me again at Christmas, ask me again at the Resurrection, and the answer's the same."

Petra rose. She left coins on the table for the tea, out of habit, and saw the widow register the insult of it and choose not to fight it.

At the door, Mrs. O'Shea caught her sleeve. Her grip was stronger than a woman her age should have had, and her face had lost all its assembly, all its warmth, and what was underneath was fear — raw, animal, old.

"Petra." Low, urgent. "Whatever's in those margins. However you worked it out. Don't go showing it to that customs man. Don't show it to *anyone*. Some entries aren't records. They're warnings. Your Tom knew the difference. God help us both if you don't learn it before the officer comes."

Then she let go, shut the door, and shot the bolt, and Petra walked back up the cliff path with the wind rising out of the southwest and the small cold paper against her heart, understanding that she had gotten her answer after all.

The widow hadn't denied it quickly.

She had denied it too completely — and too late.
