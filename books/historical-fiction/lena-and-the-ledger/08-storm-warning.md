## 8. Storm Warning

The barometer began falling on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday it had fallen like a man kneeling.

Petra watched it happen over breakfast, the needle sliding down the brass dial in small reluctant ticks, and she said nothing to Nell because there was nothing yet to say that the glass hadn't already said better. She finished her tea. She went out onto the gallery and looked south, where the horizon had gone the color of wet slate and the swell was already running long and green under it, six feet, then eight, then a sea that didn't break so much as unfold.

"Tomas would have called it by now," she murmured, and then hated herself for the murmur, because Tomas wasn't here to call anything, and the habit of talking to him was becoming the habit of talking to no one.

She worked through the morning with the economy of long practice. Extra oil up to the lamp room — two drums instead of one, hauled up the stairs on the hand truck with her shoulders screaming by the second trip. The spare wicks laid out on clean cloth. The backup lens uncovered and its gearing oiled, though the mechanism had been making a sound lately, a faint dry clicking on the return stroke that she hadn't been able to trace and couldn't afford to think about. She wrote *investigate* in the margin of her maintenance log and underlined it twice.

By noon the wind had come around to the southeast and begun to speak in full sentences.

The village read the sky the way she read the ledger. By two o'clock the boats were all hauled above the tideline, keels up, looking from the cliff path like a congregation of sleeping animals. Mrs. O'Shea stood in her doorway watching the water with an expression Petra couldn't name — not fear exactly. Recognition. As if the storm were a letter she'd been expecting for years and had only just admitted to herself was addressed to her.

At three, Father Brennan went door to door with his coat collar up, and word came along the row the way word always did: church at dusk. Anyone who wanted shelter. Bring blankets, bring bread if you had it, bring your grandmother and don't argue with her about it.

Nell appeared at the lighthouse door at half past four, cheeks whipped red, hair full of salt.

"Father Brennan says—"

"I know."

"You're not coming."

"I can't leave the light."

"I know." Nell stood there a moment, working her mitten in her teeth. "I could stay too. I'm strong now. I haul things."

Petra looked at her daughter — twelve years old, thin as a gull's leg, with her father's stubborn jaw set against the wind — and felt something in her chest pull tight as rigging.

"You'll go to the church," she said. "You'll sit with the O'Shea woman and you'll mind what she tells you, and you will not come back here tonight no matter what you hear or imagine you hear. Do you understand me? The light is mine to keep. It has always been mine to keep. That is not a thing we share."

It came out harder than she meant it to, and she saw it land, saw Nell flinch and then square up, and understood that she had hurt her daughter and made her proud in the same breath, which was perhaps the most honest thing Petra had done all week.

"Yes, Mam," Nell said, and went down into the weather.

* * *

The storm arrived at seven o'clock like something that had been given permission.

It did not build. It simply arrived — a wall of wind off the southeast that hit the tower and made it hum, a low resonant note in the stonework that Petra felt in her back teeth, in the soles of her feet, in the fillings of her jaw. Rain came horizontal. The first wave over the seawall came at half past seven, and she heard it before she saw it: a boom like a door slamming in the house of the world, followed by the long hiss and clatter of shingle dragged back down the beach.

From the lamp room gallery she watched the water climb the harbor road in sheets. The seawall held, and then it didn't quite hold — a section near the fish store let the ninth wave over it, and the tenth, and after that the sea stopped bothering to count and simply took possession of everything below the chapel steps.

The light turned. She kept it turning.

That was the whole of the work and the whole of the prayer. Trim the wick at the top of every hour. Wipe the salt spray from the storm panes — inside work, mostly, though once an hour she had to open the service door and take the glass brush to the outer faces, leaning out into a wind that tried to peel her off the tower like a label off a jar. Check the clockwork. Wind the rotation drum until the counterweight kissed its stop. Log the weather: *Wind SSE, force 10, gusting. Sea over seawall. Visibility nil.*

The log book was Tomas's once. The handwriting in it was hers now, and she noticed, somewhere around ten o'clock, that her letters had gotten taller, more urgent, as though the page itself were being blown sideways.

Between hours she thought about the letter with the customs seal, folded into the false bottom of the biscuit tin, and she found that thinking about it during a storm was easier than thinking about it in daylight. In daylight it demanded decisions. At midnight it was just another pressure against the walls, like the wind — enormous, impersonal, survivable if you kept your weight low and your wick trimmed.

A ship's bell sounded once, far out, thin as a hair pulled through cloth. Then again. Somewhere out in all that black a vessel was feeling its way toward a coast that wanted to kill it, and the only argument against the drowning of everyone aboard was turning slowly overhead, thirty seconds bright, five dark, thirty bright.

*Keep,* Petra told it. *Keep.*

* * *

She heard the knocking at half past eleven and thought at first it was the storm throwing shingle at the door.

But it came again — fists, human rhythm, three strikes and a pause — and when she got the door open against the wind, Officer Quill stood in the lee of it with rain running off the brim of his hat in ropes, looking like a man who had lost an argument with a landscape.

"The causeway road's flooded," he shouted over the noise. "The innkeeper won't have me — says the roof beams are singing. Father Brennan sent me here. Said the lighthouse takes lodgers in weather."

"He said that?"

"He said, 'Tell her Petra owes me a sermon.'" Quill's mouth did something that might have been a smile if it hadn't been so cold. "I assumed it was code."

"It's a debt," Petra said, and stepped aside. "Come in before the sea does."

He stood dripping in the entrance hall while she fetched towels, and she watched him look around — at the oil-stained floor, the coiled lines, the maintenance log open on its stand, the whole grammar of a life arranged around keeping things burning — with the expression of a man reading someone else's mail and trying not to understand it.

"You should be in the church," she said.

"So should everyone. Half of them are; the other half are filling sandbags at the fish store. I passed them on the way." He wrung out his hat with two efficient twists. "Your widow O'Shea was directing operations like a general. Small woman. Enormous voice."

"She'd have made a fine admiral."

"She'd have made a terrifying one." He hesitated, and she saw him decide to say the next part plainly, which she was learning was the only way he ever said anything worth hearing. "I helped with the sandbags. An hour, maybe. My hands aren't much use but my back is." A pause. "I've never seen people do that before. Not like that. Where I'm from, in weather, they bar their doors. Here they seem to—" He searched for the word and gave up on finding it. "They count each other."

"That's Carrickmoor," Petra said. "We keep accounts of a different kind."

She regretted it the moment she said it — the ledger, the inquiry, all of it rising between them like a stone surfacing in shallow water — but Quill only nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd already written down in some internal report of his own.

"The lamp," he said. "Can I see it? I've never — where I'm stationed there's nothing but river buoys."

So she took him up, ninety-one steps, the tower groaning around them like a ship's mast, and showed him the great lens turning in its bath of lamplight, the prisms catching the flame and folding it into a single blade that swept the darkness and came round again, patient as forgiveness. He stood very still in front of it for a long time.

"How far does it reach?" he asked quietly.

"Fourteen miles on a clear night."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight it reaches as far as it reaches." She checked the wick, wiped the pane. "There'll be men out there counting those flashes. Doing sums. *If I see three more, I'm clear of the shoal.* We don't know their names. They don't know ours. But the arithmetic has to be right or they die doing it."

Quill looked at the lens, then at her, and something moved behind his tired eyes — the look of a man realizing that paperwork, which was his whole profession and his whole armor, was in the end only somebody else's arithmetic, and that lives hung on whose numbers were true.

"Wind it together?" he said. "Show me the crank. You shouldn't be hauling that alone."

* * *

Through the small hours they kept it, the two of them, in a rhythm neither planned. She trimmed; he wound. She brushed the panes; he logged the weather in a careful clerk's hand beside her own. At one o'clock a wave came over the seawall with such violence that the base of the tower shuddered and a jar fell off the kitchen shelf and broke, and neither of them went down to sweep it up. At two, the light of the church windows showed through the rain down the hill, warm and gold, packed with the village — and Petra thought of Nell in there, mitted and solemn among the O'Sheas and the Byrnes and forty others, and allowed herself one minute of missing her, timed like a watch interval, and then went back to work.

Somewhere around three, hauling the counterweight, Quill said, without turning: "Mrs. Halloran. Whatever's coming for this house — and things are coming, we both know it — I want you to know the sandbags tonight weren't investigation. They were just sandbags."

"I know," Petra said.

And she found, to her surprise, that she believed him, and that believing him frightened her more than suspecting him had, because a man who helps you wind the light at three in the morning is a man you might someday have to lie to with your whole face.

* * *

The storm broke its back against the dawn.

Not ended — spent, rather, like a fighter leaning on the ropes — the wind backing around to the west and the waves dropping from monsters to merely enormous. Petra climbed the gallery into a light the color of pearl and looked down at the harbor road: buried in weed and shingle, a fishing punt deposited neatly in the middle of it like an offering. The seawall had lost its coping stones along twenty yards. The fish store had lost its door. And coming up the hill from the direction of the church, small against the wreckage, a figure in a borrowed coat too big for her, climbing with the dogged, furious stride of a girl who had been told not to come and had counted the hours until telling-time expired.

Behind her, slower, came Mrs. O'Shea.

Petra went down the ninety-one steps two at a time.

Nell reached her first and didn't hug her, exactly — she did something fiercer and stranger, she gripped both of Petra's forearms and looked up into her face as if checking her against a description, and said, "You're all right. You're actually all right. Everyone at the church kept saying the tower would go, and Mrs. O'Shea kept saying, *no, that one won't go, that one holds*—"

"Did she," Petra said.

Mrs. O'Shea arrived at the foot of the steps, breathless, gray-faced, her hands still black with sandbag silt. She looked up at Petra for a long moment, and there was something in her face that hadn't been there before the storm — something loosened, unstoppered, the look of a woman standing on ground that had shifted under her.

"We need to talk," Mrs. O'Shea said. "Not here. Not with the child."

"Mam," Nell said sharply, "whatever it is—"

"In the house," Petra said quietly. "Both of you. There's tea, and there's a fire, and there is apparently a conversation that's been waiting on the tide."

Mrs. O'Shea climbed the steps slowly, like a woman walking to a dock where something waited that she'd promised herself she'd never board.

"Last night," she said, reaching the door, "when the water came over the wall — I realized I'm done being afraid of the wrong things."

Petra held the door open, and the wind came in with them.
