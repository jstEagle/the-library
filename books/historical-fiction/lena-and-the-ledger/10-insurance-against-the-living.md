## 10. Insurance Against the Living

The lamp of the workshop burned low that night, and Petra let it.

She had learned in three days of study that Tomas's second hand only revealed itself when she stopped trying to read it like her husband's writing. It wasn't his hand at all — that was the trick of it. The letters leaned a half-degree left, the crosses on the t's were short, and every coded entry sat wedged between legitimate ones the way a splinter sits under skin: invisible until you went looking, then impossible to ignore.

Flour, 2 stone, O'Shea — paid.

*14th March, ebb 4:40, R.M.*

Nets mended, Byrne boys — credit.

*2nd April, flood 7:15, two crates, J.D., settled.*

Tide times. Dates. Initials. She had thought at first they were weather notes, the idle jottings of a man who lived by the sea and could not stop recording it. But weather notes didn't carry initials that matched no one who owed or was owed in Carrickmoor. Weather notes didn't repeat in pairs, always two entries within a day of each other, always after a supply boat failed to arrive — or arrived, she now understood, fuller than its manifest claimed.

She had copied them all onto a practice sheet torn from Nell's drawing paper: forty-one entries across four years. Forty-one shipments, if shipments they were. She laid them out on the workbench in columns and did what she had done with household accounts since she was fifteen years old in her father's shop: she made the numbers talk.

Forty-one entries over four years came to roughly ten a year. Ten boats a year slipping contraband past the customs cutter, through Carrickmoor's harbor, into the war-hungry economy of a coast that had been blockaded, rationed, and left to feed itself however it could. Tobacco, spirits, silk — the old trade, the timeless trade. But some entries made her stomach turn: *medical stores*, twice; *copper*, once. War goods, moving through neutral hands, or worse, moving to the other side of that gray sea where her husband's countrymen were dying by the thousands.

No. She pressed her palms flat on the bench. Not Tomas. He'd have kept it to flour and wool and brandy for the widow O'Shea's cough. He'd have—

He'd have done exactly what the ledger said he did, because the ledger was the only witness she had left, and witnesses lie to the people who love them most.

She worked down the initials column. J.D. appeared nine times. R.M., seven. T.H. — Tomas himself — appeared on eleven, always last, always followed by *settled*. And then there was C.C., which appeared only four times but was attached, every time, to sums so large they dwarfed everything else in the column.

*C.C. — £40 — settled.*
*C.C. — £65 — settled.*
*C.C. — £120 — half paid, balance owing.*
*C.C. — final settlement — cleared.*

One hundred and sixty pounds and more, passing through a village where a good year brought a fishing family thirty. Petra stared at the initials until they blurred. Then she set down her pencil, walked to the shelf where Tomas kept the parish almanac among the tide tables, and found what she already knew she would find.

Corrigan, Charles. Justice of the Peace. County magistrate. Chairman of the relief committee.

The pencil rolled off the bench and clattered on the flagstones, and Petra let it lie.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in the workshop with the ledger open and the practice page before her and watched the candle gutters and understood, with the slow terrible clarity of the small hours, what Tomas had built.

Not a record of crimes. An instrument.

Every entry cut both ways. The same page that proved the O'Shea widow took smuggled tea proved also that without that tea her children would have gone hungry through two winters. The same figures that could hang the Byrne brothers could show a magistrate taking payments four times the value of anything the fishermen ever moved. If Quill got this book, Corrigan might hang — or Corrigan, who was already circling the inquiry like a gull after offal, would get his hands on it first and buy his way clear while the little names paid the whole bill.

Her husband had died holding a weapon pointed in every direction at once. Insurance against the living. That's what it was. Not sentiment, not history — leverage. He had written down every debt in Carrickmoor, honest and dishonest alike, because a man who holds everyone's account holds everyone.

And now the book was hers.

Petra looked at her own hands in the candlelight — ink-stained, chapped, competent hands, the hands of a woman who had never wanted power over anything larger than a lighthouse log. They trembled slightly. She despised them for it.

She fed a fresh sheet to the little forge stove, the one she used to warm sealing wax, and practiced burning a corner of it. Just to see. The paper caught quickly, curled, went to ash in four seconds. Four seconds from evidence to nothing. She had imagined destruction as a hard thing, a violent thing. It was not. It was the easiest act available to her, and that ease frightened her more than Corrigan's name in the ledger ever could.

She fed another corner in. Then another. Practicing, she told herself. Learning the speed of it. The stove ate the paper and asked for more, and somewhere in the third scrap she stopped pretending it was practice and admitted she was rehearsing.

That was when the stair creaked.

* * *

Nell stood at the top of the workshop steps in her nightdress, bare feet white on the cold stone, her arms wrapped around herself against the draft. Her eyes went from the stove to the ash in the pan to the ledger, open on the bench, and Petra saw her daughter's face do the arithmetic — quick, silent, complete — that children do better than adults because they haven't yet learned to fudge the totals.

"What are you burning?"

"Scrap paper." Petra closed the ledger, too late, too obviously. "Go back to bed."

"You're practicing." Nell came down the stairs anyway, twelve years old and fearless in the way that only made Petra's chest ache. "You're practicing how fast it burns. I've seen Da do that with letters he didn't want Mam's family reading. You burn the corners first to see how it takes."

"Bed, Nell."

"No." Her voice cracked but held. "You've been down here three nights. You think I don't hear you? You think I don't see the light under the door? You're reading things in his book that aren't debts, and you're burning something, and you won't tell me, and I found the crate in the caves with the shipping mark that matches his handwriting, so don't tell me it's scrap paper."

The word *crate* landed between them like a dropped glass.

Petra rose slowly. Her first instinct — the keeper's instinct, the mother's instinct, the same instinct wearing different coats — was to protect: to fold the truth away, smooth it flat, say something soft about grown-up matters. She had been doing it for three years, since the telegram, since the folded flag, since the morning Nell asked when Da was coming home and Petra said *soon* because the truth wouldn't fit in a child's ear.

But Nell stood in the doorway with her chin up and her fists clenched, and behind her eyes Petra recognized something that terrified her more than Corrigan's name: her own face, twenty-five years younger, refusing to be managed.

"Sit down," Petra said.

Nell sat on the upturned crate by the bench. Petra banked the stove, closed the ledger, and kept her hand flat on its cover.

"There are entries in your father's ledger that aren't about debts. Not the kind we keep. They're in a code of his, dates and tides and initials, and I've spent three nights working them out, and I wish to God I hadn't." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for storm reports. "Your father helped move goods that weren't declared to the customs men. During the war. Through this harbor."

Nell didn't flinch. "Smuggling."

"Yes."

"Like Mrs. O'Shea's tea."

"How do you know about Mrs. O'Shea's tea?"

"I know everything that happens in this village, Mam. Everyone tells the lighthouse keeper's wife things." A beat. "Everyone tells you things because you write them down. That's why you're the one who knows."

The observation hit harder than any accusation could have. Petra turned away, busied her hands with the candle, gave herself a moment.

"There's more," she said. "Some of the initials belong to powerful men. One of them—" she stopped, started again. "There's an entry for a magistrate. Corrigan. Large payments. Repeated."

"The one helping the inquiry officer?"

"The same."

Nell absorbed this the way she absorbed tide tables, silently, completely. Then: "So the book could ruin him."

"It could ruin everyone. Him, us, the O'Sheas, the Byrnes, half the families who bring me eggs at Easter. It depends entirely on whose hands it lands in." Petra finally turned back. "If Officer Quill reads it, maybe justice gets done and maybe the village hangs for feeding itself. If Corrigan reads it, he burns the pages that name him and keeps the ones that ruin us. If nobody reads it—"

"Then why haven't you burned it already?" Nell's voice was quiet and awful in its simplicity. "You've been practicing. I watched you. Why is it still here?"

Because it's the last thing he wrote. Because his hand is in it, even the false hand, especially the false hand — the part of him that schemed and survived and never told me, and burning it means burning the version of him I never knew along with the one I loved. Because destroying it makes me the judge of who deserves protecting, and I never asked for that office.

"Because once it's gone," Petra said instead, "I can't give it to anyone. Not Quill, not Corrigan, not God. Whatever I decide, I decide alone, forever, and there's no putting ash back together."

"So you're keeping it as insurance." Nell said the phrase without knowing she'd coined it, and Petra felt it strike bone. "Against the living. Like he did."

"He didn't—"

"Didn't he? He wrote down everyone's sins in code and hid them in a book of debts and never told you." Nell's eyes were bright and hard. "You always say Da kept the village honest. Maybe he kept it hostage."

"Nell—"

"Someone died the week the last shipment vanished." Her daughter said it fast, as if the words had been waiting behind her teeth for days. "I checked. The last coded entry is October, the week before—" She stopped herself, and Petra watched the child understand, mid-sentence, what the timeline implied, and watched her choose not to say it aloud. Watched her swallow it. Twelve years old, and already swallowing.

The silence stretched until the candle spat.

"Go to bed," Petra said, and this time her voice was not steady, and they both knew the argument wasn't over — that it might never be over, that it had simply adjourned.

Nell stood. At the stairs she paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back with an expression far older than her years.

"You'll have to decide soon," she said. "Quill's filing whether you tell him or not. And Corrigan doesn't wait for people who are busy deciding."

Then she was gone, her footsteps fading up the stone, and Petra was alone with the ledger, the ash, and the knowledge that her daughter had just handed her a deadline.

She opened the book to the Corrigan entries one last time. *£120, half paid, balance owing.* Even a magistrate left debts unfinished. Even power ran on credit.

Outside, the wind had begun to rise again, coming round to the southeast, and far off — or perhaps only in her bones, where all the year's storms had taken up residence — Petra heard the sea change its mind and start gathering itself for what was coming.

She blew out the candle and carried the ledger upstairs, and slept with it beneath her bed like a loaded gun.
