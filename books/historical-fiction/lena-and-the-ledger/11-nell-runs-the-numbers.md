## 11. Nell Runs the Numbers

Nell had learned the code the way she learned everything — by pretending not to care until nobody was watching.

It started in October, when the nights came early and her mother spent them hunched over Tomas Halloran's ledger with a lamp turned low, as if the book might read her back. Nell would sit on the stairs with a blanket and a stub of pencil, doing sums for school, or appearing to. Really she was watching her mother's finger travel the columns. Dates. Tides. Initials. A second handwriting threaded through her father's like weed through netting — smaller, slanted, pressed hard into the paper by someone who didn't want to be seen writing at all.

Her father's hand she knew well. He'd taught her her letters on the backs of old shipping manifests, big looping capitals, patient corrections. The other hand was nothing like it. That was the first thing she understood: two people had written this book.

The second thing took longer.

She copied samples onto the flyleaf of her arithmetic primer, where no one looks — a page of sums is invisible to adults the way weather is invisible to fish. She worked out that the dates were not random. They clustered. Every cluster sat three days after a spring tide, which meant three days before one, which meant — she checked against the almanac her father kept under his bed, its spine cracked soft from use — the shipments moved when the water was lowest and the caves were walkable.

Initials next. T.O., M.B., S.O., J.C., over and over, and once, in the tight second hand, *P.H.* — which made her stomach drop until she realized it couldn't be Mother, because Mother never went down to the caves alone, because Mother was always here, always here, the lamp turning, the ledger open. It had to be her father himself. Petra Halloran. Papa signing himself by his own name in a stranger's code, like a man marking his own grave.

She should have stopped there. She was twelve; she knew about stopping places the way you know about the edge of a cliff, from being told. But the numbers kept pulling. Numbers always did. Give Nell Halloran a column of figures and an unexplained gap and she would worry it like a loose tooth until it came free.

The gap was the worst part. Pages torn from the ledger's middle section — she could see the stubs where they'd been cut, close to the spine, with a blade. Someone had removed them carefully, deliberately, and her mother either didn't know or wasn't saying. So Nell did what any reasonable person would do with a hole in a record.

She rebuilt it.

* * *

It took her eleven days.

She reconstructed from the edges, the way you mend a net — find where the mesh breaks and work inward. The visible entries gave her the rhythm: every coded shipment logged within a day or two of a supply run that never appeared in the harbor master's book. She borrowed the harbor master's ledger itself, or rather Mrs. Byrne lent her the pages her late husband had copied out for his own accounts, because Mrs. Byrne liked Nell and thought she wanted them "for school." From those she built a list of every boat that had come into Carrickmoor between March and September of 1917, and matched boats to initials. T.O. was Tom O'Leary, who ran the *Kestrel*. M.B., Michael Byrne, dead now at Jutland but alive then, running flour and brandy and — the entries got specific — rifle cartridges marked *machinery*. S.O. was Mrs. O'Shea herself. And J.C. —

J.C. she wrote down and then covered with her palm, because she knew who J.C. was. Everyone knew. James Corrigan, the magistrate, whose motorcar came through the village twice a year trailing dust and favors.

A magistrate doesn't smuggle, she told herself. A magistrate stops smuggling. But the entries said otherwise, said it in tidy slanted ink, amounts received against amounts passed through, and the amounts were not small.

Then there was the last entry. Dated the fourteenth of August, 1917, in the second hand: a shipment marked only with a question mark, and beneath it, in her father's looser script, almost too light to read: *held pending word.*

No follow-up. No confirmation. The code simply stopped, mid-breath, like a sentence interrupted.

Nell sat with that for a long time in the cold of her room, the primer open on her knees, the sea working at the rocks below.

Her father had died on the nineteenth of August, 1917. Five days after the last entry. The official account — the account Mother gave the priest, the coroner, the neighbors — was that he slipped on the seawall coming back from checking the moorings in fog, struck his head, and went into the water. An accident. A keeper's hazard. Everyone had said how fitting it was, terrible as it was, a lighthouse man taken by the sea.

But held pending word. A shipment that never arrived. A man waiting for something in the dark, five days later, on a wall above the water.

She did the arithmetic again, though she didn't need to. She'd known since the cave — since the crate stamped with the same shipping mark she'd found tucked in the ledger's margins — that the story had a seam in it. Now she had the date. The seam had a date, and the date was the week her father died.

She put the primer under her mattress and lay awake listening to the house breathe, and somewhere below, her mother's lamp went out very late.

* * *

She meant to wait. She planned to wait, actually — she had a whole strategy, weeks of it, gentle questions arranged in ascending order like steps. But strategies are for people who can sleep, and Nell could not sleep, and so the next morning she came downstairs while her mother was grinding the first polish into the workshop lens, took the ledger off its shelf, and laid it open on the kitchen table between the bread tin and the salt.

"I need to show you something," she said. "And I need you not to tell me I'm wrong, because I've checked everything twice."

Petra came in wiping her hands, saw the ledger, and went very still. Nell had seen her mother still before — still as deep water when something passes underneath. "That book stays in the workshop."

"It stays wherever I need it to stay." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Page forty-one. Look at the margin marks. Then look at the tide tables for the same days. Then tell me Papa wasn't keeping two books inside one book."

Her mother didn't move toward the table. "Who taught you to look for that?"

"Nobody. That's the point. Nobody teaches you anything in this house, so I teach myself. You're always reading it at night with the lamp low like it's going to confess something. Well." Nell turned the pages herself, fast, to the torn stubs. "Somebody confessed already. Somebody cut these out. And I put them back."

"You put them back." Petra's voice was flat, careful, the voice she used on the radio in bad weather.

"From the edges. Boats, tides, initials. I've got most of it. Not the amounts — the amounts are gone unless somebody kept a copy, and I don't think anybody did, which maybe was the whole idea." She pulled the arithmetic primer from behind the bread tin, where she'd staged it that morning, and opened it to her reconstruction. Columns in pencil, dated, cross-referenced, the question-mark shipment boxed at the bottom in red because she'd wanted it to look like what it was. "Fourteenth of August, Papa writes *held pending word*. Nineteenth of August, Papa dies. Five days. The last shipment never shows up anywhere — not the harbor book, not the code, nowhere. Either it sank, or somebody took it, or—" She stopped, because the next words were a door she couldn't unopen. She opened it anyway. "Or Papa found out something about it and somebody decided he shouldn't keep records anymore."

The kitchen clock ticked. Outside, gulls argued over something on the rocks. Her mother stood with one hand braced on the dresser, and Nell watched her face do several things quickly and settle on none of them.

"You're twelve years old," Petra said finally.

"I'm aware."

"You don't know what you've built there. You think it's sums. It isn't. It's rope, and if the wrong hands pull it, it hangs people."

"Maybe it hangs the right people." Nell heard her own voice go high and hated it, pushed it back down. "J.C. is in here, Mam. Do you understand? Magistrate Corrigan is in Papa's book taking payments. And now he's sniffing around our house being polite to you while Officer Quill does his homework for him. You want me to pretend the sums don't say what they say?"

"Yes," Petra said.

Just that. One word, quiet, no flinch in it, and it landed harder than shouting would have.

"Why?"

"Because some true things cost more than they pay. Because your father is dead and cannot be helped by being made into a mystery. Because Corrigan is dangerous in ways I understand and you don't, and every piece of paper you've just shown me is a piece of paper he would burn this house down to get." Her mother crossed to the table at last, looked down at the primer, at the neat red box, and Nell saw her hand tremble once before it stilled. "Where else have you copied this?"

"Nowhere."

"Nell."

"Nowhere. There's one. It's mine. That's why I'm showing *you* and not Quill, aren't you glad, aren't I clever, aren't I—"

"Stop." Petra closed her eyes. When she opened them she looked, for the first time Nell could remember, tired all the way down, older than the sea. "Sit down."

Nell sat.

Her mother sat across from her, the ledger between them like a third person at the table, and for a moment neither spoke. The fire ticked. Somewhere below the floor the tide filled its long throat.

"When your father died," Petra said slowly, "I believed it was an accident. For months, I believed it, because believing it was the only way to get up in the morning and trim the wick and sign the register. And then I found the second handwriting, and I stopped sleeping, and I will tell you what I have not told anyone, including myself, out loud." She put her flat hand on the ledger. "I don't know what happened on that seawall. I don't know whether he slipped, or whether he was waiting for a boat, or whether he knew too much. I have lain awake building every version and I cannot prove any of them, and the not-knowing has eaten more of me than grief ever did. So yes — I want the sums not to say what they say. Because if they say it, then your father may have been murdered for keeping accounts, and we are keeping accounts, and there is a magistrate with a motorcar who knows exactly what we are."

Nell's eyes were burning. She refused to blink. "So we burn it? Like you burned the practice page? I saw the ash in the forge, Mam, I'm not stupid, I'm good at numbers and numbers don't lie. If you burn it, we'll never know. About him. Ever. Is that mercy or is that just being afraid with extra steps?"

The silence stretched so long the kettle began its small complaint on the hob.

"Both," Petra said at last. "Mercy and fear live in the same house. They always have." She reached across and touched the red box with one fingertip, not quite touching the pencil. "Your papa used to say the ledger never judged anyone. It just remembered. I used to think that was wisdom." Her mouth twisted. "Now I think it was a warning."

"So what do we do?"

"We do what keepers do. We watch. We don't light anything we can't put out." Her mother stood, and gathered the ledger, and paused at the door with her back half-turned. "You keep your copy hidden. And Nell — if Corrigan comes here again, or Quill, or anyone with an official hat — you are a girl doing arithmetic for school. Can you be that?"

"I've been that my whole life," Nell said. "It's easy. Nobody checks."

Petra left. Nell stayed at the table with the cooling fire and her primer, and after a while she turned to a fresh page and wrote at the top, in small careful letters, the one figure she hadn't yet dared to work out properly:

*Days between last entry and death: 5.*
*Days since death: 451.*
*Days since anyone asked the right question: all of them.*

She was still staring at it when she heard hooves on the lane — heavy, unhurried, wrong for a post horse — and through the window she saw a motorcar standing at their gate, steam rising off its bonnet into the grey air, and Magistrate Corrigan climbing out with his gloves already folded over one arm, smiling up at the lighthouse like a man arriving at a property he considered half his.

He had brought a wooden case under one arm. Nell, who was good at numbers, noticed it was the exact size of a ledger.
