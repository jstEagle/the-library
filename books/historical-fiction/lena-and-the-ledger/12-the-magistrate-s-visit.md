## 12. The Magistrate's Visit

He came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult in itself. Bad news, in my experience, prefers the dignity of a Sunday.

I was in the workshop when Nell called down from the gallery that there was a motorcar on the cliff road. We saw perhaps four motorcars a year in Carrickmoor, and none of them had ever come for anything good. I wiped my hands on the rag I kept hooked at my apron — old habit; Tomas had kept one too, and I'd caught myself using it the way he did, tucked under the same finger — and went out to meet whatever the century had sent us.

The car was black and long and too clean for our roads. The man who unfolded himself from it was not what I expected, though I couldn't have said what I'd expected instead. Magistrate Corrigan was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, dressed in a good grey coat that had been cut for a heavier man and now hung on him with a certain elegant neglect, as if he wanted you to notice that he'd once been prosperous and had since suffered for his country. He wore no hat despite the wind. His hair was combed straight back and the wind made nothing of it, which told me it was thicker than it looked or pinned with vanity.

"Mrs. Halloran." He pronounced it correctly, HALL-o-ran, not the way the mainlanders did. "Augustus Corrigan. Forgive the intrusion."

"You're forgiven," I said. "You haven't intruded yet."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. A ledger entry, I thought later. He noted me, and filed me, and the entry read: *quicker than reported.*

"I've been meaning to make the acquaintance of Carrickmoor's most famous widow," he said. "They speak of you in Dublin, you know. The woman who runs the light and the village both."

"They exaggerate."

"They always do." He turned, unhurried, and looked out at the harbor as if appraising it. "A fine anchorage. Deep water close in. You could bring a vessel of some size around the point at night if you knew the tide tables, and if the light happened to be showing a particular sequence." He said it the way a man remarks on the weather. Then he faced me again, pleasant as Sunday. "But then, everyone here knows the tides. That's rather the charm of the place."

Behind me, the workshop door stood open. Through it he could see the bench, the polishing cloths, the half-dressed lens waiting in its cradle of felt. He looked past me at it for exactly two seconds — long enough for me to know he'd counted everything, short enough to pretend he hadn't.

"May I come up?" he asked. "The wind is making a liar of my coat."

* * *

I gave him tea because refusing would have been a confession of something, though I couldn't have said what. He sat in Tomas's chair by the window with his cup balanced on his knee, and he admired the room the way a man admires a room he is already pricing.

"This was your husband's?" He nodded at the chair.

"It was."

"And the ledger business. That was his also."

"The ledger is mine now."

"So I understand. They say you can tell any soul in this parish what they owe and what they're owed, to the penny, back eleven years." He sipped. "Eleven years. Your husband began it young."

"He began it when we came here."

"A record of debts and favors." He let the words sit in the air between us like fish left out overnight. "An extraordinary document, Mrs. Halloran. Extraordinary things get written down in ordinary books. And ordinary things get written in extraordinary ways, if a man is careful." He set the cup down. Its porcelain click against the saucer was very small and very loud. "I'll be plain with you, because I respect plainness in a woman who keeps accounts. There is an inquiry. It will arrive at your door within days, wearing a uniform and carrying forms. Officer Quill — yes, I know his name, you needn't confirm it — is a diligent man. Diligent men are useful and dangerous in equal measure, because they cannot be steered, only delayed. And when his report goes up the chain, do you know what happens?"

"I imagine it gets read."

"It gets *acted upon.*" He leaned forward slightly. "Names are extracted. Warrants are drafted. A village of three hundred souls becomes a list of defendants. The O'Sheas. the Byrnes. The Kellys. Half the harbor, Mrs. Halloran, fed itself through the blockade years, and the law does not distinguish between the man who ran the cargo and the widow who took flour off it so her children wouldn't eat chalk. To a court they are all smugglers. To a newspaper they are all a *ring.*"

"We're none of those things," I said, "if the evidence doesn't exist."

His eyes came up slowly, and for the first time I saw the thing underneath the courtesy, and I understood that the courtesy was not a mask over it but a leash on it, and leashes can slip.

"Precisely," he said softly. "If the evidence doesn't exist."

* * *

Nell was supposed to be at school. She was, in fact, behind the stair door with a book she wasn't reading — I could see her shadow through the gap, could see it go still whenever the conversation went quiet. I did not send her up. Some part of me wanted her to hear it. Some part of me has never entirely forgiven that part.

"There is a version of events," Corrigan went on, settling back into Tomas's chair as though it had learned to hold him, "in which this inquiry finds nothing. In which Officer Quill interviews a cooperative keeper, inspects a well-kept lighthouse, and files a report stating that the rumors regarding Carrickmoor are the product of wartime nerves. No charges. No lists. No widows in the dock. That version requires only one thing." He paused, delicately, as if offering me a sweet. "The ledger. Given to me. Today."

"Not to Quill."

"Officer Quill is honest," Corrigan said, with a small sigh, as though honesty were a chronic ailment. "An honest man with a complete ledger must act on a complete ledger. I am not required to be honest in quite the same way. I am required to be *discreet*, and discretion, properly applied, protects everyone. Even you." He smiled then, fully, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and it frightened me more than anything he'd said before, because it was practiced. A man who has smiled that smile across a hundred rooms has learned exactly how much teeth to show.

"And if I decline?"

"Decline?" He looked genuinely puzzled, a performance so smooth I almost believed it. "Mrs. Halloran, I'm offering to save your village. What is there to decline? Unless—" and here his voice dropped into something almost kind, almost gentle, the voice a doctor uses over a grave, "—unless the ledger contains something you'd rather an honest man didn't read. Something in another hand, perhaps. Entries you don't fully understand yourself. Because if that's the case, then giving it to me isn't betrayal. It's mercy. Toward yourself. Toward your daughter." He glanced toward the stair door, briefly, precisely, and my blood went cold, because he knew she was there. He had known since the motorcar stopped. "Toward the girl who will carry your name into a village where her father died a smuggler and her mother died an accomplice, unless someone with influence decides otherwise."

The fire ticked in the grate. Out at sea a gull cried once and gave it up, the way gulls do before weather.

"Why do you want it?" I asked.

"For the reasons I've given."

"No." I stood. My legs were steadier than I expected them to be. "You've given me a dozen reasons and none of them fit together. An honest man doesn't motor five hours up a coast road to intercept an inquiry he could redirect with a letter. A discreet man doesn't name names over tea in a stranger's kitchen. You want that book the way a man wants a gun he knows is pointed at him. There's something in it about you, Magistrate, and you didn't come to save Carrickmoor. You came to save yourself, and you're asking me to burn down my village to keep your smoke from showing."

For a long moment he said nothing at all. The pleasantness drained out of his face not quickly but gradually, like a tide going out, and what remained was neither rage nor threat but something worse: interest. Recalculation. A man finding that the goods were not as advertised and adjusting his offer accordingly.

"Tomas Halloran understood arithmetic," he said at last. Quietly. Almost sadly. "That's why he lived as long as he did."

My heart did something complicated and painful. I kept my face still. Eleven years of keeping a dead man's books teaches you stillness.

"He kept insurance," Corrigan continued, rising, smoothing his coat. "Everyone in that trade did. A page here, a date there, enough to make sure that if ever the law came for the little people, the big people came with them. Mutual ruin as a guarantee of mutual silence. It worked beautifully for years." He put on his hat now, finally, and the transformation was remarkable: hatted, he became entirely the magistrate, the protocol, the state itself standing in my kitchen. "Then Tomas died, and his insurance passed to a widow, and widows are sentimental, and sentiment is the one asset that cannot be hedged." He settled the brim. "I will call again, Mrs. Halloran. Before Quill does. Think about which of us the village can better afford to owe."

At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"Your lamp mechanism is cracked, I'm told. The requisition order for the workshop is already drawn. Storms are coming — the barometer has been falling all morning, and I do read instruments." Now he turned, and gave me the smile again, the good one, the priced one. "Winter is a difficult season for a woman alone with a secret. Things break. Papers go damp. Fires take a page or two if you're careless. But a whole book, Mrs. Halloran — a whole book surviving intact through everything that's coming — well. That would require a keeper."

The door closed behind him with the softness of a lid.

* * *

I waited until the motorcar sound faded down the cliff road. Then I went to the stair door and opened it, and Nell looked up at me with her father's gray eyes and her chin set in a line I recognized from the mirror.

"You heard."

"Some." She closed the book on her finger. "Mam. Is there something in the ledger about him? About Corrigan?"

I thought about lying to her. I had become, in the space of three weeks, a woman who considered lying to her daughter the way other women consider a second sugar — casually, guiltily, more often than before. But she'd heard the magistrate say *insurance,* and Nell missed nothing that involved numbers, and lies to Nell had a way of surfacing later with interest accrued.

"There may be," I said. "I haven't finished reading it."

"Then finish faster than he does." She said it simply, the way she'd say *the tide's turning.* She gathered her book and went up to the gallery, and I heard her settle at the little table under the lamp where she did her figures, and the scratch of her pencil started, steady as a metronome.

I stood in the kitchen where a magistrate had drunk my tea and threatened me with weather, and I understood at last — fully, in my bones, the way you understand a riptide only after it has taken you off your feet — why Tomas had interleaved those coded pages among the honest debts. Not greed. Not adventure. Insurance. A dead-man's switch sewn into a household account book, because in Carrickmoor the only safe place for a dangerous truth was inside a boring one, and the only person safe holding it was a woman nobody imagined could be dangerous at all.

He'd known he might die carrying it. He'd bet that I would keep it. And the terrible thing, the thing I carried up the stairs to the ledger box that night, was that he'd bet correctly.

Outside, the barometer needle had begun its long slide eastward, and the first white horses were showing past the point, and the wind had found the new note it makes in November, the one like a hinge that hasn't been oiled. Three days, maybe four, before the storm Corrigan had promised me arrived on its own schedule, indifferent to us both.

I unlocked the box and took out the ledger and set it on the table between the lamp and the sea, and I began to read.
