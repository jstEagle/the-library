## 13. Second Storm

The barometer began falling at noon, and by three it had dropped further than Petra had ever seen it fall in nineteen years of keeping the light.

She stood in the lamp room with the instrument's brass face in her hand and watched the needle pressed hard against the limit of its travel, as if it were trying to escape the dial altogether. Below her, Carrickmoor harbor was emptying. The herring fleet had run for shelter at dawn, all but two boats whose skippers had gambled on one last haul, and now she could see them beating back in against a sea that was already white-headed, already wrong.

"Nell!" Her voice cracked across the gallery. "Nell, come up!"

Her daughter came out of the workshop below with grinding paste to her elbows, blinking into the wind. They had worked through the morning on the backup lens carriage, Nell holding parts while Petra filed, and there was graphite ground so deep into the girl's knuckles it looked like age.

"Barometer," Petra said, and showed her.

Nell read it the way Tomas had taught her, lips moving over the numbers. "That's worse than the October storm."

"That's worse than any storm I've logged." Petra set the instrument back in its bracket. "Get your oilskins. Then I want the workshop shut down proper — every tool stowed, nothing loose. If the sea comes through the lower door again, I don't want it taking my files with it."

"And after?"

"After, you come up here and you don't leave until I say."

* * *

It came ashore just after dark, and it did not arrive so much as it arrived upon them — no long build of wind, no warning gusts, just a wall of it that struck the tower like something thrown by an enormous hand. The whole structure shuddered. In the lamp room, the great lens swayed in its carriage with a sound like a held breath.

Petra had weathered storms that bent railings and stripped slates and once drove a fishing smack clean onto the road above the harbor. She had thought she knew what the sea could do.

By ten o'clock she knew she had been wrong.

The waves coming off the shoals were not waves anymore; they were hills of water traveling at the speed of a train, and when they hit the seawall the concussion came up through the rock itself, through the tower's foundations, into the soles of her boots. Spray went over the lamp room — actual spray, salt water flung a hundred and thirty feet into the air — and streamed down the lantern glass in ropes.

"We can't see anything," Nell shouted. She was braced against the pedestal, both hands locked on it. Beyond the glass, the world was black churn. "Even if a boat was out there—"

"Then we make sure they see us." Petra checked the clockwork. The rotation mechanism was Tomas's pride, a beautiful arrangement of weights and gearing that turned the lens with a patience no human hand could match. It was running true. Whatever else the night took, the light would turn. "That's the whole job, Nell. Everything else is weather."

At half past eleven the light failed anyway.

There was no drama to it. The clockwork gave a single clunk, deep in its housing, like a door closing somewhere downstairs, and the lens stopped turning, and the beam froze where it pointed — out toward the open Atlantic, useless to anyone in the harbor approach.

Petra had the housing open in under a minute. She got the inspection lamp lit, angled it down into the gear train, and saw it: a crack running through the main drive wheel, clean as a scribe's line, the teeth sprung apart where the metal had finally given. Fatigue, probably. Nineteen years of turning. Or maybe the storm's shaking had found a flaw that had been waiting all along.

She stared at it and felt something worse than fear. She felt foolish. All autumn she had raced to finish the workshop repairs, filing and polishing and fitting, telling herself that if peace came the requisition would die and the work would matter. And the one piece she had left undone — the spare drive wheel, half-machined on the bench below — was the piece that would have saved this night.

"The backup," Nell said. "Can we crank the backup?"

The backup lens was the old fixed light from before Tomas's time, mounted low on the landward side, meant to be shown only if the main apparatus died completely. It had no clockwork. It had a crank. It had a handle designed to be turned by two people, in shifts, all night if necessary.

"It's not aligned for the harbor channel," Petra said. "Tomas never finished the mounting adjustment." Even as she said it she heard how it sounded — another unfinished thing, another entry in the ledger of what her husband had left undone. She looked at her daughter. "But it turns. Go down and fetch the crank handles. Both of them. And bring the oil can."

* * *

They rigged the inspection lamp beside the backup lens and Petra swung the old reflector around by feel, guessing at the bearing toward the harbor mouth, knowing she was guessing, hating herself for guessing. Somewhere out in that blackness were the two boats that hadn't made it in before dark. Somewhere beyond them, God knew what else — the mail packet out of Dunmore, due on any tide, running blind.

"All right." She fitted the first crank handle into its socket. "You turn, I steer. Slow and even. Not fast — fast makes it wobble and the beam jumps. Think of it as grinding paste."

"I hate grinding paste."

"I know. Turn."

The beam swept the darkness. Once. Twice. On the third sweep, Petra put her shoulder into the mount and hauled the whole assembly two degrees north, watching the shoreline lights through the rain-lashed glass, correcting, correcting. It was like steering a cart with a broken axle. The old bearings screamed.

They fell into a rhythm almost without deciding to. Nell cranking, Petra trimming the wick, adjusting the mount, calling corrections over the wind. When Nell's arms gave out — twenty minutes, then fifteen, then ten between rests — they swapped, and Petra learned what her daughter had been learning: that the crank did not care about grief or guilt or magistrates. It cared about arms and breath and whether you kept turning.

Somewhere past two in the morning, Nell stopped mid-stroke.

"Mam." Her voice was strange. "Look. Harbor side."

Petra leaned to the glass. Down through the murk, at the edge of visibility, she could see the harbor — and she could see why Nell's voice had gone strange. The seawall was breaking. Great sections of the old stone simply lifted and folded inward under the seas, and behind them the boats in the inner harbor surged and strained at their moorings like animals in a burning barn. And beyond the wall, out on the shoals—

A light. A rocket, climbing, red, dying.

"There's a boat on the shoal," Nell said. "Mam, there's a boat—"

"I see it." Petra's hands were already moving, swinging the backup lens, trying to lay its crude beam down the channel line, knowing even as she did it that the old light was too weak, too misaligned, too everything. "Keep cranking. Don't stop cranking. If they can see us at all, we don't get to blink."

"They'll never read that beam. It's jumping all over—"

"Then it jumps toward them!"

The second rocket went up and this time, in its red glare, Petra saw the boat itself — the *Marguerite*, Byrne boys' boat, one of the two that had gambled on the last haul, driven hard aground on the outer shoal with the sea making boards of her. Figures on her deck, tiny, clinging.

And the backup lens chose that moment to seize.

The old bearing, starved and abused, locked solid with a shriek. The crank handle kicked back out of Nell's grip and spun free across the lamp room floor. The beam died to a glow inside the housing, and out on the shoal the *Marguerite* became a shape in darkness with rockets for a voice.

For one full second Petra stood in the ringing dark and understood, completely, what failure was.

Then she moved.

"Oil," she said. "Every drop. The good whale oil, not the mineral." She was already down the stairs two at a time, into the workshop, hauling the oil store open, filling the reserve reservoir, her hands doing the arithmetic of desperation — enough oil for hours, but hours meant nothing if the light couldn't turn, and the light couldn't turn because the bearing was dead, and the bearing was dead because—

Because the work wasn't finished. Because she had run out of autumn before she ran out of list.

She came back up with the oil and with something else: the half-machined spare drive wheel off the bench, still bright-cut and unfinished, wrapped in rag. She set it on the pedestal next to the seized backup mount and looked from it to the dark window and back.

"Mam?" Nell had the crank handle in both hands, white-knuckled, waiting for orders.

Petra put her palm flat on the seized housing. Hot. The bearing had welded itself with friction. She thought about Tomas's notebooks, his margin notes on expansion and clearance, his patient little diagrams. She thought about the difference between a machine that is maintained and a machine that is merely owned.

"We flood it with oil and work it," she said. "Gently. Back and forth, never forcing. If we can break it loose without scoring it—" She didn't finish, because finishing would have been a promise, and she was done promising things she couldn't keep.

They worked it together. Oil, then pressure, then ease, then oil again. The housing smoked faintly. Nell's face in the inspection lamp's light was gray with exhaustion and streaked with oil and absolutely set, and Petra thought: *that is my child, and she has her father's stubbornness and none of his secrets, and tonight that is the better inheritance.*

On the shoal, a third rocket.

"Come on," Petra whispered to the machine. "Come on, you stupid faithful old thing."

The bearing gave with a crack like a knuckle, and the crank turned, and the beam leapt out across the water — wild, wobbling, alive.

* * *

They turned it until dawn.

Not steadily — the mount was ruined, the alignment hopeless, the beam lurching across the sky like a drunk with a torch. But it moved, and it flashed, and it flashed, and sometime in the black heart of the night, between one sweep and the next, the rockets from the shoal changed character: no longer distress signals but answering ones, the Coastguard's, fired from the cliff above the harbor where men had assembled with breeches buoy gear and were working their way down toward the water in the brief strobing glimpses the lurching light gave them.

Petra never learned exactly what the light had bought them. What she learned, standing in the lamp room at dawn with her arms dead and her hands blistered and her daughter asleep upright against the pedestal, was what the village learned by noon, told and retold in the church and the street and the O'Shea kitchen:

The *Marguerite* lost her deck cargo and her stern and nearly her crew. But the crew lived — all four, hauled up the cliffs on lines in the worst hours of the night — and every man of them swore afterward that what steered the rescuers' hands, what marked the channel when nothing else in God's world could be seen, was the crazy jumping light from the Halloran tower.

The other boat, the *Kitty Darby*, was not so fortunate. She went to pieces on the inner shoal before midnight, and her skipper, old Michael Doyle, was taken by the sea within sight of his own front door. They found him at low tide among the wrack, and they carried him up through the village under a tarpaulin while the storm, spent, drizzled indifferent rain.

Petra stood at her door and watched the procession go past, and Nell stood beside her, and neither of them spoke. There was nothing to say that the sea had not already said more plainly. The light they had kept had saved four men and failed one, and both of those facts belonged to them now, permanently, the way entries belong to a ledger — written in a hand that cannot be unsaid.

* * *

In the afternoon, Quill came up the tower path.

He looked like a man who had been sandbagging since midnight, which he had; his coat was stiff with dried salt mud, and there was a bandage on one hand. He stood in the doorway of the lamp room with his hat in his hands and looked at the seized backup mount, the cracked drive wheel laid out on the bench, the smell of burnt oil, and said nothing for a long moment.

"The clockwork's gone," Petra said. Her voice was flat with tiredness. "Properly gone. The backup's ruined — bearing's scored through. I can't show a reliable light tonight or any night until I have parts, and there are no parts, because the requisition office has my workshop earmarked for scrap instead of repair." She heard the bitterness come up in her own voice and let it. She was too tired to hold it down. "So there it is, Officer. That's the state of Carrickmoor's light. Write it in your report however you like."

Quill picked up the cracked drive wheel. Turned it over. Set it down with surprising gentleness.

"Doyle's wife asked me to come," he said. "She wanted someone official to tell her exactly what happened, and I'm the only official within twelve miles who isn't busy burying something." He looked out the lantern glass at the wreck-strewn harbor. "I told her the truth. The clockwork failed at eleven forty. The backup was misaligned and undersized. And two women turned it by hand until dawn anyway, and four men are alive because of it, and one man is dead despite it, and none of those facts cancel each other out."

"No."

"No." He put his hat back on. At the stair door he paused. "Mrs. Halloran. The requisition order for this workshop — I've seen the paperwork. Corrigan countersigned it. Which means the armistice won't save it; he'll execute it himself the moment it suits him." His eyes went to the ruined machinery, then to her. "You should know that whatever you're going to decide — about the ledger, about everything — the decision is being taken out of everyone's hands faster than any of us planned. Decide soon."

He went down the stairs. Petra listened to his footsteps fade into the sound of the recovering sea.

Nell appeared in the doorway, hair uncombed, eyes still swollen from sleep. She looked at the broken drive wheel, then at her mother.

"The spare," she said. "If we finish machining it tonight — you said yourself the lathe work is mostly done. We could have the clockwork turning again in two days."

Petra looked at her daughter for a long moment.

"Yes," she said. "We could."

"And the requisition—" Nell hesitated. "Mr. Quill said Corrigan countersigned it. Mam, does that mean the magistrate wants our workshop?"

Petra picked up the cracked wheel and held it to the gray light, reading the fracture the way she read everything now, like an entry in a book — looking for what it revealed, and dreading what it confirmed.

"No, love," she said quietly. "It means Corrigan doesn't want us to have one."
