## 15. What Can Be Carried

The Admiralty letter came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for bread and lamp oil and the small honest arithmetic of the house — not for sentences written in government ink.

Petra stood at the kitchen table with the envelope unopened for a full minute before she broke the seal. Outside, the gulls worked the tide line, screaming about nothing. Nell was at school. The fire was low. There was no one to see her hands shake, so she let them.

*Re: requisition of premises, Carrickmoor Light — workshop annex.* She read it twice to be sure of what she was reading, though she had known the shape of it since autumn. *In the event of armistice, all optical workshops engaged in war production will be consolidated under central authority. Premises to be seized upon signing. Equipment inventoried and removed.*

Upon signing. Not upon peace arriving in some slow, human way — but upon the stroke of a pen in a room she would never see, men who had never ground a lens in their lives would come for the workshop where Tomas had taught her to polish glass until it held a flame without bending it.

She set the letter down on the table, weighted it with the salt cellar, and went to fetch the ledger.

If they were going to take everything, then it was time to decide what could be carried.

* * *

She began with the house because the house was easier than the truth.

Tomas's coat first — the heavy wool one with the brass buttons gone dull at the cuffs where his wrists had worn them for eleven winters. It hung on the peg by the door where he'd left it, smelling now of dust and faintly of paraffin, which was the smell of every important thing in her life. She took it down and put her arms into the sleeves just once, foolishly, to feel the length of him across her shoulders. Then she folded it properly, along the seams, the way he never folded anything.

Into the coat's pockets she found: a pencil stub bitten at the end; a ferry ticket dated two years back; a fishing fly tied badly, which meant Nell had made it and he'd carried it anyway. She laid these on the table in a row like evidence. They were evidence. Of what, she couldn't have said to any officer.

Nell's drawings next — from the drawer in the dresser where they'd accumulated since the girl was five: the lighthouse drawn as a woman with a lantern head; a dog they'd never owned; the harbor in blue crayon with every boat named, even the ones that had been lost. Petra sorted through them and kept all of them, because she was learning that she had no discipline when it came to certain things, and better to know that now than later.

Then there was only the ledger left on the shelf, and the morning stretched long ahead of her, and the paper waited the way paper always waits — patiently, and without mercy.

* * *

She sat down with three sheets of good paper, the pen, the ink bottle, and the ledger open beside her, and she began to draft her testimony the way you'd draft anything: badly, then less badly, then honestly.

**The first version was the whole truth.**

She wrote it out plainly, because she owed the plainness at least one attempt. *On the fourth of March, 1916, my husband received a shipment marked with the Kestrel Line stamp, stored in the north sea cave, contents unknown to me. On these dates — and here she copied the coded entries out in full, dates, tides, initials, payments — goods moved through Carrickmoor contrary to blockade regulations. Half the village ate because of it. My husband kept this record. I believe he died because of it, or near enough that the difference doesn't matter.*

She looked at what she'd written. One page. It would take Quill an hour to verify, Corrigan ten minutes to destroy her with. And it would take Mrs. O'Shea's son — home from France with half a lung — and the Byrnes, and the widow herself, and old Denny at the quay, and it would hang them or ruin them by turns, depending on how Corrigan chose to use it, and Corrigan would choose the way that profited Corrigan.

And it would say, in black and white, that Tomas Halloran had been a smuggler, and whatever else that was true, Petra could not make herself hand that sentence to strangers to be read aloud in rooms without windows.

But it was the truth, and the truth cost what it cost. She set it aside and started the second sheet.

**The second version was the partial truth.**

This one was harder, because a partial truth is a constructed thing and she'd never built anything she couldn't hold in her hands. *I kept my husband's ledger of debts and favors. Certain entries are in a hand I do not recognize. I cannot account for them. The debts themselves — flour, mending, lamp oil, kindnesses — are accurate and complete. Beyond that I can testify only to storms weathered and light kept burning.*

It was almost true. It was true in every sentence and false in its shape, the way a net is true thread and false water. It would protect the village and bury nothing, including her husband, and it would leave the coded pages existing somewhere — in her memory, if nowhere else — which meant the danger would keep living in her head for the rest of her life, rent-free, patient.

Cost: it made her a liar with good posture. Cost: it left the magistrate's name sitting in her skull where Corrigan might someday pry it out.

She set it aside. Third sheet.

**The third version was silence.**

*I am the keeper's widow. I kept the light. I kept accounts of flour and fish. I know nothing of contraband.*

Two lines. The easiest to write and the most expensive of all, because silence has interest rates. If she testified to nothing, Quill would be forced to close the inquiry thin and unsatisfied, and thin inquiries get reopened by thicker men. Silence wouldn't protect anyone forever; it would only move the bill forward, addressed to whoever came after her — to Nell, most likely, grown and holding a house full of questions Petra had refused to answer.

She looked at the three sheets lying on the table: the whole truth, the shaped truth, the empty truth. Each one cost somebody. The whole truth cost the village. The partial truth cost her own soul a little at a time. The silence cost Nell.

There was no version that cost nobody. That was the discovery of the morning, and she sat with it while the kettle went cold on the stove, and understood at last why Tomas had kept the ledger as insurance instead of burning it himself. He hadn't been protecting himself. He'd been trying to find a version that cost nobody, and he'd run out of mornings before he found it.

She gathered the three drafts together. She did not burn them yet. Burning was a decision, and decisions made before noon could be regretted by supper. But she folded them into the tin box where she kept the lamp wicks, high on the shelf, and she did not look at the box again for the rest of the day, which was its own kind of lie.

* * *

Nell came home at four with cold hands and salt in her hair, which meant the sea caves again, though Petra no longer asked. There had been a time when asking was possible. That time had drowned somewhere between chapter and chapter of this winter, and mother and daughter had learned to conduct their love across a gap, calling to each other like lights.

"Post?" Nell said, nodding at the Admiralty envelope still weighted under the salt cellar. She'd inherited Tomas's eye for things that didn't belong.

"The government," Petra said. "They want the workshop."

Nell went very still, the way she did — Tomas's stillness, exact and total. "Want it for what?"

"For the lenses. For the war." Petra heard herself using the future tense out of habit. "For whatever comes after the war."

"But the armistice—"

"Is exactly when they'll come. Peace means they don't need us anymore." She said it gently, because there was no gentle way to say it. "When the signing happens, they seize the annex. Inventory, removal. Everything."

Nell pulled off her boots slowly. "The lens we're repairing. The big one. The one for the—" She stopped herself, but too late; Petra had seen the thought go past behind her eyes like a boat running dark.

"The backup lens," Petra finished quietly. "Yes."

Neither of them said the rest of it: that during the second storm, with the mechanism cracked and ships on the shoals, it had been that unfinished lens, hand-cranked by the two of them hour after hour, that had held the light. That the government's inventory listed it as equipment, and the storm had known it as a promise. Papers didn't care what things had survived.

"We finish it first," Nell said.

"If we can."

"We finish it first," Nell repeated, in the tone she'd learned from the sea itself, which does not negotiate, and went to wash for supper.

* * *

That night Petra climbed the tower alone, as she did most nights now, to check the light and to be somewhere taller than her problems.

From the gallery, Carrickmoor was a scatter of lamps below: the O'Shea window, the Byrne loft, Denny's place at the quay with its late-burning pipe glow. Forty-one houses. She knew the number because the ledger knew the number, because every one of those houses owed or was owed something, because that was the web Tomas had woven and she had inherited — not the smuggling, whatever the papers said, but the counting. The keeping track. The refusal to let anyone in this village be invisible to the others.

The Admiralty wanted the workshop. Corrigan wanted the ledger. Quill wanted, God help him, to go home. Everyone wanted to carry something away from Carrickmoor.

And what could be carried? She turned the question over the way she'd turn a lens to the light. Tomas's coat, yes. Nell's drawings, yes. The honest debts — those she would carry to her grave, entered fair in a fair hand. The coded pages? Those weighed nothing and everything. She could carry them silently for fifty years. She could put them down tomorrow. She could not, she realized, standing in the wind with the great lamp turning its steady circle above her, do either one yet — because the armistice had not signed, and Corrigan had not given up, and until the game ended she could not tell which pieces she'd still need on the board.

Below her, a light appeared on the coast road — a single lantern swinging fast toward the village, too fast for a fisherman, too late for the post. It stopped at the O'Sheas'. It stopped at the Byrnes'. Someone was walking the village at nine at night, door to door, carrying news or gathering it.

Petra counted the stops. Seven houses. All seven, she noted with a chill that had nothing to do with the wind, were names in the coded entries.

She watched until the lantern went out beyond the last cottage, then went down to check the tin box on its high shelf, the three drafts sleeping inside it like unexploded shells.

In the morning there was a knock at the door before breakfast. Nell answered it and came back white-faced, holding a second official envelope — customs seal, same heavy paper, addressed this time not to *The Halloran Family* but to *P. Halloran, Keeper*, in a hand Petra recognized from the magistrate's office.

Corrigan knew about the Admiralty letter. Which meant Corrigan had friends inside the Admiralty. Which meant the deadline she'd been counting on — the signing, the seizure, the end of the game — was not a wall between them after all.

It was a door, and he intended to walk through it first.
