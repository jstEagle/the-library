## 16. The Burning

Petra woke before the alarm and lay still in the dark, listening for the wind.

It had dropped. After three days of gale the sea had gone quiet in the way that frightened her more than any storm — a hush like held breath, the whole coast waiting to see what she would do. She could hear Nell breathing through the wall, deep and even, a child's sleep that trusted the house around it.

She got up without lighting a lamp. She knew the stairs by their sounds: the third tread's complaint, the turn at the landing where Tomas used to set his boots. In the kitchen she dressed by feel — wool stockings, the gray dress, her hair pinned with the efficiency of twenty years of doing it blind. Then she took the ledger down from its shelf.

It was heavier than paper should be. She had thought so since the night she decoded the last of the hidden entries, as if the book itself understood what it carried. Two hundred pages of flour and lamp oil and mended nets. And threaded between them, in that second hand she now knew better than her own — forty-one entries of dates, tides, initials, sums. The true ledger. The insurance. The thing that could hang a fisherman or unseat a magistrate, depending on whose hands held it when dawn came.

Corrigan wanted it. Quill wanted it, though he'd stopped asking. The village needed it gone.

She wrapped it in oilcloth and went out into the dark.

* * *

The workshop stood fifty yards from the tower, down a path the storm had half-buried in shingle. Petra walked it with the ledger under her arm and felt every stone through her boots, the way you feel a road you've traveled your whole life. The forge was at the back of the workshop, small, iron-legged, built for soldering lens frames and heating pitch. It had not been lit since October. She knelt, cleared the ash, laid kindling from the bin — offcuts of pine, dry as bone — and struck a match.

The flame caught and rose, orange against black iron, and threw her shadow huge across the wall of tools. For a moment she just watched it. Fire was honest. It took exactly what you gave it and gave back heat and nothing else. No ledger ever kept accounts like fire did.

She set a kettle on the hook above the coals, because there would need to be tea, because Mrs. O'Shea was coming, because some things were unbearable alone and some were unbearable only with a witness. That was the difference, Petra had learned, between a secret and a sin.

Mrs. O'Shea arrived while the coals were still young, a shawl over her nightdress, her boots unlaced. She came down the path without her lantern, which meant she hadn't wanted anyone seeing where she went at four in the morning, which meant she understood completely. She stepped into the workshop and stopped, looking at the forge, at the ledger on the bench, at Petra's face.

"So," she said.

"You said burn it." Petra kept her voice level. "I'm asking you to stand here while I do."

The widow pulled a crate over beside the forge and sat, unfolding her hands in her lap like a woman settling in for a long sermon. "I'll stand whatever you need stood. But I'll say my piece first, girl, and then we'll get to the burning."

Petra almost smiled at that. *Girl.* Mrs. O'Shea was sixty if she was a day and had called everyone girl since the Flood, probably.

"Say it."

"Tomas didn't keep those pages to protect himself." The widow's voice was low and rough, gravel over water. "You know that by now. He kept them because the day Corrigan or one of his sort turned on us, someone had to have proof of what was done and who ordered it done. Not who carried the crates — who *sent* them. Who took the cut and let the fishermen take the risk." She nodded at the ledger. "He was carrying a weapon, that man of yours. Loaded it page by page. And he died holding it, and now you're the one standing there deciding whether it fires."

"It doesn't fire anymore." Petra opened the ledger flat on the bench. "That's the point. A weapon like this shoots both directions, Brigid. You told me yourself. Half the village is in those margins. The Byrne boys. The Kellehers. Your own name, eleven times." She looked up. "I'm not going to hand Corrigan a list of my neighbors."

"No," Mrs. O'Shea said quietly. "You're going to hand him ash."

"Yes."

"And the magistrate? The one who signed the shipping marks? Who walks free because your dead husband's handwriting is all that names him?"

Petra was silent. This was the wound under the wound, the one she had been pressing her thumb against for two days. Somewhere in London, in Dublin, in whatever room magistrates kept their consciences, Corrigan's superior was signing requisition orders and inquiry briefs and never once thinking of Carrickmoor. Burning these pages meant he stayed safe. It meant Tomas had loaded that weapon for nothing. It meant the truth would go into the fire along with the lies, indistinguishable, the way everything went into the sea eventually.

"He'll walk," Petra said. "For now. And maybe always. But no one else hangs for him. That's the trade. I've made it."

Mrs. O'Shea studied her for a long moment. Then she reached into her shawl and produced a small cloth bundle, setting it on the bench beside the ledger. "Then we do it proper. Bread and salt. Old custom — you don't burn a book without giving it a last meal. My mother did it when the priest made her destroy her grandmother's letters. Said a book remembers being burned otherwise." Her mouth twitched. "Superstition, likely. But we're past choosing what to believe."

Petra broke the bread and laid a piece on the open ledger, and set a pinch of salt beside it, and felt absurd and grave in equal measure, the way you feel at a child's funeral.

Then she began turning pages.

* * *

This was the work she had not let herself imagine until now: the sorting. Because the ledger was not two books, not really. It was one book with two hearts beating in it, and she could not burn the whole thing without killing the good heart too. So she read. Page after page by forge-light, reading aloud sometimes without meaning to, her voice a low murmur under the crackle of the coals.

*"March 4th. O'Shea, E. — flour, 2 lb, repaid in mackerel."*

*"April 19th. Byrne boys, net mended, no charge. Told them God charges nothing for mending and neither do I."*

*"June 2nd. Lamp oil to the church, 1 gal. Father Doyle says he'll square it at Michaelmas. Father Doyle has said so for six years. Let him."*

Each time she came to an honest entry — and they were most of the book, God help them, most of the book was just people owing each other small kindnesses and squaring them late — she took up the razor and slit the page free at the spine and set it on the clean side of the bench. Each time she came to the other hand — the tight, slanted script, the tides and initials, the sums that mapped a war's worth of contraband moving through their harbor under cover of fog and blackout — she set it on the left.

Two piles grew. One of debts. One of deaths.

Nell's drawings were mixed in near the end — the child had used spare pages for sketching ships, cats, a lighthouse with smoke drawn rising from it that Petra chose to read as fog. She set those aside with the honest debts, smoothing each one flat with her palm.

Halfway through, her hands began to shake. She stopped, pressed them flat against the bench, breathed. Mrs. O'Shea said nothing, but moved the kettle off the hook and poured tea into two chipped mugs and put one at Petra's elbow, and that small ordinary act — tea, at four-thirty in the morning, in a workshop smelling of coal and brine — steadied her more than any prayer would have.

"Page sixty-two," Mrs. O'Shea said suddenly. "Check page sixty-two."

Petra found it. An entry in Tomas's own hand, plain, honest: *"Sept. 9th. O'Shea, E. — coal, 1 cwt, on account till Christmas."* But beneath it, in the other hand, faint as a watermark: a date, a tide table, and the initials **R.C.**

Richard Corrigan.

"That's the one," the widow said. "The coal was real. The debt was real. And underneath it, the same night, your husband wrote down what the magistrate was paid to look away from. Both things true on the same page, Petra. That's what this whole war has been. Both things true at once."

Petra looked at the page a long time. Then she slit it free and, after a moment, tore it in half. The honest half she set with the debts. The rest went to the left-hand pile, on top, where she wouldn't have to look at it again.

* * *

By five the sky outside the workshop windows had gone from black to the color of wet slate, and the piles stood finished: a foot of rescued pages bound loosely with string, and a brick of condemned ones squared and tied with the same string, because even the damned deserved order.

Petra stood at the forge. The coals were white-hearted now, hot enough to bend iron, hot enough for anything.

"Grief's a strange tenant," Mrs. O'Shea said, watching her. "Comes into your house and won't pay rent and breaks your crockery. And then one morning it asks you for a job." She nodded at the fire. "There's yours."

Petra lifted the bundle. It weighed nothing and everything. She thought of Tomas writing these entries by lamplight in this very building, hunched over the same bench where her elbows rested now, keeping his double record, loving his village and his family and his dangerous arithmetic all at once. She thought of him going out on the water the last time, and what he must have known, and whether he had looked back at the light.

"I'm sorry," she said to him, out loud, not caring how it sounded. "And thank you. And goodbye."

She fed the pages to the fire.

They did not go easily. Paper resists; it curls, blackens, fights the flame with its whole thin body before surrendering. She fed them a few sheets at a time and stirred the coals with the poker and watched the ink lift off the pages in ghost-shapes before the fire ate the shapes too. Dates. Initials. Sums. R.C. going up in a breath of orange. The tide tables curling like leaves. Somewhere in the middle of it a gust came down the chimney and the flames roared upright and for one instant the whole hidden history of Carrickmoor stood blazing in the air, tall and terrible and briefly beautiful, and then it was sparks, and then it was nothing.

The smell was ink and pine and something sweeter underneath that Petra decided not to identify.

She kept working until the last scrap was ash. Then she raked the forge, and when the ash was cold enough she carried it out in the coal scuttle and scattered it along the path toward the sea, where the tide would come for it within the hour and ask no questions.

When she came back inside, Mrs. O'Shea was binding the surviving ledger — the honest ledger, now, the only ledger — with fresh string, and had laid the bread and salt back on the bench.

"For the new book," she said. "Whatever you write in it next. Start fed and salted."

Petra sat down across from her and drank her tea, gone lukewarm, tasting of coal smoke, the best tea of her life.

* * *

She heard the stair-creak only afterward, reconstructing it: the third tread's complaint, twice, going down and coming back up. Soft. Careful. The particular care of a twelve-year-old who has learned, this winter, exactly how much weight a floorboard can carry and how much a silence can.

Nell had been on the stairs. Nell had seen.

Petra closed her eyes and saw it plainly — her daughter in her nightgown at the top of the workshop steps, hand on the rail, watching her mother feed a book to a fire before dawn with the widow O'Shea standing witness like a gravedigger at a christening. Watching her choose. And watching, and saying nothing, and going back to bed.

She could go up now. Wake her, explain, make it a lesson instead of a wound. *Your father kept secrets, and secrets become weapons, and weapons hurt whoever holds them.* She could give Nell the reasons, tidy as ledger entries.

But Nell was done with her reasons. Nell had run the numbers herself, had reconstructed pages Petra hadn't known existed, had stood in this kitchen and demanded the timeline of her father's death with the flat accuracy of an auditor. If Petra went upstairs now with an explanation, Nell would accept it the way she accepted everything this winter — politely, completely, and with something essential filed away behind her eyes for later.

No. Let her have watched. Let her decide what it meant. That was the inheritance, wasn't it — not the answers but the choosing. Petra's mother had given her a lighthouse and a duty. Petra was giving Nell a fire and a decision already made, and the space to make her own judgment of it.

She gathered the honest ledger under her arm and stood. Outside, the sky had turned the gray of oyster shells, and the tide was coming in below the path, unhurried, certain, already reaching for the ash.

"Go home, Brigid," she said. "Get warm. And Brigid — when the officer comes, and when Corrigan comes, and whenever anyone asks — you never saw me burn anything. You never saw this book at all."

Mrs. O'Shea rose, joints cracking, and pulled her shawl tight. "Saw what?" she said, perfectly blank, and for the first time in weeks Petra laughed — a short, cracked, real laugh that steamed in the cold air.

The widow paused at the door. "One more thing, girl. The magistrate won't take ash for an answer. Men like that don't lose a ledger, they lose face, and they'll burn the coast down looking for the difference." She looked back at the forge, at the rescuing pages bound in string. "So decide what story you're telling him. And tell it like you believe it, because by God, after tonight —"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to.

Petra stood alone in the workshop with the banked fire ticking behind her and the honest ledger under her arm, listening to the sea come in for the ash. Above her, faintly, the third stair creaked again — once, softly, deliberately, the way a knock sounds through a door.

Nell was awake. Nell was waiting. And the sun was coming up on the first morning of whatever Petra Halloran was about to become.
