## 17. Corrigan Closes In

The knock came at seven in the morning, and Petra knew before she opened the door that it would not be Quill. Quill knocked like a man asking permission. This was three blows, evenly spaced, the knock of a man who had never once been told no.

Magistrate Corrigan stood on her step with his collar buttoned to the throat and a leather portfolio under one arm, as if the whole affair might be settled with documents alone. Behind him, on the path, waited two constables from Dunmore — not village men, not faces she knew, which was its own message.

"Mrs. Halloran." He removed his hat, a courtesy performed with the smoothness of long practice. "I regret the hour."

"You regret nothing," Petra said. "Come in, or say it on the step."

Something flickered behind his eyes — the brief recalculation of a man who had expected a widow's flustered deference and received instead a woman standing square in her own doorway with flour on her hands. He came in.

Nell was already gone. Petra had heard the back door ease shut two minutes before the knock; the girl had learned that much, at least, from watching her mother. The ledger was in its place under the floorboard beneath the bread bin, and Corrigan walked past the bin without a glance, because he had already been told what it contained.

Or rather, what it didn't.

"Officer Quill filed an interim report yesterday," Corrigan said, taking the chair by the fire without being offered it. "A thin document. Curiously thin. It mentions debts of flour and mended nets and the price of a coffin for the Byrne boys' father, and it does not mention contraband, shipping marks, or coded entries. I am given to understand that the Halloran ledger — the ledger my inquiry has spent six weeks pursuing — is now" — he opened the portfolio, consulted a page with theatrical care — "'materially incomplete.' Those are Quill's words. Whose hand made it incomplete, Mrs. Halloran?"

"The sea," Petra said. "Damp gets into everything here. You'd know that if you stayed past a fortnight."

"I have stayed eleven days." He closed the portfolio. "And in those eleven days I have read enough of Tomas Halloran's surviving correspondence to know the man kept accounts the way other men keep religion. Damp did not touch them. Fire might have." He let the word sit between them like a coal dropped on the table. "Destroying evidence in an active inquiry carries a sentence of two years, Mrs. Halloran. Obstruction carries another. A magistrate's recommendation can see those served consecutively. Your daughter would be fourteen before you came home."

The fire ticked. Down in the cove, the sea worked at the seawall with its patient, grinding patience, and Petra listened to it the way she had learned to listen — as a clock, as an argument, as the only voice that had never once lied to her.

"You may charge me," she said. "Or you may tell me why a magistrate of the Crown travels four hours by mail coach, uninvited, to pursue a smuggling case so small it fed widows through a blockade. Because I've read the ledger, Mr. Corrigan. All of it. Before it went damp. And there were initials in it that weren't fishermen's."

For one moment — half a moment — the polish cracked. She saw it happen: the stillness of a man whose hand has been called, who must decide whether to show his cards or overturn the table. Then the smile returned, fitted back into place like a lens into its bezel.

"You mistake your position," he said softly. "You are not the investigator here. You are the subject. And subjects do not ask questions." He rose, settling his coat. "As it happens, I haven't come only to threaten you. The Admiralty requisition order on the lamp workshop — I have moved up its execution. The armistice may sign tomorrow or next month; until it signs, the emergency statutes stand, and I intend to use them. Constables will arrive this afternoon to inventory the workshop and seal it. You may appeal to Quill, if you like. He is, after all, the officer of record." At the door he paused, hat in hand, the portrait of procedural courtesy. "Burnt pages make poor testimony, Mrs. Halloran. But ash makes excellent accusation. Think on that."

Then he was gone down the path, and the two strange constables fell in behind him like dogs that had not yet decided whose whistle they answered.

* * *

Quill arrived within the hour. He looked as though he had slept in his coat, which Petra suspected he had done every night since arriving in Carrickmoor, and he came into the kitchen without knocking, which was new, and sat down heavily, which was not.

"He jumped my chain of command," Quill said. "Filed directly to Dublin. That's how I learned about the requisition acceleration — from my own superiors, reading it off a magistrate's letter."

"Are you going to stop him?"

Quill turned his cup slowly on its saucer, one full rotation, then another. Petra watched him and understood that she was watching a man weigh himself.

"When I took this posting," he said, "I was told it was a formality. A coastal village, some wartime grumbling about goods that came ashore without stamps, a report filed, everyone goes home. Nobody mentioned a magistrate with a personal interest. Nobody mentioned that the inquiry's paper trail bent toward Dunmore courthouse every time it got close to anything real." He stopped turning the cup. "I've been slow, Mrs. Halloran. I'll grant you that. But I'm not stupid, and neither are you, and we both know what that ledger said before the damp got it."

Petra said nothing. The silence between them had become, over these weeks, something almost workable — two people keeping different kinds of light, each aware of the other's flame.

"The requisition," she said instead. "Can he do it?"

"He can try. Emergency statute 4-C covers 'facilities material to navigation,' which the workshop technically is — but the seizure requires the officer of record to certify the inventory." Quill's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "He forgot that part. Or he assumed I'd sign whatever he put in front of me. So this afternoon, when his constables arrive, I will be there with my regulation notebook, and I will raise objections. Procedural ones. Small ones. I have become quite good at small objections — they're the only kind a man in my position can afford."

"What kind?"

"Improper notice period. Absence of a second certifying officer. An inventory conducted without the keeper present constitutes grounds for appeal, and appeals take time." He drained his tea and stood. "Days, Mrs. Halloran. I can buy you days. If the armistice signs while the paperwork is still crawling, the whole order dies unsigned and he can't resurrect it." At the door he paused — everyone paused at her door, she thought; it was becoming the most argued-over doorway in County Waterford. "One more thing. Corrigan will come at the village next. Informants, summons, the usual machinery. When he does—"

"They won't talk," Petra said. "Not to him."

Quill looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face seemed to settle something. "No," he said. "I don't suppose they will."

* * *

They came at four o'clock: the two Dunmore constables and Corrigan himself, plus a clerk with an inventory book, marching up the cliff road in the gray afternoon with the wind pulling at their coats. And the village was already there.

Petra saw them from the gallery and felt her breath catch — not at the constables but at the sight below. The O'Shea widow stood at the foot of the lighthouse path with her arms folded, planted like a fence post. Behind her, the Byrne boys, nets over their shoulders though the fishing day was done. Old Mullen with his cart, deliberately parked across the track in the manner of a man whose cart had simply broken down there, indefinitely. A dozen others, and more coming down from the village as word traveled, because word always traveled faster in Carrickmoor than any coach could carry it.

Nobody shouted. Nobody blocked anybody, formally. They simply stood where they stood, and their standing had weight.

"Mrs. O'Shea," Corrigan said pleasantly, reaching the foot of the path. "Perhaps you'd move along."

"Sure it's a public road," the widow said, not moving. "I'm entitled to stand on it same as anyone. Same as yourself, even."

"We're here on Crown business."

"Are you now." The widow's voice was flat as slate. "Well. Crown business was done in this village all through the blockade years, Mr. Corrigan, and nobody sent constables then either. Funny how it's only business when it's ours being counted."

Corrigan turned to the constables. "Clear the path."

But Quill was already stepping forward, notebook open, and his voice cut clean across the wind. "Constable. Before you lay hands on anyone — note the time, note that these persons are assembled peacefully, and note that Officer Quill, designated officer of record under inquiry 114-C, objects to this proceeding on grounds of improper notice, absent co-certification, and conduct prejudicial to a pending appeal. Read it back to me, please, so I'm certain it's entered correctly."

The younger constable hesitated. The older one looked at Corrigan, and Corrigan looked at the crowd — at the widow's folded arms, at Mullen sitting now on his cart seat as though prepared to winter there, at Mrs. Byrne come down with a shawl against the wind and no intention of using it to leave — and Petra, watching from above, saw the arithmetic run behind his eyes. He had brought force enough to clear a path and not nearly enough to arrest a village, and everyone present understood the difference.

"This is obstruction," Corrigan said quietly, to all of them.

"No, sir," said old Mullen, spitting thoughtfully into the wind. "Obstruction would be if we tried to stop ye. We're just standing. It's a free country, they say. Though I'll confess I've heard less of that lately."

A ripple went through the crowd — not laughter exactly, but the cousin of it, the sound people make when fear turns over and shows its other face. Corrigan heard it too. His jaw tightened, and for a moment Petra thought he would push it anyway, order the constables forward, turn a stalemate into a scene. Instead he closed his inventory book with a snap like a shot.

"Three days," he said, and his voice carried up the cliff path to where she stood. "That's how long your officer's objections buy you, Mrs. Halloran. Three days, and then I return with proper papers and proper numbers, and we will see how the village stands then." He put his hat on. "Armistice or no armistice. Remember that."

He marched back down the road. The constables followed. And the crowd did not cheer, because cheering was not the weapon they had chosen — they simply began, unhurried, to disperse, Mullen's cart groaning back to life, the widow pausing at the bottom of the tower stairs long enough to call up:

"Lamp room's cold tonight. I'll send Michael up with peat."

It was not defiance, precisely. It was supply lines. It was the village doing what the village had always done — keeping accounts, settling debts, making sure the light had what it needed to burn — and Petra stood in the cold gallery with the wind pulling her hair loose and understood that this, all of it, was the ledger now. Not paper. People. Every favor ever recorded in Tomas's careful hand had compounded, year over year, into this afternoon: a road full of neighbors who would stand in the wind for her, and say nothing to Corrigan, and mean it.

She went down to Nell, who had appeared from somewhere with her hands blackened from the forge and her eyes bright.

"He blinked," Nell said.

"For three days," Petra said. "Then he comes back with numbers enough that blinking won't save us."

"Then we finish the workshop." Nell said it simply, the way she said everything now, and went to bank the forge fire against the evening.

* * *

That night Petra wrote in the honest ledger — the flour, the nets, the peat promised, Michael's delivery logged in her own hand — and beneath the day's entry she added a line she did not read aloud, did not show anyone, and knew Quill would never ask to see:

*Village stands. Counted and found sufficient.*

Then she trimmed the wick, climbed the stairs, and set the light turning, and far out on the dark water the beam swept its slow circle over shoals and channels and the whole indifferent sea — three days' warning written in light, burning steadily toward whatever the dawn would bring.
