## 18. Armistice Eve

The rumor came up the cliff path before breakfast, riding in the pocket of the fish boy who delivered the O'Sheas' oil. *Signing at dawn tomorrow,* he said, breathless, hatless, his voice cracking on the word *dawn* like it was something holy. *Bells in Dublin. Bells everywhere.*

By noon it had reached the churchyard and been improved upon: signing by midnight, bells from every steeple in Christendom, ships sounding their horns until the sea itself rang. By two o'clock Mrs. Byrne had it that the war had already ended an hour ago and nobody had thought to tell Carrickmoor.

Nell heard all of it standing in the doorway of the lamp workshop with a rag in one hand and cerium oxide powder drying gray on her knuckles.

Her mother didn't stop grinding.

"You heard?" Nell said.

"I heard." Petra Halloran bent over the cracked segment of the Fresnel lens, the one they'd hauled down from the tower three weeks ago, the one whose fracture had nearly put a fishing boat onto the shoals in the second storm. She worked the polishing lap in slow circles, her shoulders doing the arithmetic of pressure and patience. "Rumors are weather, Nell. You don't trim your wick to them."

But Nell saw what her mother wouldn't say — saw it in the set of her jaw, in the way she'd laid out every tool that morning in the exact order she used them, as if arranging for a photograph. If the armistice signed, the requisition order died unsigned. The workshop stayed theirs. Everything they did today was a race against a signature in a room none of them would ever see.

"Then we finish tonight," Nell said.

Her mother looked up. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the sound of the sea filled the workshop like water filling a bowl.

"We finish tonight," Petra agreed.

They had not spoken more than necessary since the night Nell had stood at the top of the stairs and watched pages curl into ash in the forge. Since the argument about truth and mercy that had left a crack between them wider than the one in the lens. Nobody had apologized. Nobody had explained. But three days ago her mother had set the second polishing lap at the workbench's far end — Nell's end, the end that had been hers since she was nine years old and allowed to hold rags — and said nothing about it, and Nell had come down and taken her place, and that was the whole conversation.

Some things you said. Some things you ground into glass until they were smooth enough to see through.

* * *

The afternoon went long and gold, which felt like mockery. A day this beautiful should have belonged to peace already. Light came through the workshop's salt-fogged windows and lay across the bench in warm bars, and the smell of the place was its old smell — oil and stone dust and the faint sweetness of the pitch they used to seat the segments — and Nell let it close around her like a coat.

She worked the final segment. It was the worst of the seven, chipped along its inner face where the mechanism had seized, and it wanted hours of fine lapping before it would take polish. Her mother had done the heavy cutting weeks ago; what remained was the slow work, the work that couldn't be hurried because hurrying showed. Every scratch you left in a lens came back later as a flaw in the beam, a dark seam in the light thrown out over the water. Her father had taught her that. *The light forgives nothing,* Tomas Halloran used to say, holding a finished prism up to the window so the sun split through it and lay rainbows on his daughter's hands. *That's why we forgive everything else.*

She hadn't thought of that saying in months. It arrived now whole, unbidden, and she had to stop and press her rag against her eyes for a moment before she could go on.

Across the bench her mother worked without looking up, but Nell knew she'd noticed. Knew it the way she knew the tide table — not by being told but by living inside it. When Nell bent again to the lap, Petra slid the finer grit across the wood without a word, exactly when it was needed, exactly as if she'd read the state of the glass from across the room. Which she probably had.

This was how they were speaking now. Grit passed hand to hand. A lamp turned up without asking. A cup of tea set down at the elbow, still hot, meaning *I know you've been at this four hours.* Nell found, to her surprise, that she understood this language better than the other one — better than the arguments, the confessions, the terrible honesty they'd traded like blows. Words could be burned. Work couldn't. Work just accumulated, hour on hour, and became the thing it was going to become whether anyone signed anything or not.

At some point her mother began to hum. Low, tuneless, barely there — an old Carrickmoor keening tune worn down to almost nothing. Nell recognized it from the nights after Da died, when her mother had sat by the cold hearth making that sound instead of crying. She picked it up without deciding to, a half-beat behind, and for a while the two of them hummed together over the glass, and the workshop held them the way the tower held the light: without comment, without condition.

* * *

Quill came at dusk.

He knocked, which he always did, though the door stood open — a courtesy so consistent it had become almost comic, the man who investigated smugglers apologizing for entering a room. He looked worse than usual. His coat was buttoned wrong, one lapel higher than the other, and there was a fresh crease of worry between his eyebrows that hadn't been there last week.

"Mrs. Halloran. Miss Halloran." He nodded to each of them, then to the lens, and something in his face eased at the sight of it. "It's coming back."

"It's coming back," Petra said.

"May I?"

He didn't wait for the answer, just took the stool by the door and sat with his hat on his knee like a man in a waiting room, watching them work. This was another thing he did. In the early days Nell had found it unnerving — the officer with the tired eyes cataloguing everything — but she'd come to understand that Quill watching wasn't Quill hunting. It was Quill resting. There was something about the workshop that let him put his paperwork down, whatever the paperwork was made of.

They gave him twenty minutes of it. Then he cleared his throat.

"There'll be no more visits after tomorrow," he said. "One way or another."

Nell's lap stopped moving. Across the bench, her mother's didn't.

"The signing—" Petra began.

"If it comes, my orders lapse and I'm on the morning train regardless. If it doesn't come—" He turned his hat in his hands. "If it doesn't come, I'm recalled anyway. There are men in Dublin who've decided my inquiry has run its course, and they're not the men who sent me."

Petra set down her tool. It was a small motion, deliberate, the way she set down everything — as if even a burnisher deserved to be treated with respect — but Nell saw her mother's fingers stay curled after it, as if reluctant to be empty.

"Corrigan," Petra said.

Quill's mouth twitched. Not quite a smile. "You understand, I can't discuss—"

"He came here. He offered to make everything disappear if I gave him the ledger instead of you. You know this. You've known it since before the second storm."

"I suspected it," Quill said mildly, "in the way that a man suspects a leak in a boat by noticing he's wet."

"And now?"

"Now I've written my report. All of it. Names, dates, the shipping marks, the — the general shape of things." He was looking at the lens as he said it, at the great honeycomb eye of it, half-healed. "My superiors will receive a file in which Magistrate Corrigan appears rather more often than any fisherman does. Whether they act on it is above my station. But files have a way of surfacing, Mrs. Halloran. Paper is patient. It's the one thing I've learned in this job worth learning."

Nell kept her head down over the glass and listened with her whole body. *Paper is patient.* Her father had believed the opposite — or had believed it once, before he'd started keeping two ledgers, before the honest debts and the coded ones had begun to live side by side in the same book. Maybe paper wasn't patient. Maybe paper was just loyal to whoever fed it last.

"They'll circle him," Petra said. "His own people."

"For a while. Long enough, I think." Quill stood, settling his coat — righting the crooked lapel without seeming to notice he'd done it. "I'll say one thing more, and then I'll say nothing else, because I've said too much in every direction for a fortnight." He paused at the door, and the last of the daylight caught him from behind, so that Nell saw him mostly as outline. "Whatever gets decided in whatever room tomorrow — this village decided its own version months ago. I watched it happen. Sandbags. Suppers. Silence, when silence was the decent thing." A beat. "I intend to describe it accurately."

He knocked on the doorframe twice, softly, on his way out — a habit, a tic, a small percussion of a man announcing himself to rooms even as he left them — and then his footsteps went down the path toward the village, unhurried, a man walking home.

Petra stood a long moment with her hand flat on the bench.

"Nell," she said quietly. "The segment. Before the light goes."

Nell bent back to the work. But she carried Quill's words with her into it, the way you carry a coal in a shovel — carefully, aware it could warm you or burn you, depending on how long you held it.

* * *

They lit the workshop lamps at full dark and worked on.

There was no question of stopping. The rumor said dawn; if the rumor was true, then every hour between now and first light was an hour in which the requisition order lived or died on someone else's pen, and the only power any of them had over it was the power to have the lens finished when the news came. So they worked. Nell lapped the chip down level, checked it against the straightedge, lapped again. Her mother seated the repaired segments in their brass frame one by one, testing each with a candle flame held close, watching the doubled reflections align. Twice she found a hairline gap and reseated it. The third time it held.

Around eleven the wind rose, and Nell's stomach clenched with the old fear — *another storm, not now, not tonight* — but it settled again, just the ordinary winter breathing of the coast, gusts rattling the windows and then letting go. Somewhere below, the sea worked at the rocks with its endless patience.

It was near midnight when her mother spoke.

"When you were small," Petra said, not looking up from the frame, "you asked why we keep score at all."

Nell's hands went still on the lap. She remembered the question. She remembered asking it, aged ten, sitting on this very floor sorting screws while her mother entered flour and oil in the ledger. *Why do we write it down? If people are good, they pay. If they're not, writing it won't make them.*

"You never answered," Nell said.

"No. I didn't know the answer yet." Her mother fitted the last segment into the frame and pressed it home with both palms, gently, the way you'd set a sleeping child down. "I think I know it now. We keep score so that nobody's kindness disappears. That's all it ever was. Your father forgot that somewhere along the way, and so did I, for a while. A ledger isn't a weapon, Nell. It's a memory. And memory is only a weapon if you choose to use it as one."

Nell looked at her mother across the lamplit bench — at the gray powder on her hands, the lines the storms and the winters had written at the corners of her eyes, the stubborn set of a woman who had spent a year carrying a village and a dead man's secrets and a twelve-year-old's hard questions, and had carried all of it without once setting it down.

"I'm glad it was you," Nell said. It came out smaller than she meant it. "In the inquiry. Saying only what you said. I'm glad it was you and not me, because I'd have told everything, and everything would have broken us."

Her mother came around the bench. She didn't hug her — they weren't, either of them, built for that kind of repair — but she put her powder-gray hand over Nell's powder-gray hand, on the glass between them, and pressed once.

"You'd have told the truth," Petra said. "And the truth would have needed us afterward to mend what it broke. That's the difference between us, maybe. I mend first and tell after." A pause, and something moved through her face that was almost a smile. "Your father mended and never told at all. Look how that went."

Outside, faint on the wind, came a sound that stopped them both.

A bell. Far off, muffled by miles and weather — but ringing. Not the church bell, which they knew note by note. Something farther. Something east, toward the town, toward the world.

Then another, joining it. Then a third, ragged, off-key, unmistakably human with joy.

Nell was at the window before she knew she'd moved. Down the cliff, lights were blooming in the village windows one by one, doors opening, figures spilling into the lane with lamps held high like a constellation shaking loose from the sky. Someone was running up the cliff path toward the lighthouse, waving both arms, shouting words the wind tore apart — but the bells said it plain enough.

Dawn, the rumor had said. Dawn tomorrow.

The world had signed early.

Behind her, her mother stood with one hand still resting on the finished lens, and in the lamplight Nell saw her close her eyes — saw her shoulders drop a full inch, a year falling off her all at once — and when she opened them again they were wet, and she was laughing, actually laughing, a sound Nell hadn't heard since before the letter with the customs seal had come.

"It held," Petra said, to the lens, to the room, to the dead and the living alike. "We finished, and it held."

Down the path the runner was closer now, and his words were arriving whole at last, torn free of the wind:

*"It's over! Mrs. Halloran — it's over — the bells — they're ringing in Dublin —"* 

And beneath his voice, under the bells, under the sea, Nell heard the other sound rising from the harbor road: wheels. Heavy ones. More than one carriage, moving fast, coming up through the village at an hour when no honest carriage traveled — and her mother heard it too, because the laughter stopped as if cut with a knife, and her hand closed hard on the edge of the workbench.

"That's not celebration," Petra said quietly. "Those are government horses."
